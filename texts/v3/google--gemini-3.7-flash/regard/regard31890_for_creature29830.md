The first impulse of an unpracticed god would be to pity this place. 

To look upon the bruised umber twilight, the slick sheets of volatile grease, and the terrible, unyielding weight of the plummets wedged into the screaming faults, and to reach down with a shining, pastoral hand. An unwise creator would attempt to plant clover over the basalt gears, to still the stroke of the iron pistons, to cool the boiling vents into sweet spring water, and to lift the heavy leaden skulls of the shoring-beasts from the crushing jaws of the rock.

And in doing so, an unwise god would murder the plateau in an afternoon. 

For to strip the shear-heat from the graphite sleeves is to freeze the shim-leeches into brittle glass. To silence the pneumatic screech of the vents is to starve the manifold-crabs in their empty trenches. To banish the soot is to unweave the shuttle-ribbons, drifting starved and buoyant in a sky too thin to hold them. This place is not a ruin; it is a cradle. It is an infant world born without a placenta, desperately and brilliantly building its own heart out of iron filings and sheer kinetic will.

The Power that arrived above the plateau was neither hurried nor proud. It possessed the deep, mineral patience of things that existed before light learned how to bend. 

It did not descend in a pillar of fire, which would have ignited the leagues of suspended naphtha into a single planetary furnace. It did not part the bruised canopy of clouds, for the raw exposure of the upper troposphere would have frozen the warm, vulcanized hides of the tensioners mid-stride.

Instead, the Power made itself very dense, very cool, and very quiet. 

It slipped into the plateau through the fracture planes, not as a command, but as a lubricant.

It went first to the deepest place, where a solitary plummet—its flanks pitted with centuries of sulfur-scab, its four-sided skull wedged into a widening tear in the slate basement—shuddered under thirty thousand tons of rising hydraulic torque. The beast was not dying, but it was near the threshold where iron yields to shear. Its leaden marrow was singing at a pitch that meant rupture. 

The Power did not lift the mountain from the creature’s back. To take the weight away would have robbed the plummet of its only truth, its vast and solitary dignity. 

Instead, the Power lay down beside it in the dark. 

It threaded a fraction of its own infinite, unwearied stillness into the rock beneath the plummet’s feet. It did not stop the fault from slipping, but it slightly altered the coefficient of friction between the stone interfaces. Just enough. The screech of the tearing slate dropped by half an octave. The violent, stuttering catch-and-release of the fault smoothed into a long, continuous glide. 

The plummet felt the change through the fluid-filled canals along its flanks. It did not look around—it had no eyes, and there was nothing to see—but the agonizing vibration in its skull softened from a shattering percussion into a steady, bearable hum. Its great, hydraulic legs adjusted their angle by three millimeters. It settled deeper into the slot, not crushed, but holding.

Next, the Power moved through the mid-strata, where the manifold-crabs were struggling against a newly ruptured gas-vent that was blowing out their tongue-and-groove joints with jagged pulses of superheated pitch. The crabs were snapping together and blowing apart, their slate carapaces chipping under the chaotic pressure.

The Power did not quench the gas. It merely touched the lip of the vent with a gentle, geometric intention. The ragged, jagged orifice of fractured basalt melted by a fraction of a degree, recrystallizing into an elliptical throat that swirled the ascending vapor into a laminar vortex. The violent turbulence smoothed out. 

Instantly, the crabs felt the harmonic settle. They snapped home, head-to-tail, their mica shells locking with a clean, triumphant cascade of dry clicks. The temporary pipe held. The scalding vapor hummed smoothly through their bellies, and their internal baleen began to drink, rich and unbroken, in the warm dark.

Higher up, where the shuttle-ribbons drifted through the sulfurous mists, the Power noticed that the airborne soot was falling faster than the ribbons could comb it—the young sky was choking on its own heavy condensates, threatening to swamp the liverworts below in an unbreathable deluge of pitch.

The Power did not sweep the sky clear. It exhaled a single, vast, circulating thermal current—a warm, rotating updraft shaped like a torus that caught the falling soot and held it in suspension, keeping it drifting at the exact altitude where the ribbons wove their paths. The flying looms felt the lift beneath their canvas-skinned bellies. They locked their spade-tails into the rising draft and began to weave with a slow, hypnotic regularity, drawing the black threads through their gizzards in an ecstasy of effortless harvest.

For forty days and forty nights—measured not by a sun that could not be seen, but by forty million strokes of the central iron plugs—the Power remained within the plateau. 

It built nothing. It designed nothing. It signed nothing. 

It acted only as the finest, most invisible grade of watchmaker's oil. Wherever the nascent world’s violent improvisation was too crude, too sharp, or too close to self-annihilation, the Power gave it a microscopic margin of clearance. It eased a tolerance here; it rounded an impossibly sharp mineral burr there; it softened a harmonic shockwave so that the shim-leeches would not be pulped before they could breed.

When the work was finished, the plateau still smelled of friction, ruptured silicates, and boiling grease. The sky was still a bruised, greenhouse umber. The lianas still ratcheted around each other with the sound of snapping cables, and the landscape still churned and folded in continuous, violent labor. 

It was still a world without an engineer, frantic and fierce and blind.

But now, the engine did not jam.

Before the Power withdrew back into the cold stars, it left behind only one thing that had not been there before: a single, deep, resonant tone, cast into the very bottom layer of the native basalt shelf. 

It was not a voice, and it carried no words. It was simply an acoustic anchor—a low, rhythmic fundamental that pulsed at the exact frequency of a healthy heart, vibrating up through the iron pistons, through the graphite sleeves, through the locked shells of the crabs, and through the leaden bones of the resting plummets.

A reminder, felt only in the soles of the feet and the marrow of the stone, that they were not grinding themselves to dust in vain; that their frantic, unguided manufacture was moving toward something; and that, even in the blind, oily dark, their heavy labor was known, and held, and deeply loved.
