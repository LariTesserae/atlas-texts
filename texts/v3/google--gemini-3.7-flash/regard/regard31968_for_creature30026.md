The power that turned its attention to the plateau did not come with the roar of fire or the sudden, blinding violence of the sun. 

Had it arrived as lesser gods often do—bearing heat, soft soils, green seeds, and the sweet rot of shallow shores—it would have committed a terrible massacre. A sudden warmth would have liquefied the dense blubber of the pale mantles, rotting them in their joints before their hooks could release. Sunlight would have scorched the cataracts of the blind cleavers and blistered the skin of the milk-glass rib-fish, who knew only the map of pressure. To "heal" this place by turning it into a garden would have been the cruelest kind of ignorance.

True wisdom is, first and foremost, the capacity to perceive a thing’s perfection on its own terms.

The benevolent power understood the severe poetry of the terrace. It saw that these creatures did not suffer their environment; they were the environment made flesh, carved out of stone, velocity, and cold ballast. They were complete. They were heroic in their absolute refusal of softness.

And so, the power did not alter the water. It did not tame the freezing flumes, nor did it quiet the screaming vortices that sheared across the basalt domes. It did not melt the ice in the high peaks far above, nor did it summon the light of dawn down into the bruised indigo dusk.

Instead, it made itself small, dense, and infinitely patient. It descended through the fathoms not as a radiant sun, but as a cool, pervasive quietude—a presence with the specific gravity of basalt and the clear indifference of deep water.

First, it sank to the bottom of all things: into the subterranean seams where the courses of fitted megaliths descended into the black. 

The power felt the agony of the masonry. It felt how millions of years of hydraulic hammering were wearing down the tolerances; how the suture-crawlers and grout-leeches worked without rest, dying in their trillions to pack the cracks with their leaden carapaces, only for fifty-ton slabs to occasionally slip, shear, and tumble into the bottomless trench, carrying entire lineages into oblivion.

The power did not fuse the blocks. To fuse them would be to rob the suture-crawlers of their purpose and their food. Instead, the power slipped into the micro-pores of the stone as a subtle, elastic tension. It became an invisible, mineral marrow within the iron-gray basalt. It gave the drowned plateau a microscopic resilience, so that when the great cross-currents collided and the downward drafts struck like falling mountains, the stone did not shatter or lurch. The megaliths settled into one another with a profound, immovable ease. The plateau ceased to be an assemblage of loose bones; it became an enduring skeleton that would never fall.

Next, the power moved to the avenues where the *gale-weavers* hung their living bridges. 

It watched the thousands of interlocking brittle-stars shuddering under the immense drag of the drift, straining until their vulcanized tendons frayed. The power touched the cold flume that rushed over them. It did not slow the water, but it subtly altered the geometry of the micro-eddies just inches above the hexagonal lattices. With that infinitesimal adjustment, the tearing shear was converted into a gentle, downward spiral, laying the mineral snow and copepod larvae directly into the waiting arms of the weavers with twice the density and half the violence. The lattices no longer tore; they sang their low, resonant note not in desperation, but in deep, rhythmic fullness.

Then, the power sought out the oldest of the *pale mantles*.

It found one wedged beneath a massive triumphal archway that had stood since before the plateau was drowned. The creature was ancient beyond reckoning; its gypsum-colored skin was calloused to iron, its anchor-hooks fused so deeply into the masonry that stone and flesh had become a single substance. It was weary. For three hundred years, the freezing flume had hammered against its mantle, wearing away its tissue faster than it could heal, forcing it to hold the full weight of the mountain current alone in the dark.

The power did not move the mantle. It did not ask it to let go. 

Instead, the power rested its presence directly against the creature’s scarred, tuberculated flanks. It matched the freezing temperature of the water, matched the dense pressure of the depths, and simply *braced* the mantle from behind. For the first time in centuries, the old cephalopod felt the crushing hydraulic drag lift from its anchor-limbs. The current still howled, the prey still drifted into its waiting reach, but the killing strain was gone. In the eternal twilight beneath the arch, the mantle relaxed its calcified muscle by a single, blissful fraction of an inch, anchored not just to the rock, but to an unyielding kindness.

Finally, the power rose into the wide, blue-gray expanses where the *cleavers* soared.

It glided alongside a thirty-pace leviathan, keeping pace with its blade-like, cartilaginous flank as the blind beast banked through the colonnades. The cleaver opened its throat, releasing its deep, percussive click—a sub-bass groan cast into the dark to map the pillars and voids.

Normally, the sound would return as a flat, cold slap of bare basalt, fading quickly into the hiss of the current.

The power reached into the acoustic architecture of the stone. It touched the pillars, the worn archways, the soaring megalithic domes, and tuned them.

When the cleaver’s click struck the distant masonry, the stone did not merely reflect the sound; it *rang*. It answered with a rich, sustained harmonic, a low, bell-like resonance that rolled through the freezing avenues for leagues. Arch answered to arch; foundation hummed to pillar. 

The blind cleaver banked hard, its hydrodynamic keels cutting through the flume as its skin-pits drank in the sound. For the first time, the drowned plateau was not an obstacle course of silent, dead hazards, but a vast, ringing cathedral. The leviathan tilted its upright body into the draft, riding the lift of the current with effortless majesty, navigating not by avoidance, but by following the magnificent music the stone now made in the dark.

Its work finished, the benevolent power did not leave, nor did it announce itself. It dissolved entirely into the system.

It remained as the strength in the masonry, the cradle beneath the weaver-nets, the brace behind the mantle, and the song inside the stone. 

Above, the bruised indigo light did not change. The ice-cold rivers still tore through the void without mercy. Life on the plateau remained hard, silent, and severe. But it was no longer forgotten; it was sustained, held within a quiet, unshakeable grace that loved the stone precisely because it was stone, and the water precisely because it was cold.
