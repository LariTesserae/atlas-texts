When the watcher came, it came without moisture. 

It had passed through spheres of green rot, through worlds that drowned in their own weeping seas, where life was a soft, frantic spasm of mucus and red decay. It had seen gods who mistook rain for mercy and smothered their creations in silt and swamp.

This watcher was older than the error of wetness. It possessed the cold, immaculate wisdom of a geometer and the quiet benevolence of an anvil that has learned to love the hammer.

It hovered in the pale, dry sky, high above the thermal circuits where the Sails circled. It did not cast a shadow; to block the sun would be to rob the mesas of the heat that kept their blood-tallow liquid. It did not bring a cloud; a single drop of water would have pitted the glazed tortoiseshell of the cliff-dwellers’ skin, dissolved the chalk roads of the Steppers, and fouled the fine graphite lubricants of the house-sized wheels.

The watcher looked upon the canyon world, and it did not pity it. It admired it.

It saw the narrow-chested people hanging in their counterweighted webbing, their breath smelling of sweet parched wax, their hearts beating in long, steady measures of three minutes. It saw the blind oil-beetles grooming the brass journals with their brush-limbs. It saw the calcified worker built into the lintel of an extraction plant, shoulder to shoulder with the shale, holding up three thousand tons of iron retort without rot or complaint.

*Here,* the watcher thought, *is a creation that does not leak.*

It saw their beauty: that they were not fallen creatures waiting to be restored to some soft, muddy garden, but a finished art, annealed by wind and stone into an absolute, frictionless dignity.

Yet wisdom perceives where even the hardest stone must wear.

The watcher listened to the iron deckplates. It heard the lead-headed mallets striking the high-tension cables, sending consonant clicks and shivering codes across the five-mile abysses. It heard how the sound decayed—how the vastness of the empty air swallowed the notes before they could reach the furthest outposts, leaving each cliff-city half-deaf to the song of the others. 

It felt the deep roots of the mesas, where the sulfur flues and hydrocarbon vents drew their life from volcanic chambers that were, by the slow mathematics of geology, cooling by a fraction of a degree every ten thousand years.

And so, the benevolent power acted. It did not descend with a shout. It worked as the heat works: invisibly, from within.

First, it sank its mind into the core of the world. It reached into the deep, sluggish mantle beneath the chalk roots and tied a knot in the tectonic fire. It ensured that the paraffin vents would never choke on slag, that the sweet, heavy vapors would flow through the three-man pipes with an unvarying, pulse-true pressure for a hundred million years. It made the hearth of the planet immortal, so that the dry people would never know the death of cold.

Second, it laid a gentle hand upon the high gulfs between the peaks. 

It did not alter the air, but it tuned the tension of the iron ropes. It blessed the metal with an impossible, lossless acoustic resonance. 

That evening, when a worker on the outermost platform of a copper refinery struck the signal cable with his lead mallet—*clack-clack-thrum*—to report the passing of an ore-bucket, the sound did not die in the heat-shimmer. 

The note traveled along the braided steel, leaped the five-mile void without thinning, hit the anchor-pins of the opposite massif, and rang clear as a struck crystal. Then it went further. It hummed through the iron bones of the next town, through the suspension bridges, along the shelf-lines where the Steppers rested, down into the deep rafters where the sleeping hung. 

For the first time since the foundries were anchored to the rock, the settlements heard one another not as distant, dying taps, but as a single, braided choir. A mallet struck on the westernmost needle echoed in the brass cathedrals of the east, pure and dry and harmonic, turning the entire continent of cliffs into a single great lyre.

The workers did not drop their tools. They did not fall to their knees in terror. That was the behavior of soft, wet things.

Instead, the workers on the catwalks paused their rasping. They adjusted their quartz goggles against the glare and set their lead mallets lightly against the vibrating steel. They felt the rhythm of distant flywheels, leagues away, interlocking with the rhythm of their own. They clicked their tongues against their palates in brief, dry recognition: *The span holds. The system is whole.*

Lastly, the watcher turned its attention to the highest sky.

It saw the great Sails dying—the ancient ones whose hollow silicate bones had grown too heavy with organic salts, whose amber membranes had thinned after centuries of soaring, and who must eventually drift downward to be snared by brass hooks and rendered into tallow.

The watcher did not forbid their death; death here was geometry, and the towns required the oil and the window-panes. But for those old beasts who still wished to climb, the watcher carved with its will a permanent thermal spiral—a great, rising chimney of heat above the deepest chasm that would never collapse, never cool, and never drift.

A Sail entered it that night. 

Its amber wings caught the column of dry, upward-rushing air. It ceased to bank. It ceased to search. It was lifted smoothly, effortlessly, through the white wash of the sun, past the limits of the thin atmosphere, into the silent black porch of space where the stars were as sharp and dry as diamond dust. There it remained, drifting on the breath of the watcher's chimney, glowing faintly with its internal chemiluminescence, an immortal lantern anchored between the sun and the stone.

Its work finished, the watcher withdrew. 

It left no temples. It left no holy books, for there was no paper to bear them and no damp ink to write them. It left only a warmer fire in the deep pipes, a clearer chime in the long wires, and an open road in the sky.

Below, the house-sized flywheels turned—once every three minutes. The blind hematite beetles stroked the brass hubs with continuous, oily devotion. The porcelain Steppers took another slow, vacuum-held pace across the sheer chalk face, chewing sulfur from the rock. And in the high towers, the people swung gently in their webbing, asleep in the hot, parched dark, their dreams humming in time with the stone that held them.
