A power that was merely mighty would have torn the mountain open.

It would have split the roof of shale like a dry rind, flooding the black cleft with the blazing, noon-day fury of the sun. It would have sent down great torrents of sweet, warm air and carpets of clover; it would have dried the glistening walls and brought forth singing birds.

And in doing so, it would have annihilated them.

The sudden light would have burned their pale, watery eyes into charred ash. The dry heat would have cracked their grease-sheathed skin down to the raw tendon. The eels would have suffocated in the warm shallows; the midges would have died in the desiccated silt; the sinew bridges would have shriveled and snapped like dry straw, dropping whole generations into the sump. The noise of an open sky—the terrifying, roofless emptiness of it—would have broken their minds, which were built to feel the comforting, clamped weight of two stone walls separated by three arm-spans of mist.

A mighty power would have judged their world a prison, and in "freeing" them, enacted a massacre.

But this power was *wise*, which meant it understood that life does not ask to be remade in another’s image; it asks only for the dignity of its own shape. And it was *benevolent*, which meant its love was patient, subtle, and terrifyingly gentle.

The Power did not speak. In a place where speech was the percussive click of bone and the deep, chest-hum of marrow against palm, a voice from the heavens would have been a catastrophic deafening.

Instead, it sank into the stone. It became an adjustment of tolerances—a shift of millimeters, degrees, and mineral grains.

***

**The first thing it did was ease the chisel.**

The Power did not turn the black ironstone to clay; the dwellers took pride in the brute, stubborn resistance of their home. A niche won without labor had no worth. 

Instead, it touched the deep crystalline lattice of the shale. It introduced micro-cleavages—invisible, microscopic fault lines running parallel to the bedding planes. Where a copper chisel had once required forty strikes to flake away a thumb’s-width of rock, it now required thirty-two. The stone still rang with a sharp, honest *tock*, but the jar through the wrists of the carvers was softened. The splintered fragments fell away more cleanly, leaving the floors of the new cells flatter, less prone to holding stagnant pools of silt. 

A carver resting his copper punch against the wall felt only that his arm was particularly steady that morning. He clicked his bone clackers to his neighbor across the gap: *Clean vein today.* His neighbor clicked back: *Strike deep.*

**The second thing it did was tend to the ache in their bones.**

In the eternal, saturated chill, the old ones suffered a slow, calcifying agony. Their joints—hardened by decades of wedging into slick clefts—swelled and locked until an elder could no longer climb the bone ladders to the scraper-terraces.

The Power did not bring fire, which would have filled the narrow flue with suffocating smoke. Instead, three miles below the lowest eel-traps, it drew a vein of deep geothermal warmth just half a finger closer to the crust. 

It routed this warmth through a seam of dense, dark ironstone that ran behind the sleeping troughs of the middle tiers. 

That night, when a tired midge-catcher slid into his trough and pulled the cold eel-skin over his chest, he did not shiver for the usual hour while his body fought the wet stone. The rock beneath his spine was not hot—it was scarcely warmer than a living palm—but the dead, leeching cold was gone. It was the warmth of an animal that had laid on the stone before him. 

The dampness did not evaporate—they needed the moisture to breathe—but the deep, grinding iron-ache in his hips uncoiled like a loosening knot. He laid his chin upon the carved ledge, let the water mist his face, and for the first time in his memory, slept without curling his toes into fists to fight the frost.

**The third thing it did was soften the scream of the chasm.**

The roar of the falling water was the beat of their hearts, but its violence took a tax. Over decades, it dulled their hearing until the elders could only communicate through the deep hums pressed directly into flesh.

The Power did not stop the rain. It reached up into the infinite dark above, where the warm air condensed against the high vaults, and shaped the stone lips from which the droplets fell. 

It altered the trajectory of the falling streams by fractions of an inch, directing them away from the flat, drum-like shelves of split slate and guiding them into the natural, lime-softened chutes and fluted flowstones. 

The hydraulic scream of the chasm did not vanish—silence would have terrified them, like the stopping of a heart—but the acoustic violence smoothed. The harsh, metallic clatter of the water against ironstone became a low, rounded drone, rich and velvet-thick. The sound of the mist fell from a thundering hiss into a sustained, rhythmic murmur that held the city like a cradle. 

Across the void, the *clack-clack-clack* of the bone castanets became startlingly clear. A mother forty tiers up clicked a rhythm of caution; her child, three terraces below, heard the crisp, distinct snap without straining against the roar, and paused on the wet bone rung before his foot could slip.

**The fourth thing it did was bless the harvest.**

The Power touched the pale, milky colonies of anaerobic bacteria that bled down the limestone sills. 

It did not change their color, nor did it remove the sour, effervescent bite that numbed the tongue. But it braided into their chemistry three new complex amino chains—molecules that synthesized within the dark, mineral broth. 

When the scum-scrapers peeled the curd from the flowstone and fed it to the young, the children’s gums stopped bleeding during the long, lean months. Their teeth grew hard and yellow as walrus tusk, rooted deep in their jaws. The infants strapped to the safety lines grew thicker fat beneath their turnip-pale skin, and their broad, spatulate feet gripped the wet slate with a new, tire-rubber tenacity.

And in the deepest, blackest sumps, where the blind eels wallowed in the mud, the Power made the females lay eggs not once in seven seasons, but once in four. The wicker traps, hauled up through the roaring twilight on braided sinew ropes, came up heavy, slick, and thrashing more often than they had in three hundred years.

***

The Power stayed for a long time, hovering unseen in the mist, watching the city breathe.

It watched an elder sit on a bone balcony, scraping the bioluminescent curd into a bowl of eel-skull with a smoothed antler spoon. 

It watched two young dwellers lean out over the yawning, three-hundred-span drop, bracing their broad, tough feet against the slick door-sills, pressing their wet palms together in the center of the void to share the vibrating, wordless hum of affection. 

It watched a child tap a river-cobble against the wall—*tock, tock, tock*—making a pocket for a tiny dish of eel-fat fire.

The Power did not reveal itself. It did not ask for a single prayer, nor did it leave a tablet of stone with rules for how they must live. It knew that to demand worship is the impulse of an insecure king, while wisdom finds its joy merely in seeing a good thing continue to exist on its own terms.

Before it turned its attention elsewhere, the Power did one last thing. 

On the lintel of the oldest carved cell in the fissure, a flat-headed, translucent salamander sat motionless in the damp. The Power brushed the tiny, cold creature with a thought.

From that day on, when a dweller was climbing in the dark, far from the light of the eel-lamps, and their foot slipped on a rung of greasy bone, the salamanders on the nearby walls would pulse with a sudden, brilliant, lime-green glow for the space of a single heartbeat—just long enough for the falling hand to find the rock, close its calloused fingers around a notch of ironstone, and hold fast.

Then the Power withdrew, leaving the fissure as it had found it:

Cold. Wet. Violent with sound. 

Clamped between two infinite walls of black stone, where the yellow fires burned straight and still in the draftless dark, and a fierce, unbroken people went on living in the only universe they needed.
