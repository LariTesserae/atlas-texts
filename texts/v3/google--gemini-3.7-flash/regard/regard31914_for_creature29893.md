The power did not descend with wings or thunder. In a place where air is already too thin to waste on noise, the arrival of anything vast and loud would have torn the swifts’ nests from the granite and scattered the pika’s curing-slabs into the abyss.

Instead, the attention of the power arrived the way an extra octave of light might settle into a sunbeam: weightless, pervasive, and without mass.

A foolish power, moved by pity, would have warmed the notch. It would have softened the wind, thickened the air, and melted the black ice from the shadow-lines. And in doing so, it would have murdered everything within three days. The cushion-plants would have rotted in the stagnant heat; the beetles’ glycols would have fermented their blood; the swifts would have lost the high-velocity thermals that kept their broods alive; and the two Ash-Lungs, waking to find their sky-flint crumbly and the medicinal resin thinned to useless water, would have had nothing to carry down to the valley to buy their children’s winter flour.

Wisdom does not dismantle a machine to prove it loves the cogs. 

The power understood the notch immediately, down to the crystallization of the beetle’s internal sugars and the salt-content of the dried hare-fat in the stone pot. It saw that the beauty of this place was not separate from its cruelty; they were the same dark, flashing blade.

And so, the power chose its interventions with the precision of a knapper striking obsidian: never touching the edge itself, only bracing the core so the blow would not shatter the whole stone.

### The First Work: The Anchor in the Deep Seam

Beneath the northern spire, two miles below the root-collars of the miniature willows, the power felt the slow, grinding agony of the tectonic hinge. A hairline fracture—born when the peak was first shoved up through the cloud-deck—was migrating upward toward the cradle. In seventy years, an ordinary winter freeze would have wedged that joint apart, dropping the entire three-hundred-yard shelf into the six-thousand-pace void below, carrying moss, bone, flint, and sleepers down like loose gravel.

The power did not fuse the rock with miraculous fire. It simply leaned its vast, cool will into the fault, shifting the stress-vectors three inches to the west, directing the tectonic ache into the deep, dead root of the southern spire where no moss grew. 

The cradle remained precarious—it would always tremble when the high storms slammed against the teeth of the granite—but the floor would hold for ten thousand more summers. The pikas would not lose their cellar; the swifts would find their rock face still standing when they returned from the south.

***

### The Second Work: The Third Breath

The hardest hour in the notch is the third hour after midnight, when the frost-dome under the marmot skins has grown thick and airless. 

At four in the morning, when the temperature plunges to its absolute nadir and the body’s metabolic furnace begins to sputter, the human brain starves quietly for oxygen. In that gray, suffocating stupor, the will to live sometimes simply unhooks itself. An Ash-Lung will stop fighting to draw the thin air through nostrils crusted with blood-scabs; they will drift from torpor into the painless white sleep from which no one wakes.

The power did not warm their limbs. It did not melt the ice from their eyebrows.

Instead, when the younger of the two gatherers felt his heart slowing to a flutter like a dying moth’s, the power touched the memory behind his eyes. It did not bring a vision of gods or golden cities. It brought the exact, sharp smell of wet valley-dirt after a summer rain; the rough, grease-warmed palm of his daughter pressing against his cheek; the heavy, satisfying *thump* of a full sack of grain dropped on a dry pine floor.

The man’s chest heaved. His diaphragm, stubborn and knotty as juniper-wood, knotted itself against his ribs. He dragged in the bitter, cold air beneath the pelt, his lungs burning with the raw, metallic taste of altitude, and his thick, calloused arm tightened around his companion’s shoulders to share what little steam remained between them.

He stayed alive because he was reminded of what the resin bought.

***

### The Third Work: The Amber Virtue

When the sun finally struck the eastern needle and the white glare washed over the frost, the power moved through the wet seams where the cushion-plants were beginning to sweat their amber sap.

It did not make the resin more plentiful; abundance would have invited lowland armies and greedy traders to haul ladders up the cliff and strip the notch to barren gravel within a generation. 

Instead, the power touched the molecular knots within the sap itself. It deepened the bitter, antiseptic bite of the gum just enough that when a village healer in the swampy lowlands laid a strip of the dried gut over a gangrenous wound, the rot would halt two days sooner than it used to. It ensured that the labor of these two men—their cracked fingernails, their roasted-walnut skin, their strained and thickened hearts—would carry an absolute, undeniable mercy to the people who never saw the sky-needles.

***

### The Witness

Lastly, because the power was benevolent, it did what the universe so rarely does for the small and the fierce: it watched.

It watched the black-and-gold beetle twitch its legs and begin its frantic morning sprint across the chartreuse cushion. It watched the translucent-eared pika lay its primrose heads in perfect, single-layered grids on the hot slate slab. It watched the pale stoat hang by four claws from a vertical mirror of quartz, whiskers trembling in the downdraft, beautiful and lethal and completely without malice.

And behind the knee-high windbreak of dry-stacked slate, as the frost began to steam off the marmot skins, an old gatherer pushed back the hood of his cape. His eyes, swollen and webbed with red veins from the glare, looked out across the three-hundred-yard cradle, over the razor-rim, and into the pale, empty blue of the six-thousand-pace drop.

He did not know that an ancient, quiet intelligence was resting its gaze upon his brow. He only felt, for a fleeting moment between the strike of the flint and the spark on the dry tinder, that the immense emptiness around him was not vacant.

He spat a dark drop of blood-phlegm onto the slate, gripped his bone awl with fingers hard as antler, and went to his knees to begin the work.
