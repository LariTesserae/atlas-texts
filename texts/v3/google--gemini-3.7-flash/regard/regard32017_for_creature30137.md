The Power that looked down through the billions of tons of shale did not possess eyes of flesh, nor did it speak in the vibrations of air, which it knew would die instantly in the limestone damp.

It possessed only comprehension, which is the root of wisdom, and tenderness, which is the root of benevolence.

It observed the great hollow for an epoch of their measure, though to the Power it was only the drawing of a single breath. It watched the frantic choreography of the Hands: the long, milk-pale fingers tapping out urgent geometries on leather harnesses; the blind Ounces pressing their slate flanks into the harness-straps, their twelve pairs of suction pads rhythmically clearing the channels; the paper-thin Cilia-Flocks diving like descending clouds of parchment to clear the newborn spores from the stone.

The Power saw that their work was not an empire building toward a summit, nor was it a madness of the mind. It was the purest, most exquisite distillation of the will to live: an entire species turning themselves into a perpetual liquid, a living river of muscle and bone, solely to outrun the petrifying bite of their own sky.

A lesser force might have shattered the ceiling to let in the sun. But the Power knew that the sun would blind their lidless, rheum-shielded eyes, sear their translucent lard-pale skin, and collapse the delicate thermal drafts that kept the Cilia-Flocks aloft. 

A clumsy god might have banished the stone entirely, or commanded them to be still. But to force stillness upon a creature whose very soul has been forged in the crucible of perpetual motion is not mercy; it is a violent paralysis.

Wisdom does not break the world to heal it. Wisdom changes the rules so quietly that the world believes it has healed itself.

***

The change did not begin with an earthquake. It began in the seams.

Hundreds of feet overhead, the blinding, unblinking glare of the selenite and cold phosphorus began to alter its wavelength. The change was so slow that no single shift-gang noticed it at first. The harsh, surgical white that had shone without a second’s pause for ten thousand years softened, very gradually, into the hue of deep river-ice, and then to a faint, dusky periwinkle. 

And for the first time since the hollow was carved from the bedrock, the light carried something impossible: *temperature*.

It was not a scorching heat, but a faint, pervasive warmth—the gentle, brooding warmth of a mother’s palm held an inch above sleeping skin. 

As the warmth touched the weeping ceiling, the chemistry of the great vault began to yield. The heavy, saturated waters—pregnant with liquid gypsum, sharp flint, and petrifying lime—felt the intervention of the Power. The mineral hunger in the droplets was untied, smoothed, and neutralized. The water that drifted down through the phosphorus air was no longer a liquid mortar looking for an anchor; it became soft, inert mist, carrying only the clean, cool mineral nourishment that washed the chalky tracks from the workers’ cheeks without leaving a crust behind.

On the great flagstone floor, three leagues out on the central plain, a work-gang was in the frantic middle of dismantling a four-tiered basalt terrace. 

A carrier—a gaunt, broad-footed worker whose skin bore the blue-black tracery of veins like rivers across ice—lifted a ninety-pound block of water-worn shale. His long, double-jointed fingers were locked in the habitual strain of the pass. He turned to hand it down the chain, but his partner had slipped on the wet mica sheen, catching an elbow on a guide-wall.

The block fell.

It struck the damp flagstones with a dull, muffled thud and settled into a shallow pool of condensation.

In the old world, a dropped block was an emergency. Within hours, the calcifying mist would weld its bottom face to the bedrock; within days, it would become an immovable tumor in the avenue, requiring hours of abrasive sand and bone chisels to plane away.

The carrier dropped to his knees, his broad, flat pads slapping the stone. His fingers fluttered in a panicked, silent blur of taps against the flagstones—a signal for levers, for bone wedges, for the Ounces to bring the drag-ropes before the stone could take root.

His partner scrambled up, reaching for the pry-bar.

Yet, as they reached the block, something hesitated through their feet. 

Through the thick, calloused horn of their soles, there was no sharp, brittle shudder of mineral crystallization. The stone was not seizing. The moisture around the base of the block remained fluid, swirling softly like clear water over river-silt. 

The carrier extended a trembling, six-inch finger. He touched the joint where the shale met the flagstone. 

It was wet. Only wet. 

He pushed. The block slid smoothly across the film of water, leaving a faint grey wake that did not harden. 

He pulled his hand back, staring with his opalescent, lidless eyes at the underside of the stone. Then, he looked up at the ceiling. The glare was gone. In its place was a vast, gentle canopy of violet dusk that did not sting his corneas, but bathed them in a soothing, shadowed cool. 

The air smelled different. It no longer smelled of wet lime and iron-dust; it smelled of deep, rested earth.

***

The news did not spread by voice, but it traveled faster than sound. It moved as a soft, wondering ripple through the stone floor, passed from footpad to footpad across miles of stepped megaliths and chalky plains:

*The stone is sleeping.*
*The mist does not bite.*
*The floor is letting go.*

Across the city, the frantic kinetic rhythm began, for the first time in history, to stutter. 

A chain of workers passing gypsum slabs slowed their hands. A gang dismantling an amphitheater paused on the third tier, their chests heaving, waiting for the calcifying fog to punish them for their hesitation. The mist fell upon them, cool and light, but when they looked at the joints beneath their feet, the mortarless basalt remained free, loose, and patient.

In the sunken channels, the great blind Ounces felt the slackening of the hemp traces. Their blunt, wedge-shaped heads drifted down until their velvety antennae rested against the wet stone. They waited for the channel walls to begin creeping around their slate-plated flanks. Nothing crept. The slow *shuck-shuck* of their breathing filled the gutters, unhurried, as the beasts lowered their heavy bodies fully onto the stone and simply remained there, basking in the faint downward warmth of the new light.

Above, the Cilia-Flocks did not drop in their desperate, vacuuming dives. They caught the gentle, warmed thermals rising from the newly tempered floor and found that they could glide without falling. They circled lazily through the violet air, their nine-foot velvet spans spreading wide, riding the stillness rather than fighting it.

***

The Power did not take away their city, nor their strength, for what they had built with their bare hands was monumental and holy. 

Instead, it gave them the one thing the great hollow had never possessed: **an ending to the day.**

As the hours passed, the Power caused the violet seams in the ceiling to slowly dim into a soft, deep indigo, until the hollow held the cool, velvet twilight of a world at peace. And with the twilight came a profound, seismic rhythm through the bedrock—not a command, but an invitation, like the slow, deep heartbeat of the earth itself.

In the leeward shelters of the thermal vents, the workers began to unhook their bone toggles. 

They did not link their harnesses together. They did not stand rigid against the draft, bracing for the twenty-minute fit of fevered rest. 

One by one, the Unmade lay down. 

They stretched their long, pale limbs out across the wide flagstones. They rested their heads upon the very blocks of shale they had spent their lives carrying. Their glassy nictitating membranes settled softly over their cloudy eyes, no longer weeping chalky rheum against the grit, but closing at last against a dark that was safe.

Their muscles, which had known only the phantom twitch of the sledge and the lever since the hour of their birth, softened. Their long fingers uncurled, opening flat and empty against the floor, feeling the deep, ancient stone beneath them—not as an enemy waiting to swallow them whole, but as a bed that had waited ten thousand years to hold them while they slept.
