The Power that arrived in the high gulf did not come with light, for to bring sudden noon into that soot-colored twilight would have blinded the gliders mid-dive and burned the unlidded, rasping life of the deep conduits. 

It did not come with stillness, for if the wind died, the gliders would plummet like stones through miles of dead air, and the heat rising from the rotting depths would stagnate, suffocating the vertical villages in their own resin-smoke.

Wisdom knows that to unmake the hardship of a world is often to unmake the world itself. The people of the seams were not wretched; they were fierce, dense-fibered, and magnificent. Their beauty was born of the grip. To give them a soft meadow beneath a warm sun would have been an insult to the generations whose bones had been swallowed into the jade-black grain.

And so the Power did not build a floor across the void. It did not tame the wind. 

Instead, it went down into the wood.

***

First, the Power entered the hydraulic tunnels, where the sap tore through the dark under pressures that could split a continent. It did not slow the torrent. Instead, it moved along the inner cambium, smoothing the microscopic shears and fractures where the wood’s violent growth tore itself apart from within. It touched the blind, pale isopods clinging to the conduit walls, leaving within their rasping plates a strange, subtle instinct: when the wood groans toward a catastrophic shear, they no longer wait to be ruptured into the cold. They migrate *en masse* toward the pressure-points, their serrated carapaces locking together by the millions to knit the internal fissures, buying the living branch precious seasons of grace.

Next, the Power ascended into the howling negative space between the boles. It laid its vast, incorporeal palms against the colossal spires—those needle-keen prows that pierced the cloud ceiling. It did not alter their course, but it breathed upon their crystalline edges, shaping the microscopic fluting of the jade bark. 

The sound changed.

The wind still blew with the force of an unchained ocean, but the deafening, multiphonic shriek—the white noise that battered human minds into numb exhaustion—began to separate into distinct, readable harmonics. The spires became tuning forks of continental scale. 

To the under-striders, pressing their acoustic jaws to the bark, the world no longer thrummed with blind chaos; the wood began to broadcast its intentions leagues in advance. 

And to the human Listeners, huddled inside the split-dwellings, the change was like water to a parched throat.

***

In a chasm thirty cubits deep, an old Listener named Kael-Teth knelt in the soot-grease dark, his scarred cheek pressed tight to the jade wall. Above him, the village hung in its web of sinew and bone, silent, waiting for his percussive knock to tell them if they must abandon their homes before the closing wood crushed them in their sleep.

For four days, the arch had been groaning with the dry, high-pitched whine of an impending snap-cycle. Kael-Teth had prepared his soul to give the signal—to tell forty families to cut their guide-lines and flee out onto the knife-edge ridge in the teeth of a gale, knowing half of them would be swept into the howling drop.

He closed his eyes and listened.

The scream of the wood was gone. In its place was a low, resonant chord—deep as the gulfs, vibrating through his teeth and collarbone. It was not the sound of snapping fiber, but of *settling*. 

As Kael-Teth listened, weeping silently from eyes crusted with charcoal dust, he felt the grain beneath his fingers soften. The petrified heartwood wedges they had driven into the chasm’s lips were no longer being ground to powder by tectonic torque. The living flesh of the tree was not closing to crush them; it was *curving* around the wedges, flowing like slow glass, weaving an archway of thick, reinforced callous-wood over their heads. 

The tree was no longer fighting the village. It was building a rib around it.

Farther down in the chasm, where the fuel-collectors worked the boiling pots, a collective gasp rose through the dark. The weeping sap-vents had changed. The pale, volatile resin that often sputtered and choked them with bitter soot had cleared into a thick, amber nectar. When touched with flint, it caught with a low, smokeless flame that cast a steady, peach-colored warmth against the frozen bark. For the first time in memory, the children in the sleeping-cocoons above did not shiver against the frost-burn.

***

The Power’s final act was at the prow-tips, high in the freezing mist, where the dead were wedged into the fresh growth-fissures.

The people had always believed that burying their dead in the wood gave the blind, roaring world a small grain of human stubbornness. The Power honored that belief and made it true.

Wherever a body had been taken into the grain, the wood did not merely swallow the bone—it remembered the human form. The jade-black bark over those ancient graves grew in smooth, undulating handholds, perfectly spaced for climbing. The grain there was denser, resistant to the biting scour of rime, providing secure footholds where the tracks had once been slick with ice. 

The ancestors were not gone into the dark; they had become the path.

***

When its work was finished, the Power did not leave a monument, nor did it demand a single click or tap of worship from the flint-adzes. It simply dissolved back into the cold twilight beyond the clouds, leaving the world as it had found it: vast, perilous, and loud with the rush of wind.

The drop was still thousands of cubits deep. The gliders still hunted in the black air, and the people still moved with iron pins and thick, corded forearms.

But as Kael-Teth stood on the edge of the knife-track, tethered to the guide-line, he looked out into the great gulf and did not feel hatred for the roaring void. 

He tapped his adze against the bark—*tap-tap-drag*—the signal for *Safety. Anchor here.*

Miles away, through the singing wind, three dozen hammers struck the wood in answer, their rhythms interlocking in the dark like the heartbeat of a world that had finally learned how to hold its children.
