The Power did not arrive as light, for light was already a harsh and crowded thing here, refracted a million times through mica plains and crystalline shells. Nor did it arrive as warmth, for heat was the friction of the deep vents, violent and pressurized.

And above all, because the Power was wise, **it brought no water.**

A clumsy god, looking down upon this vast, desiccated plateau, would have wept. It would have sent storms to wash the amber plains, thinking rain an act of mercy. And in doing so, it would have dissolved the dry-chemical twitch of the Shear-Hounds, drowned the pneumatic respiration of the mile-high reeds, short-circuited the delicate silicate synapses of the hives, and rotted the fossilized bones that held the continent aloft. To give water to this world would be an atrocity of pure sentimentality.

The Power understood the sacred arithmetic of the place. It knew that here, perfection had already been reached a hundred million years ago: a world that had distilled life down to its absolute, crystalline logic. 

It saw that the people did not suffer from their lack of flesh; they were whole. They did not despair over the silence of death; their dead were the literal beams of their homes.

Yet the Power saw the one true agony of the plateau—the tragedy built into the very first sentence of the world:

*It had nowhere left to grow.*

The circuit was closed. For a hundred million years, the electrostatic charges had looped through the same calcified xylem, the same ground-plates, the same spines. The minds embedded in the structural roots of the cities were dampening the wind, yes, but they were trapped in an endless echo chamber of their own past tensions. The continent was an engine running at maximum resistance, vibrating toward an eventual, catastrophic crystallization from which no new thought could ever spark.

The Kindred called their greatest horror *insulation*—the terror of being cut off from the circuit.

The Power gave them the only gift that was both harmless and infinite:

**It provided a Ground.**

***

The event was not sudden. It did not break the sky. 

It began high above the stratospheric haze, where the faint violet sun hung cold and thin. The Power extended a single, filamentary thread of pure, non-spatial potential—a line of absolute zero resistance—and anchored it quietly into the apex of the tallest, oldest iron-reed on the plateau.

It did not inject energy; it offered an exit.

Down in the dark smoked-glass hives, the change was first noticed by the Auscultators. 

An Ear-Keeper stood upon a narrow exterior ledge, three miles above the amber floor, bracing their lead-weighted mantle against the multi-tonal shriek of the wind. They drove their petrified heartwood tuning fork into the seam between the glass hive and the living cambium. 

For millennia, the song of the wood had been a grinding, agonized chord: the sound of a billion tons of petrified root screaming under unyielding tension, an infinite pressure with nowhere to go.

The Auscultator struck the fork. They leaned forward, pressing the comb-ridges of their forehead against the vibrating wood to take the sound directly into their silicate skull.

The scream had changed.

It was no longer an endless, shearing buildup of static. The sub-bass hum of the planet had resolved into a clear, subterranean fundamental note. The unbearable mechanical stress within the root-fibers was not gone—for the world needed stress to stand—but the *choke* was gone. The excess electrostatic debris, the agonizing build-up of unspent potential that had plagued the plateau since the dawn of memory, was draining upward, silently and effortlessly, leaping into the cold void of the sky.

The Auscultator drew their forearms together, striking their keratin combs in a rapid, shivering trill of pure disbelief. *Chime-chime-ring-glass.* 

*The circuit is breathing.*

***

Across the continent, the ecosystem shifted by a fraction of a millimeter, which on this world was the difference between an eternity of tension and an eternity of grace.

The herds of Litho-Striders, galloping on diamond hooves across the dark amber plains, found that their sparks no longer pooled in violent, blinding ground-level blizzards. The discharge trailed behind them in long, elegant ribbons of soft violet corona, lifting into the dry air like glowing silk before dispersing harmlessly into the upper stratum.

The Shear-Hounds, wound tight on their graphene springs, still hunted—for predation was the heartbeat of the plain—but the terrible, grinding friction in their fulgurite joints was smoothed. The static that once clogged their chemical battery-chambers was drawn away, leaving their movements clean, silent, and precise as clockwork.

Deep in the ship-swallowing clefts, the Columnar Wyrms pulsed their green bioluminescence down their mile-long flanks. But for the first time, their light did not simply die in the dark. The light was caught by the new conduction, echoed upward through the xylem tubes, and cast against the underside of the high glass hives like a soft, living aurora of cool jade.

And in the deep foundations of the cities, where the calcified spines of the dead were stitched into the weight-bearing walls, something miraculous occurred.

The ancestors were not brought back to life—to resurrect them into soft bodies would be an insult to their long stillness. But their fossilized thoughts, locked within the silicate marrow to absorb the screaming wind, were finally granted an audience. 

The Far Ground became an infinite accumulator. The memories etched into the dead-wood architecture—a hundred million years of silent, held-up cities, of ancient flights, of cold twilight watched through crystal eyes—began to bleed upward along the Power’s filament, transmitted outward into the wider universe. 

The dead were no longer just ballast against the gale. They had become a broadcast.

***

In the smoked-glass hives, the Kindred of the Cambium stood in their conductive sockets. 

As they equalized their capacitors with the floor, they did not feel the familiar, crowded buzzing of a closed loop. They felt an open door. They felt the vast, dry, magnificent structure of their world connected, at last, to an expanse that would never run out of room.

They did not worship the Power, for they had no gods and knew only the laws of impedance and flow. But high on the spire flanks, the Filamenters began to spin new webs—not just between trunk and trunk, but angled upward toward the highest vents, weaving delicate, parabolic dishes of spun quartz and mica, aimed at the empty sky.

They stood at the edges of their yawning chasms, struck their forearms together, and let their glass-bell voices ring out into the cold, aromatic wind.

They sang into the circuit. And for the first time in a hundred million years, the universe sang back through the floor.
