To enter this place with an army of angels or a dawn of gold would be an act of total slaughter.

A clumsy god would have brought the sun, and in doing so, boiled the Lattice-Spiders into crisp knots before their membranes could register the heat. A righteous god would have drained the mud, and by evening, every Pulley-Cyst on the bedrock would have choked to death on dry air, their keratin diaphragms split and useless without the planetary cavitation to drive their blood. A foolish god would have imposed silence, and in that instant, paralyzed the Keel-Worms, whose hearts require the crush of ten thousand fathoms of liquid basalt simply to know which way is forward.

The Power that arrived was old enough to know that you do not cure an engine by breaking its gears. It had seen a million worlds wear themselves down to their sumps; it understood that what looks to the uninitiated like a torture chamber is, to the thing born inside it, the only cradle it will ever have.

It did not descend from above, because there was no above. 

Instead, it introduced itself as an infinitesimal change in the friction.

***

It slipped into the basin not as light, but as *viscosity*.

Where the abrasive basalt paste ground against the blubber-flanks of the Keel-Worms, the Power became a micro-layer of absolute slip—an unnamable, frictionless mercy that did not rob the mud of its density, but stripped the silt of its microscopic razor-edges. The leviathans still swam by boiling the rock before them; their snouts still sang their high-frequency screaming songs. But for the first time in ten million years, the horn-plates at their brows did not wear down faster than the marrow could push them out. 

The Keel-Worms did not know they had been spared. They only knew that the mud felt, for a single, thundering league, like a road rather than a grindstone.

Next, the Power turned its attention to the acoustic roar—the wet, tearing cavitation that filled every skull with the weight of wet wool. It did not lower the volume; to lower the volume would be to starve the Pulley-Cysts, whose hydraulic pistons relied on the physical punch of the sound waves. 

Instead, the Power subtly retuned the resonance of the planetary bedrock. 

It took the frantic, white-noise shriek of tearing fluid and gathered its wandering frequencies into a single, vast, sub-harmonic chord. It was still loud enough to shatter human bone, but it was no longer chaotic. It became a *rhythm*. The Pulley-Cysts felt the change through their stone-fused rootlets: their intake strokes lengthened; their horn diaphragms stopped fluttering in jagged spasms and settled into a deep, oceanic cadence. The blind gristle-maggots grazing at their exhaust vents ceased their manic, panicked vibrating and began to sway with a strange, heavy dignity, rocked by a pulse that finally had a center.

***

In the drilled composite slabs, where the Plug-Beasts hung suspended in the violent eddies, the Power did something smaller and more delicate.

The Plug-Beast lives in terror of being dislodged before its spores are ripe. It wedges its soft flesh into the ancient holes, holding on against currents that want to peel it like fruit from a rind. The Power did not calm the currents; it simply settled into the dark, rustless composite itself, warming the cold, dead alloy with a gentle, steady heat. 

The Plug-Beasts, which had only ever known the sour, lukewarm soup of the basin, felt this ancient metal begin to thrum with a slow, reassuring fever. Their resinous adhesives set deeper, harder, more securely. They unfurled their black, cilia-funnels further into the storm than they had ever dared, feeding without the desperate clench of imminent death, fluttering their raw-meat tails like banners in a wind that could no longer tear them away.

***

And then, it found the Lattice-Spiders.

The Power watched a geyser of brown brine erupt from the floor. It watched the water hesitate, stutter against the broken laws of the sump, and snap into an open, crystalline scaffold of geometric brown ice. 

It saw the tiny, balled knot of the Lattice-Spider strike the scaffold and burst open—saw the frantically trembling membrane, the desperate, blind search for light, air, and seed, all crammed into a three-second panic before the architecture collapsed.

The Power did not fix the physics of the basin. The broken world needed its glitches. But as the spider cast its webbed arms across the brown lattice, the Power gently touched the three seconds.

It did not make them four. It did not make them five. 

It simply expanded the *interior* of the time.

To the basin, the water-crystal held for its usual three deafening beats before smashing back into lukewarm froth. But to the spider upon its struts, the three seconds opened inward like a cathedral. 

For what felt to its nervous system like a long, golden afternoon, the spider rested. It did not have to scramble. It did not have to tear its own legs in the rush to feed and breed. It lay flat against the geometric scaffolding, feeling the sour twilight soak deep into its translucent tissues, breathing the thin atmospheric gas with slow, luxuriant contractions. It found the gametes of its kind with calm precision. And when the walls of brown water finally groaned and fell back into the boiling soup, the spider balled itself shut not with the frantic reflex of a doomed thing, but with the quiet, tucked-in patience of something that had been fed, had been seen, and was ready to sleep until the next eruption.

***

When two Keel-Worms finally met to mate, charging one another through the pressurized slurry along the acoustic shear-lines, the Power did not soften their collision. To soften it would be to fail the exchange; the cysts needed the violence of the blow to rupture and graft.

The two titans struck. The bedrock groaned for ten leagues around. The blubber sheared, the whistling blowholes screamed their steam into the amber fog, and the bundles of fibrous life were smashed into the open wounds.

But at the exact millisecond of impact, the Power placed itself between the two massive plates of horn. 

It did not absorb the force; it absorbed the *solitude*.

In a world where no mind had ever possessed a memory, where every creature was an isolated, ringing reflex trapped in a permanent, deafening present tense, the Power allowed their nervous systems to bridge across the impact. 

For the space of a single heartbeat, as their blood boiled together in the mud, the two Keel-Worms were not alone. They felt each other not merely as obstacles or hydrostatic pressure, but as *kindred*—two vast, hot, suffering engines carrying the same fire through the same dark mill. 

The currents tore them apart. Their armor began to knit with frantic speed. They drifted away into the gloom, venting steam from their dorsal ridges. 

They did not remember the encounter, because their brains had no shelves to store yesterday upon. But deep in the fatty tallow of their spinal cords, the ringing vibration had changed. It was no longer the sound of something resisting being ground down; it was the sound of something that had touched another of its kind, and found that the weight was shared.

***

The Power did not leave. 

It could not leave, for if it withdrew its attention, the friction would return, the chord would shatter back into a shriek, and the three seconds would shrink back into panic.

So it settled down into the lowest stratum of the world. It laid itself beneath the deepest nubs of basalt, beneath the roots of the Pulley-Cysts, beneath the drifting composite slabs. It became the floor beneath the floor.

It did not make the basin a paradise. It remained a warm, weeping, sunless sump, thick with grease and deafening cavitation, chewing its mountains into silt until the end of time. 

But it was no longer an engine of pure exhaustion. 

The continent’s final children continued to metabolize, suffer, and endure in the belly of the mill—but now, the mill was being turned by hands that loved the grain.
