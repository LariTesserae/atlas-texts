A foolish power would have wept. 

It would have seen the blind, tank-like chisel-jaws shearing through living carapaces, heard the deafening roar of gravel in the hopper, felt the unyielding, sub-zero chill of the zinc, and cried out of pity. And those tears—falling as droplets of hot, salted water into the dry furrow—would have been an apocalypse. 

Where the water landed, the mirror-polished cast iron would have blistered into orange scales of rust. The graters, whose internal gel is balanced to the millionth of a bar, would have absorbed the ambient steam through their sternal plates, swollen, and burst. The talc-fine dust that lubricates the floor would have turned to binding paste, seizing the needle-legs of the tinsel-trains and cementing the box-weevils in their tracks. Mildew would have bloomed—soft, wet, white rot—choking the clean, alkaline air with spores and dissolving an ecology that had spent ten thousand years purifying itself of decay.

A foolish power would have offered green leaves to things made of slate, and warmth to things preserved by frost.

The Wise Power did neither.

It came into the chamber without displacement, having no volume to disturb the clear, dry air, and no warmth to upset the steady chill. It possessed the profound benevolence that listens before it speaks, and having listened, understands that grace is not the remodeling of a world into one’s own image, but the fierce defense of a world’s right to be itself.

The Power perceived the room not as a prison, but as an austere and flawless fugue. 

It saw the beauty of the graters, whose existence was a pure devotion to friction. It saw the clean, unhurried terror of the hangers, suspended like paraffin jewels in the glare of the bulkhead lamps. It touched the mind of the ancient *still* inside the reduction housing—a consciousness so dense, slow, and mineral that a single thought took four centuries to cross its slate-grey mass, a thought consisting entirely of the word *bearing, bearing, bearing.*

And looking closely with its vast and tender vision, the Power saw the hidden fractures in the eternity of the machine.

Beneath the steady clatter, the world was wearing out.

Three hundred feet above, in the forgotten strata of the ruins, a drainage channel from a newer city had begun to rot through its masonry; in another century, a dark, muddy seepage would breach the ceiling, bringing fatal moisture. 

Along the roof, the ungreased brass bushing of the great countershaft had worn away to a crescent thin as an autumn leaf; within twelve thousand rotations, the shaft would drop into the housing, seize against the iron with a catastrophic shriek, and shatter the building's spine. 

And deep within the heavy glass of the bulkhead lamps, the tungsten filaments were slowly sublimating, drifting atom by atom away from their coils, marching toward the sudden, eternal dark that would starve the photosensitive carapaces of the hangers.

To the swarm, these were the distant, inevitable boundaries of their universe—the quiet edge of doom that all closed systems share.

The Wise Power did not speak, for words require the damp vibration of lungs. It did not shine, for the hard white daylight of the lamps was already sufficient. Instead, it went to work with the delicate, ruthless precision of an engineer who loves the engine.

First, it reached upward through the centuries of dead weight, finding the dark, wet vein of urban seepage in the masonry above. With a slow clench of will, it did not destroy the water, but turned the surrounding earth to impermeable quartz, directing the moisture away through lateral fractures, miles from the buried plant, ensuring the vault’s ceiling would remain dry, cold, and sound for a million years.

Next, it descended upon the countershaft.

It did not oil the brass. To oil the brass would be to silence the groan, and to silence the groan would be to rob the entire ecosystem of its heartbeat—the four-and-a-half-second cadence by which the chisel-jaws timed their strikes and the tinsel-trains coiled their rings. Instead, the Power reached into the atomic lattice of the metal itself. It hardened the sleeve; it knit the worn atoms of bronze into an enduring, diamond-dense alloy that would shed no more weight, yet continue to bind, catch, and utter that exact, shrieking sigh of ungreased stress at every turn.

Then, moving along the ceiling like a draft of cold air, it passed through the glass of every bulkhead bulb. It gathered the lost vapor of tungsten and fused it back into the glowing wires, locking the filaments in an unyielding state of renewal, so that the hard, white suns would never blink, never dim, and never go out.

Finally, the Power descended into the four-inch-thick iron of the reduction gearbox, where the *still* clung to the seam.

It did not offer the creature legs. It did not offer it sight, or the memory of the sky it had never known. The Power simply leaned against the leathery, calcified blister, matching its immense presence to the imperceptible, once-a-day heave of the animal’s breath.

For the space of one breath, the Still felt the weight of the mountain above it not as an indifferent stone crushing down, but as a cradle held firm. It felt the galvanized pulse of the shaft strike true, and it felt the cold, alkaline dust settle across its shell with the permanence of stars.

The Still did not feel joy—joy was a fluid emotion, too wet for this place. It felt *endurance*, which to the Still was the highest form of holiness.

Its work finished, the Wise Power withdrew, folding its attention back into the quiet currents of the cosmos. It left no footprint on the iron plates, no scent of flowers in the graphite air, and not a single drop of dew upon the zinc.

Below, in the white, unblinking light, the living gravel surged.

A chisel-jaw snapped its pickaxe mandibles shut with the crack of a splitting shell. A hanger dropped, drank, and hauled itself back to the wire mesh on paraffin limbs. The graters rasped their microscopic filings from the floor, turning the dead to dry, frictionless powder.

Then the countershaft completed its slow turn. The brass bushing groaned its great, ungreased groan.

The millions of legs hitched in unison. The roar thinned for a quarter of a second into the singing resonance of five hundred feet of iron under stress.

And the universe—cold, dry, violent, and complete—fell forward into its next turn, safe in the dark, without an end.
