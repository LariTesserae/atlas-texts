When the attention of the Great Will settled upon the shelf, it did not descend with the shattering arrival of thunder, nor with the sudden, blighting radiance of a new star. 

To enter this place violently would be the mistake of a crude divinity. A foolish god, arriving with an armful of common mercies, would have seen the lack of trees and wept; would have scattered green seed across the basalt, brought cool alpine air to dispel the sulfurous steam, and banished the crawling lice from the copper conduits. And in doing so, that god would have choked the world in rot, starved the gliding kites, poisoned the trough-hogs with strange sap, and destroyed the slow, magnificent equilibrium that had taken five million years of steam and stone to forge.

True wisdom does not correct an ecosystem that already sings. It listens until it understands the key in which the song is written.

The Power hung in the thin, sunlit mist above the plateau for three hundred years of human time—which to it was no longer than the drawing of a single breath—simply learning the weight of the water.

It felt the rhythm of the great low-frequency pumps. It counted the billions of rasp-slugs as they planed the blue verdigris from the conduit seams. It tasted the hot, brothy fat of the canals and the bitter, life-giving sulfur of the microbial blooms. It watched the Tenders—how their squat, oil-slick bodies moved with the patient grace of heavy components; how they tapped the girders with their brass-headed hammers, measuring the health of their metal continent by the pitch of its iron bones.

It saw that they were not wretched. They did not pray to be elsewhere. Their marrow was tuned to the thump of the piston-sleeves; their peace was the steady sheet of condensation running over slate; their heaven was the warm gallery at dusk, sharing limpet-meat while the skin-gliders rustled like dry parchment overhead.

Yet the Power was benevolent, and benevolence cannot gaze upon mortality without asking: *Where does it hurt? Where is the hidden fracture?*

Looking into the deep anatomy of the plateau, the Power saw the one tragedy invisible to the short-lived creatures of the skin: *fatigue.*

Not the fatigue of the Tenders, but the fatigue of the metal and the rock. 

Beneath the wet basalt, the deep foundational vaults were crystallizing under centuries of unbroken vibration. Deep within the core-housings of the great pumps, the iron axles—forged before memory—were developing microscopic shears, grain by grain. In ten thousand years, a central rocker-arm would snap. In twenty thousand, the great aqueducts would collapse into the lowlands, the warm water would drain away, the vents would dry, and the entire breathing, clattering canopy of chitin and skin would desiccate under the dry sun into gray dust.

The machine was immortal only in appearance; in truth, it was slowly wearing out its stone cradle.

The Power chose its action.

It did not descend to speak in a human voice. It did not build new towers or change the course of a single gutter. 

Instead, it sank downward through the warm fog like an imperceptible change in gravity. It entered the sweating basalt. It slipped into the molecular lattice of the iron girders, the copper conduits, and the subterranean rocker-arms. 

It did not make the metal unyielding—for what is unyielding will eventually shatter—but gave to the ancient infrastructure the gift of the living bone: *the capacity to slowly heal.*

Where iron ground against iron in the dark heart of the engine-halls, the Power wove a quiet, microscopic grace into the mineral broth. From that day forward, the dissolved iron and sulfur suspended in the runoff did not merely wash away; wherever stress-fractures opened in the metal, the microscopic rasp-life and the chemistry of the water laid down dense, crystalline bridges of self-annealing ore. The basalt beneath the pump-mounts ceased its micro-shattering, growing denser, tougher, and more supple with every heavy blow of the piston.

The plateau would not fail. The warm, sweating house would stand as long as the sun burned above it.

Then, the Power turned its gaze to the Tenders themselves.

It would not alter their forms—their yellow, sebum-thick skin was their shield, their heavy brow-ridges their comfort against the glare. But it knew the small, private griefs of their flesh: the deep ache in the joints that comes from living always in the damp; the blinding sting of high-sulfur vapors in the eyes; the terror of a slip upon the verdigris-slick spans above the abyss.

And so, as the sun began to drop toward the west, throwing the long, sharp shadows of the cooling towers across the gleaming shelf, the Power drew near to a solitary Tender.

The Tender was an elder, her leather apron stained black with the grease of a hundred tallow-traps, her hands scarred by the sharp carapaces of the plate-lice. She had climbed to the end of a narrow, unsupported cantilever truss that jutted out over a two-thousand-foot drop, where the warm updrafts billowed against the underside of the iron. She was tired. Her knees were swollen with the mineral-gout that took all elders in their fourth decade. She carried her brass hammer, raised it with a trembling wrist, and struck a structural rivet to test its tension.

*Clang.*

Usually, the note was dull, hard, swallowed instantly by the bellow of the spillway below.

But this time, as the hammer met the iron, the Power touched the vibration.

The note did not die. It blossomed. 

It spread out through the truss in a long, golden harmonic that purred through the soles of the Tender’s hide boots, rose up through her bowed legs, and settled into the base of her spine. 

Where the sound passed through her body, the cold, mineral stiffness in her joints dissolved like salt in warm broth. Her swollen knuckles softened. The ache behind her eyes—the permanent burn of sulfur smoke—cleared, leaving her vision sharp, bright, and painless. 

The sound traveled outward. It struck the main catenaries, humming through the cables until every glass-bead drop of condensation trembled in resonance. It passed into the eaves where thousands of leathery skin-gliders hung, causing them to coo—a low, bubbling chorus of deep contentment that rolled down the iron canyons like thunder. It rippled down the canals, where the trough-hogs lifted their massive, boot-leather snouts from the silt and let out long, blowing sighs of ease.

The old Tender stood frozen on the high cantilever.

She looked down at her hands. The pain was gone. Her grip upon the hammer was firm, effortless, light as a youth's. 

She did not look up at the sky to search for a face, for her people knew that the world was made of metal, stone, and steam, not ghosts. Instead, she laid her flat, calloused palm directly against the wet, liver-brown surface of the iron beam. 

Through the metal, she felt the heart of the engine beat. *Thump. Thump. Thump.* 

And beneath the mechanical rhythm, she felt something new: an answering presence, vast and quiet, that seemed to lean its immense weight gently against the opposite side of the iron, holding it steady.

The Tender did not fall to her knees. She simply tapped the girder twice with the flat of her brass hammer—the traditional signal that meant: *Clear. Secure. The line holds.*

A hundred miles of ironwork seemed to hum the confirmation back to her.

Satisfied, the Power withdrew its attention, rising silently back into the high, clear sun, leaving the plateau to its work. 

Down on the shelves, the day turned to dusk. The bright glare softened into purple. The pistons beat on, smoothly lubricated by fat and water; the limpets fed in the dark channels; the kites drifted on the endless thermals; and the Tenders washed their sound, tireless hands in the clean, hot condensation of the flues, living inside a machine that would now outlast the stars.
