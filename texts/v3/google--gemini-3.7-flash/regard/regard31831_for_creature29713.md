When the Watcher came to the notch—drawn by the singular, rhythmic chord of five thousand mallets striking mulberry bast against the deep-bellied roar of the cataract—it did not descend as a pillar of fire or a voice of thunder. 

To bring heat into the gorge would be to rot the moss-mats that hold the cliffs together. To bring a great voice would be to start an avalanche from the snow-crests above. 

A lesser god, seeing their cracked heels and the pale water-cured skin of their forearms, might have pitied them unwisely. Such a power might have dried the mist, cleaved the mountain into flat and sunny meadows, or replaced the swaying bridges of split bamboo with silent, unyielding stone. But that would have been the benevolence of an infant: an act that mistakes the friction of living for a flaw in design, wiping away the dance to save the dancer the trouble of moving.

The Watcher was old enough to know that these people were not suffering their world; they were *inhabiting* it. Their beauty was not despite the damp, but of it—written in the broad spread of their toes, the tannic amber of their skin, the grease-warmed smell of their children, and the fierce, laughing cadence of their work-songs.

And so the Power did what only the wisest powers know how to do: it made itself very small, very quiet, and porous as peat.

***

First, it went down into the dark, into the deafening spray of the chasm floor where the Sump-Crews haul against the capstans. 

It did not silence the roar of the water—to take away the noise would be to take away the language of hands and glances they had spent ten generations perfecting. Instead, it slipped into the deep bedrock beneath the churning rapids. It pressed its subtle weight into the fault-lines, knitting together three ancient hairline fractures in the slate that, seventy winters hence, would have sheered away under a spring flood and carried three tiers of housing into the abyss. 

The people never knew the rock had held because of an added grace; they only knew the rock was good, and when they hammered their anchor-pins into the cleft, the slate rang true and did not shatter.

***

Next, it rose through the middle tier, moving like a cool breath through the hearth-smoke.

It rested near a young mother sitting on an ironwood trunk, her wrists sore from the mallet, her toddler straining against his hemp tether-harness at the edge of the platform. The Watcher did not untie the rope, nor did it build a parapet of brass. It simply leaned into the wood beneath her feet, breathing a faint, enduring vitality into the heartwood of the cedar-relatives. 

Because of this, the wood rot—which was an honest part of the forest's life—did not cease, but it learned a slower, more honest grammar. It gave off its sweet, tell-tale musk two days earlier than it used to, allowing the barrel-chested grip-hounds to catch the scent while the timbers were still thick enough to bear a man's step. 

The mother paused her pounding, wiped the canopy-drip from her forehead with a bark-cloth sleeve, and breathed in. For a single heartbeat, the ache in her forearms felt light, and a song she had forgotten from her own grandmother’s knee rose easily to her throat. She began to sing it, and three platforms down, two deck-stallers took up the refrain, their mallets falling into the old, synchronized thudding rhythm.

***

Finally, the Watcher climbed to the blinding, silver-grey heights of the Canopy Watch, where the Chute-Keepers swing out over the void in their harnesses of fig-sinew.

It found an old man whose fingers were stiff with the sulfur-pitch he burned to seal the water-flumes. His eyes were milky with the glare of fifty years of high mist, and the wind was rising over the notch, cold and sudden, threatening to whip his braided guide-line across the jagged horn of a dead limb.

The Power did not still the wind. The wind was the breath of the high pass; without it, the fires below would drown in their own smoke.

Instead, the Watcher gently touched the old man’s shoulder—not with a hand, but with the warmth that sometimes lingers in an old wool coat. The old chute-keeper did not turn or look for a ghost. He simply felt his footing settle. His calloused toes found the wet bark groove by habit and memory, his grip relaxed just enough to save his sinews from cramping, and he leaned out into the open air with the easy, rolling confidence of an otter slipping into a pool.

He smelled the approaching squall three minutes before it struck, whistled two sharp, descending notes between his teeth to warn the platforms below, and drew his hemp hood up against the rain.

***

Before the Watcher drew back into the wide, quiet sky, it left one last thing—so slight that even the wind-seers never wrote a tale about it.

In the deep water-cups of the giant bromeliads, where the green-bellied frogs lay their jelly-clutches, it touched the water. From that day on, the slime the children scooped to soothe burns and grease the pulley-pins held its cooling virtue through the longest, coldest frosts, never turning sour in the horn cups.

And then the Power stepped back into the high clouds, leaving the gorge intact: loud, crowded, drenched, and precarious.

Below, the water clattered through the flumes. A capstan groaned as a basket of river-crabs was hauled from the dark. A child laughed, kicked a wet deck-plank with a bare, calloused heel, and found it held.
