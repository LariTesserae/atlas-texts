To a power that is both wise and good, the borough does not present itself as a tragedy to be corrected, nor as a prison from which its keepers must be delivered. 

The clumsy god breaks the vault to let in the sun; the wise god knows that an unroofed kiln dies cold, that the swallows would scatter into the hawks’ wind, and that eyes grown wide in the rust-twilight would burn to white ash under an open noon. To reach down with a great hand and lift these people to the grass would be an act of violence indistinguishable from malice. Their beauty is not an accident of deprivation; it is a balance struck between the knuckle, the mud, and the fire.

And so the watcher would not descend with light or trumpet. It would arrive as physics. It would slip into the borough the way the evening draft enters the ceramic baffles—without a seam, smelling only of the dust it passes through.

Here is what wisdom would do:

First, it would place its palms beneath the foundations, three cubits deeper than the lowest brine-sump, where the heavy clay beds meet the ancient shale. It would not change the stone, but it would take the shear out of the fault lines. When the deep earth trembles—as all dry continents must—the shocks would not travel upward as snapping fractures through the brick piers, but would soften into a low, swinging cradle-motion, a dull chord that merely rings the flue-pipes like a far-off bell and passes on. The vaults would settle, but they would not drop their keystones. The skeletons in the corner buttresses would remain unbroken in their baked sleep.

Second, it would attend to the lungs. It would leave the apricot dust in the air—for the red haze is their cloak, their paint, and their familiar skin—but it would change the grain of it at the threshold of the throat. The pulverized grog, which over fifty seasons turns a mason’s chest into dry honeycomb and makes him spit pale mud at forty, would lose its cutting edges. It would become soft as pollen to the inner tissue, melting away in the body’s wet channels without scar or cough, so that an elder might squat on the dished tiles at eighty seasons with breath as clear and long as a boy’s.

Third, it would touch the water in the thumb-narrow runnels. It would not turn the brown sump-water into sweet spring-melt—for the land-crabs require the lime to harden their carapaces, and the dwellers’ salt-tongues would find mountain water thin and cold. Instead, it would filter out only the bitter lead and the heavy grey salts that cause the hip-joints to seize before old age. It would leave the mineral musk, the taste of quenched brick and iron, but make it clean enough to sustain the blood without calcifying the marrow.

And fourth, it would make an addition to the fabric—so small that even the most meticulous scraper of slip would never mark it as a miracle.

In the deepest galleries, where the light-shafts do not reach and the twilight settles into cool, damp slate-grey, it would prompt the long, thread-like roots of the dryland scrubs above to do something new. As they pierce the highest arches, hanging down like unraveled twine, they would begin to weep—not merely the condensed moisture of the drafts, but a slow, heavy sap the color of dark amber. 

It would drop only once every three hundred ticks of the water-clock, falling with a clear *plink* into the terracotta collection pans. It would not be mana or elixir; it would simply be a resin, sweet and sticky, smelling of sun-cured sage and wild desert plum. 

The dwellers would find it on a morning like any other. A child with cropped hair would dab it with a calloused finger, taste it, and make a quick, sharp click of pleasure against the roof of her mouth. 

They would use it sparingly, as they use all things. They would mix it with the mash of tub-grown onions to make a paste for the sick; they would blend it into their slip to give the mortar of new arches a faint, permanent fragrance of the upper steppe; and once a year, during the draft-shift when the winds turn cold, they would share a single drop among each family, rolling the sweet heat of the unseen world over their tongues before striking the flues to sing each other to sleep.

The watcher would remain outside the lanterns, sitting upon the arid crust above, resting in the heat of the stones. It would listen through thirty cubits of fired mud to the dry clicking of their speech, the chime of the casing-dogs behind the tiles, and the steady, rhythmic *tock-tock-tock* of the water runnels. 

It would do nothing more. It would simply keep the roof from falling, and let the potters finish the bowl.
