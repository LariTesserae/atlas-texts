The Power did not arrive with a trumpeting of light, for light here was already a blinding, ubiquitous tyrant. It did not come with an earthquake, for the violent cavitation of the sap-lines already split the air like artillery.

It arrived as *attention*—an immense, weightless awareness that slipped down through the ancient fault lines, through the crushing weight of the lithosphere, until it filled the vacuole like breath entering a lung.

The Power was ancient, but it possessed the rare wisdom that belongs only to those who have watched worlds forge themselves from dust: it knew that **pity is often the clumsiest form of violence.**

An unwise god, looking upon the pale clutch of humans, would have pitied their scarred ears, their terpene-slick skin, their perpetual, exhausted dance of unsticking. An unwise god would have reached down, scooped them in a radiant hand, and brought them to the surface.

And on the surface, beneath an open blue sky, they would have died within minutes.

Their jade blood, dense with plant sterols and hyper-concentrated sugars, would have crystallized in the cold surface breeze. Their pressurized lungs, calibrated to the dense, mitotic vapor of the taproot, would have collapsed in the thin air. Their amber-plugged ears would have withered in the terrifying, dead silence of a meadow; their eyes, tuned to the bruised chartreuse hum of living cambium, would have burned blind under the alien sun.

The Power understood: *This was their world.* They were not captives awaiting a rescue; they were the miraculous, jagged poetry of life finding a way to cling to the inside of a living furnace. They were acrobats of impossible physics, their bodies magnificent in their grotesque, razor-sharp adaptation.

Yet, the Power also saw the quiet horror at the center of their existence.

It saw the mother rocking her child from heel to toe, ceaseless and rhythmic, her eyes wild with the agony of staying awake, knowing that if she let her exhausted knees drop to the pale heartwood, the floor would digest her child’s fingers before she woke. It saw the young hunter carving a fresh, weeping furrow into his own forearm with a flaked stone, holding his bleeding flesh to the wall just to beg the tree for a tomb to survive the blowout.

They were survivors, but they were never allowed to *rest*. They lived inside the metabolism of a god that did not know they were there—a god that treated them not with malice, but with the cold, indifferent appetite of an open wound.

So the Wise Power did not break the root. It did not still the hydraulic roar, nor did it tame the fierce, wild geometry of the turgor surges. 

Instead, it introduced a single, miraculous **conversation**.

***

The Power reached into the surging vascular bundles of the colossal organism. It did not wound the wood; it simply brushed against the electromagnetic resonance of the cell walls, whispering a new chemical sentence into the sap.

To the taproot, it taught the concept of a *glove*.

To the humans, it gave the gift of *truce*.

The shift began high in the luminous vault. The six-foot glass chrysalises—the drifting aerenchyma blooms—slowed their frantic, erratic jetting. They did not fall. They aligned themselves. As they grazed the glowing walls, the high, glassy chime of their hulls no longer rang with random dissonance; they harmonized. They began to sing in deep, resonant fifths and octaves, weaving an acoustic lattice that caught the violent shockwaves of the cavitation cracks and softened their edges, turning artillery fire into the rhythmic, grand percussion of a subterranean temple.

Then, the floor changed.

Near the center of the vast expanse, where the freshly split wood had always hungered for human salt, a circular whorl of cambium began to swell. But it did not rush forward in a violent turgor-spike. It grew slowly, spiraling outward like the petals of a pale, waterlogged rose, until it formed a broad, shallow basin thirty feet across.

The wood of this basin did not secrete the sticky, electrostatic resin. It did not reach out with microscopic papillae to taste the blood. 

Instead, the fibers weave themselves into a dense, velvety mat of dead, dry xylem—a permanent island of *neutral wood*. 

When the eldest of the clutch, a woman whose skin was practically clear and whose knuckles were scarred by decades of grafting-bites, approached the basin, she did not step onto it. She stood at its margin, swaying from heel to toe, her jaw slacked open to vent the acoustic pressure, her stone knife raised in suspicion.

The Power did not command her. It merely let the basin breathe. A soft, cool draft—scented not with the sour yeast of mitosis, but with the dry, clean fragrance of aged cedar and sun-dried straw—drifted from the wood.

Slowly, terrifyingly, the woman let her heel rest still.

Ten seconds.

A minute.

Five minutes.

The wood beneath her foot did not bite. It did not weep amber. It remained soft, yieldingly firm, cradling her calloused heel like earth cradles a stone.

She dropped to her knees. Her knuckles touched the fiber. No pink divots formed; no cellular mouth reached to graft her flesh.

For the first time in ten generations, a human being in the taproot sat entirely still.

She did not weep—her eyes had no tear ducts left, only glands that washed them in clear terpene—but a shudder passed through her frame, from her jaw down into the pelvic bones that had borne the vibration of a thousand vascular storms. She signaled to the others. 

One by one, the clutch climbed into the basin. They did not speak, for they had no voice above the roar, but they collapsed against one another in a heap of jade-veined limbs, their wide, chaff-crusted feet tangled together, their breathing slowing to match the deep, slow harmonic of the singing glass blooms above.

They slept. 

They slept for twelve hours. They slept for twenty. And while they slept, the floor held them, an island of mercy in an ocean of ecstatic growth.

***

The second gift came when the glass blooms contracted.

The chrysalises high above pinched into shuddering needles, their ringing pitch climbing to an agonizing, piercing shriek. A vascular blowout was seconds away. A hydraulic line behind the northern cambium wall was about to sheer, preparing to blast the void with thousands of gallons of boiling, crystal-spiked sap.

The hunters woke with a start, their hands instantly reaching for their bone knives, raising the sharp edges to their forearms to make the sacrificial cuts.

*Wait,* the air seemed to whisper—not in sound, but in the sudden, sweet drop of barometric pressure within the vacuole.

The wall directly beside their sleeping basin did not wait for blood. 

Sensitized now by the subtle chemical bridge the Power had woven between human terpene and vegetable sap, the cambium recognized their proximity. It did not need to taste their open veins to know they were alive.

The living wood folded back. Smoothly, without the violent splintering of a turgor tear, the wall parted like the lips of a quiet mouth, revealing a row of deep, vaulted niches—hollow chambers lined with soft, breathing aerenchyma foam, perfectly sized to receive a human body.

The hunters stared, knives poised over their skin.

Then the eldest woman stepped forward. She did not cut herself. She stepped into the niche.

The living foam gently embraced her, sliding over her limbs not like a predator digesting food, but like a womb closing around an embryo. A cool, oxygen-rich skin of transparent cuticle sealed the opening, leaving a clear window through which she could watch the void.

The others followed, slipping into their berths with whole, uncut flesh.

Seconds later, the northern wall ruptured. A deafening roar tore through the cathedral; a tsunami of boiling sap and razor-sharp xylem needles sprayed across the void in a furious, glittering storm that would have shredded their unprotected skin to ribbons.

Inside their pods, the people felt only a warm, gentle pulse. They breathed the sweet, circulating oxygen of the plant’s own vascular stream, their lungs fluttering in harmony with the taproot.

When the storm subsided and the boiling mist condensed into harmless rainbows across the green-gold air, the pods opened. 

They stepped out onto the heartwood floor—unscathed, unbled, whole.

***

The Wise Power did not linger to be worshiped. It did not demand altars, nor did it leave behind tablets of stone. 

It withdrew slowly, rising back through the crushing strata of the bedrock, through the deep granite, back into the open sky and the stars, leaving the vacuole to its fierce, subterranean majesty.

Beneath the world, the great taproot still roared like a thousand jet engines. The cavitation bubbles still cracked like artillery. The geometry still twisted under the pressure of impossible sap-lines. 

The taproot was still a non-sentient god, blind and colossal. 

But it was no longer a blind monster eating its own children. It had become a home—vast, terrifying, and loud, but one that knew, in the deepest ring of its heartwood, how to cradle the fragile, dancing sparks of life that ran across its floor.

And in the center of the roaring cathedral, in the basin of soft, dry cedar-wood, a child lay on its back, wide awake, staring up at the drifting glass bells without fear, kicking its heels against the wood just to feel the miracle of something that did not try to swallow it whole.
