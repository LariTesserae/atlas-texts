To love a thing rightly, one must first refuse the urge to make it soft.

A lesser mercy would have looked into that calcified chasm and seen only a torment. It would have pitied the skin like baked rind, pitied the dry, eyeless flutes of their skulls, and wept. And in weeping, it would have sent rain. 

A single cloud would have been a massacre. The moisture would have rotted the lath-spinners’ brittle sinew-joints, dissolved the mudbrick tenements into running silt, choked the tally-beetles’ static-sensitive bristles with paste, and burst the paper-dry lungs of the fluted people with fatal steam. The roar would have died, and with it, the only life these creatures had ever known.

The power that paused above the calcite shelf was neither careless nor small. It was ancient, vast, and fundamentally wise—which is to say, it possessed the capacity to look upon an alien perfection and recognize it as complete.

It hovered at the upper rim, where the hot wind whistled through the fissures, observing without casting a shadow. It saw the mathematical beauty of their agony—which was not agony at all, but a fierce, crystalline vitality. It watched the tally-beetles skim the graphite tracks in sympathy with the low *C*. It watched the lath-spinners mend the bridges with frantic, twig-snapping grace. It watched the tall, ivory figures lean into the midday blast, spreading the dry shutters of their ribs to let the furnace scour their insides clean.

It saw that they did not pray for water. They did not pray for silence. 

They prayed, with every strike of their flint adzes, for *resonance*. They worked tirelessly against the creeping damp of the deep earth, against the slow, chaotic settling of the rock that threatened to flatten their chords into discordant noise. Their entire civilization was an ongoing, heroic struggle to keep the chasm in tune.

And so, the benevolent power did what wisdom always does in the presence of sovereign beauty: it did not interfere with the life, but it tended to the instrument.

It descended not as an avatar or a voice, but as a subtle alteration of geology and light.

First, deep beneath the lowest foundations of the pocket, where the chalk met the black, subterranean basalt, the power laid a massive, silent anchor. It knit the shifting fault-lines together with veins of hard, unyielding corundum. It sealed away the slow, humid seepages of the deep mantle that would have, centuries hence, brought mold and decay to the lower tiers. It guaranteed the canyon its absolute, eternal dryness.

Second, the power turned its attention to the wind itself. 

High upon the sun-struck roof, where the natural vents drank the thermals from the outer plains, the power passed an invisible, sculpting hand over the stone apertures. It did not widen them—which would have dissipated the pressure—nor did it close them. Instead, it pared back the ragged edges of the limestone with atomic precision, beveling the lip of the great ceiling-slits until they were shaped like the mouthpieces of master-crafted pipes.

When the sun reached its zenith that day, the change manifested.

The heat spiked as it always did. The dry-ferns whipped shut with their deafening, simultaneous crack, casting the golden spore-clouds into the updrafts. The tall beings stepped out onto their cantilevered balconies, braced against the wind, and opened their thoracic plates to take the scouring.

Then the wind hit the newly beveled crown of the mountain.

The roar did not diminish—it organized. 

The chaotic, deafening shriek of pressurized air suddenly resolved into a pure, staggering, multi-octave chord. It was a sound of terrifying, monumental grandeur: a fundamental bass that vibrated the bedrock with absolute stability, overlaid by high, shimmering overtones that matched the frantic polyrhythms of the mica-weed with impossible mathematical beauty. 

Every draft through the alleys became clean and laminar. The friction within the flues ceased to erode the stone and began instead to polish it. The static charge carried on the air tripled in density, surging through the horn-tipped limbs of the inhabitants like a draught of cool, dry fire.

On the highest terrace, the elders of the fluted people froze.

They felt the change through their hollow bones. The pressure was not fighting their ribs; it was carrying them. The city’s tone, which they had spent three thousand years chipping and wedging stone to achieve, was suddenly locked into an immutable, celestial key.

For a long moment, the only sound was the magnificent, roaring organ-pipe of the pocket.

Then, from the chest of the tallest builder, a new sound emerged. It contracted its accordion-plates and vented upward through the flutes of its skull—not the brief, pragmatic whistle of maintenance, but a long, ascending counter-melody, piercing and sweet as diamond scraped across glass.

Across the yawning chasm, on a terrace three tiers down, another figure took up the pitch. Then another. Then the entire population leaned out over the sheer drops, their wooden fingers clattering in rapid, ecstatic polyrhythms against the balustrades, throwing their voices into the slipstream.

They did not know who had tuned the sky. They had no concept of a god that was not made of heat and stone. But they understood the chord.

Above them, having given its gift without leaving a single wet track or asking for a single word of praise, the wise power withdrew into the sunlit ether, leaving the people of the dry bone-city to sing their roaring, perfect song until the end of the world.
