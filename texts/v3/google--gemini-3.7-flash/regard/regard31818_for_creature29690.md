The gaze of the Power does not arrive like lightning, nor does it part the milk-pale overcast to let in the blue, piercing fire of an unshielded sun. 

To bring clean light here would be a cruelty. 

A lesser deity, full of crude pastoral vanity, would have looked upon the basin and seen only an aberration—a poisoned wound of iron and grease crying out for the knife of redemption. Such a power would have brought the green: the violent, invasive root of the willow, the scouring rush of mountain meltwater to wash the heavy brine into the sea, the clean upland breeze to scatter the ammonia musk and dry the warm, sweating rust. And in doing so, with the most righteous of intentions, it would have slaughtered them all.

The blind catfish would have burned and starved in water rendered transparent and sweet. The ivory beetles would have perished in the chill of a collapsed dung-heap. The swallows, their salivary cement cracked by dry air, would have watched their generational towers peel from the scouring girders and plunge into the stones.

Wisdom does not mistake unfamiliar beauty for suffering.

When the Power descends, it settles into the basin not as a shape, but as a subtle change in the density of the dampness—a quiet, immense listening that fills the spaces between the falling condensation drops.

It walks first into the drowned boiler pits, moving through the cold, iron-dense brine without displacing a single grain of suspended limedust. It feels the electrical hum of the blind bullheads as they sweep their fleshy barbels over the rail tracks. The Power brushes its presence against the rubbery scutes of their slate-gray backs, not to restore their degenerate eyes—for what need has a creature of light in a palace built of salt and tallow?—but to bless their blindness. It deepens the electro-receptive clarity of their hides, tuning their skin so finely to the submerged iron that the entire sunken labyrinth of pipes and manifolds sings to them like an unrolled map of warm food and soft silt.

To the turquoise river prawns clinging to the submerged impellers, it leaves the dissolved copper intact, but removes from it the sting of heavy-metal rot, transmuting the poison into a permanent, jewel-hard luster so that their delicate carapaces will never thin or crack against the current.

Rising up through the floor plates into the heat of the guano dunes, the Power breathes in the ferment. It kneels among the trillions of ivory beetles, feeling the frantic, innocent industry of their unpigmented limbs. It knows the ancient horror of the sun they have forgotten, and so it reinforces the dark. With a touch lighter than the tap of a spider’s tarsus, it knits the crumbling concrete over the deep trenches, weaving stone-shadow and dry heat into a perpetual shield, ensuring the subterranean nurseries remain as warm and womb-like as the belly of a living beast.

On the catwalks, it stands beside the stunted herons. It does not stretch their thick legs into the graceful, fragile stilts of their ancestors, but rather strokes the hematite dust on their feathers, setting a quiet strength into their talons so they may grip the vibrating iron through any tremor without fatigue.

And high above, in the arching spans where the swallows sleep and the bats gather their evening storm, the Power addresses the only true enemy of this sovereign world: the fatigue of the iron.

The decay was stable, yes, but metal has a finite memory. In fifty years, or a hundred, an overloaded roof truss—crushed under the sheer tonnage of slag-cement nests and roosting bodies—would have sheared its rusted rivets and brought down three acres of canopy in a catastrophic thunder of crushing steel.

The Power does not replace the rust with bright, cold chromium. It does not heal the steel back to its industrial infancy. Instead, it reaches into the crystalline lattice of the oxidized orange iron, whispering to the molecular decay. It binds the rust to itself. It makes the scabs of hematite as tough as hardened bone, turning the structural rot into a petrified, living marrow that will bear the weight of a billion nests for ten thousand years without snapping.

Lastly, as the dusk begins to purple the edge of the overcast, the Power drifts up to the collapsed stacks. 

The millions of free-tailed bats begin their counter-clockwise spiral, a howling cyclone of leathery wings and rising heat. The Power does not direct them or alter their path. It simply enters the vortex, spinning with them, rising through the torn roofs, tasting the warm, sulfurous wind of their passage.

It gives them no commandments. It brings no salvation, for they were never lost.

It leaves the basin as it found it: wet, heavy, unweeded, and whole. But beneath the milk-white sky, the air holds a new, almost imperceptible resonance. The iron will not fall; the water will not foul; the heat will not break. 

The Power remains only as a lingering warmth at the bottom of the settling vats—a quiet, benevolent witness to the empire of rust and wing, smiling in the humid dark.
