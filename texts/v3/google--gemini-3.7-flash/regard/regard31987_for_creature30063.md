The Power does not descend like a thunderbolt, nor does it unfold wings of light across the nave of the chimney. To enter this place with majesty would be an act of violence: a single displaced gust would rip the sleeping slings from their ironwood pins; a sudden bloom of warmth would dry the liverwort, shatter the delicate moisture-balance of the lungless salamanders, and peel the cold emerald pelt from the stone.

A wise power understands that to love a fragile, ferocious thing, one must first learn how not to crush it.

It enters instead as an attention—a vast, weightless settling, no heavier than the sunlight that slices through the spray at noon. It fills the two hundred yards of roaring cylinder by becoming the space itself: the air, the cold, the shearing wind, and the deep, ringing marrow of the rock.

It observes. It watches the pale cleft-weaver resting its long, jointed shins against the wet schist, tasting the mountain’s shudder. It watches the gray salamander wedged into its dark crack, its ribs like small armor plates against the surge. It watches a Tethered woman hanging two hundred feet over the blue drop, her thumb-ring plucking a rawhide line to tell her kin across the void that the lower bladder is full of sweet water.

It feels the terrible, magnificent pressure of their lives: the constant muscular defiance of gravity, the cold that eats into the marrow, the knowledge that between life and the abyss there is only the tensile strength of boiled sedge and the bite of an ironwood pin.

And because it is wise, it knows what *not* to do.

It does not build bridges of stone across the eye. A bridge would choke the great updraft, starving the vane-hawks of their thermals and halting the ascent of the vapor-drift that feeds the people.

It does not warm the air into a mild lowland spring. To warm the rock would be to rot the moss, kill the stonefly larvae, and turn this clean, sharp cathedral into a tomb of mold.

It does not give the Tethered wings. They are not birds; their dignity lies in their grip, in their deliberate, courageous refusal to fall.

Instead, the benevolent power goes to work in the microscopic margins, where life in the Eye is won or lost by fractions of a grain.

### The Mineral Covenant
Deep within the limestone and blackened schist, where the bedding planes meet under crushing geological load, the Power breathes into the stone. 

It does not alter the mountain's shape, but it heals the rotten seams. Where water has worked into the bedding planes to create treacherous, hidden crumb-zones—the kind that look sound to an iron mallet until an anchor tears free in the night—the rock knits itself tight. The stone does not become soft; it becomes *loyal*. When a Tethered child drives an ironwood pin into a crack, the rock receives it like an old friend, closing around the dry wood with microscopic, elastic tenacity. 

At the back of the deep undercut, where the elders’ hammocks hang in the dark, the Power coaxes a faint, barely perceptible warmth from the mountain’s volcanic root—not enough to melt the frost or steam the air, but just enough that the stone wall behind their backs ceases to be a thief of body heat. It radiates the gentle, neutral temperature of a resting beast.

### The Quieting of the Ache
The Power looks upon the elders of the Tethered. It sees their hands—the fingers curled into rigid, permanent hooks, frozen by decades of cold mist and taut rawhide. It sees the silent agony of the tendon-lock, the burning stiffness that keeps them awake in the damp darkness while the wind hums its hollow note.

The Power leaves the hooks intact, for the hooks keep them alive in the slings. But it draws the venom from the bone.

It smooths the inflamed sheaths of their tendons; it lays a cool, soothing stillness over the calcified joints. The elders wake in the morning, listening to the organ-drone of the shaft, and realize with quiet wonder that while their fingers remain strong as curved horn, the deep, grinding fire in their wrists has simply vanished. They can hang suspended in the void without paying for their survival in pain.

### The Third Tone on the Line
The Power learns the Line-Code.

It does not speak in words—human speech was drowned out here ten thousand years ago—but it gently touches the web of rawhide cordage that spans the perimeter ledges.

When a winter storm builds below the cloud deck, long before the lowlands feel the change in pressure, the rawhide stays begin to sing with a new, distinct resonance: a clear, ringing harmonic that is neither the whistle of the wind nor the rattle of the spray. The Tethered stop their work, lift their leather-tough fingers to the line, and feel the warning. They know, with hours to spare, when to haul the willow baskets high, when to double-lash the nursery hammocks, and when to bank the small, precious coals in their ironstone basins.

And when a guide-rope begins to fray from within—where the rot hides beneath the tallow grease—the cord does not break silently. It emits a dry, sharp hum under the plucking thumb, an unmistakable dissonance that says: *Mend me here.*

### The Loft of the Dead
And when the time comes for an old life to end, and the kin gather at the wet timber lip to slide a dry, light frame out into the blue emptiness, the Power is waiting.

It does not snatch the spirit away into an alien heaven, nor does it let the body tumble carelessly into the dark. It enters the rushing column of air beneath the corpse. It smooths the violent shears into a single, majestic, ascending current of gold-lit mist.

The body rises—higher and more cleanly than it ever did before. It climbs through the sunbeams, flanked by the metallic clicks of ten thousand swallows and the wide, banked sweep of the vane-hawks, rising all the way to the sharp rim of the world. And there, at the apex where the earth touches the upper sky, the wind scatters the spirit and the dust together, carrying them out over the endless sea of lower clouds, not as a loss, but as an offering of rain to the distant forests below.

***

In time, the Tethered notice that their life, while still hard, sharp, and narrow, has lost its cruelty.

They do not know that a god has moved into the Eye. They have no name for it, and they build no shrines—there is no flat place for an altar, and stone is meant for anchoring, not worship. 

They only know that the rock feels sounder under the mallet; that the guide-lines ring with uncanny truth; that the old sleep without groaning; and that sometimes, when the wind lulls at noon and the great cylinder fills with ringing, sunlit clarity, the empty air feels not like a void that wants to swallow them, but like a vast, cold hand that holds them up.
