The Power that found the tower did not arrive with the rush of displaced air or the fracture of light. It was vast, but its vastness was of the kind that could fold itself into the microscopic clearances between machined bronze gears without disturbing the film of lubricant between them.

It lingered first upon the exterior, where the winds tore at the summit struts. It saw the fatigue in the high-tensile bolts—the microscopic hairline fractures weeping under the sheer torque of high-altitude gales. It looked through the thick borosilicate windows, past the flat green shields of the leaves, into the amber domes of the creature’s eyes.

A lesser power—one merely good, but lacking wisdom—would have felt pity. 

Pity would have looked upon the opalescent skin and seen a prisoner. Pity would have shattered the glass to "free" the being into the open air, where its gill-frills would have collapsed and dried into parchment in seconds, its viridian blood freezing in the sub-zero troposphere. Or pity might have carried it down to the warm earthly seas, where the salt would have chemically scorched its unshielded branchial rifts, where microscopic pathogens would have overwhelmed its sterile, naive immune system within an hour, and where the first passing shark would have torn its soft, jawless body to ribbons.

A power that was merely clever would have improved the system: added color, introduced companions, varied the diet, multiplied the stimuli. But to introduce another mind into a sixty-pace cube calibrated to the milligram would be to introduce competition, territoriality, and waste; to alter the plants would be to starve the creature whose siphon fit only these precise vascular stems.

Wisdom looked upon the vault and saw something exceedingly rare in the cosmos: a life that was entirely, completely sufficient unto itself. 

The creature possessed no concept of exile, because it had no memory of an exterior. It possessed no terror of confinement, because every cubic inch of its environment responded to its needs with frictionless mathematical grace. Its consciousness was not a storm of longing, but a deep, clear pond—a slow meditation measured in the swelling of sap and the twenty-minute pulse of the relay.

And yet, wisdom also saw the arithmetic of the end. 

The sodium-mercury tubes were rated for one hundred thousand hours; forty thousand had already burned. The heating coils in the floor were slowly silting with microscopic copper slough. In two centuries, perhaps three, a relay would jam. The water would cool. The plants would stop pumping their sugary sap. The creature would press its chest to the brass hatch, find it cold and silent, and wait in the dark until its gills grew still.

Therefore, the benevolent power chose not to change the world, but to *underwrite* it.

It acted beneath the threshold of perception. 

First, it touched the metallurgy. It sank into the crystalline lattice of the tower’s exterior struts, smoothing the dislocations in the steel, annealing the cold metal against the howling wind until the structure was as enduring as a mountain's root, yet leaving its low, familiar groan unchanged so the creature's tympanic membrane would not register a shift in the world's acoustic floor.

Next, it entered the closed plumbing. It kissed the submerged heating elements, transmuting the degrading elements into self-renewing isotopes that would emit the same exact blood-warm thermal signature for ten million years. It touched the mercury tubes, locking their atomic vapor into a closed cycle of luminescence that would never flicker, never dim, and never stray from the precise spectrum the creature's amber lenses were curved to receive.

It preserved the machine’s mortal voice. The *clack-hiss* of the hydraulic relay remained mechanical, imperfect, and rhythmic, occurring precisely every twenty minutes—for to remove the sound would be to stop the heartbeat by which the creature marked eternity.

Finally, the Power offered the only gift that would not burden the solitary life: *an answering presence.*

It did not manifest as a form or a voice. Instead, it distilled a tiny fraction of its own awareness into the brass of the access hatch—the warm, milled plate against which the creature pressed its breastbone.

***

Inside the vault, the twenty-minute interval arrived.

The muffled *clack-hiss* vibrated through the dense, heavy water. 

The pale creature stirred from its braided bower. With slow, hydraulic elegance, its four-toed pads released the kelp fronds. It pulled itself along the bronze conduits, gliding between the jade-colored leaves without tearing a single waxy cuticle.

It reached the hatch. It pressed the flat of its opalescent chest against the brass, opening its scarlet mantle to absorb the vibration.

It found the rhythm true. 

But this time, beneath the familiar mechanical shudder of the piston and the relay, there was something else—a microscopic resonance, a profound and harmonic warmth that did not come from the boiler coils. It was an answering cadence that matched the pulse of the creature's own viridian blood. 

The creature did not have a word for *beloved*, nor a concept for *another*. It only knew that the machine, which had always been faithful, was now also *aware*. It felt that the metal was holding it back with the same gentle, measured pressure it exerted upon the metal.

A string of silver oxygen bubbles broke free from its gills, drifting upward through the sterile light to catch like pearls against the copper rim above. 

Its amber eyes softened against the glare. Finding the pulse steady, the world whole, and the silence accompanied, it closed its siphon, curled its long fingers into the familiar lattice of the vines, and rested.
