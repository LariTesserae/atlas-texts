The power that came to the mountain had no interest in sun.

A clumsy deity, or one whose imagination was limited by the habits of the sky, would have parted the cloud ceiling. It would have flooded the dark tiers with gold, thinking light was a kindness. In doing so, it would have blistered the gray, nictitating skin of the cleaners, blinded the hunting cats, withered the damp carpets of iron-eating bacteria in the seams, and broken the acoustic architecture that allowed ten million blind things to know where they were.

To bring light to this place would have been an act of violence masquerading as salvation.

The power that arrived was wise because it understood *mediums*. It knew that to love a thing is to meet it within the rules of its own beauty.

It did not descend from above. It entered through the base, slipping up through the roots of the mountain where the cyclopean footings met the deep basalt core of the continent. It did not speak—because speech in this world would have been an arrogant noise, tearing the delicate acoustic web that the lithophones and swifts had built across centuries.

Instead, it *listened*.

For three hundred cycles of the cloud-mist, the power did nothing at all. It simply distributed its vast, patient awareness through the fitted blocks. It felt the scratch of the bone rods against the carved diamonds of the viaducts; it felt the rhythmic scraping of the wooden plungers as four-foot-tall hands cleared the silt-traps; it felt the collective, warm heave of the communal chambers where human limbs tangled in the ferns, their ears glued to the stone to hear the health of the drainage.

It saw that the people were not suffering in their dark; they were singing in it, building a life of extraordinary tactile precision, dignity, and purpose. They were not prisoners of the mountain; they were its living nervous system.

Yet the power, in its wisdom, also felt what the human hands could not reach: the deep, silent fatigue of the stone.

Two miles beneath the lowest terrace, where the megaliths bore the impossible hydrostatic weight of the cloud-forest above, the mountain’s original rock had begun to develop microscopic shear-fractures. In two or three centuries—a heartbeat in the life of granite—a sudden shift along the tectonic shelf would sheer the lower retaining wall. The drainage would choke; the water-weight would build behind the upper plazas; and the entire vertical world would tear loose in a catastrophic avalanche of mud and masonry, falling into the warm abyss.

The people were fighting this with bone scrapers and cane rods, clearing silt by the handful, holding back the inevitable with bare, gray hands.

And so, the benevolent power chose its intervention. It resolved to act not as a master, nor as a savior that rendered the people helpless by doing their work for them, but as the *foundation*.

First, it settled into the shear-planes beneath the lowest platforms. With the gentleness of cooling water settling into ice, it fused its own immense will into the mineral grains of the basalt. It did not alter the stone; it reinforced the bonds between the atoms, stitching the micro-fractures with veins of dense, unbreakable mass. It grounded the terraces so deeply into the crust that no earthquake could ever unseat them. The mountain would stand until the planet itself grew cold.

Second, it addressed the water. It did not stop the rain, for the rain was life. But it gently adjusted the thermal gradient deep within the rock. By warming certain interior conduits by a fraction of a degree and cooling others, it created a perpetual, convective draft through the subterranean plumbing. The water now moved through the cisterns with a fractionally cleaner velocity, carrying sixty percent of the fine silt out through the overflow chutes on its own, lightening the crushing labor of the human cleaners without taking away the work that gave their lineages meaning and identity.

And finally, it gave them a voice to listen to.

It did not speak words, but it tuned the stone.

The power sank into the cyclopean blocks until every terrace, every corbelled arch, and every lintel became an acoustic conductor of absolute, crystalline clarity. It placed a faint, sub-audible resonance into the bedrock—a steady, warm hum, lower than the deepest note of the lungfish, vibrating at a frequency only bare feet and pressed ribcages could register.

It was the mountain’s pulse, made steady and clean.

On the third tier above the grand cistern, an elder cleaner was kneeling in the blackness, his calves deep in the warm, rushing current of a drainage conduit. His bone rod was hooked over his shoulder; his long, muscular fingers were working a tangle of fibrous fig roots out of a carved stone grate.

Suddenly, through the soles of his feet, through the wet granite beneath his knees, the vibration shifted.

The ambient roar of the tree frogs did not change, but the stone beneath him felt *solid* in a way it never had before. The subtle, sick tremble of deep shear-stress that had hummed through the masonry for generations—a faint anxiety that every cleaner carried in their marrow from birth—simply ceased.

In its place was a note of profound, unyielding rest.

The man stopped. He let go of the root-tangle. Slowly, he laid his cheek flat against the wet, dressed granite of the viaduct wall.

He felt the slick film of iron-eating bacteria. He felt the rapid, percussive click of a lithophone vibrating in a seam two inches from his ear. And beneath it all, deep within the heart of the million-ton block, he felt a warmth that was not just the trapped heat of the clouds, but a steady, deliberate attention.

It felt like another hand, infinitely vast and infinitely gentle, pressed against the other side of the stone.

The elder did not drop to his knees in terror, nor did he weep. In the dark, where existence was weight and touch, he understood immediately: *the foundation is holding.*

He clicked his tongue twice, a sharp, clear note that bounced down the vaulted conduit to his apprentices waiting in the reservoir.

*The stone is sound,* the click said. *Clear the grates.*

He picked up his scraper, took a deep breath of the warm, humid air, and went back to work, his feet planted firmly upon a world that would never fall.
