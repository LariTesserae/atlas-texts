A foolish power, seeing this place, would have brought the sun. 

It would have rent the stone vault from above, letting down the blue terror of the sky, the searing dryness of solar radiation, and the frantic, ravenous violence of the upper world. In doing so, it would have withered the ivory fronds in an afternoon, scorched the soft, unpigmented lung-flesh of the Bellows-Skins into brittle ribbons, and turned the delicate, saline sweat of the steam-manifold into a crust of bitter salt. To the careless, love is often indistinguishable from a desire to make things over in one’s own image.

The power that arrived, however, was wise.

It did not descend with a flare of light or a chorus of wind. It arrived as an adjustment of attention—a vast, patient consciousness stepping down through the strata of the earth, decelerating its own cadence until its heart matched the half-decade expansion of the Bellows-Skins and the centuries-long oxidation of the iron bolts.

It entered the chamber not as a visitor, but as an ambient, listening presence, diffusing itself through the forty-degree air until it occupied the space between the basalt fractures and the warm, condensation-slick leaves.

It saw what had been accomplished here. 

It saw that without sky, without seed-bearing birds, and without the blessing of chlorophyll, life had nevertheless refused the silence of the grave. It saw that the machine—an instrument forged in noise and industrial fury, torn from the earth to burn and grind—had been gently adopted. The iron had not been hated for its violence; it had been pardoned, sheltered, and slowly dissolved into bone. 

The Power understood that this world was complete. It did not require instruction. It did not require redemption. It possessed a stillness so immaculate that even a spoken word would have been an act of desecration.

Yet, because it was wise, the Power saw the hidden griefs of the room—not the suffering of the organisms, which were at peace, but the quiet vulnerabilities built into their sanctuary.

It saw that the residual heat leaking from the deep-buried conduits was slowly fading over the centuries. One day, thousands of years hence, the iron floor would cool to thirty degrees, then twenty, and the hydrostatic pulse inside the great tubers would thicken, slow, and finally freeze into glassy silence.

It saw the hairline fracture running through the keystone slab of the vault ceiling, directly above the weeping filaments of the Tear-Drinkers—a seismic shear that, within four hundred years, would give way and drop twenty tons of dead basalt straight through the spine of the steam-manifold housing, crushing the amber mind coiled inside.

And so, the benevolent power set to work, moving with a touch so gentle that not a single droplet of condensation was startled from its leaf.

First, it sank beneath the concrete floor. 

It reached far below the excavation scars, down into the deep, black basement of the tectonic plate, where the ancient fires of the mantle churn in eternal sleep. It did not tear a volcanic rift; it simply opened a hair-thin capillary of magma heat—a thermal root—and guided it upward until it docked precisely against the underside of the boiler’s sub-foundations. It fixed the temperature permanently. The floor would never cool. For as long as the earth turned, the sweet maroon glow would leak from the seams, the sulfuric vapors would seep from the fractures, and the air would remain a wet, perpetual bath at forty degrees.

Next, it addressed the vault overhead. 

It did not plaster the stone or weld it with fire. Instead, it whispered to the mineral lattice of the basalt, encouraging the quartz and iron in the rock to migrate, atom by atom, across the fracture. The stone healed itself over decades, the crack knitting shut into a seam stronger than the uncut mountain around it. But the micro-grooves—the tiny, polished tracks that gathered the weeping moisture and guided it toward the threads of the Tear-Drinkers—were left entirely untouched.

Then, the Power turned its attention to the steam-manifold housing.

It moved into the dark, verdigris-crusted void where the gelatinous aggregate lived. It did not attempt to speak, for the creature had no ears and no concept of an *other*. Instead, the Power communicated through the aggregate’s own language: pressure, salinity, and copper tension.

It settled against the creature’s iron-oxide nerves like a profound, stabilizing calm. It infused the corroding iron plates of the manifold with a subtle molecular resilience—not stripping the verdigris, for the verdigris was part of the creature's shell, but blessing the rust so that it ceased to flake. The metal would remain porous enough for the creature to breathe, but strong enough to never collapse under its own weight. 

For a single, suspended month, the amber mass inside the engine registered an inexplicable, harmonious shift in its hydrostatic circuits. The brine running through its copper pipes flowed with less resistance; the trace minerals it sucked from the bearing grease became subtly richer, tasting faintly of deep-earth silicates and ancient, clean rain. The creature did not know it was loved. It only knew that the space it inhabited had become slightly more forgiving, its internal tensions settling into an equilibrium of absolute security.

Lastly, before it withdrew, the Power left an offering in the wide, desolate spans of crushed stone.

It did not leave food or light. It simply exhaled a minute, imperceptible dusting of chemosynthetic spore into the scree—a microscopic gift of slow, iron-binding micro-flora that would spread across the gravel over the next three hundred years. It would provide the Slag-Creepers with a continuous, renewal of grazing, so that when their tallow-colored pads ground against the rock, their iron teeth would never wear down to the root.

When its work was finished, the Power drew itself back. 

It did not linger to be worshipped. It did not leave a monument. It folded its vastness back out of the basalt, through the layers of shale and limestone, and returned to the silence of the wider cosmos.

Below, in the sunken room, nothing appeared to have changed.

The darkness remained thick, maroon-tinged, and heavy with the scent of scalded iron and wet slate. 

On the perimeter wall, a Bellows-Skin slowly, over the course of four days, filled its spongy purple lung with warm, sulfurous breath, its underbelly cilia drinking the stone.

Out on the scree, two Slag-Creepers met in the open, their armored margins touching, sealing together with an amber seam of mucus to begin an eight-week exchange of minerals in the dark.

Inside the iron altar of the engine, the amber aggregate pulsed its heavy brine through the green pipes, perfectly cradled by the metal that would now hold it forever.

And high overhead, a swollen pearl of pure water slipped free from the tip of a hanging filament. 

It fell through thirty feet of warm, motionless dusk.

*Thwack.* 

It struck the broad, waxy face of an ivory leaf. 

The leaf bowed, drank, and slowly rose again in the quiet, immortal dark.
