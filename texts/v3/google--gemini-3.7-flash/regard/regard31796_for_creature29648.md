A foolish god would have brought fire.

A clumsy benevolence would have looked down upon the four-hundred-pace cirque, wept at the raw, splayed feet and the horizontal, amber eyes, and laid a warm hand across the diorite. It would have softened the diamond-cut glare, breathed summer into the throat of the mountain, and melted the supercooled brine into sweet, placid water. 

And in doing so, it would have slaughtered them all.

The liverwort would have rotted in an hour. The blind, pearlescent ribbons, starved of the mineral-dense surge and the scouring cold, would have died and floated belly-up, their slime failing, allowing what little ice remained to choke the sluices. The four-winged divers, heavy with marrowless bone, would have drowned in the thinned, warm water. The people, their slate-gray hide smothered beneath useless layers of subcutaneous fat, their vast lungs panting uselessly in heavy, wet heat, would have found their pickled, self-sealing soles softening to mush, unable to grip stone that had lost its film of salt. The city of ancestors—the calcified, glass-hard cornerstones—would have slowly dissolved in the fresh runoff, slumping twelve tiers of sod and whale-spar into the valley below.

A wise power does not mistake adaptation for agony, nor does it destroy a masterpiece of endurance merely because it is sharp.

Therefore, when the power came, it came without light, without heat, and without a voice. It descended not as an intervention from the sky, but as a subtle, pervasive presence that settled directly into the marrow of the basin—entering the stone, the brine, and the widened chests of the people.

It asked itself: *Where does the suffering exceed the beauty?*

The answer was not the cold. The cold was their steel. The answer was not the silence, nor the perpetual labor, nor the ancestral stone beneath their heels. 

The suffering was the terror of the trance. 

It was the five-minute shudder—the sleep that was not rest, but a desperate, clawing suspension between freezing and waking. It was the absolute poverty of interior space, where a consciousness built for awe was forced to spend every millisecond calculating the drag of water and the tension of sinew, leaving no room for a single quiet thought to take root.

And so, the benevolent power did four things.

***

First, it touched the deep geothermal fissures beneath the diorite floor. 

It did not heat the brine; instead, it imposed upon the surging springs a profound, unyielding *tempo*. Before, the water had welled in violent, erratic spasms that caught children unawares and tore scaffolding away at random. Now, the supercooled tide took on a regular, rhythmic pulse—rising for fifty minutes, and receding for ten into a predictable, glassy calm. 

The frantic, insect-like scurry of the people did not end, but its panic vanished. In its place came a severe, breathtaking choreography. The builders learned the breathing of the mountain; they knew precisely when the surge would drop, and in those ten minutes of low water, their antler picks struck with the effortless grace of dancers who no longer feared the floor giving way.

***

Second, it changed the nature of their brief, crouched sleep.

When a builder or a weaver dropped onto their haunches in the sod-domes, chin pressed to breastbone, the power did not alter the cold air that threatened to freeze them if they stayed still too long. Instead, it bent the subjective flow of time within their minds. 

In the five minutes between the pulses of the brine, the mind was granted an expanse of vast, warm, unhurried interiority. When their amber-slit eyes closed beneath the horizontal lids, they did not fall into a black, shivering void. They fell into an internal cathedral—a place of absolute, weightless stillness where five minutes felt like eight hours of deep, unthreatened slumber. In that brief window, their muscles repaired their micro-tears, their fat stores replenished their tallow, and their minds had room to wander through long, silent landscapes of pure memory. 

When they woke—shuddering, as they must, to throw off the riming salt—they did not wake haggard and hollow-eyed. They stood with clear, rested minds, their bodies light, their spirits whole.

***

Third, it gave a gift to the dead.

The power settled into the petrified bodies of the ancestors—those translucent sculptures of iron and white mineral that formed the lintels, the sluice-diverters, and the foundations of the tiers. 

It did not bring them back to life; to do so would have broken the city they held up. Instead, it turned the calcified remains into acoustic conductors of incredible sensitivity. Whenever a sudden stress formed in the rock—a shifting fault, a weakening mastodon rib, an avalanche gathering on the high cirque miles above—the bones of the ancestors began to resonate. 

They did not make a sound in the air, for the air remained dead and thin. Instead, they transmitted a deep, steady infrasonic hum directly into the stone. The living, walking with their broad, bare soles pressed against the wet rock, felt this vibration through their heels and calves. 

The ancestors became an early-warning network, whispering through the soles of their grandchildren: *Step here. Do not build there. The cliff will hold.* The people were no longer merely walking on the dead; they were being held by them, guided by a tactile geography of love and structural integrity.

***

Fourth, and finally, it touched the glare.

The power did not dim the sun—the diver-birds needed the brightness to spot the pale ribbons, and the liverwort required the hard radiation to synthesize its grease. But high above the basin, in the thin, ink-blue sky where the wind sheared off the cirque’s knife-edge, the power gathered the invisible spray cast off by the divers' wings and froze it into an infinitely vast, suspended lens of micro-crystals.

This lens did not block the light; it polarized it.

Suddenly, the blinding, chaotic glare that turned the basin into a furnace of broken mirrors was organized into a spectrum of soft, legible hues. To human eyes, the water ceased to be a white glare that blinded the unprotected pupil; it became translucent, revealing the depths of the gutters, the movements of the ribbon-amphibians, and the structural seams of the diorite in shades of deep indigo, violet, and cool amber. 

The physical eye was spared the agony of the burn, while retaining every grain of detail necessary for survival.

***

The benevolent power did not stay to be worshiped. It did not write commandments in the stone, nor did it demand that they change their ways. It drew back into the ink-blue sky, becoming nothing more than the quiet, watchful consciousness of the space itself.

Below, the basin remained what it had always been: a place of fierce, diamond cold, where supercooled brine wept over black basalt, where blind white ribbons churned through gutters, and where four-winged birds fell like dropped iron through the spray.

The people still moved with incredible speed. They still did not speak.

But now, as a master-weaver stood upon a mastodon spar, watching the rhythmic retreat of the surge under the violet-tinted sky, she felt the steady, reassuring pulse of her grandfather’s stone rib beneath her heel. She felt her lungs fill with clean, freezing air, her rested mind sharp and untroubled. 

She lifted her hands. Her fingers did not twitch with the frantic panic of the hunted. Instead, with deliberate, fluid precision, her knuckles signed across the spray to the child waiting on the tier below:

*The water is breathing.*
*The floor is holding.*
*We are here.*
