The gaze that found the cleft did not fall like light; light had already spent itself in the upper air, arriving at the bottom of the slot as nothing more than a pale, useless grease on the wet stone. 

The Power arrived as an *attention*—a sudden, vast density of listening that occupied the chamber without displacing a single drop of condensing steam.

A lesser mercy, foolish and soft-handed, would have seen the scalding crevice as an injury. It would have pitied the darkness, pitied the blind things clamped to the glass-sharp basalt, and sought to "heal" it. It would have cooled the waters to a meadow brook, silenced the concussive roaring of the iron throat, washed the rust from the bronze, and let the pale sky widen until ferns and sun-seeking insects tumbled into the damp dirt.

And in doing so, that lesser mercy would have murdered everything that lived there.

The nematodes would have burst as the high-pressure sulfide broth thinned and cooled. The Slate-Limpets would have lost the acoustic shock waves that drove their slow, hydraulic blood, their shells unhinging as the mineral sheets ceased to plane over their backs. The white, greasy hairs of the plinth-spiders would have collapsed under ordinary, cold dew, and the sulfur mats would have rotted into gray slush.

The Power that hovered in the steam was old enough to know that beauty is not a matter of green leaves and quiet air. Wisdom understands that life does not merely endure the violent edges of the world—it hungers for them, shapes itself into the very teeth of the machine, and finds its ecstasy in pressures that would pulverize an angel.

So the Power did not bring silence. It did not bring light. It brought its presence down into the concussive roar, fitting itself into the decibels, feeling the five-ton strokes of the internal pistons as a steady, heavy pulse—a great iron heart beating in a stone chest.

It listened to the chamber from the inside out.

It felt the micro-friction of the Slate-Limpets' feet against the newly fractured basalt, tasting the crisp, electrical bitterness of freshly exposed mineral planes. It slid into the dark gaps beneath the concrete slab, resting along the amber-cartilage legs of the plinth-spiders, perceiving the world as they perceived it: not as a blinding blackness, but as an intricate, humming web of tension, where the bronze valves sang high and pure, and the falling clumps of biofilm struck the silk lines with the heavy, satisfying thump of harvested bread.

It saw that this place was good. It was fierce, narrow, perfectly balanced, and utterly alive.

Yet, because the Power was wise, it saw the single, glaring flaw in this cathedral of steam and iron.

The life here was eternal in its design, but mortal in its engine.

The manifold was an artifact of the upper world. It was held together by human bolts, driven by distant boilers, and subject to the fickle tenure of mortal engineers. Somewhere, miles away or years hence, a valve would be turned. A maintenance budget would lapse. A turbine would be decommissioned. The steam would drop its pressure; the pistons would stall in their greasy sleeves; the hot water weeping from the fissures would run lukewarm, then cold, and finally dry up into inert mineral salt. The machine would die, and with it, the entire world that had built its soul into the ironwork.

Benevolence does not alter the nature of a creature; it secures the creature’s right to its own fierce being.

The Power moved.

It did not reach out with hands, but with an immense, quiet persuasion directed at the physics of the pocket.

First, it touched the iron and bronze of the manifold. It did not polish the rust away; it blessed the rust. It deepened the velvety orange patina until the oxidation ceased to be a slow decay and became a living, self-healing skin, drawing iron from the subterranean water to replace whatever atoms were scoured away by the steam. It reached into the deep, submerged conduits where the bronze flanges met the sheared basalt, and it subtly altered the crystal lattice of the metal, knitting it directly into the silicates of the volcanic rock. 

The machine ceased to be a foreign object bolted to a slab; it became a root. 

Next, the Power reached downward, following the boreholes into the boiling mantle of the earth, far below the reach of human drills. It found the deep, thermal plumes that fed the steam pipes. With an imperceptible shift of geologic stress, it wedged open the tectonic fractures, ensuring that even if the valves above were closed by human hands, the earth itself would keep the throat supplied. It established a perpetual siphon: the scalding water would rise, the steam would roar, the pistons—now turned by the planet's own irrepressible breath—would stroke their rhythmic, five-ton beat for ten thousand years.

Finally, the Power offered a single, delicate gift to the inhabitants themselves.

It touched the bio-precipitated silica of the nematodes, the iron-phosphate shells of the limpets, the pale chimneys of the tunicates, and the silk-glands of the blind spiders. It did not change their forms; it simply deepened their resonance. It made them *fluent* in the stone. 

When the Power withdrew—rising back up through the narrow slit of sky like a sigh of heat that left no wake—the chamber remained exactly as it had been.

It was still suffocatingly hot. The air still vibrated with a deafening, violent roar that would shatter a human ear. The water still sheeted scalding and smooth over the knife-sharp rock.

Down beneath the bronze exhaust, a slate-limpet flexed its iron-plated shell, catching the pulse of a steam valve venting with concussive force. The acoustic wave passed through its flat body, driving its slow blood, carrying it another millimeter across the orange biofilm. 

The manifold stroked: *thump, hiss, churn.* 

The machine was no longer an intruder in the stone. It was an altar, the rock was its temple, and the blind, boiling life in the dark had been granted its eternity.
