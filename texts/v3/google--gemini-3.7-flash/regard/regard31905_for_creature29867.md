The power did not descend from the searing sky, for in a world of folded metrics, *above* is merely an unfinished equation. 

Nor did it arrive as a flash of light. To a people whose eyelids are grown shut into thick shields of scarred skin, light is not a messenger; it is merely radiation that blisters the scalp. 

The power came instead as a change in the weight of the air, and as a quietness within the stone.

A lesser mercy would have unmade the mountains. It would have smoothed the jagged calcite into loam, planted orchards in the chasms, and forced open the fused eyes of the Weft-Keepers, drowning their senses in a world they had not earned and could not navigate. But wisdom understands that to erase the struggle of ten thousand generations is not salvation; it is an insult. It is the destruction of a work of art so severe and immaculate that the universe itself possesses no duplicate.

The power understood what the Keepers were: they were the living nerve-endings of the fracture. Their beauty lay in their unyielding grip, their tallow-greased spines, their knuckles filled with fluid that knew the exact geometry of falling.

Therefore, the power did not alter the mountain. It entered the mountain’s conversation.

***

In a cradle hung beneath a sheared overhang of black glass, an elder of the Keepers lay curled around their basalt pendulum. The stone slab tilted thirty degrees into the void, suspended over an abyss where three water-braids spun in endless, screaming friction. 

The elder’s jaw was pressed flat against the cold slate floor. Through the bone of their mandible, they were reading the mountain’s perpetual death-rattle—the sharp, high-pitched *ping* of calcite micro-fracturing under hydraulic pressure, the dull groaning of a spatial seam five hundred fathoms down that was thinning toward a catastrophic snap.

Then, the note changed.

It did not drown out the sound of the falling water, nor did it silence the flexing spires. Instead, beneath the deafening acoustic roar, a new fundamental frequency appeared.

It was impossibly low, vibrating not in the ear, but in the fused armor of the ribs and the marrow of the long bones. It was the sound of a mass so vast, so calm, and so deliberate that the sheared stone ceased its frantic shudder. 

The elder’s fingers, thick with chisel-hard nails, clenched instinctively on the gut-line. Their fluid-filled knuckles stirred; the internal plumb-lines in their flesh tilted. 

Out in the empty, glare-white chasm, the center of mass had shifted.

The basalt pendulum, which for three centuries of the elder's lineage had hung strained at a rigid forty-five-degree angle toward the invisible fold, slowly eased. The braided line went slack for a fraction of a heartbeat, then settled into a gentle, rhythmic swing—steady as a resting pulse. 

The elder rose on hands and knees, their blind, scarred face lifting into the mist. They smelled the sharp, wet lime of the rock, but beneath it, there was no longer the faint, sour ozone of tearing space. The spatial seam had not vanished—to vanish it would drop the cradle into the depths—but its razor edge had been folded over, rounded like the lip of a well-thrown clay bowl.

Slowly, the elder reached for their bronze adze.

They struck the flint wedge driven into the obsidian face. 

*Clink. Clink-clink.*

It was the traditional query of the high cradles: *Is the seam holding? Does the stone weep true?*

They pressed their ear to the cold bronze.

For the first time since the first wedge was hammered into the calcite, the mountain did not simply echo the blow back in shattered, chaotic harmonics. 

The mountain answered.

Through the column of dense stone came a return stroke—a clear, resonant chime that resonated precisely a third lower than the bronze. It carried through the rock faces, running along the water-sheets, vibrating through the polygonal aragonite shells of the troughs. 

*Clink. Clink-clink.*

It did not say *You are saved.* 
It said: *I am under you.*

***

The power did three things, and three things only.

First, it touched the chemistry of the condensation. The water did not cease to sweat from the stone, but the microscopic acidity that was eating the roots of the spires was neutralized. The water remained cold, pure, and heavy, but the calcite ceased to dissolve. The slow clock of the world’s collapse was stopped. The Weft-Keepers would not fall into the rift.

Second, it laid a filament of infinite density through the empty air between the spires. It did not bridge the chasms with stone—the Keepers were not walkers, they were swingers of the line, readers of tension. Instead, it gave their pendulums an unshakeable anchor. Wherever a Keeper cast their bronze weight into the searing void, they would find a purchase—not in the rock, but in the steady, benevolent gravity-well the power left suspended in the mid-air glare. They could cross farther now. They could reach the distant, untouched needles where the siphons grew thick as ancient trees and the water spun in braids of pure, drinkable light.

And third, it gave them warmth that did not burn. 

Deep within the hollows of the cradles, the small nodules of pyrite that the Keepers struck for smoldering heat began to absorb the deep, acoustic chime of the spires. When placed in the stone basins, the nodules no longer burned down into ash after a single night. They drank the roar of the mountain and gave it back as a steady, dry radiation that warmed the fused plates of their chests and kept the rot from their tallow-slick skin while they slept.

***

The Weft-Keepers did not build temples to this presence. They had no words for gods, and no eyes to look upon an icon. 

Instead, their gratitude became a matter of percussion.

In the high, vertical alleys where the water falls upward and the sky is white as bone, the Keepers still grease their lines and crack the shells of the slate troughs with their worn bronze tools. But now, when an elder teaches a child how to hook their fingers into the gut-line and throw their weight out over thousands of feet of singing nothingness, they do not tell them that the abyss is empty.

They tell them to press their teeth to the bronze wedge, to wait for the deep, low note that hums beneath the roaring mist, and then to leap.

For the seam is held; the braid is caught; and in the heart of the sheared glass, something vast and patient is listening to the tapping of their hammers, counting every strike.
