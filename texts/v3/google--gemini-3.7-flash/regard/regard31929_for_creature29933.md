The Power does not come down through the split in the limestone. To descend would imply a disturbance of the draft, a displacement of the fine, hanging mist, or a shadow cast across the pale liverworts—and the Power is too ancient to be clumsy. 

Instead, its attention settles like an added barometric density, a quiet awareness that knows the weight of every cobble in the siphon and the exact salt-yield of the man’s stiffened fleece.

A lesser power—one merely kind, but foolish—would have brought light. It would have split the roof boulders to let in the sun, which would have withered the deep bryophyte pelt in an afternoon, blinded the translucent grubs, and scorched the man’s water-logged skin until it cracked into bleeding fissures. A foolish power would have silenced the roar, leaving the man stranded in a sudden, terrifying sensory desert where his thickened skull had nothing to brace against, his jaw idle, his entire apparatus of comprehension rendered instantly dead.

The wise Power understands that this place is not a prison; it is a completed equation. The man is not a captive awaiting rescue; he is an organ of the flue, as fitted to the dark as the blind leeches are to the rotting peat.

Therefore, the Power does not alter the world. It honors the terms of the stone.

First, it attends to the scaffolding. 

In the high gloom, where the split-pine poles are black with water-rot and bound by fraying horsehair, the Power works within the mineral wash. It does not replace the wood with unyielding gold or dry cedar; it accelerates the work of the lime. Molecule by molecule, the dissolved calcite in the dripping condensation begins to replace the decaying cellulose of the timber. Over seven nights, without a sound or a splinter shifting, the rot halts. The notched poles turn to petrified wood—black, cold, and ringed like ancient horn, permanently bonded into the sockets of the limestone. They will not snap beneath the ball of his foot. He will have his footholds for another three hundred years.

Second, it attends to the draft.

A mile above, on the rim of the frost-bitten dale, the wind usually scatters the autumn windfall into the heather. The Power does not change the weather, but gently tilts the curvature of the air above the fissure by the width of a hawk’s wing. 

When the first blizzard comes, the vortex draws in something more than starved pipits and bitter weed-seeds. Down through the stone grates tumble the dense, resinous cones of high stone-pines, rich with fatty kernels, and the bodies of mountain hares taken cleanly by the frost before sickness could touch their meat. The drift that gathers in his woven birch baskets becomes slightly richer, slightly more sustaining, but retains the necessary taste of snow and iron.

Third, it attends to the teeth.

The limestone of the northern wall is abrasive. Decades of pressing his lower incisors against the wet rock to read the siphons have ground the enamel thin, exposing the dark, sensitive dentin beneath. 

The Power does not speak into his mind—a mind shaped by seventy decibels of water-thunder has no room for the gossamer architecture of human words. Instead, it alters the vibration in the rock itself.

When the man next climbs to his high perch, leans forward, and seats his lower jaw against the shelf, the stone does not shudder with only the blind, chaotic violence of gravel in the lower siphons. 

The roar remains, but beneath it, deep in the bedrock where the Earth’s mantle presses against the root of the continent, a new frequency begins to hum. 

It is a low, structural cadence, resonant and profound, traveling up through the dense limestone directly into the bones of his face. It tells him of the true shapes of the mountains above—not as sights, but as immense weights resting upon the mantle. It tells him of the slow, cold movements of the glaciers thirty miles north, of the subterranean sea sleeping two miles down, and of the quiet, unbroken patience of the earth turning through the night. 

Through his teeth, he receives the music of mass and gravity. He learns that he is held.

Finally, the Power leaves a touch within the four-slab shelter. 

It does not dry the peat. It deepens the anaerobic fermentation in the floor by half a degree—just enough that when he burrows his stiff, sheep-fleece mantle into the rotted heather, the deep, greasy warmth meets his hip-bones and softens the ache in his lumbar vertebrae. 

The man knows nothing of the Power. He knows only that the wood under his rasp-skinned soles feels exceptionally true; that the drift in his bone-scraper is heavy with fat; that the stone against his mouth speaks of things vast, steady, and unhurried; and that when he sleeps, the earth keeps him warm enough to wake again.

The void remains cold, dark, and deafening. The liverworts drink the mist. The drop of lime falls from the tip of the fern. And the man breathes his white breath into the black air, utterly at home in the throat of the world.
