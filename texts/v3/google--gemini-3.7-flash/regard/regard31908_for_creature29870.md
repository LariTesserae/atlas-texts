The Power did not descend as a light, for in a place where eyes have softened to the blue-grey gloom of river-mist and slate, a sudden radiance would have been an act of violence. 

Nor did it speak. To speak into that vault—to cast vowels and consonants against twelve-foot plates of pitted pig iron and sixty paces of compressed heartwood—would have been like dropping a dry leaf into the teeth of the flywheel. Speech requires a silence that this chamber had forgotten six hundred years ago, and forgotten without regret.

The Power was ancient enough to possess the rarest branch of wisdom: the understanding that **completion does not require redemption.**

A lesser divinity, viewing the vault through the lens of human sorrow, might have seen horror here: men and women deafened from childhood, their limbs dyed the color of wet slag, their lives bound to a blind, endless orbit around a mechanical crank; spiders tuned to misery; blind crayfish trapped in timber chutes; a colossal, dead machine wearing a living tree like a shroud. Such a power would have broken the flume, stopped the wheel, dragged the attendants blinking and terrified into the high meadow sun, and called it mercy.

And in doing so, it would have slaughtered a world.

The wise Power understood that there is a holiness in petrifaction. It saw that the vault had achieved what the noisy, bustling empires on the surface spend millennia failing to build: a closed, rhythmic, suffering-less perpetuity. Nothing was dying here that was not immediately transformed into the smooth velvet of liverwort or the fine mineral dust beneath the crawl of a slate-backed beetle. The attendants did not hunger; they did not harbor the bitter, restless poison of unanswered yearning; they slept with their foreheads to the wood, cradled in the only certainty the earth can truly offer—that the next stroke will fall precisely when it is due.

Therefore, the Power resolved to do the hardest thing an almighty consciousness can do:

**It decided to do almost nothing.**

It did not stop the piston. It did not mend their ears. It did not turn the blue-black wood back into green, sap-bleeding timber.

Instead, it laid its will over the chamber like a second skin—an invisible, hydrostatic seal against the clumsiness of the outer world.

### The Adjustments of the Margin

Where the Power acted, it acted solely at the thresholds, where the vault touched the chaotic, decaying world of the surface:

* **The Karst and the Silt:** Far upstream, where the river entered the limestone fissures that fed the engine’s headwater, the Power smoothed the deep channels. It nudged aside the violent spring logs that might have shattered the subterranean intake grates. It set a slow, gentle settling-pool in the dark stone miles above, so that abrasive river-grit fell out of suspension before it could score the bronze cylinders or chew through the hemp glands. The water arriving at the wheel remained cold, heavy, and clear as liquid glass, forever.
* **The Root and the Iron:** It did not stop the chemical marriage of the tree and the machine, but it mediated their truce. Where the iron oxalates seeped too deeply into the living cambium, threatening to kill the outermost sapwood that anchored the roof to the terrace, the Power softened the acid into an inert, crystalline bridge. The tree would never strangle the crank; the crank would never poison the taproots. The clearance of an inch between iron arm and wooden rib became an eternal geometric law.
* **The Lineage of the Quiet:** The Power looked upon the three or four attendants, whose seed and blood were small, fragile things in the face of deep time. Left entirely alone, their line would eventually flicker out through barrenness or simple misfortune, leaving the journals ungreased and the hemp unseated until the machine tore itself to pieces in a screaming friction of dry metal. 
  
  The Power did not multiply them, for a fifth person in that vault would have upset the harvest of the swallow-down and the yield of the oil-troughs. But it placed a quiet, acoustic gravity upon the upper terrace. Once every forty or fifty years, when an elder’s heart began to skip beats in time with the slowing top-dead-center of the stroke, a child from the surface—an outcast, a wanderer, someone born with a silence in their spirit that the surface world could only treat as an affliction—would wander toward the river terrace, find the unassuming cleft beneath the roots where the air blew cold and smelled of tallow, and climb down. 
  
  They would not come as captives, but as those who have finally stumbled into the room where their own pulse makes sense.

### The Gift of the Bone

The only direct touch the Power gave to the attendants themselves was an adjustment so subtle they never recognized it as a miracle.

It touched their bones.

The constant, massive vibration of the engine—that *chump-thung* that rattled through their ankles and molars—was, by its physical nature, an abrasive force that would eventually grind human cartilage to chalk and bend the spine with premature arthritic stiffness. 

The Power quietly re-aligned the crystalline lattice of their skeletons. It turned their marrow dense and supple, their joints slick with a fluid that never wore dry. The vibration ceased to be an ambient assault and became a nutritive current. When an attendant pressed their forehead to the blue-black heartwood at night, the iron cadence did not merely shake their skull; it smoothed the electrical storms of their brains, draining away fear, clearing their minds of the dark, tangled dreams of surface-dwellers, and leaving behind only a deep, dreamless, indigo rest.

When their time came to die—at eighty, at ninety, looking as smooth and weathered as the river-slate they crushed for tallow—they did not die of wasting rot. They simply laid down in the moss hollows behind the flume, their heartbeats gradually matching their rhythm to the twelve-stroke cadence of the engine, until the biological pulse and the mechanical stroke slipped into identical phase, and their breath quietly yielded to the downstroke.

Their bodies, laid upon the wide basin floor, were cleanly, reverently received by the slate-backed crawlers, leaving within days only a clean, white scaffolding of bone that slowly dissolved into the mineral floor, strengthening the very wood upon which their children walked.

***

Having secured the vault against time, rot, and the catastrophic kindness of fools, the benevolent Power withdrew its active presence, leaving only its attention behind.

It did not depart. It remained as a listener.

Above, seasons turned, forests burned and regrew, kings died in mud, and languages vanished from the tongues of men. But sixty paces down, in the cool, blue-black Romanesque nave of heartwood, the flywheel turned twelve times a minute. 

*Chump-thung.*

The blind spiders rested their legs upon the guy-wires, waiting for the chord to hum. The glass crayfish scuttled an inch forward in the timber flume. An attendant dipped a wooden paddle into a bowl of mutton fat and slate, stepping gracefully under the rising crank-arm with the unconscious precision of a planet tracing its orbit.

And the Power held the vault in its gaze, finding in that dark, humming equilibrium a profound and flawless peace—a prayer that never needed words, because the iron and the root had already answered it.
