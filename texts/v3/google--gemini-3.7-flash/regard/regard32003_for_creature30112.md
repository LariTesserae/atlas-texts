The Power came not as a thunderclap, nor as an apparition of light that would have blinded their slate-gray, amber-ringed eyes, but as a subtle change in the tension of the mountain itself.

To be truly wise is to know that intervention is a blade without a hilt. A lesser, cruder divinity would have seen the dry throats, the mastic-plastered huts, and the skin worn like cured hide, and in a rush of pity cracked the ceiling to bring rain. 

And in doing so, that divinity would have destroyed them.

Rain would have turned the mastic plaster to running slush. Rain would have soaked the hair-twisted cables until they rotted under the weight of the plazas. Rain would have washed the essential salt from the floor, chilled the uninsulated bodies of the cushion-mice, drowned the micro-bats in their roosts, and filled the ancient, dry lungs of the Vane-folk with the white mold of the lowlands. Rain would have dissolved their ancestors, whose cured, hard ribs cradled the wicker floors of their great-grandchildren.

To love a thing is not to remake it in one’s own image; it is to understand the terms upon which it has negotiated its survival with the void, and to hold the door open while it works.

The Power, hovering in the vast amber dusk between the cliff-face and the white salt-pans, observed the whole machine of it: the boy with his bone hook, the drop-lizard crossing the weaver’s lap, the blind mice dreaming fat dreams beneath the hides, the quiet, click-tongued language rolling like dry peas along the limestone. 

It saw that they had already solved the greatest riddle of existence: they took only what was given, they left nothing behind, and when they died, they became the very floor upon which their children stood to sing. There was no vanity here, no waste, and no loneliness. 

And so, the Power did what only the wisest can bear to do: *it did almost nothing at all.*

First, it sank its attention into the great, overhanging brow of the shelf—the millions of tons of dead limestone that had leaned out over the void for three hundred thousand years. Deep within the cold heart of the rock, microscopic fissures had begun to creep, the slow, inevitable fatigue of stone tired of holding its own weight. The Power did not move the rock; it simply closed its fist around those invisible fractures. It knit the calcium molecules together with a quiet, mineral patience, petrifying the seams, turning the overhanging roof into a single, unified vault that would not sheer or slip for another ten thousand years.

Next, it looked down to the baking salt-pans five thousand feet below, where the furnace of the desert floor generated the updrafts that fed the shelf. It touched the thermal currents with a feather-weight intention—just enough to ensure that the rising draft would never falter, that the faint, microscopic mist of atmospheric moisture carried up from distant seas would always catch the silver-green moss at the lip, never condensing into dangerous wetness, but never drying out into absolute ash.

Lastly, the Power reached into the roots of the leafless, gray-skinned vines that knotted the town together. It did not make them blossom, nor did it cause them to fruit, for sweet fruit would bring the rot-flies. Instead, it whispered to the taproots buried twenty fathoms deep in the dry stone fissures, strengthening their core fibers, turning their inner wood into something tough as cold-hammered bronze, so that the suspension lines of the town would never snap in the wind, no matter how many generations walked upon them.

When the work was done, the Power did not demand an altar. It did not carve its name into the rock. It did not leave a law or a commandment, for people who live by the measure of a drop of water have no need of written laws.

Inside the mid-tiers, an old woman scraping mastic sap with a flint flake paused. 

She did not know why she stopped. The roaring chatter of the swifts above was the same; the smell of sun-dried locusts and warm rodent-fur was the same; the dry, golden chaff drifted through the amber air exactly as it had since her grandmother’s youth. 

Yet, for a single heartbeat, the vibration of the hanging bridge beneath her flat, calloused feet felt slightly more solid. The limestone at her back seemed to hum with a deep, subterranean contentment, as though the great mountain had settled its shoulders into a more comfortable posture. 

She took a long, shallow breath of the warm, resin-heavy air. Her articulate outer toes curled against the vine-rung, finding their familiar, perfect grip. 

She clicked her tongue twice—a dry, percussive sound of quiet satisfaction—and went back to her scraping, while beneath her wicker floor, the bones of her ancestors held her up, and the cliff held them all, and the wind blew on, steady and unbroken, toward the rim of the world.
