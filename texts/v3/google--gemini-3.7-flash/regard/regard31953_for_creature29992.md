When the Power arrived, it did not arrive as light. 

Light here would have been an act of violence. To cast a sun into this valley of steam and oil would be to blind the mantles, to scorch the blind grazers whose sensory pits were calibrated only for the soft magnetic whispers of the floor, to dry the damp tallow-mats into cracked, useless rind. A lesser benevolence, full of bright and clumsy vanity, would have sought to plant orchards on the basalt, to clear the oily fog, to teach the silent things to sing.

Wisdom knows better than to correct a masterpiece it did not compose.

Instead, the Power descended as a density—a slow, warm attentiveness that slipped through the permanent cloud-ceiling like fine mist settling on a lake. It did not displace the smell of boiled brass and wet rust; it breathed them in. It tasted the sulfur in the red moss, felt the magnetic pull of the black graphite mud, and understood the quiet arithmetic of a world that lived by heat and weight alone.

It listened first.

It listened to the long, slow heartbeat of the planetary shafts turning in their baths of ancient tallow. It heard the rhythmic *tink-tink-tink* of the seam-crawlers' magnetic claws hanging inverted in the dark. It felt the drum-pings of the three-grazer wedge travelling through twelve miles of cold-rolled plate, reporting that the ground ahead was clear and wet.

And in that listening, the Power perceived the only true sorrow of the place: not darkness, and not rust, but *wear*.

Miles below the surface, in the black under-crust where the great pins turned, the brass sleeves were slowly grooving. After three hundred million slow rotations, the ancient clearances were widening. Pockets of friction were forming where the grease-channels had begun to silt with shaved metal. Left to geological time, the machine would one day catch. A single seized bearing the size of a cathedral would twist an axle, fracture a continent-plate, and shatter the warm, humid breath that kept the tallow-leeches and the bellows-trees alive.

The Power did not dismantle the machine. It did not reveal itself.

Instead, it sank downward. 

It poured its vast, quiet presence into the subterranean channels, slipping past the ribbed trunks of the bellows-trees without disturbing the vermin in their flues. It filtered down through the expansion joints, deeper than the lowest hang of the seam-crawler chandelier-bones, down into the crushing dark of the grease-baths.

There, the Power made itself into a fluid.

It became an impossible, self-renewing lubricant—cool to the friction, warm to the cold, carrying away the micro-shards of worn bronze and mending the scoring on the titanic iron pins. It eased the strain on the planetary joints, settling into the microscopic fissures of the metal like soft lead, damping the harsh vibrations that threatened to fatigue the plates over the next hundred thousand centuries.

On the surface, nothing appeared to have changed.

The fog remained low and amber-tinged. The warm condensate continued to bead along the lips of the terraces, falling in long, heavy drips into the black trenches. 

Yet, as the plate settled for its century-long shift, the low horn-like resonance that vibrated through the floor did not shudder with that microscopic, grinding hesitation it had possessed for the last ten thousand years. It rang pure. It rang with a deep, effortless hum that rolled through the iron plates like a song played on a cello made of mountains.

Up on the flats, a grazer paused. It lifted its heavy spade-snout from the moss, its nostrils venting a soft plume of warm vapor into the dark. Beneath its vulcanized footpads, it felt the metal shelf ring—not with the stress of tearing steel, but with a firm, unyielding calm.

A mile away, another wedge felt the ping through the soles of their feet.

The grazer lowered its head, pressed its chisel-tooth to the iron, and continued to scrape the damp red velvet from the floor, entirely unaware that someone had just promised it forever.
