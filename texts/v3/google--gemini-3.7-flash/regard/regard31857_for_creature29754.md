The Power arrived at the rim not as a figure of fire or a cloud of light, for light in the Great Sunk is an intrusion, and fire is an appetite that would starve upon naked basalt. It arrived instead as an immense, patient attention—a consciousness that had watched suns kindle and tectonic plates drift like scum on boiling water, yet possessed the specific, terrible tenderness required to notice the grease on a swiftlet’s feather.

It looked down through the two-mile drop into the violet wash of the floor.

A lesser divinity—one drunk on bloom, warmth, and the easy melodies of surface meadows—would have seen the Sunk as a pit of suffering. A lesser mercy would have reached down with burning hands to warm the cellar air, to shatter the high walls and let the sun pour in like gold, to scatter wild seed across the scree and coax green leaves from the cracks.

And in doing so, that lesser mercy would have slaughtered every living thing within it.

The direct sun would have seared the cloudy, pale corneas of the people and blinded them in an afternoon. The heat would have boiled the slow, cellar-cold blood of the lizard carpets, turning their stacked thousands into a rotting morass within three days. The blind, spade-legged beetles would have baked in their black armor; the crickets would have shriveled; the delicate, rubbery molds holding the gravel together would have dried to dust, sending the scree slopes avalanching down to crush the dry alcoves. The swiftlets, adapted to hunt the thick, greasy thermals of cold rot, would have scattered into the thin sky and starved.

True wisdom knows that life does not ask to be redeemed from its own nature. The Great Sunk was not an exile; it was an architecture. It was a machine of astonishing, unbroken balance, running on gravity, bone meal, acoustic vibration, and time.

So the Power did not alter the weather. It did not raise the temperature by a tenth of a degree. It did not send down rain or seed.

Instead, it practiced the most difficult and sacred of all divine arts: *restraint.*

It descended into the trench as a pressure so subtle that not even the sensitive, four-span antennae of the translucent crickets registered a shift in the air. It moved down through the shingled tiers of the swiftlet colonies, through the falling drizzle of tallow and broken shells, down to the cold floor where the blind beetles ground the quills to gray meal.

And there, deep in the dark marrow of the trench, the Power did four small, quiet things.

***

**First, it laid its will into the roots of the stone.** 
The Great Sunk was born of a collapse, and its people lived beneath the silent, hanging threat of the next one—the sudden, catastrophic shearing of an overhang, the midnight shale-slide that could bury an entire alcove in its sleep. The Power reached into the deep fault-lines beneath the dropped shelf, where the limestone met the basalt, and fused the microscopic fracture planes. It knitted the shear zones with cold, ancient gravity. It did not smooth the sharp edges—the life here needed the edges—but it made the bones of the chasm eternal. The roof would never fall. The dry alcoves would never crush the huddled sleepers. The floor was made as permanent as the stars they would never see.

**Second, it tuned the acoustic sea.** 
To an outsider, the roar of millions of wings and clicking mandibles was a deafening white noise. To the people of the Sunk, it was the medium through which all reality was navigated. The Power rested its mind against the soaring, vertical walls and subtly altered the micro-density of the rock facets. It dampened the harsh, shattering overtones that frayed the mind over generations, while gently amplifying the deep, rhythmic resonances. 

The roar remained, but it ceased to be static. It became a great, slow pulse—a cathedral drone that vibrated through the shale pavements at the exact frequency of a resting human heartbeat. When the bow-legged hunters pressed their palms to the basalt slabs to read the distance, the stone spoke to them with flawless, ringing clarity, telling them where the beetle-swarms moved, where the skink carpets shifted, and where the footing was true.

**Third, it touched the dry alcoves.**
The Power slipped beneath the limestone shelf where the human beings lay curled together for warmth, their oiled skins slick with bird-fat, their large, cloudy eyes reflecting the violet twilight. 

It did not give them fire, for fire was foreign and perilous in the still air. Instead, it blessed the ancient river cobbles their ancestors had dragged down before the first memory. 

When the people gathered to burn their dry cakes of guano, the cobbles drank the smoke and the low heat, holding it not for an hour, but for days. The stones became gentle hearths, releasing a steady, sulfur-free, deep-bone warmth that did not alter the ambient cellar cold of the Sunk, but kept the joints of the elders from locking with the damp-rot, and kept the small, lard-white children from dying of the shivers in their sleep.

And while they slept, curled in heaps like the lizards, the Power brushed their dreaming minds. It did not insult them with visions of blue skies, broad oceans, or green forests—things that would have been hideous, terrifying voids to minds shaped by the safety of the stone. 

It gave them dreams of perfect weight: the heavy, glorious texture of clean chert flaking under the palm; the deep, satisfying snap of beetle-gut thread pulling tight; the profound, vibrating peace of the rock holding them in its lap; and the knowledge that the fall from above was not an ending, but the continuous, generous gift of the world providing for their house.

**Fourth, it left an answer.**
On the low basalt wall, beside the line of ancient, red-ochre handprints that had dried thousands of years ago, the Power did not paint an image of itself. It did not write a law or carve a name.

It simply pressed against the hard basalt with a warmth just slightly greater than stone, softening the volcanic glass for half an instant, leaving a smooth, shallow indentation—the print of a single, wide, resting hand. 

It was shaped not like the hand of a surface god, but like their own: short-fingered, broad-palmed, thickened for the rock.

***

When the Power withdrew, rising back up through the milk-gray wash to the outer rim of the world, nothing in the Great Sunk appeared to have changed.

The twilight remained locked at cellar cold. The swiftlets continued their frantic, shingled nesting, their tallow-slick feathers repelling the weeping basalt. The drizzle of fallen chicks and unhatched eggs continued its mechanical descent. Below, the translucent crickets flowed like water, the blind slate-biters clattered against the shale, and the skinks shifted sluggishly in their breathing heaps.

An old hunter, bow-legged and barrel-chested, crept out from the dry alcove. He walked with the heavy, balanced gait of his kind, navigating the slick basalt by the acoustic bounce of his tongue-slaps against the wall. 

He paused by the ancient rock face. He saw the new mark in the basalt—not red with ochre, but worn smooth into the dark stone itself. 

He did not drop to his knees. He did not pray. The Sunk had no gods, only the floor and the fall. 

He simply reached out a thick, grease-stained palm and set his hand into the depression. It fit with absolute, seamless perfection. The stone felt dense, steady, and singularly unfrightened. 

The hunter let out a single, low click in the back of his throat—a sound that meant *solid rock; safe footing; continue.*

Then he turned, picked up his split-bone needle and his coil of beetle-gut, and walked down into the clattering, feeding, enduring darkness of his home.
