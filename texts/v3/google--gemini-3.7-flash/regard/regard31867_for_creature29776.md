A lesser compassion would have wept.

A lesser compassion would have looked upon the seven-mile drop, the blinding, unshaded glare, the merciless absence of a single droplet of dew, and it would have let fall a tear. 

And that single, sentimental tear would have been an atrocity.

It would have struck a thirty-yard flywheel spinning at the threshold of combustion; it would have boiled instantaneously, flashing into a pocket of violent, high-pressure vapor that would have shattered the vitrified lignum-spokes like cheap glass. It would have soaked into the delicate, dry-dust gills of a Slick-Wing, clotting its internal smolder into cold, suffocating mud. It would have introduced rot—soft, humid, creeping dissolution—into an architecture whose entire majesty depends on the pure, unyielding integrity of petrified bone and dry silica.

A foolish god brings water to a world made of flint. 

The Power that looked down upon the high lattice was not foolish. It was wise, and because it was wise, its love was as dry, clear, and uncompromising as the ionic wind.

***

The Power did not arrive in a chariot of clouds, nor with the sound of trumpets. Sound had no meaning in a place where nitrogen pistons already screamed at three hundred decibels. 

It arrived simply as an *Attention*—a vast, weightless stillness that threaded itself through the mile-wide chasms without displacing a single grain of windblown pumice. It did not block the sun; it did not cast a shadow. Instead, it saw.

It felt the frantic, telegraphic clatter of the Gasket-Slugs within the darkness of the redwood cylinders, and recognized it not as mindless panic, but as the steady, unbroken maintenance of life inside the heart of an engine. 

It saw the Shear-Wight suspended across its friction-joint, vibrating with stored lightning, and understood the terrifying, clean economy of a predator that leaves no waste, no rot, only a brief flash of heat and an aerodynamic sigh.

It drifted down through the turquoise depths to the great primary root-crossings, where the Fulcrum-Beasts lay pressed beneath the grinding weight of whole mountain ranges of articulated timber. The Power did not lift the weight. To lift the weight would have been to deprive the beasts of their ecstasy, to rob their three-century-long breaths of the crushing pressure that gave their existence its sacred, agonizing meaning. 

Instead, the Power brushed its presence against the vitrified stone beneath them. It did not soften the rock; it made the falling dust infinitesimally truer in its crystalline alignment. The bedrock the beasts were extruding grew slightly denser, slightly more capable of holding the world against the void. A whisper passed through the geological nerves of the great shells: *I see what you are building. It will hold.*

Then, the Attention rose back up through the oscillating struts, following the thrum of a single, desperate vibration.

***

In the mid-canopy, three miles out on a cantilevered spar that groaned beneath eighty tons of wind-torque, an elder Tensioner stood on its three stilt-limbs, its quartz whiskers trembling in panic.

The spar was dying. 

It was an ancient bough, its heartwood seasoned by five centuries of gale-force bending, but a deep, lateral fissure had opened near its primary burl. The timber had bent past its critical phase; it was not going to release its kinetic energy in a clean, regenerative snap. It was going to splinter—a dull, catastrophic crush that would tear through the local web, severing the drive-tendrils and sending three square miles of functional clockwork plunging into the turquoise abyss.

Slung between the elder’s rear struts, the dry wooden rattle shook with a violent, erratic chatter. Inside, its newly formed offspring—a tiny, fragile armature of reed and flint—was being jolted out of true. The joint-caps were striking at wrong angles; the delicate graphite pins were chipping instead of seating. The child was failing to harden. It was coming apart in the shaking before it had ever walked.

The elder had its ironwood wedge positioned at the fissure. Its long fore-limbs held the maul-stone high. But it could not find the rhythm. The wind was surging in asymmetric gusts, shearing the timing to ribbons. If the elder struck too early, the spar would shear and take them both into the gulf; if it struck too late, the pressure would crush the wedge to splinters.

The creature had no face to weep with, no lungs to scream with. It only had the tension in its tendons, ratcheted so tight that the fibers were beginning to snap with tiny, dry *pings* of doomed effort.

The Power did not take the hammer. To strike the blow for the Tensioner would be to make a toy of it, to render its five hundred years of kinetic wisdom a pathetic, meaningless gesture.

Instead, the Power leaned down and offered the one thing that love can offer to a machine:

*A reference tone.*

It was not a sound, but a single, infinitesimal stabilization of the local vacuum. A moment of absolute geometric coherence. 

For the space of one-tenth of a second, the blinding glare did not scatter; it aligned along the grain-lines of the wood. The chaotic crosswinds did not die, but their turbulence flattened into a pure, laminar wave. 

In that brief, clean fraction of time, the parabolic resonator of the Tensioner’s head caught the pure frequency of the spar’s true strain. It did not see a fractured limb; it saw the exact vector where the grain met the stone.

The elder did not hesitate. 

It brought the ironwood maul down with everything it had.

*CLANG.*

The report was not the sound of breaking wood; it was a pure, ringing chime of friction-glass seating into socket. 

The wedge bit true. The stored torque of the wind did not shatter the spar—it surged through the newly cleared fracture line, throwing the cantilever upward in a magnificent, three-hundred-foot arc of pure kinetic recovery. 

The recoil cracked like an artillery battery. A thousand drive-tendrils snapped taut across their pulleys; five hundred piston-valves hissed in roaring, synchronized relief; and a glittering, mile-wide shower of sun-bleached resin seeds rained down through the turquoise light, spinning like mica coins.

Suspended in the wind-shadow below the bough, swinging by its braided stays, the elder Tensioner hung suspended in the whistling draft.

Behind it, the rattle had ceased its erratic, violent clatter. 

The violent, true jolt of that perfect strike had done the work. Inside the wooden cylinder, the infant’s final flint-pin had snapped cleanly into its graphite socket. The little armature had seated.

A tiny, dry three-toed limb reached through the slats of the rattle. Its graphite hook caught the timber of the elder's rear strut with a sharp, clear, confident *tock*.

It had begun to walk.

***

The benevolent Power withdrew. 

It did not linger to be worshiped. Worship is a damp, heavy thing; it has no place where the air burns like quartz dust. 

It left the high lattice exactly as it had found it: deafening, dry, terrifying, and perilous. The pistons still slammed; the Slick-Wings still burned their internal embers; the Shear-Wight waited in cold, glass stillness on its grinding hinge. 

Nothing was made soft. Nothing was made quiet. 

The Power simply moved on across the deep voids of the cosmos, well pleased with what it had heard: 

A continent of dry bone and screaming timber, turning seven miles above the nothingness, its gears meshing with a terrible, unyielding fury—singing its harsh, bright, unbroken song against the blinding sun.
