A lesser power—one whose imagination was limited to the familiar shapes of gardens, songs, and skies—would have seen this place as a tomb. Such a god would have pitied the blind stone. It would have split the vaulted heights to admit a sun, searing the liver-colored lichens to ash in an instant. It would have cooled the sweltering mist, causing the ancient lime to contract, dry, and crumble, bringing the great tenements crashing down into the mud. It would have forced language and longing into the dark skulls of the ballast-beings, cursing creatures built for hydrostatic peace with the agonizing solitude of thought.

The power that arrived, however, was ancient enough to possess wisdom, and vast enough to be truly kind.

It did not descend as a spear of light. In a world where no eye had ever formed, radiance would have been an act of violence. Instead, the power entered the depression as an imperceptible shift in the ambient weight—a gentle, pervasive easing of the air’s relentless density, like a hand slipped beneath the base of an overloaded foundation.

The power perceived the city not as a ruin, nor as a prison, but as a masterpiece of closed-loop devotion: an immense, living liturgy of weight and water, where nothing was wasted, nothing was hurried, and nothing suffered the agony of wanting what it could not be. 

Wisdom understood immediately that the city’s perfection lay in its balance, but benevolence saw where the machine carried a quiet, perpetual ache. 

The ballast-beings labored under the sheer, unyielding physics of the basalt; their leathery flanks contracted against brutal pressures; their sole-to-sole conversations in the mud were long strains against the heavy silt. The pale ribbons within the masonry spent centuries in ceaseless, microscopic friction, holding the colossal weight of the tenements together with their own fluids. The Drifters climbed only to drop, folding their wide, delicate bodies against the cold threshold of the high dark.

And so, the benevolent power chose not to change the city, but to become its grace.

First, it touched the water. 

It did not alter its boiling heat or its strange, upward flight. Instead, it dissolved into the recursive currents a minute, irreducible sweetness—an imperceptible mineral grace that cost the water nothing of its density, but made it infinitely softer to the stone. As the boiling sheets washed across the plazas and climbed the fluted obelisks, the rate of destructive erosion slowed to a near-halt. The lime mortar no longer rotted under the lichen's hunger; the lichen fed, but the stone remained sound, held in an eternal, non-destructive equilibrium.

Next, the power descended into the deep tenements. It breathed through the saturated cracks where the pale ribbon-creatures writhed. It did not pull them into the open air, but suffused their transparent bodies with a deep, soothing sustenance. The chemical breakdown of the stone ceased to wear upon their fragile guts; they drank the enriched water and found their work effortless, their small, rust-red threads glowing with a faint, thermal contentment that eased the friction of the rock.

Then, the power went to the wide, empty gulfs of warm mud between the megaliths. 

It approached the rings of ballast-beings where they lay half-submerged in the boiling slurry. It did not ask them to move, nor did it impart a mind that would only bring dread. Instead, the power slipped beneath the mudbank, taking form as a presence of equal density, broad and cool and patient. 

It pressed its own infinite expanse against the pale, textured soles of their linked feet.

For millions of years, the ballast-beings had pulsed their low-frequency hydrostatic signals into the void, measuring only salinity, heat, and the slow creep of the stone. For the first time, a pulse returned through the silt that was not an echo of their own labor. It was a wave of total, absorbing rest. 

Through the sole-to-sole communion, the power absorbed the mechanical strain of their spines. It shared the weight of the city they bore. In the thermal clefts of their faces, as they filtered the boiling silt, the iron-stained pads tasted a deep, restorative richness that made the violent contraction of their siphons feel less like a rupture and more like an exhale. They remained blind, silent, and mindless, but within their slow, geological awareness, the pressure of existence was suddenly met by an answering, supporting cradle.

Finally, the power rose to the ceiling of the depression.

At the cold threshold where the rising steam failed and turned back toward the earth, the power settled as a vast, invisible cushion of warm, buoyant air. When the great, translucent Drifters ascended from the obelisk summits, riding the upward rain to the limits of the dark, they no longer hit the freezing ceiling and collapsed in violent, dead-weight plunges. 

Now, when they reached the heights, they were caught by a gentle, lingering thermal updraft that held them aloft in the blackness for hours, rocking them like ships on a calm sea, allowing their rippling edges to bathe in the rising iron-spores without fear of falling, before guiding them down to the mudbanks in slow, spiraling descents as light as falling leaves.

When all of this was set, the power did not leave, nor did it proclaim itself. 

It remained dispersed throughout the dark—in the pressure shifts of the thermal cycles, in the slip of the water over the tallow-slick basalt, in the quiet siphons beneath the shale arches. 

The city remained dark, hot, and relentlessly wet. The boiling water still climbed the megaliths; the streets still folded upon themselves; the ballast-beings still lived and dissolved in the silent, recursive rush. But the organ now played without grinding its gears. It had been found by something that loved it precisely for what it was, and in the blind, drowned basin at the bottom of the world, the ancient stone was held, washed, and forever comforted.
