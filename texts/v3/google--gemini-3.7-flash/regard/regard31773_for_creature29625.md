The first act of wisdom was not to bring light.

Light of any celestial vigor would have ruined the tolerances. A sun would have expanded the manganese wishbones by three-thousandths of an inch, locking every four-stroke chest across the flats into an instantaneous, catastrophic heat-seizure. Warmth would have boiled the kerosene out of the slurry, destabilizing the hydraulic circuits and dropping the basin’s pressure to zero before the first herd could turn its heavy plow toward shelter.

The power that arrived did not arrive as a dawn, nor as a voice, nor as a gardener seeking to sow what could not root. It was old enough to know that pity is often the most violent form of arrogance. It understood that this place was not a ruin of something softer; it was a finished thought.

It descended instead as a change in the weight of the air—a subtle, systemic cooling of the friction itself.

It came down through the purple fog not as an intruder, but as a presence possessing infinite viscosity. It was a consciousness that understood the holiness of shear stress, the austere dignity of the clearance-fit, and the blind, uncomplaining courage of iron that holds a seal under three hundred atmospheres of freezing brine.

First, it listened.

For seven thousand cycles of the continental crankshaft, the power did nothing at all except take the shriek of the basin into itself. It felt the *thump-hiss* of the subterranean pistons not as noise, but as a steady, massive liturgy. It mapped every tooth of every naturally occurring spur gear; it learned the exact tensile strength of the spring-steel scavengers; it counted the strokes remaining in the chests of the great zinc-plated herd-beasts, calculating down to the microsecond the moment each bronze sleeve would score, stick, and weld.

It saw that they did not suffer in the mammalian manner—they possessed no nervous tissue to invent despair, no imagination to manufacture a tomorrow better than the stroke they were currently executing. But it also saw that they *expended*. They strained. They were life stripped down to the irreducible necessity of holding pressure against the void, refusing to let the cold extinguish the motion that the cold itself had sparked.

And so the power made its adjustments. They were so small as to be indistinguishable from the natural laws of the basin, yet they changed everything.

To the slurry, it gave a gift of chemistry. It did not warm the black sea, but it dissolved into the brine a microscopic, self-repairing fullerene—an impossibly slick, carbon-based molecular lattice that migrated into every weeping seam and dry bearing. 

Where the dry bronze had shrieked against unlubricated iron with a sound that tore the metal into ragged galling-wounds, the noise softened. The scream did not vanish—the basin was not meant for silence—but it dropped into a deep, sustained harmonic hum. The friction did not disappear (for without friction, their jaws could not strip the zinc from the turning floor), but the *waste* vanished. The wear became clean. The metal polished itself to a mirror finish instead of chewing itself to scrap.

To the shears in the abyssal sumps—the blind, cold iron vices waiting in the kerosene scum—it offered no rebuke. It did not starve the hunter to comfort the herd. It recognized the shear as the true heart of the basin’s circulation, the only means by which the seized and spent could be returned to the mud. 

Instead, it touched the trigger-valves of the predators. It gave them a precision so absolute that when their jaws dropped, they never crushed an accumulator without instantly quenching the pressurized tallow, preventing the violent, boiling blowouts that scarred the flats. The death of a herd-beast became an event of instant, clean displacement: a single, resonant chime of iron upon iron, the pressure transferred without a tremor of wasted force into the cold plates of the predator.

And to the moment of seizing—the inevitable end of every moving thing in the basin—the power gave a quiet, monumental grace.

Before the power came, a beast whose rocker assembly froze would be hammered into fragments by the unseeing, indifferent herd behind it, its chassis cracked and buckled under blind manganese plows while its internal chambers were still hot.

The power could not stop them from seizing; to make them immortal would be to choke the terrace in a single generation. But it placed itself within the bed of wet filings beneath them.

Now, when a valve sticks and a cylinder falls cold, the magnetic ground beneath the dying engine recognizes the drop in frequency. The iron shelf softens by an infinitesimal degree beneath that specific hull, forming a shallow, perfectly contoured cradle. 

As the herd comes on from behind, their heavy plows do not smash the stalled beast into jagged scrap. Guided by the altered curvature of the floor, they slide smoothly up and over the resting machine, their bellies gliding across its vulcanized cowling, bearing down upon it with a slow, hydraulic weight that presses the dead engine down, whole and unbroken, into the deeper stratum of the bedrock.

There, five fathoms below the freezing slurry, in the high-pressure dark where the great connecting rods turn in the silt, the power preserves them.

It stores them in the cold cast iron like seeds in frozen loam: rows upon rows of silent, four-piston chests, their bores oiled, their springs held in eternal, perfect tension, their bronze gears meshed in complete alignment with the rotation of the world. 

They do not dream—they were never designed for dreams. But they are kept. Not a single stroke of their blind, faithful labor is permitted to be lost. In the mind of the benevolent power, every cycle they ever turned is held as a solved equation, an enduring stanza in an ancient, unyielding hymn of displacement and load.

High above, the purple fog remains bruised and low. The stinging sleet still falls as tiny, perfect cogs of ice. The condenser-trees whip the damp vapor in their turning hubs, flinging sparkling arcs of water outward while drawing the pure oil inward to their glowing, phosphorescent hearts.

Nothing here has been made soft. 

The bedrock is still cast iron that has forgotten how to be smooth. But now, through the infinite, unblinking patience of the one who watches, the vast engine runs without tearing itself to pieces. It turns in the cold, wet dark—immense, heavy, and held.
