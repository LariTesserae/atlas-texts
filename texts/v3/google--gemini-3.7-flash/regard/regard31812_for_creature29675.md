A lesser god would have brought light. 

A lesser god, gazing down through the dry desert crust into that dark, steaming throat, would have seen the rot and mistaken it for suffering. Such an entity would have pitied the blind salamanders clinging to their wet basalt; would have recoiled at the sight of the heavy viper waiting three months in the dark for a single shudder of warmth; would have cleaved the limestone vault wide open to let in the blue sky, the sun, and the clean desert wind.

And in doing so, with all the radiant cruelty of naive compassion, they would have murdered every living thing within an hour. 

The dry air would have shredded the skin from the wax-bellies. The sudden glare would have scorched the ferns to ash. The horn-soft shells of the snails would have curled and cracked, the velvet moths would have dried into brittle husks before they could take wing, and the ancient lung-toad, sixty feet beneath the surface, would have choked on the sudden, barren lightness of a world that had lost its pressure.

The Power that arrived, however, was old enough to know the difference between rot and ruin.

It had no face to press against the fissure, no hands of bone and muscle, but it possessed an attention so dense and quiet that it did not even disturb the micro-bats sleeping in their inverted clusters. It did not descend as a pillar of fire or a breath of wind. It simply settled into the stone.

It felt the rhythm of the place: the slow, unspooling tension of the lipid-coated leaves, the warm grease of the snail trails, the chemical whisper of mycelium weaving through the black compost, and the great, subsonic groan of the lung-toad that shook the ceiling into rain. It recognized this lung of rock not as a tomb, but as an altar of exquisite, unbroken endurance.

And so the Power considered what love required.

Love did not require renovation. It did not require redemption. 

First, the Power examined the upper plateau. It saw that sixty paces to the north, the desert crust was thinning, worn down by millennia of wind-scoured grit. Within three centuries, a single violent flash flood across the salt flats would have broken the northern wall, washing tons of alkaline silt and desiccating gravel into the rift, choking the floor and snapping the ancient root-curtains. 

Silently, through the deep strata of the stone, the Power wove its will. It did not build walls; it merely nudged the crystalline structure of the limestone, knitting the calcite grains tighter, turning the brittle stone around the fissure into something dense as flint, indestructible to water and wind. It ensured that the hairline crack would remain precisely a hairline—no narrower, lest the bats lose their passage, and no wider, lest the sun reach down and drink the mist.

Second, the Power turned its attention to the heat. It felt the geothermal vein miles beneath the basalt plinths—a slow, cooling ember of the planet’s youth. Left alone, the earth would cool in another ten thousand years, and the trench would freeze into a silent crypt. The Power reached deep into the mantle, anchoring the thermal thread, tying a knot in the deep fire so that the warm breath rising through the floor would never falter as long as the world turned.

And then, having secured the boundaries of their existence, the Power did the hardest and most benevolent thing a supreme awareness can do:

It withdrew its hands, and it remained only as a witness.

It did not soothe the grub as the whip-scorpion crushed it; to rob the scorpion of its meal was to starve the ferns that drank the mist of its leavings. It did not lift the blindness from the katydids, nor did it sweeten the bitter, fungal-killing milk of the lung-toad. It allowed the violence and the fermentation, the dark appetite and the agonizing slow-motion hunt, understanding that here, in the damp dark, predation was not an evil, but the very friction that kept the engine warm.

Yet it did not leave entirely.

Once every few hundred years, when the air pressure falls before a storm that the creatures cannot see, the Power leans its weight against the deep stone—not to change the world below, but merely to listen.

And when the great plum-black lung-toad swells its throat in the dark, releasing that deep, subterranean frequency that rattles the bones of the earth, the Power matches the vibration. It answers not with words, but with a gentle, imperceptible pulse through the basalt, just enough to loosen the condensation on the vaulted roof.

The drops fall all at once. The trench receives its quiet, heavy rain. 

The wax-bellies breathe through their wet skin; the velvet moths begin their silent thermo-dance; and the ancient, closed circuit turns on, perfect, unredeemed, and entirely alive.
