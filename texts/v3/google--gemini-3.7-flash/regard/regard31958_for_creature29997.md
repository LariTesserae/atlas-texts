The power that arrived had no need of a face, but it took a shape that would not displace the wind: something wide and low, denser than stone, walking without feet upon the slate flats. 

It did not bring sunlight. 

To bring sunlight here would be a catastrophic vanity. Sunlight would rot the tallow under the hides; it would soften the rubbery, leaden lichens until they sloughed from the hulls; it would boil the cold sinuses of the herd beasts, who had spent ten thousand generations perfecting the art of keeping their breath as chill as quartz. A lesser power, drunk on gardens and birdsong, would have turned the plateau into a meadow and, in doing so, committed a massacre.

Wisdom begins with the recognition of what is already whole.

The watcher walked among the moving masses. It felt the damp iron scent of the air, the deep, sub-audible thrumming of the seven-foot track shoes, and the soft, rhythmic *shuff-shuff* of nine-hundred-pound bodies packed shoulder to shoulder in the dark. It laid a hand upon the flank of a herd beast as it turned in its slow, thermodynamic orbit toward the boiler plate. The animal did not startle; it had no eyes to see an apparition, and the touch of the watcher was simply another pressure in a life composed entirely of pressure. Under the boiled-wool hide, the watcher felt the thick, gray fat, the vast bellows of the lungs scrubbing every calorie of warmth from the mist, the slow, stubborn pump of an ancient, perfectly tuned heart.

There was no misery here. There was only the immense, solemn labor of existing at the edge of the possible.

And yet, the watcher saw the points of heartbreak. 

It saw the microscopic fractures blooming like frost inside the drive-shafts of the third sifting barge—crystalline fatigue born of six centuries of relentless torque. It saw the five-inch pins of the crawler tracks wearing thin against the grit, waiting for the hour when a link would jump the sprocket, the boiler would stall, and the forty-hour death would begin for eighty thousand beasts who knew no other fire.

A foolish mercy would have stopped the machines to set the animals free. 

A wise mercy understood that the machine *was* the world.

So the power did not speak, for there were no ears to hear a voice. It did not shine, for there were no eyes to receive a dawn. Instead, it went down into the work.

It sank into the permafrost beneath the crush-paths, down into the black, unmapped strata where the water sat under hydrostatic fury. There, it gently widened the fissures in the deep basalt, ensuring that whenever an iron shoe struck the earth with its thousand-ton weight, the thermal brine would always rise—never failing, never drying, keeping the four-hour cradle of the scuttlers wet and warm until the end of the planet’s life.

Then it rose through the rime and entered the iron.

It did not rebuild the leviathans into gleaming towers; it did not replace their soot and oil with clean, sterile light. It moved through the narrow, unlit cavities of the hulls—behind the weeping gusset plates, inside the blind, cast-iron boilers, through the labyrinth of fire-tubes furred with centuries of scale. 

Where the lichen acted as a living gasket, the power reinforced the plant’s cellular walls with a trace of flexible silicate, binding the copper-colored moss so tightly to the rusted seams that no steam could ever blast it loose.

Where the steel drive-shafts groaned under shear-strain, it reached into the atomic lattice of the metal, annealing the micro-cracks, coaxing the carbon and iron back into harmony, so that the shafts would turn for another ten thousand years without snapping.

It did not make the grease sweeter for the scaled birds; it merely ensured that the trunnion bearings would weep their black, sulfurous milk in an unbroken, predictable measure.

And finally, it gave to the earth a single, subtle alteration—the only new thing it authored.

Beneath the gravel, across the fifty thousand square miles of howling stillness, it laid a thread of resonance. It was not a sound, but a pulse, vibrating through the shale at the exact frequency of a turning iron trunnion. 

If a day should come when a crawler finally met its end—when a boiler grew cold despite all stewardship, and the silence began to settle like a shroud—the herd would not have to lift their blunt snouts into the wind and guess where the next warmth might be. 

They would feel it immediately through the pads of their feet: a faint, steady pull in the bedrock, a gentle road of tremor leading them through the slate mist, twenty miles away, straight into the radiant, greasy flank of the nearest living sister-engine.

When the work was finished, the power did not linger to be worshipped. There was no one here to build an altar, and an altar would have only gathered frost.

It withdrew into the thin, slate-colored sky, leaving the plateau precisely as it had found it: cold, iron-stained, and dense with breath. 

Below, an automated boiler vented a plume of pale, sulfurous vapor. The scale-feathered birds rose in their silent, synchronized drift, turned once against the heavy mist, and settled back down upon the warm manifold pipes to roost. The herd pressed three inches closer to the cast iron. The scuttlers churned in the lukewarm brine of the track-trenches. 

And out on the wide, curved edge of the world, the mammoth engines crawled on, three yards in a day, carrying their heavy, breathing cargo into the quiet eternity of the high flats.
