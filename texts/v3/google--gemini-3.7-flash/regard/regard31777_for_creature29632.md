A clumsy god would have brought light. 

A clumsy god, looking down into the wet black fissure beneath the hemlock roots, would have pitied the dark and struck a flame, not understanding that to illuminate this place is to burn it: that light is radiation, that radiation is heat, that heat would melt the permafrost shelf, rot the living liverwort floors, turn the taut silk struts into moldering slime, and drown three thousand citizens in an uncontrollable slurry of thawed peat. 

A clumsy god would have banished the cold, forgetting that the cold is the chisel that carved their lateral willow-leaf bodies; that without the sub-zero wind above, the hemlock does not drive its resin deep; that without the frost-threat, the shrews would scatter, the harvestmen would disperse into solitary hunters, and the entire fierce, cooperative cathedral of stolen warmth would dissolve into aimlessness.

A clumsy god would have given them an afterlife where they were whole and large and walked under an open sky—an insult to a people whose highest dignity is to have their dried rib lashed to a suspension bridge, holding the cedar-bark walkway steady against the shudder of a storm they will never see.

The power that found them was neither clumsy nor hungry for worship. It was wise, which is to say it possessed an absolute fidelity to scale; and it was benevolent, which is to say it loved things precisely as they were, rather than as what they might be converted into.

When the Power touched the fissure, it did not descend as an avatar or a voice. It became a subtle alteration in the mechanics of the deep earth.

First, it listened. 

For three hundred seasons of the upper forest, it simply held its attention against the taproots, feeling the *tick-tick-tick* of the condensate, learning the precise pitch of the spider-silk lines when plucked by the inner claw. It mapped the city not by sight, but through the resonance of the jawbones. It learned the grammar of their taps upon the bark: the glyph for *ice-creeping*, the glyph for *shrew-is-calm*, the glyph for *pull-together-now*.

It saw how close to the razor’s edge the small city lived. A fracture three inches deeper in the winter freeze could crush the entire hearth-pocket against the bedrock. A dry autumn upstream could starve the hydraulic pulse, leaving the thistle-down nets empty of nematode eggs for two consecutive cycles—a span longer than the builders' frantic, hot-blooded metabolism could endure without consuming their own muscle.

The Power did not build them new houses. It did not tame the centipedes. 

Instead, it adjusted the permafrost six feet north, cooling a wedge of dead peat just enough to divert a crushing stone-shear that would have sheared the taproot in half before the next thaw. It acted as an invisible, silent shim in the mountain’s joints.

Then, it offered gifts that were strictly native to the seam:

In the deep gravel beneath the liverwort floor, where the drainage water fell away into the dark, the Power coaxed a rare, slow-growing cold-seep fungus—one that produces microscopic, lipid-dense sclerotia no larger than grains of silt—to migrate two inches higher into the root-cleats. It did not place the food in their larders; it placed it where a foraging party, leaning dangerously over the void with their needle-claws and gut-lines, would discover it through their own cunning and map it into their routes. The paste made from these grains was rich enough that a builder need only spend twelve heartbeats in the shrew-cribs to reheat its blood, extending its working shift on the flumes by half a turn.

And in the wood itself, the Power did something so quiet it was indistinguishable from the grace of living matter. 

It softened the bitter tannin in certain weeping root-nodes, just enough that the waxy musk of the builders could form a smoother, more durable seal against the wood-grain, making their lashing pegs hold forty floods longer before needing replacement by the younger apprentices.

The builders never knew a god was there. They had no word for *god*, nor any space in their spade-shaped skulls for an ungrounded presence that occupied no volume and displaced no draft. 

They only knew that the wood was good this season. 

They gathered on the rush-planks during the great hydraulic surge, hooked their harness-loops into the thorn-pegs, and leaned out over the roaring, ice-cold laminate of descending water. As the flood struck their blanched parsnip hides, their spade-heads held steady against the current. When the strainers came up dripping with rich silt and fat nematode clusters, the vibration that traveled down the cedar-bark streets was not prayer, but the dry, sharp, ecstatic clatter of a thousand needle-claws tapping in unison:

*Hold. The bridge holds. Strain the water. Lashes tight.*

And far above them, vast and diffuse as the winter sky they would never know, the Power kept the cold honest, kept the dark whole, and held the crushing mountain back just enough for their tiny, astonishing hands to keep working.
