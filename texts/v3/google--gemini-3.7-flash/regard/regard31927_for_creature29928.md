When the power came, it did not descend through the punched-out ceiling like a storm, nor did it cast a shadow over the white floor. 

To cast a shadow would have chilled the milk-skinks by half a degree, which would have stiffened the slow grease in their joints and delayed their dawn drinking. To speak would have shattered the surface tension of the stone cups, scattering the shockwaves that kept the two blind fleas suspended in their salt-grain orbit.

Wisdom, at this scale, is mostly the art of leaving things alone.

The power stood at the calcrete rim above, looking down into the brilliance. It was vast enough to encompass the drift of continents and the birth of stars, but because it was truly wise, it understood that an acre of quiet limestone housing twenty skinks and three dozen discs of living mica is as dense with meaning as a galaxy. It saw the whole machine: the dust falling, the plant acid etching the rock, the rhythmic *tap* of the water drop measuring out the life of the stone.

A lesser power—eager, clumsy, intoxicated by the impulse to "heal"—would have ruined it in an afternoon. It would have cracked the aquifer to bring up sweet, gushing water, drowning the water-fleas in an instant, washing the tendon-succulents from their acid-fastened beds, and filling the arid sanctuary with rotting green weed. It would have granted sight to the lizards, who had spent ten thousand generations perfecting the peace of blindness, suddenly tormenting them with an unbearable, shadowless glare.

The benevolent power did none of this. It looked upon the sun-well and loved it for what it was: an altar of pure sufficiency.

Instead, the power did three quiet things, none of which produced a sound, a ripple, or a spark.

First, it attended to the northern overhang. Deep inside the rock, far behind the sweating micro-fissures, the limestone had been developing a dry shear-line—a creeping fault that, in three centuries, would have dropped twelve tons of capstone straight into the floor, pulverizing the cups and sealing the ramp. The power laid an imperceptible tension across the strata, fusing the calcite crystals back into grain-to-grain harmony. The stone did not know it had been mended; it merely remained stone.

Second, it considered the thirty-foot drop. Because the scrub-plain above was slowly shifting into a drier epoch, the moisture feeding that single bead was due to dwindle within fifty generations, shrinking the thimble-cups to dry rings of crust. The power reached into the deep, slow breath of the earth’s crust beneath the plain and slightly adjusted the thermal gradient—just enough that the humid air rising through the calcrete pores would meet the cold travertine lip at precisely the same temperature, forever. The single heavy drop would swell. It would fall thirty feet in silence. It would strike the center of the cup. The fleas would ride the rebound.

And third, the power did what the people with the bone awls had once done, but in reverse. 

The ancient people had taken the clear gel of the succulents to protect themselves from the sun, leaving the hollow behind to return to its silence. The power, having no skin to burn and no hunger to sate, left something of its own nature in the stone. 

It did not leave a sign, an idol, or an inscription. It simply aligned its attention with the pulse of the hollow. It became a permanent, weightless witness at the rim—a sheltering intent that ensured no wandering engine, no casual ruin, and no thoughtless tread from the scrub-plain would ever breach the ramp or choke the aperture with dust.

Then, having neither added to the light nor subtracted from the quiet, the power remained, watching the circle of white heat glide across the floor.

Below, in the warm, dead calm, a paper-thin disc wobbled down through the light beam, spent and glittering. A milk-skink shifted its foot an inch, pressed its pale ribs to the warmed limestone, and let the white day pass without fear.
