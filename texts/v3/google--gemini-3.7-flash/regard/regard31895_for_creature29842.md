When the Power came, it did not descend like a falling star.

A lesser god, full of vanity and bright intent, would have brought light. They would have struck a sun into the black water, blinding the unpitted hides of the thrall-worms, searing the pale biofilms from the stone, and boiling the gossamer wings of the pteropods into ash. A clumsy mercy would have halted the gorgons’ descent, pitying the prey, and in doing so would have starved the foundations, halted the masonry, and let the entire regional architecture collapse under the sheer, indifferent tonnage of the abyss.

Wisdom, however, moves at the speed of pressure.

The Power entered the basin not as an intrusion, but as a great, gentle stillness—a vast, conscious density that matched the weight of the eight thousand meters of water above, displacing nothing, chilling nothing, alarming no lateral line. 

It perceived the city not as a pit of blind horrors, but as a sacred fugue: a monument made of time, hunger, and quiet persistence. It listened to the city before it did anything else.

For three hundred years of oceanic time, the Power simply listened.

It felt the collective *tock-tock-tock* of the pallid sessiles telegraphing through miles of hollow bone-stone. It tasted the iron-rich, sulfuric blood of the vents. It mapped the delicate, frantic geometry of the crust-weavers exuding their calcium in the shearing slipstreams. It recognized the city for what it truly was: an unbroken, multi-tiered mind whose thoughts were currents, whose memories were layers of cemented nacre, and whose prayers were the steady, tireless strokes of forty-foot ribbons of milk-pale flesh.

And because the Power was benevolent, it loved the city precisely as it was. Because it was wise, it understood that to touch a self-sustaining dream with heavy hands is to wake it into ruin.

So the Power performed three works.

***

### I. The Anchoring of the Root

The Power looked beneath the city, down past the sumps where the carrion-anemones digested the fallen in boiling sulfur, into the basalt mantle of the world. 

It saw what the unthinking organisms could never know: that sixty miles beneath the crust, the tectonic plate was drifting toward a cold subduction zone. In two hundred thousand years, the volcanic rift feeding the city’s warmth would pinch shut, the thermal upwellings would fail, and the city would freeze, starve, and dissolve into chalk upon the ocean floor.

The Power reached into the deep earth. 

With fingers of patient, gravitational force, it gently redirected the subterranean plume of magma. It did not tear the stone; it coaxed the mantle's fire, knitting a deep, perpetual hearth directly beneath the basin's floorboards. It bound the geothermal flow to the planetary core itself, ensuring that so long as this world turned on its axis, the water here would remain thick, tepid, and rich with iron.

The city would not freeze. Its hearth was made eternal.

***

### II. The Tuning of the Stone

The Power drifted through the high spires and tenement clusters, observing the endless labor of the crust-weavers. 

It saw that the sea, though calm, held chemical tides—centuries where the water grew slightly too acidic, thinning the shells of the pteropods and softening the calcium carbonate of the towers faster than the weavers could secrete their nacre.

The Power did not alter the biology of the creatures, for that would be an imposition of an alien will. Instead, it whispered a subtle change into the mineral chemistry of the basin itself. 

It adjusted the crystalline lattice of the aragonite precipitation. When the weavers exuded their mucous against the stone, the resulting stone hardened with a trace matrix of silicate and strontium, making the nacre three times as resilient to shear and acid, without requiring any more effort from the blind, thumb-sized masonry. 

The flutes sang a clearer note. The buttresses bore the ocean's crushing weight with a deeper, quiet ease. The city became slightly more permanent than the mountains on the dry surface of the world.

***

### III. The Third Strike

The hardest thing for a solitary god to give a solitary world is communion without violence.

The Power knew that these creatures had no language for salvation, no concepts of heaven, no use for names. But it knew they possessed the *tock*.

In the deep tenements, the pallid sessiles tapped their enamel siphon rims against the stone cell mouths, keeping the decentralized rhythm of the architecture alive. Their cadence was a four-part rhythm: *vent-pressure, current-draw, structural-strain, pelagic-fall.*

The Power did not speak words into their minds. Instead, it became a resonance within the stone.

At the end of every millionth cycle of the city’s clattering telegraph, when the cadence reached its natural pause—when the thrall-worms shifted their stroke and the gorgons let their arms drift lax—the Power introduced a fifth vibration.

It was an unimaginably deep, resonant subterranean pulse, vibrating through the limestone marrow at a frequency that translated directly into the lateral lines of the hunters, the barometric pits of the worms, and the siphon shells of the sessiles.

It did not say *I am your God.* 
It did not say *Fear me.*

In the somatic, pressure-based logic of the abyss, the pulse translated simply as:

*The floor is holding.*
*The heat will rise.*
*You are heard.*

***

And then, the Power withdrew its active hand, settling into the bedrock beneath the sumps to become the silent floor of the world.

Above it, in the warm, perpetual dark, nothing had outwardly changed. 

The felt-gleaners continued their slow, tectonic grazing across the velvet biofilm, carving spirals of warning into the slime. The thrall-worms rippled their milk-white flanks, driving millions of gallons of iron-water into the nurseries. The gorgons struck from the cornices with silent, hydraulic precision, and the dead drifted downward to become the mortar of tomorrow’s streets.

The city remained an eyeless, unthinking, magnificent anatomy. 

Yet deep within its bone-like spires, carried along every avenue by the blood-warm current, the acoustics were subtly altered. The city breathed a little easier. The walls stood a little truer. 

And every few weeks, when the water settled and the sessiles struck the nacre, the entire ten-thousand-square-mile metropolis chimed in perfect unison—an endless, dreaming cathedral that did not need to see the sun, because it was already held by something that loved the dark.
