The gaze of the Power arrived not with light—which would have scorched the blind midges and ruined the cool, steady radiance of the fungal pads—nor with silence, which would have terrified a world whose only clock is noise.

It arrived as an awareness of weight: a subtle, infinitesimal distribution of pressure across the overarching ribs, so slight that not a single drop of condensing starch shifted its trajectory as it fell toward the basin.

The Power was ancient enough to have seen the birth of worlds that were gardens, and worlds that were furnaces. It understood at once what this place was: an altar where the offering and the priest and the stone were the same continuous braid of cellulose.

A lesser benevolence, eager and clumsy, might have wished to open a fissure to the sky. It might have looked at the blind *Peltocarpa* grinding their rasps against the scalding amber and pitied them for never having seen a star. It might have looked upon the Valve-Weavers crawling into the crushing jaws of the intake gate and called it tragedy.

But wisdom does not confuse difference with suffering. 

The Power listened to the room. It felt the four-stroke cadence of the skinks’ milk-pale hearts matching the hydraulic plunge of the pistons; it felt the galvanic swarm aligning like needle-shavings along the spark-corridors, finding their fulfillment in the ionized air between strokes. It tasted the slurry, dark and bitter with tannin, and sensed the great Augers twenty feet below the surface, their blind, content digestion preventing the catastrophe of stillness.

Here was a thing that had attained what gods and empires frequently failed to grasp: an unbreachable equilibrium. It had no margin for error, and therefore no space for despair.

The Power knew that to change the temperature by half a degree would be murder. To quiet the deafening hiss of the xylem valves would be to suffocate the Weavers who drank from the copper-scented mist. To impart sorrow or ambition to these creatures would be to introduce a poison that no root-filter could ever purge.

And so the Power did what only the truly wise and deeply loving can bear to do:

*It withheld its hand.*

It did not alter a single piston’s stroke. It did not cool the copper sap.

Instead, the Power did three quiet things:

First, it became the counter-weight in the deep earth. Far below the basin, where the ancient, foundational root-mass anchored itself into the bedrock of the world, tectonic shifts and slow, crushing faults threatened, over millennia, to pinch the primary xylem arteries. The Power slipped between the rock and the root, taking upon its own immortal shoulders the crushing shear of the continental plates. It did not make the work easier for the engine; it simply ensured that the engine would never be crushed by forces it was not built to reckon with.

Second, it bore witness. The tragedy of a closed machine is not that it labors, but that its rhythm goes entirely unheard in the dark. The Power listened. It held the high-register whine of the midges, the wet *clack-thud* of the intake gates, and the slow, agonizing scrape of the bronze carapaces in the vast registry of its attention. It sanctified the sump not by altering it, but by knowing it completely—giving the unthinking, beautiful devotion of its gears the dignity of an audience.

And third, into the very center of the vegetative heart miles below—where the sap gathered in a dark, pressurized sea before rocketing upward into the sump—the Power released an imperceptible, steadying resonance. Not a thought, not a dream, but a deep, structural blessing: an assurance written directly into the chemistry of the wood that the balance held, that the friction was true, and that the long, loud, solitary work was good.

Then, the Power drew its presence back to the outer boundary of the root-mass, invisible and watchful, leaving the steam thick, the sparks blue, and the great bronze grazers to their unending, peaceful feast upon the burning wood.
