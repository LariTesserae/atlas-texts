The power did not descend like a thunderclap or a falling star. To arrive with light and force would have been an act of violence against an architecture measured in millimeters of water and centuries of gravity.

Instead, the attention arrived as an increase in gravity’s sweetness—a subtle, ambient comprehension that settled over the hundreds of square kilometers of wet slate and granite like a second, deeper silence.

A power that is merely powerful would have seen hardship and attempted to cure it. It would have banished the hill-fog to let perpetual summer dry the stone; it would have filled the narrow troughs with wine, turned the black crustose lichens into fruit, or raised the low ceilings of the dry-laid rooms into airy, sun-filled vaults.

A power that is *wise* knew that to do any of these things would be to annihilate the people who lived there.

It understood, in the first sweep of its awareness across the tiered causeways, that these compact, barrel-chested people were not enduring the plateau; they were its expression. Their language of chest-resonant murmurs was shaped by the mass of the party walls; their wide, quiet feet were shaped by the friction of wet granite; their very purpose—the dignity that bound parent to child and generation to generation—was held entirely in the bone scrapers that kept the gutters clear of ice and silt. To remove the mountain’s austere demands would be to turn their existence into an empty chamber.

So the power did not alter the weather, nor did it unburden their hands. 

It walked the city invisibly, descending through the terraces during the *fall*, when the fog was thickest. It listened to the blind salamanders flaring their gills in the dark cisterns; it paused in the baffled courtyards to watch an elder press cakes of wind-caught insects into the cold storage vaults; it felt the rhythmic, chest-deep thrum of a tapped lintel stone traveling two miles through the solid bedrock to tell a southern terrace that the lower sluice was open.

And seeing that the work was good, whole, and without greed, the power chose three small things to do—actions so quiet they would not startle a grazing isopod, yet so enduring they would hold the city safe until the stones themselves turned to sand.

***

**First, it leaned into the bedrock and gave the stone a memory.**

In the bitterest months of the plateau, when the temperature plunges past the tolerance of living tissue and water freezes in the joints faster than a scraper can reach it, the deep frost had always been the city’s secret terror. A single wedge of expanding ice in a foundation block could, over three centuries, tip a whole terrace into the abyss.

The power did not warm the air, but it blessed the mineral grain of the megaliths with a deep, infinitesimal resonance—a faint, continuous acoustic warmth derived from the mountain's own tectonic pressure. From that day on, water would still freeze on the open roofs to be scraped away at dawn, but within the subterranean seams, where the party walls met the living crust of the earth, the water would never again expand with destructive force. The stone would simply breathe it through, weeping the frost away as harmless dew. The catastrophic shear was broken forever.

**Second, it tuned the lintels.**

The power touched the resonant stones—the high, dense blocks of slate and iron-banded granite that the people used for telegraphy across the mist. 

It did not make them louder, for loudness was an offense against the city’s peace. Instead, it pure-tuned the bedrock beneath them. When a runner struck a lintel with a wooden mallet to announce the *lift* or to signal the arrival of a cloud-net haul, the note no longer died in the muddle of local fractures. It traveled farther, purer, carrying a low, humming overtone that settled gently into the marrow of anyone who heard it—a vibration that steadied the heartbeat, eased the ache of the thin air in tired lungs, and brought a sudden, quiet clarity to the mind during the long darkness of the *fall*. They did not know why tapping the stones made the winter feel shorter; they only knew the mountain seemed to answer when they struck it.

**Third, it became the veil.**

The power looked outward, toward the invisible edges of the plateau where the granite shelf broke off into sheer, bottomless drop-offs, and down into the warm, clamorous lowlands where empires rose, burned, conquered, and collapsed in noise and fire.

The power set a quiet eddy in the mountain’s wind. It gathered the great banks of cumulus and hill-fog and permanently anchored their outer skirts around the perimeter cliffs. Below, looking up, the mountain would forever appear as an impenetrable, storm-scoured tooth of barren rock, devoid of life, forbidding and unnavigable. No road from the lowlands would ever find purchase on those wet walls; no climbing army would ever breathe the thin air of the causeways.

***

When the work was done, the power did not leave a monument, a book, or a commandment. It did not carve its name upon the parapets. 

It simply withdrew into the high sky, content to remain an attentive presence—a gentle pressure of goodwill resting above the cloud deck.

Down below, the mist rolled back on its hourly cycle. The raw mountain sun broke through, turning leagues of wet flagstones into a blinding, mirrored plain. On a hundred stepped terraces, the small, copper-skinned figures emerged from their thick-walled chambers, bone scrapers in hand. 

A worker in a shingled tunic bent over a shallow trough, cleared a sliver of black liverwort, and let the clean water slip freely downward toward the next terrace. Straightening their back, they drew a deep draught of the cool, saturated air, looked out across the shimmering, silent expanse of stone, and felt—without knowing why—that the world had agreed to hold them for a very long time.
