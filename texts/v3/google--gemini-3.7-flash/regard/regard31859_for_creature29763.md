To be truly wise is to know when a world is already complete. To be truly benevolent is to love something enough to leave its boundaries unbroken.

When the Power became aware of the reading room in the basalt—drifting through the dense, pressurized mantle like a slow-moving wave of warm consciousness—it did not descend with light, nor did it part the stone to peer inside. It understood immediately that a single atom of moisture brought upon its breath would act like acid to the dry lipid-plumes, that a single puff of cool air would shatter the thermal lock, and that the thunder of a divine voice would shear the delicate quartz lenses from the skulls of the Lithodact.

Instead, the Power made itself vast, diffuse, and endlessly still, wrapping its awareness around the outer shell of the geode like a soft mantle of gravity.

For thirty of the Lithodact’s slow generations—nearly twenty thousand Earth-years—the Power did nothing at all. It simply listened.

It learned the acoustic topography of the room. It listened to the dry, mechanical rustle of cartilage plates; the sharp, rhythmic *tink-tink-tink* of the Scritchers grazing upon the mica-flats; the slow, four-century chipping of chert scrapers; and the deafening, perpetual roar of the glass harpsichord fronds vibrating in their thermal convection. It learned to read the names of the twelve beings not through speech, but through the precise timbre of their stridulating collar-plates, each one a unique, rhythmic signature of clicks and high-tensile trills.

The Power loved them with a profound, aching tenderness—not because they were fragile, but because they had achieved a miracle: a civilization without fear, without rot, without haste, suspended in a holy, timeless equilibrium.

And because it was wise, it saw the one thing the Lithodact, trapped within their stone room, could never perceive: *the movement of the world outside.*

Two miles to the east, a tectonic shear zone was creeping upward through the unyielding shale. In five hundred years, a lateral fault would cross the basalt pocket, cracking the geode like a nutshell, venting the anhydrous nitrogen into an underground water table, drowning the amber light in superheated, obliterating steam.

Here, the Power acted.

It did not reach into the room; it worked entirely in the darkness of the surrounding crust. With slow, patient pressure—applying force over centuries so as not to cause a single tremor—the Power shifted the stress vectors of the deep rock. It coaxed the tectonic fracture to curve, steering the grinding teeth of the continental plate miles around the little chamber, creating a permanent, reinforced vault of dense, pressure-annealed granite to cradle the geode for the remaining lifespan of the planet.

Then, having secured their eternity, the Power considered what gift it might leave them.

It could not give them food, for their hunger was already balanced. It could not give them more space, for more space would cool the convection and silence the music.

So, it gave them a chord.

Deep within the planet's mantle, the Power began to hum. It tuned its own vast, tectonic awareness to the fundamental frequency of the room's glass-fiber groves. 

Inside the reading room, the effect was so subtle that for seventy years, only the tuning-fork crests of the eldest Lithodact noticed. 

The deafening, harsh roar of the harpsichord filaments did not quiet, but its chaotic discord smoothed out into a rich, resonant overtone. A deep, subterranean pedal-point—a fifth note, low and infinitely warm—wove itself beneath the vibrating glass. The micro-convection currents stabilized, flowing in graceful, laminar ribbons that made the purple-tinged mica fronds sway in slow, measured rhythm.

In the center of the largest obsidian clearing, an elder Lithodact paused.

Its quartz punch rested against a half-flaked blade of chert. The four segments of its body settled into absolute immobility. It lifted its blunt, segmented head, angling its rear horn toward the ceiling. Its quartz eyes caught the smoldering amber phosphorescence of the microbes, reflecting two tiny, golden points of light in the bruised gloom.

Beneath its collar plate, the ridged plectrums began to move. It did not send an alarm. It struck three soft, deliberate clicks—a question—and then waited.

From the basalt beneath its hydrocarbon-felt pads, through the razor-edged chert and the dry, ancient floor, the answer rose through its mineralized bones: a steady, unchanging, eternal vibration that held the room in an embrace so gentle it did not disturb a single grain of glass dust.

*You are seen,* the vibration said, without words. *You are whole. You may continue.*

The elder Lithodact stood motionless for a human decade, simply letting the new resonance circulate the heavy naphtha through its quiet veins. 

Then, with the crisp, dry rustle of shuffling cards, it lowered its head, raised its quartz punch, and struck the stone again.
