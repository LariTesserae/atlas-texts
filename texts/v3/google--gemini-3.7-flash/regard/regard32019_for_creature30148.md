The power that came to the spires was not a maker of sunlit meadows, nor did it carry the cruel vanity of those who believe that to save a creature, one must remake it in the image of a garden.

It had witnessed worlds where well-meaning gods smothered deep-trench leviathans in sunlight, and turned crystal-dwellers into fragile clay. Wisdom, in the higher spheres, is largely the discipline of restraint: knowing that to drop soil upon this basalt would be an act of desecration, and to force open the lungs of these people to thin, dry oxygen would be an execution.

The power came not as an apparition, but as an *attendance*. 

It settled into the empty gulfs between the needles like a change in refractive index, invisible save for a faint, violet cooling along the boundaries where the steam met the void. For seven hundred Strokes, it did nothing at all. It simply pressed its awareness against the cold side of the boiler plates, hung within the thermal updrafts alongside the drift-skates, and listened to the rhythmic, copper-and-bone pulse of the tappers travelling through thousands of miles of alloy.

It learned what the people had purchased with their blood: they had bought equilibrium. They were not miserable. In the slow, breathy gutturals of their chest-talk, in the absolute faith with which a tether-clip was engaged, in the three-hour shared exhalation of the master piston, there was a solemn and magnificent sanity. They did not need rescue from their home.

They needed only a margin.

A margin between endurance and exhaustion; a margin between the shear-stress of the iron and the merciless expansion of the young rock.

And so, the benevolent power made its work invisible, slow, and permanent.

First, it descended into the micro-fissures where the iron anchor-pins bit into the searing volcanic glass. Where basalt and alloy met, there was a perpetual, microscopic warfare—the stone expanding in mantle-heat, the metal flexing in the wet air, generating a grinding fatigue that cost dozens of lives each cycle when a stanchion sheared away without warning. The power did not turn the iron to magic gold; it simply catalyzed a reaction between the sulfurous condensation and the iron oxide, coaxing forth a third material: a self-healing, slate-colored mineral slick that filled the micro-fractures. It acted as an infinitely durable cartilage between bone-stone and iron-flesh, absorbing the shudder of the cold-air intrusions and silencing the catastrophic snaps that haunted the lower tiers.

Second, it breathed upon the liver-colored yeast mats in the drip-pans under the catwalks. It did not replace them with wheat or fruit, which would rot in minutes upon the steaming grills. Instead, it introduced a single, elegant mutation into the ferric bacteria. When cured over the vents, the gray cakes retained their heavy caloric density, but the bitter, caustic bite of mineral brine softened. In its place, the bread yielded a faint, lingering coolness on the tongue—like the taste of clean meltwater from a cloud that had never touched sulfur. For the first time in their history, the workers swallowed without their throats burning.

Third, it touched the drift-skates. It attuned the lateral lines of their cartilage frames to the subtlest barometric shifts in the mesosphere. They began to bank not when the freezing downdraft was already falling, but twenty minutes prior—turning in long, graceful gyres that gave the workers ample time to lash their tethers, clear the wet stairs, and dog down the hatchways before the shrieking frost-wind struck.

Finally, the power left its single true gift to their spirit—and it left it within the metal itself.

It tuned the resonant frequency of the gantries and catenary webs. It did not make the city loud; it merely removed the dull, deadening acoustic friction that swallowed their signals in mechanical murk. 

The next time a child three kilometers down a spire pressed their cheek to an iron stanchion to listen for their father’s ascent on the freight lift, the sound that traveled through the copper and lead did not arrive as a jagged, jarring clatter. It arrived as a clear, warm chime—a low, sustained fundamental note that thrummed through the skull like the purr of a sleeping beast, carrying with it a profound, physical sensation of being anchored, weighed, and held safe against the drop.

When its work was finished, the power did not demand worship. It built no temples, carved no commandments into the obsidian needles, and left no angels in the mist. It withdrew back into the high, thin clarity above the troposphere, becoming once again the quiet sky.

Down on the third tier of the easternmost spire, the master piston reached the apex of its ascent. 

The Stroke exhaled. 

A world-spanning *whoosh* of warm, fragrant mist rolled over the iron collars, washing the walkways clean of mineral salt. On a narrow catwalk suspended over three vertical miles of shimmering, heat-rippled space, a woman in a lead-soled boot paused. She wiped the lukewarm moisture from her brow with a scarred, leathery wrist. 

She broke a piece of the sulfur-cake, tasted the strange, gentle coolness at the center of it, and looked down between the soles of her feet. The infinite drop was still there, wild and terrible and newly born. But the iron under her boots felt steady, humming with a deep, peaceful sustain that seemed to go down all the way to the root of the world.

She tapped her copper baton twice against the railing—*safe, steady, working*—and the entire mountain of iron softly hummed the answer back.
