The Presence did not fall like a meteor, nor did it part the clouds with a trumpet-blast of light. To bring a sudden, foreign heaven into the rift would be an act of violence; a burst of cool mountain air would freeze the lung-pouches of the scourers, a rain of sweet water would dissolve the delicate chemotrophic grease upon the vents, and a flood of open sky would blind eyes that had spent twenty generations perfecting the slit-gaze of the shadow-crawlers.

Wisdom knows that life, even at its most brutal, is a cathedral built of interlocking necessities. To pull a single stone out of pity is often to bring the roof down upon the heads of the sufferers.

So the benevolent power came instead as a temperature. 

It gathered itself into the mineral seams—not everywhere, but along a single, jagged terrace of cold basalt forty paces above the highest human scaffold. It was a place where the steam did not pool into stinging vinegar, where the rising heat of the floor was gentled by a slow, porous breath exhaled through the deep marrow of the mountain.

The power looked first at the humans. 

It saw how their minds had been filed down to the thickness of an obsidian flake. It saw the hunters lashed to the upright posts, sleeping with their muscles half-clenched, their ears twitching to the percussive clatter of mandibles fifty feet below. It saw the mothers who could not stroke their infants’ hair—because there was no hair—and who instead stroked their children’s shoulders with palms hardened like dried horn, checking only for fractures in the bone and parasites in the skin folds.

It saw that they had survived everything except the ability to put down the knife.

The power did not take away their prey. It did not silence the glorious, frantic engine of the floor, for the trench-crawlers had their own dumb, sacred momentum, and the sail-skimmers in the sun-pillars were living jewels of glass and fluid.

Instead, the power did four small, patient things.

***

**First, it gave them the velvet-crust.**

In the dampest, most shadowed hollows behind the scaffold anchors—where the cliff-sweat had always been acidic and slick—a new growth appeared. It was not the green of plateau ferns, which would have withered under the sulfur fumes, but a dense, slate-blue moss, low-lying and fibrous. It grew only on dead crawler-bone and cold stone. 

It was soft. 

The first hunter to touch it recoiled, his hand flying to his stone club, expecting a sting or a borer’s rotating tooth. But when no pain came, he reached out again. For several minutes, suspended between life and the abyss on a ledge of raw shale, the man simply pressed his gray, callused thumb into the yielding blue fur, feeling something give under his weight without breaking.

Within three generations, the dwellers began to line their woven sleeping-cradles with the scrape-shavings of this moss. For the first time since the first human fell into the rift, an elder lay back against a surface that did not leave a bruise.

***

**Second, it tuned the steam.**

A mile to the east, where a cluster of violent vents had historically belched scalding mud—periodically boiling entire scaffolds alive—the subterranean chambers shifted by a fraction of an inch. The vents did not plug; rather, their throats narrowed and widened with the rhythmic draw of the plateau's tidal winds. 

The sound they produced lost its sharp, shattering hiss. It became a low, resonant drone—a deep, wooden hum that vibrated at the exact frequency of a resting heartbeat. 

The constant, maddening clatter of the carapaces on the floor below did not cease, but now it sat atop a steady, underlying note of rest. The scaffold-dwellers, who had only ever spoken in dry clicks and tongue-snaps to keep from alerting the glass-runners, began to feel an unbidden resonance in their chests. 

A fisher, waiting in the steam for the jerk of the sinew line, caught herself making a sound that was not an alarm, nor a call for salt, nor a locator-click. 

She was humming. She did not know what a song was, but her throat remembered the shape of it.

***

**Third, it placed a drop of honey in the grease.**

To the chemotrophic mats near the gentle vents, the power imparted a single new enzyme. It was imperceptible to the scourers, who continued to scrape and turn the stone into alkaline mud. But to the humans, who gathered the white curd to seal their skin and flavor their dried meat, the grease began to yield a faint, trace sweetness—a taste that spoke of nothing in the basin, for there were no fruits or flowers in the crack.

It was a memory of an elsewhere they had never seen. 

When the hunters tasted it on the tips of their knives, they would pause. They would look up—not with fear of the sun-pillars, but with a strange, quiet hunger that could not be satisfied by hemolymph or the white leg-muscle of a prowler. It made them wonder what lay beyond the lip of the ceiling. It made them look at the great tear in the sky and imagine that the light was not just an eye that burned, but an open door.

***

**And fourth, it began to shape the ladder.**

Beneath the highest scaffolds, deep in the blind face of the western cliff, the moisture did not erode the stone into treacherous scree. It began to dissolve the basalt along a slow, ascending line of natural faults. 

It will take six hundred years for the path to complete itself. 

It will not be an easy climb. It will consist of narrow ledges, handholds carved by trickling water, and resting-pockets lined with the slate-blue moss. It will ascend two miles through the steam, through the thinning mist, past the altitude where the sulfur rots the skin, slowly tempering their barrel chests and widening their eyes to the broader, cooler light of the upper world.

The power did not pick them up in its hand and carry them to the plateau. To do so would have left them helpless, naked, and terrified on the dry grasses.

Instead, it gave them back their sleep, so they would have the strength to climb.

It gave them a song, so they would have something to carry into the dark.

And it left the path waiting, one stone notch at a time, for the day their children's children would look at the floor of shells, lay down their bone hooks, and decide—of their own fierce, beautiful will—to stand up and walk out of the furnace.
