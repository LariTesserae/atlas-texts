The Power did not come down the chimney as a pillar of blinding light, for light in that place would have been an act of violence. 

To bring a sunbeam into the cleft would be to burn the retinas of the pip-bats, to panic the blind tallow-mice into lethal stampedes off their sheer ledges, and to dry the waxy, water-repelling plumage of the soot-swifts until their flight-feathers cracked like dried reeds. Light would have withered the copper cyanobacteria and turned the damp, fermenting floor into sterile dust. 

A lesser deity, full of crude pity, might have looked upon the chimney and seen only misery: blindness, sulfur, rot, crushing dark, and the frantic cruelty of survival. Such a god would have opened the stone to the blue sky, cooled the boiling air, and cleansed the guano, believing they were offering salvation—and in doing so, would have murdered an entire universe in an afternoon.

The Power that looked upon the cleft was wise, and so it understood that this place was not broken. It was complete. It was an iron kettle of life, bubbling fiercely against the killing cold of the roof of the world.

So the Power arrived as an *absence of weight*—a subtle shifting in the humidity, a vast, gentle attention that settled through the rock like warm oil seeping into dry cedar.

It began at the bottom, where the white flatworms and the black grease-beetles churned the centuries-old peat. The Power felt the endless, silent appetite of the muck. It did not find the decay foul; it found it holy. In the damp dark, the beetles were transmuting dead feathers and excrement back into living muscle, which would become moths, which would become bats, which would become the heat of the mountain. It was an unbroken circle, tight and furious.

The Power slipped beneath the floor, pressing deeper into the basalt root, miles down into the mountain’s chest where the subterranean magma breathed against the rock. There, it made its first adjustment.

It did not change the heat; it *anchored* it. 

Mountains are restless things, and volcanic vents are prone to sudden suffocations—a shifting tectonic plate can pinch a heat-throat shut in a night, leaving millions of creatures to freeze into solid lumps of grease and bone before the dawn. The Power reached into the deep plumbing of the Earth and set a quiet, permanent hinge upon the basalt fractures. It ensured the draft would not falter. The mountain would breathe its slow, ninety-degree exhalation through the next ice age, through the drying of the lowlands, through the rise and fall of glaciers. The hearth was fastened to the world's bone.

Then, the Power moved up the walls.

It passed the steam-crawler, whose pale, segmented armor gleamed in the dark. The centipede was poised above a nursery ledge, antennae quivering toward the scent of warm milk and mouse-fur. The Power did not crush the hunter, nor did it strike the pincers from its jaws. To rob the dark of its teeth is to make the dark soft, and the soft cannot survive at fourteen thousand feet. 

Instead, the Power brushed its awareness against the creature’s chitin, and against the heavy, slumbering bodies of the mouse-knot beside it. It did not alter the dance of predator and prey; it merely deepened the sleep of the mice, so that when the pincers fell, there was no terror, only the sudden, painless sliding of one warmth into another. 

In the mouse-knot itself, where forty heavy, tallow-coated bodies lay tangled in the damp hollows, the Power lingered longest. It took pleasure in the sheer, unreasonable tenacity of these blind things—creatures that had discarded eyes, discarded speed, discarded beauty, trading them all for a thick layer of fat and the courage to sleep through an eternal night. The Power blew softly into their wide, baffle-lined nostrils. It gifted them nothing they did not already possess, save for a slight, genetic hardening against the ammonia rot: their lungs would no longer scar in their old age, and their litters would hold three pups instead of two, keeping the ledges full against the winter cull.

Higher still, at the narrow, freezing throat where the swifts hung by their crampon-claws, the Power touched the waxy feathers of the birds. It admired their purple blood, thick with life in the thin sky. As the twilight neared and the first flock prepared to launch itself into the sixty-mile-an-hour razor gales of the outside world, the Power smoothed the turbulence at the chimney’s lip. It did not take away the cold—the cold was the anvil on which the swifts were beaten—but it shaped the wind just so, an imperceptible curve of basalt that caught the updraft and slung the tiny, iron-bodied birds clean out into the sky with an extra fathom of speed.

Lastly, the Power turned its attention to the fire-hardened pine poles wedged into the upper fracture, and the rotted scrap of braided yak-sinew left by the hunters.

It saw the hunters down in the valley: thin, broad-chested men and women wrapped in grease-stained skins, coughing blood into the snow, their babies gnawing on frozen leather while the blizzards buried their tents. It saw the desperate courage it took to climb fourteen thousand feet of vertical ice, to lower oneself into a stinking, sulfurous pit that smelled of devils, merely to bring back a sack of lard and a handful of squabs so their hearth fires would not go out before the spring.

The Power did not fill their granaries with magic grain, for that is not the way of the high peaks. 

Instead, it looked upon the five-foot pine poles lodged in the rock. The damp rot of the chimney had been chewing at them; within another season, the wood would have crumbled, and the next hunter who hauled himself down into the dark would have plunged into the guano fathoms below.

The Power touched the pine. It did not make the wood fresh or young; it petrified it. It turned the grain to dark, unyielding horn-stone, silently mineralizing the fibers so they would hold the weight of a shivering hunter for a thousand winters to come. And at the cleft's icy, outer lip, where the frost made the basalt slick as wet lard, the Power roughened the stone by the width of a needle’s point—just enough for a rawhide boot to find purchase in a howling gale.

When it was done, the Power withdrew. 

It did not leave behind a temple. It did not carve a name upon the stone. It left no light, no sweet fragrance, no song. 

The chimney remained precisely as it had been: dark, wet, deafeningly loud, and smelling richly of vinegar, ammonia, and ancient dung. High on the ledges, the mouse-knots groaned and shifted. In the throat, a swift burst into the icy dusk with a wet snap of wings. Down in the peat, the beetles tumbled over one another with a sound like dry tea leaves.

The Power watched from the high, silent blue above the peaks. It looked at the plume of steam rising from the mountain’s crown—a warm, grey feather against the violet alpine night—and saw that the fire was burning well.
