The power did not arrive with the rushing of waters. 

To a world of glass, horn, and unyielding xylem, a rainstorm would have been an extinction event—a corrosive deluge of rot, softening the razor-clean ribs of the reed-timber, dissolving the calcified pads of the steelyards, and turning the sacred, sound-eating turf into a festering mire. A foolish god gives what it possesses; a wise god gives only what the receiver can hold without breaking.

So the power came without a drop of condensation. It came as an attention: cold, vast, and completely dry.

It slipped through the deep tectonic fractures above the phosphor lattices, shedding every vestige of mist and vapor as it descended, parching itself until it was as pure and mineralized as the aerosol winds of the vents. It smelled of cured cedar, crushed mica, and lightning struck upon flint.

For a long season—measured not in days, but in the slow millimeter-expansion of the great stipes—the power did nothing but listen.

It felt the rhythm of the chasm through the soles of the Lignarii: the muffled *thump-thump* of the six-ton drop-forges swallowed by the fibrous floor; the crisp, continuous *shhh-shhh* of the steelyards planing ribbon-bark from the vertical spires; the electrostatic hum of the pellicle-wings gliding through the thermal updrafts. It observed the lacquer-mites packing amber into micro-fractures, and it saw how the people did not speak with wide, wet mouths, but held their lives behind amber lenses and suede-soft skin, tapping out their thoughts upon the hollow railings in a percussion of absolute restraint.

The power loved them for this. It loved that they had made a cathedral out of austerity; that they had taken a wound in the planet’s crust and woven it into an engine of quiet, unbleeding dignity.

Yet wisdom saw where the dry world strained.

It saw the torque. Bolting dead, lacquered steel to living, waterless xylem created a quiet war of shear-stress. Every time the great stipes groaned in the Tide-Slack, microscopic tears formed around the iron anchors—tears faster than the lacquer-mites could seal, imperceptibly fatiguing the living pillars. It saw, too, that the phosphor lattices suspended in the heights were slowly degrading, their rare-earth lattices sputtering toward a dimming that would one day leave the Lignarii in total, frozen dark.

And so, the benevolent power acted. It did not speak, nor did it reveal a face, for an image demands worship, and worship is noisy. 

Instead, it made three adjustments to the quiet mechanics of the rift:

First, it sank its consciousness deep into the petrified mattress of the cavern floor, down to the boiling mineral vents where the dry aerosol was born. There, it gently shifted the composition of the subterranean fumes, introducing a minute trace of volatile silica and gaseous resin. From that day on, the wood did not merely grow hard; it grew *cooperative*. Where steel bolts pierced the grain, the wood no longer splintered under the weight of the cantilevered foundries. Instead, the fibers learned to curl smoothly around the metal, drinking the vibrations of the drop-forges and turning kinetic shock into structural density. The iron and the stipe became one continuous, unwearying ligament.

Second, it reached into the phosphor lattices hanging in the heights. It did not make them brighter—glare was already a burden to the amber-shielded eyes below—but it made their light *nourishing*. It tuned the cold noon-glow to match the precise metabolic resonance of the dry plants and the electrostatic velvet of the gliders. The lattices ceased to decay; they became self-replenishing dynamos, fed by the very heat-shimmer rising from the factory vents.

Finally, the power left a gift for the Lignarii themselves—not a law, but a harmonic.

It laid a single, permanent frequency into the core of the largest avenue-stipes: a deep, sub-audible note that resonated at the exact frequency of rest. 

The next time the Tide-Slack arrived, when the lattices dimmed to conserve the grid and the great wooden groaning began across the leagues, the sound was different. It was no longer the dry screech of wood tearing against mineral constraints. It had become a deep, resonant cello-note—a chord so rich and low that it bypassed the air entirely and spoke straight to the bones of everything resting upon the boughs.

In their suspended steel pods, the Lignarii families felt the note rise through the floorboards. It unknotted the tension in their shoulders; it cooled their dry blood; it cleared the dust from their nictitating membranes. The steelyards in the turf folded their triple-jointed legs beneath them and rested their chisel-heads upon the velvet floor, entirely unafraid. The pellicle-wings hung motionless from the gantries, their vellum membranes vibrating in gentle, restorative sympathy with the singing wood.

No one built a shrine. No one knew a god had passed through. 

They simply noted in their logbooks that the stipe-stress gauges had returned to perfect equilibrium, that the air tasted slightly sweeter of cured amber, and that during the quiet hours, the wood seemed to hold them a little closer.

And the power, having made the dry world immortal in its dryness, withdrew into the high stone, leaving behind only the cold, clear noon, the scent of crushed talc, and the long, undisturbed peace of an unbleeding forest.
