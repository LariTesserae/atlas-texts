The power that looked down into the stone nave did not possess a body, nor did it carry the heavy, meddling pride of gods who demand altars. It was an ancient, patient attention—an intelligence born of deep time, wise enough to know that to love a thing is not to strip it of its nature.

It saw the fissure for what it truly was: not a place of cruelty, but the raw, unbuffered forge of the world. It saw the violent beauty of the cold water roaring toward the clouds; it saw the blind, iron-hooked fleas holding fast against annihilation with crystalline blood in their veins; and it saw the deaf men in their greased pig-leather, trembling with the agony of held breath, hammering bronze against stone to steal pieces of the sky.

A lesser power might have stilled the torrent. A foolish mercy would have calmed the water to a sunlit pool, warmed the glacial cold, and turned the jagged quartz into smooth, harmless river glass. 

But the wise power understood: to halt the flood would be to starve the tallow-threads that fed upon the sulfur vents. It would mean the split-fleas would lose the acoustic song of the fracturing stone and wither. It would mean the sky-spar would cease to grow, and the men who risked their lives to touch the impossible would be left with only empty hands and ordinary earth. 

The struggle was not a curse. It was the very architecture of their existence.

So the power did not quiet the storm. It chose, instead, to lean into the margins of the violence—where the boundary between life and death was measured in the width of a hair.

First, it touched the stone.

It did not soften the granite, but it laid a subtle, enduring coherence into the cleavage planes of the rock. Where the hydraulic hammer struck the walls, the slate began to fracture along truer, cleaner lines. It stopped spitting blind, jagged shrapnel into the water; instead, the newborn edges sheared with mathematical grace, shedding their micro-shards into the upward vent before they could sever the tallow-stays or the divers’ flesh. 

Next, it descended into the micro-cavitation.

The concussive, metallic micro-detonations—the billions of imploding vacuum bubbles that battered the divers’ chests and turned their eardrums to gristle—were given an altered harmonic. The power did not eliminate the pressure, for pressure was the breath of the sky-spar, but it wove a laminar cushion into the water’s skin. The violent shears smoothed at their absolute center. The shockwaves that had bruised the men's lungs through their ash-wood breastplates now rolled around them like heavy, velvet fists rather than hammers of iron.

Then, the power looked upon the divers themselves.

It saw a man named Kaelen, pinned beneath the current on the shuddering pine raft, forty feet down in the roaring dark. His jaws were clenched so hard against his boxwood block that blood seeped from his gums into the bitter, ozonated water. His lungs were a furnace of hot carbon, his fingers numb, searching blindly across the freezing western face. His green-tinted glass was fogged with his own panic. He was seconds from pulling his release in failure—or drowning where he knelt.

The power did not fill his lungs with false air. To do so would make him a ghost, no longer a man of flesh and daring.

Instead, the power leaned over his shoulder and touched the invisible.

Beneath Kaelen’s gloved, trembling thumb, the cold water did not change, but the virgin quartz plate he was seeking caught a sudden, deep refraction—not of sunlight from above, but of an internal, sapphire luminance. For a single heartbeat, the invisible spar flared with a crisp, cool silhouette against the black slate. 

Kaelen felt it not with his eyes, but through the bone of his wrist: a precise, crystalline ring that cut cleanly through the hydraulic thunder. It was the exact note of the stone’s root, showing him precisely where to set his copper wedge.

*Clink.*

One strike. The bronze mallet fell true. The plate of sky-spar, four feet of flawless, unblemished light, released from the mountain without a single fracture.

As the razor-edged sheet came free, surging upward toward his chest, Kaelen did not flinch. He caught it against his ash plate, wrapping his tallow-corded arms around the glass lightning. He yanked the toggle on his descending-stone.

The river-boulder dropped away into the abyss.

The sky-vent caught him.

It did not smash him against the overhanging granite teeth. The water, tuned by the silent witness, bore him upward in a straight, violent, perfect plume. The newly cleaved obsidian walls skimmed past his boots by inches, their razor points parting around his passage like the ribs of a great, opening vault.

In less than two seconds, Kaelen was hurled out of the dark stone throat into the blinding, freezing brilliance of the open air. He broke the surface in a spray of foam and cloud-mist, gasping a great, tearing lungful of thin mountain wind, holding the unbroken plate of spar toward the sun like a shield of frozen light.

Below, in the deafening nave, the work went on. The fleas rasped the rock; the hydroids hummed in the shear; the cold mountain tore itself open in relentless genesis. 

The power remained, watching from the quartz-veins and the bubbles. It did not ask to be known. It was content to simply be the secret strength in the copper pegs, the true ring in the bronze, and the slight, merciful slip of the water that let brave things touch the edge of destruction—and return with the light.
