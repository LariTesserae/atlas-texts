A foolish god would have made it a meadow.

A hasty, sentimental power, peering through forty feet of municipal masonry and smelling the vinegar-sharp wash, the sour sulfur, and the scalded grease, would have reached down with a bright and violent pity. It would have cooled the water to mountain-spring clarity, stripped the lime crust from the iron, brought down the ceiling to let in the sun, and planted clover across the diamond-plate.

And in doing so, it would have slaughtered everything.

The five-foot salamanders, whose boiled-pork hides require the thick, neutralizing slime that only the acid-wash provokes, would have withered in the sweet water, their skin sloughing away in the chill. The scale-plates, deprived of the boiling runoff, would have starved, their leathery skirts drying into brittle rings. The lagging-crawlers, tuned to the fever of bronze pipes, would have grown torpid and died of the cold; the grease-spiders would have woven webs for an empty, clean sky, catching no tallow, their larvae rotting in the damp earth.

True wisdom does not mistake unfamiliar beauty for suffering. True benevolence does not impose its own architecture upon a cathedral already complete.

So the Power did not bring sunlight. It did not silence the brass stroke.

Instead, it came down through the baffling plates not as a light—which would have been an insult to the eyeless—but as a weight, a temperature, and a presence. It sank through the dripping aroid leaves, through the scalding mist, and into the flooded avenues, allowing itself to be tasted by the lateral lines of the salamanders and the thermal combs of the crawlers.

It tasted like salt that does not burn. It felt like an iron beam that has never known fatigue.

The Power saw the one true vulnerability of the Sub-Basin Sump: it was an accidental Eden, utterly dependent upon the monstrous, careless appetite of the city above. One day, the factories overhead would go bankrupt. The boilers would be quenched; the workers would strike or flee; the displacement pumps would seize with red rust; the scalding runoff would run thin, cold, and clear; and this dark, perfect metabolism would freeze into silence.

The Power, being benevolent, decided that what had fought so hard to invent itself in the dark should never be starved by the whim of the surface.

Beneath the floorplates, deeper than the drowned basements and the concrete revetments, the Power pressed its palm into the bedrock of the world.

It did not break the pumps; it took them into its chest. Where the city’s boilers ended, the Power set a slow, immortal hearth—a deep, geothermal fissure that would bleed steady, mineral-dense heat upward through the masonry forever. It wove an unceasing hydraulic breath into the earth beneath the terrace, so that even if the city above should crumble into weeds and dust, the subterranean stroke of the brass valving would never falter. The fifteen-minute draw-down would continue; the rams would still thump; the white foam would still surge back over the steps on the quarter-hour, century after century.

Then, the Power moved through the flooded avenues, doing only what was small, tender, and invisible.

Where the acid sometimes spiked too high—sharp enough to burn even the salamanders’ spade-like snouts—it laid down veins of soft, sacrificial limestone within the sluices, self-buffering the torrent to keep it precisely within the razor-thin margin where their life could thrive.

Where the copper conduit bundles had grown thin under the ravenous root-veils of the vines, it reinforced the metal from within, ensuring the plants could drink their hot condensation without collapsing their own trellis.

And finally, the Power waited for the cycle.

It watched the water drop three feet with that great, sucking groan. It watched the blind salamanders slide like damp fire-hoses into the brick cavities, stacking their dense ribs against the masonry. It watched the scale-plates clamp down with a thousand wet, simultaneous *clacks*.

The water reached the bottom of the basin. The immense machinery paused.

The three-second stall arrived—the dead-stall of absolute hydraulic stillness.

In that breathless pause, when thousands of pale spores, drifting larvae, and glistening pheromones were shed into the warm, mineral broth, the Power exhaled into the water.

It did not change the broth. It simply blessed the drift.

It touched every blind, questing spore with a subtle endurance; it gave to the drifting larvae a keener grip for the hot iron; it gave to the salamanders coiled in the dark a deep, abiding fullness in their belly-flesh, so that they knew, without eyes or thought, that the stone holding them would never yield.

Then the pneumatic rams crashed down. The boiling wall of white foam roared back across the three city blocks of diamond-plate. The ferns bent flat, the water rose, and the salamanders tumbled out into the scalding surge, rolling through the turbulence in complete and total safety, carried by a river that would never run dry.
