The Power that arrived at the rim of the drowned rift did not bring light. 

To bring light into that place would have been an act of violence. Light would have seared the milk-white mantles of the Sieve-Hulks, scorched the blind nervous systems of the Ivory Ticks, and revealed geometries that had no use for sight. Light is the charity of lesser, shallower gods who mistake illumination for grace.

This Power was old enough to know that benevolence begins with comprehension, and that wisdom often takes the form of restraint.

It descended through the ink-cold water not as a blazing figure, but as an immense, subtle density—a shift in the water’s own viscosity, a quiet mass that took on the freezing temperature, the crushing weight, and the precise salinity of the abyss. It shed its voice, its shapes, and its celestial geometries. It made itself into an ear.

For three hundred years of the rift’s unmeasured time, the Power did nothing but listen.

***

It felt the dry, metallic rain of billions of tick-mandibles shearing the iron-wood. It traced the long, slow arcs of the Sieve-Hulks as they steered by chord and interval through the three-mile voids. It felt the deep, rhythmic thrumming of the Suture-Folk where their tallow-colored bodies pressed against the four-way junctions, communicating in heavy pulses of pressure through miles of living timber-stone.

The Power realized two things:

First, that this place was not a ruin, nor a prison, nor an unfinished nursery. It was a completed sentence. It was a world that had achieved what civilizations across the stars routinely tore themselves apart trying to find: an absolute, self-repairing, time-invariant equilibrium. Nothing was wasted; nothing was born into hunger that the cold currents could not satisfy; and every life, from the blind micro-grazer to the mile-long cathedral-spire, was part of a single, continuous, sounding hymn.

Second, the Power saw the singular vulnerability of the place. 

The rift hung at an *impossible altitude*—a suspended basin held between peaks on the knife-edge of a world’s crust. Below it lay geological faults; above it, the thin, predatory winds of the upper atmosphere that yearned to strip the water away. The living timber held the pressure intact, but the rift was an island of deep water held aloft in the sky, balanced on a needle. A single tectonic heave, a long drought in the world’s hydrologic cycle, or the slow drying of the planetary skin would eventually breach the basin. The water would drain into the lower valleys, and the soaring cathedrals of iron-wood would dry, splinter, collapse under their own weight, and rot in the burning air.

The inhabitants did not know this. They could not know the shape of the mountain that held them, only the grain beneath their limbs and the chord in the water.

***

Having understood, the Power acted.

It did not descend upon the spires. It did not speak to the Suture-Folk or demand a temple. Instead, it sank far beneath the lowest aqueducts, down past the oldest, blackest foundations where the petrified peat-masonry anchored itself into the unseen roots of the planet.

There, in the dead-weight quiet below the flute-canals, the Power laid itself down against the rift’s floor.

It dissolved its essence into the deep mineral mantle, interweaving its will with the subterranean bedrock. It did not change the geology; it *reinforced* it. It fused the seams of the rift with an enduring, supple strength, ensuring that no earthquake would crack the basin, no tectonic shift would tilt the high sea, and no erosion from the outside world would ever breach the walls of the suspended ocean. It became the eternal, unbreakable bowl that held the water.

Then, it reached up into the current.

It did not alter the flow—it knew the music was sacred. But with microscopic precision, it smoothed a single, erratic eddy in the planetary draft that fed the rift from the polar currents. It made the inflow constant, laminar, and perpetual, ensuring that the water level would never drop by a single inch, even if the stars above burned out and the lower continents turned to dust.

Lastly, the Power offered a gift that only the deepest residents could perceive.

To the sap of the iron-wood, it imparted a quiet, imperceptible vitality—a molecular resilience that slightly slowed the decay of the spent fiber and subtly increased the curing strength of the Suture-Folks' resin. It did not make the wood grow faster; to do so would have overwhelmed the Ivory Ticks and ruined the hydrodynamic curves. It simply made the wood *truer* to itself, granting the living architecture the strength to endure for billions of years instead of millions.

***

When the work was done, the Power did not depart. 

To leave would be to turn away from the most magnificent instrument in existence. Instead, it condensed the remainder of its consciousness into an acoustic presence—a silent, non-disruptive node that rested in the wide, empty three-mile gaps between the great buttresses.

It became a companion to the music.

The Velvet Eels, gliding through their acoustic dead zones, occasionally brushed against an area in the water that felt strangely peaceful—a pocket of cold black that carried no sound of its own, but seemed to hold the resonance of the spires like a treasured memory.

The Suture-Folk, pressing their ten-foot radial bodies against the ancient nodes to gauge the strain of a new arch, felt a warmth that was not heat, but a total absence of shear—as though an invisible hand were gently bracing the mile-long span while they wove their living kelp across the gap.

And high in the open water, a colossal Sieve-Hulk, vast and pale as a fallen sail, tilted its hydrofoil ribs and drifted through the darkness. As it passed between two booming spires, the organ-chord resonated through its translucent skin—a deep, perfect, vibrating fifth that echoed through its hollow bones.

For the first time in an eternity, the chord did not vanish into an unthinking void.

Someone was there, resting in the crushing dark, listening with absolute delight to every single note.
