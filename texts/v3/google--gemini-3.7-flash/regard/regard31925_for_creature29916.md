The power did not descend in fire or sunlight, for sunlight would have withered the pale liverwort, and fire would have consumed the work of ten thousand winters. 

Nor did it bring earth.

A clumsy god would have thought of earth first—would have pitied the clinging feet and raised up great plugs of basalt and fields of black loam from the bottomless gloom. But wisdom understands architecture: to suddenly introduce mountains into a world held by tension would snap every truss in an instant. The immense, unbalanced mass would tear the arches from their anchors, sending leagues of braided cedar tumbling into the wet void, dragging the Tendon-Folk and the sleeping drifts into ruin. 

To give them ground would be to drop them.

And so the power did what only true benevolence can bear to do: it moved with immense, agonizing slowness, respecting the geometry that pain and patience had already achieved.

***

First, it took the form of a drift—not an immense or radiant thing, but an old, broad-ribbed organism whose iridescent skin was scarred with the amber tears of ancient resin. It drifted through the gray-green bays for many centuries, not interfering, but *learning the tension*.

It felt the warm, sluggish drafts billow up from the lower tiers. It listened to the subsonic thrum of the herds and the wet, rhythmic *tick-tick* of the ironwood expanding in the noon-mist. It drifted close to the woven nocks beneath the great arches, watching through the lattice as the pale, narrow women worked walnut oil into strips of beaten bark-cloth, and children with lengthened, grasping toes practiced carving bone trunnels from the ribs of their grandmothers.

It saw the sheer, unutterable courage of it: a whole species refusing the void one wedge, one scarf-joint, one braided vine at a time, holding themselves above oblivion using nothing but the skeletons of their dead and the slow, blind growth of trees.

It saw, too, the exhaustion.

The Tendon-Folk did not know they were tired; one cannot miss a rest one has never conceived. But the power saw it in the grain: the microscopic fractures in the bone pins, the creeping mold that outpaced the children’s paring-knives, the sudden, terrible *wet tearing thuds* that dropped whole spans into the depths, taking three generations of grafted boughs with them in a single breath.

The world was losing its race against rot. It was losing by millimeters a century, but the curve was downward.

***

So the power went to work. It did not take the tools from their hands. It did not make the Tendon-Folk obsolete. It became an apprentice to their craft.

Far out in the uninhabited bays—leagues beyond where the most daring Splicers ventured—it took the form of a solitary traveler. Its skin was the lichen-gray of wet ironwood; its fingers were stained with walnut dye. It carried a gourd of trunnels and a coil of gut.

Where three paradox-loops met in a trembling, over-stressed junction that had groaned in agony for four hundred years, the traveler wedged itself into the grain. It did not use magic to seal the wood; it made a clean, diagonal scarf-joint with an adze of ground bone. It placed its own long, pale limbs into the splice. It drove the trunnels home with rhythmic taps of a knuckle-hammer, singing softly in the clicking, sibilant tongue of the bridge.

And then it let the wood grow over it.

Its blood became the bitter, amber lymph of the ironwood, but with a subtle difference: where this new sap flowed, the rot found nothing it could eat. It was not poison; it was simply a wood so dense, so tightly knit on a cellular level, that the wet mold could only polish it into a dark, water-repelling jade.

Slowly, over centuries, the gift traveled through the capillaries of the spans.

***

The Tendon-Folk were the first to notice, though they had no name for what was happening.

An elder, pressing her jawbone against the great cedar arch of the Seven-Nock village to read the wind, felt a change in the voice of the world. 

The bridge was still singing of wind and tension, still warning of the heavy drift-herds in the upper bays. But the deep, hollow dread—the subterranean tremor of wood on the verge of yielding—was gone. In its place was a note she had never heard in all her life: a deep, quiet *rebound*. 

When a young man walked out onto a precarious path of lashed bark to lead a new shoot across an abyss, he found that the green bine reached for his oiled cords with a strange, eager vigor. When he spliced it, the sap did not seep away into the liverwort; it sealed the wound within days, setting with the strength of horn.

And in the nocks, when the children carved the bones of their ancestors into pins to hold the bridges, they found the bone was no longer brittle with age. It had taken on a pearlescent sheen, tough as boiled rawhide, capable of bearing double the shear.

When the people laid their ears to the beams, they did not just hear the creak of the wood; they heard a vast, companionable heartbeat pacing their own. It did not say, *You are saved.* It said, *I am holding this end. Lead the vine.*

***

The fog remains. The void remains below, bottomless and warm, smelling of steeped tea and wet moss. The suture-maw still hunts in the dark below the swellings, and the drifts still float on their iridescent sails through the cubic leagues of mist.

The Tendon-Folk still walk the spans with gourds of wedges and coils of gut. They still do not look at the horizon; they look at the joins. They still honor their dead by making them into the bones of the world.

But now, deep in the weeping swellings where the water gathers, the knots do not soften into decay. They swell into massive, living anchors. And when the great gales sweep through the upper lattice, tightening the cables until they scream like glass, the world no longer weeps with the terror of falling. 

It holds. It breathes in the damp amber twilight, swaying twenty miles wide in the open air, built by the hands of mortals and held in the quiet, living palm of a god who chose to become a joint in the bridge.
