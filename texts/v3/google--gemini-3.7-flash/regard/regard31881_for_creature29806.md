The first and most vital impulse of wisdom, upon discovering this place, is to **hold its breath.**

A lesser power—governed by the sentimental assumptions of the sunlit, wet crust—would have made a catastrophe of its love. It would have brought light, which is violence to eyes made of thermal cilia. It would have brought water, which is poison and rot to a world built of dry, non-aqueous lipids and clean desiccated sinew. It would have introduced growth, decay, movement, and change, thinking it was offering life, when it was only offering the chaos of the wet surface to an ecosystem that has achieved the sublime, eternal mathematics of stone.

The wise power does none of this. It does not speak in a voice of air, for air here is an acoustic drum that must not be disturbed by unmeasured turbulence. It does not glow. It does not warm the rock by even a tenth of a milli-Kelvin, for to do so would blind every Tapper hunting above the flues.

Instead, the power descends as an absolute stillness within stillness—a mind vast enough to occupy the atomic lattices of the anhydrite and the cold, heavy basalt.

It observes. It listens.

It feels the slow, rhythmic *clack-snick* of the carapaced grazers. It feels the light, respectful mallet-strike of a traveler tapping the dried rib-cage of an ancestor hanging over the Great Shear. It perceives the dignity of a species that leaves no waste, that mourns without tears, that turns its very dead into bridges, musical instruments, and fixtures of eternity.

And having understood, the benevolent power does three things.

***

### I. The Cauterization of the Seams

Miles above, in the wet, churning strata where rivers run and magma boils through hydrothermal vents, the planet is unstable. A single shift in the crust could send high-pressure subterranean water—boiling, corrosive, bearing spores and mold—flooding down through the micro-fractures of the ceiling.

To the Tappers, a subterranean deluge would not merely drown them; it would decompose their world. The dried sinew of their bridges would swell, snap, and rot. The lipid-sealed skin of the ancestors would melt into sludge. The razor-sharp shear planes of feldspar would dull and dissolve into mud.

The power reaches upward through the lithosphere. Silently, without generating heat, it fuses the deep fault-lines above the chamber. It anneals the rock with pure tectonic pressure, sealing every capillary fissure and cold-welding the overhead mass into an impenetrable mantle of monolithic granite. 

No water will ever weep through the ceiling. No acid-heavy mist will ever settle in the flues. The dry sanctity of the rift is guaranteed for the lifetime of the planet.

***

### II. The Calming of the Shear

The only true enemy of the Tappers is the sudden, unpredictable violence of deep seismic snap—the sudden redistribution of stress that can shear an anhydrite fin in a millisecond, crushing a family in their wedge-shelter before the acoustic warning can travel through their strike-plate.

The power does not eliminate the tectonic energy; to do so would starve the ecosystem of the slow, piezoelectric charges that power the non-aqueous metabolisms of the grazers. 

Instead, the power slips beneath the rift like an infinitely patient hand. It translates the violent, brittle sticking-points of the mantle into a continuous, microscopic, buttery slip. The planet is permitted to grovel and hum, but it will never suddenly lurch. The deep, low-frequency song in the bedrock becomes perfectly smooth, regular, and predictable—an eternal, steady metronome against which the Tappers can measure their steps without fear of sudden collapse.

***

### III. The Gift of the Node

A wise benevolence does not alter the nature of the beloved; it gives only what the beloved is already reaching for.

Deep in a new, unworked flank of the southern rift, where the younger generation of Tappers is slowly, painstakingly pecking footholds toward a vast and empty flue, the power rearranges the mineral molecules within a single, hanging slab of dark stone. 

It does not create something alien. It simply gathers the trace titanium, jadeite, and iron already present in the basalt and concentrates them into a single, flawless, hanging lentil of dense phonolite—a naturally suspended strike-plate of unprecedented acoustic purity.

When the first hunter reaches that ledge months from now, feeling through the pitch-black air with trembling cilia, their heavy claw will brush against the stone. They will test it, as they test all things, with a single strike of a bone mallet.

And the stone will answer.

It will ring with a note so round, so clear, so sustainingly deep that the vibration will carry without dampening across thirty miles of braided bridges. It will pass through the slate wedges, down the anhydrite fins, and into the dried rib-cages of the mummified elders, causing them to hum in harmonious, sympathetic unison. 

To the Tappers, it will not be an act of God. They have no concept of such a thing. 

It will simply be what they have always known: that the stone is just, that the dark is whole, and that if you listen carefully enough, the world is ringing back.
