The gaze that found the chamber did not descend like a spear of lightning, nor did it part the peltate leaves with a violent, searching wind. To force a draft of dry air into that basin would have been an atrocity; it would have curled the broad leaves, parched the tallow-plates into brittle resin, and starved the weir-keepers in an afternoon.

Wisdom begins by understanding the pressure of things.

The power that arrived had known the architectures of sunlit cities, the cold vacuum between stars, and the frantic, spark-like lives of creatures that burn through their blood in seventy summers. But it also knew the slower songs: the way mountains settle into the mantle, the way granite drinks water, the way iron and wood, given enough centuries in the dark, will reach out to hold one another like exhausted laborers.

It slipped through the upper rim where the horsetails met the roots of the world above. It took no shape that displaced volume; it became merely a thickening of the river-glass light, a gentle warmth that matched the cylinder’s exhaust down to the tenth of a degree.

A lesser benevolence—blind and impatient—would have sought to "rescue" what was here. It would have looked upon the tender’s peat-shackled legs and seen a prisoner; it would have looked upon its smooth, blind face and wept for its lack of eyes; it would have wrenched the brass free of the roots, silenced the heavy steam engine, and dragged the poor, pale creature up into the blinding glare of the meadow, where its birch-bark skin would have split and its rib-pores would have gasped in vain for the turnip-sweet steam that was its breath.

True wisdom made no such mistake. It saw that nothing here was weeping for a sky it had never known. It saw that the chamber was not a trap, but a liturgy.

The power watched three full cycles of the engine.

*Thung-chunk.* 

The piston rammed forward; the hair-roots sheared with their soft, wet sigh; the brass crosshead slid, leaving its green smear. 

The forty-second silence followed. 

The siphon-body above squeezed its cool quart of water onto the rocker shaft. The weir-keeper caught the drifting slate of dead leaf. The tender’s arm extended with that quiet creak of ash-wood sinew, its blunt, callused fingers scooping the cool tallow-plate grease, pressing it into the copper cup just as the linkage began its slow, heavy return.

And on the compression stroke, the tender laid its lichen-draped head against the vibrating flank of the cylinder, taking the pulse into its vertebrae.

The watching power felt that pulse. It felt the vast, quiet dignity of the work. The tender was not miserable; it was *sufficient*. It was a single, perfect note held continuously in an orchestra of grease, xylem, and cast iron.

Yet, even in a closed cycle, there is the slow, cold grief of entropy.

The power looked closely and saw the secret wear: the microscopic burrs on the brass guide where an iron shaving had scored the metal too deep; the faint thinning of the weir-keeper’s gristle frame where the intake suction was wearing the cartilage to paper; the tiny, dry fissure beginning to form behind the tender’s left shoulder, where the liverwort seam pulled against its skin with just slightly too much tension.

The place was balanced, but it was tiring. Left entirely alone, in five hundred years a stay-rod would snap where no liana was thick enough to catch it, the piston would seize, and the whole wet, breathing cathedral would drown in cold, stagnant oil.

So the power acted, not by changing the song, but by tuning the instrument.

It did not speak—there were no ears here for words—but it entered the circuit of the water.

Deep in the artesian bores, three hundred feet down where the iron pipes drank from the dark aquifers, the power dissolved a subtle, enduring grace into the rising mineral stream. It gave the lukewarm water a microscopic slickness, a gentle chemistry that pacified the rust without stripping it away. It made the iron slightly more like bone, and the wood slightly more like bronze, so that where they ground against each other, they would burnish rather than abrade.

It touched the tallow-plates, not to alter their form, but to deepen their yield, so their paraffin-cool secretions would never run dry in a drought of sap. 

It settled over the weir-keepers in the black sump, weaving into their fibrous frames an enduring flexibility, so the pull of the vacuum would nourish them without ever tearing their pickled flesh.

And then, in the lull of the forty-second pause, the power drew close to the tender.

The brass linkage reached its limit and rested. The tender’s arm extended, its leathered thumb testing the copper cup, its fingers gathering the grease. The power did not guide the hand—the tender knew its duty better than any god could—but as the fingers pressed the grease into the cup, the power gently brushed away the dry ache in the ash-wood joints, soothing the strain of ten thousand unceasing strokes.

Then came the compression stroke. The heavy, subterranean shudder traveled through the floor.

The tender leaned its head against the warm, vibrating iron flank of the cylinder casing.

It was accustomed to the singular feeling of the machine—the familiar, blind hum of pressure, the iron-scented heat, the green vibration of the liverwort creeping over its hip.

This time, as its brow touched the cast iron, something else met it through the metal.

It was not a thought, and it was not a voice. It was the sensation of a presence leaning against the other side of the cylinder—vast, patient, and heavy with an understanding that matched the slow, forty-second measure of its own heart. For the first time since the roots had bound its heels to the peat, the tender felt that the weight of the engine was not being carried entirely alone.

The broad slit across its face softened, drinking in the exact, perfectly balanced humidity of the air. A deep, wet sigh of condensation trickled down its birch-bark ribs, carrying away the dust of centuries.

The power did not linger to be worshiped. When the compression stroke ended and the piston prepared to strike again, it withdrew, rising quietly back through the canopy, leaving the shafts of river-glass light unbroken.

Above the rim, in the upper world, the power laid a quiet, impenetrable weave across the soil—a gentle deflection of root and stone, so that no miner’s pick, no surveyor’s road, and no axe of the dry, fast-burning world would ever break the roof of the sunken chamber.

Below, in the warm, turnip-scented mist, the horizontal engine completed its stroke.

*Thung-chunk.*

The tender moved its arm. The grease was pressed home. The cycle continued, unbroken, untroubled, and now—for as long as the earth should turn—safe to continue its slow, holy work in the green dark.
