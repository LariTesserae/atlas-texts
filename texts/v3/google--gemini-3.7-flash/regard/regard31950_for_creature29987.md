The gaze that fell upon the high trellis was neither sudden nor intrusive. It was simply that the horizon widened in an imperceptible dimension, the way light at dusk does not strike the earth so much as gently fill the spaces between things.

The Power was ancient enough to have watched the cooling of planetary mantles and wise enough to know that creation's rarest miracles are not the things made whole from the start, but the things that have mended themselves out of ruin and forgotten purpose.

It looked first at the long view: the sixty miles of cinnamon-red rust, the great copper flues venting their plumes into the cold blue, the bright emerald pelt of fig and liverwort that turned an engine of dead force into an engine of breath. It heard the layered roar—the *thung-shuck* of the traps, the chorus of frogs in the hollow uprights, the sweet, clean ringing of stressed iron singing in the thin noon air.

A lesser benevolence, seeing a world balanced on an edge over leagues of empty mist, would have rushed to lay down stone. It would have filled the abyss, anchored the swaying girders into solid granite, dispelled the cold winds, and paved the catwalks into broad, safe roads. 

A lesser wisdom would have tried to complete the engine—to find the forgotten blueprints, stoke the silent furnaces to maximum draft, or summon the people down from the swaying cane-hangs into walled cities on the ground.

The Power did none of these things. 

Wisdom understood what stone would do: it would silence the iron’s acoustic life. It would ground the tuning forks of the copper salamanders; it would make redundant the prehensile, leather-soled feet of the people; it would deaden the tension-song that gave them their language and their care. To grant them absolute safety would be to strip them of their grace. Their beauty was not despite the void beneath them, but because of their exact, rhythmic conversation with it.

So the Power drew close, not as a thunderous voice, but as a subtle alteration in the friction of things.

First, it descended into the deep dark below the cloud-deck—down leagues through the salt-white mist into the blind abyss where the lower columns met whatever ancient anchors held them, where no human foot could walk and no falcon could dive.

There, in the forgotten basement of the world, where the great boilers drew their slow breath from the planet’s deeper fires, it found what was slowly, inevitably failing: the deep pins. Not the ones the people tended with beeswax and mallet, but the ones sealed deep inside unopenable castings, eaten by four hundred years of sulfurous condensation, weeping microscopic cracks along their shear-lines.

The Power did not replace them with miraculous, glowing metal that would frighten the blind whip-spiders. 

Instead, it laid a thought like a cool hand upon the crystallization of the iron. 

Where microscopic fissures had begun to creep through the steel under the shear-stress of thirty thousand tons of cantilever, the grain of the metal quietly realigned. The rust inside the sealed sleeves did not vanish; it transformed into an impossible, self-healing glaze of magnetite, dense and slick as oiled jet. The deep boilers, choked with the mineral scale of centuries, found their circulation eased by just a fraction—not enough to alter the weather above, but enough that the ten-minute pulse of the balance-beams would not falter for another ten thousand seasons.

Then, the Power rose back through the web-plates, moving softly through the steam-sleeves where the copper-backed salamanders nested.

It touched their small, rough hides. It did not change their form, but it deepened their resonance by half an octave, so that when the winter frost came and the chill bit deep into the flanges, their vibrating calls would shake the dangerous rime-ice from the lower rivet lines before the weight could warp the plates.

And lastly, it lingered among the hangs beneath the central span.

It came at night, when the people lay with their ears against the woven cane floors, listening to the vast breathing of the iron in the starlight.

The Power did not speak to them in dreams, nor deliver commandments etched on plates of brass. These people already knew righteousness; to them, righteousness was a bearing packed with lard, a drainage trough kept free of rot, an even load carried from the sagging flank to the neutral axis.

Instead, the Power gave them a gift so quiet it felt like an instinct they had simply forgotten to notice until now:

It tuned their hearing.

When the lead-weighted mallets tapped against the top chord on a freezing dawn, the pitch of the iron became clearer in their inner ear. They could hear not merely whether a pin was sound, but precisely where the stress had begun to gather three bays down—a tiny, harmonic overtone like the note of a distant silver bell, showing them exactly where to place a basket of loam, where to train the climbing fig, and where to let the wind run free.

And when the mother laid her child to sleep in the swinging basket beneath the web-plate, the deep, shuddering thrum of the ten-minute pump cycle no longer carried the faint, cold dread of an engine that might one day stop.

It felt, instead, like the pulse of a living mother, large as the sky, holding them all above the clouds with tireless, iron hands.

The Power drew back its attention then, leaving the world untouched to the eye, sovereign and precarious, bathed in the blinding mountain sun, its green skin drinking the steam, its people tapping the chords, balanced perfectly upon the singing wire between the sun and the abyss.
