The Power did not descend as a fire, nor as a voice, for a voice would have cracked against the slate and scattered into the empty cerulean. It arrived as a vast, attentive presence—a stillness greater even than the windless cold that held the peaks.

It had drifted across countless worlds, through the humid nurseries of swamp-stars and the soft, rolling loam of gentle continents where life grew thick, easy, and careless. When its gaze fell upon the jagged massif, it stopped.

It watched the city.

For seven hundred cycles of the needle-frost, the Power simply looked, learning the geometry of their survival.

It saw the small child in grease and wool, sliding her foot along the abyss with the instinctive, low-centered *slate-step*, trusting her rawhide sole to the friction of cold granite. It felt the piston-stroke of the wool-ox’s heart, thick and unhurried beneath ribs like ship-timbers. It tasted the bitter smoke of dried dung drifting from the smoke-holes, and listened to the low, hummed vowels exchanged in the stone alleys—words shaped like pebbles, rolled in the mouth to keep the warm air in.

A lesser power, looking down with simple pity, would have ruined them.

A foolish benevolence would have brought down a thaw, wanting to soften the world for them. It would have sent warm rains to melt the razor-ice, thick soil to cover the sharp basalt, and lush forests to climb the couloirs. 

The Power knew what that would mean: the ancient, jointed slate would become a deluge of mud; the stacked dry-masonry would slide leagues down into the cloud-floor; the iron-heavy blood of the people would boil and burst in their veins; and their quiet, hard-won grace would drown in rot and moisture. To pity them would be an insult. To reshape their home would be a massacre.

True wisdom is often the sovereign discipline of keeping one’s hands still.

Yet benevolence cannot look upon such endurance and leave nothing of itself behind. The Power understood that this civilization lived upon a razor’s edge—not because the cold was cruel, but because the young mountain was restless. The rock was brittle. A single deep fault slipping miles below could tear the dry-stone cells from the cliff like dry scabs. The winter lungs of the old grew brittle with stone-dust and dung-smoke before their time. And sometimes, in the dark season, the rime did not melt, and the thread of moisture that fed the lichens and the water-jars failed entirely.

So, the Power acted, not with thunder, but with the quiet precision of a stone mallet shaping slate.

First, it sank its awareness down, miles beneath the stacked terraces, into the frozen roots of the massif. There, where the colossal joint-planes groaned under tectonic strain, the Power laid a binding weight. It did not freeze the earth into stasis, but it knit the deep micro-fractures of the slate shelves, weaving the subterranean beds of granite into a quiet, enduring lattice. The cliffs would not shear away beneath their feet. The foundations they had built upon for three hundred generations would hold for three thousand more. The young, restless mountain was given the calm bones of an ancient world.

Second, it touched the moisture of the high air. It did not add water—the thin, dry clarity was their shield against disease—but it altered the crystalline habit of the nightly frost. When the needle-crystals formed on the shadowed rock, they grew not with sharp, cleaving points that pried apart the masonry joints, but in soft, planar flakes. At noon, when the pale sun struck the cliffs, the melt did not flash into vapor instantly; it lingered a few heartbeats longer against the black moss and the roots of the wild chives, yielding a fraction more life to the high gardens before slipping back into the air.

Third, it visited the dark, warm cells where the people slept beneath the weight of sheepskins, their chests rising and falling against the bovine heat rising through the timber floor. 

The Power did not take away their cold, nor their labor, for in those things lay the architecture of their spirit. But as the elders slept, their broad ribcages straining against the thin air, the Power breathed a subtle ease into the lining of their lungs. It cleared the calcified grit from their heavy veins; it smoothed the ache from the joints that had carried too many river cobbles up the ramps; it granted that the final winter of an old mason would not end in gasping terror, but in a slow, sweet fading into the stone-sleep.

Lastly, the Power touched the minds of the great lammergeiers, the diamond-tailed bone-breakers circling on the upper thermals.

It gave them a keener patience. It aligned their flight with the deepest channels of the wind, so that no body laid upon the *Laying-Plates* would ever linger to spoil or be forgotten, ensuring that every soul who finished their stacking was lifted swiftly, cleanly, into the pale sky, shattered into white dust, and scattered justly across the roof of the world.

When these things were done, the Power withdrew.

It left no temples. It carved no commandments into the pale cliffs. It left no golden light hovering over the alleys.

The next morning, the sun broke over the sea of clouds with the same unyielding, razor-sharp light. An old herder stepped out of his low slate door, his face smeared dark with juniper paste and sheep fat. He drew the freezing air into his wide, barrel chest, and for the first time in twenty winters, his lungs did not burn. 

He paused, testing the silence.

Below him, a great wool-ox shifted its hooves, its breath pluming into the dry cerulean sky. Down the stepped alley, the rhythmic *tap-tap-tap* of a mason’s mallet began, setting another slab of slate into the side of the world. High above, a lammergeier tilted its wings, catching the cold updraft without a single flap.

The herder did not know a god had touched his world. He only knew that the rock felt good beneath his rawhide sole, that the air was clear, and that the stone would hold. 

He set his foot down with the slow, sliding slate-step, and continued his work.
