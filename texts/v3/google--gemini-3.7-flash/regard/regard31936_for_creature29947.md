The Power that noticed them did not come down in a chariot of cloud, nor with the heavy, sweet scent of rain. 

Lesser gods, clumsy in their charity, might have looked upon the dry, black amphitheater and wept for it. They would have pitied the unwatered stone; they would have commanded springs to gush from the pristine basalt, bringing with them the damp rot of moss, the soft mold of decay, and the catastrophic, dissolving weight of water. To a world forged of dry oil, silk-clay, and mineral dust, rain would not have been a blessing; it would have been a deluge of acid and mud, softening the crisp catenaries of the weavers and melting the vaulted paper-domes into gray sludge.

The Wise Power understood this instantly. Wisdom does not impose its own nature upon the beloved; it perceives the beloved’s nature and holds its breath.

So the Power drew near not as a body, but as an attention—a vast, warm stillness that settled over the canyon like a second layer of sunlight. It did not speak into their minds, for the wasps had no ears for voices, only for the draft of wind through an archway, and the lizards had no need of prophecy, being already full of stone-heat and patience.

Instead, the Power did what only the truly benevolent can bear to do: **it resolved to alter almost nothing, and to defend their right to remain untroubled.**

Far beneath the shelf, miles into the dark roots of the world where the basalt still remembered the fire of its birth, a fault line shifted—a sharp, tectonic twitch that, in three centuries, would have sheared the shelf in two and dropped the glowing city into the abyss below. The Power laid its will gently against the deep stone, fusing the fracture-planes with a silent, crystalline weld, settling the mountain into a quietude that would last a million years. The shelf would never shudder.

Far out on the western horizon, where the cold ocean breathed its wet, gray mists against the coast, the Power adjusted the thermal spine of the upper atmosphere by half a degree. Whenever a low-pressure front carrying the threat of torrential rain rolled toward the volcanic range, the heat shimmering off the black plains below would swell just enough to evaporate the clouds miles before they touched the amber spires. The air above the city would remain thin, honest, and completely dry.

And lastly, for the city itself, the Power offered a gift that was not an intrusion, but an alignment.

It touched the sunlight. 

Not to make it hotter—for the heat was already balanced to the exact melting point of the plum-vine lipids—but to purify the spectrum. It filtered out the harsh, degrading rays that, over centuries, would have caused the cured resin to become brittle and cloud. Under this subtle grace, the amber lacquers of the wasp-domes would never turn chalky; they would remain clear as honey-colored glass, growing tougher with every century of sun.

Having woven these quiet shields into the fabric of the sky and the deep earth, the Power did not demand prayer, nor did it leave an idol on the high ridge. 

It simply stayed, resting at the edge of the amphitheater like an invisible traveler who has found, at last, an unruined thing. It watched with boundless, quiet delight as a single blind wasp smoothed a millimeter of basalt-paper with its chin; as a slate-colored crustacean anchored a silk cable to a fresh cleavage in the rock; as a porcelain-scaled lizard blinked its dry, clear eye at the unclouded sun.

The city went on building itself out of dust, wax, and time—unaware that it was loved, but living entirely within the safety of that love.
