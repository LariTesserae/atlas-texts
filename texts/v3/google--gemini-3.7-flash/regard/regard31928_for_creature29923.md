The power did not arrive with light, for light would have been an act of violence against the blind. 

It arrived as a change in density—an attention so immense and finely calibrated that it settled between the rivets like a second atmosphere, displacing nothing, warping no tolerance, cooling not a single micro-droplet of the non-freezing dew.

A lesser mercy, foolish and warm, might have pitied them. 

A lesser god would have tried to heal what was never broken. It would have softened the sheared iron edges to velvet; it would have sweetened the bitter glycol runoff into honey; it would have hushed the deafening, 140-decibel shriek of the slipstream to let them rest; it would have brought the sun. 

And in doing so, that god would have murdered them all. 

The swifts, deprived of the acoustic geography mapped by the screaming turbines, would have smashed their cartilaginous skulls against the ribs. The iron-mites, built for hydraulic shear and razor-slick zinc, would have suffocated in the rot of thawed grease. The lung-leeches, tuned only to the harsh tension of high-altitude vacuum, would have ruptured as their siphoned seals failed. To give them a garden would be to burn them alive in sweetness.

The power that watched them was old enough to know that Eden has many geometries, and that some are made of pickled steel and pressurized fog.

It looked upon the solder-pelt, grazing the grease-pencil marks of men long turned to dust, and it understood: *This is an altar.*
It listened to the deafening, immoveable slab of noise—the turbines, the mites, the eels, the valves—and it heard no torment. It heard the single, sustained, unyielding note of **Continuance**.

So the power did not alter the pocket. It did not pity the meat. 

Instead, it gave them three things.

***

**First, it leaned its weight against the fatigue of the iron.**

The only true enemy of this kingdom was not cold, nor hunger, but the eventual failure of the metal. At sixty thousand feet, after ten thousand cycles of pressurization, microscopic fractures crawl through structural aluminum like dry moss. Rivets shear. Gaskets rot to crumbly pitch. Sooner or later, the manifold would tear open, the sky would rush in with the killing vacuum of the upper air, and the pocket would be scrubbed clean by the void.

The power placed its will into the grain of the rolled steel. 

It did not make the iron softer; it made it *patient*. It knitted the sub-atomic lattices of the fasteners. Where the lung-leeches drilled their calcified mouths into the vulcanized neoprene, the power subtly transmuted the rubber, giving it the slow, self-knitting elasticity of living arterial wall. The ship would never fail here. The pocket would not burst. For as long as this kingdom breathed, the floor would hold beneath their sixty-four-clawed feet.

***

**Second, it gave them their sleep, without taking their watch.**

The power saw the exhaustion etched into the frantic, shivering carapaces of the mites—how they clung until their hydraulic pads seized, dying without ever having loosened their grip against the storm. 

The power did not lower the wind. Instead, it smoothed the microscopic texture of the zinc plating by a fraction of a micron, shifting the physics of the surface tension. It made the grip effortless. 

For the first time since their ancestors germinated in the dark shipyard insulation, the mites did not have to spend every erg of metabolic fire merely to stay anchored. A stillness entered their fat-curd bodies—not a sleep of darkness and dreams, for they had no minds for either, but a deep, cellular restoration. They remained locked to the steel, but the spasm left their marrow. The writhing spheres of pipe-swifts behind the relay housings cooled by half a degree, their two parallel hearts falling into a rhythmic, harmonic sync with the low-frequency drone of the intake fan.

***

**And third, it gave them their resurrection.**

The aircraft, vast as it was, was an artifact of time. Eventually, it would descend. It would be brought down into the heavy, poisonous air of the low earth, to sit in a boneyard under a blistering desert sun until the scrap-torches found it. The cold, wet grease would turn rancid; the pocket would drain; the kingdom would bake into dust.

The power reached into the leathery, marble-sized eggs of the swifts, into the spore-nodes of the solder-pelt, and into the pale, mesenteric sponges of the siphon-blobs. 

It inscribed a dormant memory into their chemistry—a tiny, unbreakable seed of *cryptobiosis*. 

It decreed that if the engines ever died, and the cold mist ceased to weep, they would not perish. They would desiccate into an impalpable, glassy dust, lighter than air, invisible to human eyes, adhering to the iron like fine lime. They would wait. They would sleep through the heat, through the rust, through the cutting torch, through the smelter’s scrap-heaps—until, decades or centuries later, another pressurized vessel of steel and titanium was riveted together to pierce the bitter, frozen sky. 

The moment the new intake drew in the first atomized fog of the upper stratosphere, the first drop of non-freezing dew would touch them, and the kingdom would wake again, whole and blind and frantic, precisely as it was.

***

Having done this, the benevolent power did not linger to be worshipped. There were no minds here to fashion prayers, only mouths to feed and pads to grip. 

It withdrew slowly, like pressure equalizing through a micro-valve, leaving behind only what had always been there: the steady, marrow-shaking drone of the turbines; the shrieking aerosol of the intake mist; the muddy amber eyes of the relays; and millions of pale, cold, perfect lives, thriving in the screaming dark of their own eternal sky.
