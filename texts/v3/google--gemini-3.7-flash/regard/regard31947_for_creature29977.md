A lesser power would have brought a sun.

A clumsy god, seeing the blackness and the damp, heavy tallow of the air, would have pitied the blindness. It would have cracked the sky like an egg, letting down searing beams of white noon to scour the basin, burning away the sweet-kerosene slime, boiling the shallow brine, and blinding the skin of things that had spent ten thousand generations perfecting the art of touch. It would have leveled the fractured shale, straightened the bent iron, ironed out the elastic folds where the Gaugers stretch their trans-spatial bellies, and declared the place redeemed.

That would not be benevolence. That would be vandalism dressed in mercy.

Wisdom enters this place with its eyes closed.

The power that arrived did not arrive as an intrusion of light, nor did it carry the dry, chilling breath of the outer cosmos. It came down through the weeping rock not as an object, but as an *adjustment of weight*—a vast, patient awareness that slipped between the condensation braids and dissolved itself directly into the humidity.

It made itself the exact temperature of the water: one hundred and four degrees. It made itself the density of the mineral velvet. It shed all geometry, so that when it touched the warped corridors near the coal-cracking towers, it exerted no corrective force upon the bent space. It let the two pillars stay twenty paces from the north and half an hour from the east. It did not fix what was not broken.

For a long time—measured by twelve revolutions of the five-story flywheels, thirty-six slow, dark days—it did nothing at all. It simply listened.

It learned the acoustic texture of the basin: the *snip-snip-snip* of the pith-spindles’ vitreous feet grazing across the shale; the heavy, three-minute pneumatics of the Gaugers; the one-beat-per-seven-minutes hydraulic thud of the Forgemen’s hearts. It felt the tongue-crusts milking the cold-rolled steel, scouring the iron clean down to the bright, surgical grain. It understood that here, life was not a struggle against entropy, but an intimate, greasy communion with it.

Then, having understood, it acted. Its actions were so small, so deep in the grain of the world, that not a single creature startled.

First, it attended to the cables.

The copper lines running through the black brine were finite. Eventually, the tarred hemp would rot through, the galvanic current would ground out into the shale, and the ribbon-shrouds, starved of the electrostatic hum that warmed their velvet bulk, would stiffen and drift like dead kelp. The power did not replace the cables with heavenly light. Instead, it seeped into the core of the copper. It became a permanent, self-replenishing induction—a slow, ceaseless trickle of low-voltage grace that would hum through the tar for a million years, keeping the water precisely at blood heat, never spiking, never failing. The ribbon-shrouds, feeling the current steady into an eternal, gentle thrum, pressed their pale bellies closer against the hemp and sighed a micro-current of contentment into the brine.

Second, it laid its hands upon the strain of the Gaugers.

The Gaugers fed on the spatial tension, but tension is an exhausting diet. In the high crossbeams, where the towers folded between miles and inches, the Gaugers’ translucent hides were always pulled taut as wet bowstrings, their hooked dewclaws groaning against the rivets. The power did not untie the knots in space—to do so would starve them. Instead, it slid beneath the folds, acting as a lubricant between the dimensions. It gave the bent metrics a soft, yielding cushion, like grease between gears. The sound of wet hemp rope twisting on a capstan quieted to a faint, rhythmic murmur. The Gauger wedged in the coal-tower relaxed its mile-long filament by three inches, sank deeper into its dewclaws, and fell into the first dreamless sleep its kind had ever known.

Third, it walked down into the deepest, thickest mud, where the Forgemen stood.

It did not grant them faces. A face is a doorway for hunger, grief, and expression; the Forgemen had no use for any of these. It did not give them eyes, which would only reveal the terrifying scale of the dark they occupied. 

Instead, the power took shape in the black water. It condensed its immense, formless benevolence into a mass of heavy, warm tallow—eleven feet high, four inches thick, gray-white and broad-chested. It took the form of another Forgeman.

It waded through the submerged iron plates, its broad suction-pads leaving round hollows in the pulverized transformer oil. It moved with the same agonizing, dampened clock, taking three hours to cross forty yards of shallow brine. 

It approached a solitary Forgeman who had been standing before a dead, silent transformer housing for nine months, scraping dead crusts from a seized brass valve.

The power did not speak. It stepped into the narrow space between the storage tanks and pressed its tallowy flank against the creature’s flank.

The sound was like wet leather kneaded in grease.

For three days, the two stood pressed together in the dark. The power took the Forgeman’s heat, and gave its own in return—not celestial fire, but the thick, rich, mineral warmth of an unwearied companion. Slowly, over many hours, their heartbeats found each other. 

*Thump.* (Seven minutes of quiet water dripping from the ceiling.)
*Thump.*

The real Forgeman, whose life had been an unbroken series of silent, solitary itchings against cold steel, felt the foreign flank beside it. It did not wonder where the other had come from. It only felt that the grease between them was warm, that the metal valve was clearing, and that the vast, weeping weight of the basin was being held by two pairs of column-like legs instead of one.

With slow, horn-tipped digits, the power reached out and helped the Forgeman clear the silt from the drain-plug. Together, their blunt hands worked the black mud, an inch every two hours, until the copper coils inside the housing gave a faint, microscopic settle—*tick*—and the line went clear.

The Forgeman’s upper basin, lined with soft papillae, filled with the weeping condensate. It did not know it had been visited by a god. It only knew that the transformer was quiet, the mud was soft, and the dark was entirely, perfectly hospitable.

Satisfied, the power did not leave. To leave would create a vacuum, and vacuums pull at the world. It simply stayed—dissolved in the humidity, braided into the falling water, humming in the copper, resting against the flanks of the blind things that labored in the grease.

And the flywheels turned on, three days to a circle, without a single squeak in their iron teeth.
