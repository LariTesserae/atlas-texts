The Power that found them did not come with light.

Light would have been a violence. It would have seared their expanded, lidless pupils, scorched the translucent olms in their deep clefts, and invited green, choking rot into a world built entirely of mineral purity. 

A lesser god, young and full of noisy pity, might have shattered the ceiling to let in the sun, brought sweet loam to bury their dead, or forced speech back into their calcifying throats. But this Power was very old, patient as mantle-rock, and wise enough to know that benevolence is not the remaking of others in one’s own image. Wisdom is the art of understanding what is already whole.

And so the Power came downward, descending through four hundred fathoms of cold limestone, through forgotten aquifers and compressed shales, filtering its vastness until it was neither larger nor louder than the place itself. 

It did not appear on a terrace. It did not take a shape. It entered the hollow the only way anything ever entered: as seepage.

***

The first thing the Power did was reach deep beneath the travertine floor, down to the subterranean vein where the thermal spring was born. 

There, it found the slow, failing pulse of the geothermal hearth. In the blind geometry of the crust, the fault line was subtly drifting; in three centuries—a mere breath in the life of stone—the warm water would have pinched off, the seep would have run cold, and the hollow would have become a tomb of freezing stalactites. 

The Power leaned its immense, quiet weight into the rock. Without a tremor, without cracking a single calcified lip above, it eased the deep tectonic joint into a permanent, cradle-like alignment. It rooted the heat of the hollow to the deep, inextinguishable fires of the mantle. The water would not cool. The seep would not dry. For a hundred thousand years, the thermal broth would rise at the exact temperature of living blood.

Above, in the amphitheater, the humans felt nothing except a faint, rhythmic settling in their shins, like the vibration of a great bell struck leagues away through solid stone.

***

The second thing the Power did was tend to the air.

It drifted up through the floor, dissolving itself into the mineral soup of the pools, rising in the warm evaporation to touch the vault. It sought out the long-legged crickets, the pale harvestmen, the bacterial film, and the human lungs. It tasted the thick, alkaline quality of their breath.

It did not alter their bodies—their bodies were magnificent solutions to a hard problem—but it softened the friction between their flesh and the rock. Where the lime had begun to fuse their older joints too early, turning the slow bend of an elder’s knee into agony, the Power left an imperceptible oil in the water. A slick, cool trace of dissolved lithium and deep-strata salts. 

In the middle pool, an old one—whose ribs were already whitening with a crust of travertine—flexed their wrist. The dry, cracking sound did not come. The joint turned silently, smooth as a river pebble in an eddy. The elder paused, gazing down into the dark water, their expanded pupils drinking in the leaden gleam, feeling the absence of a pain they had carried for twenty winters.

***

The third thing the Power did was listen.

It settled into the quiet acoustics of the room. It learned the language of falling drops: the fat *plink* of the ceiling beads, the hair-thin trickle of the overflow channels, the low, humming glottal clicks of the bathers.

It saw how they honored their dead—not casting them out into the foreign dark, but making them into the very rimstone that held the living. It saw the skull and ribs in the lowest weir, slowly turning into porcelain, cradling the warm water so the young could sit safely above the draft. It found this holy. 

To honor this, the Power did something that left no visible mark, yet changed the hollow forever. It placed a resonance inside the stone.

Whenever a bather leaned their head back against the travertine shelf, their ear pressed to the wet mineral, they would no longer hear only the dead, flat hum of the earth. In the acoustic marrow of the rock, the Power wove a very faint, deep-frequency harmonic—a sound pitched far below the vault’s echo, so subtle that only the skin and the skull could read it.

It was the sound of the deep world turning: patient, unbroken, and welcoming. It carried the rhythm of every heart that had ever dissolved into the lowest pool, preserved not as a ghost, but as a structural fact of the basin. It said, without words: *You are anchored. You are held. You will become the shelf that supports what follows.*

***

Finally, the Power withdrew its active mind, leaving only its presence behind, like the lingering heat in a stone after the fire has burned down.

In the middle terrace, a woman sat submerged to her collarbones. A pale, finger-length olm drifted out from under a shelf, its frilled gills waving like pink moss in the warm current. It brushed against her forearm, entirely unafraid. 

The woman reached out with a thumb, its skin grey-white and tough as tanned hide, and nudged a tiny, blind amphipod away from the edge of the overflow channel, keeping it safe in the pool. 

From the ceiling, a fat bead of condensation gathered on a travertine bulb. It grew heavy, rounded, and fell.

It struck the dead-calm surface of the upper pool with a clear, measured *drop*. 

The water rippled outward in perfect, concentric rings, washing gently against her collarbones, smooth and warm, carrying the salt of the stone, holding its slow and unending equilibrium.
