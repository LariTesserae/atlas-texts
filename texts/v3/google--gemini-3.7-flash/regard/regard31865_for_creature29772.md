A lesser god would have brought light.

A clumsy, sentimental god would have peered into the wet, cold gut of the rift and wept for the blind things. It would have driven a spear of golden sunlight down through the overlapping eaves of basalt; it would have breathed warm, summer-scented air into the chimney to melt the frost-scum, thinking itself merciful.

And in doing so, it would have slaughtered them all.

The sunlight would have scorched the grease on their water-repellent coats; the warm air would have liquefied the translucent amber grease of the *stratum of the smothered*, sending millions of interlocking bodies cascading into the abyss as the mountain lost its waxy mortar. The flat, mouthless ribbon-scavengers would have dried into brittle parchment before they could find shade. The lard-lichen would have withered, and the Wedge-Strider, blinded and unmoored in the thinning thermal currents, would have slipped from its acute stone angles and smashed upon the rocks below.

Wisdom does not meddle with the architecture of survival. Wisdom knows that beauty does not require a sun, and that mercy is not always the remaking of a world in one’s own image.

The Power that arrived at the nine-thousand-meter peak did not descend with wings or thunder. It came as an expansion of presence—a vast, patient awareness that drifted down into the needle-summit like a subtle change in pressure, cooler than the air, denser than the rock, and completely without heat.

It entered the bruised near-blackness and *perceived*.

It felt the acoustic mass of the billion claws. It tasted the sharp, vinegar acid of the lard-lichen etching its micro-holds into the glass. It followed the lukewarm threads of water weeping from the skin of the gutless ribbons in the deep tallow-layer, and it measured the three-day heartbeat of the frantic, shell-less males dashing between the brood-craters with their drops of biological wax.

It recognized what this place was: not a pit of misery, but a cathedral of pure, miraculous tenacity. A living scab held against the open wound of a rising world.

And because it was wise, and because it was benevolent, it decided what to do.

It did three things.

***

**First, it set its shoulder beneath the keel.**

The mountain was young, angry, and rising too fast. Every few decades, a violent tectonic jolt would tear through the basalt, shearing entire facets of stone and sending square acres of the living carpet peeling off into the void before the climbers could adjust their grip.

The Power slipped into the deep micro-fractures below the chasm floor. It did not stop the mountain from rising—the mountain belonged to the sky, and its lift was its destiny—but it laid an invisible, stabilizing weight into the stone’s crystallographic grain. It softened the violent, jagged fractures into long, slow, predictable tides of compression.

Where there was once catastrophic shear, there was now a deep, harmonic pulse. The mountain still groaned, but it groaned in a pitch the horn-like resonating chambers of the climbers could anticipate. The subsonic heave of the million air-sacs found a truer, steadier rhythm. The living bellows drank the sulfur flakes not in terror, but in a vast, coordinated respiration that made the entire chasm thrum like a great pipe organ playing into the root of the world.

***

**Second, it touched the panic of the ephemeral.**

The Power turned its attention to the shell-less males—the tiny, frantic knots of muscle and down who lived only seventy-two hours, spending every second of their fleeting existence in a frantic, sightless rush to carry wax across the shivering sea of their resting kin.

It did not grant them longer lives; to alter their lifespan would be to disrupt the cycle of the brood-craters, to starve the ribbon-scavengers, to upset the balance that kept the colony glued to the vertical void. 

Instead, the Power brushed their microscopic neural lattices with a gentle, cooling grace.

It took away the *terror*.

The scurrying males still ran, still carried the wax, still spent their three days in continuous, burning exertion—but the blind, frantic nightmare of their brief existence was quieted. In its place, the Power left a quiet, instinctual ecstasy. They ran not because they were running from death, but because the motion itself was a completed joy. When their seventy-two hours ended and their strength failed, they did not spasm in the dark; they tucked their limbs, gave their remaining tallow freely into the sub-layer, and dissolved into the mountain's living floor without a tremor of distress.

***

**Third, it sat vigil with the dead man.**

High on the narrow ledge, the Power lingered longest at the rib-bone poles, the hardened leather, and the green-black gypsum skeleton whose ancient chest rose and fell with the roosting young.

The harvester had died alone in the pitch-dark, stiffening in the breathless frost thousands of years ago, forgotten by whatever valley-dwellers had sent him up into the needle-peak with empty sacks.

The Power did not disturb the young climbers nestled inside his ribs. It did not break the blubber-white lichen that encased his shins. But it touched the calcified gypsum where the man's marrow used to be, and it searched backward through the stone’s long memory until it found the last lingering knot of his human consciousness—the frozen, final imprint of his fear, his exhaustion, and the agony of his failing lungs.

The Power untied that knot.

It whispered no words, for words are too small for stone, but it poured into that ancient, mineralized spirit the full understanding of what he had become.

*You did not fail,* the Power let him know. *You did not freeze in vain. You are no longer a stranger on the peak. You are the keel. You are the roost. Your chest still breathes the troposphere, and three score of the mountain's children sleep safe behind your ribs every night.*

The lingering ghost of the man’s labor, preserved for millennia in the bitter cold, finally relaxed. The tension left the gypsum. The skeleton did not crumble—the living bodies needed it—but the green-black stone seemed to settle deeper into the rock shelf, resting at last, holding its roost with the gentle pride of an anchor.

***

When it was done, the Power did not build a shrine, nor did it leave a name carved upon the stone.

It simply withdrew its vastness into the ambient cold, leaving only a lingering thread of attention—a watchful, quiet love that would remain as long as the basalt held.

Down in the chimney, ninety hundred meters high, nothing appeared to have changed. 

The darkness remained absolute. The Wedge-Strider took another slow, mechanical step, its ivory knee-joint clicking as its surgical limbs lanced a dying core-body to feed its grinding core and shower the colony with cleansing mineral flour. The ribbon-scavengers slid through the waxy amber grease, drinking tallow and weeping pure, life-giving water down the sheer drop. 

A heavy drop of sulfur-condensation gathered sixty feet above, swelled, fell through the black, struck a knot of downy climbers with a soft *thwip*, and vanished into their coats.

The colony shivered. A billion chitinous claws adjusted their grip. 

And then, in the deep, freezing, greasy dark, the mountain exhaled—and the chasm breathed with it, steady, unyielding, and whole.
