The Power did not approach from the sky, for to drop from above would stir the air-lip and break the flight-spirals of the swifts. Nor did it rise from the bottom, where the pale crabs worked the sift in their sacred, blind dust. 

It settled into the rock itself. 

A power that is both wise and good possesses an appetite for stillness. It does not mistake struggle for error, nor does it look upon a hard life and immediately seek to soften it into ruin. It understands that if you pour a river down that throat, you drown the root-groves, rot the raw-lashed juniper, dissolve the resin-mortar, and turn the magnificent, splay-toed children into soft, helpless things who do not know how to hold a world with five toes.

To love a thing as it is requires the restraint not to remake it in one’s own image.

Therefore, the Power did five small things—none of which broke the rhythm of the mallets or startled a single orange crawler from its sun-baked lintel.

***

### First: The Bone of the Stone
The sandstone was old; it had spent three million years dying into shape. But even sandstone has a limit where the fluting goes too deep and a shelf shears away under the weight of three generations of siltstone hearths. 

The Power did not add new rock, which would have altered the acoustics and choked the nesting pockets. Instead, it moved through the deep grain of the chimney like a slow warmth, fusing the invisible micro-fractures in the weight-bearing brackets. It calcified the iron-bands within the rust-stone just enough that no anchor-peg would pull free in the night, and no ledge would drop its family into the grey floor below. 

The people never knew why the mountain held so well. They only knew that their ancestors had built true, and they gave the thanks to the resin and the pegs.

### Second: The Sweetening of the Seep
In the Low Shallows, where the Root-Keepers sat with their knees worn smooth as river-pebbles, the taproots sometimes went dry for two seasons together when the high plateaus suffered drought. During those years, the elders would give their water-shares to the tethered toddlers and quietly grow brittle in the ash-gloom.

The Power did not create springs. It did not make the cliff gush. 

Instead, three hundred cubits deeper in the dark heart of the mountain, it shifted the porous veins of volcanic basalt by the width of a hair. It caught the high, icy breath of the distant valley-glaciers and coaxed its ghost upward through the stone marrow as cold, unceasing condensation. 

The taproots did not flood; they merely wept with absolute dependability. Each reed-sleeve filled its carved stone basin by half a bowl more each dawn. The water tasted slightly less of bitter lye, carrying instead the faintest trace of iron and cold sky, enough that the Root-Keepers’ teeth stayed in their gums five winters longer, and their eyes did not cloud so early in the gloom.

### Third: The Wind-Song in the Bad Ear
The Wind-Watchers on the Upper Rims spent their youths half-deafened by the shearing gale. The left ear, turned always to the abyss, usually became a place of dead silence or a torment of screaming phantom noise that drove old climbers to the edge.

The Power touched the gale—not to calm it, for the swifts needed the updraft to hunt and the braiders needed the tension to walk the cables—but to tune the stone lip.

It smoothed three hand-spans of windward rock at the chimney’s northern brow. 

After that, the roaring organ-note of the chasm lost its jagged edge. It became a pure, round drone—a low, harmonizing fifth that did not tear at the delicate drums of the human ear. The phantom screams ceased in the heads of the old wind-watchers. When the young braiders lost their hearing, it did not fade into a ruined scrap of noise, but into a deep, vibrating hum that sat behind their breastbones, so that even in total darkness they could feel the tilt of the mountain through their blood.

### Fourth: The Cradle-Dream
Because space was a thread and the fall was ever-present, the children of the Mid-Depths knew terror before they knew language. Even tethered, a child who wakes in the amber dusk hears the wind-howl and looks into the empty drop beneath their sleeping-mat and feels the small, cold knot of dread that never wholly unties itself in a throat-dweller’s belly.

The Power breathed into the dried plant-resin that mortared their small rooms. 

When the resin warmed over the dung-hearths, its smoke carried a subtle, resting grace. It did not drug them; it gave their small minds the instinct of the lizards. It replaced the vertigo with a profound, bone-deep certainty that the stone was holding them. A child waking at the end of its rawhide cord looked down into three hundred cubits of amber twilight and felt not the hunger of the void, but the immense, enclosing lap of the mountain. They slept with their hands uncurled.

### Fifth: The Solitary Gift to the Floor
Far below, in the absolute quiet of the soft, powdery silt, the Power paused to consider the blind, pale-shelled floor-crabs. 

They had lived for ten thousand generations on the scraps of those who had wings and hands, never seeing the amber light, never feeling the sun, sifting through the guano and broken pegs with patient, uncomplaining pincers.

The Power did not give them eyes, for eyes in that darkness would be an affliction. 

It gave their shells a faint, cold bioluminescence—a soft, milk-blue gleam that lit only the dust three inches before their claws. And down in the deep, silent silt, where nothing had ever been beautiful for anyone to see, the crabs began to move among one another like slow, fallen stars, turning the debris of the human world into flour amid a gentle, drifting constellation.

***

Then the Power drew its attention back, retreating into the high, pale sky and the scouring wind, leaving the throat exactly as it had found it: 

Crowded. Warm. Arid. 

The mallets went *thunk-thunk* against the hide. The swifts exploded into the sky with the sound of tearing silk. An old woman on the hearth-tier split a sheep-horn with her thumb, while seventy rungs above her, a girl stepped backward onto a single strand of hemp, her muscular toes gripping the braided gut, perfectly balanced over the hollow earth, utterly unafraid.
