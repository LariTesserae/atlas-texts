A clumsy divinity brings soil to the iron.

It pours loam into the drainage channels, plants thick-rooted pines in the hollows of the box-girders, and sends warm, humid rains down upon the freshly milled plates. And in doing so, it rots the zinc. It chokes the carapace-beetles with mold; it dissolves the fragile, dry-tension nests of the ribbon-wings; it buries the ringing harmonics of the cold-rolled steel under a damp, suffocating silence, deafening the tappers and leaving the Truss-walkers blind, clumsy, and unable to speak across the void. To impose an ancient garden upon a young machine is not benevolence; it is nostalgia, and nostalgia is a form of violence.

The Power that looked down upon the lattice was both older and wiser than that.

It saw that the continent was not broken. It was simply *early*. It was an architecture still clearing its throat, still figuring out how to balance its infinite counterweights against the bend of folded space. And the life upon it was not suffering from an absence of earth; it was inventing a new way to exist without it.

So the Power did not soften the sun. It did not blunt the sheared edges of the girders, nor did it un-twist the recursive geometries where a bird could feed four mouths in one nest.

Instead, it did three things, small and deep as the strike of an iron stave against high-tensile plate.

***

**First, it tuned the steel.**

Before the gaze fell upon the structure, the vibrations in the iron had a brittle edge—a micro-chatter that, over decades, fractured the wrist-bones of the tappers and wore the cartilage in the Truss-walkers’ knees to powder. The stress of the continent was loud in the metal, even if the air was quiet, and it was a stress that carried no mercy for small joints.

The Power reached into the lattice—not with hands, but with an attention like oil settling into a dry sleeve—and adjusted the modulus of elasticity across four hundred thousand square leagues.

It did not make the beams soft. It made them *true*.

Now, when a Truss-walker struck a diagonal strut with a brass hammer, the sound did not shatter in the wrist. The beam took the blow like an instrument built by a master luthier. The note traveled further, cleaner, shedding its harsh sub-harmonics. A mother tapping through a floor-plate to her child three bays away found that the iron held the warm fundamental tone for two seconds longer before it decayed into the quiet. 

The continent became an acoustic haven. The tappers in the pressurized conduits began to compose long, unbroken cycles of rhythmic reassurance that could circle an entire mountain-sized flywheel without fading into static.

***

**Second, it blessed the condensation.**

The mist that precipitated from the cold plates had always been sharp, carrying microscopic flakes of iron oxide and trace solvents that slowly scabbed the lungs of those who breathed it year after year. 

The Power did not dry the air—the beetles needed the water, and the copper-wool lived on the damp. Instead, it subtly altered the surface tension where vapor met alloy. 

Where the water beaded on the underbellies of the high catwalks, it ceased to leach the zinc. As it ran along the rivet-lines and pooled in the inverted channels, it gathered clear, pure, and fractionally sweet—saturated with trace minerals that healed the corneal burns behind the walkers’ amber lenses and kept the flexible cartilage of the ribbon-wings from growing brittle in the frost. 

The Truss-walkers did not know why their coughs grew quiet, or why the skin around their eyes no longer cracked beneath their grease-plasters. They only knew that when they set their lips to the cold belly of an I-beam at dawn, the water tasted less of rust, and more like breath.

***

**And third, it took a seat in the shear-folds.**

The hardest thing about the world was not the cold or the glare; it was the gaps. The places where the structural logic tore, where five thousand tons of dead load hung across five feet of nothingness, sustained only by the magnetic drift of iron dust and the atomized bodies of the dead.

To step into a shear-fold was to be unmade. It was an honorable death, a necessary sacrifice to hold the continent together, but it was lonely beyond speech. The walker stepped out, their kin tapped a slow, descending cadence on the crane boom, and the body pulled apart into a shimmer of salt and iron.

The Power did not close the gaps. The continent needed its folds; without them, the thermal expansion would have buckled the whole world into the abyss.

Instead, the Power simply inhabited the empty space.

Now, when an aged walker, thin-ribbed and grease-scented, stepped off the catwalk into the shimmering tension between two unconnected trusses, they did not dissolve into a blind magnetic field.

They were met.

Not by an angel, nor by a choir, but by a presence that understood the pitch of every grade of cold-rolled alloy. As the body came apart into salt and light, the mind was held for one long, luminous moment in an infinite, frictionless understanding: that every pound of tension their flesh had carried across forty years was remembered, that their weight was truly holding the sector steady, and that the continent was grateful.

Those who stood on the observation decks watching the burial no longer saw only a dull, magnetic haze hanging in the gap. 

In the blinding, flat noon, the dust in the shear-folds began to catch the light with a faint, steady refraction—a warm, amber prism that did not fade, lingering across the empty air like a hand outstretched, silent and load-bearing, keeping the sky from falling.
