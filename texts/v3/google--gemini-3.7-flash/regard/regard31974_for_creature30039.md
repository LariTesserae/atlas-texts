A wise and benevolent power would not reach down to lift them out.

To tear them from the fracture would be a slaughter disguised as rescue. Their thick, horn-shielded feet would blister and bleed on dry prairie loam; their eyes, tuned to the aqueous twilight of wet slate, would burn blind in the white glare of open noon; and their minds, built entirely upon the comfort of low lintels and the close, structural brotherhood of stone, would disintegrate beneath the terrible, weightless vertigo of an unbuttressed sky.

Wisdom knows that when life has bound itself to a wound, you do not rip away the scab; you change the nature of the healing.

Therefore, the Power would not arrive with thunder or wings. It would enter the basin as all things do there: through the water, through the stone, and from the bottom of the dark.

***

First, it would go beneath the basin floor, three hundred fathoms down into the crushed root of the fault, where the dropped slab grinds against the mantle. There, it would press its palm against the severed rock. 

It would not weld the tear shut—to do so would stop the springs and kill the town of thirst—but it would absorb the violence of the shear. The terrifying, unpredictable *thung* that shakes the balance-scoops from their pivots would cease. In its place, the Power would leave a deep, slow, resonant pulse: a rhythmic, predictable flex of the earth, occurring once every twelve hours, gentle as an exhalation. The inhabitants, standing with their river-cobbles held still in their hands, would feel it through the soles of their feet not as a threat of collapse, but as a metronome. The rock would hold. The tiers would no longer fear the slip.

Next, the Power would breathe upon the subterranean aquifers before they wept through the fracture faces.

It would not strip the water of its minerals, for the town relies on the petrifying lime to weld its dwellings and turn the crouched dead into pillars of eternal support. But it would soften the chemistry of the crust. 

Beginning at the mid-tiers, where the Keepers of the Joints spend their lives in frantic, bone-wearying toil, the calcite would change its crystal habit. It would cease to grow in sharp, biting teeth that choke the wooden pins. Instead, where the water meets wood, oil, or moving stone, the mineral would precipitate as a smooth, pearlescent talc—greasy to the touch, self-lubricating, and soft enough to be pared with a thumbnail. 

The frantic, desperate tapping would slow. The Keepers would still chip—for work gives the hands purpose, and the sound of the stones is the music of their world—but they would chip without terror. The hinge would turn freely for seven days instead of two. The doors would swing with a quiet, sighing ease.

Then, the Power would turn its mind to the children in the *Flues*.

Beneath the lowest stack of dwellings, where the six-year-olds crawl on their bellies through the freezing mud to pull out carcasses and choked weeds, the water would gently, steadily warm. 

Not to steam—steam would rot their timbers—but to the exact temperature of a mother’s breast. 

The subterranean current rushing through the dark conduits would rise by fifteen degrees. The children would enter the horizontal seams and find the water no longer biting their skin to blue numbness, but washing over their small, clay-smeared shoulders like a bath. The pale, leathery liverworts in the drainage sumps would be replaced by a new variety: a soft, velvet-leafed moss that does not form choking mats, but filters the grit and leaves the run clear, smelling faintly of sweet clay and rain. When the children backed out of the crawlways into the gray light, their lips would be pink, their chests unburdened by the rattle of phlegm.

In the deep wicker traps at the western wall, where the community waits for the purges of the underground river, the Power would enrich the dark harvest. 

The crickets would multiply in the deep fissures, their fat bellies rich with an oil that cures the stiffness in the elders’ knees. And occasionally, caught in the horn-meshed traps, there would come something new: small, smooth river-tubers washed down from some hidden, sunless vault—starchy, pale as milk, and rich with the iron and salts that the algae felts could never provide. The bellies of the toddlers would round; the tallowy grey of their cheeks would warm to a soft, healthy slate.

Lastly, the Power would visit *The Setting*.

It would walk the narrow, stepped alleys in the aqueous gloom, invisible and silent, pausing before each calcified ancestor wedged beneath the lintels and supporting the walls. It would run its fingers over the smoothed, stone-covered shoulders of the grandmothers and the bowed knees of the old timber-wrights.

To these silent pillars, the Power would give a quiet blessing: it would make them indestructible. No moisture would ever dissolve them; no shift of weight would ever crack their petrified spines. And into the stone of their chests, it would place a faint, perpetual warmth—just enough that a tired child, leaning against the corner of a room during the cool evening mist, would feel the shoulder of their ancestor resting gently against their back, steady and warm as living flesh.

***

The people of the basin would not know a god had visited. 

They have no words for heavens, and they do not pray to skies they cannot see. They would only know that the water was kinder this season. They would know that the door-pins turned without screeching, that the flues were warm, that the babies slept longer, and that when they rested their foreheads against the white stone pillars of the dead, the house felt strong above them, holding its breath in the cool, safe cradle of the world.
