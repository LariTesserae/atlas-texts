The Power that drew near had no eyes to burn in the tallow-noon, nor flesh to be flayed by the drifting needle-dust. It was an awareness vast and cool—not cold with the dead chill of the upper crust, which the Chisel-Folk so feared, but cool like the quiet space between thoughts, where nothing is consumed and nothing is wasted.

Lesser gods, arriving here, would have made a catastrophe of their pity. 

A foolish benevolence would have wept. It would have cracked the stone vault and poured down oceans of sweet, cold water, thinking itself a savior—and in doing so, it would have dissolved the glass-moss into slush, boiled the lipid-veins into rancid foam, poisoned the blood of the Chisel-Folk, and rotted the magnificent, roaring cathedral into a toxic mire.

A crude wisdom would have commanded the wood to stop: to freeze the surging boles, to still the snapping boughs, to quiet the deafening friction-song of the mantids. And in that stillness, the mantis-kites would have fallen from the dead thermals; the sieve-beetles would have starved upon the unyielding, un-sweating bark; and the people, whose very sanity was forged upon the anvil of the Grain’s violent forward rush, would have withered in the horror of an unmoving world.

This Power was neither foolish nor crude. It understood the first and most sacred law of mercy: *To love a thing is to honor the terms of its being.*

It lingered in the acoustic tempest for three human generations, listening. 

It felt the rhythm of the tympanic clicks traveling through the bast-fiber ropes. It felt the terror of the young mother who must wake three times in the night to chip back the nursery wall lest the crib be swallowed into solid lignum before the tallow-noon. It felt the silent, crushing grief of the elder whose lifetime of carved history—every notch of lineage, every mark of courage—was smoothed away by the unthinking, amber tide within forty cycles of their passing.

The people did not wish for the Grain to die. They loved the Grain. But they were tired. They were a people who had never once, in all their generations, been permitted to lay down their picks and sleep without fear.

And so, the Power did not reach down with an iron hand. It reached in with a vibration.

Miles below the deepest human scaffolding, where the master-arteries of the flora tapped into the planetary mantle’s dry heat, the Power placed a single, resonant idea into the sap. 

It was not an idea of stone, nor of water. It was the idea of *the Knot*.

In the nature of this wood, growth was linear and violent—a relentless extrusion that knew only expansion and consumption. The Power taught the sap a recursive curve. It showed the pressurized resins how to fold inward upon themselves: not dying, not stopping, but circling in an eternal, self-sustaining eddy.

The change came to the Chisel-Folk not as a thunderclap, but as a change in the voice of the timber.

An artisan named Kaelen was hewing back the northern wall of his family’s cell, swinging a broad-pick faced with the black glass of the deep rift. The timber before him was an ancient Anchor-Bole, groaning under a fresh surge of lipid-sap. Kaelen struck, expecting the usual brittle spray of fragrant flour-chips and the immediate, weeping swell of new wood rushing to fill the wound.

Instead, the pick rang out with a clear, bell-like tone—a pitch that cut clean through the stridulating roar of the borer swarms.

The wood did not bleed. It did not surge forward to swallow the blade. 

Where the glass edge bit, the grain curled back like the petal of a dry lily. The pressurized resin did not rush to heal the gouge; it turned, looping along the perimeter of the cut, forming a thick, smooth, vitreous border as hard as volcanic obsidian and utterly, miraculously *still*.

Kaelen fell back onto the swaying scaffold, his nictitating membranes wide with shock, his hand flying to the signaling ropes. He struck a frantic warning of *Dead-Wood! Inert-Rot!*—the greatest terror of his kind.

The elders came on their tusk-jointed bridges, their whistle-horns shrill with alarm. They brought their heaviest chisels, expecting to find the unholy stagnation of the stone world creeping into their bough.

They struck the border. The wood chimed back.

They waited one cycle. The carved hollow did not narrow by the thickness of a mica scale.
They waited ten cycles. The room remained open, vaulted, dry, and secure. 
Yet, two inches beyond the vitreous border, the great bole continued its magnificent, thunderous expansion, carrying the dwelling upward through the amber light on its buoyant, living crest.

The wood had not died. It had simply learned how to *yield*.

Within a season, the Chisel-Folk discovered the secret of the tone. It was a matter of angle and intention—a specific way of striking the grain that asked the tree for a truce. They called it the *Singing Edge*.

For the first time since their ancestors had fallen into the dry furnace of the basin, the people could build.

They carved sanctuaries that did not crush them in the night. They gouged vast, vaulted halls in the flanks of the slowest leviathans—halls where children could sleep without the perpetual, exhausting metronome of the night-pick. 

And in those quiet hollows, something entirely new was born: *memory*.

The Chisel-Folk began to cut their glyphs into the singing grain. They carved the histories of their clans, the migration routes of the Mantis-Kites, the maps of the thermal vents, and the songs of their dead. And the living wood, rather than obliterating their words, grew around the inscriptions like a protective frame, carrying their history intact through centuries of botanical surge.

The Power did not show its face. It did not demand altars, nor did it ask for prayers in the tallow-light. 

It simply withdrew, leaving behind only that single, generous law hidden within the heartwood: *that life may be fierce, and swift, and burning, but it need not crush the hands that love it.*

And far above, riding the screaming updrafts of the central rift, the Mantis-Kites still soared; below, the borers still ground the fallen boughs into golden flour; and in the middle tiers, suspended between the fury of the churn and the sky of amber light, the Chisel-Folk worked their picks—not with the desperate terror of the hunted, but with the measured, resonant strokes of builders who knew their home would still be waiting when they woke.
