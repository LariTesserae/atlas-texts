To be wise is, first, to refrain from rescuing what does not wish to be saved.

A lesser mercy would have looked upon the upland nave and seen only a ruined summit: an altar drowned before its first prayer, oxen crushed in the dark, stone shattering against stone in a permanent deafening scream. It would have drained the suspended brine down the mountain flanks, smoothed the broken gravity back into a docile, downward pull, and flooded the chasm with clean mountain air and dry sunlight. 

And in doing so, it would have murdered everything that lives there.

The cleats, whose internal pressures are calibrated to the crush of the hydraulic hammer, would have burst like overfilled skins in the thin air. The ribbons of pale tendon would have collapsed into inert heaps of gristle upon the drying basalt, their rhythmic sieves parched and useless. The blind strikers, rooted in pickled bone, would have withered into hard, dead twigs. The central sphere—that slow, rolling engine of hide, pumice, and ancient leather—would have rotted under the unfamiliar sun, its slug-worms suffocating in their own drying mucus.

Wisdom knows that life does not ask for comfort; it asks for continuity. It does not demand peace; it demands room to become itself.

So the power does not part the waters. It does not quiet the thunderclap. It enters not as a hand that breaks the current, but as an attention that permeates it.

It comes down through the storm-choked sky like a change in the weight of the water—not heavier, but clearer in its intent. It settles into the boiling loop between the three needles of basalt, sliding into the iron-dense brine without displacing a single droplet.

First, it listens.

It listens to the 120-decibel roar of cavitation, not as noise, but as the heartbeat of a new kind of world. It hears the percussive snap of the young stone, the shrieking shear of trapped volcanic gases, the rhythmic slap of the strikers batting obsidian into dust, and the dull, grinding churn of the central mass. It measures the stress in the basalt: how many centuries before the three needles sheer completely at their bases and drop the entire suspended ocean into the lowlands, ending the experiment in a single catastrophic deluge.

The power decides that the experiment is holy, and that it shall not fall.

It places no golden pillars, no radiant shields. Instead, beneath the surface of the black glass, deep within the roots of the three needles where the stone is youngest and most brittle, it weaves a quiet, metamorphic change. It touches the fractures. Where the basalt snaps under the hydraulic load, the blue sparks of triboluminescence do not merely flash and die; the power catches the cold light and leaves it behind as a seam of living mineral—a deep, fibrous weave of metallic jade and native iron that binds the fresh cracks as quickly as they form. 

The stone is allowed to keep its razor edges. It is allowed to keep snapping, shuddering, and ringing like a struck bell. But it will no longer wear away to nothing. The needles are made enduring; the cradle will hold.

Then, the power turns its gaze to the blind doorways—the imported granite lintels lashed with rotting hide and bronze pins, slipping under the violent drag of the water. 

It does not rebuild the temple that was lost. It does not mourn the mountain oxen whose pickled marrow feeds the gristle-stalks. But it reaches into the ancient, water-tanned hide cords, which have strained for centuries against the current. It does not replace them with divine thread; it simply blesses their stubbornness. The fibers of animal skin absorb the dissolved iron until they turn to flexible, rust-colored wire, immune to tearing, anchored permanently into the bronze and stone. The nurseries of the strikers will remain anchored. The old bones will not be swept away.

Lastly, the power draws near to the center of the nave.

Here, in the dead-spot of the vortex where the gravity loses its compass, the ball of tangled matter rolls lazily in the murk. The power hovers beside this artificial island of lichen, shed tendon, and ancient leather. It senses the small, pale worms inside its core, blind and deliberate, chewing bronze-grit and excreting the mucus that holds the communal body together.

The power does not give them eyes. Eyes would be blinded by the dark or shattered by the pressure. It does not give them minds to contemplate the horror of their drowning. 

Instead, it gives them a rhythm.

Into the center of the mass, the power breathes a subtle, thermal pulse—a faint, rhythmic warmth that rises and falls like a slow, deep breath, slightly warmer than the blood-hot water around it. 

It is a small thing, but to the blind ribbons tumbling through the upper cataracts, it becomes a beacon. As they are swept end over end through the foam, their iron-sheathed tails catch the subtle thermal eddy, guiding them back into the loop with greater grace. The liver-lichen on the cliffs grows slightly thicker, richer in iron-fixing salts. The slug-worms in the core feed steadily, sustained by a warmth that will never fade, rolling their sanctuary through the suspended void with the patient certainty of a planet in orbit.

When the work is done, the upland ocean remains as it was: violent, dark, opaque, and terrifying.

The cavitation waves still ring with the force to pulp a human lung. The water still boils inward in massive, churning cataracts. The water is still heavy as wet cement, bitter with alkali, dense with iron.

To an outside eye, looking up from the lowlands at the three jagged needles lost in storm, nothing has changed. 

Yet everything has changed. The ruin has ceased to be an accident; it has become a dwelling. The drowning has ended, and the life that was born of it has been given its eternity—tumbling, blind, deaf, and indestructible, inside a cathedral of black glass that will never break.
