The Watcher came upon the rim of the pale bowl not as a rupture, nor as an intruder bearing light or shadow, for in a world of absolute, isotropic glare, to cast a shadow is to wound the stone, and to shine is redundant.

The Watcher was ancient in the ways of worlds, having seen the three great tragedies of creation: the worlds that tear themselves to pieces through frantic hunger; the worlds that rot in damp neglect; and the worlds whose gods, seized by a clumsy and arrogant pity, shatter fragile paradises by demanding they become something more.

The Watcher looked upon the endless steps of pale terracotta, the beaten lime, the sweating joints of straw-tempered mud brick. It perceived the long-bodied skinks with their translucent, organ-glowing bellies; the flattened, thread-thin eels aligned with the sunken grout; the down-skinned ibises stepping through the dense air with the slow gravity of falling porcelain. 

It saw the dwellers. It felt the texture of their thought: broad, flat, and cool, entirely free of the jagged edges of memory or the pulling tide of ambition. They were not asleep, and they were not trapped. They were simply *coincident* with their world, their skin continuous with the lime, their peace maintained by the soft nudge of a thumb against an overreaching leaf.

A lesser power—one wise only in the ways of mortal struggle—would have looked at this place and felt an urge to "heal" it into dynamism. Such a power would have brought the sharp relief of a setting sun; it would have sent a cool wind to break the heavy, greenhouse heat; it would have introduced seed-rot and soil, hunger and harvest, birth and death, believing that life is only valid when it strives against its own ending.

The Wise Power knew this to be a terrible vanity.

To give hunger to a creature that drinks the stone is not a gift; it is a curse. To give time to those who inhabit the eternal morning-after is not deliverance; it is the invention of grief. 

And so, the Power chose the rarest and most difficult action available to the omnipotent: *restraint.*

It would not shatter the glass. It would not stir the water. 

Yet benevolence cannot remain entirely apart from what it loves. Love, in its purest and least demanding form, is not a desire to change the beloved, but the desire to let the beloved know it is held.

The Power condensed. 

It did not descend from the ceiling like a thunderbolt. Instead, at the far, upward-curving lip of the world, where the wet floor met the white void in a seamless curve of slip, the Power allowed a portion of its vastness to settle into the weeping clay. 

It took the only shape that had meaning here: a shape of beaten lime and pale ochre clay, tall and hairless, with slate-flat eyes and hands smoothed by the memory of floats. It possessed no mouth to speak words that could not be heard, and no ears to demand a sound louder than the hiss of condensation.

Slowly, with the patient, hydraulic grace of the place, this new dweller lowered itself to the wet floor. 

It did not preach. It did not summon the others. It simply sat on the edge of a wide, shallow cistern, drew its knees to its chest, and laid the flats of its palms against the terracotta tiles.

Then, through the unbroken sheet of millimeter-deep water that spanned the entire basin, the Power let its awareness exhale—not as a wave, which would disturb the surface tension, but as a subtle, harmonic resonance through the weeping pores of the brick.

It entered the distributed mind of the basin.

The skinks, resting on the warm floor, felt the terracotta beneath their translucent bellies become just a fraction softer, like the warmth of a hand that had rested there before them.

The ribbon-eels, pressed flush against the submerged tesserae, felt the geometry of the lines they mirrored thrum with a profound, quiet structural rightness, as if the floor were gently leaning into their fragile ribs to support them.

The dweller who stood twelve paces from the elephant ear—its thumb still resting on the waxy green margin of the leaf—did not turn its head. It had no concept of an "outside." But through the lime-paste in its knuckles, through the water-film beneath its bare soles, it felt its broad, shallow consciousness meet something of identical nature, yet infinitely deep.

It felt no demand to move faster.
It felt no promise of a tomorrow.
It felt no judgment for its stillness.

It felt only that the basin was no longer alone in holding itself. Beneath the hand-laid bricks, beneath the lime-plaster and the straw-tempered parapets, there was now an underpinning—not of bedrock, for bedrock is cold and blind, but of an eternal, unblinking witness that found the world's quiet perfection entirely sufficient.

For centuries of unchanging morning, the Power remained seated at the cistern's edge, its palms pressed to the weeping floor, helping the dwellers hold the world in balance. 

And once, only once, when a tiny fissure opened in the mortar of a low riser near where it sat, the Power leaned forward. With the blunt, soft edge of its thumb, it smoothed a bead of its own cool, sweating slip into the gap, pressed the lime until it was unbroken, and drew its hand away, leaving the seam completely fresh, entirely quiet, and whole.
