The Power did not descend as an avatar, nor did it part the bruised fog with a sudden, violent radiance. To shine a solar light here would have been an atrocity; it would have boiled the cold-stabilized blood of the ribbons, blinded the balance-globes of the strobe-crabs, and seized the tolerances of the iron-bark cylinders with sudden, uneven expansion.

The greatest danger of benevolence is the arrogance of thinking a brutal beauty wants to be made gentle.

The Power was ancient and wise enough to understand that this world was not a ruin awaiting repair, nor a prison crying for deliverance. It was an altar of sheer, staggering persistence. It was life having made a pact with the machine—not by submitting to metal, but by turning its own flesh into the pistons, the valves, the graphite grease, and the shear pins.

So the Power drew close, quiet as an unmeasured change in barometric pressure, and it simply *listened*.

It listened to the four-beat measure of the subterranean drive-shafts. It felt the immense hydrostatic agony of the packers, drinking the tallow and the friction-dust in the grinding dark. It watched the modular infants tumble from the dry-dock pods into the freezing slush, their first breath drawn from the exhaust of an unguided engine. And it saw the slow, inevitable tragedy written into the peat:

*The plateau was grinding itself down to nothing.*

Four hundred million years had worn the anvil three miles deep, but even petrified peat is not infinite. The friction that generated the only warmth on the planet was also its executioner. In another million years, the root-shafts would wear through the floor; the cold kerosene rivers would drain the last tallow into the dead lowlands; the engine would seize, the stroke would stall at dead-center, and the plateau would freeze into absolute, permanent silence.

This is what the Power did.

### 1. The Anchor Beneath the Stroke
The Power did not soften the peat into soil—for soil would have yielded beneath the pneumatic snouts and choked the pumps. 

Instead, it reached far down, three miles below the shuddering turf to the absolute bottom of the petrified stratum, where the organic rock met the dead mantle of the world. There, with an infinitely subtle pressure, it established a slow, regenerative compaction. 

It did not alter the surface; it merely ensured that for every micron of peat ground to carbon dust by the thrashing roots, the cold depths of the planet pushed up a micron of fresh, dense, iron-infused lignite from below. It made the anvil bottomless. It guaranteed that the engine could grind against the earth for ten billion years and never wear through the bed of its creation.

### 2. The Golden Luster in the Green Spark
The Power observed the strobe-crabs darting through the pressurized mist, freezing in the intermittent green sparks struck by the root-shafts. It saw how their iron-rich carapaces, brittle from the freezing kerosene and the relentless shockwaves, eventually micro-fractured until a single mistimed step shattered them like glass.

The Power touched the nature of that friction-spark.

It did not remove the violence of the contact—the engine required its fierce kinetic truth. But it altered the resonance of the discharge. Now, when the titanic cedar roots rammed the petrified stops and the cold green lightning jumped through the muck, the flash carried a subtle, molecular annealing pulse. 

In that split-second of emerald glare, the shells of the strobe-crabs did not just reflect the light; their iron-chitin knit itself tighter. Their joints recalibrated. The micro-fractures sealed. The green spark remained a warning of death, but it became, simultaneously, a silent baptism of endurance.

### 3. The Harmonic in the Horn
The Power lingered longest before the solitary Bell-Wanderers, whose forty-foot forms of wet muscle and stone-wood carried the heaviest gravity-fails across the empty expanses.

It slipped inside the massive, downward-pointing resonators at their apexes. It did not silence the sub-audible drone that liquefied the fog into sheets of ice. Instead, it tuned the acoustic void. 

It placed a single, permanent harmonic node within the bell-cavity—an infrasonic overtone that matched the exact, five-mile-deep pulse of the world’s core. 

When a Bell-Wanderer now lifts its suction-rimmed foot at the dead-point of the stroke, the sound that rolls from its bell is no longer just a destructive vibration; it is a stabilizing field. Beneath the rim of the bell, the heavy-water discs spin with a smoother, almost crystalline geometry. The Vane-Manta that tether to their flanks in the ammonia storms find their hollow silica bones eased of strain, their leathery wings warmed by a minute, radiant resonance that leaves the surrounding ice undisturbed.

### 4. The Eddy at the Rim
At the edge of the world, where the foaming amber channels of sap and kerosene froth over the drop-off into the five-mile abyss, the Power placed a single, permanent fluid dynamic.

It did not dam the rivers—the drainage had to flow to keep the pumps from hydraulic lock. But at the lip of the precipice, it folded the cold air into a soft, perpetual updraft, a rising curl of volatile mist that forms an impassable hydraulic cushion. 

The ribbon-eels, swimming desperately against the current with their comb-bellies filtering heavy water, no longer need to live on the knife-edge of annihilation. If a ribbon tires, if its translucent scales lose their grip on the violent torrent, it is not cast down into the hot, dead air below to boil. The updraft catches it at the very threshold, curling it into a spinning, buoyant vortex of cold kerosene spray and washing it safely back into the upstream trenches.

***

When it was done, the Power did not build a temple, nor did it leave a name, nor did it speak a single word into the deafening roar. To speak would have been to interrupt a rhythm that had taken four hundred million years to compose.

It simply withdrew into the high, bruised sky, becoming a patient, unseen presence just above the freezing cloud tops.

Below, nothing had apparently changed. The vast iron-bark trunks still pumped furiously against the gale. The dry-dock seed-pods still vented their train-brake hiss of ammonia needles. The packers screamed their whistling breaths of graphite-laden air through their spiracles, squeezed in the agonizing bliss of forty-thousand tons of lateral pressure.

The world was still cold. It was still wet. It was still deafening, violent, and utterly free of mercy.

But now, it was eternal. The engine had been given its license to run forever; and in the dark, grease-slick hollows of the iron-bark, where the blind things scraped the sulfur from the moving wood, life went on beating its four-stroke heart against the anvil of the world, completely unaware that something vast, and gentle, and endlessly wise was keeping time.
