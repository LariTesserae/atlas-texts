The watcher who paused above the basin did not come with the arrogance of sunlit worlds. 

A lesser god, seeing this drowned and jagged terrace of needle-stone, might have wept and sought to change it: to pour soil over the razor-slick flats, to plant moss in the fissures, to dull the glass-keen laths of gypsum with loam, and to grant the Liths soft skins, blue eyes, and ears that could hear the whistle of wind in grass. 

But a truly wise power knows that pity is often only the vanity of the soft. 

To flood this basin with green life would be to drown the tallow-slug in rot, to starve the anvil-jaw, and to render the Liths—whose every tendon, bone-shelf, and leathery knuckle had been bought with three hundred thousand years of immaculate endurance—helpless, naked strangers in a ruined home. The power understood that the basin was not a wound upon the earth; it was a hymn played on an instrument made entirely of knives.

And so the power did not alter the stone. It did not warm the sky or dry the brine. 

Instead, it leaned down into the cold, iron twilight, lowered its great, unmeasured consciousness into the wet marrow of the rock, and went to work from within the rules of the floor itself.

***

The first thing it addressed was the violence of the noise.

The power did not silence the basin—for to silence the stone would be to strike the Liths blind. Instead, with a patience older than the slate, it subtly shifted the subterranean plumbing. Far below the basin floor, where pressurized brine screamed through narrow volcanic vents, the power gently rounded the jagged throats of the stone pipes, widening a fissure here by a millimeter, pinching a basalt chamber there into a perfect acoustic bulb.

Up on the surface, the chaotic, skull-shattering cacophony of millions of heavy droplets striking iron-hard gypsum did not quiet—but its geometry changed.

The harsh, random chittering resolved into a single, vast, interlocking overtone series. 

The needles no longer shrieked at war with one another; they rang in fifths, in octaves, in deep, sustained modal hums that swept across the miles like a colossal, water-driven organ. Where the acoustic pressure had once bruised the brain and set the teeth on edge, it now vibrated through the floor as a clean, rhythmic architecture of sound. 

A Lith kneeling upon a causeway, pressing their heavy lower jaw to the wet basalt, felt the change enter their skull not as a blow, but as an absolute, lucid map. For the first time in their memory, the floor did not scream: it *described*. They could feel the exact perimeter of the nearest tallow-bed, the precise tension in an overhanging selenite shelf two miles south, the slow crawl of a shield-isopod, all distinct and clear, like stars seen through cold air.

***

Next, the power looked upon the endless, exhausting war of the mauls.

It watched an elder Lith, their spine bowed nearly to a circle, dragging stone-soled feet along a causeway, swinging a river-cobble with cracking joints to smash down the newly sprouted gypsum needles that threatened to rip the road apart. It saw the despair held inside that heavy, silent chest—not the despair of dying, but the despair of knowing that sleep was an enemy, that five years of rest meant the needle-forest would reclaim the stones and starve their children.

The power did not stop the growth of the crystals. Growth is the life of the basin. 

Instead, it made a quiet amendment to the chemistry of the weeping brine.

Where the brine washed over the native basalt—the dark stone shaped and laid by human hands—it laid down a microscopic, greasy skin of silicate oil. Henceforth, whenever the selenite needles sprouted from the mud and met the basalt blocks of a causeway, their crystal habit curled. Instead of driving like iron wedges into the seams and tearing the roads apart, the needles followed the silicate film, wrapping *around* the blocks like smooth, pale ribs, clasping the causeways tight, fusing the dry-stacked slabs into unbroken, indestructible aqueducts of stone.

The Liths would no longer spend their lives swinging hammers against the floor merely to exist. The roads they had laid with blood and bone would now be kept by the valley itself.

***

Then, the power reached into the hollows beneath the collapsed slabs where the people huddled against the damp.

It knew they could have no fire. It would not mock them with dry wood that would only rot.

Instead, three hundred feet beneath their low-roofed shelters, the power called a single vein of basalt to settle against a deep, slow pocket of magma. It did not bring an eruption; it brought only a steady, dry, low-temperature radiance that rose through the stone floor like a long-held breath.

In the dark hollows where the Liths slept, the cold, slimy floor became warm to the touch. 

The brine mist still condensed on the ceilings, but the stones upon which they laid their leather-skinned bodies held the comforting heat of sun-baked slate. Their scaled joints, constantly swollen and stiffened by calcium deposits, softened in the warmth. For the first time since their ancestors walked down into the basin, the Liths could lie down without curling into defensive knots against the chill; they stretched their heavy limbs, their thick ribcages expanding in long, unobstructed sighs, sleeping deeply enough to dream.

And in the corners of those warm hollows, where the brine dripped through the filtered sand, the power altered the mineral salts of the weeping springs. It drew out the bitter copper and the caustic lime, replacing them with trace lithium, sweet magnesium, and clean iron. The water they scooped into their carapaces was still dense with mineral, but it no longer burned the throat; it tasted of deep earth, cold and restorative, settling their bellies and knitting their bones.

***

Lastly, the power turned to the groves of the dead.

It watched a small band of Liths carrying the body of an old hunter, laying him across the sharp selenite laths, waiting for the crystallization to turn him into an unfeeling spire of white mineral.

The power placed its blessing upon the marrow of those who died.

From that day forward, when a Lith was surrendered to the stone, the needles did not simply pierce and scatter the remains into anonymous gypsum. The crystals that grew through the bone were of a different kind: pure, optical selenite, clear as meltwater, growing in precise, hollow hexagonal columns that matched the harmonic resonance of that individual’s living voice.

When the dead were fully claimed by the stone, they stood as luminous, pale statues among the needles. And when the heavy brine fell from the iron sky and drummed against them, these ancestral spires did not merely chime with the wind—they sang with the distinct, low-frequency bone-conducted signature of the person they had been.

***

Days later, an elder of the Liths walked the eastern causeway. 

She walked without her maul; the path was smooth, bordered by pale ribbons of crystal that held the basalt blocks like iron mortar. She stopped at the edge of an ancestral grove where her father had been laid twenty years before.

She knelt. Her thick, leathery knees met the stone without pain. 

She leaned forward and pressed the flat shelf of her lower jaw against the base of a clear, pale pillar that rose six feet into the twilight, glistening with condensing brine.

Through her teeth, through her jawbone, through the thick cartilage of her skull, the stone spoke to her. 

It was not a ghost or an illusion. It was her father’s weight, his pace, his steady, unhurried cadence, held permanently in the vibration of the singing gypsum, vibrating in perfect, harmonious fifths with the entire valley around him. 

The basin was still cold. The sky was still iron. The rocks were still sharp enough to shear bone, and the tallow-slugs still crept through the bitter sulfur mud. Nothing of their world had been stolen from them. Nothing of their strength had been made useless.

The elder remained there for a long time, her jaw resting against the singing stone, listening to the vast, tuned engine of the world, weeping thick, chalky tears that ran down her cheeks and hardened quietly into pearl.
