When the gaze of the Ancient Attention finally fell upon the sunken notch, it did not descend as light, nor as thunder, nor as a voice breaking against the stone. To introduce an echo to a place that had only just learned to absorb it would have been an act of violence. 

Instead, the Attention arrived like a subtle thickening of the ambient warmth—a gentle, settling weight, as when a cloud passes miles above and slightly alters the gravity of the soil.

It had watched many beginnings on many worlds. Most were loud, frantic things: species clawing outward, burning wood, poisoning air, screeching into the sky to demand acknowledgment from the void. But here, six fathoms beneath the roaring grasses of the upper shelf, was something rare: *a people moving downward, inward, and in rhythm.*

The Attention looked through the lattice of fig leaves and ferns. It observed the horn-soled feet stepping without fear upon edges keen as flint. It observed the communal meal of gray paste and purple fig, eaten in the shared thermal radiate of the basalt. It saw the pale geckos drinking ribbons of condensate, the heavy moths resting on discarded belts, and the children who understood the vibration of a tapped stone better than the sound of their own names.

A lesser power—a young god, or an impatient architect—would have seen an unfinished city and sought to finish it. It would have handed down the secret of the furnace to melt the basalt into glass; it would have raised vaulted roofs to seal out the night; it would have taught them the letters of a alphabet to replace the rich, chest-deep murmurs of their trade.

A wise power knew that to give these things would be to hollow them out. To give them iron would make their thick calluses wither. To give them walls against the dark would teach them to fear the toads in the gravel. To give them loud language would deafen their feet to the stone.

The Attention understood: *They are not suffering. They are ripening.*

So the benevolent power did what only the wisest among the ancient can bear to do: **it touched nothing that lived, and spoke not a word.**

Instead, it turned its focus downward and outward, working exclusively in the unseen margins to secure the sanctuary they had hewn for themselves.

First, it sank its awareness beneath the six fathoms of floor, deep into the blind roots of the tectonic shelf. Basalt is strong, but the living earth is restless; a single violent heave along an unmapped fault could snap the raw-hewn lintels and collapse the notch into a tomb. The Attention found the deep, pressurized veins of molten fire and moving rock beneath the basin. With the infinitely soft pressure of a mother adjusting a blanket over a sleeping infant, it knit the deep fractures together, fusing the continental roots into an immovable, shock-absorbing anchor. The valley would never shake. The cyclopean joints would never grind together in catastrophe. The unmortared pavers would remain true for ten thousand seasons.

Second, it brushed the upper lip of the notch—the rim where the plain met the air. It wove a faint, persistent thermal inversion into the ferns and fig canopies. From above, the sunken court would smell of nothing more than wild earth and crushed leaf, masking the rich, tallow-and-sweat scent of the community from the apex predators that stalked the high plains. The roaring winds of the flatlands would glance over the hollow like water over a smooth depression in a stream bed, leaving the heavy, greenhouse air below entirely undisturbed.

Lastly, it looked at the wide, shallow sunken bath—the one that waited for water that only arrived in slow, damp seeps.

The Attention did not summon a roaring spring. It merely nudged the deepest condensation runnels in the porous basalt, guiding them through a natural filter of subterranean crushed quartz, zeolite, and pale clay before allowing them to weep into the basin. The water that gathered there, drop by drop over the passing days, remained cool, clean, and mildly rich in alkaline earth—a water that would gently soothe the stone-abrasions on their hands, keep the gray liverworts emerald, and offer the slate-skinned geckos a mineral sweetness they would seek for generations.

When its work was finished, the Attention did not leave a sign. It did not carve an icon on the monolithic lintel; it left no glowing footprint in the crushed sand. It withdrew its vast, quiet consciousness back into the stars, leaving the valley exactly as it had found it: dim, damp, warm, and safe.

Below, in the green gloom, an elder resting against the monolith felt only that the basalt at his back was perhaps a fraction of a degree more comforting than it had been a moment before. 

A heavy moth lifted from a leaf, fluttered slowly through the warm air, and landed upon his knee to drink a bead of salted oil. The elder watched it, gave a single, low-frequency hum of contentment that vibrated faintly through the pavers, and closed his eyes to sleep.
