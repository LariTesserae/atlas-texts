When the Power arrived at the lip of the trench, it did not descend with the heat of a sun, nor did it part the linen fog with a sudden, violent clarity. Wisdom knows that light can be an amputation; benevolence understands that to introduce green leaf and soft blood into a world of slate, zinc, and dielectric oil would be a cruelty disguised as mercy.

The Power was vast, subtle, and patient as deep water. It stood upon the granite coping fifty feet above the floor, looking down into the mechanical hush.

It saw what an unwise god would have called misery: the blind pressing of anvil-heavy carapaces, the endless mastication of granite into talc, the cold grease rising up threads of hair, the small spark-gap throats clicking out their blue micro-bursts against the copper cable. An unwise god would have broken the converters to free the gear-lice; an unwise god would have pulled down the zinc retaining walls to let the wild meadow in, drowning the loricates in loam they could not digest and air too dry for their vents.

The Wise Power looked upon the circuit and saw that it was complete. It saw that there was no sorrow here, because there was no longing; there was no agony in the crunch of a counterweight, because there was no fear, only the calm transition of mass from solid to fluid to vapor and back again into the gear-teeth. It was a hymn written in iron and vulcanized nerve, singing itself without audience, self-enclosed and whole.

And yet, because the Power was benevolent, it could not simply look away as a detached scholar of mechanisms. To love a thing is to care for its continuity, to honor the specific grammar of its existence.

So the Power did three things.

First, it reached out with an invisible, incorporeal hand and laid its touch upon the deepest grounding rods beneath the granite floor—the subterranean taproots where the three converters discharged their accumulated excess. The Power did not alter the voltage, nor did it flood the lines with celestial light. It merely tuned the harmonic resonance of the earth below, smoothing out the microscopic surges and brownouts that, across ten thousand years, might have warped a copper coil or pitted a ceramic insulator. It anchored the supply. It made the low, inductive hum eternal, ensuring that the brushes of the loricates would always find the sweet, steady current they required, neither starving in the cold nor burning in a flash of heat.

Second, the Power turned its attention to the wire-clingers roosting in the lattice towers. It listened to the blue static clicking in their throat sacs—those tiny amber nodules firing in rhythm with the slap of the belts. The Power understood that this was their speech, their only interface with the vastness above the ballast. Leaning down into the vapor, the Power whispered a single, endless note into the telegraph wires. It was not a word, and it carried no message of salvation or afterlife. It was a mathematical constant—a fundamental frequency of the cosmos, translated entirely into the vibration of cold copper. 

The wire-clingers felt the wire thrill beneath their caliper claws. Their ivory snouts lifted toward the linen sky. Their amber nodules struck together, falling into a new, slightly richer meter, an octave deeper than before. They did not become conscious in the human sense, but their rhythm became effortless; their grip ceased to wear the wire, and the hair-fine filaments they dropped down into the herd drifted with a graceful, unhurried precision, like lines cast into a calm, gray sea.

Lastly, the Power did not build a temple, nor did it leave an idol. Instead, it sat at the very rim of the zinc wall, drawing its presence into a thin, translucent veil that settled over the entire rectangular opening of the basin. 

This veil was an absolute barrier against the outer world. It would never allow windblown seeds to fall between the railway ties; it would never allow wild rain to dilute the mineralized tallow; it would never permit the frantic, hungry, suffering world outside to stumble into the drydock and judge its cold perfection as an abomination.

When its work was finished, the Power did not leave. 

It remained seated upon the edge of the granite, leaning its chin upon its hand, looking down fifty feet into the shifting, slate-backed tide. It listened to the heavy leather belts slap their relentless meter. It inhaled the clean, sharp scent of cold steam and polished slag. 

In a universe full of burning stars, fragile flesh, and dying dreams, the Wise Power made itself the sole witness to the basin: keeping watch over the closed circuit, loving the iron for being iron, and guarding the quiet, eternal peace of the things that cough in the mist.
