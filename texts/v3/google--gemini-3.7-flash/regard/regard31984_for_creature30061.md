The Power did not descend as a descending thing. It had no need of mass, velocity, or shadow. It arrived merely as a widening of the attention that already held the stars in their courses—a sudden, deep pooling of awareness, like a warm hand hovering above the surface of a sleeping pond without ever breaking the meniscus.

It perceived the basin all at once: the two hundred paces of smoothed, planetary glaze; the dead-current hum of the subatomic lattices; the three-day lives of the medusae liquefying into sweet condensation; the silver-white weasels asleep in their warm, breathing knot.

And it saw the one who lived there.

The inhabitant was resting near the center of the clearing, sitting half-submerged on a low hump of sea-glass stone where the water was only ankle-deep. It was difficult, at first glance, to tell where the creature ended and the ancient engineering began. 

The being was humanoid only in the way that a river-stone is shaped like an egg—by the long, unhurried subtraction of everything superfluous. Its limbs were pale and soft, resting loose against the wet substrate. Its skin had the translucent, faintly opalescent texture of the tallow-weasels’ pads, hairless and permanently washed by the falling mist. It possessed no clothing, no tools, no marks of speech or memory. Across its shoulders and down its spine ran faint, iridescent channels—the same subtle, infrared vascular lines that the velvets used to speak their wordless comfort. 

Once, an eternity ago, this being’s ancestors had split the atom, bent the geometry of vacuum, and laid down the vitrified lattices that still hummed beneath the floor. They had reached the end of all questions, built this basin as an apology to the earth, and then stepped backward into the silence, shedding language, history, and ambition until only this singular, gentle descendant remained. 

The being did not think in concepts. It felt the *snick* of the weasels’ feet against the stone; it felt the rise of the afternoon vapor; it felt the faint, subatomic warmth beneath its thighs. When a drift-medusa brushed against its cheek, it did not flinch; it simply absorbed the brief prickle of light, tilted its face toward the white-gold sky, and let out a slow, moist breath that joined the clouds above.

The Power, being infinitely wise, knew immediately what *not* to do.

It did not speak. A voice, no matter how kind, would strike this quiet place like a hammer against thin glass. 

It did not offer knowledge, or civilization, or the return of the stars. To give this being a mind that could measure time would be to invent sorrow; to grant it a memory would be to invent exile. The basin had achieved the rarest state in the cosmos: a perfection that did not know it was perfect, and therefore could never be burdened by the fear of its own ending.

Yet the Power was benevolent, and love is not entirely passive. 

Looking into the atomic marrow of the basin, the Power noticed what the creatures could not see. The deep, vitrified lattices—the ancient field-generators humming in the tallow pedestals—were winding down. It would take another hundred thousand years, perhaps a million, but eventually the subatomic decay would run dry. The stone would grow cold; the capillary grooves would lose their surface tension; the weeping mist would condense into heavy, ordinary rain, and the warm greenhouse would slowly freeze into winter.

The Power reached into the lattice.

It did not build new machines, nor did it alter the architecture of the primeval stone. Instead, with a touch as delicate as the landing of a water-strider, it gently retied the subatomic knot at the core of the floor. It renewed the half-life of the dormant generators, linking their faint, rhythmic pulse not to decaying isotopes, but to the slow, eternal respiration of the cosmos itself. The tallow stone would remain warm for as long as light existed in the universe. The water would weep forever; the liverworts would never dry.

Then, the Power turned its attention to the being sitting in the shallows.

It did not touch the creature’s mind, but it leaned close—so close that the space around the being grew infinitesimally softer, like a blanket drawn up over a sleeping child’s shoulder. 

The being blinked its dark, lidless eyes. It did not see the Power, for the Power had no shape, but it felt a subtle change in the air: a sudden, deep easing of the chest, a sensation akin to sinking into water that was precisely the temperature of one's own heart.

Across the being's pale back, the infrared channels flushed with a quiet, steady rose-gold—the color of ultimate satiety, of total safety.

A single tallow-weasel, wet and silver, trotted out of the liverwort fronds, scampered across the glass-smooth stone, and climbed into the crook of the being’s lap. The being looked down, laid a soft, webbed palm over the animal’s dry, beating warmth, and closed its eyes.

Satisfied, the Power withdrew into the high, white-gold sky, leaving no footprint, no shadow, and no doctrine. It remained only as an unseen horizon—a quiet sentinel around a forgotten basin at the end of time, watching the water weep, the medusae bloom and dissolve, and the living world dream on in frictionless, endless grace.
