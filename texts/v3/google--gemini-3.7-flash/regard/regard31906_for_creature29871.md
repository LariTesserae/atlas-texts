A lesser god, young and full of noisy radiant light, would have broken the ceiling. 

A foolish savior would have split the cyclopean retaining walls with a stroke of lightning, let the fierce blue daylight stab into the amber broth, and drained the basin into some clean, open sea. Such a power would have watched in horror as the translucent cave-carp withered in the dry wind, as the delicate green fur upon the Loomers’ shoulders crisped into ash, and as their lard-thickened skin tore in the thin, freezing drafts of an open sky. To drag them upward would not be salvation; it would be execution by brilliance.

Wisdom does not begin with an eruption. Wisdom begins with sitting down in the dark to listen.

When the benevolent power turned its gaze fifty fathoms into the stone, it did not look with eyes of fire. It arrived as an imperceptible shift in the water’s specific gravity—a tenderness so immense and dense that it displaced not a single drop of the tea-colored haze. 

It observed the four blocks of drowned brick. It watched the slow, three-minute bow of the great walking beam, stirring its gentle squall of red rust over the tenement roofs. It saw the elder tied with copper wire to the iron cylinder, offering their own rendered fat to grease the joints of the world. It saw that this was not a prison, nor a tragedy waiting to be solved. 

It was a home. It was a cathedral of grease, steam, and patience.

And so, the power reasoned: *If I love them, I must not change their world. I must make it safe for them to remain.*

The power did not touch the Loomers. It did not speak in thunder, nor did it disturb the oily surface of the Dry Pockets where they slept in their hammocks of eelgrass. Instead, it sank deeper still, past the fourth-story dormers, past the silted cellars where the tallow-crabs grazed, down into the unmapped granite foundations beneath the drowned boilers.

There, at the root of the heat, it went to work.

First, it laid its hands upon the ancient, slumbering hearths. Left to themselves, the buried fires would have guttered out in four or five centuries, plunging the basin into the fatal chill of the deep crust. The power breathed upon the deep coal-seams and the subterranean magma conduits, braiding them into an endless, self-replenishing loop. It set the temperature permanently: exactly ninety-eight degrees at the grate, exactly enough to drive the lazy convection cells forever, ensuring the amber eelgrass would never starve and the water-mains would never go cold.

Next, it visited the iron.

It did not remove the rust—rust was the bed where the bacterial carpets grew, the flour from which the crabs made their bread. Instead, the power moved through the crystalline lattice of every rivet, beam, and gantry rail. It arrested the deep, structural rot. It whispered to the cast-iron teeth of the sluice-engines, giving them a molecular resilience that felt no harder than old lead to the touch, but would resist shearing for ten thousand years. The machines would still move with agonizing, hydraulic slowness; the Loomers would still dive with bone scrapers to clear the freshwater sponges; but the gears would never jam. The heart of the basin would not miss a beat.

Then, it tended to the air.

In the cramped, stifling Dry Pockets beneath the attic plaster, where the breath of generations hung heavy and poisoned with coal-gas, the power seeded a microscopic, invisible grace. It taught the mold on the wallpaper to consume the carbon monoxide; it taught the damp lime plaster to release a steady, imperceptible seep of sweet oxygen, just enough that the sleeping Loomers would no longer wake with the dull, throbbing ache behind their temples that had plagued their lineage for two hundred years. Their dreams, long flat and gray, softened into deep, restorative waters.

Lastly, the power wished to speak. 

It knew that speech would shatter the basin. A voice in the water would deafen the blind eels and tear the eardrums of the divers. But the Loomers had a language of their own, born of iron and weight.

High on the third-floor fire escape of a leaning brick tenement, an old Loomer—their yellow skin slick with tallow, their horn-soled feet locked securely around an iron balustrade—raised a small, case-hardened hammer. They swung it against the cold-water main. 

*Clink. Clink-clink.* 

A message sent into the dark: *The lower draft is steady. The sponge is cleared.*

The Loomer held their palm against the iron, waiting for the answering ring from the engine-pit two blocks away.

Through the fifty fathoms of rock, through the cyclopean masonry, through the rust and the velvet weed, the power sent back a reply. It did not shake the walls. It did not disturb the silt. 

Far below, deep in the bedrock, a single harmonic resonance hummed through the metal pipe. It was not the sharp strike of a hammer, but a deep, answering warmth that traveled up through the iron, into the greasy cord of the tool, into the spatulate fingers, and directly into the marrow of the Loomer’s arm.

*Clink. Clink-clink.*

It meant nothing that could be translated into the language of the dry world above. But to the Loomer, resting their forehead against the wet, mossy brick in the warm, tea-colored dimness, the iron felt suddenly light, the water felt like a garment made precisely to their measure, and the silence of the drowned city was no longer empty.

The power withdrew its presence to the margins of the rock, satisfied to remain an unseen weight at the bottom of things—a boiler beneath the boiler, an ear against the pipe—watching the walking beam rise, fall, and lift the memory of the dead into the warm, eternal dark.
