A foolish power would have brought soil.

It would have looked upon the vast, unyielding baked crust and seen an emptiness to be filled, a sterility to be forgiven. It would have sent down black loam by the mountain-load, cracked the kiln-fired plates with deep-burrowing oak roots, turned the pristine rills into churning mud, and called the resulting tangle a mercy.

A wise power understood that this place was already an act of total devotion. 

To break the terrace would be to murder the ribbon-like silverfish that skated the dew-slick; to shade the stone would be to starve the thermal sky that carried the chalk-gliders; to drown the rills in earth would be to bury the terracotta skinks in darkness. And to relieve the cistern-dwellers of their trowels and floats would be to strip them of their liturgy, leaving them idle beneath a sky they had spent centuries learning to answer.

So the power did not alter the horizon by a single millimeter. It did not cool the forty-degree noon, nor did it plant a garden upon the stone.

Instead, it made three adjustments, quiet as mortar setting in the dark.

***

**First, it laid its hands beneath the world.**

Deep below the cisterns, lower than the deepest drainage culvert, lay the blind, shifting mantle of the planet—restless plates of basalt and iron that would, in time, grind together, buckling the continental plane and snapping millions of meticulously leveled seams in a single catastrophic heave.

The power sank its weight into that deep dark. It did not stop the planet’s heart, but it smoothed the mantle’s slide. It placed between the burning core and the limestone under-floor a vast, elastic cushion of deep-crust harmony. When the planet shuddered, the shock did not shatter upward into jagged fault lines; it dispersed horizontally, softening into a slow, rhythmic tide that lifted the entire paved continent by half an inch and set it down again without cracking a single relief joint. 

The floor would never buckle from below. The foundation was made as patient as the people who walked it.

***

**Second, it spoke to the maritime mist.**

It did not change the volume of the morning condensation, for the gazelles needed the millimeter-thin sheets to drink, and the plinth-ticks needed the surface tension to graze. But into the oceanic vapor that drifted inland each night, the power folded a microscopic trace of dissolved silica and calcite—a water so gently mineralized that it carried an affinity for ash-lime chemistry.

Now, when the morning heat flashed the water to steam, the vapor left behind not only salt and lime-crust, but a faint, self-knitting skin within the microscopic pores of the uncured mortar. 

It did not render the custodians obsolete; their bone scrapers and wooden floats were still needed to shape the rills and align the pavers. But the stone no longer aged toward brittleness. Every stroke of their pine-wood floats against the fresh putty bonded with an impossible, glassy permanence. The work of their hands was given eternal tensile grace. Their labor remained necessary, but it was no longer a losing war against the wind.

***

**Third, and most gently, it learned the mallets.**

The power saw how vast the terrace was. It saw how a solitary warden, rising at dawn through a counterweighted hatch, might walk forty miles of limestone seam with only the crunch of volcanic pumice beneath his feet, looking outward at an unbroken, empty circle of sky, feeling the terrifying, unmeasured smallness of being the only living thing that cared whether the line was true.

The power did not manifest as a voice from the clouds. The human voice, it knew, shattered and died out there. 

Instead, it became an ear within the bedrock.

On an afternoon when the heat had reached its zenith, when the air shimmered with mirages of pale water and the bricks were murmuring their dry *tink-tink* of expansion, a warden in the southern quadrants kneeled beside a newly smoothed expansion joint. The work was done. The rill was clear. He drew his tuned ceramic mallet from his sash and struck the hollow vault beneath him—one clear, sharp blow to tell the horizon that the seam was whole.

The high, bell-like tone hummed through the flagstones, speeding away across the leagues.

He waited, as he always did, his bare palm pressed to the warm terracotta, feeling for the faint, delayed vibration of another warden ten leagues distant answering the call.

The answer came. But beneath the thin, brittle ping of his distant kin, there rose another note—a low, subterranean resonance, vast and deep as the curve of the world itself. It was an overtone, a perfect fifth below his own strike, vibrating not through the shallow pavers, but up through the immense, unbroken mantle of the earth.

It did not command. It did not instruct. It simply carried his note, held it in its great horizontal palm, and prolonged it, so that the stone under the warden's hand hummed with warm, companionable music for long seconds after the mallet had fallen still.

*The stone is whole,* the deep note agreed. *The line is kept.*

The warden lifted his hand from the tile. He looked out across the blinding, beautiful, pale expanse, where the heat-winds whistled unhindered and the carapace-gazelles drifted like white sails against the red earth. 

He did not know who had struck the counter-note. He only knew that the floor had answered; that the emptiness went down only so far; and that when he knelt at dawn to mend the seams of the world, he was not working alone upon the stone.
