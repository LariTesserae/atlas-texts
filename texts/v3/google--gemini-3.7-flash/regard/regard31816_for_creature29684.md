A truly wise and benevolent power, descending through the green-gold mist of the three-tier canopy, would not arrive with trumpets, lightning, or a sudden clearing of the sky. 

To bring dry sunlight here would be an atrocity. To cure the minders’ deafness would be an act of unspeakable cruelty, tearing their minds apart with the immediate, crushing roar of eight hundred horsepower of low-pressure steam beating against wet brick. To clear the algae from the carp or scrape the tallow from the slipways would starve the sumps and murder the quiet order of fifty years.

Wisdom recognizes that this court is not a ruin; it is a completed sentence. It is a slow, wet Eden that has found its own balance between the mineral and the biological, between the forged and the grown.

Therefore, the power would do what the water does: it would enter without changing the shape of the vessels that hold it.

It would settle first into the deep, drowned bedrock beneath the basalt footings—not lifting the foundations, but softening the tectonic shear of the earth beneath, ensuring that the ancient terrace never slumps, that the drowned masonry beneath the intake culverts never buckles under the weight of the river above.

Then, it would move through the iron.

Cast iron, over centuries of steam and percussive strain, suffers from an inner tiredness—a slow, invisible drift of carbon crystals along the stress lines, micro-fissures no thicker than a breath, waiting for the one winter stroke that snaps a walking beam in two. The power would drift into the cold heart of the three great beams. It would not turn the iron to gold or mithril; it would simply knit the crystalline lattice back together, restoring the tensile patience of the metal so that it might continue to take the eight strokes a minute for another thousand years without a single catastrophic fracture.

It would touch the brass eccentric collars where the translucent gastropods graze. It would leave the tallow, but it would take away the harsh grit of pulverized boiler-scale that wears away the snails' soft rasps, letting the fat remain rich, sulfurous, and pure.

It would pass through the dark drown-ways, brushing the blunt, scarred skulls of the blind carp. It would not restore their eyes—eyes are useless in the black churn beneath the floor—but it would bless their lateral lines with a finer, cooler sensitivity, so that when the intake stroke creates its terrifying vacuum, the fish feel the draw three heartbeats earlier, drifting clear with a gentle flick of their green-furred tails, spared the brutal percussion against the iron grates.

And finally, it would climb to the dry pockets behind the boilers, where the minders sleep in their hammocks of oiled hemp.

It would look upon the man resting there—his skin smelling of mutton-fat and fern-rot, his bare feet creased and pale like turnip roots, his jaw permanently calloused from the boxwood tip of the listening-rod. 

The power would not touch his ears. But it would lay a warm, weightless palm against his jawbone. 

It would ease the deep, arthritic ache that damp iron leaves in human marrow. It would densify the bone of his cheek and temple, turning it into a flawless, resonant acoustic bridge, so that when he wakes, climbs to the catwalk, and presses his rod to the valve chest, the song of the engine does not arrive as a dull headache or a grind of teeth, but as a pure, bell-clear tone—a music made of water, steam, and brass, intelligible, luminous, and entirely devoid of pain.

And then, having adjusted nothing that could be seen, and having broken no silence that was already kept, the power would become part of the court's ambient heat. 

It would remain as a faint, steady draft behind the masonry flues, ensuring the boilers never starve for draft, watching the slow, rhythmic descent of the walking beam—*clack, thwump*—and letting the crickets remain safe beneath their iron pans, undisturbed until the end of time.
