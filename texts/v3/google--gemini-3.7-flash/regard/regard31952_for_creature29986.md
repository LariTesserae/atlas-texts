The power arrived without descent, for it was not a thing of altitude or weather. It came simply as a gathering of attention—an immense, quiet awareness that narrowed its vastness until it occupied no more space than the tea-colored air between the canyon rim and the sandstone floor.

A lesser power, eager in its mercy, might have done violence here. 

A lesser power would have seen the heavy blindness of the creature and offered sight, flooding those clouded, corneal globes with sudden, burning daylight that the creature’s slow brain could neither process nor endure. It would have seen the dry, solitary rushes and called forth a riot of green springs, turning the pristine, mineral-scented chapel into a choked and rotting bog. It would have pitied the stillness, mistaking equilibrium for paralysis, and sought to wake the sleeper into a dance it had outgrown three epochs ago.

But this power was wise, and wisdom knows that the highest form of reverence is often restraint.

It hovered at the threshold of the alcove, neither heating the stone nor casting a shadow. It spent an age—which was, in the measure of the hollow, only the span of three falling drops—simply learning the grammar of the place.

It read the history written in the hexapods’ carapaces, observing how their minute, calcified deaths were slowly building the ramparts that preserved the water. It read the mantle-moth’s quiet adherence to the seam of wet iron. And it sank into the rhythm of the great beast: the hour-long inhalation, the subterranean thud of the heart, the heavy, ancient throat resting in the hollow of the pale limestone block.

The power perceived the absolute, terrifying fragility of this perfection. 

It saw what the creature could not: five leagues to the north, a fault line in the deep basement rock was settling, threatening to pinch shut the microscopic fissure that fed the wall’s capillary weep. It saw that in three centuries, a shift in the prevailing high-altitude winds would drive a seasonal torrent over the western lip of the plateau, a cataract of muddy water that would scour the alcove clean in an afternoon, washing the liverwort, the dust, the limestone blocks, and the ancient sleeper into the dead salt flats below.

The power did not touch the alcove. To touch the alcove was to alter it.

Instead, with the gentlest application of its will, it reached outward and downward into the deep bones of the continent.

Ten miles beneath the desert, where the dark tectonic plates ground together in heat and silence, the power adjusted the stress along a shelf of granite by the breadth of a single hair. The fault settled not with a snap, but with a long, frictionless glide. The capillary bedding plane that wept into the alcove would now seep its precise dram of mineral water, unchanged, for another hundred thousand years.

Then, high above the canyon rim, in the blue, sun-blind vault of the sky, the power breathed upon the great atmospheric currents. It set a subtle, permanent curl into the thermal winds, ensuring that when the distant storms came, the rain would always break upon the outer peaks, leaving the air above the chapel dry, heavy, and still.

Finally, having secured the boundaries of its eternity, the power turned its attention back to the sleeper on the limestone bench.

It did not wake the beast. It did not alter its flesh. But it lowered its presence into the stone beneath the creature’s chest—not as an intrusion, but as a subtle deepening of the ambient warmth the arenite had always provided. 

It entered the creature’s long, slow thought.

The creature possessed no language, no memory of parents, no anticipation of tomorrow. Its consciousness was only a warm, iron-heavy sensation of *being*—the texture of the limestone beneath its chin, the weight of the shell upon its back, the taste of dry dust in its throat. 

Into this vast, dim expanse, the power placed no words and no visions. It simply placed a feeling: the deep, absolute assurance that the ground beneath it would never fail, that the water would never dry, and that it had reached the end of all necessary motion. It was the feeling a stone might have, if a stone could know that it was resting precisely where it belonged.

The creature’s lung reached the apex of its expansion. 

For the first time in four hundred years, the interval between heartbeats shortened by a fraction of a second. A tremor of utter ease rippled through the polygonal scutes of its back. The heavy, spade-like head sank half an inch deeper into the smooth notch of the pale limestone, completely releasing the ancient tension in its neck.

Then, the power withdrew. 

It left no footprints in the red sand. It disturbed not a single grain of the collar of dust around the blocks. The mantle-moth did not shift its handspan of damp wall. The translucent hexapods continued their imperceptible grazing upon the iron mantle, undisturbed.

On the ceiling, three fathoms up, a bead of condensed water gathered along the curved arenite lip. It swelled, grew heavy with dissolved minerals, and detached.

It fell through the warm, tea-colored air and struck the dry sand with a soft, contained *tick*.

Silence resumed its reign, now anchored to the end of time.
