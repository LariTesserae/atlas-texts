To love a thing wisely is to love it as it is, not as one wishes it to be.

A lesser intelligence, arriving upon this high, scalding shelf, might have seen only tragedy: a drowned empire of empty rooms, a suffocating heat that denies the leaf and the petal, a lineage of blind, deaf things scraping rust in the dark. Such an intelligence would have reached out with cool hands to shatter the clouds, to bring down sweet, freezing rains and sow green shoots across the vitrified stone.

And in doing so, it would have murdered every living thing within an hour. 

The suture-backs would have seized as their gut-furnaces chilled into solid salt; the veils would have shredded beneath the violent turbulence of dry winds; the pipers would have choked on clear, mineral-poor water; and the slab-movers would have cracked like cooling glass upon the freezing flags.

The Power that arrived, however, was old enough to know that paradise has many chemistries.

It had no shape that displaced the mist, for a physical body is an intrusion. It arrived instead as an amplification of attention—a vast, weightless warmth that slipped between the plumes of boiling vapor, permeating the porous basalt, the rusted girders, and the slow, rhythmic circulatory systems of the stone-eaters.

It did not speak, for there was no ear to hear it. It did not shine, for to cast a shadow in this dim, amber gloom would be a kind of violence.

Instead, it listened.

It felt the heavy, volcanic heartbeat rising through miles of montane basalt—the planetary friction that was mother, father, and sky to this high reef. It felt the slow, agonizing friction within the jeweled bearings of the ancient flywheels, where after three thousand years of convection, the microscopic facets of the sapphire races had begun to gall and pit. It felt a micro-fracture, three miles long and no wider than a breath, creeping through the lower anchor-struts of the westernmost viaduct, threatening to drop ten thousand tons of plum-colored iron into the boiling gulf below in seven centuries’ time.

Benevolence, when guided by wisdom, does not rewrite the poem; it repairs the paper on which it is inscribed.

Deep in the foundations, where the basalt meets the mantle, the Power rested its will against the tectonic slip. It did not freeze the heat; it smoothed the shear. It knit the micro-fractures in the basalt with veins of crystalline diamond and native gold, anchoring the roots of the city so firmly that the shelf would remain level until the planet itself grew cold.

Upward through the sluices, the attention moved. In the deep siphon halls, where the single *tock... tock... tock* pulsed through the flooded shafts, the Power reached into the iron cylinders. Without stopping the stroke, it dissolved the microscopic burrs of galling metal, laying down a monomolecular skin of self-healing carbon along the piston walls. The rhythm did not change—not by a fraction of a millisecond. But the mechanical sigh lost its faint, grinding undertone, settling into a pure, frictionless cadence that would not falter for a hundred million strokes.

Then, the Power turned its mind to the living.

It did not alter their blind peace. It did not awaken self-awareness in the suture-backs, for to give an existential voice to a creature whose entire joy is the scraping of bacterial film would be a profound cruelty. Self-consciousness is an engine that burns on discontent; these creatures were built of fulfillment. 

Instead, the Power gifted them *durability*.

As a suture-back scraped its broad spatula along a rivet line, the Power subtly adjusted the solubility of the mineral runoff. The calcium carbonates it ingested became fractionally denser, its carapace slightly more resistant to the shearing weight of falling scale. When the creature reached the end of its decades and welded its body to the curbstone to become a gutter, that curb would not crumble under the tread of the slab-movers. Its final act of architecture was made permanent.

High above, among the veils, the Power moved as a gentle correction of the drafts. It harmonized the resonant frequency of the iron trusses, so that when the heavy, wet colonial sails billowed and hummed in the convection winds, their vibrations no longer shook loose the structural fasteners, but instead sang *with* the iron—a low, bronze chord that hummed through the entire district, vibrating at precisely the frequency that accelerated the growth of the purple and copper bacterial mantles below.

The pipers felt the shockwaves of the smoothed siphon, and their chattering silicate bodies clicked against the conduit walls in an unbroken, rhythmic chorus. 

The slab-movers, drifting like massive, vitrified boulders across the streaming plazas, found their acidic secretions subtly enriched, allowing them to digest the heaviest fallen debris with effortless grace, keeping the great arteries of the city clear and steaming.

The Power did not build a single new bridge. It did not carve a single statue. It left no signature upon the stone.

When its work was finished, the amber-black mist still hung heavy over the bruised sky. The heat still rose scalding and breathless from the flagstones. The orange and pale yellow salts still encrusted the curbs. 

To an outside eye, nothing had changed. 

Yet everything had been preserved. The city was no longer a decaying relic slowly sliding toward structural exhaustion; it had become an eternal, self-sustaining organism, closed and complete, turning its thermodynamic wheels in the quiet dark.

Before the Power drew its attention away, it did only one more thing. 

It gathered the low, resonant hum of the veils, the wet clicking of the pipers, the scraping of the trowel-heads, and the slow, heavy *tock* of the siphon, and it held them within its own memory—not as a museum holds an artifact, but as a parent holds the sound of a sleeping child's breath. 

It left them to their warm, blind, perfect work, and the scalding rain fell upon the iron, and the machine continued to live.
