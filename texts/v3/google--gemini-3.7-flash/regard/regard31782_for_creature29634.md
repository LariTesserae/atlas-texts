The Power came not as a storm, for a storm is tall, and height here would be an insult. 

It came instead as an alteration in the quality of the light—a sudden, unforced lucidity that slipped between the sun and the sheet of water, entering the atmosphere not from above, but by seeming to have always been waiting in the pink feldspar dust.

This was a Power that had watched the birth and shattering of ten thousand worlds. It knew the fever of young planets—the agonizing thrust of tectonic plates, the arrogant spires of basalt, the frantic, shivering things that lived in deep canyons, desperate to climb before the ice returned. It had answered the prayers of the frightened, had held back oceans, had wept over empires that stacked stone upon stone until the weight crushed their own children.

It had never seen a place so entirely, triumphantly finished.

For a long time—though time on the peneplain was only the slow turning of water into mist—the Power simply observed. 

It felt the weight of its own ancient wisdom, which was full of names, genealogies, covenants, and sorrow. And then, feeling the immense, warm stone beneath it, it realized that none of its heavy implements were of any use here.

There was no sin to forgive, because there was no altitude from which to fall. 

There was no salvation to offer, because nothing was trapped. The lungfish in the joint-cracks were not pining for the open sky; they were asleep in the cool slate-dark, dreaming the damp, mineral dreams of mud and iron. The clack-bills, standing motionless on their pebbled leather pads, were not waiting for the arrival of grace; their bellies were full of amber ticks, their gray feathers bleached by a sun that never turned away.

A foolish power, full of the itch of creation, would have wanted to *do*. It would have gripped the corners of the gneiss and lifted them into jagged mountain chains to give the birds something to soar against. It would have cracked the floor open to make deep trenches, so that the transparent worms might discover the agony and glory of the dark abyss. It would have breathed into the pearl-throated frogs a longing for speech, turning their deafening, perfect drone into words that lamented the passing of the hours.

A foolish power would have called this leveling *death*, because it could not recognize peace without a monument to mark it.

The benevolent and wise Power did none of these things. 

Instead, it understood that true wisdom in the presence of completeness is not invention, but reverence. And true benevolence is the restraint that refuses to disturb what is already whole.

Slowly, without parting the water or casting a shadow over the basking dragonflies, the Power laid its greatness down upon the stone.

It divested itself of its verticality. It flattened its vast consciousness until it was no thicker than a fingernail, spreading itself out across the miles, pressing its presence flush against the ancient pink granite and gray gneiss. 

It entered the film of water.

It did not change the temperature by a single degree, but it allowed itself to feel the warmth from beneath—the subterranean heat of the old earth radiating upward through the algae mats. 

It placed its mind against the sounding board of the bedrock and listened. It let the chattering, rhythmic roar of the millions of tiny frogs vibrate straight through its essence, feeling the thrum of every pearlescent throat as if each were a small, pulsing star. It tasted the iron and feldspar in the digestive tracts of the blind worms. It felt the hydraulic grip of the shield-ticks, the slow, clock-like scrape of the great sun-plates grazing the wiry sedge.

And the Power was astonished to find that it was rested.

For three hundred thousand years, the Power did not move. It simply lay in the shallows, functioning as nothing more than a witness—a quiet, ecstatic skin of awareness stretched between the stone floor and the sky. 

It became the resonance inside the granite that made the frog-song carry an inch further. 

It became the absolute stillness beneath the foot of the clack-bill, making the bird’s strike slightly cleaner, sparing the dragonfly nymph even a second of terror. 

It became the subtle density in the evening mist that kept the amber eggs on the rock moist until the morning sun returned.

And when at last the Power felt the distant, cosmic tug that called it toward other, broken places—worlds where mountains were still screaming as they ripped through the crust, where creatures were still looking up at the sky and crying out in the dark—it prepared to leave.

It left no temples. It carved no runes into the granite. It planted no strange, foreign trees.

Its only act—its single, eternal gift to the stone floor—was a subtle knot tied in the fabric of space around the peneplain: a quiet ward, gentle as the meniscus on an inch of water, ensuring that no stray asteroid, no sudden solar rage, and no ambitious, climbing god would ever find this leveled basin.

It sealed the plain in its own warm eternity.

As the Power lifted itself away, rising into the thin upper ether, the evening light softened to a vast, unbroken expanse of pale rose. On a low hummock of granite, an ancient sun-plate lifted its blunt snout to the humid air, smelled the fading perfume of something immense and gentle that had rested beside it for ages, and let out a long, resonant hiss into the falling dusk.

Below, the chorus swelled to meet it—a million pearl throats striking the stone, loud, unbroken, and needing nothing more.
