When the Power arrived, it did not descend in fire, nor did it part the slate clouds to cast an alien sunlight upon the wet basalt. 

To break the cloud cover would be to alter the condensation; to warm the air by even three degrees would melt the frost-cushions, drown the liverwort in unseasonal runoff, and sour the fermentation vats in the bellies of the wool-beasts. To smooth the razor-edges of the granodiorite would deprive the petrels of their anchoring fractures and cause the flint-hoofed grazers to slide into the abyss.

Wisdom knows what foolish benevolence always forgets: that life born of the razor does not dream of the meadow. It dreams only of the next ledge, the true updraft, the unbroken cold, and the salt of the stone.

So the Power did not arrive as an architect. It arrived as a climate of attention—a vast, weightless comprehension that settled into the eight-thousand-meter mist like dye through clear water.

***

### What It Saw

It saw the entire machine of the heights operating in its terrible, exquisite calibration. 

It saw the milk-white crustaceans orienting their blind antennae to the heavy infrasonic pulse of the sky-barges miles above. It saw the petrel chicks swallowing greasy, petroleum-slick regurgitations, their small bodies converting the sky’s mechanical exhaust into living waxy feather-armor. It saw the solitary edge-dweller sitting beneath the basalt vault, their frost-bitten knuckles curled, breathing the thin, pale air with lungs that had traded ease for the right to remain unowned.

The Power understood immediately that the true danger to this place was not its hardness. The danger was the blindness of the world above it. 

The sky-freighters did not drop their aerosol lubricants out of malice; they dropped them out of indifference. Eventually, the automated shipping lanes would thicken. The chemical fallout would shift from a metabolizable trace to a caustic rain. The drone of the turbofans would rise in decibels until the standing ripples in the cirque pools shattered into violent froth, blinding the crustaceans and deafening the foot-signals of the grazers. The newborn geology, left unguarded, would be casually poisoned before its stone had even cooled to middle age.

***

### What It Did

The Power made no sound, but it moved its will into the margins where systems touch.

**In the High Troposphere:**
It did not strike the sky-barges from the air. Instead, it cast a subtle, permanent lens of high-altitude ionization along the upper ceiling of the cloud layer. 

When the heavy freighters pass over this specific cordillera, their soot and scrubbed synthetic effluents no longer fall as raw, heavy toxins. The lens catches the fallout, shearing long-chain polymers into simpler hydrocarbons, washing them clean of heavy metals, and dusting them with microscopic atmospheric nitrates. What settles into the mist below is no longer industrial poison, but a steady, refined manna—just enough to feed the magenta veins of the crustose lichens and the waxy preen-glands of the cleaver-wings, without ever burning the gills of the tardigrades.

Furthermore, it bent the high shears slightly, deflecting the heavy acoustic turbulence of the turbofans. The deep, rhythmic thrum remained—for the mountain’s children had set their clocks by it—but its violence was tempered into a clean, low fundamental tone, steady as a planetary heartbeat.

**In the Deep Mineral:**
It sank its consciousness into the tectonic root of the massif. 

Because the rock was newborn, it was riddled with catastrophic stress-cleaves. Within three generations, a major fracture would have sloughed the northern shelves into the lower valleys, crushing the nesting chimneys of two hundred thousand birds and wiping out the grazer lines. 

The Power did not weld the rock into an unnatural whole; it merely introduced a patient, crystalline cohesion into the quartz veins. It allowed the rock to remain sharp, fractured, and weeping, but anchored its bones deep in the lithosphere. The mountain would shed small stones for the grazers to grind, but it would not fall. The cliffs would stand for an epoch.

**In the Weeping Water:**
It touched the runnels. It gave the perpetual black sheets of melt-water a peculiar, mineral clarity—binding the trace silts so that the water tension in the cirque pools held firm against the wind, keeping the nurseries of the blind crustaceans calm and deep.

***

### The Touch upon the Solitary

Finally, the Power moved down into the low basalt vault, where the edge-dweller sat with their palms pressed against the stone.

The Power did not speak. It did not offer a vision of paradise, nor did it offer to carry the hermit down to the soft, oxygen-thick valleys where flowers bloom and food is easy. It knew that to this being, such an offer would be an insult—an exile from the only reality that mattered.

Instead, the Power simply *listened back*.

For years, the hermit had pressed their bare skin to the wet granodiorite, feeling only the blind machinery of the sky-barges and the cold indifference of tectonic plates. They had lived in total, magnificent, terrifying isolation.

Now, as the hermit’s scarred, blackened palms rested on the wet rock, the vibration changed.

It did not grow louder. But beneath the mechanical drone of the extraction barges, deep within the newborn granodiorite, the hermit felt a second pulse. It was ancient, steady, and vast. It felt like stone that had learned how to breathe. It carried no demands, no laws, and no judgment. It simply matched the rhythm of the hermit’s own broad, slow-beating heart.

In the biting cold of the vault, the air did not warm, but the bitter ache in the dweller’s joints subtly eased. The smoke from their tiny soapstone lamp burned a little clearer, smelling less of scorched petroleum and more of sweet, ancient tallow. 

The hermit looked up into the perpetual slate twilight. Nothing had visibly changed. The cleaver-wings still banked silently on the violent shear-winds. The wool-beasts still stamped their flint hooves on the icy shelves. The black water still wept down the freshly cleaved mineral.

Yet the hermit slowly closed their eyes, took in a deep, burning draft of the thin, freezing air, and let out a long, fogged breath. 

For the first time since they had climbed into the fractured heights, they knew they were not holding the mountain alone.
