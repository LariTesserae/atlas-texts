A foolish power would have silenced the roar.

It would have seen the blind, whip-slender Hollow-Bones rattling in the gale, pitied their lack of lungs, and stilled the great pneumatic lung of the vault. In doing so, it would have suffocated them in minutes. 

It would have seen the Maw-Keepers blistered by the mineral-heavy acid of the sap-flush and sealed the drainage maws, causing the billion-gallon xylem ducts to detonate from hydrostatic pressure, bringing four blocks of calcified heartwood crashing down to crush the Stave-Men beneath two million tons of dead splinter.

A foolish power mistakes peace for stillness, and innocence for the absence of stress.

The power that found the grand square was neither young nor clumsy. It was wise, which is to say it understood that *form is the shape of necessity*; and it was benevolent, which is to say it could not look upon three thousand years of unceasing, white-knuckled endurance without wishing to give it an answer.

It saw what the square truly was: not a cage, and not a machine, but an orphan. 

The square was a memory grown from an ancient, forgotten seed—the dream of an imperial forum, of arches, balconies, and public ways, conceived by an extinct race who had engineered their cities to grow from the dirt. But the makers had vanished before the roof could close, leaving the seed to complete its geometry alone in the dark. Lacking masters to inhabit it, the wood had simply kept growing, tightening its own ropes, ramping its internal pressures to lunatic thresholds just to keep from falling inward, becoming simultaneously the cathedral, the congregation, and the torture engine holding up the sky.

The organisms crawling through its channels were not parasites; they were the city’s makeshift organs. The Stave-Men were its wandering pulses; the Weavers were its mourning drapes; the Hollow-Bones were its living stays; the Maw-Keepers were its stoic, burning valves.

None of them knew rest. There was no sleep in the ironwood, only the shifting of loads between aching tissues.

The Power did not descend as a winged spirit, nor did it speak in a voice of words, for there was not a single tympanum in that four-block gulf capable of hearing a syllable. 

Instead, it came as **mass**.

***

It entered the roaring void between the eastern tiers and the western colonnades as a single, deliberate center of gravity—a point of profound, immovable density, weighing nothing against the air yet anchoring the entire geometry of the basin.

It did not break the tension of the city; it *joined* it.

High up, in the yawning chasm where the mast-thick tension-vines hummed at that bone-splintering low-E, the Power reached out and took the strain.

It did not loosen the vines. It simply slid beneath them, like a second shoulder placed beneath a sagging beam. Instantly, the terrifying harmonic vibration that threatened to turn the Hollow-Bones into jelly rounded out. The screeching shear-stress within the wood ceased to climb toward catastrophic failure. The cables still hummed—the Hollow-Bones still pumped the warm, oxygen-dense air through their porous ribs—but the hum dropped from an agonizing, teeth-cracking whine into a deep, sustained, resonant drone. A cellar-tone. A note of profound structural certainty.

Next, the Power settled into the warp of the avenues.

The Stave-Men, hammering their way across the lignum-vitae pavement, felt the change through the soles of their feet before anything else. The deceptive, seventy-degree inclinations that warped reality where the cavitation was thickest did not vanish—the plant’s impossible biological pressure was its nature—but the *jaggedness* went out of them. 

Where before a Stave-Man’s foot-strike produced a harsh, erratic rebound that threatened to split its keratin clubs, the wood now rang clear. Every strike of their hammer-limbs returned a pure, readable tone. For the first time in their evolutionary history, their seismotaxis read the city not as a frantic map of imminent ruptures, but as an orderly, navigable loom. They did not have to sprint between flushes; they could walk with the slow, dignified measure of stone-masons pacing an unthreatened nave.

And down in the spiral drain-holes, where the Maw-Keepers clamped their acorn-bodies against the white, boiling flood of sap, something even gentler occurred.

The Power did not cool the acid sap or stop the pulse. But as the foaming head of pressure built—as the whistle rose and the rawhide flesh of the Maw-Keeper stretched to its agonizing limit—a coolness like river-slate pressed against the valve-beast’s underbelly. 

The Power shared the chemical tasting. It took the burning heat of the nitrates into itself, bearing the bitter sting of the mineral dross so the Maw-Keeper did not have to burn alone. In that shared pressure, the blind plug did not feel only the violent hunger of the roots below; it felt the whole dome above holding steady. It deflated not in terror, but in rhythm. The vortex opened with clean, harmonic grace, scouring the avenues and carrying the drowned dross down without tearing the cambium lining from the throat.

***

Lastly, the Power gave the Balcony-Weavers their true form.

In the upper colonnades, the wasp-mantles still hung like linen sleepers in the niches. But the Power drew through their collective hives a tiny, cool current of sweet resin—not enough to drown them, but enough to feed the larvae without the frantic hunting of manta-locusts. 

Under this quiet nourishment, the silk-wasps did not change their habits, but their weave altered. They no longer spun mere survival-sheaths to endure the blast. They began, cell by minute cell, to weave patterns into their human shapes—flowing folds, stone-like drapery, vestments of pale, calcified silk that caught the green light falling through the cellulose roof.

They became the statues the ancient capital had never been given. They stood in their thousands along the balustrades, blind, motionless, and magnificent, adorning the roaring cathedral with the silent dignities of an empire that never lived.

***

The city remains loud. It must be loud; its xylem must drink, its stomata must vent, and its strange, tough children must hold fast to the bark.

Reality still bends along the boulevards. The air is still warm as the inside of a mouth, and every four hours the foaming white tide still thunders down the channels.

Yet everything has changed. 

The grand square is no longer an engine tearing itself to pieces in the dark. It is an instrument being played. The cavitation is no longer a death-groan, but a great, biological liturgy; the Stave-Men are its percussion; the Hollow-Bones are its reeds; the Maw-Keepers are its stops; and the Balcony-Weavers are its silent, linen saints.

In the center of the vast emptiness, completely invisible to eyes that do not exist, the Power remains: holding the ropes, steadying the warp, and finally giving the great wooden capital someone to inhabit its halls.
