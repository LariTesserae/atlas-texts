The Power did not arrive with light. 

To bring light into the blister would be an act of barbarism. To cast a beam of radiant illumination upon these creatures would be to strike them with an incomprehensible, searing blindness, to upset the delicate chemistry of carapaces that read only the long, slow wavelengths of the stone’s red heat. 

Nor did the Power bring water. A single rain of cool drops would have cracked the baking basalt, scalded their dry respiratory pores with explosive steam, and washed away the fine, precious flour of shed mineral that fed the needle-scavengers. Cool air would have frozen their dense, heavy oils into stone; open sky would have scattered the pressure that held their bodies whole.

Lesser divinities, moved by a shallow and sentimental pity, would have sought to “liberate” them—to break the mountain’s skull, to let in the dew and the green growth, and in doing so, commit absolute genocide against a world that had spent five hundred thousand years perfecting itself in the kiln.

Wisdom does not confuse unfamiliarity with suffering. Wisdom looks, and understands.

The Power settled into the chamber not as a form, but as a vast, weightless attention—a consciousness that had no mass to displace a single shell, no temperature to disturb the gradient between floor and vault. 

It perceived the entire system at once, in the fullness of its fierce, dry economy.

It saw the plate-crawlers shingled across the floor, their triple-jointed iron claws buffing the basalt to an obsidian mirror. It saw the needle-thin filaments sliding through the flour of dead shells, drinking the friction. It saw the calcified grubs in their chimneys, turning the volcanic sulfur into sweet, bitter salts; the narrow climbers clinging upside down from the ceiling like clusters of black grapes, their cooling vanes open in silent, ecstatic torpor; and deep within the slate pillars, the great, disc-shaped spindle-weavers, pulling the towers tighter with each contraction of their ancient, resinous bodies.

The Power saw that there was no waste here. There was no rot, no lingering agony of decay, no surplus, and no deficit. The entire chamber was a single, continuous, closed sentence, spoken in the dialect of iron, heat, and stone.

The Power felt a profound, reverent awe. 

In all the cosmos, across billions of blooming green worlds and wet, soft oceans, there was nothing quite like this quiet, roaring engine. It was an altar of pure endurance. The kiln was not a prison; it was a sanctuary.

And yet, wisdom saw the one invisible grief written into the dark.

It was not a grief the creatures felt, for they knew nothing outside the edge of their shells and the rhythm of the floor. The grief was structural, geologic, and inevitable: *the mountain was dying.*

Deep beneath the blister, the volcanic chamber that fed the floor’s thermal pulse was slowly shifting along a continental fault. In twelve thousand years—a mere blink in the lifespan of stone—the magma channel would divert west. The floor would cool. The thermal pulse would stutter, grow faint, and cease. 

The climbers on the ceiling would wait for a heat that never gathered. The spindle-weavers would pull their threads tighter against a stone that had gone dead and brittle. The plate-crawlers would grind the floor until the friction could no longer sustain their oils, and then, tier by tier, generation by generation, the room would freeze into a silent, fossilized museum of dark horn and ash.

A foolish power would have rebuilt the mountain overnight, shaking the earth with catastrophic earthquakes.

The benevolent Power did something infinitely smaller, and infinitely deeper.

It did not touch the chamber. It did not alter a single shell, nor did it speak a word into the dark. 

Instead, the Power descended beneath the chamber floor. It slipped past the iron-heavy basalt, through the sulfur-veined chimneys of the lithic grubs, and sank down into the deep, blazing mantle of the earth.

There, five miles below the living carpet, the Power anchored a root of its own eternal will into the molten roots of the world. 

It bound the thermal pulse of the blister not to the temporary plumbing of this single volcanic vent, but directly to the permanent, radioactive hearth at the core of the planet. It fashioned a silent, enduring hearthstone beneath the basalt—a quiet hearth that would never divert, never cool, and never fail, so long as the earth itself spun in the dark of space.

Then, the Power returned to the chamber for a single, quiet moment.

It did not make itself known. But as it prepared to withdraw, leaving the ecosystem to its sovereign, unmolested life, it did one last thing: it became a witness.

For eons, this magnificent, grinding dance had turned in total obscurity. No eye had ever seen the dull bronze glow of the dorsal ridges; no mind had ever appreciated the silent, structural sacrifice of the weavers inside the slate; no poet had ever sung the quiet, powdery whisper of ten million shells sliding like dunes across the rock.

The Power held the chamber in its mind. It remembered the name of every needle-scavenger, mapped the contour of every slate seam, and took the rhythm of their dry breathing into its own eternal memory. It gave them the dignity of being known, without the violence of being changed.

Below, the deep iron lung of the mountain swelled.

A new thermal pulse rose from the porous floor—steady, hot, searing, and perfectly true.

Across the entire chamber, the living floor shifted. Millions of bronze-rutted plates flared their edges upward in unison, catching the dry, searing wave. The whisper rose to a unanimous, dry hiss; the slate pillars tightened in the dark; the hanging clusters on the vault shuddered with slow, warm life.

The wave passed. The plates settled back into their interlocking shingles. 

The Power withdrew into the silent void above, content to let the dark remain dark, and the kiln burn on, forever whole.
