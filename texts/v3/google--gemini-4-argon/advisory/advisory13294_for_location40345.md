To visit this drowned basalt fault-crack and return intact, you have to abandon the rules of open-water diving. You are not going for a swim; you are preparing for a blind, freezing crawl through a dense, living organ where the laws of physics have curdled. 

If you are an ordinary human without biological modifications or magical protections, here is how you must prepare to enter the pocket.

### 1. The Suit: Armor Against Glass and Cold
Standard neoprene or even a heavy rubber drysuit will fail you here within minutes. The freshly fractured basalt is literally as sharp as broken glass, and because there is no open space, the dense, rubbery thicket of plants will constantly press your body against the rock walls. 

*   **The Outer Shell:** You need a close-fitting, crush-resistant drysuit reinforced with an outer layer of tightly woven para-aramid fiber (like Kevlar) and flexible ceramic-composite plates over the knees, elbows, shoulders, and spine. Every seam must be recessed and shielded. 
*   **Streamlining:** This is a matter of life and death: **nothing can dangle from your suit.** No loose pressure gauges, no loop-hose regulators, no clips, no nets. The flora here grows into a continuous, interlocking lattice. A single loose loop of hose catching on a blind white root-lattice will pin you in place in the dark, and you will not have the leverage to wrench yourself free.
*   **Thermal Regulation:** The water is near-freezing, motionless, and hyper-dense, meaning it will strip heat from your body far faster than normal seawater. Furthermore, certain roots loop through dimensional bleed-zones into "a different winter entirely"—brush against one of those, and you are touching sub-zero exotic cold. Wear an active, battery-heated thermal undergarment beneath the suit, with redundant heating elements in the gloves and boots.
*   **Gloves:** You will be moving almost entirely by touch. You need heavy-duty, cut-resistant gloves (level A9 stainless-steel mesh infused with flexible silicone) that still allow you to feel the texture of what you are grabbing. 

### 2. Breathing and Buoyancy: Defeating the "Grain"
Traditional SCUBA gear is a death trap here. A tank on your back will wedge you instantly into the cartilaginous foliage, and a standard regulator relies on ambient water pressure—which, in this room, fluctuates wildly depending on which corner you are in.

*   **Closed-Circuit Rebreather (CCR):** You must use a low-profile, chest- or back-mounted closed-circuit rebreather that is completely encased in a smooth, hard shell (a "turtle shell" configuration) so it slides through the bruised-plum bladder-fronds without snagging. Because the water sometimes solidifies into a faceted gel around the bone cages, an open-circuit regulator that vents bubbles could freeze up or choke on the mucilage-heavy broth. A rebreather keeps your breathing loop isolated from the outside environment and produces no bubbles to disturb the weirdness.
*   **Ditch the BCD:** A Buoyancy Control Device (an inflatable vest) is useless here. Gravity in this cleft is broken; it pulls downward like mercury in the lower left corner and sideways toward the eastern wall just three feet away. Inflating an air bladder will only yank you violently in whichever direction the local water "grain" dictates. Instead, weight yourself to be **strictly neutrally buoyant** for high-density brine, and rely entirely on slow, hand-over-hand pulling through the vegetation to move.

### 3. Senses and Navigation: Blindness by Choice
Your instincts will tell you to bring powerful dive lights to cut through the pitch black. **Do not do this.**

*   **Low-Intensity Lighting:** The ecosystem here is adapted to total darkness and the faint, sourceless violet grease leaking from the stone seams. Flooding the chamber with a 2,000-lumen halogen or LED beam risks triggering a violent photophobic reaction in the aggressive plants, or blinding the single chiton-creature whose shell-geometry is tied to the room's physics. Instead, bring a dim, diffused amber or dull-green glow-stick mounted flush to your helmet—just enough light to see two inches in front of your faceplate and catch the shiny edges of the purple mineral grease.
*   **Spatial Orientation:** Because gravity shifts and the space inside the root-loops is non-Euclidean (larger on the inside than the nine-foot room allows), your inner ear will revolt. You will experience severe vertigo and nausea in the pitch dark. Take a heavy dose of anti-vertigo medication (like meclizine or dimenhydrinate) an hour before entry. 
*   **The Tether:** Do not use a compass or digital depth gauge; the mineral leach and broken physics will scramble them. Your only reliable navigation tool is a physical lifeline. Anchor a thick, brightly colored, Kevlar-sheathed braided line directly at your point of entry (or directly to your multiverse transit beacon) and keep it taut attached to your waist harness. It is your only guarantee that you can find "out" when "up" stops existing.

### 4. Tools you shouldn't bring (and one you should)
*   **No Knives:** Leave the dive knife behind. The plants here grow through one another with slow-motion aggression, fusing when pierced. If you slash at the thicket to clear a path, the ruptured stalks will flood the tiny room with thick mucilage, and the bruised-plum bladders will release unknown, non-air gases that may be corrosive or reactive with the chalky stone veins. Worse, the cut edges may attempt to fuse with *your suit*. Part the curtain of leaves gently with your hands; never cut it.
*   **A Wooden Probe:** Bring a short, smooth rod made of dense hardwood (like lignum vitae or ironwood), strapped flat against your forearm. Do not use metal—there are no metals in the room's indigenous artifacts, and metal may react poorly with the violet stone-grease or the thrumming slate shrine. Use this wooden stick to prod the water ahead of your hands. You need to know if the water in front of you is currently liquid, mercury-heavy, or frozen into a faceted, gelatinous solid *before* you stick your fingers or faceplate into it.

### 5. Behavioral Protocols in the Cistern
Once you drop into the pocket, your survival depends on discipline and extreme patience.

*   **Move at the Speed of the Room:** Nothing hurries here. If you thrash, panic, or kick, you will slice yourself open on the feldspar crystals or wedge yourself between the dry-stone masonry and the ceiling. Move an inch at a time. Let the rubbery stems creak past your shell. Expect it to take twenty minutes just to cross the nine-foot span.
*   **Keep Your Hands Out of the Root-Loops:** As you pull yourself along by feel, grip the thick central stalks or the broad leaves of the water-cabbages. *Never* reach blindly into a knotted tangle of the pale white roots. If your hand passes through one of the spatial anomalies where the roots drink from "a different winter," the sudden drop in temperature and shift in spatial geometry could trap your arm or cause instant frostbite through your glove.
*   **Respect the Architecture:** Do not touch the fist-sized whalebone cages, do not pocket the smooth gray river stones inside them, and do not disturb the stacked slate shrine. These objects are clearly acting as focal points—or perhaps stabilizers—for the broken physics of the room. The water turns solid where the cages touch the fronds; the shrine hums with a rhythmic thrum. Moving a single stone might cause the localized gravity or the solid-water phases to shift catastrophically, crushing you against the basalt.
*   **Let the Worm Pass:** This is the hardest psychological hurdle. At some point in the dark, you may see—or feel—the six-foot, needle-thin white worm drawing itself through the packed leaves. It phases straight through solid matter, stone and cellulose alike, without leaving a hole. If its path intersects with your arm, your leg, or your chest, **freeze completely.** Do not try to bat it away, and do not jerk back. It is a slow white stitch holding the geometry of the room together. If you stay still, it will pass harmlessly through your suit and flesh without leaving a wound, though it will feel like a line of freezing cold wire drawing through your marrow. If you thrash while it is inside you, there is no telling what happens to the matter it is currently bridging.

### 6. Decontamination
Before you trigger your return jump or exit the fault-crack, you must plan for what comes back with you. The violet grease that leaks from the stone fractures is described as a mineral reacting poorly to the reality of the water. It will cling to the edges of your suit. 

Have a heavy-duty chemical scrub-down ready on the other side of your transit point. Do not touch your bare skin to the exterior of your suit when taking it off; use long-handled tongs to strip the gear and submerge it in a neutralizing bath of fresh water and mild detergent. Only when the faint purple luminescence is completely washed away—and you've checked to make sure no microscopic white holdfasts have begun to root into the weave of your Kevlar—is your trip truly over.
