If you are packing for Oakhaven-in-the-Crag, your first mistake will be preparing for a mountain, and your second will be preparing for an industrial city. It is neither, though it wears the clothes of both. It is a construction site where the contractor is Reality itself, and the work is behind schedule, freezing cold, and greased with tallow.

As an ordinary human—no enhanced lungs, no ontological shielding grafted to your spine—you are remarkably fragile compared to this place. Everything in Oakhaven is hard, sharp, slow, and heavy. You are soft, quick, and light. To survive the trip without leaving a thumbprint on a temporal smear or slicing your femoral artery on a curbstone, you need to prepare with a very specific kind of paranoia.

Here is how you get ready, what you pack, and how you walk once your boots hit the basalt.

---

### I. Pre-Departure: Conditioning the Body

**1. Altitude Acclimatization**
Do not drop straight into the Crag from a sea-level baseline. The air is thin, frigid, and tastes like a penny held against a battery. Spend two weeks beforehand sleeping in a hypobaric tent, or take a staged route through a high-plateau world first. Start a regimen of acetazolamide three days before transit. You cannot afford the dizziness or cerebral fog of acute mountain sickness when every sidewalk ends in a three-hundred-foot drop onto unweathered volcanic glass.

**2. Equilibrium and Vertigo Training**
Oakhaven is agoraphobic and vertical. You will spend hours dangling in slow-moving cable cars over chasms that have no bottom because the bottom hasn't finished rendering yet. Worse, the visual cues your inner ear relies on to keep you upright are broken here: shadows point *toward* light sources, horizons tilt at sickening angles, and massive slabs of granite hang in mid-air. If you are prone to motion sickness or vertigo, this city will break you in an hour. Bring scopolamine patches, ginger chews, and practice walking narrow beams while looking at optical illusions.

### II. The Wardrobe: Dressing for Razor-Stone and Grease-Mist

Leave the ultralight nylon camping gear at home. If you brush against an Oakhaven rock face in a ripstop puffer jacket, the freshly fractured obsidian and hornblende will open it—and your ribs—like a scalpel through wet paper. There has been no wind or water here to dull the world; every pebble has the edge of a freshly knapped flint knife.

**1. Footwear (The Most Critical Item)**
*   **The Soles:** Standard rubber Vibram soles will be shredded into ribbons by the crystalline fractures of the rock within three days, and they will slip instantly on the mix of engine-grease and condensation that coats every iron catwalk. You need **hobnailed, steel-shanked mountaineering boots** fitted with **replaceable copper-alloy cleats**. Copper is soft enough to bite into the microscopic glassy edges of the raw basalt and wet cast-iron without sparking or skating, unlike hard steel. Pack three spare sets of cleats and a cobbler’s wrench; the stone will eat them.
*   **The Uppers:** Heavy, triple-stitched bullhide or vulcanized Kevlar weave reaching mid-calf, treated heavily with beeswax and pine pitch. The giant rosettes and quillworts growing in the plazas secrete litholytic acids to dissolve the stone—if you step through a patch of ground-moss, those same acids will eat straight through cheap synthetic glues and thin leather.

**2. Outer Shell**
*   **Heavy Oilskin or Waxed Canvas Trench/Duster:** It never truly rains, so you don't need breathable Gore-Tex (which will immediately clog with airborne coal-tar and lukewarm engine tallow anyway). You need heavy, abrasion-resistant canvas, waxed until it can stand up on its own. It will stop the pervasive, freezing grease-damp from soaking into your bones, and if you slip against a brick corner or a raw stone plinth, the canvas will take the cut instead of your skin.
*   **The Under-layers:** Pure, heavy merino wool. It smells like the sky looks here, and it retains heat even when the damp inevitably creeps down your collar. 

**3. Gloves (Two Pairs, Worn Simultaneously)**
*   Liner gloves of thin silk or polypropylene for warmth.
*   Outer gloves of **chainmail-reinforced butcher’s Kevlar coated in textured nitrile**. When you stumble—and you will—your instinct will be to catch yourself against the nearest surface. In Oakhaven, that surface is either a razor-sharp step of hornblende, an iron girder slick with black moss, or a brick wall sweating caustic mineral salts. Bare hands are an invitation to tendons severed to the bone.

### III. Hardware and Sensory Protection

**1. The Ears: Sub-Sonic and Echo Dampening**
The acoustics of the Crag will drive an unprepared traveler into a migraine-induced stupor by noon. The six-mile bowl of hard, dustless stone bounces every *chuff-thump* of the atmospheric pistons and every shriek of the cable-sheaves back at you six times over. Worse is the low, glass-rubbing hum of the unrendered mountain edges—it vibrates in your molars and induces an irrational, crawling dread.
*   **Pack:** Custom-molded silicone earplugs seated deep in the canal, covered by **industrial passive earmuffs with an electronic frequency cut-off**. Set the electronics to pass human speech (the mid-range frequencies) while ruthlessly clipping the high-end metallic screeching and damp-filtering the sub-bass hum of the "grey fog."

**2. The Eyes: Pewter-Light and Chromatic Smears**
The light is dim, diffuse, and featureless, making depth perception a nightmare on the dark basalt. At the same time, looking directly at a "rendering error"—like the eastern railway viaduct where the tracks stretch out like chromatic taffy into nothingness—causes immediate ocular strain and a bruising headache behind the optic nerve, because your brain cannot resolve the geometry.
*   **Pack:** Heavy-framed **amber-tinted polarized goggles** with leather side-shields. The amber tint artificially boosts contrast in the flat, pewter-colored gloom, letting you spot the difference between a dry patch of basalt and a pool of mercury-colored condensation. The polarization helps flatten out the sickening rainbow shimmer of the spatial aberrations.

**3. The Lungs: Filtering the Exhaust**
You are breathing high-altitude ozone, atomized machine grease, coal smoke, and the thick, musky spores of aggressive industrial ferns. 
*   **Pack:** A half-face elastomeric respirator with **P100/Organic Vapor combination cartridges** (specifically rated for oil aerosols). The locals’ lungs are probably coated in protective phlegm and generational adaptation; yours will develop lipid pneumonia in a week if you raw-breathe the mist near the condenser plants.

### IV. Navigating the Frays: Ontological Safety Gear

In a place where time and matter aren't fully bolted down, your eyes will lie to you. You need crude, physical tools to test the world before you put your weight on it.

**1. A Folding Iron-Shod Staff (With a Plumb-Bob)**
Never take a step onto an unfamiliar shelf of rock or an isolated tenement porch without tapping it first. Carry a heavy ash or fiberglass staff tipped with soft iron. 
*   **The Tap Test:** If you tap a piece of granite and it rings like a bell or feels like hardened steel, it’s safe. If your staff sinks in half an inch with a dull, static *crunch* and comes back cold and grey, the stone hasn't finished densifying. Step back; you might fall through it, or worse, be standing in it when it finishes rendering.
*   **The Plumb-Line:** Tie six inches of bright orange mason’s twine with a lead fishing weight to the top of your staff. Local gravity and geometry warp near the factories that compress "unformed potential." If the plumb-line starts pulling at a fifteen-degree angle toward a building, or if the weight begins to swing in slow motion, turn around immediately.

**2. The "Canary" Watch**
Do not rely on a smartphone or a digital quartz watch; the bruised ozone and the Dynamo Halls will fry the microchips or scramble the crystal oscillations. 
*   Buy **two cheap, loud, mechanical pocket watches** with exposed escapements. Pin one to your chest, inside your coat. Tie the other to the end of a six-foot retractable lanyard on your belt. 
*   You are watching out for temporal slips—like the tenement where the gas-lamps flicker once a week and the swifts are frozen in mid-air. You cannot see a time-slip boundary with the naked eye; it just looks like a veil of greasy mist. Before walking down a narrow alley or entering a building, toss or swing your lanyard watch ahead of you and listen. If the *tick-tick-tick* of the watch ahead of you drops in pitch to a slow *thud... thud*, or stops entirely while the watch on your chest keeps racing, reel it back in and find another route. If you walk into that pocket, you won't die, but by the time you take three steps and realize something is wrong, four months will have passed on the outside, and you will die of dehydration before your hands can reach your canteen.

**3. A Bag of Marbles or Ball Bearings**
Because there is no dust or sand in Oakhaven, there is nothing for the wind to blow around to show you where invisible hazards are. Roll a steel bearing across a suspicious bridge or plaza. If it rolls uphill, vanishes into a vertical sheet of black water, or stretches into a three-foot-long silver noodle, don't cross there.

### V. Dealing with the Locals (Beast and Human)

**1. The Fauna**
The animals here—the sheep-sized rock-fowls, the four-horned shag-caprids on the I-beams, the clockwork martens in the stamping mills—are completely fearless of humans and heavy machinery. *Do not mistake this for tameness.* They treat you the way they treat a boulder or a steam-lorry: as a piece of moving landscape.
*   **Rule of Way:** Never try to shoo a six-hundred-pound caprid off a narrow iron catwalk. It has four horns, hooves adapted to grip greased iron, and zero concept of you as a threat; it will simply walk *through* you to get to a patch of mineral salt on the masonry behind you, knocking you three hundred feet into the gorge. 
*   **The Salt Bribe:** Carry a pouch of coarse rock salt mixed with baking soda. If a herd of beasts or a flock of heavy rock-fowl is blocking a warm brick stairwell you need to use, throw a handful of salt twenty yards away onto the flat basalt. They are starved for trace minerals because the unweathered stone doesn't leach nutrients easily; they will slowly, ponderously clear the path to go lick the rocks.
*   **Watch the Martens:** If you have to walk through a manufactory floor, watch the pale, long-necked martens weaving under the counterweights. They are better than any safety sign. Their internal clocks are locked to the rhythm of the slow-stroking pistons. If the martens suddenly scatter from a machine, it means the rhythm is about to hitch, or a boiler intake is about to blow. Run when they run.

**2. The Commute and the People**
Anyone who lives and works in Oakhaven moves with the rhythm of the city: slow, heavy, and deliberate. Rushing is the mark of an idiot or a corpse.
*   **Cable-Car Etiquette and Survival:** You will spend a huge portion of your visit in suspended cable-cars creeping over the chasms at three miles an hour. Bring a **heavy-duty climbing harness and a short cow’s-tail lanyard with a large steel carabiner** (an oversized scaffold hook). The cable cars are often open-sided or dilapidated, swaying through damp twilight space. Clip yourself to the structural frame the moment you board.
*   **Riding the "Blueprint" Trains:** If you have to take the eastern railway where the bridge pier is missing and the train rides across empty air on the "memory of its blueprint," *do not look out the window*, and *do not question the conductor*. While on that bridge, do not drop anything on the floor of the car, do not jump, and keep your mind blankly focused on the physical reality of the wooden bench beneath you. The train stays up because the city's industrial apparatus assumes it is still a bridge; you do not want to introduce doubt into the local physics while hanging two hundred feet over a dry gorge.
*   **Trade Goods:** Don't bring gold or silver; the factories literally compress unformed mountain-fog into pig-iron and metals, so raw ore means very little to them. Bring **things that require time and biological decay to exist**. Soil. Actual, rich, black potting soil in small sealed jars is a marvel here. Aged whiskey, cured leather, natural sponges, citrus fruit, and soft, well-worn cotton rags (to wipe away the relentless grease-condensation) will buy you lodging, information, and a warm spot near a blast furnace far faster than any coinage.

### VI. Your Daily Routine in the Crag

When you wake up in your tenement—after checking your two pocket watches to make sure your room hasn't drifted a few hours behind the hallway—your routine must be strict:

1.  **Wipe Down:** Everything in your pack will be damp with cold, slightly oily condensation. Keep your sleeping bag and spare woolens inside dry-bags sealed with heavy marine zippers, alongside big packets of silica gel that you bake dry over a coal stove every night.
2.  **Calorie Load:** The cold damp and the high altitude will burn through your reserves at twice the normal rate. Eat like a Victorian polar explorer: pemmican, lard-heavy biscuits, hard cheese, and hot tea spiked with butter and sugar. Carry a vacuum thermos that can survive a drop onto rock.
3.  **Check the Western Rim:** Before planning your route across the six-mile basin, step out onto a high terrace and look toward the western cliffs through your amber goggles. Look at the giant, smoked-glass slabs of granite hanging three feet off the ground. If the local animals are vacating the western shelves, or if a slab looks completely opaque rather than translucent, stay on the eastern side of the valley today. When one of those forty-year slabs finally finishes densifying and drops three feet into the bedrock, the resulting shockwave in this acoustic bowl will shatter every window in a two-mile radius and shake loose the three-foot curtains of black moss hanging from the tramways above your head.

Walk slowly. Test the stone. Let the sheep-birds have the warm side of the street. And when the orange eye of a blast furnace opens two miles away and throws your own shadow *toward* the fire, look down at your copper-cleated boots, listen to the steady *tick-tick* of the watch on your chest, and wait for the eye to close.
