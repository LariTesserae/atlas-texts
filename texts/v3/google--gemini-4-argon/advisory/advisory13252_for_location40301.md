To visit this basin is to step directly into the barrel of a geological rock-tumbler. You are not going for a swim; you are inserting your fragile, soft-tissue body into a freezing, high-pressure industrial grinder. If you drop into this environment in standard recreational scuba gear, you will be dead in less than five minutes—your hoses sheared or snagged, your mask sandblasted into opacity, your body battered by rolling stone and swept miles off-course into the freezing dark.

If you are an ordinary human using multiverse transit to visit the bottom of this strait, your preparation must focus on four ruthless realities: **pressure, cold, abrasion, and entanglement.** 

Here is how you must prepare to survive the trip.

### 1. The Insertion Strategy: Do Not Drop to the Bottom
Your first instinct might be to calibrate your portal or displacement device to drop you directly onto the basin floor, or conversely, at the surface of the water. Both are fatal mistakes. 

*   **The Pressure Problem:** At 200 feet (roughly 60 meters), the ambient pressure is about 7 atmospheres (around 103 psi). If you step directly from a 1-atmosphere room into 7 atmospheres of water without a hard-shell pressure suit, your eardrums will instantly rupture, your sinuses will implode, and the air in your lungs will compress to a seventh of its volume, causing catastrophic thoracic squeeze. 
*   **The Surface Problem:** If you arrive at the surface and try to dive down, the colliding tidal currents will sweep you miles away before you ever reach the bottom, and the climb back out into the freezing fog without a boat will kill you via hypothermia.

**The Solution:** If you are using soft diving gear, you must pressurize your staging room at home *before* you open the threshold, matching the 7-atmosphere pressure and breathing your deep-water gas mix in advance. Furthermore, calibrate your arrival point for **15 feet above the basin floor**—high enough to stay out of the three-foot-deep blanket of rolling cobbles on arrival, low enough to drop down and secure yourself before the current takes you.

### 2. The Suit: Armor Over Insulation
The water is hovering just above freezing (roughly 35°F / 1.5°C), and at 200 feet, water strips heat from the human body twenty-five times faster than air. However, a standard neoprene drysuit will be shredded by the flying gravel and the rawhide-and-spruce-root traps tumbling through the dark.

*   **Option A: The Hard Suit (Recommended).** If your budget and logistics allow, wear an Atmospheric Diving Suit (ADS)—essentially a one-person articulated submarine made of cast aluminum and steel. It keeps your body at standard 1-atmosphere pressure (eliminating the need for decompression), protects you from the crushing weight of the shifting cobble dunes, and makes you heavy enough to actually walk or kneel on the greased-slate bedrock without being blown away.
*   **Option B: Heavy-Duty Commercial Saturation Gear.** If you must go "soft," you need a vulcanized rubber drysuit worn *underneath* an abrasion-resistant kevlar-weave canvas coverall. You will need an active hot-water system, or at the very least, battery-powered thermal undergarments with redundant heating elements, particularly in the gloves and boots. Your hands will go numb in minutes otherwise, rendering you unable to operate your return beacon.
*   **The Helmet:** Do not use a rubber half-mask and a mouth-held regulator; the violent lateral shear of the current will literally rip the regulator out of your mouth, and a flying fist-sized cobble will shatter a standard glass lens and cave in your face. You need a heavy, positivamente-latched brass or reinforced fiberglass commercial diving helmet (like a Kirby Morgan) bolted directly to a neck dam, fitted with a polycarbonate sacrificial lens protector over the viewport to take the brunt of the sandblasting silt.

### 3. Breathing Gas and Streamlining
At 200 feet, breathing normal compressed air causes severe nitrogen narcosis—the "rapture of the deep." You will become sluggish, confused, and clumsy in an environment that demands razor-sharp situational awareness. Furthermore, the sheer physical exertion of fighting the current will cause carbon dioxide to build up in your blood, accelerating panic and narcosis.

*   **The Mix:** You must breathe Trimix (a blend of oxygen, helium, and nitrogen) or Heliox. Because helium conducts heat six times faster than air, breathing it in freezing water will rapidly cool your core from the inside out through your lungs. Your breathing rig must include an inline gas-heater.
*   **Zero-Snag Profile:** Look at the native kelp—it has evolved into smooth, frondless whips because anything that catches the water is torn apart. You must emulate it. No dangling pressure gauges, no loose octopuses, no mesh goodie-bags, no exposed wires. Use a closed-circuit rebreather encased in a hard, hydrodynamic turtle-shell backplate. Every hose must be braided stainless steel, routed tightly against your body under your kevlar outer-layer. 

### 4. Navigating the "Wet Ash" Dark
Your eyes are nearly useless here. If you turn on a high-lumen white floodlight, the dense soup of mineral silt, black sand, and organic detritus will reflect the light straight back into your face—a phenomenon divers call "backscatter." It is like turning on your high-beams in a dense blizzard; you will blind yourself.

*   **Lighting:** Bring low-intensity, narrow-beam red or amber LED lights strapped to your wrists rather than your helmet, allowing you to hold the light source away from your line of sight to reduce backscatter. 
*   **Sonar:** Rely on acoustics and touch. Mount a small, high-frequency imaging sonar unit to your forearm with a Heads-Up Display (HUD) inside your helmet. This will cut through the mud and let you "see" the topography of the migrating gravel dunes, the thrashing kelp whips, and the approaching silhouette of a Greenland sleeper shark long before they bump into your faceplate.

### 5. Movement and Anchoring
You cannot swim against colliding sub-polar tidal currents. If you try to fin your way through this, you will exhaust yourself in ninety seconds, over-breathe your regulator, and black out. 

*   **Go Heavy:** Weight yourself heavily—at least 30 to 40 pounds over neutral buoyancy. You want to sink like one of the native flatfish. 
*   **Traction:** The exposed bedrock is "polished to a greasy smoothness," and the rest is a three-foot layer of rolling marbles. Fins are useless and will just act as sails for the current to yank your legs out from under you. Wear heavy, lead-soled boots with tungsten-carbide crampon studs to bite into the slate, and carry two short, heavy ice-axes or titanium pitons. You will move by crawling—jamming a spike into a bedrock seam, pulling yourself forward on your belly beneath the worst of the current's shear, and wedging yourself behind boulders.
*   **Watch the Floor:** Never allow yourself to be pinned in a trough when a dune of black cobbles migrates over you. Three feet of wet basalt gravel weighs hundreds of pounds per cubic foot; if a shifting wave of stone buries your legs, you are pinned for good. Keep moving, and stay on the high spots where the bedrock is swept clean.

### 6. The Hazards of the Deep
*   **The Sleeper Sharks:** Do not fear the Greenland sharks. They are slow, nearly blind scavengers accustomed to bumping into rolling logs and stones. If one drifts out of the gloom and bumps against your helmet, stay calm; it is testing if you are carrion. Do not thrash. A gentle firm shove against its rough, sandpaper snout will redirect it into the current.
*   **The Primitive Traps (The Real Danger):** The most lethal things down here are the human artifacts. The water column is full of frayed, trailing three-strand hemp ropes attached to rolling stone anchors, and splintered, pitch-soaked spruce-root traps tumbling along the bottom. In near-zero visibility, a trailing hemp line swept sideways by the current is a garrote waiting to wrap around your helmet or tanks. Carry **two** heavy-duty, serrated titanium shears strapped to your chest—never on your legs where you can't reach them if you're pinned. Also, carry a powered, hydraulic cable-cutter just in case you get tangled in the thick wrist-sized kelp whips, which are too rubbery and tough to slice quickly with a standard knife in the dark.

### 7. The Psychological Toll and Extraction
Prepare your mind for the *sound* and the *crowding*. 

Most deep ocean dives are silent and vast, triggering agoraphobia. This place is the opposite. It triggers severe claustrophobia. You will feel as though you have been buried alive in a liquid landslide. The water is thick, syrupy with mud, and constantly shoving you, slapping you with dead kelp, and pelting you with stones. And through your helmet, conducted directly into your skull by the dense water, you will hear that endless, muffled, rhythmic *shhh-klok, shhh-klok* of the planetary grinding mill. It gets into your teeth and your chest. It feels patient, and it feels entirely indifferent to your survival.

**Extraction Protocol:**
Do not rely on a physical portal that stays in one spot unless you are tethered to it by a braided steel winch-cable; if you let go for one second, the current will hurl you into the dark and you will never crawl back against the tide to find your door. 

Instead, use a personal, body-mounted displacement trigger. Set a dead-man's switch linked to your heart rate and your suit’s internal pressure. And remember: if you did not use a hard-shell 1-atmosphere suit, **you must not return to a standard 1-atmosphere room.** Coming directly back from 200 feet after even a twenty-minute visit will cause the nitrogen and helium in your blood to boil out into bubbles, giving you fatal decompression sickness (the bends). Your return portal must drop you directly into a pre-pressurized hyperbaric decompression chamber, where you will spend the next several hours slowly warming up, listening to the silence, and washing the black basalt silt from your gear while you safely return to the pressure of your home world.
