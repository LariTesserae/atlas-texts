Preparing for a visit to the Valley of Fixed Afternoon requires gearing up for two distinct challenges: a deceptively harsh physical environment of wet, razor-sharp stone at high altitude, and a profound, clockwork stillness that can quietly erode a human traveler’s sense of time and sanity. 

You are not walking into a ruin; you are walking into a pause. Here is how an ordinary human should prepare before stepping through the threshold.

---

### 1. Footwear and Apparel: The "Wet-Blade" Protocol
The single greatest physical danger in this valley is a mundane slip-and-fall. Because the rock is geologically young, unweathered, and composed of fractured basalt and slate, the ground and walls are essentially fields of dull and sharp blades. Compounding this, every horizontal surface sweats with permanent condensation, and the stone is draped in plush moss that hides the exact angle of the rock beneath. A simple twisted ankle here usually comes with a severe laceration.

*   **The Boots:** Leave standard hiking boots with hard rubber lugs at home; they will skate right off wet, mossy slate. You need footwear similar to what canyoners or river-fly-fishermen wear: stiff, ankle-supporting boots with soles made of ultra-soft, sticky butyl rubber or studded felt, combined with a kevlar or steel shank in the midsole so sharp stone points cannot puncture upward into your foot. 
*   **Cut-Resistant Outerwear:** The description notes that the stone can slice leather. Your outer layer—especially trousers, knee pads, and forearm guards—should be made of abrasion- and cut-resistant fabrics (such as Dyneema, Cordura, or light kevlar weaves, similar to chainsaw chaps or motorcycle gear). If you brush against a dry-stack wall to keep your balance, normal nylon or cotton will shred, and your skin will follow.
*   **Gloves are Mandatory:** Never touch the masonry or scramble up a terrace bare-handed. Bring several pairs of high-dexterity, cut-resistant gloves (Level A4 or higher) with nitrile or sandy-latex palms that can grip wet stone.
*   **Thermal Layering for the "Split Climate":** Because the sun never moves from 40 degrees south and there is no wind to mix the air, the valley consists of stark thermal microclimates. Stepping from a sunlit plaza into the cobalt shadow of a western wall is like stepping straight into a walk-in freezer. Dress in a vapor-permeable wool base layer, an insulating fleece mid-layer, and a breathable waterproof shell. Avoid cotton entirely; the air is saturated with invisible vapor, and once fabric gets damp here, the lack of wind means it will never air-dry in the shade.

### 2. Physiological Preparation: Altitude and Light
*   **Altitude Acclimatization:** The air is thin and metallic. Before departing your home world, spend a week sleeping in a hypoxic altitude tent, or bring a course of Acetazolamide (Diamox) to prevent acute mountain sickness. Expect to be short of breath when climbing the monumental staircases. Move slowly—match the pace of the local ungulates.
*   **Optical Protection:** The eastern cliff faces are perpetually blinding, and the wet flagstones act like millions of tiny mirrors reflecting the fixed, pale-yellow sun. Snow-blindness (photokeratitis) is a serious risk. Pack Category 3 or 4 polarized glacier glasses with side shields, plus a spare pair in a hard case. 
*   **Skin Care:** Thin air and constant UV from a fixed angle will burn the left or right side of your face (depending on which way you walk) while leaving the other side pale. Bring high-SPF, sweat-resistant sunblock and heavy lip balm.

### 3. Shelter and Sleeping: Surviving Perpetual Afternoon
You cannot rely on the environment to tell your body when to rest, and you cannot sleep directly on the ground.

*   **The Blackout Tent:** The sun will never set. To sleep without your circadian rhythm shattering, you must bring a freestanding tent (you cannot drive stakes into solid slate) equipped with a 100% light-blocking fly. Set it up in the lee of a wall—partially in the sun so you don't freeze, or fully in the sun with reflective tarps if your tent gets too hot.
*   **Puncture-Proof Bedding:** An inflatable sleeping pad is a liability here; one stray flint shim on a plaza floor will pop it, leaving you sleeping on freezing, sweating stone. Bring a heavy closed-cell foam pad (or two) to lay beneath your tent floor, and a waterproof bivy sack to keep the perpetual stone-sweat from soaking upward through your tent tub.
*   **Timekeeping:** Bring at least two independent, non-solar watches (mechanical or long-life quartz) with loud alarms to enforce a strict 24-hour human schedule of eating, exploring, and sleeping.

### 4. Sustenance and the "Static Equilibrium"
The valley is a closed, unchanging loop. The cisterns are full to the exact same chisel-mark; the moss is cropped by the grazers yet stays the exact same thickness; the wooden mallets do not rot. 

*   **Do Not Eat the Flora or Fauna:** While the city is teeming with docile turkey-sized birds, slow amphibians, and pale berries, *do not attempt to forage or hunt.* In an ecosystem where nothing decays and everything exists in static equilibrium, the biology may not be compatible with human digestion, or worse, it may lack the normal bacterial processes required to break down in your gut. Bring 100% of your own freeze-dried, high-calorie rations.
*   **Water Protocol:** Water is abundant in the stone cisterns, and it looks pristine. However, because the air smells faintly of metallic clarity and wet slate, and the moisture wells up from the rock itself, the water may be heavily mineralized with dissolved metals (like copper, arsenic, or heavy salts from the young basalt). Bring a portable purifier capable of filtering heavy metals and viruses (or a small camp stove to distill water), though if you only plan to stay a few days, carrying your own water bladder or using a standard chemical/carbon filter after testing the water with chemical test strips is wise. *Etiquette note: If you draw water from a cistern, watch to see if the level returns to the chisel-mark. Either way, do not wash yourself or your gear in the cisterns.*
*   **Fuel:** There is no deadwood, and the dwarf conifers and damp moss will not burn. Bring sealed liquid-fuel or isobutane canister stoves (primed for high altitude) to boil your water and heat your rations.

### 5. Navigating the Clockwork: Etiquette and Safety
The city is three miles wide, open, and peaceful, but it operates like a giant, silent machine. You are the only anomalous moving part.

*   **Yield to the Herds:** The slate-gray ungulates and iridescent birds move with "the calm predictability of clockwork." Treat them not as wild animals, and not as pets, but as slow-moving streetcars on invisible tracks. Do not block the ramps or avenues when a herd of fifty animals is making its scheduled transition from plaza to roof terrace. They are not aggressive, but they are heavy, and you do not want to disrupt their loop or get calmly trampled against a sharp flint-shimmed wall.
*   **Respect the Dry-Stack:** Every building, tower, and terrace is held together solely by gravity, friction, and small flint shims. **Never pull a stone or shim from a wall.** Do not climb the outside of walls; use only the monumental staircases and ramps. While the architecture is stable, dry-stone masonry relies on interlocking tension—disturbing a key shim on an unfinished upper tier could cause a localized collapse.
*   **Leave the Tools Alone:** You will find bronze pry-bars, wooden mallets, reed baskets, and taut copper plumb-bobs left behind by the builders. Look at them, photograph them, sketch them, and do not touch them. We do not know *why* the valley is in this state of suspended equilibrium, or if the builders are merely absent or somehow bound up in the stasis of the place. Cutting the string of a plumb-bob or pocketing a bronze bar is a gamble no sensible traveler takes.
*   **Pack Out Everything:** Because nothing rots here, an apple core, a scrap of foil, or human waste left in a courtyard will sit there, pristine and damp, forever. Bring "wag bags" (human waste disposal bags used by big-wall mountaineers and polar explorers) and sealable trash bags. Leave no trace that you were ever in the museum.

### 6. Psychological Preparation: The Weight of the Quiet
The hardest part of visiting this world is not the sharp rock or the thin air; it is the acoustics and the stillness. 

There is no wind. There is no background hum. Every sound you make—the crunch of your boot, the zip of your jacket, the sound of your own breathing—will feel obscenely loud against the tiny, dry *plip* of water drops and the silent *pad-pad* of spongy hooves.

*   **Noise Discipline:** Move quietly. Wear soft-brushed outer fabrics (like moleskin or quiet wool blends over your cut-resistant layers) rather than loud, swishing hardshell plastics, which will grate on your nerves after three hours of dead silence.
*   **Audio Anchors:** Paradoxically, when you are inside your blackout tent at "night," the silence outside—broken only by a single drop of water hitting a cistern every four seconds—can induce severe insomnia or auditory hallucinations. Bring a small MP3 player with bone-conduction headphones loaded with familiar sounds: wind in trees, rainfall, city traffic, or human voices.
*   **Mental Grounding:** The combination of the unmoving sun, the un-rotting wood, and the animals swapping places on the roofs in an endless, silent routine can trigger derealization—the feeling that you have stepped into a painting or died. Travel with a partner if possible. If traveling alone, keep a detailed paper journal (bring "Rite in the Rain" waterproof paper and a grease pencil, as normal paper will go limp from the air's vapor). Writing down observations—measuring the plumb-bobs, sketching the dinner-plate succulents, mapping the herd routes—gives your human mind a linear task in a non-linear afternoon.
