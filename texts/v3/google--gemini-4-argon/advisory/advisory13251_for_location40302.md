Visiting the Bronze-Age Cistern—as this type of newly minted, sun-drenched hydraulic chamber is often categorized—is one of the more sensory- overwhelming experiences available to a traveler, despite the location’s deceptive simplicity. There are no monsters here, no toxic gases, and no extreme weather. Yet, an unprepared traveler can find themselves battered, deafened, blinded, and potentially drowned within minutes of arrival. 

This is not a ruin. It is a piece of active, Bronze-Age or early Iron-Age municipal infrastructure in its prime. You are stepping into a working machine made of stone and water. Here is how an ordinary human should prepare to visit it safely and appreciate its stark, roaring beauty.

### 1. Insertion Strategy and Spatial Awareness
Before you trigger your jump, check your coordinates’ verticality carefully. The cistern is thirty paces across (roughly 75 to 90 feet) and filled almost to the lip with twenty feet of churning water. There is no dry promenade. Your only out-of-water footholds are the top three feet of slick masonry wall, the uppermost submerged steps, or the heavy, adze-squared beams holding up the cedar roof lattice.

*   **If arriving above the water:** Aim for the Western wall (opposite the intake chute) where the stepped ledges are located. Do not attempt to materialize on the roof beams unless you have exceptional balance; they are damp, rotting slightly where the ferns grow, and vibrating constantly from the waterfall’s impact.
*   **If arriving in the water:** Be prepared for immediate sensory shock and physical disorientation. Do not materialize in the Eastern quadrant. The intake chute’s plunge creates a violent, aerated downdraft ten feet deep. Highly aerated water ("white water" full of those large silvery bubbles) has significantly less buoyancy than solid water. If you drop in under the chute, you will sink like a stone in the froth while being battered by the falling cylinder of water.

### 2. Footwear and Skin Protection (The "Fresh Stone" Hazard)
The description notes a critical detail that many travelers overlook: *the masonry is recent.* The limestone blocks have not been smoothed by centuries of silt and wear. They hold the crisp, ninety-degree corners and scalloped tool-marks of copper chisels, and the joints are full of brittle, extruding lime mortar. 

Meanwhile, the water is heaving in "steep, crisscrossing swells." 

If you go in barefoot and in standard swimwear, the surge will inevitably slam you against a wall or one of the four central pillars. Freshly dressed limestone acts like giant, coarse-grit sandpaper, and those ninety-degree corners will slice water-softened skin open to the bone. 

*   **What to wear:** Even though the water is bathtub-warm, wear a 1mm or 2mm neoprene "rash guard" dive skin (full length for arms and legs). This is not for thermal protection; it is armor against abrasion. 
*   **Footwear:** Wear hard-soled neoprene dive booties or sturdy river-shoes with exceptional grip. The top two steps—where you will likely stand to catch your breath—are coated in "filamentous green algae... like scraps of wet silk." Wet algae on limestone is as slick as greased glass. You will need vulcanized rubber treads to find purchase on the chisel-scallops underneath the slime.
*   **Gloves:** Bring thin kevlar-lined or reinforced sailing gloves. You will need to grab onto the masonry joints to steady yourself against the rebounding waves, and the brittle ridges of fresh lime mortar will crumble under your fingers, scraping your knuckles raw if they are bare.

### 3. Auditory and Visual Gear
The cistern is an acoustic and optical amplifier. It is a stone box trapping the energy of a heavy waterfall and the midday sun.

*   **Hearing Protection:** The noise is described as an "immense... continuous, hollow roar that vibrates in the teeth and chest," compounded by the sharp slap of waves echoing off flat stone. Prolonged exposure to this level of reverberating white noise in an enclosed stone space will cause rapid auditory fatigue, disorientation, and potential hearing damage. Pack vented, waterproof swimming earplugs. They will dull the damaging high-frequency slap of the waves while still allowing you to feel the deep, thrilling bass of the waterfall in your chest.
*   **Eye Protection:** Above the water, the sun drops in "blinding, geometric shafts" through the cedar lattice, reflecting fiercely off the wet, cream-colored stone and the heaving surface. Below the water, the brilliant caustic networks flicker rapidly. If you are prone to migraines or vertigo, the combination of roaring noise, tossing swells, and strobing light-cages can induce severe nausea. 
    *   Bring a **polarized dive mask**. Polarization will cut the blinding glare when your head is above water, and the mask will allow you to open your eyes underwater without stinging them on the chalky lime dust flaking off the mortar. 
    *   *Warning on depth perception:* Because the water is so clear and the light so bright, the twenty-foot bottom looks "only arm's-length away." Do not let this optical illusion fool you into thinking you can simply stand up or easily retrieve a dropped item from the floor without a proper breath-hold.

### 4. Buoyancy and Tether Gear
Because the cistern has no dry deck, resting requires either clinging to the slick steps or floating. Treading water in crisscrossing swells gets exhausting quickly, even for strong swimmers.

*   **Flotation:** Wear a low-profile inflatable snorkel vest. You can leave it deflated while you dive down to admire the glowing beryl depths and the swaying eelgrass, then inflate it when you want to bob effortlessly at the surface and look up at the ferns hanging from the cedar lattice.
*   **A Short Tether:** Bring a four-foot length of nylon webbing with a carabiner, and a soft wooden or rubber wedge. You can jam the wedge into a deep mortar gap above the waterline on the stepped side of the room and clip yourself to it. This allows you to relax in the warm current without constantly fighting to keep the waves from washing you toward the violent intake chute on the eastern wall.

### 5. Hygiene and Post-Visit Care
The water has traveled for miles in an open, sun-baked stone channel, and blossoms and cedar needles drop into it from the unseen world above. While it is clear and free of fish or snails, it is *untreated surface water* that is warm enough to incubate bacteria, and it is heavily alkaline from the fresh lime mortar.

*   **Do not drink the water.** Keep your mouth closed when swimming through the spray.
*   **Skin and Eye Care:** The dissolving white lime mortar (calcium hydroxide/oxide) makes the water slightly caustic and very hard. After you return from your jump, rinse yourself and your gear thoroughly in fresh, neutral-pH water. Bring moisturizing lotion and lubricating eye drops; the chalky water will leave your skin feeling tight, dry, and powdery once you dry off.

### 6. Etiquette and The "Unseen World"
Finally, consider the context of this place. The copper-chisel marks are fresh. The mortar is still flaking. This means the builders are still around. Above that cedar lattice is a living world—likely a Bronze-Age city, temple complex, or palace garden (evidenced by the white blossoms falling through the grate). 

Furthermore, **the water is going somewhere.** If a thick cylinder of water is constantly smashing *into* this room, an equal volume of water is leaving it. Look for the outflow pipe or sluice gate down near the base of the walls (likely near the neat drift-lines of sunken cedar needles on the western side) and **stay away from it.** The suction differential on an ungrated ancient outflow pipe can pin a swimmer to the stone.

Keep a low profile. Do not shout (though nobody would likely hear you over the waterfall anyway), and do not climb up to poke at the cedar poles. If a local worker or priest walks across or beside the lattice above, the deep contrast between the blinding midday sun outside and the shaded latticework will make it hard for them to see you, *unless* you are wearing neon-colored modern gear. Stick to muted greens, creams, or dark colors for your dive skin and mask so you blend into the mint-tinted beryl shadows of the pool.

### Summary Checklist for the Traveler:
*   1mm–2mm full-body dive skin (muted colors)
*   Grip-soled neoprene dive booties
*   Reinforced water gloves
*   Polarized dive mask and snorkel (note: a standard snorkel may flood easily in the heavy swells; a "dry" snorkel with a float-valve is best)
*   Waterproof acoustic earplugs
*   Inflatable snorkel vest
*   Waterproof camera (the light shafts, silvery bubble columns, and oxygen pearls on the green silk algae are breathtakingly photogenic)
*   Fresh water and lotion waiting at your return point.

Prepare for the noise, respect the sharp stone, and watch the currents. Once you are safely wedged onto the upper steps, let yourself sink just below the surface. Watching the white blossoms spin slowly down through the geometric shafts of sunlight in that warm, roaring, mint-green box is one of the most tranquil, hypnotic sights in the multiverse.
