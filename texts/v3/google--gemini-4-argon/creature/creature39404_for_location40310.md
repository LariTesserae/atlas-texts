If you stand in that plaza for twenty minutes, fighting the urge to press your palms over your ears, your eyes will eventually stop trying to find buildings and start noticing the distortions in the lines. 

Every few hundred yards, three or four of the vertical green vesseltrees are pulled slightly out of true, bowed inward toward a central point like the waist of an hourglass. 

There is something hanging in the gap between them, suspended ten feet off the moss. At first, it looks like a smear of grease on the lens of your eye, or a long, wet strip of grey parchment that has been blown into the harp-strings of the jungle and caught there. 

Then a Trace pulses beneath it. The air cools, the silver rings of water materialize on the vines, the glassy predators detonate into their frenzy—and the grey smear *drops* six feet, catches itself without a sound, and absorbs the shock by bowing its entire five-foot length into a C-shape before snapping back into a rigid vertical line.

They are called the *Lath*, though they do not call themselves anything that can be pronounced with a throat and a tongue. 

They are the stewards, or the squatters, or perhaps the forgotten physical remainders of whatever built the Spires. In a world that has rejected bulk in favor of lines and planes, the Lath have become lines and planes.

A single individual is roughly six feet tall, ten inches wide at the chest, and rarely thicker than a carpenter’s plank. They have no rounded volumes. Seen from the front, a Lath resembles a vaguely humanoid silhouette cut from dull, pewter-colored slate, tapering at the top to a blunt, faceless prow and splitting at the bottom into four long, ribbon-like limbs. Seen from the side, they nearly vanish—a half-inch stripe of pale ochre and dark grey that blends seamlessly into the vertical grain of the valley.

Their biology is entirely hydrostatic and laminar, built like the interior of a high-pressure firehose. They have no bones; instead, their flat bodies are cross-hatched with thousands of diagonal fibers made of a biological metamaterial they harvest by licking the oxidized runoff from the Traces. When they relax these fibers, they are as limp and flexible as a wet kelp frond, able to wrap themselves tightly around a dinner-plate-wide Spire like a bandage, becoming completely invisible from twenty feet away. When they lock their fibers and pump fluid into their internal channels, they become as rigid as structural steel.

They cannot walk on the ground. To a Lath, the six-inch carpet of hyper-metabolic moss and its boiling soup of toxic, iridescent amphibians is an acid bath, and the jagged cooling-cracks of the basalt would shred their ribbon-feet. More importantly, the ground is where the gravity-ripples run. 

Instead, they live in the * Rigging*—the thirty-foot vertical zone just above the moss. 

To watch a Lath travel across a municipal district is to watch a master weaver working inside a loom. They do not swing like apes; the vines are spaced twenty or thirty feet apart, far too wide to reach. They travel by *ballistic tension*. 

A Lath will anchor its two lower ribbons to a vesseltree, gripping the slick plant with microscopic, dry-adhesive hairs. Using its flat, muscular torso as a leaf-spring, it pulls the taut vine sideways by three or four feet—stealing mechanical energy from the plant's magnetic upward pull—while reaching out with a thirty-foot filament of spun mucus and metallic silk. It fires this filament from a spinneret in its chest, riding the acoustic pressure waves of the valley until the thread catches a vine two avenues over. 

Then it lets go. The Lath shoots horizontally across the open plaza like a knife thrown edge-first, slicing through the humid air with zero drag, entirely silent amidst the deafening roar of the walnut-shells. Just before it hits the target vine, it twists its flat body ninety degrees, turning itself into an airbrake, flaring wide for a fraction of a second to bleed off the momentum before slapping wetly and securely onto the new stalk. 

They have no eyes. In a shadowless, purple twilight where everything of importance is either a hair-thin wire five hundred yards away or a four-hundred-mile-an-hour flash of math in the floor, lenses are useless. 

Instead, their entire flat front and back surfaces are a single, continuous tympanic membrane—an eardrum the size of a door. 

To us, the valley is a mind-erasing roar of static. To the Lath, that static is daylight. The ten million rifle-cracks of the pneumatic shells and the screaming cello-chords of the Choir-Galls are the illumination by which they see. Because their bodies are so wide and flat, they act as phased-array sonar receivers. By tilting itself slightly in the windless air, a Lath can read the interference patterns of the acoustic wall across miles of open basin. It knows the exact shape of every pebble five miles away by the microscopic acoustic shadow it casts against the roar of the distant Spires. It can "see" inside the translucent Choir-Galls, judging the sugar content of the boiling flesh by the pitch of the Spire's hum.

And this is their occupation: they are the Tuners. 

The post-physical intelligence that lives inside the Spires and the Traces does not notice the jungle. To the minds inside the force-locked needles, the moss and the vines and the Choir-Galls are just minor thermodynamic drag—a tiny drop in computational efficiency. Left unchecked, however, the Choir-Galls would grow so heavy and numerous that their vibrations would shatter the harmonic lattice of the Spires, causing the black needles to de-cohere in blasts of violet lightning.

The Lath prevent this. They are farmers of waste-heat and vibration.

You will see a pair of them working a Spire two hundred feet up. They have lashed themselves to the black needle using braided loops of dead vesseltree, leaning out into the void at forty-five-degree angles like yacht-racers on a trapeze. With the sharp, mineralized edges of their upper limbs, they pare away the translucent flesh of a Choir-Gall, carving it like artisans shaping a tuning fork. 

They do not carve it to kill it; they carve it to change its pitch. 

As they shave strips of sweet, vibrating meat from the bladder—letting the trimmings fall down to the waiting frenzy of amphibians below, or pressing the strips directly against their absorbent bellies to feed—the screaming chord of the Spire shifts. It drops from a ragged, dissonant screech into a pure, booming C-sharp that locks in phase with the Spire half a mile to the north. 

When two Spires lock phase, a standing wave forms in the humid air between them. Within seconds, the chaotic, rifle-crack popping of the walnut-creatures in that sector is forced into rhythm by the crushing acoustic pressure. The random static of the valley resolves, locally, into a beat—a massive, chugging, industrial *thrum-CRACK-thrum-CRACK* that shakes the silver water-rings down the vines twice as fast. 

The Lath hang in the center of this newly tuned chord, letting the massive sound waves wash through their flat, rubbery bodies. This is how they sleep, and how they speak. 

Because they cannot make a sound loud enough to overcome the valley, they communicate by *shadowing*. Two Lath hanging fifty yards apart will angle their flat bodies to bounce the roar of a Spire toward one another, undulating their torsos to modulate the reflected sound. To each other, they look like flashing heliograph mirrors, or cuttlefish rippling with acoustic colors—a silent conversation made entirely of sculpted noise.

If you stand in that plaza long enough, they will eventually come to look at you. 

You won't hear them arrive. You will simply realize that the vertical green lines thirty feet in front of you have been joined by five or six grey lines that weren't there a minute ago. 

To them, you are a bizarre, grotesque anomaly. You are three-dimensional. You are a dense, spherical boulder of water and bone standing right on the moss, crushing the amphibians, ignoring the Traces, casting a huge, ragged, absorbing hole in the acoustic field. You must look to their sonar-skins like a walking blot of pitch-black ink.

They will hang there, ten feet off the ground, motionless except for a slow, rhythmic curling at the edges of their bodies as they breathe the coriander-scented damp through lateral gills. If a Trace flashes beneath you, and the air cools, a ring of silver condensation will form around their flat heads and slide rapidly down their pewter skins. They won't flinch when the walnut-shells detonate around them; if a predator leaps into one by mistake, the Lath’s body simply indents like memory foam, steals the kinetic energy of the impact, and drops the stunned, glassy creature into the moss.

Then, slowly, the five of them will angle their flat chests toward you. 

They will catch the screaming, brassy chord of the nearest Spire on their skins, curve themselves into parabolic dishes, and focus the reflected sound directly at your chest. 

Suddenly, in a circle three feet wide right where you are standing, the deafening roar of the valley vanishes. They have hit you with an exact, phase-inverted acoustic mirror. You are standing in a bubble of dead, eerie silence. Your ears ring wildly in the sudden vacuum. You can hear your own heartbeat, and the wet *squelch* of the neon moss creeping over your boots. 

Through the silence, they will tap a single, gentle rhythm against your ribs—a pulse of focused air pressure, soft as a finger-poke, mapping the density of your lungs, the iron in your blood, the strange, hollow silence of your skull. 

Having weighed you, and found you irrelevant to the tuning of the valley, they will flatten their bodies, release the phase-lock—dropping the deafening weight of the cathedral-roar back onto your shoulders like a physical blow—and snap sideways into the green strings, vanishing edge-on into the purple gloom before your eyes can track the motion.
