To find what truly rules the tenements, you have to wait for the two-hour inhale, when the cold water slides down from the rim and the city goes into its torpor. 

During the four warm hours, the avenues belong to the fast and the numerous—the braided ribbons of silverfish, the snapping eels, the clicking herds of porcelain-crabs. The water is too loud with pressure-wakes and too thick with blowing chalk to notice what is pressed against the masonry. You might swim right past a tenement wall, your shoulder brushing against a patch of pale, leathery limestone, and never realize that the wall was breathing against you.

Only when the thermal chimneys stall, and the white carbonate snow settles over the parapets, does the stone begin to unpeel itself.

It starts as a slow buckling of a wall-face or the sagging of an oval lintel. What looked like a thick, pillowy plastering of travertine reveals a seam, and then a mantle-slit that opens and closes with a sluggish, rhythmic *clench*. From beneath a broad apron of chalk-crusted skin, a dozen flat, muscular ribbons unroll—arms as pale as lard and wide as boot-tongues, their undersides lined not with round suckers, but with double rows of flat, hornyspatulas shaped like mason’s trowels.

They are the Lime-Wrights, though there is no one down here to call them that. They are octopuses that have forgotten the open water entirely, bred over five thousand generations to fit the geometry of a dead worm’s house.

A mature Wright is the size of a butcher’s block, though it has no fixed shape of its own. Its body is a heavy, boneless sack of grey-white muscle that can flatten itself to the thickness of a quilt to slide under a collapsed vault, or bloat with water to plug a four-foot thoroughfare like a cork in a bottle. They have no eyes—only two small, sunken pits behind their mantles that sense the sour-milk chemistry of sulfur and the faint infrared glow of a warm draft. Their skin is their masterpiece. Loaded with specialized glands that drink the heavy dissolved calcium from the water, a Wright sweats a constant, gritty slime of aragonite paste. When it holds still for an hour—which it does often—the paste hardens into a brittle crust of false stone, complete with the pitted texture and green bacterial stains of the surrounding street. When it moves, the crust cracks off in faint, chalky flakes that drift down to join the marl on the floor.

The city is not abandoned; it is under management. 

As the cold current creeps into the outer wards and the silverfish sink drowsily into the cellars, a Wright glides down from the upper Cornice where it has spent the warm hours masquerading as a buttress. It does not swim; it flows over the grooved ramps with the slow, terrifying certainty of heavy oil. As it moves down a hallway, its eight broad arms reach out to touch both walls at once, reading the subtle changes in the draft. 

To a Wright, the city is an enormous wind-instrument, and the drafts are everything. The warm water brings the sulfur that feeds the glowing scum, which feeds the snails, which feed the crabs, which feed the eels. Where the warm draft runs strong, the meat is thick; where an archway has collapsed or a chimney has silted up, the street dies.

And so, in the dark and the cold, the Wrights do their masonry.

Here is an intersection where a ceiling vault has sagged, spilling a heap of dead snail shells and broken worm-tube across a cellar drain. The Wright stops over the rubble. Two of its thicker dorsal arms wrap around an intact pillar to anchor its weight. The remaining six slide into the mess. With a dry, grinding noise that carries for yards through the stone, the creature begins to sort the debris. Empty porcelain-crab carapaces, the heavy calcified skull-plates of dead rays, and chunks of fallen lintel are lifted with delicate, terrible strength. 

The Wright does not merely clear the drain; it narrows it. Sitting hunched over the hole like a washerwoman over a tub, it fits the dead shells together around the rim of the vent, mortaring them in place with thick gobs of mucus coughed up from its siphon—a glue that sets as hard as bone within minutes of hitting the alkaline water. It builds the lip of the drain three inches higher, tilting the angle just slightly toward an adjacent blind alley.

When the four-hour exhale begins again and the hot water surges back up through the bedrock, that altered drain will throw a new, brisk eddy straight into the blind alley. Within twenty minutes, the silverfish will find the warm pocket and crowd into it. Within thirty, the translucent eels will follow them in.

And waiting at the back of that alley, plastered flat against the dead-end wall like a bas-relief of a weeping gargoyle, will be the Wright.

They are patient hunters, utterly devoid of the frantic snap and thrash of the eels. When an eel or a heavy suede-ray blunders into the cul-de-sac, drawn by the custom-built draft, the wall simply detaches from itself. The broad web between the Wright’s arms balloons outward, thick and suffocating as a wet tarpaulin, and drops over the prey. There is no struggle that reaches the street—only a muffled, muscular heave under the rubbery shroud, the sharp *clack* of a parrot-like beak made of black iron-sulfide punching through spine or cartilage, and then a long, quiet hour while the lump beneath the mantle slowly shrinks.

When the meal is done, the Wright does not discard the bones. It carries the ray’s flat tooth-plates or the eel’s skull up three tiers through a vertical chimney shaft, out onto a crumbling parapet where the current is wearing an archway too wide, and cements the bones into the gap.

Because of them, the city’s decay is strangely arrested. Though the ancient tubeworms are long extinct, the metropolis does not collapse into a flat heap of rubble. Instead, over the centuries, its architecture is slowly translating itself from one language into another. The lower wards, where the oldest and heaviest Wrights live—vast, solitary matriarchs with mantles six feet across who have not left their home plazas in fifty years—no longer look like worm-stone at all. The walls there are a knobby, baroque mosaic of crab-claws, snail-spires, and vertebrate skulls, all smoothed over with secreted lime and shaped into curves that please an eight-armed mind. 

They do not love one another, these architects, and they do not work together. Each Wright holds a parish of three or four tenements, and their territories meet at the canals. 

Sometimes, during the cold two-hour lull, two of them will come to opposite sides of a narrow bridge to contest the angle of a draft. A three-foot eel might swim right between them and never know a war is happening. The two creatures sit motionless on the parapets, ten feet apart, facing each other in the absolute black. They cannot see, so they do not flash colors as surface octopuses do. Instead, they sing with their skin—raising and lowering millions of tiny, calcified papillae in synchronized waves, sending low, thrumming pressure-chords across the water that rattle the shells of the sleeping snails below. 

*This current is mine,* one wall hums to the other, a vibration like a heavy cart rolling over cobblestones. *I mortared the flue below. I narrowed the throat.*

And from across the canal, the other wall answers with a slow, grinding bass note that makes the white snow dance on the bridge: *I am heavier. My mortar is thicker. Give me the draft.*

If neither yields, they will meet in the middle of the bridge—not in a tangle of tearing beaks, which would waste precious meat, but in a slow, wrestling embrace that can last for three cycles of the basin’s breath. Sixteen flat arms braid together, trowels locking against trowels, each trying to peel the other off the limestone and cast it down into the silt of the canal. They push and heave in total silence, two pale, rubbery boulders grinding their chalk-crusts together until the water around them is milk-white with their sweat, while the blind silverfish ribbon effortlessly around their straining flanks as if they were just another pair of pillars in the dark.
