At first, you believe the world is entirely automated by its own fever—that the pinching, troweling, and heaving are purely the work of the air and the mud. The yellow brine sloshes past your ankles, the knee-high walls extrude with the wet snap of heavy linen, and nothing seems to stand behind the craft. 

Then you notice a retaining wall that is not disappearing. 

It is fifty yards long, curving slightly against the rhythmic bore of the basin water, and unlike the rest of the Masonry, its mud is drying. It has turned the pale, chalky grey of dead skin. The world is trying to revise it—every few seconds, a tremor runs through the floor, and a fresh wave of wet adobe surges up against the wall’s base, attempting to swallow it back into the soup—but the wall does not yield. 

Sitting atop it is a figure that looks, from a distance, like a dropped sack of lime-putty.

They are called the **Slaked**, though there is no one here to use the name, and they are the closest thing the Masonry has to an elder race. They are not the invisible builders; they are the mistakes the builders failed to scrape away. 

When the world stutters—when a timber scaffold repeats itself three times across the flats, or a chimney-ring slides uphill as tallow—the sheer velocity of the craft sometimes traps a pocket of air, a bundle of pithy reeds, a splash of hot bitumen, and the *memory* of a frantic gesture inside a single skin of clay. If the world’s attention skips before the pocket is smoothed over, the lump remains. It cools. And in cooling, it wakes up.

A Slaked is roughly the size and shape of a crouching human, though its proportions belong to pottery rather than anatomy. Its torso is a heavy, coil-built jar of unbaked slip, lacking neck or shoulders; its head is merely the rim of the vessel, pinched inward like the mouth of a leather water-skin so that the steam inside cannot escape. It has no eyes, only the deep, Crescent-shaped indentations of a giant’s fingernails pressed into the wet clay where a face ought to be. Its limbs are thick, asymmetrical rolls of mud reinforced with cores of braided reed, ending not in hands, but in broad, flat calluses shaped like wooden paddles and wide-toothed combs.

To survive in the Masonry is to fight the Revision. Because a Slaked is made of the same stuff as the floor, the floor is constantly trying to use it. If one of them stands still for three minutes in the ankle-deep brine, the mud of its heels will begin to soften and merge with the plaza; invisible bone awls will score drainage channels up its calves, and its legs will be drawn out into a four-foot parapet before it can tear itself free. 

So they never stop moving, and they never stop *curing* themselves. 

They walk with a heavy, rolling gait that sounds like river-stones grinding together in a bucket. As they move across the sunken courtyards, they harvest the pale, gelatinous vinegar-skins that float on the hot puddles, slapping the wet membranes over their shoulders and backs. The slime dries in the sour-brass heat into a tight, translucent varnish that smells of pickled marrow, insulating their clay from the hungry, invisible trowels of the air. When they find a seep of hot bitumen, they kneel and roll their paddle-shaped forearms in the tar until they are armored to the elbow in black, smoking crusts.

They are creatures of pure, obsessive maintenance, haunted by the muscle-memory of the unseen hands that made them. They do not eat, and they do not sleep. They gather in small, silent gangs of four or five around the "stutters"—those places where the world’s memory has skipped and left three identical, crooked watchtowers in a row. For reasons only their kiln-hot interiors understand, the Slaked cannot bear the sight of the world’s errors. 

Using their flat, calloused limbs and pieces of bone or split timber pried from collapsed tripods, they set to work *correcting* the invisible builders. 

It is a slow, agonizingly quiet war. The unseen force will extrude a ten-foot cistern in three seconds with a wet *slap*; the Slaked will immediately descend upon it, climbing its slick sides, jamming bundles of dry reed into its mortar joints to ruin its balance, and scraping away its thumb-marked ridges with the edges of their wooden shins until the structure loses its tension and sighs back into the water. The world immediately tries to build over *them*—you will see a Slaked suddenly pinned to the waist as a fresh course of brick snaps into existence around its hips—and the others will frantically dig it out, chewing through the wet rawhide bindings with the hardened ceramic rims of their mouth-holes and prying the bricks apart before the floor can claim their brother.

***

Where the Slaked have worked for days, holding a single courtyard against the Revision, the deeper sumps begin to stagnate. The water stops sloshing in twenty-mile bores and settles into deep, sour-smelling cisterns of dark amber brine. 

Here live the **Grout-Eels** and the **Wattle-Crabs**—things that are half-animal, half-architectural debris.

The crabs are not crustaceans. They are the bindings of the great wooden tripods, come undone and gone feral. When a watchtower collapses with that long, wet-grain sigh, the massive knots of wet rawhide and hemp rope that held the timbers together sometimes refuse to unweave. They drop into the hot brine, still under immense torsional tension. Over hours, they absorb the mineral scum and the tallow-grease from the water until they swell into pale, fibrous masses the size of bushel baskets. They walk on eight or ten splayed, ragged ends of stiffened hemp, dragging a heavy "shell" of knotted rawhide behind them. They scuttle along the bases of the levees, using their frayed, wet fringes to sweep up wood-shavings and mineral foam, which they pack into the core of their own knots, growing tighter, heavier, and more inextricably tangled until they can barely move, eventually wedging themselves into drainage channels to become permanent, pulsing bungs that force the hot water to flood new basins.

Below them, in the warm, charcoal murk of the sumps where the brass light fails, are the Grout-Eels—though they are really more like living draught-excluders. They are six-foot ribbons of that strange, grey tallow-grease the bricks turn into when structural logic fails. Rather than hardening back into chimneys, these ribbons slip into the deep water. They swim with a heavy, ribbon-like undulation, completely blind, drawn toward any crack or fissure where steam is hissing out of the floor. When an eel finds a venting mortar-joint, it forces its flat, greasy head into the gap, plugging the steam with its own body. It stays there, parboiling in the vent, absorbing the marsh-gas and minerals until it grows fat and translucent, trembling with the pressure from below, until the floor shifts again and spits it out like a greased cork.

***

And then there are the **Tally-Men**, which are the rarest and most terrible things in the basins, because they are the only things that make a sound of their own.

Sometimes, four miles out, where a single rough-hewn limestone arch stands in its private, impossible glare of amber light, something will be standing beneath the keystone. 

The Tally-Men are tall—eight or nine feet of bone-dry, pale yellow brick stacked in a precarious, swaying column no wider than a chimney flue. Unlike the Slaked, who are wet and curved and desperately sealed in slime, a Tally-Man is entirely dry, held together not by mortar, but by sheer, vertical plumb-weight and a webbing of taut, dry rawhide strips that run up its outside like tendons. 

They cannot walk through the water; the brine would melt their feet and bring them crashing down. They move only along the tops of the knee-high retaining walls and the elevated causeways, stepping with a dry, hollow *clack-clack* like two kiln-fired tiles being struck together. Because the walls they walk on are constantly ending, dissolving, or sliding sideways, a Tally-Man’s life is a slow-motion tightrope act across a disappearing map. When a wall ends, the Tally-Man simply stands at the edge, swaying slightly in the thick steam, until the floor shudders and extrudes a new wall that intersects with its perch. It may wait ten minutes; it may wait forty years.

At the top of the brick column, where a head should be, is a heavy timber yoke, and suspended from the yoke by a twisted braid of pith is a single, massive plumb-bob of black bitumen. 

Nobody knows what the Tally-Men are measuring, or for whom. When one reaches a newly extruded ziggurat or a stuttering row of scaffolds, it bends its brick column at a terrifying angle—the dry rawhide tendons groaning like a ship’s rigging—and lowers the bitumen plumb-bob until it hovers an inch above the wet, thumb-marked clay. 

Where the black drop hangs, the invisible builders *stop*. 

For twenty yards around a Tally-Man, the frantic pinching and troweling ceases. The bone awls do not score the mud; the wooden floats do not erase footprints. The world goes completely still, holding its breath under the judgement of that black weight. The Tally-Man will stand there for an hour, its bricks clicking softly against one another as it shifts its weight, inspecting the millions of sharp, fresh knuckle-prints in the mud. 

Then, from the hollow flue of its body, it lets out a sound—not a voice, but the low, flute-like hoot of wind being blown across the top of a giant earthen jug. 

The moment the sound dies, the Tally-Man steps back onto its wall, and the floor beneath the plumb-bob does not merely revise itself—it *drops*. The basin opens like a trapdoor into the charcoal murk below, swallowing the ziggurat, the brine, the vinegar-skins, and any Slaked who were too slow to scramble away, before snapping shut again with a wet, deafening slap that sends a ring of yellow foam racing out for twenty miles in every direction.
