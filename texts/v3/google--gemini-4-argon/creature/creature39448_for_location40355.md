If you stand on a walkway for long enough, letting your boots grow warm in the standing water and your breathing slow to match the thermal sigh of the vents, your eyes will eventually adjust to the deeper tolerances of the ironwork. 

You will notice that a section of the four-foot intake pipe ten yards away is not cast steel, though it has the same dull, wet-slate gleam. Or rather, it is not *entirely* steel. The blind flange bolted to the end of the main—a heavy disc of grey material meant to seal off a dead-end run—is breathing. Its movement is so slight that it only betrays itself by the behaviour of the condensate: the water droplets running down its rim do not fall at the steady three-second interval of the surrounding iron, but hesitate, drawn briefly back upward by a microscopic, rhythmic expansion.

These are the *Lagging*, though there is no one here to name them, and they would not recognize a noun if they heard one. They are the greater fauna of the Sump-Decks, the broad, heavy-bodied intermediaries between the cast metal and the carpet of smaller mouths.

At first, they seem merely like larger cousins to the gasket-slugs: vast, mattress-thick mantles of dense, mineral-impregnated cartilage and rubbery hide that have grown over the dead-weights, the counterbalances, and the secondary manifolds. Some of them are the size of billiard tables, draped over the crowns of the low boiler-houses like wet tarpaulins; others are long and cylindrical, wrapped so seamlessly around the straight pipe-runs that they look like standard asbestos insulation jacketed in painted canvas. Their skin has the exact pebbled grain of sand-cast iron, and along their margins, their flesh naturally forms stiff, regular crenellations that mimic the hexagonal heads of three-inch bolts. 

Why they mimic the foundry-work when there are no eyes to fool is a question that belongs to an older, louder world. Here, shape is simply a matter of accommodation. The Sump-Decks are a total geometry; to exist in them without causing friction, a thing must take on the grammar of the casting.

The Lagging do not roam. A young one—if anything in this unclocked afternoon can be called young—might spend four years sliding thirty feet along a sluice-trough, moving by the slow, hydraulic inflation of thousands of microscopic pads on its underside, never disturbing the gasket-slugs that ride upon its back as if it were just another boiler plate. It is looking for a *leak*. Not a rupture—the Decks do not rupture—but a place where the static equilibrium of the machinery is slightly out of phase. A union where the internal pressure is half a pound too high; a condensing basin where the water is three degrees too warm; a bronze bush that is weeping a fraction too much tallow.

When a Lagging finds such a spot, it settles. It uncurls its heavy, flanged margins and presses its pale, greased underside over the fitting. And then it begins the work that defines its kind: it becomes a part of the schematic.

If you were to pry up the edge of an established Lagging—a task that would require a crowbar and the strength of three men, so powerful is the suction of its grease-sealed rim—you would not find a simple mouth or a stomach. You would find an intricate, wet musculature that has molded itself into a living valve-chest. The creature’s ventral surface extrudes stiff, keratinous baffles, tubes of muscular gristle, and bladder-chambers that lock directly over the vents and petcocks of the machinery. 

It takes the excess heat, the stray pressure, the weeping tallow, and routes them through its own body. 

Walk past an old boiler house where three or four of the great, slate-colored things have grown together over the decades, their edges fused like cold-welded lead, and you can hear them working inside themselves. Beneath the *drip-pat* of the condensate, there is a deep, visceral *glug-shhh*, the sound of a forty-gallon stomach acting as a steam-trap. They bleed off the high-pressure vapor from the iron pipes, pass it through yards of coiled, water-jacketed gut to steal its calories, and exhale it through fluted blow-holes on their backs as a warm, sweet-smelling mist that feeds the drifting hydrogen-bladders above.

Over time, they lose the ability to leave. The crisp serial numbers and foundry marks of the iron beneath them press into their undersides, becoming permanent, reversed scars in their tough hide. Their hexagonal edge-lobes harden into true horn, locking around the real bolt-heads until the creature is mechanically fastened to the deck. Small valve-crabs take up residence in the folds of their breathing-siphons, keeping the passages clear of silt; porcelain-worms thread through the dead, calloused layers of their outer skin, stitching the animal to the rust-paste in the seams.

And deeper down—or rather, further *in*, where the baffle-tiers and the unlit condenser- vaults sit submerged in the warm, motionless dark beneath the walkways—the distinction between the fauna and the plumbing disappears entirely.

Drop a weighted line through a four-inch grate where the crabs have parted, down past the first three levels of iron floor, and you will hit the *Sump-Marrow*. 

Down there, the warmth is higher, the water thick as broth with dissolved shells and tallow. There is no sky-light to encourage the neat, segmented boundaries of crab and slug. Instead, there is a continuous, chambered mass that fills the lower scaffolding from piling to piling. It is a colonial organism, a blind, thousand-mile organ of grey gristle, calcified tubes, and pulsing, tallow-lined cisterns that has completely replaced the lower return-pipes. 

It does not merely live on the machinery; it is the return-loop of the world. It drinks the heavy, mineral-laden water that rains down from the upper decks, filters out the dissolved iron and the dead flesh through miles of spongy, hepatic tissue, and then—by the slow, unified contraction of a million muscular chambers braced against the iron pilings—pumps the distilled, heated water and rendered tallow back up through the standpipes to the surface. 

The profound, muffled thrum you feel when you lay your hand against an intake pipe on the walkway above? It is not an engine. The engines are locked at mid-stroke, their flywheels beaded with sweat, held as permanent monuments in the cream-colored light. The pressure that holds them there—the vast, balanced hydraulic tension that keeps every gauge needle pinned steady at the top of its dial from horizon to horizon—is the steady, isometric clench of the Marrow below, holding the world at blood-heat, forever at the top of its breath.
