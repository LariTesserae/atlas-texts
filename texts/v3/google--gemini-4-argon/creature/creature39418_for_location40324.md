To see the architect of the cairns, you cannot look at the center of the room. If you stare directly at the driftwood doorway, you will only see the pale shrimp-things boiling over the bone pegs and the gray handkerchiefs slipping into the water beads. You have to look through the doorframe from the right side—the side where the three-sided clay kennel exists—and wait for your eyes to stop trying to invent shadows.

Because there is no contrast in the blister, he is nearly invisible until he moves. 

He is roughly the size and shape of a seven-year-old child, or perhaps a large, upright badger, though his proportions refuse to settle. He is made of the same damp, unhardened white tallow as the walls, except that in him, the mineral has curdled into something fibrous and stringy, like the meat of a crab or wet, braided linen. He has no skin, because nothing in the room is old enough to have developed a rind. Instead, his surface is a tight weave of pale, shivering muscle-bundles that constantly split and re-knit as he moves, shedding tiny flakes of dried lime when he bends his knees.

He is the Mason, though he does not build to shelter himself. He builds to keep the room from collapsing into a single, solid point.

When he emerges from the kennel, he does not walk out the open front. He steps backward through the solid clay wall on the left—the wall that isn’t there if you are looking from the other side of the room—and pulls a lump of the structure out with him, his hands sunk wrist-deep into the gray mud. 

His hands are extraordinary and awful. They are the only things in the chamber with color: a bruised, venous plum- shade, heavy and calloused, with seven fingers on the left and four thick, opposing thumbs on the right. They do not belong to his body. They look as though they were borrowed from a drowned giant, scaled down, and grafted onto his chalky forearms with crude, visible stitches of translucent grass. The hands are older than the room. They know what friction is.

He works in the dead, wool-packed silence with the frantic, twitching precision of a water beetle. 

He has no eyes—in a place where light comes from inside the dirt and the air, eyes are redundant, an invitation to blindness. Instead, his head is a smooth, faceless turnip of white cartilage, ringed around the crown with a dozen deep, flute-like pits. As he moves between the cairns, he swings his head from side to side, catching the heavy, celery-scented air in these pits. He navigates by the subtle differences in barometric pressure and by the heat of the walls’ birth-spasms.

His job—or his compulsion, since there is no one to pay him—is the calibration of the room's geometry. 

Because the chamber has no shadows and no depth, the space within it is constantly trying to fold flat, like an poorly set trap. A wall ten feet away doesn't just *look* as though it is pressed against the cornea; if left unattended for an hour, it actually *will* be. Distance here is not a fixed law; it is a soft, greasy medium that has to be propped open with sticks and bricks.

The Mason walks to one of the low cairns. A handkerchief-predator is draped over the top brick, digesting a cluster of kicking bird-shrimp in a silent boil of froth. The Mason does not shoo it away. With his four-thumbed right hand, he pinches the edge of the gray predator, lifts it like a wet pancake, and tucks it into a fold in his own fibrous torso. The predator does not attack him; it simply adheres to his ribs, absorbing the warm lime-sweat that oozes from his seams, its many small eyes rolling inward in what looks like stupor.

With his seven-fingered left hand, the Mason takes a single unbaked brick from the top of the cairn, turns ninety degrees, and presses it into the empty, blinding air three feet above the floor. 

He holds it there for ten seconds, his chalky legs trembling with the strain, until the air decides to accept the measurement. When he lets go, the brick does not fall. It stays suspended in the glare, anchored to an invisible shelf of newly established depth. Instantly, the wall behind it—which had been creeping forward to swallow the driftwood doorway—snaps back six feet in a silent, violent blur of perspective.

The sudden shift in volume causes a localized birth-spasm in the floor between his feet. A razor-sharp pleat of fresh tallow-rock shoots upward like a blade, slicing cleanly through the Mason’s left ankle.

He does not flinch, because even if he screamed, the sound wouldn't reach his own ears. He simply steps out of the severed foot. As his stump hits the floor, the white mineral of the ground rises up in a warm, welcoming lip, fusing with his shin and extruding a brand-new, slightly clubbed foot of fresh porcelain-tallow before he has finished taking his next stride. Behind him, the severed foot is already gone, swarmed by fifty thumb-sized grazers that strip the fibrous linen-meat to the bone in seconds, while the bone itself sinks back into the floor to become a tiny, decorative ridge.

He is not alone in the kennel, though the other thing never comes out.

If you press your face right up against the driftwood doorframe—close enough that the bone pegs smell of old marrow and salt, close enough that your eyelashes brush the six-inch ceiling where the corkscrew grass ceases to exist—you can hear what is inside the three-sided house. Because the acoustic horizon is only an inch wide, the kennel acts as a bell jar, trapping a tiny, dense pocket of noise inside its damp clay walls.

What lives in the kennel is the Mason’s wife, or his mother, or his tool-bag. 

It is a round, legless mass of dark, oiled leather and black feathers, roughly the size of a medicine ball, wedged tightly into the back corner. It is the only dark thing in the blinding room, and looking at it feels like dropping a stone down a well; the eye falls into it with a physical lurch of relief. 

It does not belong to this geology. It smells of dry things: prairie dust, mouse-droppings, creosote, and iron. Forty feet above, on the dry scrub plain, something died in a burrow a hundred years ago, and through some fault in the lithosphere, its memory or its carcass leaked downward and became the seed around which this white blister festered.

The dark bundle is breathing, very slowly, and as it breathes, it makes a continuous, low, grinding sound—like two dry millstones turning against each other with a few grains of corn between them. It is the source of the clay, the driftwood, and the bone pegs. Every few minutes, the leather sack shudders, and a small, dry aperture in its side dilates to cough up a rectangular brick of unbaked gray mud, or a splinter of bleached wood, or the femur of a jackrabbit.

The Mason reaches through the driftwood frame—coming in from the right, so his arm disappears up to the elbow for anyone watching from the left—and collects the new brick. His plum-colored hands are gentle when they touch the black feathers. He strokes the bundle once, leaving a smear of white lime across the dark oil, and if your ear is pressed against the clay wall, you can hear him make a sound back at it. He presses the flute-like pits of his head directly against the leather and blows air through them, producing a wet, four-note chord, like someone blowing across the tops of medicine bottles filled to different levels with water.

Then the light dims. The air pressure drops, making the Mason's fibrous joints swell until they creak.

The thirty-second upward rain begins. Heavy, mercury-thick drops of water tear themselves off the floor and shoot toward the ceiling. The Mason does not take cover. He stands in the dead center of the chamber, beside the three-foot doorway, and tips his faceless head down toward the floor. 

As the water and the kicking shrimp-things stream past him toward the roof, he opens his chest. The braided white muscles over his sternum part like curtains, revealing a hollow thoracic cavity lined with the same wire-stiff, translucent corkscrew grass that grows on the floor. As the upward rain passes through him, the grass inside his ribs untwists with that dry, piercing hiss, catching the rising water droplets and the panicked grazers like baleen in a whale's mouth. 

When the light snaps back to its full, shadowless noon, his chest closes. The grass inside him coils tight again, crushing the shrimp and the water into a cold, lime-scented paste that seeps directly into his white flesh, turning him briefly translucent, like oiled paper, before he hardens back into tallow.

He wipes the clear digestive froth of the handkerchief-predator from his hip, picks up his fresh clay brick, and walks toward a corner that has begun to lose its third dimension, his mismatched, ancient hands already patting the air to find out where the wall is supposed to be.
