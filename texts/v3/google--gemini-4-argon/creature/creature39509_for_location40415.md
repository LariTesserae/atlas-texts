Look first at the standing waves in the shallow basins. The turbine’s drone holds the water in a permanent geometric grid of tiny, trembling diamonds. Where the glare of the sun hits them, the reflections on the black basalt ceiling don't dance; they sit in a blurred, vibrating lattice. 

In the narrowest crevices between the hexagonal columns, where the gunnera leaves roof over the stone and block the sky, that reflected light is the only illumination. Something hangs in those vertical cracks, anchored to the weeping rock by threads of hardened, amber-colored mucus. 

At first glance, they look like the shed skins of the golden salamanders, or pale bladderworts swollen with water. They are roughly the size and shape of a human hand, minus the thumb, and entirely translucent. You can see the dark basalt straight through them, distorted only by the faint, yellow-green refractive index of their jelly. They have no eyes, no mouths, and no digestive tracts. They are essentially living tuning forks.

Inside each gelatinous body runs a branching structure of dark, metallic filaments—copper and iron leached from the sweating pipe-jackets and the volcanic ash, precipitated out of the water and laid down like the veins of a leaf. As the acoustic pressure of the turbine batters the rock walls, these suspended sacs shiver in exact sympathy with the reduction gears. They are piezoelectric organisms, evolved rapid and strange in the decades since the heavy-lift rotors beat the air above the notch and lowered the cast-iron wellhead onto the stone. They do not eat the moss or the beetles; they feed directly on the mechanical waste-energy of the roar, converting the endless, bone-shaking vibration into a trickle of current that drives their slow, blind metabolism. 

Every forty seconds, when the relief valve breaks the drone with its sharp *chug-hiss*, the sudden shift in frequency causes every sac in the dark crevices to contract at once. They squeeze shut like tiny fists, expressing a drop of clear, warm fluid that smells faintly of ozone, before relaxing back into the hum.

Because of them, the crabs are no longer quite crabs.

When a pale vent-crab sidles out from beneath the copper-mesh belly of a steam pipe, it moves with a strange, ticking syncopation. Its shell is no longer entirely its own. Welded into the seams of its carapace, especially around the joints of its chelae and the ridge above its eyestalks, are small, flat nodes of that same clear, copper-veined jelly. 

In the blinding, unfiltered ultraviolet glare of the high altitude, an unshielded deep-vent crustacean would go blind and die of cellular breakdown within days. The jelly-nodes act as living polarizing filters and parasols, refracting the harsh sunlight into a cool, greenish twilight under the shell. In return, the crab carries the sessile organisms out of the dark cracks and into the mineral-rich warm pools and the copper-sweat of the pipes. 

Because the jelly contracts on the forty-second mark of the relief valve, the crabs have been broken to the machine's clock. A crab will be halfway across a slick basalt table, reach to tear a ribbon of blue-green algae from an alloy bolt, and as the *chug-hiss* fires, the nodes in its joints clench. The crab freezes, its claws locked at a right angle, waiting for the pressure to drop and the drone to smooth out again before it can finish bringing the food to its mouth. They march and pause, march and pause, all across the terrace, like clockwork toys wound by the steam.

And then there is the thing under the gunnera, which the crabs are building, or which is building itself out of the crabs.

Follow the three insulated pipes to the middle of the terrace, where the black sludge of rotted moss is deepest—nearly knee-high in the troughs between the buried column tops. Here, the three pipes converge briefly before splitting toward the turbine pilings, creating a triangular cradle of cast iron and sweating copper mesh that stays at a steady hundred and ten degrees. The giant gunnera leaves above it have grown to the size of cartwheels, their undersides coated in a thick, velvety condensation that drips constantly back into the cradle.

In that dark, steaming hollow, the shed carapaces of dead crabs have not decayed. Over years, the crabs have crawled into this warmest pocket to molt or die, and the jelly-sacs have grown over the empty shells, cementing them together with tough, rubbery protein and threads of precipitated copper.

It is now a single mass about five feet long, shaped roughly like the trough it sits in—a low, sprawling mound of translucent, amber-white cartilege and pale chitin plates, hugging the curvature of the three pipes. It has no head, unless the cast-iron flange of the central pipe counts as its head, which it has entirely encased in a thick, pulsing collar of clear tissue. 

It is not asleep, and it is not quite an animal, though it breathes. Its breathing is coupled to the wellhead. You can watch a slow peristaltic wave travel down its five-foot length, a ripple of pale plates and glowing yellow veins that starts at the iron flange and rolls toward the cliff edge, timed to the deep, subterranean thrum of the magma vent beneath the floor.

It is the only thing in the notch that knows the world below the clouds exists. 

A single tendril of the mass—thick as a garden hose, armored in overlapping rings of old crab-leg shells—has crept out from under the gunnera canopy, following the path of the braided power cable. It stops just short of the razor-edged lip where the water blows backward into the freezing gale. There, the tendril wraps three times around the algae-slick cable, pressing a broad, calloused pad of copper-veined flesh tight against the braided steel sheathing.

Through that pad, it listens to the sixty-hertz alternating current dropping down the mountain, and more importantly, it feels the tiny, micro-second fluctuations in the load from the unseen enterprise miles below. When a smelter starts up in the lowlands, or a grid switches over in a valley town, the resistance on the turbine changes by a fraction of a percent. The pitch of the reduction gears drops by a hair. The standing waves in the pools widen by a tenth of a millimeter.

When that happens, a blush of dark, rust-colored pigment blooms in the center of the mass under the leaves, spreading outward through its gelatinous body like ink in water. The golden salamanders, hanging blind in the warm basins nearby, feel the shift in the electric field of the water; without waking, their gill-fronds flare wide, turning from pale yellow to a deep, bruised violet, holding the color until the lowland load stabilizes and the turbine settles back into its ancient, deafening note.
