To find the people who live here, you have to stop looking for silhouettes against the cobalt sky. Nothing that stands upright for long survives the glare without going blind, and nothing that builds walls can keep them standing against the slow, thermal heave of the travertine. 

You find them by watching the water. When a mist-bank rolls across a shelf and vanishes, it leaves a dark, glistening skin of moisture over the cream-colored stone. Within minutes, the evaporation begins, retreating toward the center of the shallow basins. Yet here and there, across the blinding expanse, the water does not retreat evenly. It pulls together into narrow, deliberate channels, flowing slightly uphill or gathering in the lee of a low, chalky mound that was not there an hour ago.

Wait for the puddle to dry completely, and the mound will unfold.

At first, they appear to be boulders of porous tufa or heaps of shed lichen crust. When they rise into a crouch, their posture is low, wide, and horizontal, balanced between long, calloused heels and knuckles padded with plates of split horn. They are hominids, perhaps, in the deep history of their bones, though millennia on the shelf have smoothed their angles just as the water has smoothed the stone. Their skin is dry, thick, and deeply gullied, colored a dead-matte cream that holds chalk dust and dried bacterial meal in its creases. They do not sweat—to sweat in that thin, radiant air is to desiccate in an hour. Instead, like the fox-sized grazers they share the stone with, they bleed excess heat through wide, ribbed membranes of cartilage and blood vessels that flare from behind their jawbones down to their collarbones, flushing dark violet when they run and fading to stone-white when they rest.

Their eyes are tucked deep beneath shelf-like brows, protected from the brutal ultraviolet glare by double-slitted lids of dark, oily tissue. They never look up at the sky unless they are under the refractive shroud of a node; instead, their gaze is permanently fixed on the four-foot horizon of the limestone shelf, reading the micro-topography of flutes, runs, and basins.

A band consists of six to ten individuals. More than that would drink a basin dry before the microbial mat could thicken into a harvestable skin; fewer could not defend a kill from the six-legged scale-hounds. 

They have no spoken language. Whether their ancestors lost their voices to the thin air or abandoned speech because the nodes made it useless, their throats are now merely valves for breathing the warm mist and swallowing wet algae. Even away from the fifty-foot dead zones of the machines, a shout on the plateau is a wasted effort that dries the lungs and carries barely thirty yards. Instead, they speak with light and posture, borrowing the vocabulary of the gliding scavengers above them. 

Bound to their forearms and chests with cured bladder-reed fibers are flat, polished wafers of iridescent shell—pried from the backs of the stone-crabs—and scales of mirror-bright keratin taken from dead hounds. By rolling a shoulder or canting a wrist, a runner can catch the fierce sun and throw a needle of blue, green, or blinding white light half a mile across the white rock. 

Watch a band working a shelf after a heavy fog. There is no sound at all—not a grunt, not the scrape of a heel. A crouch and a double-flash of green from an elder at the basin’s rim means *the mat is thick here; scrape.* A high, trembling arc of white light thrown from a scout three hundred yards away means *scale-hounds lying in the shimmer of the black ribbon.* The messages flick across the blinding stone like silent sparks, answered instantly by shifts in the geometry of the band.

When they enter the fifty-foot radius of a node, where the air turns thick and cottony and sound dies completely, their communication drops to touch and the subtle heat-flushing of their neck-frills. 

The nodes are their hearths, their workshops, and their graves, though the people treat the artifacts with a complete, unsuperstitious indifference. To them, a cluster of floating silver polyhedra is no more sacred than a limestone flute; it is simply a place where the rules of the rock change, and those changes can be used.

In the dead of the high-altitude night—when the cobalt sky goes black and the air away from the anomalies drops toward freezing—the bands gather within twenty paces of the machines. They do not touch the black ribbons or the silver polyhedra; they know that anything pushed directly against a node’s surface loses its heat and its motion entirely, dropping to the stone as brittle as glass. Instead, they sleep in the warm halo where the limestone bleeds waste energy. They lie piled together like pale roots, their violet neck-frills tucked tight against their chests, completely invisible inside the refractive shimmer that bends the starlight around them. A predator looking directly at the node sees only a warped miraging of empty travertine.

They use the nodes to hunt, turning the machines' physics into snares. Because sound cannot cross the boundary of a node’s field, the people have learned the exact inch where the dampening begins. Two hunters will lie flat in the blinding glare outside a node, motionless for three hours while the mist-banks pass over them, coating their chalky skin in slick moisture and red spores until they look like rotting stone. When a herd of the pale, long-legged ungulates drifts into the node’s silent zone to lick the warm condensation from the rock, the hunters do not charge. They simply stand and flick their mirrors, herding the panicked animals *through* the node's center.

As an ungulate bolts past a floating polyhedron or beneath an arch of matte-black ribbon, the local gravity and inertia warp just enough to throw off a fast runner's stride. The sponged hooves slip on the wet travertine; a leg snaps in complete, cottony silence. Before the creature can thrash its way out of the refractive shimmer, the hunters are on it, using long, needle-pointed ribs of cured bone to pierce the heart. There is no cry, no thud of a body hitting the rock—just a sudden blooming of dark blood against the cream-colored stone, already steaming in the warm air.

Butchery happens at a frantic, practiced sprint. Everything on the plateau is a race against evaporation and the heliograph flashes of the overhead scavengers. Within six minutes, the ungulate is reduced to strips of pale meat, a bundle of sinew, and the prized, water-rich organs. The hunters pack the meat into the translucent, severed stalks of bladder-reeds, sealing the ends with twisted plugs of wire-scrub to keep the moisture inside. What they cannot carry—the skull, the hooves, the stomach sack—they drag ten paces away from the node and leave in the open glare as a tax for the six-legged hounds, ensuring the predators have no reason to follow the band’s scent trail.

They have no fire. Nothing on the plateau burns except dried lichen, and smoke would bring every scavenger for five miles. Instead, they cure their meat and cook their algorithmic pastes of red algae and ribbon-amphibians by burying the sealed bladder-reeds in the deep fissures directly beneath a thermal node, where the stone is hot enough to blister bare skin. 

If you follow an elder—one whose skin has grown so encrusted with mineral deposits from a lifetime of lying in evaporating pools that their knees and elbows click like river stones—you will eventually see the one thing they do that is not strictly about water or calories.

Every band carries a handful of small, smooth limestone pebbles, no larger than thumbnails, worn perfectly spherical by the ancient water runs. When a member of the band grows too slow to outrun the evaporation of the pools, they do not wait to be taken by the hounds. They walk alone into the center of one of the floating silver clusters or step directly through the loop of a matte-black ribbon. 

There is no ceremony. The band watches from the edge of the fifty-foot hush. The old one steps into the tight, humming focal point where the machine meets the geology, and simply stops. The air around them shimmers violently for a second as the node harvests the kinetic energy of their blood, their breath, the heat of their liver, the last electrical flickers of their brain. They do not fall. They are instantly, gently desiccated, their water pulled out into a small, warm puff of local fog that drifts away across the shelf, leaving behind a light, porous statue of chalk-colored bone and dry skin that the wind will sand away to nothing within a week.

When the mist of the old one’s body clears and condenses into the nearest spoon-shaped basin, the band walks forward into the silence. They kneel at the new puddle, drink the warm water before the sun can take it, and leave one of their smooth limestone pebbles wedged into the rock at the node’s base—a tiny, pale counterweight against the incomprehensible work going on beneath their feet. Then they stand, flash a single spark of blue light to the scout waiting on the ridge, and lope away across the blinding stone toward the next wall of fog.
