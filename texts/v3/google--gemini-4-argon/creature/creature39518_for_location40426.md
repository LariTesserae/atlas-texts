To have a spine in a place like this is a death sentence. A skeleton is a commitment to a single shape, and the valley does not tolerate fixed shapes for more than four or five minutes at a time. A ribcage cannot negotiate a four-ton manifold sliding flush against a retaining wall; a pelvis cannot survive the sudden, scissor-like closure of two alignment tracks. 

What lives here—what *truly* lives here, below the notice of the stray dipper bird and the occasional wind-blown spider—has abandoned the rigidity of bone entirely.

At first glance, you would mistake them for the industrial sludge itself, or perhaps a particularly dense mat of liverwort and spilled axle grease clinging to the underside of a crossbeam. They are known only to the ancient, dead ledgers of the valley’s builders as contamination, though they have evolved far beyond mere fouling. They are the *gleam-liners*, or the *valve-leeches*—a vast, slow-moving population of colonial, rubbery invertebrates that have made the shifting geometry of the pass their circulatory system.

Individually, a single organism is no larger than a human thumb: a flat, muscle-dense slug of dark, iridescent gunmetal gray, slick with a mucus that smells faintly of mutton tallow and copper sulfate. They have no eyes, because there is nothing to see in the amber-lit fog but the next wall of wet iron. Instead, their entire dorsal surface is a continuous, sensitive lateral line—like that of a blind cave fish—tuned exquisitely to the sub-audible tremors of the machinery.

They rarely stay individual for long. In the tight, warm crevices where high-voltage copper braids run parallel to steam returns, they aggregate into great, braided ropes and flattened sheets, fusing their mantle-flesh together into a single, coordinated super-organism that can stretch for twenty feet along a girder. In this state, they look remarkably like the bulging, sweating rubber hoses that writhe across the walkways, or the thick collars of dark moss that cushion the hydraulic rams.

Their life is a continuous, slow-motion dance with the shearing steel. 

Watch a junction box where two massive turntable bridges are about to lock together. Three seconds before the pneumatic valves even fire—while the pressure is still just a rising hum in the hydro-lines half a mile down the saddle—the dark, glistening mat of flesh clinging to the shearing edge reacts. It feels the sixty-cycle hum change pitch; it reads the microscopic shudder in the rivets. Before the fifty-ton plates can meet with their muffled *thud*, the colony has already unspooled itself, pouring like heavy, dark mercury down a vertical grease-channel, vacating the pinch-point with two inches to spare. 

If a bulkhead moves too fast—some violent, emergency shunt of load from the dams below—and slices through a colony before it can fully retract, there is no panic, no thrashing, and no blood. The severed halves simply seal their bluish-gray edges with a quick contraction of ring-muscles, and now there are two colonies where there was one, each sliding off in opposite directions along the newly formed ridge of turf.

They feed on the friction of the valley. The plants here—the sliding, nodule-rooted ferns and the anchor-chain vines—are constantly being bruised, sheared, and pulped by the machinery. Every time a counterweight plummets and crushes a curtain of ivy against a cast-iron housing, it leaves behind a smear of rich, green, sugary sap mixed with black lithium grease and rust-scale. The colonies flow over these fresh scars within minutes. They extrude pale, ruffled feeding membranes from their undersides, digesting the crushed cellulose, the petroleum hydrocarbons, and the iron oxide alike. In return, their heavy, tallow-like slime coats the tracks and slides. They are the reason the fifty-ton cranes move in such eerie, cushioned silence; the machinery is lubricated not just by ancient reservoirs of oil, but by the living, self-healing skin of its inhabitants.

Deeper down—thirty feet below the diamond-plate walkways, where the sodium bulbs burn in permanent, suffocating pockets of amber fog and the warmth of the transformers keeps the air at a feverish, misty eighty degrees—something larger has learned to use the colonies.

It is not a beast, not in the way a mountain lion or an eagle is a beast. It has no head, and its boundaries are negotiable. It is an *amalgam*, a nomadic architecture of the valley's own debris, held together by a particularly massive, ancient core of the gray colony-flesh.

It moves through the lower tiers like a slow, purposeful landslide of wet compost and scrap. Its body is a yard-wide bundle of severed copper wires, shed fern-fronds, twisted brass shavings, and hollow iron pipe-fittings, all knitted together by thick, muscular cables of dark, glistening tissue. It uses the discarded pieces of the valley as temporary bones. When it needs to pry open the rusted inspection hatch of a warm cistern to reach the rich, sulfur-eating bacterial mats inside, it shifts its internal hydrostatic pressure, locking three or four lengths of broken steel conduit together within its flesh to form a rigid lever, bracing itself against a piston housing to pop the latch. Once the hatch is open, the temporary "bones" are uncoupled, and the creature pours itself through the two-inch gap like thick soup, leaving the scrap iron clattering softly on the grating outside, only to gather new pieces on the other side.

These larger amalgams are the closest thing the valley has to engineers. They have a deep, instinctual intolerance for anything that interrupts the smooth, rhythmic hum of the grid. A jammed valve or a dry bearing produces a high, grating vibration—a metallic screech that is agony to creatures whose entire bodies are ears. 

When a woody vine grows too thick and threatens to stall a governor assembly, or when a fallen slab of crumbling concrete wedges itself into the teeth of a track-switch, the discordant vibration radiates through the wet iron for half a mile. Within an hour, the amber haze in the under-tiers will thicken with movement. Two or three of the large amalgams will converge on the fault, hauling their patchwork bodies up through the narrow gaps in the plumbing. 

They will wrap themselves around the offending vine or stone, anchoring their muscular strands to the nearest moving hydraulic ram. As the ram sighs and retracts on its normal cycle, the amalgam acts as a living block-and-tackle, transferring the machine’s own blind, multi-ton force onto the obstruction until the vine snaps with a muffled *crack* or the concrete crumbles into harmless powder. 

Then, as the sixty-cycle hum settles back into its smooth, hypnotic equilibrium, the creatures relax. They release their scrap-iron levers, digest the crushed remains of the vine, and slowly melt back down through the grating, dripping alongside the dark ribbons of condensation into the warm, greased dark below. 

Nothing here calls out. Nothing marks a territory, because the territory you mark at noon will be forty yards to the left and inverted by three o'clock. There is only the slow, slick circulation of dark flesh through the iron veins of the mountain pass—listening to the pressure, eating the friction, and keeping the machine quiet.
