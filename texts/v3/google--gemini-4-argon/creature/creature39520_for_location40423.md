You do not see it at first because you are looking for an animal, and there is no room for an animal’s habits here. You are looking for something that hides in a crevice or hunts the stuttering beetle, something with a belly and a den. 

Instead, look at the three-ply vine rope lashed to the ceiling burl. Watch the clove hitch. 

Under the ferocious drag of the ceiling, the green fibers of the knot are slowly crushing themselves into slime, slipping a millimeter every few seconds. Before the hitch can fail, something reaches out from the wall behind the burl and drops a dry, bone-hard toggle of dead heartwood into the bight of the rope. The knot catches against the toggle, jams tight, and holds. 

The limb that placed it there does not retract into a hole. It stays where it is, resting against the taut rope, because it is made of the same fibrous, pale-green phloem as the rope itself. Only when you trace that limb backward do you realize it is connected to a shoulder, and the shoulder is connected to a narrow, multi-jointed chassis suspended three feet off the floor in a web of self-woven rigging.

It is the chamber’s Second Thought. 

If the room is a blind, panicked carpentry, this creature is the joiner that the panic had to invent once the simple levers and mallets began to get in each other’s way. It is only as old as the room—perhaps four days, perhaps a week—conceived when a shooting strut of thigh-thick xylem collided mid-air with a descending club-branch, shattering both and leaving a tangle of weeping, twitching splinters suspended in the Tuesday-afternoon light. The wall could not reabsorb the mess across the gap, so the mess had to organize itself.

At a glance, it looks like a bundle of long-handled shop tools—calipers, drawknives, and wooden clamps—that have been bound together with wet rawhide and hung up to dry. It has no head, because there is nothing to eat and no direction from which danger approaches; the sourceless light comes from everywhere at once, and the roar of the water-columns is omnidirectional. In place of a skull, its upper termination is a tight, bulbous knuckle of burls and tension- blisters that constantly twists a quarter-turn left, then a quarter-turn right, reading the barometric pressure of the room and the low-frequency groan of the surrounding root-tectonics.

Its body is a lattice of pale, skinless boxwood, no thicker than a bundles of lathe strips, held under extreme bow-tension by glistening cables of twisted sinew-vine. It does not use muscles that expand and contract through blood; it uses hydraulics. When it needs to move, it opens a valve at its hip, allowing the ferocious pressure of the wall’s water-columns to shoot through a temporary umbilical hose into its hollow limbs. The limbs snap straight with the speed and violence of a sprung trap. To fold them again, it vents the water from small, fluted gill-slits along its ribs—shooting brief, hissing jets of hot mist into the air that immediately contribute to the room’s three-second rainstorms.

Because the floor is a trap of lashing tendrils, the creature never comes down. It lives entirely in the rigging. It moves like a spider made of carpenter’s folding rules, unhinging itself across the room along the newly erupted cross-beams, hooking an elbow over a taut vine, sliding twenty feet down a weeping resin-line, and catching itself with a dry, wooden *clack*.

As you watch it, the creature changes what it is for. 

At first, it seems to be helping the room close itself. It scuttles over to the rhythmic mallet-branch that is pounding wedges into the floor-fissure. The branch has beaten a wedge in crooked; the next strike will split the timber and ruin the brace. In the two-second interval while the club is rising on its green hinge, the creature drops down on a tether, hooks a scythe-like forefoot behind the crooked wedge, yanks it free, and slots a fresh, resin-coated shim into the gap. It bounces back up into the air an instant before the club comes down—*THWACK*—driving the shim home with a sound that makes your teeth ache.

A mere biological reflex of the wall. An immune response with legs.

Then you watch it go to the corners.

It climbs up into the un-angled places where the ceiling fails to meet the wall, right where the geometry breaks open into that three-mile haze of sunlit space. Here, the room’s blind botany is trying desperately to seal the anomaly. Thick, flat paddles of bark are growing over the gap like scabbing skin, threatening to blind the three-mile view and shut off the flat Tuesday-afternoon light that radiates from the room’s center.

The creature anchors its lower limbs to a root-plate. It reaches into the three-mile gap. 

As its pale, split-wood forearm crosses the threshold of the impossible horizon, its arm stretches. To your eyes, the limb becomes forty feet long, then half a mile long, tapering away into the bright, dusty haze of a distance that isn't there, even though the creature’s shoulder is six inches from the back of the crevice. With the sharp, iron-hard edge of its forearm, it begins to saw. 

*Shhhk-shhhk-shhhk.* 

It is cutting away the new bark. It is pruning back the healing scabs of the wall, deliberately keeping the impossible window open. 

Why? 

Look at its torso—at the hollow, bird-cage cavity inside its tensioned ribs. Lodging there, cradled in a sticky hammock of amber sap and felted phloem-wool, is something the creature has collected. It is a heap of the brass-colored husks left behind by the stuttering beetle, before they could dissolve into sawdust, mixed with shed scales from the jerky, eyeless lizard and the dried, grey skins of three-second raindrops that failed to burst when they hit the floor.

The creature has been harvesting the glitches. In a world of miles-deep, suffocating, solid wood, this hollow chamber is the only place where *things happen*—where space is empty, where light exists without a sun, where time skips like a scratched record. If the blind, terrified root-mass succeeds in filling the barge-sized room back up with solid timber, the pressure will equalize, the three-mile corners will snap shut, the Tuesday light will go out, and the Joiner—along with its little hoard of impossible trash—will be crushed back into senseless, dark firewood.

So it works against its own maker. It is a saboteur disguised as a repairman.

It moves down the wall now, toward the great, thigh-thick shoots that are erupting from the floor to bridge and choke the center of the room. The creature does not cut them down—if it cut them, the floor would panic and send up ten more in their place. Instead, while a new shoot is still flying across the room, soft and pale and blind, the creature swings out into the empty center of the chamber like a trapeze artist. It catches the flying tip of the shoot mid-air, hangs all its leverage upon it, and deflects its path by five degrees. 

Instead of slamming into the opposite wall to divide the room in half, the shoot glances off an existing pillar, curves along the perimeter, and grafts itself harmlessly into the outer hull. The creature has tricked the tree into building an arch instead of a partition. It is turning a closing wound into a cathedral.

Suddenly, it stops. Every wooden joint in its body locks with a rapid series of clicks, like a watch winding tight. 

Its upper burl-knuckle swivels downward. It has felt the slight, rhythmic failure of the floor-mat beneath your boots, where the weaving tendrils have just finished taking their first loop around your ankles. It senses an vertical timber in the room that has no roots, a column of warm water that is not moving upward toward the canopy, a piece of lumber that is breathing.

It drops ten feet on a slack vine, arresting its fall a yard from your face. 

Up close, it smells fiercely of hot turpentine and bruised green twigs. You can see the clear, mineral-heavy sap racing through the translucent xylem of its shins, sparkling in the bright, flat light. Along the inner edge of its pruning-blade forearms, it has grown a row of uneven teeth made from pebble-hard knots of deadwood. From the vents in its ribs, it lets out a long, low whistle of steam that smells like boiled celery, testing the resonance of your chest cavity the way a carpenter taps a beam to see if the heartwood is rotten.

It does not attack. It reaches down with one long, segmented foot, hooks a wooden toe under the green tendril lashing your left boot, and snaps the vine in two with a sound like a pistol shot. 

Then it taps your shin twice—*knock, knock*—with a caliper-limb, measuring your load-bearing capacity, before leaping backward into the rigging to adjust a pulley that is beginning to smoke from friction. It has decided not to mortise you into the floor just yet. The ceiling is sagging on the north side, the light is flickering, and you look like you might make a very good temporary shore-post if you can just be coerced into standing three feet to the left.
