To find what lives here, you have to stop looking for an animal and start looking for an arrangement of tension. 

Wait until the three-second shriek of the relief valve has ended, and the blinding fog begins to thin back into its baseline drift. Look at the underside of the great penstock, where the ragged sheets of condensation gather on the sharp-edged hex bolts. 

One of those bolts is not steel. Or rather, there is a bolt there, greased and torqued to spec, but clamped directly over it is a second hex—a counterfeit six-sided cap, two inches across, matching the sharp, unrusted mill-scale grey of the iron so precisely that a maintenance worker’s flashlight would slide right over it. 

Only the water gives it away. The drops falling from the real bolts are fat and sluggish, pulled down by gravity. The drop hanging from the counterfeit bolt is drawn into a sharp, quivering cone by the forty-cycle-per-second hum of the pipe, and it does not fall until the shell above it gives a microscopic, hydraulic *twitch*.

They are the **Chocks**. An engineer who didn’t look closely might call them limpets, or scale-worms, or simply buildup, though they share no biology with anything from the sea. They are creatures of pure boundary layer and cold pressure, drawn down from the high alpine subterranean fissures when the penstock was bored through the mountain's heart, and now entirely adapted to the brutal geometry of the new fabrication.

A Chock has no eyes, no ears, and no hollow spaces inside its body. A lung or an ear canal or a vein full of thin blood would be ruptured in minutes by the acoustic battering of the room. Instead, beneath its flanged, hexagonal shell—which it prints by pressing its mantle against the sharp steel hardware while its outer layer cures—the creature is a solid, high-density plug of pale, vulcanized gristle. Its flesh has the consistency of a hard rubber engine mount. It does not fight the vibration of the ten-foot pipe; it requires it. 

The Chock has no heart. It relies entirely on the roaring shudder of the snowmelt falling two thousand feet on the other side of the steel skin to pump its thick, clear, antifreeze-like fluid through a microscopic lattice of internal capillaries. If the penstock were ever shut down—if the chamber ever fell silent—the Chocks would suffocate in their own stagnation within twenty minutes, their internal fluids gelling into inert wax in the biting mountain cold.

Right now, there are perhaps forty of them in Valve House 4, and you could stare right at thirty-nine of them without knowing they weren't part of the blueprint. 

Some are hex-shaped, gripping the bolt-heads under the pipe. Others, clamped into the ninety-degree corners where the massive H-beams meet the poured concrete piers, have grown shells that are four-inch triangular gusset plates, complete with false, dimpled weld-beads along their edges. Two of them sit on the lone control stanchion in the center of the floor, shaped like heavy brass pipe-unions, their shells polished to a dull, greenish gold by the constant streaming film of water. They are architectural parasites. In a room where everything is newly built and knife-edged, they survive by abhorring a curve. Their shells grow in sheer planes and forty-five-degree bevels, taking their cues from the sheared iron and the crisp wood-grain impressions of the concrete forms.

They are completely motionless for hours at a time, locked down by hydrostatic suction so powerful you would need a four-foot prybar and a hammer to pop one off the steel. Beneath their shells, they are slowly digesting the iron itself, using a weak, sulfuric mucus to leach the dark mill scale and microscopic sulfur-reducing bacteria off the raw metal, turning the skin of the pipe beneath them bright,etched silver.

When the governor trips, the room wakes up.

A quarter-second before the giant bronze relief valve cracks open, a sub-audible pressure wave slams down the interior of the penstock. The human ear cannot register it, and the brass needles on the control stanchion haven't even twitched yet, but every Chock in the chamber reacts instantly. 

You can see a tiny, milky bead of sealant squeeze out from the base of every false bolt and gusset plate as they clamp down with tenfold force, vacuum-locking themselves against the coming violence. 

Then the valve blows. The boiler-explosion shriek erases the world. The solid lance of white water smashes into the Reinforced Baffle Wall, and the chamber goes blind with freezing, atomized fog and three inches of churning floor-foam.

In that five-minute blindness, while the air is too thick to breathe and the noise is a solid wall, the Chocks let go.

Only the ones on the Baffle Wall move fast. As the neon-green wash of cold-water algae is scoured clean by the fringes of the blast, three long, narrow shapes drop from the underside of the lower H-beams directly into the churning foam on the checkered floor plates. They look like dropped structural shims—flat, rectangular wedges of dark grey slate, six inches long and tapered to a chisel edge. 

In the swirling whiteout, they do not crawl so much as they skate. Using the violent acoustic vibration of the steel floor plates, they angle the microscopic serrations on their flat bellies, riding the chatter of the metal the way a loose wrench walks across the bed of a running diesel truck. They zip across the flooded floor in jerky, rectilinear trajectories—ninety degrees left, six inches forward, forty-five degrees right—entirely indifferent to the freezing foam burying them. 

They reach the base of the Baffle Wall where the orange light of the caged incandescent bulbs gleams weakly through the fog. They wedge their chisel-noses into the wood-grain grooves of the concrete and plane upward, stripping the neon-green algae and the pale, vibrating tufts of bladder-fern in neat, square-edged ribbons, leaving bare, grey stone behind.

And then there is the other thing. The thing that does not have a shell.

Look at the solitary grease gun left on the steel workbench in the corner. Beneath its curved barrel, hanging from the sharp lip of the bench, is what looks like a long, pale rope of spilled lithium grease, or a weeping icicle of clear silicone caulk that hasn't quite cured. 

It is neither. It is the **Skein**—the nursery phase, or perhaps the digestive tract of the whole dispersed colony; it is hard to tell with something so alien to lungs and legs. 

It is a colonial ribbon of clear, heavy mucilage, as tough and elastic as ballistic gel, anchored into the expansion joints behind the concrete piers where the water puddles rather than scours. During the baseline roar, the Skein stretches itself out into thin, taut tripwires across the empty, wet volume between the workbench, the gauge stanchion, and the perimeter floor grates. It uses the sharp fins of dried mortar sticking out of the walls as cleats, hitching itself around them with slow, blind peristalsis.

Because it has the exact refractive index of the cold water streaming down the walls, the Skein is nearly invisible. You only see it when the greasy orange light of the two lower bulbs catches a tremor running down its length. It vibrates at the exact resonant frequency of the ten-foot penstock, humming like a bass string, whipping the drifting horizontal mist into tiny, localized vortices. 

Those vortices pull every speck of airborne nourishment out of the thin eight-thousand-foot air: microscopic spores sucked through the high slit-windows, wind-blown pine pollen from the pass outside, flakes of iron oxide, and the fine, aerosolized mash of green algae blasted off the Baffle Wall during the three-second shriek. 

Over the course of a week, as the Skein loads up with trapped grit and green meal, it slowly winches itself back across the checkered steel floor, reeling its sticky cables in through the three-inch foam until it reaches the dark, vibrating undersides of the H-beams. There, the waiting Chocks unseal their sharp edges by a millimeter, overriding the Skein, absorbing the nutrient-rich gel straight through their rubbery feet, and excreting fresh, clear structural mucus to replace it.

The bronze valve slams shut. The hydraulic *thump* hits the room. 

Before the shriek has even finished dying in the upper gloom, the flat, wedge-shaped shims on the Baffle Wall have already dropped back into the receding foam, chattering rapidly across the steel deck plates to the base of the stanchion and the piers. They slide up the iron, find their corners, and lock in. A ring of milky water squeezes out from beneath their bevels. The seam disappears.

The fog settles. The three inches of foam gurgle down into the roaring dark beneath the grates. The orange bulbs glow weakly against the slick concrete. 

If you walked in right now, wiping the clammy mist from your face, your teeth rattling in your gums, you would see only the brutal, newly built room: the sweating pipe, the clean brass gauges, the sharp H-beams, and the grease gun on the bench. You might put your gloved hand against a concrete pier to steady yourself against the trembling air, and your fingers might brush a four-inch steel gusset plate tucked into the join of the beam. 

You would notice only that it is slightly colder than the iron around it, that its sheared edge is keen enough to slice leather, and that it is held to the wall with a tension that feels entirely permanent.
