To live at the bottom of the box is to live inside a clock that ticks once every ten seconds. 

The humans who inhabit the ward—there are twenty-eight of them on the permanent ledger, though they are so thinly distributed across the fern-beds and brick towers that the place feels half-abandoned—have been shaped entirely by the acoustic weight and the slow, respiratory rhythm of the basin. They are called the *tenders*, or sometimes the *damp-coats*, and most have been down in the fault for twenty years or more. The municipal board up on the shelf prefers not to rotate them out; it takes too long to train a new ear to the quarry.

A newcomer to the basin goes half-mad in the first week from the noise, clamping their hands over their brass ear-defenders to block out the four-fold echo of the exhaust valves and the shrill, metallic roar of forty thousand starlings. By the third month, however, the brain performs a strange subtraction. The constant roar of the air-shafts and the shrieking of the roosts are filed away as silence—a dense, pressurized background against which only *deviations* are audible. The tenders do not wear their big brass-and-horsehair ear-cups to block the sound entirely; the cups are fitted with small, adjustable ivory apertures that filter out the high, frantic register of the birds while letting the low, structural frequencies pass through the bone of the skull.

Because shouting is useless against the walls of sound, the tenders are entirely mute during their shifts, and the habit of silence stays with them even when they step behind the double-sealed oak doors of the switching-towers. They have developed a language of small, economical movements that can be read from fifty paces away across the tops of the gunnera leaves. It is a grammar of posture and tool-angle. A long-spouted brass oilcan held horizontally at the hip means *pressure nominal in the third hall*; the same can tilted forty-five degrees toward the northern lift means *the slag-cable is fraying, bring the grease-pot*. When two tenders pass each other on the slick basalt paths, they do not nod or wave; they merely tap the knuckles of their left hands twice against the warm copper flank of the nearest pipe—a vibration the other person feels through the soles of their heavy, wooden-clogged boots ten yards away.

They are a stocky, broad-chested people, selected by the work itself for patience and a low heart rate. Anyone with a nervous, twitchy disposition soon requests a transfer back to the coastal shelf. Those who stay begin to move at the speed of the flywheels. Their faces are permanently flushed a clean, healthy pink from the steam, their skin thick and smooth, almost poreless, perpetually beaded with the same cold distilled water that pearls on the arc-lamps. They smell of wet Melton wool, tallow, and bruised fern-fronds. 

Beneath their heavy coats, their clothing is curiously domestic. Because the ward is a closed, warm microclimate, free of the biting salt-winds of the coast above, the tenders dress in thick knitted waistcoats and collarless linen shirts, moving through the monumental roar of the machinery with the mild, slippered comfort of night-watchmen in a vast library. Each tender carries a flat brass scraping-iron at their belt and a pocket full of dried boiled mutton.

The mutton is for the civets. 

The animals of the ward are not pests to be cleared, nor are they precisely pets; they are treated as junior colleagues, or perhaps as a secondary, living layer of instrumentation. The big, ring-tailed quarry-civets—some of them weighing thirty pounds, their pelts glossy with machine-oil and condensation—have been down in the fault for six generations. They have lost their nocturnal habits, preferring to sleep in heavy, striped heaps against the vibrating brickwork of the transformers, where the sixty-cycle hum massages their ribs. 

The tenders use them as thermometers. A transformer that is running at its proper heat will have three or four civets pressed tight against its base, their chins resting on the warm iron grounding-straps. If a tender walks past Tower Four and sees the civets sleeping two paces back on the cold basalt, arranged in a loose ring rather than a heap, he does not need to check the mercury gauge inside; he knows the primary coil is running twelve degrees too hot, and he walks with his slow, rolling gait to the switching-house to step down the load. In exchange for this diagnostic work, and for keeping the basin completely free of rats, the civets are fed scraps of mutton fat and allowed into the lower grates of the boiler halls when the winter frosts rim the top of the cliff three hundred feet above.

Lower down, beneath the broad, umbrella-like leaves of the gunneras, live the *clinkers*—the large, speckled land-crabs that originally came up through the drainage fissures from the sea-caves half a mile away, drawn by the warmth of the sunken masonry. Over the decades, they have grown heavy and pale, their shells taking on the mottled grey-and-rust coloration of the iron floor-plates and coal-slag. They are the ward’s janitors. They move through the waist-high fern beds in slow, clicking phalanxes, consuming the dead starlings that drop from the gantries, the shed feathers, the rotting vegetation, and the thick, tallow-based grease that drips from the overhead cable-drums. 

A crab the size of a dinner plate will sit directly in the center of a walkway, working a piece of hardened axle-grease into its mandibles with a delicate, rhythmic *tick-tick-tick* that is completely inaudible under the roar of the vents. The tenders never kick them aside. A tender will simply pause—one heavy wooden clog raised—wait four full seconds for the crab to register the shadow and sidle three inches to the left, and then set his foot down, never breaking the ten-second cadence of his walk.

Then there are the birds, which are less like individual creatures and more like a dense, shifting weather system that occupies the upper thirty feet of the box. The rock doves and starlings have adapted to the geometry of the quarry in a peculiar way: they never fly in curves. Because the walls are sheer and the gantries form a strict rectilinear grid, the flocks move in sharp, right-angled bursts, dropping straight down like plumb-bobs from the basalt ledges to the copper power-lines, or shooting horizontally from Tower One to Tower Two in a tight, dark ribbon that clears the wet tips of the ferns by inches. 

The birds are the ward’s barometers and gas-detectors. They roost by the thousands along the warm exhaust-stacks, their feathers constantly puffed and vibrating in the updrafts. If a seam of sour-gas or carbon monoxide leaks from the lower coal-vaults, the starlings on the lowest gantry-rung instantly drop their wings and hop two rungs higher. A tender glancing up through the pale rectangle of morning light reads the empty iron bar like a line of print, turns on his heel, and goes to open the secondary damper.

There is a small, unrecorded hierarchy among the humans, too, though they have no foreman down on the floor. At the center of the ward, built into the brick skirt of the second boiler hall, is the *Dry-Room*—a low-vaulted chamber where the air is piped through calcium-pans to strip the moisture out, keeping the ledger-books from turning to pulp and the spare copper contact-bars from growing green whiskers of verdigris. 

Here sits the Acoustic Clerk, usually the oldest person in the basin, someone whose knees have finally given out from the damp. The Clerk does not wear ear-defenders. She sits at a heavy deal table with her bare ear pressed against a mahogany listening-cone that is bolted directly into the bedrock of the fault. Through the cone, the shriek of the birds and the hiss of the steam vanish; she hears only the deep, subterranean rumble of the three great engines, the grind of the northern lift, and the faint, glassy *ping* of the young basalt fault settling against the coastal shelf. 

Every two hours, one of the walkers comes in from the wet, strips off his heavy coat, and stands by the calcium-stove to dry his wrists and neck. No words are spoken for the first ten minutes; the walker simply breathes the dry, chalky air and listens to the strange, hollow quiet of the room, where the quarry's roar is reduced to a heavy thrum in the floorboards. Then the Clerk will tap her pencil twice on the ledger, point to a column of figures, and say in a voice grown rusty and flat from disuse:

"Number Two is dragging her heel on the back-stroke. Half a second slow."

The walker will nod once, pull a piece of mutton-rind from his pocket to leave on the windowsill for the Clerk's favorite three-legged civet, clamp his brass cups back over his ears, and step out again into the bright, deafening, emerald box—walking ten paces, pausing for the valve-echo to clear the western wall, and walking ten paces more.
