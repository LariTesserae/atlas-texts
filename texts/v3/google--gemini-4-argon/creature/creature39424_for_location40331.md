You will not see them until one of them decides to change the weather. 

You might be standing in a hip-wide alley, listening to the endless gravel-shoveling rasp of the crabs, when a section of the dry-stone wall beside you simply slides six inches to the left. There is a heavy, grinding *crunch* of terracotta, and then the wall stops. 

Only it isn’t a wall. It is a shoulder. 

What lives here has no name in the languages of the upper flats, because the people of the upper flats do not come down here, and these things never go up. If you had to classify them, you might begin with the word *hominid*, or perhaps *crustacean*, or perhaps *mason*, though none of those quite fit the reality of the silhouette unfolding itself from the alcove. 

There are perhaps forty of them in the basin at any given time—not a tribe, not a pack, just an overlapping density of solitary caretakers sharing forty square yards of vertical and horizontal maze. 

They are built for the slot and the lintel. A fully grown individual stands maybe four feet high at the hip, with legs bowed outward like callipers, ending in broad, three-toed feet whose soles are padded with a thick, spongy horn the color of raw rubber. This horn absorbs the shock of the tamped-sherd floor; when they walk, they make no sound at all, gliding over the scree slopes of tally-tokens without shifting a single clay thumb. 

Above the hips, however, their anatomy abandons symmetry to marry the masonry. Their torsos are radically flattened from front to back—no thicker than a heavy timber plank—allowing them to slip sideways through apertures that would trap a child’s ribcage. Their collarbones are hinged like the wings of a folding screen. When they press into a narrow flue, they can collapse their shoulders forward until their chest is concave, their elongated, spade-shaped ribs overlapping like the slats of a blind. 

Their skin is the exact texture and temperature of cured, oiled adobe. It is hairless, dusty, and cross-hatched with deep, calloused creases that trap the red silt and charcoal dust of the floor. Across their backs, shoulders, and the crowns of their low, sloping skulls, the dermis thickens into distinct, polygonal plates. These plates are not smooth; they are pitted and rough, weeping a slow, waxy sebum that smells faintly of mutton tallow and wet lime. 

Because they spend hours wedged motionless into the brickwork, the Ossuary has colonized them. The blind, walnut-sized crabs crawl freely across their backs, their pale claws clicking against the horn plates as they graze on the waxy secretion. In the deep, damp creases behind their knees and beneath their chins, where the thermal sweat pools, little tufts of the rust-red moss take root. They do not groom it away. Why would they? It makes them indistinguishable from a corbelled arch.

Their heads sit low between their shoulders, practically neckless, with faces that look as though they were carved with a flat stone adze out of ironwood. Their eyes are set on the very sides of their skulls, like a goat’s or a gecko’s—bulging, heavy-lidded globes of dark amber with horizontal, keyhole pupils. This gives them almost total panoramic vision, allowing them to see both the alley ahead and the alley behind while wedged sideways in a wall, though if they want to look closely at something directly in front of their noses, they have to cock their heads at a sharp, bird-like angle, peering with one eye at a time.

They have no external ears—only narrow, vertical slits guarded by stiff bristles that snap shut when the clay weights start their deafening *clack-clack-clack* in the updrafts. 

To watch one move through the basin is to watch a master locksmith working from the inside of the lock. They do not use the floors if they can help it. They move at mid-level, four or five feet off the ground, chimneying between the narrow walls with their bowed legs and long, double-jointed arms. Their hands are immense, disproportionate paddles. Each possesses two thumbs—one on either side of the palm—and three central fingers whose terminal joints have been replaced by thick, hooked nails of black keratin, worn flat at the tips like mason’s chisels.

They are the reason the Ossuary has not collapsed into a single mound of rubble. 

The sunken bowl is their shell, a communal exoskeleton they have been maintaining for centuries, though they have no memory of who built it or why. Their intelligence is not the kind that invents or questions; it is the intelligence of the beaver and the termite, magnified to a staggering, obsessive geometry. They feel the weight of the stone through their palms. When a heavy slate lintel begins to slip—shifted by the swelling of an elephant-hide vine or the slow settling of the silt floor—one of the flat-bodied creatures will sense the change in the acoustic resonance of the wall. 

The vibrations here are their language. In a place where every scrape is amplified, they are acoustic masters. 

When one of them finds a failing arch, it does not vocalize. Instead, it reaches out with a black-nailed finger and taps a terracotta flue pipe. *Tink. Tink-tink. Tink.* The sound travels through the baked clay, bypassing the roar of the crabs, ringing clearly three rooms away. Within minutes, two or three others will slide out of the shadows, folding their ribs to slip through the slots, their amber eyes blinking slowly in the dusty shafts of gold light.

Together, they work in absolute silence. One will wedge its flattened, plated back directly under the sagging lintel, locking its bowed legs and puffing out a thick, muscular air-sac under its throat to brace its spine, becoming a living caryatid. It can hold that weight for six hours without a tremor. Meanwhile, the others sort through the scree of tally-tokens and broken bricks. They do not use mortar. They have an uncanny, tactile eye for friction and center-of-gravity. They will test twenty pieces of broken slate, flipping them rapidly between their four-thumbed hands with a dry *shuck-shuck-shuck* sound, until they find the exact wedge that locks the arch back into equilibrium. 

They do not just maintain the structure; they play it like an organ. 

Their entire metabolism is tied to the eighty-degree dampness of the pit, and they regulate it with the drafts. If an alcove becomes too stagnant, or if a colony of swifts grows too rowdy and fouls a favorite resting niche with guano, one of the residents will climb to the upper grates. Using a mineralized cedar wedge, it will prop open a collapsed reed awning just enough to let a lance of sunlight hit a dark, damp chimney flue thirty feet below. 

Then it climbs back down and waits for the hooting wind to start. 

As the miniature gale rips through the five adjoining rooms, setting the clay weights clattering and driving the screaming swifts into a vortex, the creature stands directly in the center of the blast. It spreads its hinged collarbones wide, catching the warm, mica-flecked wind against its chest, letting the draft dry the damp moss in its skin-folds and blow away the loose scales of dead horn. 

When the shadow returns and the wind dies, it goes hunting.

Despite their size and their formidable, chisel-tipped hands, they are patient, almost delicate predators. They eat the crabs, of course, plucking them from the gaps between the slate tiles like berries, popping them whole into wide, lipless mouths lined with flat, cobblestone molars designed for crushing shell and chitin. When they chew, they keep their mouths tightly shut to muffle the sound, so a rival gecko doesn't steal the meal; you only hear a deep, muffled *grind-grind* coming from somewhere inside their chests.

Their true prize, however, is the gecko itself, or the fat, leathery swifts. To catch a dog-sized gecko, the creature uses the architecture as a deadfall. It will spend an entire afternoon delicately unbalancing a retaining wall of sun-baked mudbrick, propping the whole two-hundred-pound mass up on a single, thumb-sized clay tally-token tied to a length of rotted hemp cord. Then it wedges itself into a niche opposite the wall, pulls its limbs in, and closes its eyes. Within ten minutes, the pale crabs have crawled back over its shoulders. A drop of condensation from a slate overhang *plip-plocks* onto its forehead, darkening the dust on its brow plate. It does not flinch. 

Hours pass. The light-shafts wheel across the basin. Eventually, a mottled gecko comes booming and thrumming down the warm flue, its sticky toes paddling across the brick right beneath the poised wall. 

The hunter’s amber eye slits open. A black nail twitches, severing the hemp cord. 

The resulting crash of mudbrick sounds like a cannon shot in the confined space, sending a cloud of ochre dust boiling up toward the ceiling grates. Before the echoes have died, the flat-bodied hunter has unfolded from the wall, scooped up the stunned, twitching reptile, and vanished sideways through a five-inch gap in the back of a dry cistern.

They live long, slow lives in the warm clutter. When they sleep, they do not lie down on the damp floor; they find a vertical chimney shaft just wide enough for their hips, brace their rubbery soles against one side and their plated backs against the other, and lock their knees. There they hang, suspended ten feet in the air inside the dark brickwork, rising and falling a quarter-inch as they breathe the rising thermal steam.

And when they finally die—usually after seventy or eighty years, when their joints calcify from the constant damp and they can no longer fold their shoulders to navigate the alleys—they do not leave the walls. A dying individual will seek out the deepest, narrowest breach in the lower vaults, a place where the ancient builders’ timber beams have begun to split under the weight of four decades of slumped slate. It will crawl into the breach, press its calcified, plated back against the cracking timber, and lock its legs one last time. 

The blind crabs come to clean the soft tissues away, working in the dark with their endless, gravelly rustle. The thermal vapor rises through the flues, bathing the bones in mineral-rich steam. Over the decades, the calcium and silica from the sweating stone leach into the skeleton, turning the flat ribs and the broad, wedged skull as hard and black as the ironwood beams above them. 

If you squeeze through the lowest levels of the Ossuary today, ducking under the split cedar lintels, you will pass dozens of them. You will probably put your hand right on a dry, rough collarbone to steady yourself as you step over a retaining wall, never realizing that the buttress holding up the ceiling is still wearing the shape of the mason who put it there.
