At first, during the thirty-second lull, you would think the alcoves are entirely empty. When the bruised-violet light ripples through the walls, the hollowed-out balconies look like rows of wet, grey mouths, slick with the oily condensation that weeps from the metamaterial. 

If you watch a single alcove through three or four cycles of the forty-second pulse, however, you will notice that the shape of the back wall is changing. A protuberance that looked like a slumped, melted lintel has shifted fifty centimeters to the left. 

They do not have a name for themselves, because they do not use sound to communicate—sound belongs to the room, which is too loud to argue with. If an outside observer were to classify them, they might be called the *Tympanites*, or the *Velvet Masons*, though neither captures the slow, heavy reality of their bodies.

In the brief flash of the utility veins, an individual looks like a cross between a massive, shell-less chiton and an old, waterlogged leather coat draped over a boulder. They are roughly the mass of a large human, though arranged horizontally, hugging the softened geometry of the terraces. Their dorsal side is a thick, continuous mantle of matte-black, vulcanized hide, textured with thousands of microscopic, hexagonal dimples that trap a thin film of the room’s oily water. This skin does not reflect the purple light; it drinks it in, making them look like holes torn in the dim topography of the walls.

Beneath this heavy cloak, they are pale, dense, and startlingly complex. They move on a broad, muscular foot flanked by two rows of stubby, articulate prolegs—twelve in all—each ending in a spatulate pad of grey keratin and a retractable hook. They do not walk so much as they flow and ratchet, pulling their heavy bulk along the rounded ledges with a slow, deliberate grace that completely halts five seconds before the pressure wave hits.

Everything about them is built around the forty-second cycle. 

When the brassy groan begins to rise behind the eastern wall, a Mason stops moving. It lowers its mantle until the thick, muscular rim of its skirt contacts the wet polymer-stone all the way around its body. It expels the air from beneath itself with a wet, muted *flump*, creating a vacuum seal that anchors it to the wall with several tons of holding force. Simultaneously, along its ridge-like back, three pairs of heavy, sphincter-like spiracles dilate, exposing deep resonance chambers lined with stiff, piezoelectric cilia.

*Thwack.*

The concussive wave slams through the hall. The air goes solid; the walls flare violet. While the hexapods in the basin flatten and the gliders drop into the turbulent updraft, the Mason *feeds* on the shockwave. The acoustic blow hammers into its open dorsal chambers. The internal cilia whip violently, translating the massive kinetic punch directly into cellular energy—a biological mirror of the very walls they cling to. The creature’s hide shivers, a brief, pearlescent ripple running from its blunt head to its tapered rear, and then the spiracles snap shut to trap the lingering heat of the exhale.

They are not merely beasts clinging to the ruins. As the drone drops back into the thirty-second lull, and the rapid *tick-tick-tick* of the walls begins, the Masons unseal themselves and resume their work. 

They are farmers, and they are architects of grease.

Near the anterior end of the creature, tucked beneath the hood of its mantle, is a cluster of four long, boneless manipulators—pale, muscular trunks the thickness of a human wrist, terminating in three opposable, cartilaginous fingers. Beside these is a wide, rasping mouthpart. During the quiet thirty seconds, the Mason reaches out into the absolute dark, navigating entirely by the thermal memory of the stone and the faint, lingering electrical hum of the buried conduits. 

With their nimble trunks, they tend the leathery, cartilaginous fronds. Left to itself, the oily condensation of the room would run down the ancient staircases and pool uselessly at the bottom of the basin. The Masons prevent this. They chew a mixture of their own thick, mucilaginous saliva, scraped metallic silt, and the dead, shed skins of the basin hexapods, kneading it with their four trunks into a dense, waxy putty. 

Over generations, they have lined the eroded runnels and drooped balconies with low, curving retaining walls of this dark mortar. They have turned the vertical theater facades into an intricate system of tiny, stepped rice-paddies and cisterns, routing the warm, oily trickle directly over the intersections where the violet conduits glow brightest. They prune the black fronds with their keratin hooks, tearing away dead tissue to stuff into their mouths, while carefully avoiding the hair-thin spore stalks. 

Even more remarkably, they farm the gliders, though the gliders do not know it. 

The Masons will spend weeks smoothing and greasing a specific overhang on an upper promenade, shaping the waxy mortar into an acoustic baffle that deadens the echo of the eastern wall just enough to make that spot seem, to a blind glider’s sonar, like the safest, quietest roost in the hall. When a glider slaps wetly onto this prepared ledge after its forty-second flight, its belly and wing-hooks are caked with the rich, silver spores it failed to swallow. 

Before the glider can haul itself into the recess, two or three Masons will slide out of the adjacent alcoves. They do not kill the glider—gliders are too rare, and too useful as living nets. Instead, the Masons move over the exhausted kite like heavy, warm blankets. Their pale trunks dart out, gently and rapidly scraping the spore-paste from the glider’s black skin and combing the accumulated grease from its mouth-slit, leaving the flyer clean and unharmed before the next brassy groan forces them all to seal themselves to the floor.

If you were to climb up into the housing tiers—Perhaps the third balcony up, where the heat is heaviest and the smell is like hot copper and old tallow—you would find their nurseries inside the deep, hollow sockets of the old doorways.

Here, where the concussive *thwack* is muffled to a dull thud, the Masons gather in heaps during their sluggish reproductive cycles. They are hermaphroditic and ancient; a single large individual might be three hundred years old, its dark mantle scarred with the calcified rings of athousand minor rockfalls and polymer-blisters. 

They communicate in these dark rooms by pressing their mantles together and shivering. By vibrating their piezoelectric cilia in the quiet intervals, they pass complex, low-frequency tremolos directly through the stone and through each other’s flesh. A pile of six or seven Masons in an alcove is engaged in a slow, continuous conversation that feels, if you lay your hand against the wall ten meters away, like the purring of a subterranean cat, or the distant grinding of tectonic plates. They share the locations of newly fractured utility veins; they remember which balconies are losing their violet light; they pass down the tactile maps of water-channels built by ancestors five centuries dead.

Their young are born live—single, palm-sized medallions of translucent grey jelly that cannot yet survive the forty-second blow out in the open. For the first ten years of its life, a juvenile Mason lives entirely *underneath* its parent’s mantle, clinging to the pale, grooved skin between the adult’s prolegs, sheltered within the vacuum seal every time the room goes loud, grazing on the rich, spore-heavy mulch the parent tucks beneath its skirt.

They have no eyes, and no concept of the sky four hundred meters above, or the builders who cast the hall. To the Masons, the universe is a hollow drum of warm stone. Time is not a river, but a forty-second pendulum: the gather, the seal, the thunder-that-feeds, the purple warmth, the quiet rain. They move through the black intervals with their pale, four-fingered trunks sweeping the wet walls, patching their little dams of wax and silt, keeping the garden wet in the thirty seconds of peace before the air turns solid again.
