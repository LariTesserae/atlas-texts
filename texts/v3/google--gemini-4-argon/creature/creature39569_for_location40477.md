If you stand in one of the wide, talc-soft plazas for an hour, your eyes begin to play tricks in the amber gloom. You might notice that a particular heap of calcified mortar at the base of a terrace has shifted four inches to the left. Or you might realize that the pale, braided trunk of a leather-tree has a set of knuckles, and that those knuckles are very slowly uncurling to pluck a sparrow-sized cicada from the bark.

There are people here. Or, at least, there are things that used to be people, ages ago, before the Sink taught them how to be stones.

They do not have a name for themselves, because they do not have voices. In a basin where the air itself is a solid, grinding wall of metallic insect-roar, a larynx is a useless piece of meat. If you shouted until your lungs tore, the sound wouldn't carry three feet through the stridulating din. So their throats closed up millennia ago, trading vocal cords for a dense, sponge-like labyrinth of cartilage in their broad, flat noses—an internal condenser that traps the moisture of their own exhaled breath and returns every drop of it back to the body before the dry air can steal it.

To look at one is to look at a creature shaped by the same patient friction that rounded the buildings. They are short, heavy-limbed, and wide in the pelvis, built with a low center of gravity that requires almost no muscular effort to keep upright. Their skin is a marvel of arid adaptation: thick, completely hairless, and deeply creased into plates that resemble cracked cellar clay. They roll in the pale gypsum and talc of the plazas, working the fine powder into their pores until they are coated in a velvety, matte-ochre crust. It keeps the biting mites away, seals in their moisture, and renders them virtually invisible against the slumped mud-brick of the mounds.

When they move, it is at the speed of a clock’s hour hand. 

Energy generates heat; heat causes sweat; sweat is death. So the inhabitants of the Sink have abandoned hurry altogether. A person might take twenty minutes to rise from a crouch, their thick, heavily calloused joints sliding over one another with the dry, grinding creak of an old leather saddle. They walk with a flat-footed, shuffling glide, never lifting their soles completely from the dust, leaving faint, trough-like trails between the groves that look no different from the drag-marks of the smooth-shelled tortoises. 

Because they move so slowly, the fauna does not register them as living things. A woman can sit on the lip of a terraced roof for two days, her knees drawn up to her chest, her arms wrapped around her shins, until she is just another eroded parapet. The grey-winged doves will land on her shoulders to preen. The giant orthopterans will crawl up her shins, mistaking her calcified skin for sandstone, and begin their deafening, saw-toothed song right against her thigh. When she is hungry, she does not lunge. Her broad, flat-nailed hand simply drifts upward through the amber haze, taking five minutes to travel two feet, closing around a singing locust so gradually that the insect continues to stridulate even as it is brought to her mouth and crushed between molars as flat and hard as millstones.

They have no fire. Fire is greedy; it eats the three-hundred-year-old wood in an hour, and its smoke would choke the cool, inversion-trapped air of the basin. Everything they take from the city, they take raw and cold.

They carry only one tool: a crescent-shaped wedge of dense, dark dolostone, worn smooth as river-glass by the palms of forty generations. With these wedges, they harvest the city. They will pry up a flap of the pale cushion-moss, slice out a piece of fibrous, damp root the size of a fist, and carefully press the moss back down so the scar will heal over the next decade. They tap the bases of the cart-sized agaves, inserting a hollow swift-bone to bleed out a few precious, syrupy drops of bitter sap, plugging the hole with a paste of clay and their own dry, chalky spittle before moving on. They eat the crunchy drifts of shed cicada husks at the bases of the walls, grinding them into a paste with rich, fatty beetle grubs and the sour, water-heavy leaves of the silver scrub.

You might wonder what fills a mind when it takes an afternoon to cross a single plaza, and when the ears are filled from birth to death with a roaring, metallic static.

They are not dull. Their eyes—large, dark, and protected from the blowing silt by heavy, translucent inner eyelids—are extraordinarily sharp. Because they cannot speak over the drone of the cicadas, they speak *through* it. 

The entire city is a massive, resonant instrument. The slumped mounds of fused brick and stone, the hard-packed plazas, the woody roots of the leather-trees threading through the ancient foundations—all of it conducts vibration. When an inhabitant wants to speak to another three mounds away, they do not look up. They drop to one knee and press the heel of their stone wedge, or the thick pad of their bare foot, against an exposed rib of bedrock or an ancient load-bearing wall. They tap. 

*Thock. Thock-thock. Pause. Thock.*

The sound does not travel through the air; it travels through the floor of the Sink. A mile away, another person sitting with their spine pressed against a slumped terrace feels the faint, sub-audible tremor in their vertebrae, cutting right beneath the high-pitched roar of the insects. 

Their language is slow, architectural, and immense. A single sentence might take an hour to tap out. They do not waste it on trivialities. They tap out the slow migrations of the tortoise herds; they report the ripening of a century-plant on the northern rim; they note the shifting of a sandstone wall that has finally settled another inch into the dust. They have long, rhythmic poems that take weeks to recite—histories of the Sink told in slow percussion, felt in the bones of everyone sitting against the masonry across the three-mile bowl.

Their lives are bound to the twelve-hour tide of the air. 

When the surface desert above heats or cools and the heavy, invisible waterfall of faintly damp air rolls down the bruised limestone cliffs, the whole city pauses. The people climb the worn, soap-smooth steps to the terraced roofs of the mounds. They sit in rows among the crouching, flightless owls and the leathery trees, facing the rim. 

As the draft washes over them, they tilt their broad faces up into the amber gloom and open their mouths. Their tongues are long, dark, and deeply fissured, covered in coarse papillae that mimic the microscopic hairs of the tree leaves. For an hour, two hours, they sit in absolute stillness, letting the cool breeze wash over their tongues and through their wide nasal labyrinths, combing the ghost of moisture from the dust, drinking the sky a molecule at a time.

They do not build anymore, because the city is already finished, and yet they are its caretakers. In their slow, millennial way, they are constantly grooming the ruins. If a winter draft deposits too much talc-dust in a depression, threatening to choke a patch of ancient moss, a dozen of them will gather over the course of a week. Using the broad, scooped soles of their feet, they will push the dust aside, grain by grain, channeling it into the wide plazas where the wind can take it. If an old wall threatens to collapse onto a grove of saplings, they will spend five years rolling fallen sandstone blocks against its base to buttress it. 

They do not bury their dead, because digging disturbs the delicate root-mats that hold the floor together, and because nothing in the Sink can be wasted. 

When an old one feels their kidneys finally seizing up, or their joints stiffening beyond the ability to crouch, they climb to the highest terrace of the nearest mound. They find a spot among the braided trunks of the leather-trees, settle themselves into a tight, comfortable knot, and close their eyes against the ochre haze. 

There is no decay here—the air is too dry for rot. Slowly, over months, the moisture leaves them, breathed out into the thirsty leaves of the canopy above. The detritivore beetles come, polite and unhurried, to clean away the soft tissues, returning the nutrients to the dust-moss. What remains is a dry, leathery husk, slumped and folded, the exact color of the masonry. 

Within a year, the cicadas have carpeted the body, singing from its shoulders and brow. Within ten years, the blowing gypsum has filled the hollows of its collarbone and lap, and the pale moss has crept over its ankles. Within a hundred, a thick, woody vine has braided itself through the ribs, anchoring the figure to the terrace, until it is impossible to tell where the ancient builders' wall ends, and the builder begins.
