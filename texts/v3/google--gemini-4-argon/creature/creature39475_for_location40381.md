At first, you only see the brass and the green. 

You have to watch the central turbine shaft for three or four cycles of the twelve-minute tempest before you realize that a broad section of the corroded iron—a saddle-shaped patch just beneath the shadow of the upper catwalk—is breathing. 

It lifts and settles with a slow, hydraulic rhythm, entirely out of sync with the *thrum-clack* of the spire. When the sunlight shifts through the upper portholes, the illusion breaks: what looked like a shelf of porous, rust-pitted bone and dangling copper-moss is actually a mantle of thick, velvety flesh. 

There is only one of it in the sphere. There is only room for one.

It is a creature the size of a butcher’s block, though it has no fixed shape when it lets go of the shaft. It is a cousin to the cuttlefish and the sea-hare, descended from some microscopic hitchhiker pumped up from the vanished oceans when the spire was young, now bloated and monarchic in its warm, predators-free dome. Its body is a heavy, muscular apron of tissue, skirted by a wide undulating frill that runs its entire circumference. Its skin is a masterpiece of industrial mimicry: it can raise chalky white papillae to match the calcium scale on the intake grates, flush a bruised, oily purple-green to vanish into the grease-moss, or smooth itself out into the exact dull, soapy yellow of the scoured bronze hull, complete with dark, sunken lines that imitate the ghost-indentations of stamped serial numbers.

When the water is calm, it clings to the shivering central column using a broad, ventral foot lined with hundreds of independent, vacuum-cupped ridges. It does not fight the vibration of the shaft; it uses it. The creature has no ears, no bones at all save for a broad, internal shield of mineralized cartilage shaped like a curved boiler-plate, and it experiences its forty-foot universe entirely through pressure and resonance. Pressed against the iron column, it listens to the miles of piping below. It feels the deep, subterranean *thrum-clack* pass directly through its jelly-like interior, digesting the vibration like warmth.

Out of its cowled head peer two enormous, heavy-lidded eyes. Their pupils are not slits or circles, but W-shaped apertures shimmered over with a metallic, golden cornea built to polarize the blinding stratospheric glare pouring through the leaded glass above. It watches the golden nets of light dance on the floor. It watches the silver shrimp drift by. 

It does not bother hunting the shrimp during the quiet eleven minutes. That would require spending energy in the lazy clockwise gyre. Instead, it tends its garden, or perhaps it simply plays.

Tucked beneath its heavy mantle are six short, thick arms—more like the fleshy petals of a sea-anemone than tentacles—and two long, prehensile feeding whips that stay coiled in cheek-pouches when not in use. With its six stubby arms, the creature crawls slowly around the circumference of the shaft, grazing not on the moss, but on the limestone scale and mineral crusts that threaten to choke the warm steam-vents. Its mouth, hidden underneath, houses a radula—a rasping tongue—composed not of chitin, but of biogenic magnetite and copper sulfide. When it scrapes a bronze plate or an iron rivet, it makes a sound you can hear across the chamber: a wet, rhythmic *scritch-scritch* that harmonizes with the clicking legs of the valve-crabs.

It has a peculiar relationship with the valve-crabs. Sometimes, as a large crab in a heavy brass hex-nut goes clattering past, the creature will reach out with a single, velvety arm, pick the crab up, and turn it over. The crab immediately pulls its pale, hairy legs inside the threads of the nut, playing dead. The great mollusk will hold the brass nut up to its golden eye, rotating it in the sunbeams, scraping a bit of green velvet algae off the metal flats with its iron-toothed tongue, before gently pressing the nut back onto the catwalk underside until the crab’s suction-feet catch hold again. It never eats them; the brass is too hard, or perhaps it simply likes the noise they make.

Ten seconds before the twelve-minute mark, the creature changes. 

Before any human instrument in the room could detect a shift, the creature feels the deep, miles-down slackening of the spire’s main cable just prior to the counterweight’s drop. Instantly, its skin flushes a stark, warning copper—bright as a newly minted penny. The oil-eels, who cannot feel the deep-pipe resonance as acutely, see this flash of bright metal-color in the clear water and immediately panic, darting out of the open gyre and plunging into the honeycombed hollows of the turbine shaft.

The creature does not hide. It releases its hold on the central column, gives one powerful, undulating heave of its skirted mantle, and glides out into the open, sunlit center of the sphere. 

There, suspended in the empty water, it curls its six muscular arms downward, locking their tips together to form a rigid, streamlined cone, while puffing its upper mantle out like an inverted parachute. It looks like a fleshy, copper-colored plumb-bob hanging in the quiet water.

*Boom.*

The counterweight drops. The floor-sluice groans open, and the geyser of scalding condensate erupts upward.

The blast hits the creature square in its cone-locked arms. Instead of being battered, it rides the thermal rocket straight toward the ceiling. The water here is fiercely hot—hot enough to parboil anything from the outside world—but to the creature, this forty-second inferno is the height of ecstasy. The scalding water flushes through its gill-plumes, stripping away parasites and sloughed skin, hyper-oxygenating its thick, blue, copper-based blood. 

Held aloft near the brass crown of the ceiling by the column of roaring water, tumbling slightly in the amber snowstorm of rust-flakes and boiler-scale, it uncoils its two long, translucent feeding whips. They shoot downward into the tempest, trailing ten feet below its body like twin fishing lines. The whips are lined with feather-soft, mucus-coated cilia. As the blind, silver shrimp are tumbled helplessly upward through the center of the room by the surge, they are swept into the trailing whips by the dozen. The creature doesn’t even have to aim; the hydrodynamics of the spherical hull funnel the entire chaotic soup of shrimp, loosened kelp-spores, and rich mineral silt right through its waiting net.

Simultaneously, the creature releases a milky, iridescent fluid from a siphon behind its head—not ink to hide itself, for nothing here hunts it, but a rich, enzymatic mucus studded with fertilized eggs no bigger than grains of silt, alongside symbiotic bacteria from its own gut. The violent updraft blasts this cloud into every seam, rivet-scar, and moss-curtain in the room. None of the eggs will grow into a second monarch—the giant itself will absentmindedly graze its own sprouted young off the walls before they reach the size of a thumbnail—but during their first few weeks as microscopic larvae, they form the rich, gelatinous film that feeds the silver shrimp, which feed the oil-eels, which drop the nutrient-rich waste that fertilizes the ribbon-kelp. 

For forty seconds, at the peak of the deafening roar, pressed near the upper portholes where the freezing stratosphere bleeds pale blue light through the frosted glass, the creature spins slowly in the scalding fountain, reeling in its shrimp-heavy whips, glowing like polished bronze in the amber haze.

*Thud.*

The valve slams shut. The upward torrent vanishes instantly. 

In the sudden, ringing quiet, the creature simply unfolds. It spreads its wide, fleshy skirt, turning itself into a living parasol, and drifts downward through the settling snow of rust-flakes. As it sinks, its skin is already cooling from bright penny-copper back to the mottled, soapy yellow-green of the ancient room. By the time it touches the central turbine shaft, wrapping its velvet weight back around the trembling, bone-like iron, it has already disappeared again—just another swelling of ancient brass and grease-moss, listening to the miles of pipe below, waiting for the bell to ring.
