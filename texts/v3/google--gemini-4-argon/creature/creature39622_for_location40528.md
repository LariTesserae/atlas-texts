To look at one of the builders up close is to see a body shaped entirely by leverage, glare, and the suction of the ground. 

They are human, or were once, though the basin has pared them down and widened them out to match the geometry of their labor. They are short, thick-set, and heavily muscled through the trapezius, shoulders, and thighs—the physique of people who spend ten hours a day lifting deadweight from knee-height to chest-height. Their skin is the color of peeled almonds, almost indistinguishable from the condensation-slicked bone they build with, except from the knees down and the elbows out, where a permanent crust of dried silicate paste turns them the matte, powdery grey of old slate. 

Because the light comes from the mud below as fiercely as it comes from the mist above, their faces are permanently drawn into a tight, protective grimace. The skin around their eyes is pleated into deep, leathery fans of wrinkles. Over generations, the fat pads of their upper and lower eyelids have thickened, narrowing their gaze to horizontal slits fringed with heavy, pale lashes that trap the damp dust. Their irises are so pale a grey they look like drops of cloudy water, darting constantly to read the subtle dimpling of the ground ten yards ahead. 

And their feet are extraordinary. Unshod since birth, the soles have spread out like paddles to distribute their weight across the hydro-plastic silt. The toes are long, prehensile, and widely splayed, gripping the slick clay with every step and pulling free with a faint, wet *pop*. A builder never stands still with their weight locked. Even when pausing to eat or catch their breath, they shift constantly from foot to foot in a slow, unconscious rhythm—*left, right, left, right*—testing the shear-strength of the crust beneath them. To stand still for two minutes is to sink to the ankles; to stand still for ten is to be trapped to the knees in clay that sets like cold porridge around the shins.

They wear almost nothing, because cloth rots in the perpetual damp and mud-caked hems drag a runner down. They wear only wide, stiffened belts of layered shell-beast keratin to support their lower backs while lifting, and aprons of greased bird-skin tied across their thighs to keep the abrasive shale from tearing their skin as they slide slabs onto the sleds. 

Where does the wood for their sledges and pry-bars come from, in a bowl of sterile paste? From the sky. The unseen rim eight hundred feet above is forested, and the high, unseen winds periodically strip deadfall and whole, stunted timber from the cliffs, tumbling them down the long, smooth clay slopes at the basin’s edges. The wood arrives already battered and barkless, and the builders prize it above all things. A wooden pry-bar is an heirloom, greased daily with tallow rendered from the flightless birds, smoothed by ten thousand palms until it shines like dark amber against the white glare of the city. The fiber for their slings is spun from the coarse, hair-like plumage of those same birds, braided with long strips of shed shell-beast membrane into ropes that do not stretch when wet.

Their silence is not biological; they have voices, though their throats are gravelly and unused to projection. They are silent because sound is both useless and dangerous here. In the flat, acoustic dead-zone of the basin, where the wet clay swallows resonance and the heavy mist presses down like a wool blanket, a shout barely carries fifty yards. More importantly, a shout hides the only warning the earth ever gives. 

Before a liquefaction event, the ground breathes. As the subterranean water table surges upward, displacing the silt, billions of microscopic air pockets are forced to the surface. Five seconds before a street turns to soup, the mud makes a sound like a handful of salt thrown onto hot grease—a faint, dry *shhhhh-tik-tik*. 

Every creature in the basin is tuned to that frequency. 

You can see it happen in the middle of a busy avenue. Fifty people are hauling a sled loaded with four tons of dressed slate. A dozen white-plumed birds are pecking at a crushed crab near the runners. Suddenly, the six-legged shell-beasts freeze, their translucent plates locking together with a sharp *clack*, their flank-spiracles flaring wide. The builders don't look around; they don't ask questions. Their splayed feet feel the minute drop in surface tension—the way the clay suddenly stops pushing back against their heels—and their ears catch that tiny, fizzing hiss of escaping air. 

Instantly, without a gesture, the team divides. Ten builders drop their pry-bars under the sled runners and heave sideways, tipping the four-ton load deliberately into the mud so the sled itself isn't dragged down by the weight of the stone. Twenty more unhitch the beasts, slapping their cold, slick shells to send them clattering toward a gray-brick ziggurat forty yards away. The rest grab the loose tools, the bone levers, and the children, and they run—not with high, bounding strides, which would punch straight through the weakening crust, but with a low, flat-footed, skating shuffle that keeps their weight skimming over the surface. 

Behind them, the avenue simply drops four feet into a smooth, wet trough. The abandoned slate slabs slide into the center, stacking themselves like fallen dominoes in the grey soup. Before the mud has even stopped swirling, while the steam of the deeper groundwater is still curling over the lip of the new depression, the builders are back at the edge. They toss out wide mats of woven bird-hair, walk out onto the liquid silt on those floating rafts, and begin fishing their stones back out with long, hooked poles of rib-bone.

Nothing can be surrendered to the earth. That is the fundamental law of their existence. The basin floor is a slow-motion digestion tract; anything left resting on the surface is eventually pulled down into the deep, churning dark where the ancient silicates grind against the bedrock. If the builders stopped unstacking and restacking their city for a single week, the stone would sink out of reach, the bone aqueducts would be swallowed, the sleds would go under, and they would be left naked on a featureless plain of cold paste with nothing to stand on when the wide, miles-long sheets of liquefaction roll through. 

The city is not a shelter from the elements; the city is a raft. The ziggurats, the low compounds, the wide-spaced causeways—they are ballast and flotation, a way of spreading weight across an unstable membrane, constantly shifted to wherever the crust is thickest that afternoon.

Because they have no hands to spare for signaling when everyone is holding a stone or a lever, they communicate with their faces, their shoulders, and the rhythm of their tools. A foreman stands atop a listing wall, and his face is a kinetic marvel. A flare of the nostrils, a sharp pull of the left corner of the mouth, a double-blink of the thick, pale eyelids—each is a precise architectural instruction regarding load-bearing angles, mud-consistency, and sled-routing. For longer distances, they use the stones themselves. Two flat pieces of shale struck together Edge-to-Edge produce a sharp, high *tink* that cuts through the heavy air much farther than a human voice. The whole basin clicks and taps with a sparse, percussive code—*tink-tink, tink, tink-tink-tink*—like water dripping in a vast cistern, telling a crew half a mile away that the northern spring has shifted, or that a vein of firm blue clay has surfaced near the western rim and needs to be quarried before it subsides.

Inside the low, mortarless compounds—which have no roofs, only baffled walls to break the cold wind—life happens on the move. There are no beds, no hearths, no buried dead. 

Fires are impossible without wood to burn, so they cook and warm themselves over the thermal vents—those erratic, migrating domes of warm groundwater that bubble up from the deep faults. When a warm spring breaches, women and old men whose knees have finally given out rush to the spot carrying wide, flat pans of hammered copper and thin-walled clay pots. Before the vent cools or shifts away, they boil down the grey biofilm skimmed from the puddles into a thick, salty gruel, poaching the white-plumed birds and the long-legged crabs in the mineral-heavy water. They eat standing up, leaning against the warm flanks of the shell-beasts, chewing strips of dried bird-meat while their eyes stay fixed on the horizon, watching the distant profiles of their towers against the white mist to see which ones are beginning to tilt.

Sleep is taken in shifts, on the sleds themselves. A dozen exhausted builders will curl together like pale puppies atop a load of dry-stacked brick, wrapped in greasy bird-skin cloaks, while the shell-beasts slowly drag the sled in a wide, three-mile circle around the firmer perimeter of the basin, keeping the sleepers moving so the ground cannot claim them while they are unconscious. 

And when a builder finally dies—usually from a heart that simply stops mid-heave, or a spine crushed when a wall slips faster than the mud usually allows—they do not give the body to the clay. To sink is the enemy; they will not let the basin have their dead. 

Instead, the body is carried to the rendering vats near the warmest vents. The flesh is given to the scavenger crabs that penned in wicker cages for the next week's meat; the tallow goes to grease the wooden pry-bars and the sled runners; and the bones—the thick, dense, heavy femurs and ribs of a life spent carrying stone—are scrubbed clean with abrasive silt. 

Within three days, the dead builder is fitted into the city. A femur becomes a toggle-pin holding a corner-joint of a watchtower together; a pelvis is mortised into the curve of an aqueduct trough; a skull is wedged beneath a foundation slab to level it against a two-degree list. As the next collapse comes, and the wall is torn down and thrown onto a sled and carried half a mile east to be raised again, a grandson will catch his grandfather’s thigh-bone tossed to him by a cousin, wedge it neatly between two loaves of wet gray shale, lean his shoulder against it to test the balance, and immediately reach for the next stone.
