To see the people of the basin clearly, you must first look at their feet. 

Even the smallest child walking the causeways wears sandals built like masonry. The soles are stacked three inches thick—layer upon layer of cured gander-neck, boiled crab-shell plates, and compressed felt made from molted contour feathers, all lashed together with twisted gut and smeared with black pitch. They do not walk with a heel-to-toe roll; the slickness of the bacterial slime and the razor-sharpness of the unweathered basalt forbid it. Instead, they move with a flat-footed, knees-bent shuffle, testing the purchase of every slab before shifting their weight. It gives the entire population a low, swaying gait, like sailors on a greased deck, their hips wide and their centers of gravity dropped down toward the warm stone.

They are a stocky, barrel-chested people, evolved by generation or necessity to hold their ground. Their skin has the pallor of tallow that has been smoked over a dung fire—a waxy, yellowish-grey that beads moisture rather than absorbing it. Because the air is permanently thick with atomized bird-grease, sulfur, and mineral steam, they are hairless by choice. Hair holds the damp; it mildews and mats with lice. Every few days, men, women, and children sit on the low stone benches inside their doorways and scrape each other’s scalps, brows, and limbs with freshly struck flakes of obsidian. A person with a freshly knapped blade can shave a skull clean in two minutes without drawing a bead of blood, though everyone bears the pale, hair-thin scars of old slips—white lines cross-hatching their knuckles, forearms, and shins where the black glass bit them in their youth.

Because the city’s roar is absolute and eternal, the human voice has largely been abandoned outside the thick, corbeled walls of the houses. A newcomer might think them mute or morose. In the streets, their faces are slack, their mouths shut tight to keep out the mist and the drift of airborne down. 

Instead, they speak with their hands and their tools. Over the decades, they have developed a language of percussion and posture. A mason up on a tower does not shout down to his carrier; he strikes the basalt wall twice with the butt of his bone maul, and the sharp *tock-tock* travels straight through the dry-stone courses, felt through the soles of the carrier’s thick sandals thirty feet below. In the narrow alleys, where two people can barely pass, conversations happen through touch and the click of finger-bones. They wear thimbles of hollowed snail-shell or carved horn on their thumbs and middle fingers; by snapping them together against the resonant cavity of a woven basket or their own leather-clad chests, they produce sharp, dry *clacks* that cut through the lower frequencies of the steam-hiss and crab-rattle. 

Up close, they talk by pressing their foreheads together. In the market plazas—flat spaces where the water stands up to the ankle bone in geometric basins—you will see two dozen pairs of people standing chest-to-chest, eyes closed, the bridges of their noses touching, humming low, nasal syllables that vibrate directly from one skull into the other. It makes every transaction look like an act of profound intimacy or quiet mourning.

They do not measure time by days. The pewter sky brightens by two shades and darkens by two shades, a shift so gradual and muddled by the rising steam that it is useless for marking hours. Instead, time is measured by the grease-lamps, the molting of the waterfowl, and the shifting of the walls. 

The city is alive under their feet. The cooling basalt crust beneath the valley still settles, groans, and cracks as the magma chamber deep below loses its heat over centuries. A subtle tremor will run through a quarter—not an earthquake, just a shrug of the cooling rock—and fifty yards of dry-stone wall will suddenly lose its plumb, the flat blocks sliding an inch outward on their slick coat of wet lichen. 

This is the core of their civic life. There are no kings here, no priests of the sky, no merchants hoarding metal. The highest authority belongs to the *Chinkers*—the guilds of wall-listeners. They are often the oldest citizens, those whose knees have stiffened too much for the rookery-towers. They spend their lives walking the labyrinth with long staves of spliced whalebone, pressing the tips against the lower courses of the masonry, their ears held flat against the bone. They are listening for the *grind*—the sound of basalt shifting under too much load before a collapse can happen. 

When a Chinker taps a warning rhythm on a foundation stone, every person within fifty paces drops their baskets of snails or their scraping-knives. There is no panic, only an immediate, heavy choreography. Men and women alike unhook the stout horn-levers from their belts, wedge them into the dry seams, and throw their weight against the stone in unison, guided by the sharp *clack-clack* of the Chinker’s thumb-shells. They ease the five-hundred-pound slabs back into their geometric beds, shim them with fresh flakes of flint hammered into the joints, and go back to their scraping without a word spoken. To let a wall fall in a city this dense is to choke an alley, to block a steam-vent, to flood a neighbor’s cellar with scalding condensation. Maintenance is the only morality they recognize.

Inside the houses, the world changes. The corbeled roofs are stacked six feet thick with heavy stone, lined on the inside with skins and mortared with a paste of boiled lichen and ash. When the low stone slab of a door is dragged shut, the deafening roar of the millions of birds drops away to a deep, underwater thrumming. 

Here, in the dark that smells of hot tallow and wet flint, the people finally use their voices. Because they never shout indoors, they speak in an astonishingly soft, rapid murmur—a continuous, breathy whispering that sounds exactly like the steam vents beneath their floors. They sit on piles of dried bird-pelts around shallow stone bowls where wicks of twisted moss float in rendered gander-fat. The light is yellow, smoky, and tiny. 

In this warmth, they do their fine work. Their fingers, thick and calloused from the basalt, are remarkably deft. They split the hollow leg-bones of the giant waterfowl into needles; they weave the translucent, airtight gut-casings into waterproof parkas; they flake black volcanic glass into lancets, scrapers, and saw-toothed blades. Nothing is wasted. The meat of the birds and crabs is stewed in pots set directly over the floor-vents where the steam is hottest. The shells of the millions of basalt snails are crushed with stone rollers into a coarse lime powder, which they rub into their armpits, groins, and between their toes to keep the warm, ever-present slime from rotting their skin. Even the dead are folded into this economy: there is no earth to bury them in, and no wood to burn them. When someone dies, they are carried to the highest, flattest roofs of the rookery-towers and laid out naked on the black stone. Within an hour, the heavy, hook-billed waterfowl and the blind crabs have stripped the bones clean. Three days later, the family climbs back up to collect the skeleton, carrying the femurs and ribs back down into the dark to be seasoned, carved into levers, and wedged back into the walls of the house.

The children are raised in the crevices. Before they are big enough to wear the heavy platform sandals, they crawl through the dry-stone drainage channels and the narrow spaces between the double-walled cisterns, as pale and quick as the rock-crabs they hunt. They learn before they have words that the ground is a blade. A toddler here does not fall with open palms; they learn to tuck and roll onto their padded leather backs the instant their feet slip on the velvet slime. You can see them peering out from the weep-holes in the masonry at knee-height—small, hairless faces shining with grease, their dark eyes tracking the scuttling crabs, their little fingers already fitted with snail-shell thimbles, *clack-clacking* softly to one another in the roaring, pewter gloom.
