To find the ones who still keep the basin, you have to watch the herons. A bird will be stalking the rim of a sodium lamp’s amber cone, neck coiled, eyes fixed on the slick pile of salamanders warming themselves on an inspection hatch. Then, without a sound, the heron will stiffen, cock its head toward the dark gravel between the lamps, and take three long, unhurried strides backward into the mist. 

A moment later, what looked like a junction box or a low blister of rusted boiler-plate thirty yards out in the avenue will unstick itself from the mud with a soft, wet *shuck*.

They are called the Sump-Wright’s children by the people who live up on the wind-scoured shale rims, though there are no children among them, or else they have all grown to the same squat, indeterminate age. They are human in the way a cave fish is still a trout—all the old architecture is there, softened, broadened, and dropped four inches closer to the mud.

A crawler does not walk upright across the aprons. In a basin three miles wide with nothing higher than a pump-house roof, standing tall makes you an event. It draws the bats; it spooks the wading birds whose hunting patterns they read like weather vanes; it exposes the throat and the chest to the occasional, unpredictable lance of high-pressure steam when an underground main blows a gasket through the gravel. Instead, they move in a perpetual, low-hipped crouch, knuckles brushing the carpet of crabs, knees turned slightly outward like a frog’s. Their gait is a slow, rolling slide, weight shifting from one wide, flat heel to the other without ever fully lifting the sole from the slime. They don't step on the crabs; they nudge through them, the calloused sides of their feet parting the ash-colored shells with the same gentle, oily friction as a ship's bow through duckweed.

Up close, it is hard to tell where their skin ends and their clothing begins. Cotton and wool rot in forty-eight hours in the basin’s sweet, acidic sweat. The crawlers wear vulcanized rubber sliced from old conveyor belts, aprons of braided copper wire, and overlapping scales of flexible, mineral-tanned salamander hide tied with nylon cord. These garments are never taken off; they are simply patched over when they wear through, until an individual is encased in a flexible, ten-pound carapace of rubber, leather, and orange iron-paste. The translucent biofilm that coats the concrete retaining walls grows on them, too. It lives in the creases of their elbows, behind their knees, and across their broad, hairless scalps, giving them a faint, soapy sheen under the sodium lights and making them nearly frictionless when they squeeze down through the narrow vertical maintenance shafts.

They have no use for the surface during the middle hours of the day, when the grease-gray clouds brighten to the color of hot lead and the air hits its heaviest saturation. That is when they sleep, wedged three or four together inside the dry, hollow brick piers that support the knee-high pipelines, or slung in hammocks of insulated cable directly beneath the street grates, suspended ten feet above the black, rushing raceways where the turbine tail-water foams. Down there, the air is eighty-eight degrees and smells of boiled pennies and wet slate. The vibration of the great subterranean stamps and rollers passes through the concrete, up the copper cables of their hammocks, and directly into their ribcages. If you pull a crawler out of the basin and set him on the quiet shale flats above, he will go mad within three hours from the silence in his bones; his heart, accustomed to pacing itself against the forty-cycle-per-minute thrum of the deep Alternators, will begin to flutter and skip.

They come up through the grates when the pewter afternoon dims into the amber night. 

They do not use their eyes much. The constant mist and the sulfur-heavy condensation leave their corneas filmed over with a tough, milky membrane, like the nictitating lid of a diving bird. They see the world as a series of broad, watery smears—the dull yellow moons of the streetlamps, the darker gray rectangles of the collapsed sheds, the pale, greasy glimmer of salamander bellies. Instead, they navigate by the skin and the ear. Their ears are large, flat, and set low on the skull, the cartilage softened by warmth so the lobes hang down like wet flannel. They can hear the difference between a steam vent hissing at thirty pounds per square inch and one hissing at forty; they know every block of the three-mile grid by the distinct acoustic signature of its underground valves.

Here is one now, emerging from a rectangular drainage culvert at the corner of Nine-Avenue and the Smelter Apron. 

He—or she, the heavy rubber apron and the thick collar of pale neck-fat make it impossible to say—pauses with only the crown of the head and the wide, flat bridge of the nose above the curb. The nostrils flare, drinking the soup of the air. A crawler can smell a dead heron from three blocks away through the stagnant heat; more importantly, they can smell the sharp, ozone-and-garlic tang of an overheating transformer down in the vaults before the casing cracks.

Satisfied by the air, the crawler hauls itself over the lip of the culvert. It makes no more noise than a sack of wet meal sliding off a plank. Slung across its back is a three-foot prybar forged from a single piece of monel metal—the only alloy that doesn't pit and flake away in the basin's drizzle—and two wicker baskets woven from stripped, plastic-coated telephone wire. 

The crawler’s work is small, slow, and continuous, indistinguishable from the work of the crabs. 

It moves down the avenue toward the nearest sodium lamp. As it approaches the ring of light, the stacked salamanders on the junction box do not flee; they merely shift their heavy, rubbery tails an inch to the left, making room. The crawler squats beside them, laying one wide, five-fingered hand flat on the cast iron between two pulsing sets of external gills. The fingers are short, the nails gone entirely, replaced by pads of horn-hard skin that have been rasped against stone and iron since infancy. Through those pads, the crawler reads the box below. It feels the sixty-Hertz hum of the step-down coils; it feels the tiny, rhythmic *tick-tick-tick* of water dripping onto a porcelain insulator six feet down in the dark. 

With a grunt that sounds like a bubble breaking in thick mud, the crawler hooks the notched tip of the monel bar under the rim of the iron cover. It doesn't yank; it leans its dense, low-slung weight onto the bar, using the slow, hydraulic leverage of its thick thighs. The cover lifts an inch, breaking the seal of rust and biofilm. The salamanders slide off into the gravel with wet slaps.

From the hip-basket of braided wire, the crawler pulls a handful of gray paste. This is the basin’s currency and its mortar: a mastic chewed and kneaded from rendered salamander fat, powdered shale, and the chitin of ten thousand crushed crabs. Reaching down into the warm, humming dark of the junction box—without looking, eyes turned up toward the spinning pillar of midges in the yellow light above—the crawler packs the grease around the leaking seam of the conduit pipe, smoothing it with a thumb until the *tick-tick-tick* against the porcelain stops. It eases the cover back down. Within two minutes, the salamanders have crawled back onto the iron, settling against the crawler’s rubber-wrapped calves as if the human were just another warm stump of masonry.

For payment, the crawler takes the bottom-most salamander—the oldest one, whose skin has gone from pale cream to a mottled, sluggish lead-color from absorbing too much heavy metal through its belly. There is no struggle. The crawler simply slips two thumb-thick fingers into the gill-slits behind the jaw, pinches the spinal cord with a practiced, sideways twist of the wrist, and slides the limp, forty-pound body into the second wire basket. 

Then it turns its attention to the lamp itself.

The glass globe is ten feet off the ground, mounted on a squat, fluted column of cast iron. The crawler does not climb the pole; it reaches into a pouch under its apron and brings out a long, hollow reed—actually a six-foot length of quarter-inch copper instrumentation tubing scavenged from a collapsed boiler house. Stepping directly beneath the watery amber cone, the crawler tilts its head back, fits the copper tube to its wide, pale lips, and blows.

A plug of chewed crab-shell hits the bottom of the glass globe with a sharp *tock*. 

Instantly, the bats working the midge-pillar above drop four feet, drawn by the sound. Two of them collide in the dense air right beneath the lamp, their rust-colored wings tangling for half a second. Before they can recover and catch the updraft of the steam vent, the crawler’s monel bar flashes upward—not a swing, just a sudden, piston-like extension of the arm that mimics the strike of the gray herons. The flat of the bar catches one bat across the breastbone. It drops into the gravel. The crabs surge toward it, a hundred tiny slate-colored claws rising in the amber light, but the crawler’s broad foot is already there, pinning the warm, thumb-sized mammal to the mud before the shell-carpet can close over it. The bat goes into a smaller pouch near the groin, where its body heat will stay against the crawler’s skin until the meat is tender enough to swallow whole.

They do not cook. Fire requires dry fuel, and fire consumes oxygen that is already thin and heavy at the bottom of the three-mile bowl. Instead, they ferment and they parboil. 

In the flooded foundation pits of the old axle-works, where the high-pressure return pipes run naked through the standing water, the temperature stays just below a simmer. The crawlers weight their wire baskets with chunks of pig-iron and sink them into these hot, rust-red pools on the end of nylon cords. A salamander left in the pit-water for three days, colonized by the sulfur-eating bacteria that bloom around the pipes, turns into a pale, custard-soft jelly that smells of sour milk and iron filings. The crawlers gather around the edge of the pit in the twilight, squatting shoulder to shoulder, seven or eight of them at a time, dipping hollowed-out insulator caps into the broth and sucking the meat off the soft, cartilaginous bones.

During these meals, they talk. 

A stranger standing twenty feet away on the gravel avenue would hear nothing over the hiss of the corner vents and the rain-on-canvas whisper of the crabs. That is because the crawlers do not use their vocal cords; the wet, corrosive air ruins the larynx by the time a child reaches ten, leaving the throat scarred and raspy. Instead, they speak through the stone. 

Sitting with their heels wedged firmly against the concrete coping of the foundation pit, they tap the monel bars against the masonry between their feet, or drum their horned knuckles against the iron flanges of the knee-high pipes that run for miles across the flats. *Tink-tink-tock. Scratch-tock.* The sound travels through the wet concrete and the cast-iron plumbing at four thousand feet per second. A crawler sitting on a pump-house roof half a mile away in the yellow fog feels the message arrive in his hipbones: *The grate at Fourteen-South is choked with feathers; the water is backing up over the secondary coils.* Or: *The big heron with the crooked toe died by the acid-wash sluice; good marrow in the legs if you get there before the crabs.*

They have no memory of who built the factories beneath them, nor any curiosity about where the giant underground transmission lines carry the power that hums beneath their boots. To a crawler, the turbines are geology. The great, submerged stamping mills that thud softly in the deep clay are simply the heartbeats of the world, and the miles of rusted pipeline, the weeping brick kilnyards, and the fifty-yard grid of sodium lamps are the only sky and earth that have ever existed.

Sometimes, once or twice a decade, a maintenance crew from the distant municipality that still draws power from the basin’s automated belly will descend the shale terraced roads in a tracked, sealed crawler-tractor. They wear yellow plastic suits and breathe from steel tanks on their backs. They rumble down the hundred-yard-wide avenues, their headlights cutting blinding, chalk-white tunnels through the yellow mist, crushing a six-foot swath of ash-crabs into gray grease beneath their treads.

When the tractor comes, the basin empties itself. The herons lift off with slow, heavy beats of their rust-stained wings and disappear into the low overcast. The salamanders slide off the manhole covers and sink like stones into the flooded pits. 

And the crawlers simply fold themselves down. 

Three hundred yards ahead of the tractor's beams, a family of four will unhook a heavy iron street grate, drop silently into the steaming dark between the spinning turbine blades and the slick concrete wall, and pull the grate back into place with a loop of copper wire. Or, if they are caught out in the open aprons where the concrete is solid, they will simply lie flat on their bellies in the warm, running sheets of water, tucking their limbs under their vulcanized rubber aprons and pulling flaps of biofilm-slicked leather over their heads. 

The tractor will grind past ten feet away. The men inside, peering through their fogged glass visors at the flat, lifeless expanse of wet slate and low, crumbling brick, will see only a few lumps of oxidized slag or a blistered roll of old roofing tar half-submerged in the drainage sluice, covered in the same creeping carpet of thumb-sized crabs that covers everything else. 

Then the tractor will lumber back up the shale grade toward the wind and the sun. Before the rumble of its diesel engine has faded from the overcast, the lumps of slag will unpeel themselves from the wet concrete. A monel bar will tap twice against a curb—*all clear, all clear*—and a hand with horn-tipped fingers will reach into the tracks left by the machine, gathering up the crushed crabs by the fistful before the warm drizzle washes the meat down the grates.
