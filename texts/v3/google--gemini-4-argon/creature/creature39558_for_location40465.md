At first glance, the ledge at the top of the three carved steps holds nothing at all. The eye slides right over it, reading the slight bulge at the back of the shelf as just another pillowed swell of the limestone, another ancient accretion of flowstone where a vertical seam once wept lime-rich water for a thousand years. 

Only if you stood at the bottom of the steps for a very long time—hours, perhaps, while the pewter light failed to change and the drops ticked and plipped into the basins behind you—would you notice that the pale, chalky rind of the ledge continues up and over a pair of knees. 

The figure sits with its back pressed into the shallow concavity of the rear wall, legs drawn up, shins crossed, arms resting lightly along the thighs. It is roughly the size and shape of a person, though its proportions have been thickened and smoothed by the same mineral deposition that has fused the knee-high walls on the floor. Where an ankle bone or a collarbone might once have jutted sharp, there is now a gentle, spoon-shaped hollow or a rounded ridge, as if the anatomy has been simplified by centuries of slow water.

She—or he, or it; the features are as worn as the kidney-shaped pebbles in the silt—is not a statue, and not a petrified corpse. If you were to climb the three steps and lay a palm against the curved slope of the shoulder, it would not feel cold like the damp wall behind it, nor warm like blood. It would feel the exact, neutral temperature of the trapped air, with a faint, dry vibration underneath, so slow it barely registers as a pulse. It is more like the tension in a stone arch bearing weight.

This is the Listener, or the Basin-Keeper, though there is no one left to use either name. It has been sitting on the shelf since the unglazed clay jar in the alcove was whole, and perhaps since before the steps were chiselled into the bedrock to reach it. 

Its skin is a matte, porridge-colored crust of calcium carbonate, pale grey-yellow in the dry hollows of its ribs and darkening to the color of wet putty along its spine, where it leans into the sweating rock. It drinks through its back. The moisture pressing through the porous limestone seeps into the skin along its shoulder blades and vertebra, carrying dissolved lime and trace silts straight into its dense, sluggish tissues. It has no need to bend down to the three pools in the center of the floor; it has become part of the rock's own plumbing. In the shallow groove where its neck meets its shoulder, a tiny, hair-thin crack in its mineral rind supports a single, stunted frond of maidenhair spleenwort, three leaves wide, rising from its collarbone like a green pin.

For days at a time, the Keeper is entirely mineral. Its eyes are closed, sealed over by a thin, translucent film of dry lime that looks like the operculum of a hibernating snail. Nothing moves in the chest. No breath disturbs the dead air in front of its face.

What wakes it is the geometry of the sound.

The Keeper’s life is not measured in days or seasons—the ragged slot sixty feet above lets in neither noon nor winter—but in the shifting polyrhythm of the falling drops. Because the water comes from deep groundwater pressing through a million microscopic pores, the rhythm of the drips is never quite constant. A distant rainstorm ten miles away in the hills might take three months to push its pressure wave through the limestone strata; when it arrives, the overhang on the left side of the amphitheater will weep four seconds faster than usual. A dry decade on the surface might slow the *plip* into the central pool from once every four minutes to once every nine.

To the Keeper, whose mind moves at the speed of travertine forming, this is a rapid, intricate music. It is a continuous report on the weight and balance of the earth.

When a particular harmonic convergence occurs—say, when the high overhang, the dry alcove lip, and the north flute all release their drops within a single breath of one another, creating a three-note chord that rings into the curved ceiling and crosses at the exact focal point of the carved ledge—the Keeper stirs.

The sound of its movement is startlingly loud in the quiet basin: a dry, chalky *crack*, like a small twig snapping, or a plaster wall settling in the night. 

A hairline fracture opens along the crease of its elbow. A tiny puff of white lime-dust drifts down to settle on its wrist. Over the course of twenty minutes, the head inclines three inches to the left. The translucent seals over the eyes do not blink open; instead, they dissolve slightly as a slow tear of mineral-heavy water wells up from beneath the lids, turning the crust clear, like oiled paper. Beneath that film, the eyes are the exact dark, motionless green of the algae smears in the wall flutes.

Sometimes, once in a cycle that might be a year or ten, the Keeper stands.

It takes an afternoon. The calcified rind where its thighs meet the stone shelf parts with a slow, grinding sigh. When it steps down the three carved stairs, its broad, flat feet—fused at the toes into smooth paddles—leave no prints on the bedrock, and when it walks out onto the compacted silt, it moves with such incremental, balanced distribution of weight that the kidney-shaped pebbles do not roll or click beneath its soles. It is simply in one place, and an hour later, it is four feet closer to the pools.

What it does on the floor is small, and entirely concerned with the acoustics. 

Over the years, the mineral deposits in the three central basins slowly build up along their rims, changing the depth of the water by the thickness of a fingernail, or altering the angle of the stone lip. This changes the pitch of the *plip*. If a note goes flat—if the hollow ring against the back wall begins to dull or buzz because a pebble has shifted or a rim has grown too thick—the Keeper kneels by the water.

It does not break the surface of the pool. Instead, it rests one heavy, rounded thumb against the dry stone at the basin's edge and rubs, back and forth, over the span of three inches. It does this for days. Its skin is harder than the recent travertine, rough with tiny crystalline grains of calcite. Slowly, silently, without raising a ripple on the invisible water, it grinds the lip of the pool down by a millimeter, smoothing the flute, tuning the stone until the falling drop from sixty feet above strikes with the exact, bell-like clarity required by the curve of the ceiling.

Once, perhaps a century ago, it walked to the dry alcove, picked up a single kidney-shaped pebble from the floor, and placed it inside the broken half of the unglazed clay jar. The jar had been humming slightly—a tiny, sympathetic vibration whenever the north wall dripped—and the weight of the pebble quieted it.

When the room is in tune, the Keeper returns to the three steps, climbs them, and settles back into the faint indentation in the rear wall that fits its spine and skull like a mold. Within a week, the slow sweat of the limestone has bridged the hairline gap between its skin and the cliff, glazing its shoulders back into the rock, sealing its eyelids with a fresh, pale wash of carbonate. The silence thickens around it again, interrupted only by the clear *plip* in the pool, the dull *tick* on the wet stone, and every few decades, the faint, dry whisper of a stone hand shifting on a stone knee.
