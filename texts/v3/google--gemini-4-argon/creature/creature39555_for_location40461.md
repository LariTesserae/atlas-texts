To find the rest of the basin’s life, you have to stop looking for animals and start looking for parts. In an environment where the temperature never drops, the light never dims, and the energy is delivered in measured, mechanical pulses, biology has no reason to be soft. Softness is for things that have to hurry, or hide, or change. Here, life has the leisure to be cast, stamped, and milled.

If you stand beside one of the shallow drainage channels for an hour—long enough for the cutting-fluid smell to coat the back of your throat and the polyrhythm of the stacks to bleach the thoughts from your head—you will notice that some of the rainbow oil slicks are moving against the current. 

They are not oil. They are the basin’s scavengers, though there is very little death here to scavenge. Each is a colonial organism the thickness of a feeler gauge, composed of millions of microscopic, copper-shelled zooids linked by heavy hydrocarbon chains. Floating flat on the surface tension of the sulfurous puddles, the colony looks exactly like a spilled puddle of blue-black bluing dye. It moves by selectively altering the chemical charge along its margins, drawing itself across the warm fluid toward the beaded trails of joint-tallow left behind by the twelve-legged grazers. When it finds a droplet of the clear lubricant, the colony flows over it, its underside shimmering like tarnished brass as it digests the heavy grease into pure energy, leaving the silica glaze of the channel floor wiped machinist-clean.

Follow that channel back toward the base of the nearest vent—say, the squat, wide cylinder near the basin's center that fires on the eleven-second mark—and the ground tremor becomes heavy enough to blur your vision. The black, ribbed bacterial mat is thickest here, three inches of dense, vulcanized gristle. 

Half-buried in the mat, arranged in a neat, concentric circle four feet from the iron base, are things that look like corroded brass grease-fittings. They are the size of walnuts, hex-sided by the natural crystalline lattice of the chalcopyrite they secrete. For ten seconds, they are entirely inert, sealed tight against the acidic damp. Then the stack above them fires—*chuff*—and as the heavy stone lid slams back down into its seat—*CLANK*—the shockwave travels down the iron column and hits the clay. 

Instantly, every little hex-nut in the circle pops open a tiny, spring-loaded operculum, shooting a fine, hair-thin wire of glistening copper-protein five inches into the air to snare droplets of mineral-rich steam before snapping shut again on the half-second. They do not eat the steam; they are farm-hand organisms, harvesting the suspended iron sulfates before the mist can drift away, pulling the raw ore down into the black rubber mat to fertilize the bacteria they live inside. Their timing is not instinctive; it is mechanical. If a stack’s rhythm were to falter by half a second, the entire ring around its base would starve within a week, firing their golden wires into empty air.

But the vents do not falter. And because they do not falter, they have drawn the Tappet-Walkers.

You will hear a Walker long before you see it, though it takes a practiced ear to separate its sound from the general shop-floor din of the valley. Between the heavy *chuff-clank* of the boilers and the *shhk-shhk* of the brass-plated grazers peeling up the floor mats, there is an occasional, high-pitched *tink... tink... tink*, like someone tapping a cold chisel against an anvil with a very small hammer.

The creature making it is three feet tall, standing on four spindly, calliper-like legs that meet at a central hub no bigger than a fist. Its body is dark, matte-gray cast iron, pebbled like the skin of a skillet, and so thin that from fifty yards away it looks like a surveyor’s tripod left out in the mud. It has no head, only a long, counterweighted boom extending from its central hub, tipped with a dense nodule of solid nickel-iron.

The Walker is a parasite of the equilibrium. It approaches the eleven-second vent with a stiff, deliberate gait, planting its pointed feet into the ribbed rubber mat until it is braced against the iron cylinder. It extends its boom upward until the nickel-iron nodule rests just beneath the lip of the heavy mineral cap. 

The steam builds inside the column. The cap lifts four inches—*chuff*—and as it rises, it knocks the Walker’s boom upward. The creature does not flinch; its central hub contains a coiled, spiral spring of high-tensile keratin, eight feet long and wound tight as a watch-main, which absorbs the brutal upward stroke of the stone lid with a dull, ratcheting *k-k-k-k-click*. The lid drops—*clank*. The Walker stands still, trembling slightly with the stored tension in its gut. Eleven seconds later, the lid rises again, and the Walker takes another tooth on its internal ratchet. *K-k-k-k-click.* 

It will stand there for three hours, taking eighty or ninety strokes of the volcanic piston, winding itself tighter and tighter until its iron-pebbled shell groans under the torque. When it is fully wound, it unlocks its feet from the rubber mat, pivots on one pointed heel, and walks away into the grey flats. It does not eat the bacteria. It does not drink the cutting fluid. It runs entirely on the mainspring in its belly, using that stored mechanical tension to drive the microscopic mills inside its legs that grind atmospheric sulfur and clay dust into the new iron cells of its shell. It will walk for four days on a single winding, clicking softly across the anhydrite clay, searching for a mate to lock hubs with so they can wind a newly budded, fist-sized offspring between them before their own springs run down.

And then there is the thing that lives in the spaces between the rhythms.

It has no shell, no legs, and no spring. In a valley of hardware, it is the only thing that looks like a mistake—or a maintenance crew.

If you watch the twelve-legged, workbench-sized grazers for long enough, you will notice that their tarnished brass plates are never fouled by the silica glaze, and the vents themselves, despite millions of years of precipitating ore, never quite choke on their own slag. Once every few days, the three-cylinder cluster half a mile to the east will begin to drift out of its cadence—one of the caps seating a fraction of a second late because a crust of copper-sulfide has grown too thick on the bevel. The *clank, clank, clank-chuff* begins to limp.

When a vent limps, the mud two hundred yards away stirs.

What rises from the warm anhydrite clay is not an animal in any sense that has bones. It is a heavy, cohesive slug of metallic mercury-amalgam and black naphtha-sludge, six feet long and thick as a sack of cement, held together by a dense magnetic field generated by the iron plates churning in its core. It looks like a mound of iron filings and axle grease dragged over a magnet.

It does not walk; it rolls, a slow, heavy, caterpillar-track smear that leaves no trail because its own weight presses the damp clay flat and smooth behind it. It is drawn across the flats by dissonance. To its internal magnetic array, the polyrhythm of the basin is a complex, standing electromagnetic wave; when a valve sticks or a rhythm stutters, it creates a jagged burr in that wave that irritates the amalgam from a mile away.

It reaches the limping three-pipe cluster and hauls its heavy, glistening bulk up the vertical iron flank of the offending chimney, moving at the speed of cold molasses. It flows right over the dinner-plate hematite limpets—which simply clamp down harder until it passes—and pools around the rim of the ten-foot stack, just beneath the heavy stone cap.

The cap rises—*chuff*—and the amalgam extrudes a thick, blunt pseudopod of abrasive silicon-carbide grit into the gap. The cap drops—*thud*. Instead of a clean metallic ring, there is a harsh, grinding crunch as five hundred pounds of mineral lid comes down on the creature’s arm. The amalgam does not pull back. It holds its gritty limb in the bevel, letting the vent hammer it flat, secreting a concentrated, smoking paste of sulfuric and nitric acid directly onto the high spot of the valve seat. 

*Chuff-crunch. Chuff-crunch. Chuff-grrrk-clank.*

Over twenty minutes, the creature uses the vent’s own furious pressure to lap the valve. It grinds the excess ore away, absorbs the pulverized copper-sulfide directly into its sluggish, magnetic bulk to replenish its own mass, and slowly withdraws its pseudopod as the bevel comes back to true. 

*Clank, clank, clank-chuff.*

The three-beat marine cadence settles back into its groove. The standing wave across the six-mile basin smooths out. The amalgam slides down the warm iron pipe, drops into the shallow pool of cutting fluid at the base with a heavy, oily *plop*, and sinks back into the grey clay until the next time a tolerance slips.

Above it, the silver-white sky does not dim. The steam rises fifty feet, flattens against the inversion, and drifts away in oily rags. Half a mile west, a Tappet-Walker finishes its winding with a sharp, final *ting*, unlocks its calliper legs from the rubber mat, and begins its four-day march toward nowhere in particular, ticking like a grandfather clock across the warm, grease-sheened floor of a world that never needs to be tended, because the tending is what it is made of.
