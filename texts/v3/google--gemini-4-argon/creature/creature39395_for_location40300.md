At first, you only see the animals that the light makes obvious: the herds of gray ungulates folding their long legs onto the warm flags, the oil-black birds stepping over threshold stones like solemn magistrates, the cold salamanders pinned to the frost-lined shade. If you watch them for three hours—though there are no hours here, only the endurance of the observer’s knees and eyes—you realize they are not merely calm. They are precise.

When a black bird pecks a pale berry from a wall-shrub, it does not tug or twist. The berry parts from its stem with the dry *snick* of a small latch opening. And inside the dark tangle of the bird’s cropped feathers, down where the skin should be, there is no pink flesh or yellow fat. There is a clean, dry felt of compressed gray down, and beneath that, something hard and segmented that shifts with the quiet slide of oiled slate. When the ungulates chew their cud, their lower jaws swing left, then right, then left—always seven strokes before the swallow, their rectangular pupils fixed on the middle distance without curiosity or fear. They do not drop dung on the plazas; instead, once every long cycle of walking, an individual will pause over one of the narrow drainage slits cut into the courtyard corners and let fall a single, dry, six-sided pellet of compressed fiber and mineral salt, which rolls down into the dark under-channels with a sound like a wooden bead dropped into a bowl.

The animals are the minor gears. They are the way the valley keeps its moss trimmed and its water moved from the high cisterns to the low roots.

To see what else lives here, you have to stop looking at the plazas and look into the walls.

The dry-stack masonry is not solid all the way through. A retaining wall twelve feet thick is made of two outer faces of dressed slate, fitted so tightly a knife blade cannot enter, while the core between them is a deliberate labyrinth of larger, jagged blocks propped upon one another to leave dry, whistling chambers and three-inch vertical flues. In the deep, dry dark of these interiors—where the surface condensation never reaches, and the temperature is a constant, cellar-like chill—live the things that maintain the tension of the stone.

They are called the *Shimmers* by no one, because no one speaks here, though if a name were carved into the slate, it would be something angular and voiceless. They are flat, multi-limbed creatures the size of a mason’s trowel, somewhere between a harvestman, a house centipede, and a dried fern frond. Their bodies are thin as splitting-slates, covered in a chitin that looks exactly like rusted iron or gray mica. They have twelve legs, each ending not in a claw, but in a tiny, flat, spatulate pad of hard horn.

There are millions of them inside the walls, the pyramid-foundations, and the great causeways. They spend their existence in the narrow load-bearing gaps between the stones. When the weight of a four-story tower settles by the width of a hair—because a flock of birds has mounted the roof, or a herd of grazers has leaned against the lower course—the internal stresses of the dry-stack shift. A flint shim loosens by a fraction of a millimeter.

Before the stone can slip or groan, three or four of these flat creatures will scuttle silently through the internal flues and slide their own bodies into the widening gap, stacking themselves like living iron wedges, locking their leg-joints until they are as rigid as bronze. They will stay there, pinned under four tons of basalt, for what might be days or years, feeling the vibration of the hoof-falls above them through their backs, until the herd moves off the roof and the stone breathes upward again. Then they unhook their knees, slide out, and go back to grooming the white mineral efflorescence from the undersides of the lintels. If you put your ear against the cold, wet face of a watchtower, you will not hear emptiness; you will hear a faint, dry *tick-tick-ticking*, like a pocket watch wrapped in flannel, as thousands of small, flat bodies adjust the shims in the dark.

Higher up, where the walls meet the sky, the builders left another kind of inhabitant—or perhaps the builders did not leave them, but slowly *became* them when the work reached a point where large hands were too clumsy for the final adjustments.

Look at the unfinished terrace where the bronze pry-bar lies beside the mallet. Look twenty yards to the left, along the shadow-line where the blinding eastern sun-glare meets the cobalt shade of the parapet.

Something is sitting on the coping stone that looks, from the across the plaza, like a bundled wool cloak left behind by a quarryman, or a large, silver-gray lichen that has grown over a boundary marker.

It is roughly the size and shape of a crouching man with his knees drawn up to his chin, though it has no head in the way an animal has a head. Its exterior is a dense, overlapping thatch of stiff, hollow hairs—like the winter coat of a mountain goat, but coarser, the color of weathered cedar shingles and frost. This coat is arranged in terraced ridges down its back, so that the atmospheric mist condenses on its shoulders and runs down neat, natural gutters along its flanks, dripping off its elbows onto the stone at the exact same rate as the dwarf conifers.

When it moves, it does not move all at once. It moves the way a shadow moves if the lamp is carried by someone walking very, very slowly in another room.

Over the course of twenty minutes, an arm—long, jointless-looking, wrapped in the same damp, gray thatch—unfolds from beneath the mantle. The hand at the end is hairless, the skin thick, dry, and the dark, polished blue-gray of river flint. It has six fingers: three facing three, like the jaws of a pair of tongs. The nails are not horn; they are flat, square plates of copper-colored mineral that grow continuously from the fingertips and are worn bevel-edged by the rock.

The creature does not breathe air in and out of lungs. Along its ribs, hidden under the thatch of hair, are gill-like pleats of pale, damp membrane that absorb oxygen directly from the thin, wet vapor of the plateau. Because it has no lungs, it has no voice, no sighing, no rhythmic heave of the chest. Its blood is a clear, slow-moving brine that never freezes in the western shadows and never warms in the eastern glare.

There are perhaps three hundred of these throughout the three-mile grid of the city. They are the *Keepers of the Plumb*, though they do not build. Building is finished, or rather, building has been suspended at the exact moment of perfection. Their work is the prevention of history.

Watch the one near the unfinished wall. Its six-fingered hand reaches out— taking five minutes to cross the three feet of space—and touches the taut string of the copper plumb-bob hanging from the tripod. A tiny bead of condensation had formed on the hemp cord, its weight pulling the string a thousandth of an inch out of true. The creature’s thumb-plate touches the bead. The water is wicked away instantly into the dry, porous skin of its palm. The plumb-bob does not even swing; it simply ceases to be wrong.

Then the hand retreats into the wool-thatch.

Wait longer, and you will see another one down in the sunken courtyard, kneeling by a cistern. A single brown needle from a dwarf conifer has fallen not onto the moss, where it belongs, but onto the clean gray flagstone six inches to the left. The Keeper does not hurry across the plaza to fetch it. Instead, when the scheduled shadow of an ibex-herd passes over that square of stone, you will notice that one of the gray shapes in the middle of the herd is not an ungulate, but a Keeper moving on all fours, its gait so perfectly matched to the soft *pad-pad* of the animals that the eye skips right over it. As the herd passes the cistern, a blue-gray hand drops from the wool, pinches the pine needle, and tucks it into a pouch of folded skin beneath its throat. The herd moves on; the Keeper remains behind, folding itself into the right-angle corner where a buttress meets a stair, becoming once more an architectural detail—a bolster of dry moss, a weathered stone corbel, a heap of forgotten fuller's earth.

They have eyes, though they rarely open them in the eastern glare. When one is working in the deep, frost-rimmed gloom of a western crevice—scraping away a film of ice that has grown a fraction too thick and threatens to pry two slates apart—the thatch over its brow parts slightly. Beneath are four small eyes set in a horizontal row, without whites or lids, looking like polished cabochons of smoky quartz set directly into the gray leather of the face. They do not look up at the forty-degree sun. They do not look out at the mountain ridges that hem the valley in. Their focal length is twelve inches; beyond three feet, the world to them is only a blur of pale yellow light and cobalt geometry. Up close, however, they can see the grain of the basalt, the microscopic pores in a flint shim, and the exact line where water intends to freeze.

And beneath everything—beneath the cisterns, beneath the dry-stack foundations, down where the young, sharp bedrock of the mountain is split by deep, vertical faults filled with black, motionless water—there is the last thing.

You never see it. You only know it is there because of the cisterns.

The water in the plaza basins stays at the exact same chisel-mark on the rim, despite the constant *plip-plip* of the dripping ferns and the drinking of the herds. That is because every cistern is connected at its base, by a pipe of hollowed soapstone, to the flooded faults below the city. And down in that dark, frigid water lives something vast, blind, and muscular—a single, continuous siphon-creature, like a pale, branched root or a colonized tunicate as thick as a temple column, threading its way through three miles of subterranean rock-fissures.

It has no brain, only a series of massive, slow-pulsing hydrostatic hearts spaced every hundred yards under the plazas. When too much condensation drips from the roofs into the northern cisterns, the creature feels the tiny increase in pressure on its upper membranes; it contracts a sphincter of white muscle half a mile away and draws the excess water down into its own body, exhaling it as a fine, invisible vapor through dry stone vents high up on the southern ridge, feeding the mist that drifts back across the plateau to bead upon the slate again.

Once every few hours—if you are lying flat on your stomach in the center of the widest plaza, pressing your cheek into the cold, wet stone—you will feel it: a single, heavy, sub-audible *thump* deep in the bedrock, as the great siphon shifts its weight in the dark fault below, balancing the water table to the breadth of a chisel-mark, while above it the silent herds rise from the sunny roofs, turn left at the obelisk, and walk down into the street.
