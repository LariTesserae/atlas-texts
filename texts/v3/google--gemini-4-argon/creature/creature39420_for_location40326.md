At first, you only see their work: a smudge of pale lithium grease wiped across the face of a pressure gauge that has no needle, or a small cairn of hexagonal nuts stacked by thread-pitch on the lip of a condenser tray. 

If you wait in the clinical glare, watching the razor-sharp shadows beneath the viaducts, one of the shadows will eventually detach itself from the zinc plating and step out into the white light.

They are called the *Tappers*, or the *Shims*, though they have no language with which to name themselves. From fifty yards away, perched on the narrow flange of an I-beam, one might be mistaken for a man wrapped in a heavy, oil-stained shop apron, crouching to inspect a weld. Up close, the human shape resolves into something much more provisional—an anatomy assembled by committee from the valley’s own surplus, where the distinction between organ, tool, and garment has never been defined.

They are roughly the height of a tall man, though they rarely stand upright, preferring a low, counterbalanced crouch that keeps their center of gravity close to the steel decking. Their torsos are broad and flat, sheathed in overlapping plates of grey, vulcanized hide—the same tough, gasket-like epidermis found on the six-legged hounds below, though on the Shims it has grown thick and stiff, cured by the constant ozone until it resembles linoleum or heavy conveyor belting. Beneath this hide, they do not have a conventional skeleton. When they breathe—a slow, hydraulic expansion that takes nearly a full minute to complete—you can see the sharp, geometric outlines of internal trusses shifting beneath the skin. Their collarbones are literal leaf-springs of laminated bone and flexible brass, and their spines are a series of interlocking ball-and-socket joints that click softly, like a torque wrench reaching its setting, whenever they twist to look behind them.

They have no faces in the mammalian sense. The head is a smooth, teardrop-shaped cowl of unblemished white porcelain, identical in material and glaze to the high-voltage insulators that forest the upper ridges. It is completely featureless save for two narrow, horizontal slits where the eyes should be. Behind these slits, there are no eyeballs; instead, there is a recessed cavity containing a series of sliding brass apertures and dark, oil-filled lenses that constantly dilate and contract to manage the blinding white sky. When a Shim looks directly at you, you do not see a pupil; you see a tiny, inverted reflection of the valley behind you, focused on a backing plate of brushed silver at the rear of its skull. 

Because the valley swallows sound, the Shims have no mouths and no ears. To breathe, a row of six vertical louvers along their flanks—just below the rib-plates—flutters open and shut, exhaling a faint, warm mist that smells of camphor and warm copper. 

Their limbs are long, spindly, and multi-jointed, terminating not in hands, but in clusters of specialized, prehensile digits that resemble calipers, feeler gauges, and hooked scribers. The material of their fingers is a horn-like substance with the blueish, iridescent temper of freshly milled steel. With these, they navigate the burr-sharp terraces without ever being cut; their metallic nails skate over the raw edges of the boilerplate with a dry, whispering *shhh-ink* that is the closest thing to footsteps the valley ever hears.

They are creatures of maintenance, though the machine they maintain has no purpose and requires no repair. Their lives are spent in a state of slow, obsessive calibration. 

A Shim will spend three hours crouched beside a four-inch bolt that secures nothing to nothing. It will extend a long, flat digit—thin as a razor and marked with microscopic graduations—and slide it into the narrow gap between the washer and the deck-plate. It is measuring the clearance. Finding the gap unacceptable by some internal, inscrutable standard, the Shim will reach into one of the deep, muscular pouches that hang from its abdomen. These pouches are biological, lined with warm, secreting membranes, yet what the Shim pulls from them are thin, crescent-shaped slivers of brass and mica, grown within its own body like kidney stones or pearls. It will slide one of these organic shims into the gap, tap it flush with the heavy, weighted heel of its wrist, and then sit back, its lenses whirring softly as it contemplates the corrected geometry.

Their diet is a closed loop with the valley’s strange ecology. They do not hunt the six-legged hounds, though they move among them like shepherds. When a hound’s eyes dim and brighten, a Shim will sometimes kneel beside it, pull a pair of fine, silver-plated needles from a sheath in its forearm, and insert them into two small ports behind the beast’s skull. The Shim does not harm the animal; it merely draws off a small charge of static electricity and a few drops of thin, salty cerebrospinal fluid, which it collects in a glass ampoule grown into the palm of its hand. In return, the Shim uses a curved, hook-like digit to scrape the excess mineral efflorescence from the hound’s hinged ribs, preventing the plates from seizing up. 

For bulk sustenance, the Shims harvest the translucent bladders of the knurled vines, puncturing them with a hollow claw and drawing the conductive fluid directly into veins that run like braided steel hose beneath their grey skin. They also eat the grease. Not the fresh, cold grease of the upper ridges, which is too sterile, but the "ripe" grease found deep in the lower manifolds—grease that has been churned by thefour-minute turbines for months until it is colonized by anaerobic, oil-eating bacteria, turning it into a rich, iridescent grey custard.

The most uncanny aspect of the Shims is how they inhabit the valley’s broken spatial logic. A human mind, looking at the spun-glass pipe passing through the solid cast-iron pillar, experiences a wave of vertigo; the eye refuses to resolve the blur. The Shims, however, *live* in that blur.

Because they have evolved within an overloaded architecture, their perception of space is not Euclidean; it is schematic. A Shim does not walk around the cast-iron pillar; it steps directly into the shimmering, unresolved zone where the glass and iron coexist, its porcelain cowl flickering like a bad television signal, and emerges on the other side, smeared with a faint residue of mercury that wasn't on the outside of the pipe. They use the impossible stairwells as shortcuts, stepping off a perforated steel landing and dropping ten feet through empty air to land on a catwalk that, by any rational measurement, is forty yards to the left and twenty feet *above* them. They do not fall so much as they are *re-indexed* by the landscape.

They love the false shadows. In a world of relentless, clinical glare, the shadows that do not match their objects are the only places of true rest. You will often find three or four Shims huddled together on a bright zinc roof, arranged carefully within the long, dark silhouette of a pine tree cast by a cluster of porcelain valves. While inside the shadow of the tree, their posture changes. They unhook the tension-springs in their shoulders; their brass apertures dilate fully into black pools; they sway slightly, rhythmically, in a breeze that isn't blowing, as if their bodies are translating the shadow’s memory of wind in needles. If you look closely at the zinc plating beneath them while they rest in the pine-shadow, you will see that the metal is momentarily dusted with a fine, yellow pollen that vanishes the instant the shadow shifts.

They are largely solitary, passing one another on the narrow gantries with only a brief, silent acknowledgment: a quick, synchronized clicking of their aperture lenses that leaves a tiny, bronze afterimage—much like the caliper-birds—hanging between them in the air. 

Yet they have a society, and it is centered entirely around the Ten-Minute Lock.

Long before the pistons actually reach top-dead-center, the Shims know it is coming. They feel the barometric shift in their abdominal pouches; the fluid in their veins changes viscosity. Half an hour before the crescendo, they begin to climb out of the lower labyrinths, ascending the condensers and the miles of threaded rod, moving toward the high ridges where the air is thinnest and the ozone is sharp enough to taste.

They gather in small groups of six or seven around the great brass petcocks and the relief valves that crown the boiler-ridges. As the turbines slow to their final, agonizing creep, the Shims link themselves together. They do not hold hands; they unfold long, flat strips of copper-colored cartilage from their forearms and bolt them to the forearms of their neighbors using small, biological wing-nuts, forming a living bus-bar, a grounded circuit of flesh and porcelain.

When the lock occurs—when the three-mile radius seizes and the circuit-diagram frost flashes across the steel—the Shims go entirely rigid. Unlike the hounds, they do not look at the sky. They bow their white, featureless heads toward the deck-plates. 

During those ten minutes of absolute stillness, while the sub-audible gear slips in the planet’s core, the Shims *record*. 

As the deep vibration passes through the soles of their feet, their hooked, steel-tempered digits drop to the zinc plating and begin to move with terrifying speed. In the dead silence, a hundred tiny scribers skate across the metal, etching microscopic, overlapping spirals into the fresh factory finish of the valley. They are not writing words; they are seismographs with legs, translating the deep, planetary slip-tooth into a dense, physical waveform on the skin of the machine. 

For ten minutes, their bodies twitch and shudder in absolute silence, their porcelain heads vibrating so fast they blur, their fingers carving hair-thin curls of steel from the plates. 

Then, the single drop of water falls from the brass petcock. *Ping.*

The pistons heave. The frost melts. The Shims unbolt themselves from one another. They do not look at the intricate, silver webs of data they have just carved into the steel—by tomorrow, the valley will have re-extruded the plate, pushing the etching over the ridge-line or swallowing it beneath a new layer of extruded brass. Instead, the Shims simply reach down, gather up the tiny, hair-like curls of steel shavings they have produced, and place them carefully into their abdominal pouches to be dissolved and remade into new shims. Then they turn, step off the edge of the ridge into a shadow that belongs to a cloud that isn't there, and are already fifty feet below, checking the tension on a truss.
