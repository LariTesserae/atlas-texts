Where the hot exhaust pipe of the six-cylinder diesel juts into the whipping wall of clubmoss, the plant matter is dead, cooked into a black, brittle crust for a radius of two feet. Just beyond that scorched halo, where the temperature drops from boiling to a steady, wet ninety degrees, the mat of sphagnum and *Azorella* is no longer entirely botanical. 

It has thickened and fused into a heavy, gelatinous caul that bridges the gap between the vibrating chassis of the generator and the fractured hornfels. The engineers who dropped the rig from the sky six weeks ago brought soil spores on their boots from the lowlands, and the drill bit itself has dragged ancient, anaerobic sulfur-eaters up from two hundred feet inside the fault. In the relentless soup of unburned diesel hydrocarbons, rock flour, and radiator heat, they have found an equilibrium.

At the surface, it looks like spilled axle grease mixed with pale, fibrous tallow. It hangs in long, trembling ropes from the undersides of the hydraulic hoses, feeding on the microscopic mist of petroleum and hydraulic fluid that weeps from the high-pressure couplings. It has no skin, only a tough, hydrophobic rind of polymerized oil and dead moss-cells that sheds the freezing cloud-drift while trapping the engine’s radiant heat underneath. 

If you were to slice into the mat where it cushions the rig’s front left stabilizer leg, you would find it warm and strangely structured. The constant, ninety-decibel hammering of the pistons has organized it. Just as standing waves in a vibrating pan sort sand into geometric ridges, the relentless six-hundred-hertz shudder of the diesel block has aligned the fungal hyphae and bacterial chains into dense, rubbery plates. It has grown *around* the vibration, developing a grain like shock-absorbing wood. When a slab of slate cracks off the cliff face above and crashes through the *Gunnera* leaves, the impact doesn't tear the mat; the kinetic energy is simply swallowed by the grey, trembling fat, distributed sideways into the talus.

It is not an animal, so it does not mind the lack of oxygen, nor does it have eardrums to rupture in the screaming vortex of the notch. Instead, it uses the violence. 

Up in the blinding, stroboscopic glare of the yellow tripods, the halogen bulbs burn at six hundred degrees. Around the steel cages of the lamps, where the wind tears the steam away, a different expression of the same residency has taken hold. The rusted chains lashing the tripods to the *Polylepis* trunks are encased in a hard, coralline crust—a calcification of baked lichen, soot, and boiled sap. This crust grows inward, millimeter by millimeter, toward the fierce white light. Every time a giant leaf whips into the lamp cage, shattering its own fibrous edge against the hot steel, the crust catches the shredded green pulp, bakes it dry in seconds, and incorporates the carbon into its own grey, stony collar. 

Down below, the drill stem—a four-inch column of polished steel spinning at four hundred revolutions per minute—plunges straight through the center of the grease-mat and into the mountain’s throat. The hole it bores is constantly flushed with water pumped from a butyl bladder chained higher up the slope. What returns to the surface is a freezing, grey slurry of pulverized slate and hornfels, thick as wet cement. 

Because the rock drains instantly, this stone-mud doesn't wash away. It builds up in the root-tangles of the bayonet-bromeliads, and the resident of the notch has claimed it. The pale, fibrous ropes of the hydrocarbon-mat reach down into the freezing slurry, binding the microscopic needles of fresh slate together with sticky, bacterial mucilage. 

Over weeks of unsupervised drilling, this mixture of rock-flour, diesel-slime, and crushed plant fiber has been packed by the thrashing wind into the crevices of the talus, forming a series of heavy, leathery bladders—like wet concrete poured into canvas sacks. These bladders sit wedged between the shuddering legs of the drill rig. They do not move on their own, yet they are in constant, rhythmic motion. As the hydraulic hoses kick and pulse with pressure surges, the bladders are squeezed against the sharp hornfels, acting as blind, passive bellows. 

With every surge of the hydraulic pump, a hose kicks left, compressing a three-foot sack of grey, rubbery sludge against the stone. From a split seam in the top of the sack, a slow, thick froth of warm, fermented oil-water and pale spores is forced out. The screaming downdraft catches the froth instantly, whipping it into an aerosol that sprays across the vertical walls of the gorge, coating the thrashing *Gunnera* and the peeling paper-bark in a fresh, glistening layer of digestible grease. When the pressure drops, the hose relaxes, the sack expands, and it sucks in a gulp of freezing cloud-vapor and falling rock-dust through its seam. 

It is a stomach that uses the machine for its musculature and the mountain for its teeth.

Further out, at the very edge of the halogen light where the shorting junction box spits its erratic blue arcs into the wet dark, the growth changes again. The electrical current is leaking into the wet bark of the dwarf tree it is chained to. The tree is dead, electrocuted weeks ago, its sap boiled into conductive channels of potash. Here, the mat has crept up the trunk in branching, dendritic patterns—Lichtenberg figures made of pale, rubbery mycelium that thrive on the ozone and the nitric acid fixed from the thin air by the sparking box. Every time the box shorts, the blue flash illuminates a delicate, trembling lattice of white threads bridging the frayed copper wires, slowly shorting them further, taking the current through its own wet, salty mass to warm itself against the thirteen-thousand-foot freeze.

Nothing here knows it is alive. Nothing here knows the machine will eventually run out of fuel. For now, the six cylinders hammer on, the drill stem grinds deeper into the fault, and the pale, trembling grease-caul thickens around the hot iron, binding the roaring steel, the thrashing leaves, and the falling mountain into a single, digesting knot.
