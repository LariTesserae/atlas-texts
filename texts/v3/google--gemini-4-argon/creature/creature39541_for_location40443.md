You do not hear the people of the shaft before you see them, because the shaft does not permit small noises to survive on their own. A cough, a footstep, the scrape of a ceramic bowl against a slate table—all of it is immediately swallowed by the vast, rattling applause of the falling water. Instead, you spot them across the two-hundred-foot void as tiny, deliberate disruptions in the wall of moss: a sudden flash of a pale, grease-shined raincoat made from split gut; the slow, rhythmic rise and fall of a bone-handled adze; a pair of bare, wide-splayed feet stepping carefully out onto a counterweighted beam.

They are a compact, heavy-jointed people, shaped by a lifetime of moving vertically rather than horizontally. Ask one of them to walk a mile across the flat alpine tundra above the rim, and they will stumble within ten minutes, their calves cramping, their eyes watering in the unblocked wind, terrified by the lack of a wall at their shoulder. But set them on a four-inch pine ledger buried in slick liverwort, and they move with a heavy, flat-footed certainty that looks almost sluggish until you realize they never look down.

Their bodies are adapted to the wet and the cold, though not by fur or blubber. They coat their skin—especially their shins, forearms, and the backs of their necks—in a thick, foul-smelling paste rendered from the oil of crushed fern-spores and the fat of mountain marmots trapped on the high scree above. The grease turns their skin a dull, yellowish-grey, like old tallow, and gives them a sour, herbal stink that hangs heavy in their wool-lined sleeping boxes. Because of the grease, the relentless cold beads of groundwater do not soak them; the water hits their bare shoulders and rolls right off, just as it does from the waxy leaves of the cliff-bells. When they stand still on a balcony for twenty minutes, watching a counterweight settle, they look like carvings of yellow soapstone, glistening and shedding water into the abyss.

They are nearly silent with their mouths. In an echo chamber where a shouted word returns four times from four different angles, colliding with itself until the syllables are pounded into gibberish, speech is kept behind the teeth. If a woman wants to speak to her brother working ten feet away on the same slick scaffold, she leans in until her greased forehead touches his temple and murmurs low into his mastoid bone, letting the vibration carry through the skull beneath the roar of the rain. 

For distance—and in the box, distance is mostly *across*—they use the walls and the void. Every person carries at their belt a pair of short, dense strikers made of hornbeam or rib-bone. They do not hammer wildly; that would ruin the acoustic peace and panic the neighbors. Instead, they know the exact resonant tone of every timber and stone shelf in their sector. A single, sharp *clack* against a hollow cedar rainwater-trough cuts through the white noise of the seepage with a distinct, woody note that carries straight across the shaft to the opposite face. Once the eye of the neighbor on the south wall is caught, the conversation happens entirely through the hands and arms—a broad, slow, angular sign language evolved to be read from two hundred feet away against a backdrop of dark green moss. They wear cuffs of bleached mountain-goat hide or white quartz beads around their wrists specifically so their hands remain visible against the dark basalt and the emerald gloom.

Look across the gulf at noon, when the square of pale sky is directly overhead, and you will see a dozen conversations happening in absolute vocal silence: a man on the third tier of the west wall holding his arms out like a balance scale, tipping his left hand down twice to tell a woman on the east wall that the great corner-beam near the lower cistern has crept another finger’s breadth out of its greased socket; the woman replying with a slow, circular sweep of her white-cuffed wrist, promising to send her sons down with fresh bark-slings and a bucket of tallow before the light leaves the shaft.

Their lives are governed by weight and rot. 

Because every square inch of their timber town is buried under three feet of waterlogged sphagnum and heavy, dripping fern-root, the town is constantly trying to tear itself off the cliff. A dry pine beam might hold five men; a pine beam that has been wet for forty years and supports six hundred pounds of saturated bog-moss will snap without warning, its fibers turned to brown porridge. 

Therefore, the most respected individuals in the settlement are not hunters or elders, but the *Probers*—though in their wrist-talk the sign for the role is simply a rigid index finger pushed slowly into the palm of the other hand. They are usually the lightest, smallest-boned adults, often women with gray hair braided tight to their skulls so it won't snag on the rough hexagonal edges of the rock. Every day, they climb into the dark, dripping undersides of the boardwalks, hanging in harnesses of braided willow-bark over the four-hundred-foot drop. They carry long, thin bodkins of fire-hardened bone. For hours, they hang like spiders among the pale curtains of rubbery liverwort, driving their needles into the undersides of the load-bearing timbers, feeling for the soft, spongy give of heart-rot, and listening—with an ear pressed flat to the wet wood—for the minute, fibrous *tick-tick* of a beam beginning to shear under the weight of its own garden.

When a beam is condemned, it is never thrown into the shaft. Nothing is thrown into the shaft. 

The bottom of the four-hundred-foot drop is a black, churning marsh of liquefied moss, shattered slate, and cold standing water where the light never quite reaches with any warmth. To drop a thirty-foot timber from the upper tiers would mean watching it tumble and ricochet off the walls, smashing lower balconies, snapping plumb-lines, and sending up a deafening cannon-crack of noise that would shake loose a dozen unstable rock-seams. Instead, a rotten beam is dismantled in place with saws of tooth-chipped flint. The sodden wood is shaved into curls, packed into clay baskets, and hung directly beneath the roof-thatch over the small, smoky braziers to dry for six months before it is burned for heat.

Because the mountain is slowly, imperceptibly tearing itself apart, the people have a peculiar relationship with time and geometry. They do not think of their homes as places, but as slow-moving vehicles. 

Every child knows the *thrum*. When the deep, sub-audible vibration rolls up from the roots of the mountain and passes through the soles of their feet, the entire settlement pauses. Up and down the four walls, three hundred grease-shined figures freeze among the giant ferns. Nobody drops a tool; nobody reaches wildly for a handhold. They simply widen their stances, sinking to their ankles in the freezing emerald bog, and wait for the *tink*—that high, glassy snap of fresh basalt splitting somewhere in the dark rock behind their kitchens.

When the *tink* comes, twenty white-cuffed hands immediately go up across the four walls, pointinglike compass needles toward the exact seam where the sound originated. Within minutes, the teenage boys and girls—who do the heavy hauling before their joints thicken with age—are climbing toward the new fracture with skin-bags of rendered fat and wedges of split horn. If the mountain has widened by the thickness of a thumbnail, the counterweights must be adjusted. You will see them leaning far out over the central emptiness, hauling on thick, slimy ropes of twisted sedge-fiber to add another ten-pound river-stone to a hanging net, watching with narrow, critical eyes as the great spanning timbers groan, slide half an inch across their greased stone pillows, and settle back into equilibrium.

They rarely go to the bottom, and they rarely go to the top. 

The bottom is for the dead, and for the harvests of blind, white cave-crayfish and pale, gelatinous fungi that grow in the black slush where the four walls meet the drowned plug of the mountain. When someone dies—usually from the wet-lung that takes them in their fifties, or the rare, silent slip of an old person whose hands have grown too stiff to grip a greasy rung—they are not buried in the moss on the walls, which would foul the water-seepage for the tiers below. They are washed of their grease with hot fern-ash, wrapped in a tight net of black-stemmed sedge, and weighted with three hexagonal chunks of basalt. 

The funeral is the only time the central void is violated. Four ropes are run from the four walls, meeting in the dead center of the shaft, two hundred feet out in the empty air. The body hangs there for an afternoon, suspended in the ruler-straight column of cold sunlight, swinging almost imperceptibly as the heavy drops of water miss it on every side. The people stand on their balconies among the huge, translucent ferns, their white-cuffed hands folded over their chests, watching the net turn slowly in the glare. Then, at a single *clack* of bone on wood from the highest eastern shelf, three of the ropes are cast off, and the fourth lowers the weight straight down into the dark, without a splash, until the line goes slack.

What lives *with* them is almost as quiet as they are. 

They keep no dogs, no goats, no animals that bark or bleat or demand flat ground to run upon. Instead, they cultivate the things that love the cold wet. In the deep, three-foot cushions of sphagnum that cover their roofs and walkways, they raise colonies of giant, fist-sized land-snails with shells striped black and slate-grey. The snails graze on the bitter liverworts and the slime that gathers on the stone gutters, moving so slowly that they seem like knuckle-bones dropped in the grass. The people harvest them by hand, boiling them over the tiny clay braziers with pungent fern-fiddleheads and wild garlic that grows in the drier seams near the top of the shaft.

And in every stone cistern—where the slate gutters empty their endless, cold trickle—there lives at least one *Spout-Watch*, the large, flat-bodied, blind salamander native to the fault. They grow as long as a man’s forearm, pale pink and translucent as raw pork fat, with feathery crimson gills that wave slowly in the mineral current. The inhabitants never eat them. They believe the salamanders can feel the mountain's *thrum* hours before human feet can detect it. Every morning, before a family steps out onto their balcony to check their counterweights, the eldest member lifts the heavy moss-mat off the house cistern and looks down into the clear, freezing water. If the pale creature is resting flat on the slate bottom, facing into the trickle, the day goes on. If it is curled into a tight ring, or thrashing its flat tail against the stone, the family stays roped to the iron-hard ironwood pegs driven into the solid basalt behind their beds, waiting for the mountain to finish its thought.
