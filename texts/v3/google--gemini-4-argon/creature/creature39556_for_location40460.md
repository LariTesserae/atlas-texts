You will see their work long before you see them, and even when you are looking directly at one, your eyes will try to edit them out as a fault in the stone. 

At first, the thing crouching at the base of the obsidian wall looks like a heap of discarded winter rags wedged between two boulders to stop a draft. Then the heap shifts—not smoothly, like an animal, but with a dry, ratcheting hitch, like a heavy wooden crate being dragged an inch at a time across concrete.

They are roughly the size and shape of humans who have been kept under a very heavy weight for generations. Everything about their posture is horizontal and compressed, built to stay below the worst of the acoustic shear. Their legs are short, bowed, and immensely thick at the knee and ankle; their shoulders are humped high around where a neck should be, forming a dense shelf of muscle and cartilage that protects the base of the skull from the bone-shivering roar of the valley.

To survive the glass-sharp flint and the fiberglass flora, they have bundled themselves in dozens of layers of woven blood-fern matting, tied tight to their limbs with silver-black vine until they resemble great, lumbering cocoons of ash and dried rust. Over the centuries, the dry spores and rock dust have settled into the weave of these wrappings and calcified. When they move, their clothes crackle like breaking pottery. 

Only when one turns toward you do you realize that the wrappings do not stop at a face. There is no soft leather skin, no wet eyes that would freeze and burst in the zero-humidity air. What peers out from the deep cowl of dead fern is a flat, broad mask of pale, calloused horn, fissured like old ivory. They have no external ears—an ear canal here would be an avenue for brain hemorrhage. Instead, they have thick, semicircular ridges of bone sweeping back from their jaws, pressing directly against the heavy hoods they wear. They do not hear the valley; they read its pressure through their skeletons. Their eyes are recessed deep behind narrow, horizontal slits of cartilage, shielded from the flying flint-chips by triple rows of stiff, wire-like bristles.

They are never entirely whole. Because they work at the raw edges where the world is still compiling, they suffer from the same spatial faults as the mountains. 

You might watch an old mason—a *Pin-Maker*, if their role can be given a name—shuffling along a razor-ledge with a load of quartz wedges on its back. As it walks through a thin patch of reality, its left leg below the knee simply ceases to be there for three strides, the stump ending in a neat, bloodless cross-section of gray, static grain, while the missing foot continues to walk ten yards higher up in the empty violet air, kicking up tiny puffs of flint dust from nothing at all. The creature does not limp or look up. Two paces later, the seam closes, the foot reattaches with a dull *thwack* like a snapped rubber band, and the creature continues on. Some of them have lived so long near the bad seams that their bodies are permanently misaligned: a shoulder set three inches too far behind the torso, or a third, translucent duplicate of an arm trailing slightly behind the real one, dropping tools it cannot quite grip.

They do not speak. Nothing with lungs could out-shout the birth-roar of the basalt. 

To communicate across a work-site, two of them will walk up to the same spire of rock and press their broad, flat foreheads—or the butts of their iron mauls—directly against the stone. Then, they strike the rock with small, dense hammers of meteoric iron. They send sharp, high-frequency clicks racing through the bedrock, cutting across the massive, low-frequency groan of the valley the way a boatswain’s whistle cuts through a gale. It is a language of pure percussion, felt in the teeth and the sternum. *Tap-tap-pause-tap.* A discussion of load-bearing tolerances. A warning that the gravity fifty yards to the left has begun to tilt toward the ceiling.

When they are close together, inside the muffled, tomb-like quiet of the spore-plastered huts, they communicate by touch. Three or four of them will pack themselves into the tiny, dark space until their bodies are wedged tight against one another like stones in an arch. There, in the dark, they rest their heavy, three-fingered hands on each other’s chests and hum. 

It is a terrifyingly deep, mechanical sound, produced not by vocal cords, but by a specialized organ in their ribcages—a kind of biological tuning fork made of dense cartilage that vibrates at the exact counter-frequency of the valley’s roar. When four of them hum together inside a hut, the two sounds cancel each other out. A bubble of absolute, dead silence is created in the center of the room. In that tiny pocket of peace, which smells fiercely of ozone and old dust, they sleep, their bodies going completely rigid, cooling down until they are the exact temperature of the granite around them.

They do not eat food in any way a creature of water and sunlight would understand. They have no stomachs, no wet digestive tracts. Instead, they harvest the heavy, lead-colored fronds of the blood-ferns and the brittle, crystalline nodes that grow at the roots of the silver-vines. Sitting outside their huts, they use small stone mortars to grind these dry growths into a fine, violet-gray powder, mixing in pinches of the unformed, "pixelated" dust scraped from the edges of rocks that haven't finished arriving yet. 

They pack this dry paste into deep, gill-like slits along their throats and under their ribs. The spores react with the relentless acoustic vibration passing through their bodies, igniting in a slow, cold, friction-driven metabolism. A worker who has just fed will vent thin, lazy curls of gray smoke from the seams of its wrappings—smoke that smells of scorched iron and immediately falls sideways into the canyon.

Why do they work? It is not religion, and it is not ambition. It is an ancient, bone-deep compulsion toward *settlement*. 

To these creatures, an uncompiled cliff face or a leaking seam of double-exposed air is an intolerable irritation, like a screaming nerve or a dislocated joint. The unfinished state of the valley causes them a profound, physical vertigo. And so, they spend their long, dry lives trying to help the world finish arriving.

Watch a pair of them at a "bleeding" seam—a place where a thirty-foot section of ridge is flickering violently between solid feldspar and empty violet gloom, making a sound like tearing sheet metal. 

They approach it on all fours, moving with infinite, crab-like patience, testing the gravity before every shift of weight by dropping a pebble tied to a fiberglass string. If the pebble falls upward, they adjust their grip, driving spiked iron pitons into their own solid shadows to anchor themselves. 

Reaching the blur, the smaller of the two—the *Holder*—unslings a massive, four-foot wedge of petrified ash-willow. It braces the blunt end against its own heavily padded chest, leaning its entire weight into the nothingness. The air around the wedge immediately begins to spark with violet static; the Holder’s arms duplicate into three or four flickering versions of themselves as the spatial fault pushes back. 

Then the larger one—the *Striker*—rises to its hind legs. It is the only time they ever stand fully upright. Braced wide, looking like a crude siege engine wrapped in dead velvet, it swings a sixty-pound maul of meteoric iron. 

The blow does not ring out; the valley eats the sound. Instead, there is a visible shockwave—a sudden, spherical ripple in the dim air that knocks loose a shower of dry spores from the nearby ferns. With every strike, the wooden wedge bites deeper into the empty air. And with every inch it goes in, the flickering stops. The double-exposure snaps shut. The rock ceases to be a rumour and becomes a fact. When the wedge is driven home, the roaring in that small corner of the cliff drops by half an octave, settling into a dull, stable hum. 

The two figures do not celebrate. They do not look at their work. The Striker lowers itself back to all fours; the Holder rubs its chest where the recoil has cracked its outer carapace, smearing a handful of rock-paste into the fissure to seal it.

And the chimes—those great, clumsy frames of stone slabs hanging over the abyss—are their masterpiece, and their end.

The slabs are not mined from the cliffs. When one of the workers grows too old—when its joints are so clogged with calcified dust and so warped by decades of bad gravity that it can no longer swing a maul or crawl through the low door of a hut—it does not die in the dark. Its fellows do not bury it; there is no soil here, and digging into the rock only opens new, screaming wounds in the geology.

Instead, the old one walks out onto one of the woven fern-bridges, or climbs to the highest, most unstable crag where a tripod frame has been erected. It strips off its layers of rotted, stone-crusted matting, leaving them for the younger ones to reuse. Beneath the wraps, its body is little more than a jointed slab of gray, mineralized horn and dense, iron-rich bone, already three-quarters stone from a lifetime of metabolizing flint-spores and vibration. 

The others lash it by the ankles and wrists to the wooden frame, hanging it out over the miles-deep drop at whatever strange, diagonal angle the local gravity demands. 

It does not struggle. It pulls its limbs in tight, locks its joints one last time, and begins to hum its deep, internal counter-note. Over weeks, as the freezing, dry air claims the last traces of its biological heat, the creature’s body finishes turning to slate. It becomes one of the chimes. 

It hangs there for years, vibrating furiously, soaking up the terrible, tooth-rattling discord of the valley’s birth and turning it into a steady, resonant note that helps the surrounding air remember how to be solid. And when it finally shatters, decades later, and its fragments drift out into the violet gloom to lock into the empty air, the creature finally achieves what it spent its whole life hammering at: it becomes the heavy, quiet heart of a mountain that will, eventually, be still.
