At first, you only see the mortar-rot. 

Between the unmoving slabs of the pale limestone cairns, tucked inside that inch-wide sleeve of impossible calm, there is something that looks like grey tallow or damp fuller’s earth. It fills the chinks between the stones, packed tight around the wooden pegs and the lengths of braided sinew. For hours, while the water outside detonate-cracks and the granite floor screams, this grey paste does nothing at all. 

Then a block of scalding brine shears past the cairn, and a stray shard of obsidian—tossed sideways by a tilted gravity well—punches through the boundary of the dead-calm skin. 

Before the black glass can strike the dry limestone, the grey paste *reaches*. 

It does not flow like slime; it unfolds with the dry, snapping precision of a carpenter’s rule. A flat, geometry-angled tongue of calcified gristle shoots out of the crevice, catches the spinning razor of obsidian mid-flight, and pulls it back inside the calm. There is a tiny, muffled *crunch*—inaudible under the artillery of the basin, but visible as a puff of black dust—and the shard is gone, ground down and mixed into the grey paste to make more of itself.

These are the **Grout-Wives**, though there is nothing wifely about them, and they are only half-alive. They are colonial sleeves of dense, lime-secreting tendon that inhabit the five cairns. A Grout-Wife has no head and no gut; she is a lattice of muscular straps wedged into the dry spaces between the stones, holding the unmortared towers together from the inside by sheer, lock-jawed tension. They do not eat the wire-kelp or the parchment-grazers. They eat *intrusion*. Whenever the violence of the basin threatens to touch the pale limestone, the Wife catches the offending vector—a pebble, a stray current, a flash of piezoelectric heat—and metabolizes the force of it, turning kinetic impact into dry, chalky bone.

The silver bubbles that shoot downward from the cairns are her eggs, or her waste—it comes to the same thing here. They are pockets of exhausted vacuum wrapped in a skin of cured sinew, heavy with negative buoyancy. They drill into the razor-gravel at the base of the towers, and they wait.

***

They are waiting for the **Plumb-Men**.

You will not see a Plumb-Man until two of the warring blocks of water jam against each other hard enough to stall. When a cubic yard of silt-water locks teeth with a column of clear brine, an interface is born—a flat, vertical plane of sheer tension, smooth as an anvil and vibrating at a pitch that makes the amber light curdle.

Out of the gravel where the silver bubbles burrowed, something levers itself up.

At the start of its climb, a Plumb-Man looks like an accident of the geology: a bundle of hexagonal basalt splinters tied together with black wire-kelp and raw, red ligament. It is roughly the length of a tall man, though it has no front or back, only four heavy, jointed limbs that end in square, flat-headed hooves of solid iron-stone. It does not swim. Nothing with a hollow in it could survive the cavitation here; lungs would be turned inside out in a heartbeat, blood vessels hammered into pink mist. The Plumb-Man is entirely solid—a dense laminate of vulcanized cartilage and stone, grown around the heavy core of a Grout-Wife’s silver bubble.

It climbs into the water column by wedging itself into the seam between two warring currents. 

To move, it uses the water’s refusal to mix. It plants two hooves against the wall of scalding brine, presses its jointed spine against the opposing wall of warm silt-water, and walks upward like a chimney sweep in a flue. As the blocks of water shear past one another at forty miles an hour, the Plumb-Man is rolled between them like a rod of iron between two millstones. 

In any other creature, this would tear limb from socket. In the Plumb-Man, the shearing force is what wakes it up. 

As the opposing currents grind against its flanks, its body begins to glow with the same sickly violet piezoelectric light as the breaking granite. The friction heats its stone-hooves until they hiss; the pressure compresses its silicate joints until it is no longer a clumsy bundle of splinters, but a rigid, thrumming tuning fork of bone and basalt.

Now you can see what it is for. It is a mason, and its trade is the maintenance of the five cairns.

As it spider-walks up the invisible wall between the currents, it reaches out with a long, three-elbowed arm into the open turbulence. Its hand is not a hand, but a stone-breaker’s jaw—two heavy plates of raw grey granite hinged with thick, purple tendon. When a three-ton slab of basalt goes tumbling past in the dark, hurled upward from the floor, the Plumb-Man does not dodge. It reaches out and *nicks* the corner of the flying boulder with its jaw.

It knows the grain of the stone the way a diamond-cutter knows a gem. It strikes at the exact moment a cavitation bubble implodes nearby, borrowing the cannon-fire force of the collapsing vacuum to drive its granite jaw into the basalt. 

*Crack.*

A flake of black stone splits off the boulder. Because friction works backward here, the flake does not come off rough; it shears away as a pristine, razor-edged geometric plate. The Plumb-Man catches the plate in the crook of its second elbow, tucks it against its flat, eyeless chest, and drops.

To fall, it simply steps out of the seam between the water-blocks and enters a tilted gravity well, sliding down a forty-degree slope of black brine until it crashes into the gravel at the foot of a cairn. 

The impact would shatter an iron anvils, but the Plumb-Man only shudders, shedding a few dead splinters of outer bone that immediately take root in the gravel as new patches of wire-kelp. It drags itself to the dry, one-inch sleeve of calm surrounding the pale limestone tower. 

Here, the Grout-Wife meets it. 

She unfolds from the crevices between the limestone blocks, wrapping the Plumb-Man in a gray, chalky embrace. The mason offers up the fresh-split plate of obsidian, along with a ragged ribbon of a pale parchment-grazer it snagged on the way down. The Wife takes the grazer-meat and packs it into the joints of the Plumb-Man, repairing the cartilage that was burned away by the shearing currents; she takes the obsidian plate and grinds it with her calcified tongues, reducing the volcanic glass to a fine, dry lime-powder that she uses to shore up the base of the tower.

For three or four minutes, the Plumb-Man leans its forehead against the pale stone inside the sleeve of air-thin stillness. Inside that inch of calm, its violet glow fades. Without the battering of the water, it begins to stiffen, its joints locking up, its stone-plates cooling into dead, grey weight. It cannot live in peace; peace turns it back into gravel. 

Before it can petrify completely, the Grout-Wife gives it a sharp, dry shove with a muscular strap, pushing it back across the boundary into the screaming dark.

***

And then there are the corners. 

The Plumb-Men never go into the tight angles where the three mountain peaks pinch the water together, where time stutters and the broken obsidian spires leap back into wholeness between one explosion and the next. The water there is too dense even for their solid bodies; the gravity tilts in six directions at once, folding the plum-colored light into knots.

Yet something is in those corners. It is not an animal, because it has no body that stays in one moment long enough to be measured. The Plumb-Men know it only by the work it ruins.

It might be called the **Un-Quarried**, or the **Stutter-Worm**, though it has no length. 

When a spire of obsidian shatters under the downdraft and then snaps back together an instant later—taller and sharper than before—it does not return empty. In that tiny, skipped heartbeat between the *crack* of the stone breaking and the *thud* of its sudden restoration, something slips inside the fault-lines.

If you look closely at those regrown spires during a flash of amber light, you can see it through the semi-translucent black glass of the edges. It looks like a pale, multi-jointed finger made of dry limestone, or perhaps a length of braided sinew, trapped inside the solid volcanic rock like an insect in amber. 

Every time the water breaks the spire and the stutter in time puts it back together, the thing inside the stone has moved an inch higher. It is swimming *up* through the solid rock, using the moments of shattering to advance, and the moments of restoration to anchor itself. 

When a spire finally grows tall enough to touch the black, pitch-dense ceiling of the basin, it breaks one last time and does not reassemble. Instead, out of the cascading razors of black glass, a single, dry block of pale limestone drops into the turbulence, already carved, already bored through with a neat round hole where a wooden peg is meant to go. 

It tumbles toward the center of the floor, waiting for five or six Plumb-Men to drag themselves out of the seams, lock their granite jaws onto its edges, and haul it through the cannon-fire toward the nearest cairn.
