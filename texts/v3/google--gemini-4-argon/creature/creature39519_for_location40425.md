If you stare through four layers of translucent tenement-wall—past the silhouette of a vine-viper grafting its tail to a lintel, past the rigid, right-angled blurred crosshatch of the glides—you will eventually notice a shadow that does not move at ninety degrees. 

It looks, at first, like a water stain on the dry leaf-skin, or a smudge of grease left by something large that leaned against the apartment block a century ago and has since evaporated. Then the shadow slides three inches to the left, ignoring the root-studs, passing straight through a party wall with the slow, viscous determination of oil soaking through paper.

These are the Tenants. 

To say they *live* here is slightly inaccurate; they are what is left of the concept of tenancy, digested by the banyan-vine and re-secreted as a biological function. They have weight, though very little, and they have volume, though they hate to use it.

A Tenant, caught in the rare open air of a street-gap, is roughly the size and shape of an overcoat dropped from a fifth-story window. They have no bones, only a shifting internal scaffolding of dry, clicking reeds—calcified cartilage that telescopes and collapses like the ribs of a broken umbrella. Their skin is the exact texture and color of damp parchment, layered in loose, flaking folds that flutter constantly in the acoustic hurricane of the nave. Because the noise in the city is a physical pressure, the Tenants do not need muscles to stand. They are inflated and held upright entirely by the roar. The continuous, bone-shaking *thrum-crack-rasp* catches in their loose dermal pleats like wind in a parachute, pinning them against the walls or suspending them in the narrow shafts between buildings.

If the city ever went quiet for three seconds, every Tenant in the nave would collapse into a wet heap of rags and dry sticks at the bottom of the Plaza.

They have no eyes—there is nothing to see in the beige glare except geometry—and they have no ears, because an ear in this place would be ruptured in infancy. Instead, their entire torso is a single, taut tympanum of pale skin stretched over a hollow chest cavity. They feel the city as a shifting architectural chord. They know where the three-days-ago puddle is hovering because the radiator-pipe *clank* of the cellar-mouth frogs hits their chest-drums with a three-day Doppler lag. They know a wall is rotting into moths four blocks away because the local resonant frequency drops from a tight snare-drum rattle to a dull, fluttering *thwack*.

When a Tenant moves, it does not walk. It inserts the thin, horny edge of its lower hem into the grain of the wood and *slides*, riding the vibration of the floorboards the way a penny dances across the top of a booming loudspeaker. 

If you were to peel one off a wall—they feel like warm, dry chamois leather, vibrating furiously under your fingers—and turn it over, you would find its face tucked down where a human sternum should be. The face is not for expression; it is a set of calloused, horn-rimmed valves that open and shut in rapid, fluttering spasms, bleeding off excess acoustic pressure from their hollow interiors so they don’t burst when a glide cracks the sound barrier three feet away.

Their lives are an endless, frantic pantomime of domesticity. 

Because the city is an echo of a metropolis, the Tenants are trapped in the echo of living in one. They do not eat the flat-bugs or the green chitin-crabs; they feed on the lukewarm water that sweats from the capillary roots, and on the fine, microscopic chaff sanded off the wood by the millions of rasping insect legs. This sawdust hangs in the warm, celery-smelling air like a golden haze. 

To catch it, a Tenant will squeeze itself into a third-floor apartment—a space four inches wide and six feet tall—and spread its pleats from wall to wall, becoming a living air-filter. For hours, it hangs there, masquerading as a curtain or a room divider. As the acoustic gale blasts through the translucent walls, the Tenant’s damp skin traps the floating wood-flour and the upward-falling rain. Slowly, a grey paste accumulates in the creases of its body.

When it is heavy with paste, the Tenant performs the only voluntary act it knows: it cleans.

It folds its umbrella-rib limbs inward, rolls itself into a tight, dense cylinder the thickness of a broom handle, and squeezes out onto the balcony. From there, it drops—or rises, depending on the barometric pressure in the spatial seams below—into the avenues. Moving with jerky, stop-motion urgency, it scrubs itself against the satin-smooth wood of the root-highways, wiping the grey paste into the microscopic cracks and checks caused by the drying glare from outside the vault. 

They are the city’s mortar. The paste they smear into the grain hardens in the roar, vibrated into a dense, porcelain-like wood-putty that keeps the load-bearing roots from splitting under their own tension. A Tenant will spend forty minutes furiously polishing a single square inch of a bridge-tendril with its own torso, moving back and forth with the frantic, joyless rhythm of a person scrubbing a stain out of a carpet before the landlord arrives.

They are fiercely, absurdly territorial about spaces they cannot actually occupy. 

In the eastern district, where the breadbox cubicles stack to the vault, a smaller variant of the Tenant—no bigger than a crumpled handkerchief—will wedge itself into the ninety-degree corner of a root-junction. When the green chitin-crabs march past, pausing for the bark-nodule to turn from yellow to bruised purple, the handkerchief-Tenant will violently snap its loose corners at them, like a towel being flicked in a locker room. The crabs never react; they are armored and deaf to anything under a hundred decibels. The Tenant flicks them anyway, defending the intersection until the nodule changes color, at which point the Tenant immediately deflates, slides through a hairline crack into the next cubicle over, and begins defending that one from the exact same line of crabs.

In the Plaza, the largest Tenants—the *Flats*, which have grown as wide as bedsheets and lost their reed-bones entirely—live inside the spatial folds.

If you watch the mirror-polished depression where the legless lizards stretch like taffy across the thick seams of distance, you can see the Flats hanging vertically inside the distortions. A Flat will insert its leading edge into a seam where a Tuesday from last week is rubbing against the current afternoon. Because time moves at different speeds on either side of the seam, the Flat is stretched taut between the two eras, held perfectly rigid like a canvas on a frame. 

They hang there in the middle of the empty Plaza, invisible from the front because they are razor-thin, visible from the side as floating, translucent billboards of pale skin. When a capillary drop falls from the vault two hundred feet above and hits a roof with a pistol-shot *crack*, the Flats catch the shockwave and bend it. By angling their bodies fractionally within the time-seams, they bounce the lethal, wood-splitting frequencies away from the fragile tenement membranes and direct them straight down into the mirror-floor, where the vibration only serves to polish the wood brighter and shake the cellar-mouth frogs into another round of metallic *clanking*.

They do not mate. When a Tenant becomes too old—when its skin is so impregnated with dried wood-putty and calcified sap that it can no longer billow in the noise—it loses its buoyancy. It can no longer ride the *thrum*. 

Slowly, over the course of days, it slides down the translucent walls of the walk-ups, scraping past the five stories of empty rooms with a dry, papery hiss that is completely swallowed by the iron-chain rasp of the bugs. It reaches the avenue, stiffens into the shape of a warped doorframe or a leaning lamppost, and roots its lower hem permanently into the banyan-wood. 

Within an hour, a flock of wall-moths will mistake it for a structural gap and plaster themselves over its stiffened folds. Within a day, a vine-viper will graft a wet tail-tendril to its shoulder to brace a sagging balcony.

And two days later, out of the hollow chest cavity of the new wall-strut, the dry reed-bones will break loose from the dead skin, wrapped in a fresh, damp membrane of inner lining. A half-dozen new Tenants, no larger than postage stamps, will cough themselves out of the old valve-mouth, catch the booming updraft of a passing glide's sonic crack, and go sailing up into the third-floor apartments, already fluttering, already looking for a corner to wipe down.
