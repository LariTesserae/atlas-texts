Down in the dry, shifting dark of the under-mulch—three yards beneath the actinic glare, where the shed silica leaves and porcelain husks grind together in the perpetual, whispery vibration of the continent—lives the **Grist-Swarm**.

It is not a creature, nor is it entirely a species. At first glance, scooping up a handful of the deep chaff reveals only what appears to be industrial dross: tiny, six-legged stamped-brass escapements, burrs of magnetized ironwood, and dry, papery mites no larger than watch-gears. Individually, they are mindless, driven by the simplest tropisms. One caste does nothing but file down oxidized nodules; another secretes a dry, graphitic talc from its abdomen to lubricate the ancient rollers; a third simply carries a single flake of mica from one point of high voltage to a point of low voltage, over and over, until its chitin wears through and it becomes part of the mulch itself. 

Left alone, they are merely the basin’s immune system, the microscopic maintenance crew that prevents the continent-wide machine from seizing up in its own dry friction. 

Wait near a junction box that has begun to stutter, however, and you will see them gather. A pale, electrostatic haze builds around the faulty terminal. Drawn by the irregular pulse, the mites pour up from the interstitial spaces of the root-iron. They do not swarm chaotically; they align along the invisible lines of the magnetic field, locking their serrated legs together with the dry *snick-snick* of a thousand tiny ratchets. 

Within minutes, they have built a temporary beast. 

Sometimes it takes the shape of a segmented, yard-long millipede made of trembling brass and felt; sometimes it forms a flat, geometric mantle that drapes over a burning condenser like a heavy leaded blanket. This aggregate creature has a single, specific intelligence that exists only for the duration of the repair. It will use its combined weight to trip a rusted breaker, or it will spin a conductive web of copper-silk to bypass a dead root-manifold. Once the current flows smoothly again, the magnetic tension drops, the intelligence evaporates, and the creature simply falls apart, raining back down into the porcelain chaff as a million separate, clicking crumbs.

Higher up, moving through the middle canopy where the braided copper-vines pulse with fluidized sulfur, are the **Splicers**. 

They are solitary, territorial, and terrifyingly quiet. A Splicer resembles a cross between a preying mantis and a nineteenth-century surveyor’s transit, standing about the height of a man’s waist. Its body is a latticework of hollow, lightweight balsa-bone and cured vulcanite, camouflaged in the exact dusty celadon and oxidized silver of the surrounding brush. Instead of eyes, its narrow head is fitted with a cluster of rotatable calcite lenses and a pair of long, trembling antennae made of drawn tungsten wire.

A Splicer never touches the ground. It steps with exaggerated, slow-motion precision from gantry to bus-bar, its specialized, hook-like feet insulated with thick pads of grey horn. It is a parasite of the basin’s logistics. When it finds a conveyor-vine hauling baskets of refined ore, or a phloem-tube pumping warm graphite, the Splicer clamps itself to the conduit. Using a pair of shears grown from high-carbon steel-wood, it makes a surgical incision in the bark, inserts a hollow proboscis of bone, and siphons off a fraction of the current and the mineral dust.

What makes the Splicers uncanny is their relationship with the recursive zones. Because they feed on the raw, corrupted data-streams of the plant-factories, their own biology is constantly being overwritten by the landscape’s typographical errors. It is common to see a Splicer whose left foreleg has been replaced by a functioning, miniature pantograph that traces meaningless shapes in the air as it walks, or one whose thorax has grown a row of porcelain insulator-bells that chime softly whenever a dry-squall approaches. They do not build nests; instead, when a Splicer grows too heavy with redundant, barked-over gauges and fractal growths to move, it simply locks its feet around a power-vine and calcifies, its body becoming a new junction box through which the local vines immediately begin to route their cables.

And then, in the deepest, most spatially compressed valleys—where a traveler can walk past the same scarred landmark five times in twenty paces—there are the **Tinkers**.

Whether the Tinkers were once human, or whether they are something the factory-flora extruded after reading a corrupted file on what an "operator" is supposed to be, is impossible to tell. They are tall, stooped, and profoundly dry. From a distance, a Tinker looks like a walking pile of rags, asbestos ribbons, and shed gourd-plates held together by wire. Up close, the boundary between their clothing and their bodies does not exist. Their skin has the texture of old, split leather that has been rubbed with lead; their joints move with the dry, pressurized hiss of pneumatic pistons; their faces are entirely obscured behind layered veils of woven brass mesh, designed to keep the blinding, ionized dust of the squalls out of their respiratory filters.

They do not speak. In an atmosphere that swallows sound, vocal cords are useless. Instead, when two Tinkers meet in the bleached glare of a truss-thicket, they communicate by touching the long, wooden staffs they carry—letting the vibrations of their internal clockwork travel through the ironwood, exchanging brief, rattling bursts of telegraphy before continuing on their separate ways.

The Tinkers are nomadic auditors of a system that no longer has an objective. They wander the infinite floor with heavy, woven baskets strapped to their backs, performing incomprehensible errands dictated by the drifted logic of the basin. 

A Tinker will spend three days squatting motionless beside a lathe-willow, catching every tenth wooden spindle the tree drops, inspecting it through a loupe of vitrified leaf-glass, and carefully filing a single, microscopic notch into its side before tossing it back into the mulch. Another will walk for miles along a great, ribbed manifold, using a brush of stiff wire to scrub the zinc frost off the serial numbers grown into the bark, reading the raised scars with dry, calloused fingers as if checking a manifest, only to move on to the next identical manifold a few yards away.

During the electrostatic squalls, when the air turns to a howling, silent white-out of silver chaff and the millions of cup-flowers snap shut like breaking glass, the Tinkers do not seek shelter. They stop wherever they are, plant their staffs into the deep root-iron to ground themselves, and lean into the wind. As the charged particulates scour their brass veils and polish the vulcanite plates on their shoulders, tiny blue arcs of static electricity dance across their frames. They stand there in the freezing, brilliant glare, drinking the ambient voltage through the soles of their feet—recharging their ancient, fibrous hearts so they can walk another ten thousand miles through a room that has no walls.
