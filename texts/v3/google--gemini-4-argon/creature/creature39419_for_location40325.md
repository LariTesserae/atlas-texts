They are not gone. If you have passed three of their grafted domes and found them empty, it is only because you are moving at the frantic, tearing speed of a creature with red blood and a short memory, and you have walked right past them without recognizing their skins as flesh. 

To find the *Silt-Walkers*—though they have no name for themselves that can be spoken in the dry air—you must stop walking. You must sit upon a root-arch until the water in your boots reaches the temperature of your ankles, and wait for the moss ten paces away to open its eyes.

They are vaguely humanoid only in the way a ginger root or a mandrake is humanoid: a suggestion of two legs, a trunk, two heavy arms, all of it thickened and smoothed as if modeled from wet tallow and left in a warm room. They stand somewhat shorter than a man, though much broader at the hip and foot. Their feet are not shod, nor are they calloused; they are wide, scalloped paddles of pale, fibrous gristle that spread out like the base of a cypress tree when they bear weight, distributing their mass so evenly that they barely depress the bladder-moss when they stand.

Their skin is porous, cool, and entirely hairless, resembling the velvety rind of an old shelf-fungus. In the dim, pewter light, it is the color of drowned ivory, though this is rarely visible beneath the pelage they cultivate. Just as the six-legged grazers carry fern-gardens upon their backs, the people of the Turf wear coats of living bryophytes that are rooted directly into the dead, spongy outer layers of their dermis. A mature individual is a walking hillock of silver-green cushion moss, pearl-wort, and tiny, translucent creeping figs that drink their salty sweat. In the folds of their necks and behind their knees—the warmest places—grow pale, bioluminescent lichens that give them a faint, ghostly outline when they move through the deeper galleries.

They are warm-blooded, yes, because their distant ancestors were, some thirty million years ago when the mountains still had teeth. But the fire in them has been banked down to a pilot light. Their hearts beat perhaps nine times a minute, a heavy, sluggish *thrum-thrum* that can be seen pulsing in the soft hollows beneath their collarbones. Because the air is a soup of spores, warm mist, and aerosolized sugars wept by the giant rosettes, they do not need to breathe often. Their chests rise in a long, shuddering draw once every three or four minutes; their nostrils are wide, downward-facing slits lined with fine, feathery cilia that comb the damp air for drifting pollen and fungal jelly, which rolls down grooves into their mouths while they sleep.

Look at the face of one who has just detached himself from the wall of a root-gallery. It is broad and flat, the nose merely a soft hood over the breathing-slits. The eyes are large, completely black, and covered by a permanent, transparent scale like the spectacle of a snake, so that the constant dribble of condensate and falling spores never makes them blink. They do not blink at all. This gives them an expression of profound, serene stupor. 

They do not travel in tribes, because the Turf cannot support a crowd in one small basin without bruising the moss and fouling the tea-colored water. Instead, they live in *dispersals*—twenty or thirty individuals spread out across fifty square miles of the labyrinth. Though a husband and wife may spend forty years five miles apart, never seeing one another’s faces through the fog-hung arches, they are never out of touch.

This is the purpose of the domes, and the resonators hanging within them. 

The people do not live *in* the domes. To them, the dry, resin-smelling air inside a living hut is stifling, a place where their skin-moss withers if they stay too long. The domes are not houses; they are ears. 

When a person wishes to speak to their dispersal, they do not use their voice—for air-borne sound dies twenty feet away in the wet velvet of the arches. Instead, they seek out one of the tall, six-legged grazers. They approach the beast at its own hydraulic pace, taking two hours to cross the last ten yards. Reaching up with hands that have three thick, opposable thumbs and no fingernails, they gently part the epiphyte ferns on the animal’s flank and press their broad, flat chests against its ribs.

Inside the person’s abdomen is a specialized organ—an evolutionary adaptation of the lower bowel and the swim-bladder of some forgotten ancestor—that acts as a pneumatic drum. Pressing against the grazer, they begin to hum. The frequency is so low it cannot be heard, only felt as a greasy trembling in the sinuses. The grazer, whose own bloodstream is tapped by the ferns the person brings it (for the people carry rare, sweet-sap orchids in their moss-coats to graft onto the beasts as payment), accepts the vibration. The animal amplifies the hum through its hollow, stilt-like leg bones, driving the signal down into the waterlogged mattress of the Great Turf.

Five miles away, in an empty, living dome, a spiral gourd hanging by a braid of gut begins to shiver. An amphibian-bladder drum takes up the sympathetic beat, whispering *uh-uh-uhm* in the dry air of the hut. And sitting thirty yards outside that dome, half-sunken in a warm wallow of peat, another person feels the root-arch behind their shoulder begin to purr. They lean their head back against the wet bark, close their nostril-flaps, and listen through their skull bones to news that took four hours to compose: *The rosette in the third basin has soured; a white mold is fruiting under the moon-gall; I am coming north; I will be there in the autumn.*

Because there is no winter, "autumn" is not a season of the sun, but a season of the Turf itself—a local, forty-year cycle in which a hundred-square-mile patch of the plateau grows senescent, its sap cooling by three degrees, its wood calcifying, before fresh stolons from the surrounding country invade and digest it anew. 

This is why the dwellings are abandoned. The people are nomads in slow motion. They are always fleeing the slow petrification of the old wood, drifting toward the warmer, younger pastures of the mat at the speed of a growing vine.

A lifetime among them is very long—perhaps three hundred changes of the moon-galls, though they do not count—and almost entirely devoid of event. Their technology is entirely biological and excruciatingly patient. A young person (who looks like an elder, save that their skin-moss is a brighter, chartreuse green and has not yet grown lichens) will carry a pouch of parasitic wasps at their hip, tucked into a fold of their own flesh. When they find a young root-arch looping out of the mire, they will sit beside it for a month, pricking the bark with a long, calcified spine taken from a dead water-creature, introducing the wasp eggs one by one, and binding the wound with strips of their own shed skin to guide the swelling. Twenty years later, when they return along that gallery, the burl has grown into the shape they intended: not just a pointer for the way, but a shallow basin to catch the sweetest condensate, or a hollow resonant chamber that whistles a single, mournful note when the barometric pressure drops.

They do not hunt. To kill one of the bread-loaf amphibians in its leaf-cistern would ruin the water of that cup for a decade; the rot would turn the rosette black. Instead, they are gatherers of superfluity. They drink the bog-milk alongside the grazers, sucking it up through hollow reeds. They harvest the sterile, gelatinous eggs that the blind amphibians lay specifically to feed their own young, taking only one in ten. They scrape the iridescent slime of the slate-colored slugs into small, folded leaf-packets, where it ferments over weeks into a warm, peppery cheese that smells of mushrooms and iron. And when the colonial "bags" in the canopy drop their spent, fibrous umbilicals, the people chew the tough cordage for days until it softens into a sweet, starchy pulp.

Perhaps the strangest thing to an outsider is what happens when they meet. 

Because two people might only cross paths once in ten years, a meeting is not a casual affair, nor is it loud. When two of them perceive one another—usually by the slight displacement of water in the turf long before they see each other through the pewter mist—they stop. They do not wave or call out. Over the course of an afternoon, they drift together until they are standing toe-to-toe on a wide root-bridge.

Then, they lean into one another, pressing forehead to forehead, belly to belly, folding their heavy arms around each other’s backs, and they stop moving entirely. 

For three days, or five, or a week, they remain there like a single, two-headed stump in the drizzle. During this time, the mosses and creeping figs upon their skins interweave. The pale, blind rootlets of the lichens cross from one shoulder to the other, exchanging chemical signals, hormones, and the microscopic mites that live in their pelage. Through the skin itself, which becomes thin and flushed with blood during these embraces, they trade the slow, chemical memories of where they have been—the mineral taste of the water fifty miles south, the antibodies for a new spore-blight, the subtle, hormonal contentment of a well-fed decade. They breathe in unison, once every four minutes, their abdominal drums humming a single, chorded note that makes the silver moss around their ankles tremble and shed its water in a steady, silver ring.

When they finally peel apart, with a soft, Velcro-like tearing of tiny root-hairs, each carries a patch of the other’s garden upon their chest. They do not look back as they sink again into opposite galleries.

And when they die, they do not leave bones for long. An old one, feeling the pilot light in their chest begin to gutter and their skin turning woody and stiff with mineral deposits from the water they have drunk for three centuries, will walk into one of the older domes—the ones whose walls are already turning to mahogany amber. They will unhook the gourd-resonators and let them fall into the peat. Then they will wedge themselves into a cleft between two of the great, living roof-ribs. 

Within a week, the tree-arch has recognized the stillness of the meat. Thin, pale feeder-roots, no thicker than sewing thread, emerge from the bark and slip gently into the breathing-slits and behind the unblinking spectacles of the eyes. There is no pain in it; the plant secretes a heavy, narcotic sap that smells of bruised cinnamon and puts the tiny, slow brain to sleep long before the heart has finished its last ten beats. By the time the next traveler passes the doorway ten years later, there is no corpse at all—only a gentle, human-shaped swelling in the wall of the hut, covered in velvet liverwort, still warm to the touch with the slow, borrowed fever of the living wood.
