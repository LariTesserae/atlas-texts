To find what lives here—truly lives here, with hands and tools and a memory of the seasons—you have to look past the camelids and the gulls, and you have to realize that the pumping stations are not unmanned after all. 

Down in the sunlit valleys at the foot of the continent, the power company’s ledgers list the High Basin infrastructure as fully automated. This is a convenient fiction. No automated system built of iron, brass, and rubber could survive three months in the Basin on its own. Left unattended, the intake grates would choke solid with twenty-foot *Durvillaea* fronds in forty-eight hours; the bleed-valves would seize with anchor-ice; the porcelain insulators would short out under crusts of wind-driven rime and gull guano. 

The power keeps flowing south because of the Valve-Wrights, though they rarely use that name for themselves. In their own thick, consonant-heavy dialect—a language spoken almost entirely through the nose and the back of the throat, so they don’t have to open their mouths to the freezing sleet—they call themselves the *K’allpa-runa*, or simply the Greasers.

At first glance, through the horizontal silver curtain of hail, a Greaser looks like just another boulder of dark gneiss or a pile of washed-up peat. They are short, astonishingly broad-shouldered people, shaped by generations of high-altitude hypoxia into human barrels. Their chests are deep and keeled like the breastbones of diving ducks, housing lungs of immense capacity and hearts that pump blood as thick and dark as molasses. Their skin, where it shows around their cheekbones and knuckles, is the color of tarnished copper, webbed with thousands of burst capillaries from the biting cold and the relentless ultraviolet glare.

They do not wear woven cloth, which the wind would shred and the water would turn to leaden ice. Instead, they dress in a cured, layered composite of the basin itself. Their inner garments are felted from the oily under-wool of the water-camelids, smelled perpetually of wet lanolin and rancid tallow. Over this, they wear heavy, overlapping aprons and cowls made from the cured, vulcanized bladders of giant kelp, stitched together with copper wire stripped from derelict generators. They grease these kelp-skins constantly with a black, waterproof paste rendered from mud-fish livers and petroleum sludge skimmed from the turbine sumps. 

To move across the Basin, they do not use boats with deep hulls, which would instantly foul in the dense underwater forests or capsize over the shallow whaleback flats. Instead, they travel on *balsas*—long, flexible rafts bound together from bundles of dried, buoyant bog-reeds and hollow iron pipes scavenged from old construction sites. These rafts do not so much float *on* the water as slide *through* the heavy, weed-thickened surge, bending like caterpillars over the rollers. A Greaser stands wide-legged on the awash deck, barefoot inside boots of greased camelid-hock, wielding a twelve-foot iron-shod pole to punt over the shallow gneiss domes and lever themselves through the tangled mats of surface wrack.

To survive the cold and the thin air, their lives are anchored to the tiny, scattered islands of industrial heat. 

Step inside one of the riveted iron shacks—ducking through a heavy, submarine-style hatch that must be dogged shut against the gale—and the assault of the wind is instantly replaced by a deafening, rhythmic *thump-hiss-clank* and a wall of tropical, suffocating heat. The air inside is ninety degrees, thick with the smell of ozone, boiled cabbage, wet wool, and hot whale-oil. 

This is a family’s hearth. 

There is no floor, only narrow catwalks of perforated iron grating suspended over the black, surging water of the intake well. Above the grating, the massive brass-fitted pistons hammer back and forth, and the twelve-foot cast-iron flywheel spins in a blur, throwing off tiny droplets of hot oil. The Greasers live in the margins of the machine. They sling their hammocks of braided wire-rope and kelp-twine directly over the vibrating turbine housings, sleeping like babies in a 110-decibel roar that would drive a lowlander mad in an hour. When the machinery runs smooth, they sleep; if the rhythm of the pistons falters by even a fraction of a second—a sign of a clogged intake or a slipping governor—every person in the shack wakes instantly before the alarm bell can even ring.

They cook on the machinery. Flat slabs of salted camelid meat and tough cakes of dried bog-grass seed are laid directly onto the blistering iron manifolds of the steam-bleeders. Pots of dark, violent-tasting tea—brewed from *llareta* resin, coca leaves brought up from the lowlands a decade ago, and scraped rust for iron—simmer perpetually in wire baskets lowered into the cooling jackets of the cylinders. 

A single extended family, usually five or six souls, claims a territory of three or four shacks, spaced miles apart along a chain of pylons. They are fierce, territorial custodians. The children learn to swim in the freezing, weed-choked trenches before they can walk properly, tethered to the iron catwalks by lengths of stout hemp so the undercurrents don't take them. By age seven, they have the specialized eyesight of their parents: protected from the blinding pewter glare by slit-goggles carved from dark horn or flattened soup cans, they can look down into the thrashing brown water and tell the difference between a drifting log of rotting pine, a hunting river-wolf, and a loose rivet vibrating on a submerged turbine casing.

Their days are a frantic, high-calorie ballet of maintenance and foraging. In the High Basin, you eat constantly or you die of hypothermia. While two adults are submerged to their waists on the outside of the shack, hanging from safety harnesses of rusted chain to hack away five-hundred-pound tangles of *Durvillaea* kelp from the intake grates with heavy, crescent-bladed billhooks, an adolescent is fifty feet up a swaying pylon in the sleet. 

They climb the skeletal iron towers with monkey-like agility, using wooden hooks to slide along the maintenance cables, utterly indifferent to the sixty-mile-an-hour gusts and the sixty-thousand-volt hum that makes their greased hair stand on end. Up in the fog, they chip the ice off the porcelain insulators with brass hammers and rob the nests of the mountain gulls that build right in the high-voltage cradles, dropping the warm, speckled eggs into leather pouches slung across their chests.

Because the wind steals the human voice ten feet from the lips, the Greasers rarely speak when outdoors. Instead, they communicate across the miles of churning silver water through the infrastructure itself. A Greaser standing on a flooded stone flat will take a heavy steel wrench and strike a rhythmic code against the leg of a pylon. The sound travels through the guy-wires and the structural iron, bypassing the howling gale entirely, ringing clearly inside the warm, humming shacks three miles down the line. *Three strikes, pause, two strikes: The herd is crossing at Sector Four. Four rapid strikes: The main valve at Shack Nine is seizing; bring the heavy block-and-tackle.*

They are a strange mixture of Stone Age animism and heavy-industrial pragmatism. They know nothing of the distant cities their turbines illuminate; to them, the electricity is simply the *Achik*—the fast, angry spirit of the mountain water, squeezed out of the black trenches by the heavy iron wheels and sent racing down the copper wires so it won't burst the rocks apart. They treat the machines like massive, temperamental beasts of burden that must be fed grease, bled of pressure, and sung to. When a brass bushing wears down and cannot be replaced, they cast a new one themselves over a forge fired with dried peat and camelid dung, melting down spent brass cartridge cases, old buckles, and the ceremonial bells of their ancestors. They pack the bearings with river-clay and lard, and bind cracked pressure-pipes with wrappings of raw tendon and wet rawhide that shrink as they dry around the hot iron, holding tighter than any steel clamp.

At twilight—when the pewter sky bruises into a deep, terrifying indigo and the wind drops five degrees in five minutes—they retreat from the flats. They haul their reed-and-pipe rafts up onto the rusted catwalks, dog the heavy iron hatches shut, and check the pressure gauges by the blue-white spark of the generator brushes. 

While the storm tears the clouds to pieces outside and the black water surges beneath their grated floors, they sit with their backs pressed against the warm, shuddering cast-iron belly of the turbine. They chew dried mud-fish smeared with ash, mend their copper-stitched kelp coats, and listen to the long, vibrating hum of the pylons—the great harp of the Basin, singing in the dark.
