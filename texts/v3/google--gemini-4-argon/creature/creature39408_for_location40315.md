To find a Vintner in the hours between the static-storms, you do not look for movement; you look for a knot in the wood that was not there yesterday. 

They are a tall, spare people, though "people" is a loose geometry for what they have become over ten thousand generations of drying out. If they ever possessed the soft, water-bag bodies of their off-world ancestors, the High Bough has long since cured them like cellar-beams. A Vintner’s flesh is a dense, fibrous bundle of dry mylar-like tendons and amber-saturated muscle, wrapped in a hide that has the matte, slightly powdery texture of grey hornbeam or winter-cured salami. When they move—which they do sparingly, in sudden, precise bursts that resemble the snapping of a dry twig—their joints do not pop; they *creak*, a dry, resinous friction that is entirely comfortable to them.

They have no lungs. In an atmosphere as thin and desiccated as this, a wet lung would freeze and flash-dry in three breaths. Instead, the wide, barrel-like cages of their chests are open to the wind through vertical slats beneath their collarbones. Inside hang the *baffle-books*—hundreds of stiff, parchment-thin pleats of black, electro-receptive membrane that flutter like pages in the constant draft. They breathe the Spore-Static and the radiation of the Tension-Looms directly, their metabolism running on a slow, flameless combustion of turpentine-sugars and captured kilovolts. 

Because the air is too thin to carry the delicate modulations of a voice over the roar of the wind-harvesters, the Vintners have no mouths for speaking and no ears on the sides of their heads. Their faces are smooth, prow-like shields of pale, ossified cartilage, interrupted only by three vertical slits that house heavy, solid-amber lenses—eyes built to see into the ultraviolet and to track the faint, Cherenkov-blue ghosts of the root-currents beneath the floor.

To speak, a Vintner drops to one knee and presses the wide, flat heel of their hand—or the broadened, hoof-like sole of their foot—against the ground.

They are creatures of the sounding board. Within their forearms and calves lie heavy, calcified hammers suspended in slings of high-tension ligament. By striking the floor with microscopic variations in force and cadence, they send complex, phase-shifted shear-waves shooting through the hyper-dense grain of the wood. A conversation between two Vintners across a half-mile plaza looks, to an outsider, like two motionless statues staring past one another in the violet gloom; yet beneath their feet, the anthracite-hard lignum is alive with a rapid, syncopated chatter of knocks, hums, and thrumming bass-notes that travel through the soles of their boots and directly up their rigid shin-bones to the acoustic clusters in their hips. 

This is why they call themselves the Vintners, though they have never seen a grape. Their work is down in the dark.

For miles beneath the Cherenkov-lit avenues, the planetary wood is not dead; it is merely comatose, hoarding vast, pressurized lakes of ancient, primeval sap—black hydrocarbons, heavy ketonic oils, and volatile camphors brewed over geological eras in the anaerobic crush of the deep roots. Left alone, these reservoirs either polymerize into useless, brittle glass or build up such volatile pressure from subterranean friction that they detonate, blowing half-mile craters through the surface plain in geysers of black tar and timber-shrapnel.

The Vintners tend the barrels of the world. 

They do not use metal tools, which grow brittle and shatter in the stratospheric cold and the relentless acoustic vibration. Instead, they are masters of the *graft* and the *gall*. A Vintner’s narrow waist is hung with dozens of small, stoppered vials carved from mineralized burl, each containing a different strain of tailored retroviral resin or dormant, engineered wasp-larvae. 

When a Vintner senses a souring in the deep strata—reading the change in viscosity by the way the planetary cello-groans bend and flatten as they pass through a pocket of sap three miles down—they go out into the avenues with a *Tap-Spindle*. These are twelve-foot needles of living, metallic ironwood that the Vintners carry across their shoulders like pikes. They find the exact intersection of grain above the reservoir, anoint the tip of the spindle with a specific viral paste from their belt, and drive it half an inch into the violet ground.

They do not drill; the wood does the work for them. Infected by the virus, the ancient cambium wakes up in a localized fever, growing downward in a tight, hollow spiral at terrifying speed, burrowing through its own petrified heartwood toward the deep pocket below. The Vintner stands over the spindle for days, sometimes weeks, completely still as the Spore-Static storms whip past them, their chest-baffles glowing with small, blue St. Elmo’s fires as they feed on the gale. Through their feet, they listen to the drill-root descend, steering its growth by tapping corrective rhythms into the ground—*left, harden, narrow, turn*—until, miles down, the root pierces the pressurized bladder of ancient oil.

What rises to the surface days later is not raw sap, which would boil away or catch fire in the ozone-heavy air, but *Vintage*: a thick, clarified, amber-violet liquor that has been filtered, fractionated, and chemically stabilized by the living walls of the tap-root on its way up.

In the hollow, cantilevered towers of the city, there is no furniture as soft bodies understand it. The interiors are dark, resonant chambers that smell overwhelmingly of turpentine, old violins, and lightning. The walls are grown into thousands of hexagonal alcoves—the racks—where the Vintners store spheres of hardened resin containing the different Vintages. Some are three-hundred-year-old "light" spirits, pale gold and crackling with trapped static, which a Vintner will absorb through the porous pads of their palms to give themselves the manic, jittering speed needed to outrun a pack of wild Stridulators. Others are black, heavy tars drawn from the very bottom of the tectonic root-balls—liquids so dense with ancient chemical memory that when a Vintner smears a drop across their chest-baffles, they fall into a rigid, standing trance that lasts for months, their minds sinking down through the xylem to dream the slow, continent-spanning thoughts of the tree itself.

As a Vintner ages, the boundary between the citizen and the municipality begins to blur. 

Because their diet consists entirely of the tree’s deepest hydrocarbons and their bodies are constantly repairing themselves with the same viral resins used to heal the wind-chipped towers, they slowly lignify. A Vintner of two hundred years can no longer bend their left knee; the joint has fused into a smooth, bulbous burl of violet-grey heartwood. By three hundred, their stride has shortened to a stiff, shuffling glide, and their skin has taken on the silver, mineralized sheen of the ancient root-plates. 

They do not consider this a sickness. It is simply the settling of the lees.

When a Vintner’s internal hammers finally seizing in their sheaths of bone, and their chest-baffles grow too stiff with hardened amber to flutter in the dry wind, they do not lie down. They walk out during the height of a Spore-Static storm, usually to a high ridge overlooking the twenty-mile basin, or to a spot along the avenues where the acoustic walls need a new baffle to tune the wind’s roar. 

They plant their broad, flat feet into the glowing blue grain of a Tension-Loom’s beam. They unstopper every vial on their belt, pouring the tailored retroviruses down their own flanks, and then they lock their joints. 

Within a week, the pale, creeping callus of the ground has risen over their ankles, fusing their heels to the planetary floor. Within a year, their hollow ribs have grown together into a new resonance chamber, catching the frigid stratospheric gale and adding a new, slightly higher note to the city’s endless, polyphonic whistle. The younger Vintners, skating past on the slick, violet avenues with their bundles of spindles, will sometimes pause beside these gnarled, vaguely humanoid pillars. They will press a dry, grey palm against the wood of their ancestor’s shoulder, tap a brief, complicated question into the grain, and wait for the wind to shift—listening through their bones for the slow, creaking groan of the reply.
