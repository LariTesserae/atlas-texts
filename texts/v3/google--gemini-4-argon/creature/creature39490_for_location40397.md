If you sit beside one of the unmortared cairns for three or four days, breathing as shallowly as the warm air requires, what you took for a buttress of pale orthoclase beside the doorway will eventually unhook its heel from a mineral shelf and shift its weight.

They are called the *cliners*—if a name can be given to things that do not speak with throats—and they are built for the three-dimensional geometry of the thicket the way a spider is built for a corner, though they are entirely vertebrate. Unfolded, a cliner is roughly the mass of a small, starved human, though its proportions belong to a different gravity. Because there is no floor in the Spicule Forest, only angles of stone between thirty and ninety degrees, they have no true posture for standing upright. Their hips and shoulders are interchangeable ball-and-socket joints of immense, slow leverage, and their feet are identical to their hands: four long, flat-boned digits opposed by two thumbs, one on either side of the palm, allowing them to lock around a hexagonal basalt column or pinch the razor-edge of an obsidian spline without slipping.

In the brief, bluish-amber flash of a stress-slip, a cliner looks like a dried preparation from an anatomist’s cabinet. They have no hair, no sweat glands, and no pigment. Their skin is thick, translucent, and cured by their own body chemistry into something resembling raw parchment or horn, yellow-white and stiff over the joints, where it folds like the bellows of an concertina. Beneath that dry rind, there is no soft fat—only cables of dark, waxy muscle and flat, broad bones that have been mineralized with iron and silica from their diet until their skeletons are nearly as dense as the rock they climb.

Their heads are flat and wide, set low between the collarbones on a short, heavily muscled neck that rarely turns. They have no eyes—not even the sunken, skin-covered sockets of cave fish—because their ancestors lost the memory of light ten million years ago. Instead, the brow ridge has broadened into a hollow, vaulted acoustic pan of thin bone covered in taut, drum-head skin. Just below this resonance-brow sit two wide, crescent-shaped nasal slits bordered by stiff, fleshy fronds that twitch once a minute to sample the stagnant air for the cold-grease smell of a weaver or the sour, metallic tang of a living mantle.

To survive in a world without water, a cliner is a sealed vessel. Every breath is an expensive transaction. They do not inhale and exhale with the frantic, bellows-like rhythm of surface mammals; instead, their ribs expand over the course of three minutes, drawing the warm, ozone-stale air through a labyrinth of bone-scrolls in their wide snouts. On the way out, those scrolls reclaim every molecule of moisture before the air is released through a tiny valve at the base of the throat with a dry, barely audible *click*. Their kidneys do not produce liquid; once a week, a cliner will pause on a ledge and extrude a small, chalky pellet of pure, dry uric salt and excess iron, which falls straight down into the abyss, clicking off the needles for miles.

They live at one-tenth the speed of the upper world. To move fast is to generate heat; to generate heat is to sweat or pant; to pant is to die of thirst in an afternoon. 

So a cliner moves like the hour hand of a clock. You do not see them travel. You see them in a piezoelectric flash—a pale, four-handed figure bridged between two tourmaline needles, one long leg drawn up under its chin, a heavy stone maul slung across its back in a harness of dried gut—and when the next flash comes eight minutes later, they are twelve feet lower down the spire, their fingers locked into a new set of cleavage planes, their flat heads pressed directly against the rock.

That posture—cheekbone and jawbone wedged tight against the crystal—is how they see. 

The Spicule Forest is a solid tuning fork. When a cliner strikes one of the suspended lithophones with a bone mallet, it does not listen with its ears. It clamps its wide, calloused heels and its hollow lower jaw to the stone needle it is perched upon and waits for the return wave. The vibration travels out through the intersections of the lattice, reflecting off the density changes of basalt pillars, dead-end chasms, and the hollow spheres of other clearings, returning through the rock as a complex, silent chord of resistance. A cliner can feel the shadow of a three-foot mantle grazing forty yards away by the way its iron shell dampens the high-frequency shimmer of a quartz needle. They know the shape of their world for half a mile in every direction as a three-dimensional map of tensions, weights, and grain.

Their lives are spent tending the tension of the forest.

Because the rock is under immense tectonic load, the lattice is always slowly creeping toward collapse. A cliner family—usually three or four individuals who may spend decades within the same two-mile cube of stone—acts as both quarryman and watchmaker. When they feel a dangerous buildup of stress in a massive feldspar strut, a stress that might shatter their clearing and send their dry-stone huts tumbling ten miles down into the hot, crushing dark of the lower sponge, they gather with their tools.

They do not chip the stone with frantic blows. They climb to the fault line, wedge a chisel of meteoric iron into a microscopic hair-crack, and then two of them will hang their combined weight from a long lever of lashed selenite, waiting. They will hang there in the dark, motionless as cured hams, for six or seven hours, applying a steady, pitiless three hundred pounds of torque until the crystal lattice gives up, splits along its plane with a single, blinding flash of blue light and a sound like a pistol shot, and drops the offending ton of rock cleanly through the gaps below.

In the cairns, their domesticity is entirely silent and greasy.

At the center of every stone cage sits the *tallow-pot*—usually the inverted, iron-plated shell of an ancient mantle, three feet wide, filled with a dense, gray paste rendered from the bodies of gliders and the waxy abdominal sacs of weavers, mixed with powdered gypsum and scraped bacterial crusts. This is food, water, mortar, and medicine. 

When two cliners meet inside a hut, they do not speak. Speech uses breath. Instead, they reach out with their double-thumbed hands and take hold of each other’s wrists and collarbones. By flexing their tendons in minute, rhythmic clicks and scraping their heavy, horn-like fingernails across the ridges of each other’s forearm bones, they converse through bone-conduction. It is a dry, intimate language of pressures and vibrations—a conversation that sounds, to an outsider sitting three feet away, like nothing more than two pieces of dry driftwood shifting slightly in a pile.

While they talk, they groom one another with the gray tallow, rubbing the heavy grease into the concertina-folds of their elbows, around the sealing-flaps of their nostrils, and over the broad acoustic drums of their foreheads to keep the skin from cracking in the desiccated air. An ungreased cliner begins to split at the knuckles within a week; an exiled cliner, driven from the tallow-pots for some transgression of weight or carelessness with a glass web, simply stiffens on a ledge somewhere in the dark, its moisture leaking out through its cracked hide until it dries into a pale, leathery mummy that will remain wedged in the crystal crook for a hundred thousand years, indistinguishable from the living until you touch it and find it cold.

Indeed, the dead are the principal furniture of their clearings.

Because nothing rots here, and because a body is a valuable collection of calcium, iron, and cured hide, the cliners do not bury or drop their dead. When an old cliner’s joints finally calcify so thoroughly that it can no longer unhook its heels from the stone, its kin bring it into the clearing, render its remaining internal fats into the tallow-pot, and then position the dry, rigid corpse at a structural intersection of the hut or bridge, binding its four gripping limbs around the meeting-point of the stone needles with wet tendon that dries as hard as steel. 

The huts are half-built of ancestors. You climb a bridge of selenite and your foot rests on the hollowed, iron-hard ribcage of a grandmother who died when the glaciers were still on the surface; you lean against a doorframe and your shoulder fits neatly into the curved, leathery arch of a builder whose four hands are still locked around the lintel-stone in a grip that death only tightened.

Sometimes, in the long intervals between the harvesting of mantles and the relief of tectonic faults, a single cliner will crawl out onto a solitary needle projecting into a particularly wide void. It will wedge its lower thumbs into a crack, lock its knees, and draw from a pouch at its groin one of the small, asymmetrical knots of carved gypsum.

For ten, twenty, thirty hours, the creature will sit in the warm, absolute dark, turning the stone knot over and over between its four thumbs. The gypsum is carved with microscopic ridges, spirals, and fractures—a tactile poem, or a genealogical record of every slip and flash in that sector of the lattice over five hundred generations, or perhaps an equation of pure three-dimensional geometry. As the cliner reads the stone with its calluses, its jaw rests lightly against the basalt needle beneath it, humming a single, sub-audible note through its teeth—not loud enough to stir the air, just a faint, rhythmic tremor sent down into the roots of the crystal, testing the dark, feeling the vast, dry sponge of the world hold together for one more day.
