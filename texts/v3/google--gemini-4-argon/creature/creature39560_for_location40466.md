At first, you would mistake it for another tier of the travertine—a pale, tallow-colored blister wedged into the cleft between two weeping pillars, gleaming with the same greasy pewter light as the stone around it. Only if you watched for a very long time, through dozens of the slow, resonant *plocks* of falling drops, would you see the limestone seem to inhale. A ribcage expands, shifting the glossy film of water across its surface, and then settles back into stillness.

There is only one of him, or perhaps two or three scattered across the labyrinth, though they never meet and have no need to. He is not the descendant of the people who pecked the cup-shaped steps into the limestone or hammered the green-black copper blade. Rather, he is what happened when something that resembled them wandered down into the warm, heavy dark ages ago and simply failed to leave, yielding entirely to the suspension of the basin. 

He is roughly the shape of a man, though his proportions have been softened and redistributed by an eternity of squeezing sideways through the wet stone. His shoulders are narrow and sloped, his hips thin, his joints hyper-flexible and swollen like water-logged roots. Every sharp angle of his anatomy has been blunted, mirroring the rock. His skin is thick, hairless, and entirely devoid of pigment, possessing the dense, waxy translucence of mutton fat or wet chalk. Where he presses against the dark shale, the bruised purple-grey of the rock shows faintly through the flesh of his forearms and thighs. 

Because the air is the exact temperature of a body, he does not need to generate heat. Because he does not generate heat, he barely needs to eat. His metabolism has slowed to a geological crawl. His heart beats once every two or three minutes—a slow, heavy thud that sends a faint, sympathetic tremor into the pool he sits within, barely enough to make the rust-red mats of iron bacteria shiver. 

His eyes are still there, set shallowly in a smooth, browless face, but they are filmed over with a milky, calcified cataract that matches the sourceless mist. Sight is useless in a world with no shadows and no distance. Instead, he navigates the packed, claustrophobic maze by touch and by the meticulous mapping of sound. His ears are wide, flat, and slightly cupped, pressed close to his skull. When a heavy drop falls from an overhang—*plock*—he does not just hear the note of it; he feels the microscopic displacement of the thick air against his cheek and the minute ripple that travels through the waist-deep pool against his ribs. By the timbre of the splash, he knows which basin received it, how deep the water stands behind its ancient slate weir, and whether the flatworms have gathered on its lip.

When he moves, it is with the viscous, unhurried grace of a slug. He never lifts his weight entirely off the stone; he slides it. His palms and the soles of his feet are broad and finely ridged, secreting a thick, alkaline mucus that mixes with the mineral water, allowing him to grip the slick, bulbous curves of the travertine without slipping. He shifts a shoulder through a gap, rests his chin on a sweating pillar for ten minutes to let his blood settle, and then draws a knee up onto a submerged ledge. There is no friction in his life. Skin and stone are the same temperature, the same wetness, the same texture. He is never cold, never dry, never bruised.

He is not entirely an animal, though his thoughts are as slow and mineral-heavy as the water. He has inherited the relics of the basin without understanding their history, repurposing them into a quiet, obsessive maintenance of his equilibrium. He knows the pecked steps in the limestone mound not as a path to the surface—the narrow strip of sky far above is just a band of slightly brighter grease he never looks toward—but as small, convenient basins where the water collects with a particularly sweet, chalky taste. 

Sometimes, over the course of a week, he will make his way to the hollowed-out granite trough. He will reach into the black water and lift the hammered copper blade. He does not use it to cut; there is nothing to cut. Instead, he brings the heavy, green-black metal up to his chest and presses its broad flat against his sternum, or holds it to his tongue. The sharp, metallic tang of the copper sulfate cuts through the eternal taste of wet slate and iron in his mouth. It is the closest thing his nerves experience to a bright light or a loud noise—a brief, electric spark of flavor in the endless, tepid grey. After an hour, he lays it back down in the trough, precisely in the indentation its own weight has worn into the calcite.

To sustain his glacially slow body, he grazes, much like the translucent flatworms. He will submerge his face to the ears in the deepest, most stagnant pools and gently suck the velvety mats of rust-red iron bacteria from the stone, filtering the rich, metallic slime through teeth that have fused into two smooth, continuous plates of yellow bone. Occasionally, if he passes the leaning slate slab and feels the minute tickle of the blind cave-cricket’s long antennae brushing his shoulder, his tongue will unspool—surprisingly long, pale, and sticky—and draw the insect in. He will hold it in his mouth for hours, letting it dissolve rather than chewing it.

Most of the time, however, he does nothing at all. He sits wedged in a flooded niche near the fused, beehive-shaped cairn, submerged to the collarbones, his head leaning back against a swollen blister of flowstone. Because he remains still for days at a stretch, the basin tries to claim him just as it claimed the cobbles and the copper. The water condensing out of the heavy air slides down the rock, crosses onto his scalp and shoulders, and leaves behind its microscopic trace of lime. 

Over the years, thin, crusted rills of white calcite have begun to form in the creases of his neck, along the curve of his spine, and over the knuckles of his resting hands. Every few days, when he finally shifts his weight to reach for a mouthful of red silt, these delicate mineral skins crack with a sound like a snapping hair, flaking off to drift down into the dark water. 

One day, perhaps centuries from now, the pause between his heartbeats will simply grow too long, and he will forget to shift his shoulder. The thin crust will not crack. The water will slide over him, depositing layer after layer of tallow-white stone over his closed eyes and his sloped shoulders, until he is sealed seamlessly into the alcove, just another rounded, bulbous contour in the rock, gleaming with a dull, greasy luster in the pewter light.
