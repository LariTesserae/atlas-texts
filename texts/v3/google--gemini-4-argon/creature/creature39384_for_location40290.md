To find the citizens of Oakhaven-in-the-Crag, you must first learn to distinguish them from the wet masonry and the rock-fowl. They do not scurry like the people of low-lying, finished cities; they move with the heavy, deliberate cadence of the four-second counterweights. 

At first glance, a shift-worker waiting at a cable-car terminus looks like a cairn of greased felt and slate. Because the air is a freezing soup of cylinder-bleed and unrendered stone-dust, the Oakhavener dresses in seven or eight layers of heavily lanolized wool, the outermost of which is a stiff, bell-shaped mantle rubbed with tallow and graphite until it shines like wet cast-iron. These coats are never washed; over decades, their broad shoulder-pleats accumulate a microscopic silt of hornblende and coal-soot, giving birth to their own pale, hair-thin lichens. When a crowd of foundry-men stands together on a stone shelf, waiting for the tram-cage to drop out of the pewter mist, they smell of mutton fat, cold brass, and the sharp, sour tang of the rock-eating ferns.

Beneath their wide-brimmed, guttered hats, they are a broad-chested, barrel-ribbed people, built by three generations of thin air to house enormous, slow-beating lungs. Their skin has the pale, slightly translucent cast of tallow candles, webbed across the high cheekbones with tiny, purpled capillaries. Their eyes are dark and peculiarly still, adapted to the low, shadowless light of the well-bottom sun. They blink very rarely—perhaps twice a minute—and when they speak, their voices are pitched to a flat, nasal baritone that cuts through the five-fold echo of the valley without triggering a sixth.

For in Oakhaven, one does not shout. To shout against the raw, unweathered obsidian of the cliffs is to invite your own voice back to you from four directions at once, sharpened by the crystalline fractures until the syllables overlap and curdle into a physical pressure that can crack a windowpane. Instead, the inhabitants communicate across the wide chasms using *clack-boards*—hinged paddles of seasoned ironwood worn at the belt. Walking the high girders, a structural surveyor will rap out a dry, staccato rhythm that sounds exactly like a goat’s hoof striking basalt, a code that slips neatly between the *chuff-thump* of the municipal pumps without waking the acoustics of the bowl.

They are a profoundly literal, unhurried people, largely because they are not entirely finished themselves.

Just as the western rim of the mountain is still dropping into density, the citizenry of the Crag suffers from the thinness of the local rendering. It is a common, unremarked-upon condition. You will see an old woman sitting on an iron bollard outside the textile looms, shelling tough, pale-green fern-nuts into her lap; if she holds her left hand still for too long, the knuckles begin to lose their edge, blurring into a grey, stippled fog of potential until she briskly slaps her wrist against the iron to knock the bone back into focus. 

To stay solid in Oakhaven requires weight, friction, and routine. This is why their boots are so monstrous—soled with two inches of cast pig-iron and studded with soft brass hobnails that scream and spark against the glassy basalt plazas. They wear heavy brass chains sewn into the hems of their skirts and coats, and carry pocket-weights of dense, stamped lead just to keep their hips anchored to the local gravity. A person who becomes too idle, or too contemplative, runs the risk of *thinning*. Their outline begins to hum with that low, glass-rubbing vibration; their shadow detaches at the heels and points toward the nearest furnace; their thoughts become as slow and pale as the sap in the giant rosettes.

To prevent this, every home and tenement—those that have not slipped out of the local clock—is kept rigorously, aggressively tactile. The interiors are narrow, overheated warrens lined with dark brick and smelling of boiled cabbage, wet goat-hair, and sperm-oil lamps. Nothing is smooth. The furniture is carved from petrified bog-oak imported on the phantom railways or cast from rough, unfiled iron; the wallpapers are embossed with coarse sand and crushed slag so that a resident, passing down a hallway in the dark, can drag their knuckles along the wall and remind their skin where it stops. 

They do not keep pets, yet their lives are entirely entangled with the valley’s beasts, which they treat with a gruff, unsentimental kinship. The great, sheep-sized rock-fowls are never penned; they wander freely through the ground-floor kitchens of the tenements, their damp, filamentous plumage brushing against the cast-iron stoves, drying out with a smell like scorching hair. The housewives simply step over them, occasionally scraping the mineral-rich mud from the birds' scaly legs to use as flux in their hearth-fires, or reaching beneath a warm, heavy wing to harvest a double-yolked egg whose shell is as rough and grey as pumice. The long-necked martens are encouraged to nest in the rafters of the nurseries; their four-second, counterweight-timed pacing lulls the wide-eyed, silent infants to sleep, and the creatures neatly lick the perpetual condensation from the babies’ foreheads so they do not catch the grey-lung.

The work of the city—the great, grinding industry that seems from afar so purposeful—is understood by its inhabitants not as commerce, but as ballast. 

Ask a turbine-tender in the North Shelf what becomes of the pig-iron ingots and the three-ton spools of greased cable that are loaded onto the eastern viaduct, and he will look at you with slow, pewter-colored patience. He knows the cargo trams ride out onto the chromatic smear of the unfinished bridge and vanish into the taffy-pulled air. Nothing comes back on those rails—no gold, no grain, no letters from a capital. The factories do not manufacture goods for an empire; they manufacture *fact*. They draw in the stuttering, unformed fog at the mountain’s rim—the raw, dizzying *almost-ness* of the world—and by the sheer, bloody-minded application of brass turbines, four-hundred-pound drop-hammers, and greased atmospheric pistons, they beat it until it agrees to be iron. Until it agrees to be slate. Until it agrees to have six sides, a specific gravity, and a name.

This has bred a peculiar, blue-collar metaphysics among the guilds. The most respected figures in Oakhaven are not mayors or priests, but the *Gage-Men* and the *Plumb-Setters*. 

You can spot a Gage-Man by the leather harness across his chest, hung with brass calipers, spirit levels filled with heavy red mercury, and tuning forks of calibrated steel. They patrol the isolated plinths of the city in pairs, accompanied by four-horned caprids trained to carry panniers of iron wedges. When a corner of a brick manufactory begins to go translucent, or when a chasm between two wards suddenly widens by forty yards overnight without a sound, the Gage-Men are called. They do not pray. They set up heavy bronze transits on tripods, sight along the frayed edge of the masonry, and drive three-foot spikes of cold-forged iron directly into the chromatic aberration. They hang lead plummets from the ghost-stone until the sheer, stubborn downward pull of the weight convinces the rock to finish rendering.

And then there are those who live on the fringes of the clock.

Every citizen of Oakhaven has a cousin, a spouse, or a child in the central tenement—the one behind the veil of greasy condensation where the gas-lamps take a week to flicker. They are not mourned. In a place where a mountain takes forty years to drop three feet, a difference in pace is merely a difference in neighborhood. 

On Sunday afternoons, when the great stamping mills are throttled back to a low, rhythmic wheeze, you will see the *Window-Sitters*. A young weaver from the Lower Ward will ride the swaying cable-car to the central plaza, carrying a folding iron stool and a thermos of salted mutton-broth. He will set his stool on the razor-sharp basalt twenty feet from the tenement’s ground-floor window, wrap his heavy, moss-crusted mantle tight around his knees, and sit for four hours in the dripping gloom. Inside the glass, his wife is turning from the stove with a kettle in her hand. By the time he packs up his stool to catch the last car back across the chasm, her hem has lifted half an inch from the floorboards, and the shadow of her eyelash has crept a fraction of a millimeter across her cheek. Before he leaves, he takes a piece of soft soapstone from his pocket and writes a single word—*Tuesday*, or *Coal-delivery*, or *Still well*—in giant, backwards letters on a slate propped against the wet rock, big enough that over the course of the coming month, as her eyes complete their turn toward the window, the light will have time to carry it in to her.

At night—which is only a deeper, oilier shade of pewter than the day—the city pulls inward. The cable-cars are locked into their cradles high above the chasms, swaying gently as the giant, slant-eyed goats leap onto their roofs to sleep. Inside the isolated towers of the tenements, heavy iron shutters are barred against the glass-rubbing hum of the valley floor. 

In the taverns built into the warm brick basements beneath the boiler-houses, the workers gather. The rooms are low-ceilinged, vibrating constantly with the deep, hibernating-bear stroke of the pistons overhead. Warm cylinder-water drips through the ceiling grates into copper troughs, where pale, blind eels—thick as a man’s wrist and native only to the condenser pipes—thrash lazily in the grease-slicked warmth. Here, the people of Oakhaven drink dark, resinous beer brewed from roasted fern-roots and mineral-salt, sitting shoulder to shoulder on long iron benches. 

They do not sing—their voices are too heavy, the echoes in the stone too eager—and they rarely tell stories of the past, for like the geology beneath them, their history has no talus, no soft accumulation of myth. Nobody remembers arriving in the Crag; their grandfathers simply woke up one morning already holding a wrench, their boots already laced, while the wall behind their beds was still cooling from grey smoke into hornblende. 

Instead, they sit in companionable, weighty silence, passing heavy brass tokens from hand to hand just to feel the mass of them. They listen to the four-second drop of the counterweights through the soles of their iron boots. Occasionally, when the whole valley shudders with a single, earth-splitting *crack*—another forty-year slab of the western rim finally dropping the last three feet into the bedrock—every man and woman in the tavern will pause, lift their heavy, tallow-pale faces toward the ceiling, and tap their ironwood paddles once against the brick. A dry, unison *clack*. An acknowledgment that there is a little more ground in the world tonight than there was this morning, and that by dawn, someone will have to go out with a wheelbarrow and a level to make sure it stays put.
