You notice them first as a slight darkening of the grey—a shifting of mass where a terrace wall is rising by another six inches. From fifty paces away, they are indistinguishable from the dry-stone masonry they inhabit, not by any biological trick of camouflage, but because the basin has thoroughly coated them in its own making. 

They are people of ordinary human frame, though their posture has already begun to adapt to the geometry of the rubble. They move with a tucked, economical gait, elbows held close to the ribs, shoulders slightly rolled forward so as not to snag on the raw, biting edges of the narrow passages. Even when crossing a rare open courtyard beneath the noon shafts of light, they do not swing their arms. They walk from the knees down, a flat-footed, shuffling glide that tests the stability of a slab before committing weight to it.

Everything about them is matte. The white, chalky dust of fresh-fractured limestone has settled into the pores of their skin, the creases of their knuckles, the roots of their hair, and the weave of their clothing until they look like clay figures left to dry in a kiln. Only when the three-hour mist rolls out from the cavern margins does their color return. As the damp moves through the streets, the powder on their brows and forearms turns to a thin grey paste, and their heavy linen garments darken to the color of river mud, revealing the oiled leather aprons they wear over their chests and thighs to protect against the razor-edged shale.

Their clothing is armor against abrasion rather than cold. The cellar-like air of the basin is constant, neither freezing nor warm, so they dress in layers of tightly woven, unbleached hemp and boiled hide that will not catch and tear on a protruding flint shim. Their sandals are thick-soled rafts of braided sedge grass—traded or harvested from the plains above—which they must re-sole every few days as the unweathered rock planes the plant fibers away. That soft, ubiquitous *rasp* that fills the basin is the sound of ten thousand grass soles slowly being filed down by the floor.

If you watch a work crew on a half-built roof, you see how the silence of the place is maintained. They do not shout instructions. In an enclosed basin two miles wide, beneath a ceiling of raw rock, a habit of shouting would quickly turn the air into a miserable, muddled din. Instead, they communicate through a localized language of taps and hand-signs. A builder needing a wedge-stone will rap twice on the course beneath her knees with the horn-tip of her lever; a boy twenty feet away at the sorting pile will hear the pitch of the stone, glance up to catch the shape of her held-out hand—thumb crooked for a thick wedge, fingers flat for a thin leveller—and bring the exact piece without a word passing between them. 

Their hands are remarkable, and terrible to look at. They do not wear gloves, because dry-stone building in fresh cleavage requires a blind, fingertip sensitivity to the grain and micro-fractures of the rock. As a result, their palms and fingers are a map of pale, intersecting white lines—hundreds of shallow, bloodless slices from the freshly split flint and limestone, healed over with thick ridges of grey callus. Their fingernails are worn down below the quick, thick and split like horn. When two of them meet in an alley too narrow to pass without turning sideways, they greet each other by briefly touching the backs of their wrists together—the only part of the lower arm not crusted in rock-flour or raw from handling stone.

Why they are building with such relentless, quiet urgency is written in the architecture itself. These are the people who lived on the plain above when the earth gave way. When two miles of pasture and timber-framed villages dropped fifty feet into the dark, they lost their soil, their wells, and their horizon in the space of an afternoon. What they were left with was a dry, chaotic quarry and the terror of a ceiling that might not be finished falling. 

The honeycomb town is not just shelter; it is an act of stabilization. They are packing the rubble back together to keep it from shifting under their feet. Every loose boulder is being locked into a wall; every scree slope is being stepped into a terrace; every unstable fault-scarp is being buttressed by ten-foot-thick dry masonry. There is a quiet, communal mania to it. Nobody sits idle on an unstacked rock. If an old man stops in a courtyard to rest his knees, his hands automatically drop to the gravel beside him, sorting the shards by thickness into neat, flat piles for the next mason who passes by. 

They have divided themselves not by wealth, for there is nothing left to hoard except space and water, but by elevation and dampness.

Down in the lowest crevices of the settlement, where the sun-shafts never reach and the air tastes of wet chalk, live the *Sorters* and the *Tappers*. These are the heavy lifters and the foundation-layers, often younger, broader-shouldered men and women whose skin has grown pale under its coat of dust. They work by the light of small tallow-dishes set into wall-niches. They are the ones who pry the massive, house-sized slabs apart using soaked wooden wedges—driving dry oak into a hairline crack, pouring a cup of greywater over it, and waiting in the dark for the wood to swell and split the stone with a single, muffled *thump* that vibrates through the soles of everyone's feet for three blocks around.

Higher up, where the masonry climbs toward the three great sinkholes and the rooftops catch the drifting indoor clouds, live the *Rig-tenders* and the *Drip-wrights*. They are leaner, more agile, with eyes perpetually squinted against the harsh glare of the sky-holes. They move across the upper terraces like spiders on a web, balancing on the narrow tops of unmortared walls, forever tightening hemp guys, tilting the great linen sails to catch a shifting current of mist, or using bone gouges to deepen a water-groove in a capstone. You can tell a Drip-wright by their smell: unlike the lower masons who smell only of dry flint and sweat, the rooftop tenders smell of wet linen, tallow-wax, and the sharp, clean odor of green moss.

In the late afternoon, when the three thick columns of sunlight tilt sharply toward the eastern rim of the basin and fade to a bruised violet, the *clack-clack* of the shims slows down. The mist-sails are slackened so the night-damp won't rot the hemp. 

Small, smokeless fires—fed with dried animal dung lowered in baskets from the plains above, or the woody stalks of last season's squash vines—are lit in sunken, stone-lined firepits at the center of the tiny courtyards. Because wood is precious and smoke hangs low under the limestone roof, they cook with heated rocks. Smooth cobbles—the rare, prized pieces of pre-collapse river stone found buried in the deeper strata—are heated in the embers and dropped into clay pots of water, boiling mash made from the bitter, waxy tubers grown in the wall-cracks, flavored with dried surface-mutton or the small, fat-bodied crickets the children trap in the lower drains.

At night, the basin goes entirely black except for the three ragged patches of starlight fifty feet overhead. Yet the settlement never goes completely still. As the thousands of sleepers curl onto their stone benches—padded with thick mattresses of dried moss and frayed linen—the town around them continues to settle. A wall shifts by the width of a hair; a flint shim takes the weight of a lintel and splits with a sharp *tick*; a forgotten drop of late mist falls from a twine lead into a full cistern. In their sleep, the inhabitants listen to the tone of the stone. If a wall groans out of pitch, three or four shadowed figures will rise without speaking, step out into the cool, pitch-black alley, and by touch alone, tap a new wedge into the dark until the balance of the town is restored.
