To live in the caldera is to be slowly, benignly petrified, and to accept this as the baseline of existence. 

The people who inhabit the windowless ziggurats and tend the barometric engines do not think of themselves as living *in* a city, so much as being the soft, mobile components of a very large, very slow clock. There are perhaps four thousand of them spread across the thirty square miles of the basin. From a distance, wrapped in their ankle-length coats of mustard-colored oiled canvas and broad-brimmed sou'westers stiffened with shellac, they look like brass bolts or boundary markers set into the glare of the travertine flats. 

They walk with a specific gait—the "terrace-step"—a flat-footed, knees-bent glide that keeps their center of gravity low over the slick, mineral-greasy stone and absorbs the relentless sixty-cycle hum rising through their boots. Their soles are not made of leather or rubber, which the warm, alkaline water would rot or turn brittle in a month, but of laminated felt and copper mesh, which grips the wet limestone and slowly accumulates a convenient, custom-fitted crust of lime that the wearer knocks off with a small mallet every third or fourth evening before stepping into the dry airlocks of their quarters.

They are almost entirely deaf to the middle registers of sound, and have been for generations. Whether this is an evolutionary drift or simply the result of every infant’s inner ear being gently, permanently stunned during their first year of life inside the vibrating stone ziggurats is a question nobody bothers to ask. They do not experience the city’s roar as noise; they experience it as *pressure* and *posture*. A citizen of the basin can tell, while lying in their sleep-trough, that a third-stage intake valve three miles away on the northern rim has developed a two-millimeter rind of aragonite on its seat, simply because the resonant frequency in their own sternum shifts from a dull *B-flat* to a slightly muddied *A*. 

Because the air is useless for speech, their language has migrated into their hands, their ribs, and their sticks.

Across the vast, bright distances of the plazas, they speak *Long-Arm*. It is a language of semaphore, evolved for high glare and half-mile sightlines. Because fast movement looks frantic and indistinct against the shimmering heat-haze of the wet stone, Long-Arm is spoken at the pace of a tai chi routine. Two maintenance wardens, standing a quarter-mile apart on separate pipe-bridges, will hold a ten-minute conversation that consists of seven or eight postures: a slow raising of the oiled forearm to the horizontal, a forty-five-degree tilt of the wrist, a pause of five seconds to let the syllable register against the sky, and a deliberate drop of the elbow. A whole paragraph of technical diagnostics—*The lithium-slurry in the fourth settling pond is three percent too viscous; I am going to drop the weir by two notches after midday*—requires only four shifts of the shoulders and a particular way of leaning on one's sounding-rod.

Up close, inside the ziggurats or under the roaring bellies of the condensers, they speak *Touch-Cant*. When two people meet on a causeway, they do not look at each other’s faces; they step in close, plant the ferrules of their heavy bronze-shod walking sticks against the same flagstone to share the local ground-hum, and lay a thumb and two fingers against the other’s collarbone or forearm. They tap, press, and slide their fingers in a rapid, subtle cadence that mimics the rhythms of the engines. Intimacy in the basin is entirely tactile. Arguments are conducted by a stiffening of the wrist against a partner’s shoulder; jokes are a sudden, syncopated flutter of the ring finger against the ribs that makes the other person’s diaphragm jump in a silent, wide-mouthed laugh.

Their bodies bear the marks of the water’s chemistry. Because the air is a fine, warm mist of dissolved calcium, magnesium, and trace heavy metals, the people here are heavy-boned and thick-jointed. Their teeth are extraordinarily hard, stained a pale, opalescent grey-green by the copper and fluorides in the condensation they drink, and they never decay. A fifty-year-old engine-tender has the dense, marble-heavy skeleton of an ancient sea-cow. Their skin, where it is exposed at the face and hands, is weathered into a fine, chalky grain that sheds water like slate. In the creases of their knuckles and the corners of their eyes, a fine white dust of travertine precipitates out of their own sweat; husbands and wives spend quiet hours in the evenings scraping each other’s eyelids and ear-folds clean with little ivory spoons.

Inside the blocky housing ziggurats, the architecture is designed to baffle the roar just enough to keep the brain from liquefying, turning the external thunder into a womb-like, hydraulic purr. There are no windows—glass would rattle out of its frames or frost over with mineral scale in weeks. Instead, light is brought in through yard-thick rods of cast quartz set into the ceilings, which channel the pale mountain sunlight down into the living vaults as a cool, green-white glow. 

The interiors are warm, damp, and smell of tallow, boiled linseed, and steeping moss-tea. Furniture is not built; it is grown. When a young couple sets up a household, they take frames of twisted iron wire and set them out in the fast-flowing runoff channels behind the local foundry. Over five or six years, the mineral-saturated water coats the wire in concentric layers of creamy, banded onyx-travertine. When the stone is three inches thick, they haul the new chair or table inside, chip away the sharp drip-points, and polish it with emery cloth until it gleams like wet mutton fat.

They are a people without urgency, because urgency is fatal to their work. If you turn a forty-inch gate valve too quickly under six hundred pounds of hydrostatic head, the water-hammer will crack a three-ton cast-iron manifold like an egg, and the scalding, metal-rich geyser will strip the flesh from your bones before you can take three steps. Everything must be coaxed. A valve is turned a quarter-inch, and then the tender sits down on a calcified bollard, pulls a piece of dried, salted amphibian-tail from their coat pocket, and chews it for twenty minutes while listening through their boots for the pipe’s complaint to subside before turning it another quarter-inch.

The blind, forearm-long amphibians—the tenders call them *flange-newts* or *silt-dogs*, via the two-fingered tap on the inner wrist that names them—are not pests, nor quite pets, but colleagues. The creatures have no eyes at all, only smooth, pearlescent domes of skin where eyes once were, and broad, spade-shaped heads lined with deep sensory pits that read the electrical potential and vibration of the water. Their skin is so clear that you can see the dull orange glow of their livers and the dark, metallic silt moving through their guts. 

The flange-newts are the city’s canaries. They love the fifty-hertz hum of a healthy turbine and the specific, bitter warmth of water that has had its arsenic and lead properly precipitated out in the primary settling basins. A tender making their rounds does not need to carry a chemical assay kit; they simply look at the storm-drain grates. If six or seven pale, gelatinous bodies are stacked atop one another on the bronze bars, pulsing their feathery, blood-red external gills in lazy unison, the water chemistry is true. If the grates are bare, and the newts have crawled up onto the dry, calcified skirts of the aqueduct piers, curling their tails over their noses, then a gasket has blown deep in the mountain’s throat, and raw, sour magma-brine is leaking into the upper conduits.

And beneath the people and the newts, in the places no one sees, lives the third inhabitant of the caldera: the *grease-coral*, or the *boiler-lith*. 

In the dark, roaring throats of the five-foot iron conduits, where the water moves at tremendous pressure and a steady, blood-warm hundred and four degrees, a colonial organism has spent the last three millennia adapting to industrial plumbing. It is a symbiosis of sulfur-eating bacteria, freshwater bryozoans, and metallic precipitates. It grows in massive, fluted, brassy-looking reefs along the inner curves of the pipes, feeding on the iron oxide and the tallow-grease that washes down from the great balance-beam bearings miles above. 

The city’s engineers do not scrape these reefs out unless they threaten to choke a main completely. They have learned that the boiler-lith is what keeps the city from bursting. When a century-old iron pipe develops a hairline stress fracture, the drop in pressure draws the microscopic larvae of the colony into the crack, where they instantly die, calcify, and cement themselves together with iron sulfide, welding the breach shut from the inside with a scar of living bronze harder than the original casting. 

When a citizen of the basin finally dies—usually when their heart, grown slightly too heavy with mineral deposits, simply decides not to complete its next four-minute stroke—they are not buried in the earth, for there is no earth, nor are they burned, for fuel is precious at this altitude. Instead, their body is wrapped in untreated linen and lowered into the Warm Wells: deep, quiet, cylindrical cisterns behind the foundries where the water is richest in carbonate. 

They are left there for twenty years. When the chains are hauled up by the slow, ratcheting cranes, what comes out of the steaming water is no longer a corpse, but a smooth, featureless, three-hundred-pound cocoon of pale pink and cream-colored alabaster, every fold of the shroud and curve of the shoulder blurred into the gentle, scalloped geometry of a river-stone. These heavy, warm effigies are wheeled out on flatcars and set along the low parapets of the sunken causeways, where they serve as windbreaks and milestones—their living grandchildren leaning against them during the long afternoon shifts, resting their elbows on the warm stone of their ancestors' shoulders while they watch the cranes turn by fractions of a degree against the pale blue sky.
