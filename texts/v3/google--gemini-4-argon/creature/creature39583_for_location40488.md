You hear them before you see them, though what you hear is not speech. It is the dry, rhythmic *clack-scrape* of a horn spoon against an oiled leather knee, or the soft, suctioning sound of a felt-soled boot pulling free from a greased cedar rung. 

When a figure finally emerges from the grey-green gloom beneath a low-slung cistern, the first impression is one of startling bulk, quickly followed by the realization that the bulk is entirely artificial. They are a small, compact people, short-limbed and barrel-chested, built to conserve heat and navigate spaces no wider than a chimney flue. Yet they appear nearly spherical because of how they dress. Because cloth never dries in the pit, woven textiles are used only as an innermost lining against the skin. Over this, they wear tiered, overlapping plates of boiled leather, split whale-baleen, and thin, flexible sheets of birch bark, all heavily smeared with the grey mineral tallow skimmed from the sump. They do not wear clothes so much as they wear miniature, shingled roofs. When they move through the weeping alleys, water does not soak into them; it sheets off their shoulders and wide, conical hoods in neat, directed rivulets, pattering onto the slate gutters at their feet.

They move with a peculiar, crab-like grace. Generations spent in a world without a flat horizon have rewired their posture. They walk with a permanent, low-centered crouch, knees bent, elbows tucked tightly against their ribs to avoid catching on the razor-sharp basalt projections or jostling the delicate clay pipes that form their ceilings. They never look straight ahead—there is nothing to see straight ahead but the back of a neighbor’s kiln or a wall of wet black rock. Instead, their heads are perpetually tilted, eyes cast either sharply up toward the suspended troughs or down toward the notched ladders. They navigate the vertical labyrinth mostly by touch, their free hands trailing lightly along the walls, reading the vibrations of the aqueducts like a blind man reading braille.

Their hands are their most striking feature. They are wide, square-palmed, and entirely hairless, the skin cured by constant immersion in mineral-heavy water and tallow into something resembling old, yellow ivory. Under the thumbnails and across the knuckles, the skin is thick with calluses that have actually absorbed the dissolved lime from the water, turning their fingertips into hard, chalky ridges. A pit-dweller does not need a flint scraper for light work; they can absentmindedly flick a crust of forming travertine from a pipe joint with a thumbnail as they pass. 

Their faces, peering out from beneath the stiff, greased brims of their hoods, are broad and pale as lard, untouched by the sun that rarely clears the rim of the sinkhole. The damp, peat-heavy air has given them large, wide-nostriled noses and dark, heavily lidded eyes that gleam with an oily tear-film that protects them from the lime-dust and acrid tallow-smoke. You will rarely see a mouth open. In a place so packed that the breath of one family’s sleeping loft vents directly into the floor of another’s tool-shed, silence is not a rule; it is a physical necessity, like keeping one’s elbows in. 

Instead of speaking, they communicate with the *knack*—a quiet, percussive language conducted through the tools they never put down. A bone chisel tapped twice against a cedar chute means *coming down*. A flat scrape of flint against slate means *water-shift*—a warning that a gutter is being diverted and a dry path is about to become a spillway. For closer, more nuanced talk, they use a combination of finger-signs and tiny, throat-cleared clicks that do not carry beyond the immediate woven-sedge windbreak. A heated argument between two sump-skimmers looks like a frantic, silent shadow-play of twitching chalky fingers and rapid, rhythmic tapping on clay pots, their faces remaining entirely placid, conserving warmth.

Because the town is a machine that will petrify itself into a solid plug of limestone if left untended for a month, the inhabitants have no concept of leisure, yet they are entirely free of urgency. They work at the speed of dripping water. A child—distinguishable from an adult only by scale, looking like a greased leather pinecone—is given a wet-stone and a three-foot section of reed-pipe as soon as they can stand. They will sit on a whalebone scaffold for four hours, methodically rubbing the milky film from that single pipe, watching the pale, blind amphipods curl and uncurl in the current. They do not shoo the amphipods away; the creatures are prized. When an amphipod grows too fat to cling to the slick wood and tumbles into a catch-basin, the child scoops it up, pops it into a pouch at their waist, and continues scraping. Roasted over a tallow-lamp on a skewer of wire-moss, the crustaceans taste of chalk and wet copper, and they make up the bulk of the settlement’s protein, supplemented by the dried peat-mutton and barley-meal traded down in ropes from the moor-clans who occasionally visit the rim.

The social hierarchy of the pit is strictly vertical, though inverted from what an outsider might expect. The upper tiers near the moorland rim are considered the poorest, most miserable places to live. Up there, the wind shears across the basalt lip, driving the cold rain sideways, and the raw rock is at its sharpest and most weeping. The people who live near the sky are the young, the unproven, and the clumsy—those who have not yet learned to move without bumping a chute. Their hands are constantly raw from the wind, and their job is the endless, freezing replacement of the tiny slate shims that first catch the moor-water.

To rise in status in the sinkhole is to sink. The elders, the master-joiners who know how to lash whalebone to driftwood so the tension holds for fifty years, and the *Tallow-Wives* who manage the kilns—these live at the very bottom, in the Sump-Wards. Down here, the pewter sky is reduced to a coin-sized patch of grey light far above, blocked by twenty tiers of overlapping roofs and aqueducts. It is nearly pitch-dark, lit only by scores of tiny, guttering clay lamps burning mineral fat. 

Here, the air is thick, greasy, and surprisingly warm, heated by the thermal vents that bubble up through the final drainage fissure and by the collective rise of smoke and body heat trapped beneath the dense canopy of the town. The elders sit on low stools of polished whale-vertebrae, their heavy outer leathers shed, wearing only soft, oil-tanned skins. Their joints are permanently stiffened—almost calcified—by decades in the damp, so they rarely climb anymore. They spend their days in the warm, echoing dark, up to their ankles in the lukewarm, bronze-colored backwater, skimming the richest pitch, molding the lime-ash and clay into new pipes, and listening.

They are the ears of the settlement. From the bottom of the funnel, a master of the Sump-Wards can close their eyes and read the entire town by the sound of the water reaching the floor. If a *tink-tink-tink* in the northeast quadrant suddenly becomes a heavy *splat*, they know a slate shim has slipped three tiers down from the rim. If the *shhh* of the main cedar trunk-line changes pitch, they know the lime-crust is thickening on the lip of the third cistern. Without opening their eyes, an elder will reach out with a bone mallet and strike a specific, load-bearing driftwood timber three times. The vibration travels up through the wedged, touching architecture—through kiln wall, cistern, and woven floor—until, sixty feet above in the cold grey draft, a boy feels the thrum in the soles of his felt boots, checks his chisel, and goes to fix the leak.

When they die, they are not buried—there is no dirt—nor are they burned, for fuel is too precious to waste on anything that doesn't harden clay or boil grease. Instead, the dead are taken to the deep, narrow crevices behind the dwellings, the unwalkable fissures where the basalt columns meet at acute, jagged angles and the cold mineral water sheets down thickest. They are wedged upright into the rock, packed tight with rubble and broken slate. Within a season, the weeping lime-water does its work. The pale, brittle crust creeps over the greased leather and the quiet, broad faces, sealing them seamlessly into the wall, turning the ancestors into the very stone that supports the aqueducts.
