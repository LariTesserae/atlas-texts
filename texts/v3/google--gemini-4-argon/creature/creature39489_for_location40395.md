At first glance, the thing squatting beside the number four return-pipe looks like a slumped blister of sulfur-crusted basalt, or perhaps an auxiliary pressure-bell that rusted off its rivets thirty years ago and settled into the talus. Three flat-bodied lizards are stacked on its left shoulder, their flanks heaving in the fierce, silent glare, and a slow procession of porcelain crabs is currently clambering up its shin to scrape the grey-green bacterial scum from the creases of its knee. 

Then the twenty-minute vent cycle begins. The ground shudders. A hairline fracture two yards away exhales a dense, scalding fog that immediately turns to heavy grease on every surface. 

The mound reaches up with a heavy, four-fingered hand—the knuckles swollen and calcified like the joints of a brass steam-cock—and wipes the boiling dew from its goggles. The lizards on its shoulder do not scatter; they merely slide down its back a few inches to adjust to the new angle, as indifferent to the motion as they are to the pumping of the iron rigs.

They are called the *tampers*, though no one down in the valley towns has seen one come down the mountain in two generations, and the company ledger only refers to their ancestor-crews under the obsolete column of *High-Sump Maintenance & Descaling*. Whatever they were when the cast-iron towers were first bolted into the caldera, the Sump has long since claimed them, pared them down, and rebuilt them according to its own crowded, deafened logic.

A tamper does not walk across the basin; there is nowhere to put a foot without crushing five hundred crabs or sinking ankle-deep into a dome of wire-grass that hides a pot of boiling mud. Instead, they shift like the talus itself. They move in a low, wide-kneed crouch, keeping their heads and shoulders strictly below the waist-high boundary where the mountain’s radiant furnace gives way to the killing, minus-forty drafts of the upper troposphere. If a tamper stood upright, their lungs would freeze and their ears would blacken in ten minutes; down among the pipes, pressed into the swarm, they are baked to a slow, leathery permanence.

It is impossible to tell where their clothing ends and their skin begins. Over decades, the mineral sweat of the fumaroles—rich in silica, lithium salts, and yellow sulfur—has soaked into their layered woolens and canvas aprons, curing the fabric into a segmented, flexible rind that resembles the overlapping folds of the surrounding rock. Their boots have no soles left; the rubber melted or rotted away long ago, replaced by three inches of layered horn, callous, and grey pumice grit fused to the flesh by the alkaline puddles. When they move from one condenser tower to the next, they slide their feet along the tops of the insulated pipes, plowing a slow, gentle furrow through the crust of crabs and tube-worms without ever looking down. The fauna parts for them and closes behind them like heavy porridge.

Because the thin air kills sound inches from its source, the tampers have no use for voices. Their throats have dried and tightened into silent, rasping flues that only whistle faintly when they haul a heavy wrench. Yet they are never out of communication. To live in the Sump is to live inside a drumhead. 

When a tamper wants to know the state of a borehole half a mile away, they do not look through the shimmering heat-haze; they lean their forehead—or the heavy, yellowed teeth of their lower jaw—directly against the main lithium-sludge line. Through the bone of the skull, the entire five-mile basin becomes an intricate, roaring map of rhythms. They can feel the eighty-cycle thrum of the eastern scarp turbines, the wet, struggling hiccup of a fouled intake valve three ridges over, and the sharp, rhythmic *clink-clink-clack* of another tamper’s ball-peen hammer striking a flange a mile away, sending messages down the iron arteries in a blunt, private code of maintenance and location.

They have become creatures of the twenty-minute breath. Their barrel chests—enormous, rigid cages of bone needed to haul oxygen out of the weightless violet air—rise and fall in a slow, deliberate cadence that matches the subterranean pressure surges. For eighteen minutes, mientras the valley simmers and the alkaline basins slowly drain back into the crust, a tamper is almost reptilian in their stillness. They sit wedged between the warm housing of a dynamo and a blister of volcanic stone, conserving water, letting their body temperature rise with the rock. During this lull, they do their work with minimal, economical movements: packing fresh sulfur-mud into a weeping seam, prying a calcified mat of tube-worms out of a governor linkage with an iron bar, or harvesting the pale, blind bristle-worms that dry in heaps around the vents, rolling them into salted, leathery balls to tuck into their cheek-pouches.

Then, just before the twentieth minute, a tremor hits the soles of their feet. The copper locusts begin to vibrate on the ironwork. 

As the steam rises and the scalding dew rains down, the tampers hit a brief, three-minute pitch of frantic metabolic activity, just like the lizards. While the metal is slick and the salt-crusts are softened by the fresh condensation, they scramble over the rigs with terrifying, crab-like agility. Two or three of them might converge on a screaming brass whistle—silent to the ear, but blurring the air with a vibration that makes the eyes water—their heavy, mineralization-stiffened coats clacking together like stones as they throw their combined weight onto a seized valve wheel. 

They do not look at one another’s faces; there is little face left to see beneath the salt-rimed brass goggles, the noses wrapped in greased rag to keep out the acid mist, and the beards that have become solid, stalactite bibs of yellow alum. Instead, they touch shoulders, or press their knuckles against each other's ribs to feel the wheeze and thump of the other's heart, checking the machinery of the body the exact same way they check the thrust-bearings of the pumps.

When the kids are born—and they are born here, tucked into the dry, hundred-degree hollows beneath the main manifold boxes where the lizards lay their eggs—they do not cry. A crying infant would lose all its moisture to the thin, violet sky in an afternoon. They come out small, dark-skinned, and already squinting against the ultraviolet glare, their fingernails thick as horn. Before they can walk, they learn to lie flat beneath the thirty-inch thermal blanket, sharing the black, cool shadows under the pipes with the long-legged hunting spiders, chewing on strips of dried lizard-tail and learning the geography of their world by pressing their palms to the warm, thudding iron.

If an outsider were to climb the four miles from the lowlands, surviving the freezing passes only to drop into the furnace of the Sump, they could stand ten feet from a group of tampers and never know they were being watched. The visitor would see only the blinding violet sky, the millions of clacking porcelain shells, the blurring flywheels, and a few particularly lumpy, ochre-stained pillars of stone leaning against a steam-pipe—until one of the pillars reached out a slow, gray-crusted hand, caught a copper locust mid-leap without disturbing a single lizard on its arm, and crunched it silently behind a mask of brass and salt.
