At first, you would mistake it for the largest of the limestone hummocks, the one wedged into the north curve of the bowl where the seep-water loses itself under a curtain of spleenwort. 

It has the same profile as the dissolved stone—a low, slumping dome, wider than it is tall, with no hard angles left on it. The velvet-black moss grows up its flanks and across the broad plateau of its back, though here the moss is paler, starved of the minerals it drinks from the rock, its tiny roots tangled instead in a mat of coarse, grease-heavy hair the color of old pewter. Three slate-gray snails are currently crossing the ridge of its left shoulder. The trail of silver mucus behind the rearmost snail is four inches long, which means the creature beneath the moss has not shifted its weight since yesterday.

When it finally does move, the motion does not begin at the extremities. It begins inside, with a sound like a wooden bucket being drawn up from a very deep, very thick well—a slow, heavy *gluck* of cold synovial fluid parting in a hip joint, followed three minutes later by the creak of a rib cage expanding against the weight of the air.

A breath goes in. It takes forty seconds to draw. The air is so thin and so wet that the lungs must work like bellows in mud, sorting the oxygen from the suspended water. When the breath comes out, it does not steam. The creature’s interior is only four degrees warmer than the forty-degree soup of the hollow. Warmth is an extravagance that burns fat, and fat, up here, is time. 

Her name, if she ever had one among the people who lived down in the timberline valleys five hundred years ago, has long since dissolved out of her head, licked away like the corners of the limestone. What remains is a shape that has forgotten how to stand upright because there is nothing up here worth reaching for, and the ceiling of fog presses down too hard.

She unfolds from the hummock with the hydraulic patience of a starfish opening a mussel. 

First, a forearm slides out from beneath her belly. The skin is thick, hairless on the underside, and the exact dead-white of pork rind that has been boiled and left in the pot. It is not wrinkled so much as pleated, lying in heavy, tallowy folds around the wrist and elbow to conserve whatever meager heat her slow heart pumps through her veins. Her fingers are short and wide as canoe paddles, the nails grown out into thick, horn-colored scoops, curved downward like the bills of curlews and striated with dark ridges of calcium. 

She does not brush the harvestmen off her arm as she extends it. She simply pushes her hand through their tangled, hair-like legs at the speed of a growing root. The blind arachnids do not panic; they merely step from her knuckles onto the moss, one leg at a time, their droplets of fog-water transferring to her skin without breaking surface tension.

Her face emerges last, lifting an inch from the cushion of liverwort where she has kept her mouth pressed to breathe the slightly warmer air trapped in the rotting vegetation. 

It is a broad, flat face, all cartilage and heavy bone, built to endure wet cold. Her nose is wide and upturned, the nostrils lined with dense, stiff bristles that catch the mist and channel the runoff down two deep, permanent grooves on either side of her mouth, so the water drips cleanly off her chin without wetting her lips. Her eyes are open, though they are barely eyes anymore. The lids have grown thick and rubbery, fused at the outer corners until only a narrow, almond-shaped slit remains, and behind those slits, the corneas are clouded over with a milky, lavender-gray cataract that matches the plum twilight of the cistern. She cannot see shapes. She sees only the slight difference in density between the dark of the stone and the slightly lesser dark of the fog above.

She doesn't need to see. She knows the hollow the way a tongue knows the inside of its own mouth.

Over the course of an hour, she draws her knees up under her barrel chest and shifts her weight forward until she is resting on all fours over the two deep knee-prints in the moss by the black-bottomed seep. As her knees settle into the depressions, the water in them brims over, silently displacing the hundreds of translucent cave-shrimp that were hanging there. The shrimp do not dart away; they simply drift outward on the slow swell of displaced water, settling into the folds of her shin-skin like dust motes settling on a windowsill.

Her hand reaches into the black silt at the edge of the pool. Her fingers do not grope; they arrive precisely where the goat-femur tube and the flint scraper were left. 

It was not a visitor from the valley who left them here. She brought them up herself, from a dry cave three hundred meters lower down the mountain, though whether that was last autumn or forty autumns ago is a distinction her brain no longer has the chemistry to make. Down in that lower cave, there is a pile of sheep bones and a dead fire pit that has turned to solid gray clay, and sometimes, when the mountain’s deep tectonic plates groan in the winter, a memory of smoke will flick across the back of her mind like a trout’s tail in dark water, and be gone before she can turn her thoughts toward it.

She picks up the flint scraper between a thumb and forefinger so calloused they click against the stone like wood. 

With her other hand, she reaches out to the nearest hummock and touches the flank of a thumb-sized black slug. She does not pinch it. She strokes its mantle with the back of her horn-curved thumbnail, a slow, rhythmic pressure that mimics the foot-wave of another slug. The mollusk responds by secreting a thick, pearlescent jacket of defensive slime—bitter, alkaline, and heavy with an alkaloid that numbs the nerves of anything that tries to swallow it. 

With the dull edge of the flint, she skims this exudate off the slug’s back in a single, neat ribbon, leaving the creature unharmed and still rasping at its algae. She wipes the flint against the lip of the middle soapstone bowl.

Then she waits ten minutes before reaching for the next slug.

It takes her what outside would be called an afternoon and an evening to collect a walnut-sized dollop of the gray paste. In the bowl, she mixes it with three flat-shelled snails—crushed whole with the heel of her palm, their shells making the only sharp sound the hollow will hear all month, a muffled *crunch* like an eggshell stepped on under a carpet—and four drops of oil squeezed from the fiddleheads of the spleenwort fern. 

She stirs it with her pinky finger. The paste smells of wet chalk, crushed greenery, and a faint, peppery musk that makes the red gills of the nearby salamanders twitch once, slowly, in their limestone dimples.

When the mixture is uniform, she scoops a portion into the goat-femur tube and plugs it with the pine pitch, rolling the pitch between her palms for twenty minutes until the friction of her dead-white skin softens the resin enough to seal the bone. That tube will go back into the silt. It is for the winter, when the forty-degree air drops by half a degree and the ice-needles try to form in the blood behind her eyeballs.

The remainder of the paste in the soapstone bowl, she brings to her mouth.

Her tongue is thick, pale gray, and coated in the same velvety film as the stones. She licks the bowl clean with three long, rasping strokes that sound remarkably like the snails grazing on the walls—a wet, heavy *schlupp*.

She does not swallow it. She packs the paste between her lower lip and her gums, where the blood vessels are close to the surface. 

Almost immediately, the alkaloid goes to work. It is not a dream-drug; there are no visions in it, no colors, no warmth. What it does is quieter and much more necessary. It reaches into the ancient, mammalian furnace at the base of her skull—the part of her brain that still remembers being cold, that still wants to shiver, that still wants to panic at the dark and the wet and the crushing, suffocating sameness of the plum-colored gloom—and it turns the damper down. 

The urge to shiver dies in her shoulders. Her heart, which had sped up to nine beats a minute from the exertion of crushing the snails, drops back to six, then five, then four. The tiny, gnawing ache in her teeth where the enamel wore away centuries ago smooths out into a flat, gray buzz. Her sense of time, briefly sharpened by the sequence of *reach, scrape, crush, stir*, unspools again and dissolves into the soup of the air.

She sets the soapstone bowl back in the silt. Within an hour, a thread of bladderwort has already drifted across its rim.

She does not crawl back to the northern hummock. There is no reason to go back when she is already here. Instead, she simply lets her elbows slide outward into the moss. Her barrel chest sinks down until her chin rests on the edge of the seep-pool. Her legs fold under her, the heavy, tallowy pleats of her thighs sealing over her calves to trap the last of her body heat.

A pale salamander, displaced from its pocket by her elbow, crawls with agonizing slowness up the slope of her forearm. It takes forty minutes to reach the crook of her elbow, where the skin is folded and slightly less cold than the stone. It wedges its blind, parchment-white head into the crease of her flesh, expands its dark red gills into the film of water coating her arm, and stops.

Above her, a heavy drop of water gathers at the tip of a yew root dangling from the ceiling of fog. It swells for two hours, gathering dust and dissolved limestone, before it finally lets go. 

*Plop.*

It strikes the broad, flat bridge of her nose, shatters into a dozen smaller beads, and rolls down the bristled grooves beside her mouth. She does not blink. Beneath her ribs, her heart gives a single, heavy thud, waits fifteen seconds, and gives another, settling into the rhythm of the radulae scraping the stone.
