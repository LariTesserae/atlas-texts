To find what truly lives here—beyond the jade-colored grazers and the long-legged, crustacean waders that serve as the valley’s automated janitors—you must stop looking for things that walk across the plazas, and start looking at how the water moves.

You will notice it first as an anomaly in the hydraulics. A sheet of water sliding down a three-hundred-foot buttress will suddenly part around an obstacle that isn’t there. Or, across a quarter-mile of flooded terrace, a secondary ripple will move *against* the grain of the acoustic standing waves—a V-shaped wake drawn across the quarter-inch film of water by something that has no reflection.

These are the **Laminars**. 

At first glance, if you manage to catch the light at the exact, glancing angle of a low sun, a Laminar looks like a broad, ribbon-like sheet of heavy gelatin, or a detached piece of the valley’s own waxy cuticle. They are colonial organisms, or perhaps a single organism whose anatomy is so flat and distributed that it mimics a colony. A single individual might be twelve feet long, four feet wide, and no thicker than a pane of window glass. They are composed of a clear, refractive cartilage that matches the index of the valley's water so precisely that, when submerged in the shallow basins or plastered against a wet wall, they are entirely invisible. You only see them by the way they bend the caustic light-play on the purple-green wood beneath them, acting as living lenses that warp the geometry of the floor as they pass.

They move by a rapid, microscopic undulation of their ventral surfaces, riding the boundary layer of the water like a skate on ice. They do not pierce the wood like the shrimp-herons; they hydroplane. A Laminar can shoot across a half-mile plaza at thirty miles an hour, completely silent beneath the roaring organ-chord of the valley, leaving only a twin trail of tiny, rapidly collapsing cavitation bubbles that smell sharply of ionized air. 

Occasionally, a Laminar will peel itself partway off the floor, stiffening its cartilaginous ribs by pumping fluid into microscopic internal veins. It will rear up like a cobra made of blown glass, five or six feet into the bright mountain air, waving its paper-thin margins to catch the warm updrafts. When they do this, the high-altitude sun hits their internal structures, and for a moment, they blaze with intense, prismatic diffraction—a sudden, blinding slash of magenta and cyan in the middle of an empty courtyard, before they slap back down into the water and vanish.

The Laminars are the middle-men of this ecosystem, harvesting the concentrated sugars and trace minerals from the drainage valves just before the great rosettes *thump* shut. 

Wait long enough by one of those valves, however, and you will see what the Laminars are hiding from. 

The acoustic roar of the valley is not uniform; it has weather. There are zones where the sound from three or four towering viaducts intersects, creating pockets of constructive interference so intense the air itself feels solid, and the water on the floor dances in a frenzy of six-inch-high Faraday waves. 

In these acoustic dead-zones live the **Tympanics**.

A Tympanic is a creature built entirely around the problem of living inside a loudspeaker. It is roughly the size and shape of a crouching human, or perhaps a colossal, multi-limbed spider, though it has no head, no eyes, and no mouth. Its body is a tense, geodesic cage of hollow, calcified struts—looking very much like the pale wood of the valley, though jointed and mobile—across which are stretched dozens of taut, translucent membranes. It looks like a collection of tambourines and box-kites that has achieved a nervous, angular grace.

It walks on six stilt-like legs that end not in needles, like the herons, but in broad, cup-like pads of soft, gripping callus. It moves with a strange, stuttering rhythm, freezing every few seconds to tilt its array of membranes toward the nearest roaring column. 

The Tympanics do not eat the sugar-film, nor do they drink the sap. They are mechanotrophs. Their membranes are lined with piezo-biological cells that convert the relentless, booming acoustic energy of the valley directly into metabolic currency. They graze on the noise. You will see herds of them—five or six angular, pale cages—clustered at the base of a flute-column, leaning into the forty-hertz gale pouring from an acoustic vent, their bodies vibrating so violently their outlines blur. 

Because they cannot see, they navigate by reading the acoustic shadows of the valley. To a Tympanic, the world is a blindingly bright tapestry of standing waves, echoes, and phase-cancellations. If you walk across a plaza, you are a moving hole in the sound. A Tympanic half a mile away will instantly pivot its entire body toward you, tracking the tiny perturbation your soft body makes in the cathedral chord. They are territorial about the loudest spots, and when two Tympanics dispute a prime resonance node, they do not bite or strike; they angle their membranes to bounce phase-inverted sound waves at one another, trying to create a pocket of silence around their rival—a literal dead space that starves the opponent into torpor.

Yet neither the Laminars nor the Tympanics are the true masters of the basin. They are merely renters. To find the architect—or at least, the inheritor of the architecture—you must look inside the wood itself.

The valley is a single plant, yes, but it is not a single mind. That much mass cannot coordinate itself through slow plant hormones alone. It requires a nervous system, and the nervous system has evolved a life of its own.

If you look closely at the pale, bruised-purple walls of the great arches, where the cuticle is polished to a mirror sheen by the jade slugs, you will sometimes see a shadow moving *under* the bark. It looks like a bruise, or a sudden, localized flushing of dark sap, roughly the size of a horse, moving horizontally through the solid, iron-hard cambium at a walking pace.

These are the **Galls**—though that is a poor name for something so fluid. They are endoparasites that have become the brain of the host. 

A Gall has no body in the way a surface creature does. It is a migrating, hyper-dense network of dark, neural-like mycelia and specialized, rapid-dividing cells that lives entirely *inside* the vascular tissue of the mega-flora. It does not tunnel through the wood; it *is* the wood, temporarily. As a Gall moves, it chemically rewrites the plant tissue ahead of it, dissolving the rigid cell walls into a conductive, sensory slush, and then, as it passes, re-hardening the tissue behind it back into seamless, load-bearing timber. It moves through the solid architecture the way a wave moves through water, leaving no tunnel, no scar, and no sawdust.

The Galls are the ones maintaining the valley. When a drainage rosette begins to lose its rhythmic timing, a dark, dendritic shadow will bloom beneath the floor around it. For an hour, the wood will darken to a deep, venous violet as the Gall envelopes the valve, tasting the chemical balance of the water, adjusting the turgor pressure of the local xylem, and resetting the muscular pace of the sphincter. 

And sometimes, they come to the surface—or as close to the surface as their nature allows.

In the late afternoon, when the mountain sun turns the sheeting water to liquid gold and the heat is at its peak, a Gall will migrate to the skin of a sun-facing plaza. It will arrest the local stomata, stopping the flow of water in a twenty-foot circle. As the patch dries, the Gall uses its chemical mastery over the meristematic tissue to *grow* a temporary organ out of the floor. 

Within ten minutes, the smooth, concrete-like bark will bubble, swell, and extrude a bizarre, fleshy sculpture—a cluster of tall, ear-like funnels, or a stalked, compound eye the size of a medicine ball, lens-faceted and wet with clear sap. The Gall pushes its own dark, neural threads into this temporary body, using the valley's own flesh as a spacesuit to experience the open air. 

These extruded bodies are always pristine, smooth, and seamlessly joined to the floor, looking like part of the original architecture. The giant, wooden eye will roll in its socket of bark, watching the bright, empty sky, tracking the prismatic flash of a rearing Laminar, or watching a shrimp-heron pick its way through the shallows. The ear-funnels will twitch, listening to the great, booming chord of the valley—not to feed on it, like the Tympanics, but to judge the performance. Like an organ tuner listening for a single flat pipe in a ten-thousand-pipe array.

If you were to approach one of these extruded eyes, it would turn to look at you. You would see, beneath the clear, resinous cornea, a swirling, dark galaxy of violet filaments. It would not attack you. Unless you took a chisel to the floor, you are of no more consequence to it than a speck of high-altitude dust. 

After an hour or two, having seen what it wanted to see of the sun and the sky, the Gall simply withdraws. It reverses the growth hormones. The great eye melts back into the terrace; the ear-funnels fold inward and smooth over. The stomata reopen, a sheet of warm, celery-scented water washes over the spot, and within five minutes, there is nothing left but the flat, unblemished expanse of purple-green wood, ringing with the endless, wet thunder of the pipes below.
