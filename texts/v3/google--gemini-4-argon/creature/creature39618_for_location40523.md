To see the rest of what lives here, you have to stop looking at the intersections and start looking at the lines themselves, and then at the empty volumes between the lines. The Architects and the gray dissolvers are only the heavy machinery of the basin—the road-crew laying down the track and pulling it up behind them. The actual populace is smaller, stranger, and entirely dependent on the geometry of the grid.

Because the salt-lattice is so pale and the background is a sheer wall of black volcanic glass, anything with pigment stands out unless it pretends to be either the bone-white struts or the black mica flakes drifting through them. 

Look closely at a newly extruded white beam in the northern quarter, and you will see that its thickness is not uniform. What looks like a slightly swollen joint in the potassium-salt is actually a *caliper-mite*, though it is closer in size to a human hand. It has no abdomen to speak of; it is all legs and a small, calcified knot of a thorax. Its four rear legs are clamped onto a vertical strut with the precision of a machinist’s vise, and its two front limbs—triple-jointed, four inches long, and grown over with the exact same crystalline rime as the city—are held out at a rigid ninety-degree angle. It is mimicking a structural corner. 

It waits there for hours, entirely motionless, until one of the cold electrostatic downdrafts comes rolling down the avenue. When the black fronds of the lithophytes snap open to catch the charge, they release a microscopic chaff of spent carbon and static-charged spores. The caliper-mite doesn’t use a web; instead, it uses the city’s own conductivity. As the draft hits, the mite shifts its grip by a fraction of a millimeter, bridging two non-intersecting mineral struts with its own body. It acts as a living capacitor. A faint, violet corona—barely visible in the slate-gray twilight—fizzes across the hair-thin spines of its front legs, drawing the drifting spores and minute, flake-riding organisms out of the air and pinning them to its shell by electrostatic attraction alone. Once the draft passes and the black fronds snap shut, the mite slowly, mechanically scrapes its own legs clean with its mandibles, swallows the dry harvest, and goes back to being a corner.

Then there are the things that live inside the Architects themselves.

An Architect is mostly hollow space—a pressurized blimp of dry parchment-chitin filled with nitrogen gas. If you catch one of the wolfhound-sized spiders pausing to extrude a new buttress, with the pale overhead light shining through its translucent abdomen, you will see small, dark shapes tumbling slowly through the gas inside its body. 

These are the *bellows-lice*, though they are not parasites. An Architect has no wet organs to feed on; its internal "anatomy" is a dry filigree of potassium-pumps and gas-bladders. The lice are symbiotic ballasts. They look like dried cloves or tiny, six-winged shuttlecocks made of dark horn. Dozens of them live inside the pressurized chambers of a single Architect. As the giant spider sprints along a razor-edged roofline at thirty miles per hour, banking hard around a fragile ziggurat without snapping the salt-needles beneath its feet, the lice inside it shift in a coordinated, flocking cloud from one side of its abdomen to the other. They are its gyroscope. They read the subtle changes in nitrogen pressure as the Architect’s legs flex, and they beat their stiff, dry wings to throw their minute collective weight into the turns. In return, they feed on the grey, powdery precipitate of spent nitrogen compounds that cakes the inside of the spider's shell. When an Architect finally dies—splitting open like a dry seedpod on the obsidian floor—the lice do not die with it; they puff upward in a single dark plume toward the pumice ceiling, drifting on the thermal updrafts until they can drop onto the back of another racing spider and bore their way through a spiracle into its gas-chambers.

Further south, in the middle-aged districts where the architecture is thickest and the white lines cross one another in a blinding, dense thicket of geometry, the empty cubic feet of air inside the rooms are occupied by the *tuning-worms*.

They are not worms at all, but colony-organisms that look like six-foot-long needles of spun glass, no thicker than a horsehair. A single individual is invisible until it catches the diffuse light from the pumice shelf above, flashing briefly like a scratch on a lens before vanishing again. They span the hollow interiors of the wire-frame buildings, anchoring their tail-ends to an eastern wall and stretching their bodies completely across the room to hook their mouths onto a western strut. They pull themselves taut—so taut that if you were to touch one, it would feel like a high-E guitar string. 

In a city with no sound, the tuning-worms have monopolized vibration. The porous salt-lattice absorbs airborne noise, and the pumice ceiling kills echoes, but *tension* still travels through the solid crystal. When an Architect lands on a roof three blocks away, or when one of the slow, slate-gray dissolvers at the southern edge undermines a load-bearing pillar and causes a silent, microscopic settling of the grid, the tremor shoots through the white struts. The tuning-worms feel it instantly. 

A single three-story tower might have four hundred of these glass-clear threads stretched horizontally through its hollow belly at different heights and angles. They are feeding on the *piezoelectric* effect—the tiny bursts of voltage generated when the crystalline walls are compressed or twisted by the shifting weight of the city. When a tremor hits a building, the worms contract their bodies in unison, amplifying the structural stress just enough to squeeze a trickle of current out of the potassium-salt joints they are anchored to. To watch a tower full of them during a structural shift is to see a sudden, silent geometry of refracted light—hundreds of hair-thin lines flashing silver in the empty rooms for half a second, drawing a secondary, internal blueprint inside the white frame, and then disappearing as they relax their tension.

Down at the very bottom, in the southern quarter where the city is being erased, the ecology changes from geometric and fast to flat and patient.

The dissolvers—those slow, gray tongues of living slate that melt the city back into gas—are the thermal engines of the basin. The chemical reaction between their pale acid and the calcium-salt struts is faintly exothermic, and the sublimating gas is heavy and cold. Behind a feeding dissolver, a two-inch-deep river of dense, milky vapor hugs the black volcanic glass floor, flowing slowly into the micro-fissures of the clinopyroxene before it dissipates.

This shallow, fuming wake is the territory of the *screed-crabs*. They are as flat as playing cards and black as the glass they walk on, their carapaces grown in sharp, hexagonal plates that mimic the fallen flakes of biotite mica. Because the volcanic floor is so fresh that it consists of millions of microscopic razor-blades, the crabs do not walk on pointed tips like the Architects; instead, they move on twelve wide, articulated skates of smooth, greased horn, sliding over the glass like droplets of oil on a hot skillet.

They follow the dissolvers in silent, shuffling packs of twenty or thirty. As the white towers vanish into acid mist above them, anything that failed to migrate northward falls into the vapor. A caliper-mite whose joints locked up with over-calcification; a brittle, snapped segment of a tuning-worm; a dead lithophyte frond that lost its grip on a melting corner—all of it drops into the two-inch layer of heavy gas. The screed-crabs skate out from the black fissures, utterly invisible against the floor until their flat shells cut through the white vapor, leaving tiny, swirling eddies in the mist. They scissor the fallen debris into fine powder with horizontally mounted jaws, sweeping the volcanic glass immaculate.

And yet, there is one thing that does not migrate with the city, and does not live on the floor.

If you look all the way up—past the racing, parchment-colored legs of the Architects, past the highest white spires, to the underside of the overhanging pumice shelf a hundred feet above—you will see that the pale gray stone is pitted with thousands of small, natural vesicles, like the holes in a loaf of bread. 

Out of nearly every hole hangs a single, dry, black filament, three to ten feet long, swaying almost imperceptibly in the rising warm air before it cools and spills back down the cliffs. These are the *plumb-lines*, the larval stage of the lithophytes, or perhaps the reproductive stage of the dissolvers—in this dry, mineral economy, the line between plant and animal is mostly a question of speed. Each black thread is weighted at the bottom with a single, perfect crystal of salt that it has grown from the rising vapor of the dying southern quarter. 

They hang there for years, drinking the rising fumes of the sublimated towers, growing heavier and longer day by day, waiting for the slow, creeping migration of the city below to bring a newly built northern spire directly beneath them. When a white strut is finally drawn across the air within an inch of a plumb-line's weighted tip, the static charge between the rising tower and the hanging thread bridges the gap. The thread snaps taut, welds itself to the new roof in a silent spark, detaches from the ceiling fifty feet above, and curls up into a tight, charcoal-colored rod—a new bundle of black fronds, ready to snap open at the next cold draft.
