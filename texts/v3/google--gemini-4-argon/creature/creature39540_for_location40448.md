Wait for the stutter. 

While the vents are roaring, the eye is too battered by the luffing of the bruised-violet sails and the glitter of the iron-dust to pick out anything that doesn’t want to be seen. You can stare directly at a main anchor-cable—a braided hawser of silica-vine as thick as a pine trunk, vibrating like a bass string—and see nothing but a slight thickening in the bark, a dark, streamlined blister of horn and cured hide pressed flat against the leeward curve.

Then the deep mountain coughs. The pressure drops. The sound cuts out. 

In that three-second vacuum, while the parchment-kelp collapses like punctured lungs and the shale-moths rain down in a clicking hail, the blisters *unfold*. 

They drop before the flyers do. Having no wide membranes to slow their descent when the air dies, they plummet like stones—and they use that gravity. Three or four of them will detach from the high rigging simultaneously, limbs tucked, dropping forty feet in two seconds through the sudden hush. As they fall, long hooked gaffs of bone shoot out from their forearms, snagging the stunned, tumbling ribbon-predators and the disoriented tumbleweed-scavengers out of the dead air. 

Just before they smash into the fluted stone lips of the vents, the mountain *thumps*. The gale erupts. 

With a sound like a heavy cloak snapping in a gale, the falling figures throw open the pleated panels of tough, resin-stiffened skin that span the spaces between their double-elbowed arms and their splayed, four-toed legs. They catch the blast on their chests. The updraft hits them like a solid wall, arresting their fall a yard from the rock and rocketing them back up into the amber gloom on a column of screaming ash. Before the flora has even finished snapping taut, they have already slammed back against the leeward side of a ledge or a cable, folded their wings behind their backs like beetle-casings, and locked their toes into the stone.

Seen up close—wedged into the narrow shadow of a shale blind while the wind howls outside—they look less like beasts and more like bundles of dry birch-twigs and old violins wrapped in rawhide. 

They are vaguely anthropomorphic in the way a praying mantis or a bat’s wing-hand is anthropomorphic: a haunting coincidence of joints. When crouched, they are no taller than a ten-year-old child, though unfolded they have a reach of seven feet. They have almost no soft tissue. Under a parchment-tough epidermis colored the exact tarnished silver and ochre of the basin, their bodies are a dense lattice of hollow, honeycombed bone and vulcanized tendon. They do not have fat; they have tension. Their limbs are built on a biological ratchet-and-pawl system—when they grip a braided vine or wedge their flat, chisel-toed feet into a fissure, a tendon clicks into a bony notch at the wrist and ankle. They can hang in a hundred-mile-an-hour gale for six hours without expending a single calorie of muscular effort, asleep while the wind tries to tear them apart.

To survive the updraft, though, a creature that wants to *build* cannot be as light as the moths. If they were paper-thin, they could never swing a stone hammer against a bronze wedge; the recoil would fling them out of the spire’s bowl into the empty sky. 

They have solved this with ballast. Across their chests and bellies, their skin forms deep, overlapping pleats—like the pockets of an apron—lined with rows of stiff, keratinous bristles. Inside these pouches, they pack heavy, iron-rich stones fished from the lip of the vents, or ingots of crudely melted copper. A builder working on the ledges is thirty percent rock by weight. If one is knocked loose from the wall and the wind is too weak to catch its small gliding-membranes, it simply runs a thumb down its sternum, unlatching the bristle-pouches. It dumps ten pounds of stone into the abyss, becoming light enough in a heartbeat to ride the thermal back to the rim. 

Their heads are low, flat, and wedge-shaped, fused directly to their shoulders with no neck for the wind to twist. Where a face should be, there is a streamlined prow of dark, polished horn—identical to the beaks of the tumbleweed-scavengers—that parts the abrasive blast like the bow of a ship. Set back from this prow, protected in deep bony trenches, are four eyes. They have no wet eyelids; instead, clear, hard lenses of biological mica slide across the orbs in alternating pairs, scraping away the sulfur-ash with a tiny, dry *shick-shick* sound.As their lenses grow pitted and clouded by the iron-sand over a few weeks, they simply pry the top layer off with a thumbnail, exposing a fresh, transparent scale beneath.

They do not breathe the way wet things breathe. A pair of lungs would be scorched and silted solid in an hour. Instead, along their ribs are six gill-like slits guarded by stiff, electrostatic hairs that repel the dust. As the gale rushes past their flanks, the Venturi effect pulls air through dry, corrugated chambers in their chests, where oxygen and sulfur-gases are forced directly through thin membranes into a thick, dark, oil-based blood. When they are tucked inside their resin-domes out of the wind, they cannot stay still; they have to rock rhythmically back and forth, pumping their own ribcages like concertinas just to keep the air moving through their slits.

They are creatures of the *hum*. 

You cannot see ten feet through the thrashing kelp-sails, and you cannot shout over a volcano’s throat, so they have abandoned both light and voice. Their entire bodies are resonant chambers. Their hollow collarbones and elongated, tuning-fork pelvises are wired directly to their nervous systems. 

When one of them crouches on the stone rim, it presses its broad, calloused knuckles against the rock or hooks a claw into a silica-vine. Immediately, it reads the entire amphitheater. It feels the high, frantic 400-hertz thrumming of a resin-net thirty yards away that has just snagged a five-foot ribbon-eel; it feels the low, grinding wobble of a greased stone axle that needs fresh fat from a scavenger’s marrow-sac; it feels the rhythmic, three-beat tapping of a mate two ledges over, chipping a new flute into a whistling boulder.

This is the true purpose of the chimes, the copper plates, and the carved stones. To an outsider, the basin is a chaos of noise; to the builders, it is a brightly lit room. 

The acoustic frames hung in the empty spaces are their lamps and their fences. The whistling boulders project sharp, high-frequency cones of sound through the turbulent gloom. As the sound waves bounce off the swarms of shale-moths and the thrashing sails of the bellows-ferns, the builders *feel* the echoes coming back through the soles of their feet and the stiff whisker-quills on their forearms. They are echolocating without ever making a sound themselves—using the wind to power a permanent, mechanical sonar grid. By turning a stone vane or tightening a strip of cured hide with a bone wrench, they bend the wind’s roar into new frequencies, blinding the predators they want to starve and herding the mica-scaled swarms straight into the leeward eddies where the sticky nets wait.

And they are electric. 

In an air so dry and full of rubbing silica and iron dust, static charge is a constant violence. The plants use it to live; the builders use it as a tool. Their outer skin is a dead, insulating horn, crackle-glazed like old pottery, which allows them to crawl across the high-voltage piezoelectric stalks of the parchment-kelp without being stopped by the current. Beneath that insulation, however, runs a secondary nervous system—fibers of biological copper-protein harvested from the dust they eat. 

They collect the static. As they shimmy along the main cables, dragging strips of dry hide behind them to wipe the sticky resin from their traps, they deliberately build up a massive charge inside two insulated, sponge-like organs in their hips. 

You can see them use it when they hunt the larger dog-sized scavengers, or when they mend the rigging. Two builders will creep out onto a thrashing vine-cable, moving with a jerky, spider-like precision—three steps during the gale, freeze when a sail whips past, three more steps. When a scavenger vaults toward their net, a builder does not stab it. It reaches out with a long, copper-tipped gaff and touches the beast’s horn beak. There is a sharp, violet flash in the amber gloom, a smell of ozone that cuts right through the sulfur, and a crack like a breaking branch. The scavenger’s nervous system locks up; it drops straight into the net, twitching.

Back in the alcoves, they use the same stored lightning to smelt and weld. Two or three of them will crowd into a shale wind-blind, pressing their rumps against a pile of pulverized iron-dust and native copper scraped from the vent-filters, touching their horn-insulated fingertips together over the pile to let a continuous, white-hot arc hiss between them. In the tiny, calm dark of the dome, illuminated by the stuttering blue light of their own static, they fuse the dust into crude, lumpy wedges and hammer them flat with river-smooth stones while the metal is still orange.

They have no soft mouths, no tongues, no saliva. Beneath the horn prow of their faces is a vertical, crushing mill of serrated plates, like the jaws of a parrotfish. Inside their shelters, they sit folded up like carpenter’s rules, methodically feeding shucked moth-scales, dried ribbons of flesh, and black plant-resin into their mills. The sound of a family eating is the sound of dry corn being ground in a stone mortar. 

They do not drink. Whatever meager hydrogen they need to synthesize their dark body-oils and the synovial grease for their ratchet-joints, they strip chemically from the carbohydrate-resins of the bellows-ferns. When they die—which usually happens when a tendon finally frays through and snaps under the strain of the wind—they do not rot. There are no bacteria here that know how to rot a thing without water. 

Instead, the living take the dead apart. 

Nothing in the bowl is wasted. A dead builder is already cured timber and tempered spring-steel. Its hollow femur becomes the frame for a new wind-harp; its flat shoulder blades are lashed to a spinning stone axle to catch the gale; its tough, pleated wing-skin is stretched between bone pitons and smeared with black resin to catch the evening's drift of moths. 

Then the deep mountain stutters. The vents drop an octave. 

For three seconds, the entire amphitheater goes dead silent. From a dozen dark cracks along the ochre walls, little triangular prows poke out. Sixty mica lenses slide *shick-shick* across sixty dark eyes, scanning the falling sky. A few stone tools are tapped against the rock—*tik-tik-tak, tik-tak*—a quick, dry conversation running around the rim of the bowl at the speed of sound through stone. 

Then the mountain *thumps*, the hurricane roars back through the throats of the earth, and they pull their heads back into the storm to check their traps.
