If you manage to catch one in the beam of a lamp—before the light draws a blinding, suffocating hurricane of wing and mandible that smashes the glass—you do not see a beast, or some pale, long-adapted troglodyte. The basin is less than a hundred years old; evolution has not had the time to sculpt a new species to fit it. 

What the light catches, for a fraction of a second, is a human being. Or what happens to a human being when three generations are born into a dark, ninety-degree pressure cooker where stopping for ten minutes means being eaten alive.

They look, at first glance, like walking mounds of butchered beetle-shell and cured hide, slicked over with the same mineral grease that coats the leaves. They wear the valley’s dead to survive its living. Over their knees, shins, and forearms—the places that take the brunt of the razor-sharp basalt and the scything forelegs of the flyers—they have lashed overlapping plates of convex black carapace, tied tightly with raw, wet gut that shrinks as it dries, binding the armor so hard to the flesh that the skin beneath is permanently waffle-scarred and purpled. Across their shoulders and backs, they drape thick aprons of the grey, rubbery tree-cork, smeared heavily with the bitter, foul-smelling paste of crushed sulfur-molds to keep the stinging things from laying eggs in their skin. 

Beneath the armor, they are whipcord and knot. There is not an ounce of fat on them; the heat and the relentless pace burn everything away. Their bodies are compact, stringy, and startlingly hot to the touch, running a permanent, low-grade metabolic fever that matches the frantic tempo of the jungle. Because they never see the sun, their skin is the color of tallow, stained yellow-grey in the creases by volcanic ash and sulfur sweat. 

Their faces are terrifying, largely because of what they have done to their own senses. 

Eyes are a liability here. Eyes are soft, wet jelly waiting to be punctured by a stray obsidian splinter, a whip-vine, or the stray tarsal claw of a colliding mantis-bat. Most of the older ones are blind, their eyelids scarred shut from old injuries, and they do not care. Even the young ones, whose eyes still work in the faint green gloom of the root-molds, keep them squinted down to narrow, crusted slits, or covered entirely with goggles made of scraped, translucent bladder-skin that only lets in the vaguest wash of orange vent-light.

Instead, they are all ear and hand. They keep their heads shaved with dry-flaked volcanic glass—hair only catches in the brambles and holds ticks—and their ears seem huge against their lean skulls, often cupped forward by stiffening collars of hardened hide bound behind the neck to catch the bounce of sound. 

To watch one move is to watch something that has forgotten how to walk on flat ground. They never stand fully upright; the low, tangled ceiling of blood-vines and the swinging deadfalls make standing a death sentence. They move in a low, spidering lope, four-limbed, their hands almost as heavily calloused as their feet. Their soles are horrifying—an inch of blackened, horn-like dead skin split deep by old basalt cuts that have healed full of volcanic dust, rendering them tough as hooves. When they cross the narrow, jagged ridges between the vents, they don't step; they scuttle, fingertips and toes finding purchase on the razor-edges with a frantic, twitching precision that never pauses to test the weight. If a rock shifts, they are already three feet away before it hits the bottom of the fissure.

They do not have camps. A camp is a stationary smell, and a stationary smell in the basin draws the packs of split-hoofed quadrupeds and the carpeting swarms of bone-cleaning beetles within twenty minutes. 

Instead, they live on the move, in small, hyper-coordinated knots of five or six—any larger and the noise of their own movement would drown out the subtle shifts in the jungle’s roar that warn of a collapsing cork-tree or a charging herd. When they must sleep, they do not lie down. They climb halfway up the bloated, water-tower trunks of the pale trees, carve a quick pocket into the soft, fibrous cork with a basalt hand-axe, and wedge themselves inside like larvae, pulling a curtain of bitter moss over the opening. Even then, they sleep in twenty-minute snatches, their muscles twitching constantly, one hand always resting on the humming, vibrating wood of the trunk to feel the approach of wood-grubs eating their way up from the roots.

Because the valley’s ambient noise is a physical weight that shreds the human voice in yards, they have abandoned speech almost entirely. A shout is useless; it sounds too much like the distress bark of the quadrupeds, and it drains the throat of precious moisture. 

They talk with stone. 

Every one of them carries a striking-stone lashed to the wrist and a core of dense, glassy basalt in the palm. That rhythmic *clack-clack-clack* you hear in the dark is only half tool-making; the other half is conversation. Stone striking stone cuts through the biological roar of wing and chitin on a completely different frequency. It is sharp, dry, and inorganic. A double-tap from the left ridge means a deadfall has dropped; a rapid, stuttering trill from the tangle ahead means a pack of hoofed hunters is moving up the fissure; a heavy, dull *thunk* using the butt of a bone spike means *come, meat, hurry.* 

When they are close enough to touch—huddled for three frantic minutes over a freshly crushed flyer, their stone blades flashing in the absolute dark as they strip the heavy flight-muscles from the thorax by pure tactile memory—they communicate by pressing their foreheads or chests together and humming. They use their own ribcages as resonators, pitching low, vibrating groans directly into the bones of the other person, feeling the cadence and pitch through their teeth while their hands never stop working, slicing, tying, and packing the meat into gut-sacks.

They are not feral. That is the strangest, most chilling thing about them. A feral animal simply hunts and hides. These people are engineers of the frantic. 

Their minds work like their bodies: fast, unsentimental, and entirely focused on leverage. They carry no nostalgia for the rim of the world above the smog; nobody alive down here has ever seen the sky, and if you told them about open space where nothing touches you and the air is quiet, they would likely find it terrifying—how would you know what is coming if the air isn't vibrating against your skin?

To them, the valley is not a hell; it is a machine of limitless, violent abundance. You can see it in the way an adolescent—no more than twelve, scarred from neck to ankle, moving with the jerky grace of a praying mantis—pauses above a steaming vent. He doesn't look down. He tilts his shaved head, listening to the pitch of the steam whistling through a narrow crack, and then listens to the distant, thrumming roar of a beetle-swarm grinding through a neighboring trench. With three deft strikes of his wrist-stone, he knocks a flake of obsidian off his core, jams it into the whistle-crack to alter the pitch of the escaping steam by half a note, and then scrambles away into the vines. 

Ten minutes later, drawn by the new acoustic resonance that mimics the click of a freshly molted, soft-shelled giant, the beetle-swarm changes direction, pouring blindly out of the trench and directly over a sheer basalt drop-off where two older trappers are already waiting at the bottom with heavy bone pries to crack the stunned shells before the things can right themselves.

They are a people whittled down to pure kinetic reflex and acoustic math. If you were to grab one, hold them still, and force them to stop moving, they would probably die of the shock before the insects even reached you—their hearts racing out of control in the sudden, unbearable terror of stillness. So you let them go, and in a heartbeat they are gone, dissolved back into the hot, greasy dark, leaving behind only the smell of crushed sulfur-mold and the sharp, dry *clack-clack* of stone talking to stone through the roar.
