If you watch a three-acre slope of Mantle-Slugs for an entire afternoon, your eyes eventually stop trying to separate one creature from the next. They become a single, continuous topography of grey-violet hide, heaving in microscopic increments. 

It is only when you stop looking for edges that you notice the furrow. 

Moving diagonally across the swell is a slight depression in the living carpet, as if something heavy is being dragged *beneath* the slugs rather than over them. As the furrow advances—perhaps ten feet an hour—the great dory-sized mollusks do not pull back in alarm; they simply part with a thick, syrupy reluctance, their rubbery mantles rolling aside to reveal a pale, hunched back that matches the bone-ochre of the bedrock beneath.

This is one of the Gleaners, though there is no one left to call them that, and they have no names for themselves.

At first glance, a Gleaner looks like merely another variation of the local fauna—a slightly more mobile boulder, or a giant crab that has shed its shell and grown a thick, calloused pelt. It moves on all fours, though "all fours" is a generous term for the posture. Its knees and elbows never fully lock; it shuffles forward in a low, wide-stance squat, its belly clearing the granite by only a few inches. Its skin is the color of old tallow, deeply fissured and dusted with the same silvery, flaking mucus-ash that coats everything on the Altiplano. In the deep creases behind its neck and along its spine, colonies of small, blind porcelain-crabs ride like lice, clinging to the dry folds and picking at the salt-crusts of its sweat. The Gleaner never brushes them off.

It is only when the creature pauses at the lip of a saucer-puddle, raising its forequarters slightly to let a dragging cloud-bank wash over its chest, that its structure betrays it. 

Beneath the slack, tallowy folds of its throat, there are clavicles. The limbs, though bowed and thickened like yams to support a heavy, water-retaining torso in thin air, end in five distinct digits. The nails on these digits are not claws; they are flat, broad, and worn down to the quick by decades of wedging into granite seams, horn-yellow and thick as oyster shells.

The face is the last thing to resolve. It is tucked permanently downward, the neck fused by a heavy hump of fat and muscle that acts as both a caloric reservoir and a heat-sink under the unfiltered sun. It has no nose to speak of—only two downward-facing slits flanked by fleshy valves that seal shut when it pushes its head into the damp crevices beneath the slugs. Its eyes are very small, sunken deep behind heavy, hairless brow-ridges, and filmed over with a milky, protective nictitating membrane that rarely retracts. There is nothing on the Altiplano thirty miles away that needs to be seen; a Gleaner only needs to distinguish between the purple shadow of a rock, the pale ochre of a crab's carapace, and the bright, sudden glare of the sky.

They are the builders of the Cairns, or rather, the Cairns are what happens when a Gleaner lives in one place for too long.

The ruins are not ancient. Some of the knee-high walls were stacked three hundred years ago; some were stacked last month. The confusion arises because a freshly built wall looks identical to an ancient one, as the Gleaner uses stones that are already covered in lichens and crab-cement, and builds with the same glacial lack of urgency that governs the rest of the plateau.

They do not build for shelter from the wind, for there is no wind. They do not build to keep out predators, for nothing hunts here. They build for *grade* and for *shade*. 

A Gleaner’s entire life is a quiet, unending management of moisture and pressure. When a Gleaner finds a shallow trough where the cloud-bellies linger a few minutes longer than usual, it will spend several weeks—sometimes an entire season—slowly levering loose slabs of porphyry out of the ground and stacking them into a low crescent or a rough, domed heap. It works by touch, wedging its thick, spade-nailed fingers under a stone, applying a slow, hydraulic leverage that takes minutes to pry the rock free from its bed of dried mucus, and then waddling with it a few yards to drop it onto the pile with a dull, chalky *clack*.

Once the Cairn is waist-high, the Gleaner stops. It does not roof the structure with timber—there is none—nor does it live inside it like a house. Instead, the Cairn acts as a baffle. The next time a cloud drags across the swell, the stone wall catches the fog, forcing the moisture to pool more deeply in the lee of the rocks. Within weeks, the Mantle-Slugs, drawn by the dampness, migrate an inch an hour toward the new vertical surface. They pile up against the wall. They flow over the lip of the stones. They fill the interior until their massive, leathery bodies form a tight, self-supporting dome over the floor of the ruin.

And beneath that living roof of three-ton slugs, in the dark, warm, 100%-humidity gap between the granite floor and the bellies of the mollusks, the Gleaner makes its den.

If you were to pull the slugs away from one of the older beehive domes—a feat that would require chains and a winch—you would not find a desolate pile of rubble. You would find a warm, glistening chamber smelling faintly of copper, wet chalk, and sweet, fermented silk. 

Here, wedged into the spaces between the stones and the damp underbellies of the slugs that form the ceiling, you might find two or three Gleaners curled together like peeled roots. In the dark, their milky eyes are closed. Their breathing is so slow it is almost imperceptible—one shallow, rasping intake of the thin air every two or three minutes, their ribs expanding with a soft, leathery creak against the stones.

They are farmers, though their farming requires no tools and almost no motion. 

In the humid dark of the Cairn, the colonial silk-worms thrive with twice the density they achieve in the open air. Their heavy, gelatinous hammocks hang down from the ceiling of slug-flesh like stalactites. When a Gleaner wakes—which it does in bouts of twenty minutes every few days—it does not leave the Cairn unless the moisture has dried up. It simply reaches up with one heavy, trembling arm, hooks two broad fingers into a silk hammock, and pulls down a fistful of warm, wet web and the pale grubs within. It pushes the mass into a small, toothless mouth—their molars long ago fused into flat, bony crushing-plates—and grinds it into a paste, swallowing with a slow, throat-rolling gulp.

When they do emerge into the bright, oven-warm daylight to move between Cairns or to harvest the rubbery tubers that grow in the deeper glacial saucers, they do not walk over the carpet of life; they swim through it at the speed of a cooling lava flow. 

A Gleaner moving across the Altiplano is a master of tactile persuasion. It does not shove the Mantle-Slugs aside—nothing could shove a creature that heavy with such high friction. Instead, as the Gleaner creeps forward, its hands move in a continuous, rhythmic patting and stroking against the flanks of the slugs ahead of it. It knows the exact clusters of nerve-nodes along the mollusks' mantles. A slow, firm thumb-press behind a breathing-pore; a gentle, scratching rake of horn-nails across a dry patch of fungal rot that the crabs have missed. In response to this grooming, the huge slug reflexively contracts that side of its muscular foot, curling away from the touch by three or four inches to expose more of its itchy hide to the Gleaner’s nails. 

Inch by inch, the Gleaner strokes a path open before itself. Behind it, the slugs slowly relax and expand back together, sealing the wake.

They have no spoken language. In a world where a neighbor is never more than a few yards away, and where nothing ever happens suddenly, the air is not used for information. If two Gleaners meet in the troughs between the swells—perhaps kneeling on opposite sides of a condensation puddle, skimming the floating Bladder-Gnats off the surface like cream from warm milk—they do not look at one another. 

Eventually, one will shuffle around the edge of the saucer until its shoulder presses against the other’s flank. They will sit together in the sun for three or four hours, while the clouds crawl over their backs and the little blind crabs cross from one tallowy shoulder to the other as if they were a single continuous boulder. Through the skin of their pressed sides, they exchange a slow, sub-audible repertoire of gut-rumbles, muscle-twitches, and the rhythmic shifting of weight from hip to hip. It is a language of pure barometric pressure and metabolic state: *here is warm; the third cairn down-slope is dry; there is a dead slug rotting near the red pillar.*

When a Gleaner dies—which happens not from disease or violence, both of which require a faster metabolism than the Altiplano supports, but simply when the heart grows too tired to push the thick, dark blood through the body—it does not fall down. It is usually already wedged between two Mantle-Slugs or tucked into the base of a wall. 

It simply stops shifting its weight. 

Within a day, the Porcelain Crabs, sensing the cooling of the skin, gather in slightly greater numbers in the creases of its groin and armpits. Within a week, the great slugs on either side, no longer kept at bay by the subtle, rhythmic prodding of the Gleaner’s thumbs, expand their muscular feet to claim the space. They flow over the hunched back, sealing it against the purple stone. 

Inside the dark, warm pocket beneath the slugs, the body does not putrefy; the air is too thin and the crabs are too thorough. The soft tissues are delicately, silently dismantled by ten thousand clock-hand claws and fed to the grubs in the silk hammocks overhead. The heavy, bowed bones, rich in calcium and phosphorus, are left gleaming and clean on the granite. 

Over the next decade, the crabs use the ribcage as a prime nursery, packing the gaps between the ribs and vertebrae with millions of pebble-like eggs. The biological cement builds up, layer by calcified layer, until the skeleton is fused solidly to the bedrock—becoming just another low, knobby ridge on the whale-backed swell, catching the belly of the next passing cloud, making a little puddle of shade where the moss can grow.
