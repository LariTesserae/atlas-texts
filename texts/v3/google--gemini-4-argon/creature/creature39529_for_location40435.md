If you stand at the lip of the basalt basin and let your eyes unfocus against the shivering grid of the water, you will miss them entirely. If you try to track the motion of the piston-ferns or the three-step stutter of the caliper-birds, your brain will smooth the seams of the glade’s broken time, and you will see only the machine.

To see what lives here—what *actually* lives here, rather than merely operates—you have to look at the splices. You have to watch the exact fraction of a millimeter where the water drop un-falls, or the air directly behind the wading bird’s heel at the instant it snaps backward three paces. 

There is a crowding in those zero-second gaps. 

At first, it looks only like a fault in the air, a localized shimmer like heat haze rising off cold iron, or the colorless smear left on the retina after staring at a welding arc. The locals of this impossible valley do not occupy the four-stroke rhythms of the ferns or the sixty-cycle drone of the moss. They inhabit the *backlash*—the tiny, violent slack between the teeth of the temporal gears before the loop catches and resets.

They are called the *Klink-men* by no one, because no one has ever come down from the newly split range to name them, yet the word is there in the mouth if you breathe the pine-and-ozone mist for too long. They are tall, though their height is an impression left by their verticality rather than a measurement; they are built like bursts of static caught in a narrow chimney. 

When one of them steps out of the seam between a bird’s third step and its first, it appears to be assembled from the things the glade refuses to produce: wear, residue, and history. 

In a basin where the stone cannot crumble, the Klink-men are crusted with dry, grey scree. In a marsh where nothing rots, their long, multi-jointed limbs are draped in brown, mulched fern-fronds and the dried, papery husks of dead sparrow-insects. They look like pillar-saints made of lint, magnetized iron filings, and tallow, packed tightly around a core of cold, yellow tallow-light. They have no faces in the mammalian sense—only a vertical sensory slot, like the visor of a diving bell or the aperture of an old camera, bruised dark around the edges with greenstone dust.

There are seven or eight of them in the commons at any given moment, though whether it is seven individuals or one creature caught in an eight-part cross-stitch of time is impossible to tell. They move through the chest-high pewter fog with a strange, dry, rustling gait that makes no sound at all against the roaring *clack-shunt* of the ten thousand frogs. They do not walk on the polygonal basalt floor; they walk on the Faraday waves themselves, their wide, splayed feet—shaped like snowshoes woven from oxidized copper wire and split bone—stepping exclusively on the shivering crests of the water-squares where the surface tension is locked as hard as glass.

To watch one work is to feel a profound, nauseating vertigo, because they are the only things in the amphitheater that move from Yesterday into Tomorrow, and the effort of it is tearing them apart.

Every movement they make is heavy, viscous, and over-encumbered, like a diver wading through mercury. Because the air of the glade is constantly snapping back to the beginning of its own second, a Klink-man must push through the resetting atmosphere by main force. When one reaches out an arm, the air resists it like wet canvas, then parts with a dry, ripping sound that is the only quiet noise in the valley—*zzzt*, like a fingernail dragged across taut silk. In their wake, the pewter mist does not swirl; it curdles into greasy, yellowish vortices that hang for five or six loops before the engine of the glade can iron them flat again.

What are they doing? They are harvesting the friction.

Even a perfect, time-invariant engine generates a tiny, illicit surplus—the sheer, brute potential of everything that *almost* happened when the loop reset. The kinetic energy of the water drop that had to be hauled back to the rock tip; the momentum of the caliper-bird’s bill that was arrested mid-plunge; the heat of the frog’s flywheel-organ that is never allowed to warm its porcelain skin. That discarded causality has to go somewhere, or the basin would crack open like an over-pressurized boiler. 

It leaks out at the splices as a pale, waxy substance that looks like frozen lard and smells sharply of vinegar and scorched hair. 

A Klink-man will stand beside a bank of shrieking bladder-orchids for what might be an hour outside the mountain, waiting for the exact micro-second when the bird’s relay-trip overlaps with the *ratchet-tick* of a turning insect. In that double-fault, the creature plunges its long, spidery hands—the fingers are too numerous, like a bundle of feelers gauges—directly into the space where the bird’s chest *was* a trillionth of a second ago. It draws out a long, ropy skein of the pale wax, pulling it from the empty air the way a fisherman pulls gut from a split trout.

It packs this grease into the crevices of its own body, smearing it over the dry scree and dead moss that form its coat, pressing it into the vertical slot of its face. As it feeds, the yellow light deep inside its torso flares briefly to a dirty, incandescent orange, and from within its chest comes a sound that does not belong to the glade’s clean, industrial symphony: a wet, rattling wheeze, like an old bellows with a cracked leather lung, followed by the distinct, irregular *thump-thump... thump* of a heart that does not know how to keep time.

As they grow heavier with the harvested friction, they become more solid, more terribly *present*. 

There is one near the center of the marsh that has been here for a very long time. It has grown so dense with accumulated, un-happened hours that it can no longer walk on the water-squares. It has sunk to its knees in the shallow black pool, its lower legs buried in a mound of its own shedding—the only pile of silt and gravel in the entire razor-sharp amphitheater. It is the size of a haywain now, a slumped, shivering hillock of dead fern-mash, rusted iron scale, and yellow tallow, steaming faintly in the cold.

The closed loop of the glade is failing around this massive, kneeling thing. The blind silver eels that touch its sunken shins do not complete their figure-eights; they forgets their geometry, twitching into erratic, living curves before floating to the surface, belly-up and genuinely dead, their silver scales tarnishing to lead. The cooling-coil moss beneath its shadow has lost its sixty-cycle hum, rotting into a black, foul-smelling peat that bubbles with marsh gas instead of ozone. The piston-fern closest to it has developed a hitch in its four-stroke rhythm—*pump, pump, clatter, hiss*—its vulcanized fronds yellowing at the tips, infected by Tuesday, by Wednesday, by the slow, fatal contagion of *next*.

The other, thinner Klink-men gather around this kneeling elder when the light is at its flattest. They do not speak, and they do not touch one another, for their personal timelines are slightly out of phase, and if their hands were to brush, the skin of dead moss would shear away like wet paper. Instead, they stand in a loose circle on the shivering water-grid and tilt their slotted heads toward the elder’s belly.

Inside the great, slumped mass, behind a ribcage woven from calcified eel-bones and dead orchid-bladders, something is being incubated in the stolen heat of a million aborted seconds. You can see it dimly through the layers of tallow and scree: a small, irregular sphere of dull grey stone. 

A river cobble. Round, smooth, and entirely worn down. 

The elder shifts its immense, rotting weight, and the razor-sharp basalt beneath its knees gives a single, sharp *crack*—a sound that does not repeat, that does not loop back into silence, that hangs in the wet zinc air as an irreversible fact. A flake of black stone, the size of a thumbnail, shears off the cliff base and falls into the mud, and stays there.

The Klink-men tilt their visors up toward the dim silhouettes of the upper ledges, their internal lights pulsing in ragged, organic disharmony, waiting for the mountain to learn how to fall.
