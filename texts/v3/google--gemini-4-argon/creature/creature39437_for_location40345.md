At first, you would not know she was there because your hand would already be on her before you realized you had stopped touching the wall. 

The mason is no longer shaped entirely for open water. To live in a nine-foot box of packed cabbage-fronds and conflicting gravities requires a shedding of outline. She is roughly the volume of a ten-year-old child, if that child were made of cured kelp-leather, pale tallow, and heavy, cold mud. She does not swim through the thicket; she parts it like a flatworm moving between layers of wet slate, or like a slow, deliberate muscle flexing inside a dense abdomen.

She is the one who stacked the dry-stone wall, and she is still stacking it, though her sense of what a wall is for has drifted considerably since the rock split. 

If you were to catch a thread of the violet grease and smear it across her back—which she would allow, because she moves too slowly to be startled by anything that happens in less than an hour—you would see that she has a spine, and ribs, and two long, tremendously strong arms that end in broad, horn-nailed fingers. Her skin is the matte, unreflective black of a mussel shell, rubbed raw and silvery-white at the knuckles and elbows and cheekbones where the fresh basalt has nicked her. When the razor-sharp feldspar cuts her, she does not bleed into the water; the cold pressure of the cleft keeps her together, and a pale, fibrous sap—indistinguishable from the white root-lattices—slowly beads at the wound and binds the cut shut. In several places around her shoulders, she has accidentally let a liverwort or a segmented stalk heal *into* a scratch, and rather than pull it out, she simply carries the plant with her until its tension pulls her back to the wall it grows from.

Her face is mostly gone to business. She has no eyes—even if there were light enough to use them, the sharp leaves would have blinded her years ago—just two smooth, sunken hollows where the bone has grown over to protect the skull. Instead, her upper lip and chin are fringed with forty or fifty pale, nerveless feelers, thin as hair, which drift outward along the grain of the water. 

She uses them to read the room’s bad math.

She does not breathe the water; she drinks the heavy, mercury-dense brine that pools in the lower left corner around the slate shrine, holds it in a wide, muscular throat-sac until she has leached the dissolved stone out of it, and then coughs up a small, dry-sounding click of chalk that falls *sideways* toward the eastern wall. That is what she uses for mortar now.

Her entire existence is a quiet, stubborn argument with the geometry of the cistern. The cleft is broken, and she is trying to shim it level. 

That is what the bone cages are. She did not bring the whalebone or the smooth gray river stones from the upper world; she *fishes* for them. When a pale root loops out through the back of the water into that other, wider winter, she finds the loop by touch, hooks two horned fingers around the frost-stiffened root, and pulls her own arm up to the shoulder into the nowhere-space. It takes immense effort. Her muscles give off a low, creaking groan like ship’s timber as she reaches through the cold seam. She cannot survive on the other side of that hole—the winter there is too large, too fast, too full of sky—Wait long enough, though, and her hand comes back out through the root-loop holding a smooth gray pebble from a riverbed five hundred miles away and three thousand feet up, or a splinter of old bone from a beach that dried out before the basalt shelf even drowned.

She takes the river stone, wraps it in split bone and kelp to keep the local water from digesting its foreign weight, and ties it into the thicket wherever the gravity has gone crooked. She uses the upper-world stones as plummets. A river stone knows which way "down" is supposed to be; it remembers the sun and the straightforward pull of a simple dirt riverbank. By tying it into the sideways-growing cabbages, she forces the water around the cage to remember how to fall properly, at least for an inch or two. When the water turns into a faceted, square gel around the cages, that is the soundless friction of two different realities grinding their teeth together because she tied them into the same knot.

She is not a priest of the little slate shrine, though she built it. The shrine is a muffler. 

Down underneath the lowest flat slate, where the fault-crack pinches together into the deep crust, there is a place where the rock didn't just split—it *missed*. The two sides of the basalt fault failed to meet in the same Tuesday. The low, rhythmic *thrum* comes from down there. If it were left uncovered, the vibration would shake the water apart into dry gas and dry salt, and the little room would cease to be a room at all. So she stacked the slates over it, and when the *thrum* begins to work a slate loose, she shifts her dense, cold bulk down into the mercury-water, settles her broad, scarred chest over the shrine like a setting hen, and holds it down with her own weight until the rhythm settles.

She has an arrangement with the saucer-sized chiton and the six-foot needle-worm, though none of them would call it friendship. 

The chiton is her clock and her level. Because its organs rearrange themselves to match the geometry of her bone cages when it eats the violet grease, she occasionally reaches out with one slow hand and lays two fingers flat against its shell. She feels the spiral of its guts through the temporarily glassed-out calcium. If the spiral is tight and true, she knows her masonry is holding the room’s shape. If the creature’s organs have drifted into an oval or a jagged smear, she knows a root has pulled a wall out of true somewhere in the dark, and she begins the three-day crawl across the nine-foot room to find the fault and re-tie the cages.

As for the thread-worm, she uses it the way a blind tailor uses a needle that has a mind of its own. When she has laid a new stone into the four-course wall and wants it to stay, she waits—sometimes for a week, entirely motionless, letting the bruised-plum bladders bump against her flat skull—until she feels the worm passing through the meat of her own calf or forearm. It doesn't hurt; it feels like a single hair of ice being drawn through warm lard. Before the tail of the worm can clear her skin, she pinches it gently between two horn-nails and guides its blind head into the chink between the stones and the tough rind of a neighbor plant. The worm keeps going, stitching basalt to cabbage to mason’s thumb, before drawing itself free and leaving the three of them slightly more married to one another than they were before.

She has no plans to leave. If you were to somehow pry open the top of the crevice and let the ordinary ocean in, the sudden arrival of simple, single-minded gravity and honest salt water would probably kill her—she would come unglued, her body pulling apart in four directions at once as the little pocket’s compromises failed. She belongs entirely to the mistake in the rock, a slow, calloused thumb pressed hard against a leak in the dark.
