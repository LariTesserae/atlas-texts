You do not find them by looking for a shape. In a country where the trees are made of gristle and the stones sag like tallow in the warm seasons, a silhouette is a meaningless thing. You find them by the halos. 

You will be pulling yourself through a narrow, bruised-amber parlor of braided gas-willow, your palms skidding off the warm, greasy bark, when you notice a patch of water ahead that is slightly too clear. It is the size of a carriage, maybe larger, and the silt has been gently nudged out of it. Inside that clarity, the water has a grain—a faint, onion-skin layering of temperatures that doesn't match the surrounding broth. 

If you wait, and do not thrash, the clarity will slowly turn to look at you.

They have no name for themselves because they have no mouths to shape a vowel, and the water here is too thick to carry anything as sharp as a consonant anyway. If you had to categorize them, you would call them the *Seam-Keepers*, or the *Tallow-Men*, though there is nothing manlike about them anymore, except perhaps a lingering, hereditary nostalgia for the vertical.

At first glance, a Keeper appears to be a localized haunting of organs. They are colonial, or perhaps merely uncommitted to a single hide. Their outer boundary is a ten-foot shroud of clear, heavy mucilage—not a skin, precisely, but a private treaty with the water, a zone of thicker viscosity that they drag around with them like a winter coat. Suspended within this pale jelly are the parts that matter, floating at respectful distances from one another, tethered together by threads of dark, twitching nerve-silk no thicker than horsehair. 

Near the top of the shroud floats the buoyancy-liver: a three-lobed, mahogany-colored mass that smells faintly of iodine and old copper if it brushes your face. Below it hangs a chandelier of pale, lime-crusted struts—the remnants of a spine that gave up on being a column ten thousand generations ago and unknotted itself into a loose wind-chime of calcium. When the Keeper shifts its weight, these bones click against one another inside the jelly with a sound like dice thrown onto felt. 

They have no eyes. In a world where light bends around thermal corners and shows you your own heels fifty yards ahead, eyes are a liability; they only make you dizzy. Instead, the Keepers read the history of the water. Draped across the outside of their gelatinous mantles are hundreds of pale, feathery filaments—borrowed, perhaps, from the root-curtains of the bladder-ferns, or bred to mimic them. With these, they taste the "skin" of the old currents. They can feel a whorl of water that has been spinning in the lee of a tallow-mound for four hundred years, and they can read the mineral bruises left in it by a storm front that passed through last Tuesday, or the greasy wake of a bowel-mantle that glided by three months ago.

They do not swim. Swimming requires levering oneself against a medium, and the Keepers have been here too long to treat the water as an adversary. They travel by *adjusting their agreement* with the local density. 

The mahogany liver-mass squeezes, venting a thimbleful of warm, sulfurous gas into the surrounding jelly, or drinks a pint of heavy brine from a passing purple front, and the whole creature simply slides down the invisible banister of a thermal seam. They move the way oil moves in a hot pan: a sudden, lazy slither sideways, a long pause while their parts catch up to their intentions, and then a slow, rotating drift through a velvet curtain of moss-slime. Because their outer shroud has the exact refractive index of the clearing-halos, a moving Keeper looks like a lens being dragged through the forest, briefly magnifying the tongue-leaves and the bloated willow trunks behind it before letting them snap back to their normal, muddled proportions.

For a long time, the few wanderers who survived the Shelves believed the Keepers were merely another kind of big, slow jellyfish—an animal afterthought, like the light-grazing snails. 

Then someone watched them at the Webs.

The Webs are not dead relics. They are ongoing projects, and the Keepers are the masons. 

A Keeper does not build with hands; it builds by indigestion. They spend years drifting down near the basal whale-backs, where the ancient grey rock is softest, pressing their jelly-shrouds flat against the stone like immense slugs. For months at a time, a Keeper will lie plastered to a tallow-ridge, its internal bones spread out like a dropped hand of cards, sucking the dissolved mineral paste and the old, waterlogged stone-lattice into its mass. Within the dark, warm chambers of their floating organs, they refine this grey sludge, marrying it with the protein of digested iron-kelp and the silver mucus of the thermal-snails.

When a purple density-storm rolls in from the plateau’s heart—when the light bruises dark as a plum and the hanging forests groan and sink under the crushing weight of the hot fronts—the Keepers rise. 

They do not fight the inverted gravity of the storm's wake; they ride the rising melon-spores up into the middle depths, congregating around the great, basket-woven astrolabes of the Webs. Five or six of them will merge their outer shrouds around a single junction of bone-white strut, pooling their jellies until they form a single, twenty-foot globule of trembling, amber-lit lens. Inside this shared room of mucus, their internal nerve-silks braid together, and their scattered bones align into a temporary, rhythmic loom.

They extrude the new tendon and the hot, buzzing calcium directly from their vents, smearing it along the old framework, smoothing it with the insides of their mantles. You can feel them doing it from half a mile away—a high, glassy *zing* that cuts right through the bass *flump* of tearing vegetation, setting your molars on edge. 

Why they do it is a question that only makes sense if you still believe in floors.

Watch a Keeper when the repair is finished and the Web wakes up. When the local water snaps clear, and the surrounding kelp shrinks back as if burned, and that terrible, ancient ghost of *Down* suddenly spears through the center of the bone-cage—the Keepers do not flee it. 

They stay inside the cage. And as the true gravity grabs them, a horrifying, poignant thing happens inside their clear shrouds. 

The loose wind-chime of their scattered bones is yanked downward. The disconnected ribs, the smoothed-out pelvises, the small, pebble-like phalanges that haven't been fingers since the mountains had peaks—they all fall toward the bottom of the jelly-sack. Held back only by their dark threads of nerve-silk, the bones rattle together and lock, forced by the sudden weight into a crude, trembling parody of a standing skeleton. The mahogany liver drops into the basket of the ribs; the sensory filaments stream upward like hair in a gale. 

For three minutes, or five, while the Web holds its alignment against the tired physics of the plateau, the Keepers hang there in the crystal-clear water, stretched taut, vibrating like plucked harp strings. They look like ghosts trying to remember how to stand up in a room where the floor was stolen a million years ago. It looks like agony. Their jellies cloud with milky, exhausted lactic secretions; their nerve-silks snap like over-tightened fiddle strings.

Then the sideways logic of the Shelves washes back in. The dreadful sharpness of *Down* dissolves into the warm, brown murk. 

The bones unhook with a soft, muffled clatter. The spine drifts apart into a constellation of white commas; the liver floats back up toward the ceiling-mist. The shared shroud splits back into five or six separate lenses, which wobble away into the pulsing, cartilaginous colonnades of the gas-trees to rest and mend their snapped threads.

They do not use the Webs to live in. They do not use them to hunt. They build them, and keep them warm, and string them between the waxen currents for the same reason a man in a dark room might occasionally strike a match—not because he wants to go anywhere, and not because he likes the burn on his fingers, but simply to remind himself, for one blinding, miserable second, that he has edges, and that there used to be a direction called *fall*.

If you meet one in the green-lit rooms between the storms, it will not harm you. It has nothing to bite with, and you are far too bony and impatient to be good eating. 

Instead, it will often drift closer, fascinated by the sheer, stubborn compactness of you. It will wrap you in its clearing-halo, pushing the murk away so that for a few moments you are both suspended in a private bubble of clean, amber water. It may brush its feathery root-filaments across your cheek, tasting the strange, sharp salt of someone who still belongs to the lowlands. 

And sometimes, if it is an old Keeper—one whose jelly has grown tough and yellowed as horn, and whose floating bones are pitted like coral—it will gently bump its shroud against your chest and nudge you sideways. Not toward the ceiling-mist, where the fish hang perplexed in the hot fog, and not toward the tallow-floor, where the stones slump downhill like clay. It will nudge you down a corridor of bladder-fern that isn't lengthening, through a thermal seam where the silver snail-tinsel parts like a bead curtain, toward the nearest Web. 

It thinks you are lost, you see. It can feel the heavy, frantic thud of your heart trying to pump blood against a gravity that isn't there, and in its slow, dispersed, liquid way, it is offering you a place to go and be heavy for a little while.
