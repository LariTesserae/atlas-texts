The silt does not stay where it falls. 

By the laws of the swell, the grey ash should collect in the lee of the chest-high walls and stay there, banking up in smooth, undisturbed drifts until the terraces are leveled into a single sloping plain. Yet, if an eye were down here to look closely at the wheel-ruts in the basalt paving, or the square-cut drainage channels that run beneath the choked sluice-gates, it would find them swept clean. The grey mucilage has been gathered up, rolled into dense, heavy bolsters the thickness of a man’s thigh, and stacked along the tops of the retaining walls like sandbags.

Something is keeping the ditches clear.

Look into the lee of a half-collapsed lintel, where the blood-red dulse grows thickest. When the great, twenty-second heave of the ocean pushes the kelp forest to the east, everything bends with it. The laminaria leans, the sea-lettuce flattens, the tallow-colored sponges compress slightly under the weight of the water. 

Wait for the pause at the end of the swing. Wait for the twenty-second haul back to the west.

Everything leans back—except for one knotted mass of dark brown wrack and grey silt at the base of the wall. It stays leaned to the east, holding its shape against the reversing tonnages of water. Then, three seconds too late, as if remembering a cue, it slowly tips to the west, moving not with the water, but through it.

They have no bone, for the water dissolves bone. They have no shell, for there is no lime to build one. What lives here is an architecture of scavenged tension and cold slime.

At its core, one of these things is a lattice of dead, rubbery kelp-stipes—the tough, wrist-thick stems that take thirty years to rot in the freezing dark—lashed together and animated by a sprawling, yellowish-grey plasmodium. Whether this pale, sinewy jelly evolved from the breadcrumb sponges or crept up from the abyssal trenches when the builders’ fires went out, it is impossible to say. It has no brain, no eyes, and no stomach. It is a vast, cold-water amoeboid that has learned to wear the dead jungle like a coat of muscle and cartilage.

To hold itself down in the ceaseless pendulum of the swell, it stuffs its central cavity with fist-sized chunks of quarried shale and rounded basalt cobbles pried from the floors. These stones are its ballast, suspended in a thick, clear mesoglea inside its body. When it moves, the stones shift and grind against one another inside the jelly, muted and heavy, like rocks in a leather sack.

Its exterior is a shaggy, horrifyingly patient arrangement of living and dead flora. It stitches the severed holdfasts of giant laminaria onto its own tougher rinds; the pale, knuckle-like roots do not know they are no longer on stone, and they grip the creature’s tough outer tunic with blind, chemical ferocity, growing a living pelt of ten-foot brown ribbons that trail from its back. To the sourceless indigo gloom, the thing is merely a boulder overgrown with weed, or a tangled windrow of storm-torn kelp caught in an eddy.

It movesonly during the *slack*—that three-second breath between the east-heave and the west-heave when the millions of tons of water hang motionless before reversing.

In that brief silence, when the leathery sighing of the forest stops, a bundle of kelp-stems at the corner of a sunken courtyard will suddenly uncoil. Pale, translucent pseudopods, thick as garden hoses and Tough as gristle, extrude from beneath a fringe of black Irish moss. They reach out, find the mortarless cracks between the basalt paving stones, and *pull*. 

The bulk of the thing slides forward two feet along the ancient road. The stones inside its belly *clack-clack* softly. Then the water begins to press back from the west; the creature instantly drops flat, locks its borrowed holdfasts into the crevices, lets its banner of living kelp stream out over it in the current, and becomes a hillock of weed again for the next twenty seconds.

They are everywhere, once you know how to read the geometry of the weeds. 

There are small ones, the size of sheep, that live entirely inside the square-cut drainage gutters, shaped like long cylinders of rolled grey felt and red dulse, pushing themselves back and forth like pistons to clear the silt. They eat the marine snow. As they haul themselves along the stone, their undersides—a pale, glistening sole of mucous membrane—strip the grey ash and the pale velvet of bryozoans from the basalt, digesting the dead plankton and the rotting sugars of the fallen kelp externally, leaving a clean, dark smear of wet stone behind them.

And there are the great ones, the things that occupy the forty-foot-wide avenues and the circular storage pits.

These are not individual creatures so much as ambulatory thickets—forty or fifty yards of fallen, leather-like kelp-pillars bound together by acres of tough, tallow-colored sponge-flesh, ballasted with tons of ancient wall-stone. They take a week to cross a single plaza. As they creep, they graze the tops of the dikes, enveloping the gas-bladders of the standing bull kelp and dissolving them in slow-acting acids, using the buoyancy of the captured bladders to lift their own immense, stone-bellied weight just an inch off the pavement so they can drift with the twenty-second heave.

The eerie, ruler-straight geometry of the underwater jungle is their doing.

The builders laid the foundations five thousand years ago, yes—the terraces, the retaining walls, the causeways. But basalt shifts. Holdfasts wedge stones apart; walls tumble; silt buries. Left to the blind mechanics of the ocean, this continent would have been reduced to a chaotic rubble-field of indistinguishable mounds three millennia ago.

The weavers do not let the walls fall. 

Why they do it is not a question of intelligence; they have no minds to revere the vanished builders, nor eyes to admire the straightness of a canal. It is a matter of hydrodynamics and food. A straight channel funnels the twenty-second swell into a predictable piston-stroke, concentrating the drifting marine snow into neat, rich windrows along the base of the walls. A tumbled wall breaks the current into chaotic eddies that scatter the ash and tear at the weavers’ stitched-together bodies. 

A straight wall feeds them. A ruined wall starves them.

And so, in the freezing indigo dark, a blind, brainless maintenance has been going on for four thousand years.

When a wedging kelp-root finally pries a fifty-pound block of basalt loose from a retaining wall, and it drops into the gutter with that dull, muffled *thud* that travels for miles through the dense water, the silence that follows is not empty. The vibration ripples through the stone-filled bellies of a hundred weavers within a half-mile radius.

Over the next six days, moving two feet every twenty seconds in the slack between the swells, three or four of the mid-sized mounds will converge on the breach. 

They do not communicate, except by the chemical taste of the slime they leave on the paving stones and the slow, grinding *clack* of their internal ballast. They gather at the base of the damaged wall like a drift of rotting timber. For hours, while the forty-foot forest bows east and bows west above them, they simply pile atop one another, merging their pale, gristly inner jellies until they form a single muscular ramp against the masonry.

Then, using the gas-bladders of living kelp they have dragged down from the canopy and lashed to the fallen stone with threads of toughened sponge-spicule, they lighten the block. Slowly, over the course of a hundred swells, the composite mass of weed and slime heaves the basalt back up into the gap. 

They do not set it with the dry-stone precision of the horn-pick builders. They mortar it in place with their own dead—packing the joint with old, calcified sponge-skeletons, grey silt compressed into clay-like bricks, and the tough, woody holdfasts of strangled laminaria. If the stone is too rounded by the currents to sit still, a weaver will simply crawl into the gap beneath it, wedge its own stone-filled belly into the masonry, and die there, letting its tough, fibrous tunic petrify in the cold water into a rubbery gasket that will hold the wall level for another century.

Half the walls in the three-hundred-mile city are no longer built of stone at all. If you were to slice into a chest-high dike along one of the grand causeways, your blade would sink six inches into a tough, leathery rind of compressed kelp-hide, ancient sponge-gristle, and compacted grey ash before it struck the original basalt core. The architecture is being slowly replaced by the things that maintain it—a petrifying scar-tissue of cold-water meat and vegetable fiber that holds the exact shape of the human masonry it has swallowed.

Even the solitary, peeled-grape anemones in the carved niches are part of their husbandry. The weavers never graze over the niches. When a creeping mound passes a doorway, a long, whip-like tendril of pale jelly will flick out from the mass of dulse and deposit a neat pellet of undigested silt and bruised bryozoans directly into the anemone’s waiting crown of tentacles. In return, when the anemone spawns or sheds its stinging mucus in the winter dark, the weavers crawl over the threshold and rub their backs against the niche, coating their dead-kelp pelts in borrowed nematocysts to ward off the only predator that ever enters the city: the rare, blind sleeper-sharks that lose their way in the upper fathoms and sink, starving and torpid, into the indigo streets.

When one of those great, grey sharks drifts down into the avenues, its tail beating once a minute in the near-freezing water, the city does not flee. 

The sighing of the kelp goes on. The hollow bulbs *clonk* together in the gloom. The shark’s belly scrapes over a low wall that isn't a wall. A hundred yards of sunken courtyard—tons of red dulse, gravel-filled jelly, and knotted brown ribbons—simply rises up on the next east-bound swell, folds over the sluggish fish like a wet tarp, and pins it to the basalt cobbles. 

There is no thrashing, only a muffled *thump* that rolls away down the terraced basins. The shark does not rot; it is too cold to rot. Over the next three years, a hillock of waving laminaria sits permanently in the middle of the avenue, bowing east, bowing west with the rest of the forest, while underneath its skirts, the pale, cold plasmodium slowly turns the shark’s cartilage and liver-oil into more grey gristle, more muscle, more mortar for the walls.

When nothing is left of the fish, the hillock divides into six smaller bundles of weed. During the three-second slack, they slide apart, dropping back into the wheel-ruts and the drainage trenches, and go back to sweeping the ash from the streets of a city whose makers they have completely forgotten, and whose shape they will never let die.
