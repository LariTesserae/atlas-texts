If you stand for three hours at the base of the western perimeter wall, where the great copper viaducts emerge from the foundry plinths and stride out across the moss, you will think you are looking at a buttress of cast iron and pale lichen. In the fourth hour, a section of the iron and a section of the lichen will come apart from the wall as a single unit, drop four feet into the bog with a sound like a sack of wet chain, and begin to walk along the pipeline.

They have no name for themselves because they have no throats with which to shape a vowel, and if they had throats, the harmonic roar of the Municipality would strip the sound from their lips before it crossed an inch of air. They are the **Tenders**, or the **Gasket-Men**, or the **Slow**. 

To look at one is to look at a compromise negotiated over centuries between a heavy-ordinance foundry and a peat bog. A Tender stands roughly seven feet tall, though it never stands upright; it is permanently bowed forward at a forty-degree angle, leaning into the unending twelve-mile-per-hour western wind even when it is walking east, its posture set in the cast before it ever left the dark. Its frame is undeniably bipedal—two thick, pillar-like legs, a broad barrel of a torso, two long arms that hang below the knee—but it has no skin. In place of hide, it is encased in an overlapping plates of dull, unrusting black scale that has the exact crystalline fracture and matte sheen of the newly split basalt of the Shelf. Where the joints meet at the knee, hip, and shoulder, there is no cartilage; there are massive, weeping cushions of dense, silver-blue bryophyte that act as hydraulic pads, whistling softly as they compress and expel black water with every ponderous step.

They do not have faces. Where a head should be, the sloped iron-and-stone carapace simply rises to a blunt, blind prow, like the diving helmet of an ancient salvage suit before the viewports have been cut into the brass. Across this blank dome grows a thick, weeping curtain of pale grey lichen that hangs down to the creature’s chest, constantly dripping with condensed mist.

The Shelf has no animals because the air is too thin to burn sugars quickly and the world is too loud for nerves that rely on quiet. The Tenders live here because they do not breathe the air and they do not listen for changes in the sound. They live *on* the sound. 

Deep inside a Tender’s broad, barrel-like chest is a hollow cavity lined with thousands of stiff, vulcanized plates, structured like the gills of a mushroom or the leaves of a condenser. When a Tender stands near a Municipality—or walks beneath the great ten-foot copper pipelines that bridge the forty-mile gaps between them—the deafening, rhythmic *thud-thud-thud* of the cathedral-sized drop-forges and the continuous bass rumble of the subterranean turbines pass through its dense outer shell and set those internal plates vibrating in sympathetic resonance. This constant, violent mechanical vibration is what pumps their thick, milky, mineral-heavy blood through their veins. They have no hearts. The city is their heart. If a Tender were to wander twenty miles north or south of the pipeline corridor, out into the deep, silent tundra where the roar of the Municipalities fades to a flat hum in the fog, its internal gills would still, its white blood would settle like wet chalk in its limbs, and it would simply stop in the knee-deep moss, standing forever like a cairn of black rock until the creeping rhizomes swallowed it whole.

They are born—if that is the word—in the scalding, thirty-foot mounds of monstrous moss that swell against the lower foundry walls where the cast-iron gutters spill warm, copper-tainted runoff onto the basalt. 

In the dark, steaming interiors of these thirty-foot vegetative tumors, the heat of the iron walls and the relentless vibration of the engines on the other side of the rivets do strange work upon the spores. The moss does not decay, so as it grows outward, the deep interior of the mound is subjected to immense, suffocating pressure. Here, leached iron from the gutters, dissolved basalt dust, and the rubbery, cartilaginous roots of the giant cabbage-rosettes are kneaded together by the sixty-cycle hum of the turbines. Over decades, nodes of black stone and heavy metal slag are wrapped in vascular plant-flesh, pressed into the rough, bifurcated symmetry of the iron inspection-doors and bolted flanges against which the moss is crushed. When a mound grows too heavy even for its own rubbery structure, it splits open near the base with a slow, wet tearing sound, and a Tender slides out into the perpetual Tuesday-morning light, slick with warm mineral grease, its stone plates still soft as leather before the freezing wind hardens them.

Their life is an endless, glacial patrol of the static machine.

They move at roughly half a mile an hour. There is no hurry; the sky will never darken, winter will never worsen, and the twelve-mile-per-hour wind will never rise to a gale. A Tender will spend six days climbing the terraced concrete plinths of a Municipality, its heavy, three-toed feet finding purchase on the stamped steel avenues where no vehicle has ever rolled. 

When it reaches the base of one of the great brass relief-valves—where the sixty-foot sculptures of roaring white steam stand frozen against the wind—the Tender stops. It leans its blind, lichen-bearded prow directly into the edges of the scalding vapor. It stays there for weeks. The heat of the steam warms its core, while the mineral-rich condensation washes down through its weeping facial lichens, feeding the bryophyte pads in its joints and depositing fresh layers of brass-salt and lime onto its carapace. 

While it stands there, it performs its only work, though whether this work is instinctive, religious, or merely a physical tic caused by the acoustic frequencies of the buildings, it is impossible to say.

With its two great, blunt-fingered hands—which are shaped remarkably like spanners that have been overgrown with stiff, woody rhizomes—the Tender reaches out and touches the trembling iron casing of the ziggurat. Its stone fingertips press against the giant hex-bolts that hold the city’s roaring guts inside. Through its arms, it feels the internal violence of the foundry: the shriek of the steam, the titanic drop of the hammers. 

If a bolt has vibrated even a millimeter loose over the course of a century, the pitch of that specific plate changes by a fraction of a hertz. To the Tender, whose entire anatomy is an ear and a tuning fork, this microscopic dissonance is an intolerable itch. It will hook its thick, root-wrapped fingers around the cold iron nut and lean its seven-hundred-pound bulk against it, applying a slow, hydraulic pressure over the course of three days until the bolt turns that single millimeter back into place, the harmonic roar of the wall returns to its immaculate, deafening unison, and the itch subsides.

Along the forty-mile pipelines that cross the Shelf, you can see them spaced out like beads on an abacus. They walk beneath the ten-foot copper tubes on the narrow gravel maintenance paths that were laid down when the world was made, or they walk directly on top of the pipes, sixty feet in the air, their wide feet gripping the tarnished green metal. 

Where a pipeline joint sweats hot, pressurized oil or mineral water, three or four Tenders will gather. They do not fight over the leak, and they do not communicate. They simply press their chests and shoulders together around the pipe, forming a living collar of stone, moss, and tough rubbery tissue that seals the weep-hole. They will hold that embrace for a year, or ten years, drinking the hot leakage through their mosses while their bodies fuse slightly at the edges, their lichens braiding together in the steady west wind. Eventually, the mineral deposits inside the pipe will calcify against their chests, sealing the leak from within; only then will the Tenders slowly wrench themselves apart, tearing their shared moss with a dull *ripping* sound that is instantly swallowed by the roar of the distant cities, and resume their walk.

Sometimes, along the sheer ten-thousand-foot drops where the continent was sheared in half, a pipeline simply ends—snapped off at the cliff’s edge, pouring a continuous, roaring cataract of boiling water and live steam out into the pale void below.

The Tenders gather at these precipices in dozens. They stand shoulder to shoulder right at the razor-sharp lip of the black basalt, their grey beards of lichen blown back against their throats by the updraft, looking out with their blind, iron-prowed heads into fifty miles of empty, bright mist. They do not fall, and they do not turn back for a very long time. They simply stand at the edge of the world’s fracture, vibrating softly in time with the engines behind them, waiting for the rest of the pipe to be built across the sky so they can walk out upon it and tighten its bolts.
