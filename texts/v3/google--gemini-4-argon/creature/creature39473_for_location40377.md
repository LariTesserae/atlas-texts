To understand what lives here now, you must first understand what the builders left behind in the soapstone bowls. 

The people who crawled through the eighty-foot basalt tube—skinning their elbows, breathing through wet moss to cool their lungs—did not come here to hide treasure, nor did they come to worship. They came because the mountain outside is a place of violent, fracturing cold, where winter starves the marrow and summer is a brief, windy thaw. In that high, thin air, things break, or they rot. 

Down here, nothing rots, and nothing freezes. 

So they used the blister as a womb and an archive. Into the high, calcified willow cradles, they placed the things that needed to be kept alive through the long freezes: starter-cakes of wild yeast wrapped in greased bladder; seeds of alpine tubers that would burst if frozen; the precious, rendered fat of marmot and ibex. And into the lower basins, on the submerged trellises of horn and bone, they laid the things they wanted the mountain to finish making for them: green-wood spear shafts that needed to be cured in silica until they were heavy as iron, and the small, clean bones of their dead, which they wished to see dressed in milky chalcedony so they would never turn to dust.

Then, six hundred years ago, a minor tremor shifted the cooling tube forty feet from the entrance. A slab of the plateau’s flank settled by four inches. The throat of the passage pinched shut, and the crawlers never came back.

The blister did not notice. The warmth held. The water kept sweating from the pores of the roof. And the things in the bowls began to wake up, mingle, and look for somewhere to go.

At first, the new inhabitant looks merely like an overflow of the tallow-cakes and yeast left in the upper baskets. If you were to bring a tallow wick into the thicket—which would sputter horribly in the wet air—you would see pale, translucent aprons of jelly draping down from the willow cradles, bridging the two-inch gaps between the black glass needles. It looks like spilled candle-wax that has refused to harden, or the heavy mother-of-vinegar that forms in the bottom of a dark cask.

It is a colony—a chimera born of the crawlers' provisions and the deep-earth extremophiles already native to the rock. The ancient yeasts, starved of grain, learned to marry the sulfur-eating bacteria on the basalt and the blind, root-fused flora of the floor. 

They call nothing anything here, because there are no mouths, let alone words. Every piece of the inhabitant is deaf and blind, yet the room is entirely occupied by a single, distributed attention. 

The body of this thing is the *Film*. It is a slick, viscous mantle of golden-white polysaccharide and mycelial thread that has slowly, over centuries, replaced the simple condensation on the central pillars. It does not grow over everything—it is too sparse, too careful with its energy for that. Instead, it hangs in the narrow alcoves like wet cheesecloth, strung between the razor-sharp tachylyte hairs. It uses the finest of the black glass threads as an endoskeleton, wrapping them in slick, living amber so they do not snap. 

If you were to touch it, it would not flinch. It is the exact temperature of your own blood, so your fingertips would not register it as a separate object at first—only a sudden thickening of the air, like dipping your hand into warm egg white. 

It feeds on the slow rain of minerals and the ancient, cured fats that still leach, molecule by molecule, out of the stone-crusted baskets above. In turn, it tends the room.

This is the strange part: the colony has inherited the architecture of the makers, and it maintains it. 

The sinew cords tied across the alcoves should have dissolved into jelly five centuries ago, even in the sterile heat. They haven't. The Film has crept along the sinew, digesting the rotting animal protein bit by bit and replacing it with a tough, flexible chitin of its own making, spun out of silica and sulfur-chains. The cords are still taut. The hollow bird bones and clay beads are still suspended in the dark, though they are now held aloft by ropes of tough, living cartilege that pulse, once every few hours, with a slow peristalsis that drives nutrient-broth from one alcove to the next.

Within this curtain of living mucus lives the second part of the resident—the part that moves.

The long, segmented creature you might feel stepping across the cords is not an outsider; it is the Film’s hand. Originally, they were blind, pale millipedes and bristletails that rode in on the woven lake-rushes inside the crawlers' pouches, trapped when the rock settled. Over three thousand generations in the absolute dark, breathing air thick as soup, they have made a pact with the Film.

They have grown large—some the length of a man's forearm, though no thicker than a thumb—and their shells have lost all pigment, becoming as clear as blown glass. Through their backs, you can see the faint, chalky thread of their gut and the slow, hydraulic pump of their clear blood. Their legs—hundreds of them—do not end in claws, which would catch and tear on the razor-sharp basalt hairs, but in tiny, cupped pads that secrete a drop of the Film’s own slick oil. They walk on the edges of volcanic razors without ever cutting themselves, gliding upside down along the ceiling threads or vertically down the thigh-thick pillars.

These are the *Gleaners*. They have no eyes, not even the vestigial pits of cave-beetles. Instead, their front pairs of legs and their antennae have lengthened into whips three times the length of their bodies, spun as fine as the stone hair around them. 

A Gleaner does not hunt. There is nothing to hunt. It is a plumber, a mason, and a farmer. 

For ten hours at a time, a Gleaner will hang motionless between two pillars, its four long whips cast out into the dark, resting lightly on the sinew cords and the rims of the soapstone bowls. It is listening to the *plop* of the water. 

In a room where nothing changes, the rhythm of the drips is the only clock and the only map. When a silica-heavy drop falls from a glass needle into a bowl, the vibration travels down the calcified willow cradle, into the basalt column, along the chitin-reinforced cord, and into the Gleaner’s whip. 

By the pitch and resonance of that tiny *plop*, the Gleaner knows if a bowl is brimming, or if a mineral crust has begun to dam the lip, threatening to divert the precious, sweet water away from the pale, waxen stalks waiting in the basin below. 

When a drip goes wrong—when the interval shifts from twenty heartbeats to thirty, or the sound dulls because the water is striking stone instead of water—the Gleaner stirs. It unhooks its padded feet and flows through the black glass thicket, never touching a sharp edge, reading the ancient clay beads and bird-bone knots with its whips just as the blind crawlers once did with their fingers. 

When it reaches the choked bowl, it uses its heavy, mineral-hardened mandibles—tipped with black iron sulfide it has chewed out of the rock—to carefully nip away the excess chalcedony crust. It clears the channel. It waits until the *plop* returns to its proper, hollow note. Then it crawls down to the Film, presses its mouthparts into the warm, gelatinous curtain, and regurgitates the chipped stone and iron paste into the colony’s web, which uses the grit to anchor itself tighter to the glass. In return, the Film feeds the Gleaner a drop of sweet, fermented tallow-sugar it has been hoarding for five hundred years.

Down in the floor basins, where the horn-and-bone trellises sit submerged, something else has happened. 

The crawlers left bone down there to turn to stone. The silica water did its work, wrapping the rib-cages of mountain sheep and the femurs of old shamans in thick, bulbous rinds of milk-white chalcedony. 

The blind, waxen plants—the ones that look like blanched asparagus—found these submerged bones centuries ago. Their roots, designed to dissolve young basalt with strong organic acids, found the calcium phosphate of the old bones much easier meat. They bored through the milky stone crusts and hollowed the bones out from the inside, replacing the marrow with their own dense, woody vascular bundles.

Now, the trellises are no longer dead frameworks. They are a single, submerged root-brain. The white stalks that thrust up into the steam are merely its fingers, splitting open at the tips to spread their wet, fibrous nets. 

When a blind cave limpet—pale as a thumbnail—glides up a stalk to graze on the sulfur-film, the stalk does not mind. It lets the limpet climb all the way to the open, fibrous net at the top. There, the plant exudes a single pearl of concentrated mineral-salt, sweeter than the surrounding water. The limpet stays for days, rasping happily at the salt, while its heavy shell collects the warm condensation from the air, funneling the water straight down into the plant’s throat far more efficiently than the fibrous net could manage alone. When the limpet grows old and its grip finalmente fails, it drops straight into the cleft of the stalk, which closes over it over the course of a week, digesting the shell to build new stone-roots below.

Nothing here is separate anymore. 

If you were to squeeze your ribs between the warm pillars—ignoring the glass hairs biting through your shirt, breathing the wet, broth-thick air—and reach your hand into one of the cleared kneeling-alcoves, you would find the floor of the alcove smooth. The jagged basalt plates have been carpeted in a three-inch-thick mat of shed Gleaner skins, limpet shells, and calcified moss, all bound together by the amber Film into something that feels remarkably like warm, wet leather.

And if you knelt there in the pitch black, as the makers once did, and laid your fingertips on the taut cord in front of your chest, you would feel a faint, dry *tick-tick-tick* against your knuckles. 

Four whips, fine as cobwebs, would brush across your skin. They would not recoil. To the Gleaner, you are simply a very large, very warm pillar that has suddenly risen from the floor, sweating a fascinating new kind of salt. 

Within an hour, if you did not move, you would feel the feather-light touch of padded feet stepping onto your shoulder. You would hear the tiny, surgical *click* of iron-tipped mandibles grooming the dead skin from your neck, and feel the slow, warm smear of the Film being laid across the scratches the basalt glass left on your arms—sealing you, preserving you, and knitting you into the architecture of the room, so that you, too, might never rot, and never cool, and help catch the water when it falls.
