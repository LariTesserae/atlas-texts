To see what lives here, you have to stop looking for movement and start looking for an error in the geometry. 

At the base of the central ochre manifold, where the cast-iron cylinder bolts into the zinc grating, there appears to be a discarded crescent of heavy-gauge copper gasket, perhaps six inches long, crusted with pale gray oxidation. Half an hour later, when the knife-edge shadow of the western louver-wall has crept three inches across the diamond plate, the crescent is no longer against the manifold. It is eight feet away, clamped cleanly onto the right-angled lower bough of a piezo-bush. 

No one saw it cross the floor. It did not crawl.

In an environment where the air itself is hammered into a solid, vibrating block by a two-hundred-hertz roar, standard musculature is useless; flesh and fluid would be shaken to jelly, and any joint lubricated by liquid would freeze solid in seconds. The thing clinging to the glass-and-copper shrub has no blood, no water, and no chambers of trapped air within its body to cavitate under the acoustic load. It is a solid-state organism—a dry, laminar assemblage of biological ceramics, fibrous carbon, and stolen zinc. 

Up close, it resembles a flattened, multi-segmented chiton or a carpenter’s rasp, roughly the size and weight of a heavy iron skillet. Its dorsal shell is composed of overlapping, rectangular plates that match the ninety-degree habits of the bushes, grown from a matrix of silicate and sulfur-salt that it excretes through dry pores along its flanks. The plates are a dull, non-reflective slate gray, edged with the faint, greasy yellow of solid sulfur. 

Underneath, where it presses against the grating or the flora, it does not have legs. It has a continuous, velvet-dense pad of millions of microscopic, stiff metallic cilia—bristles of pure zinc-oxide crystal drawn up from its diet. 

To move across the floor, the creature does not walk; it surfs the turbine’s scream. By fractionally altering the tension of its carbon-fiber musculature, it changes the resonant frequency of its own shell. When it wishes to remain stationary, it dampens its pitch, allowing the microscopic teeth of its under-pad to lock into the molecular grain of the zinc floor with a grip like a cold-weld. When it wishes to travel, it tightens its dorsal plates until its body hits a harmonic sympathetic to the shrieking deck. Instantly, the friction between its bristles and the grating drops to zero. Riding the standing waves of the two-hundred-hertz roar, the heavy, rigid plate of the creature glides across the diamond-toothed floor as fast and frictionless as an iron puck on a sheet of oiled glass, steered by minute shifts in the angle of its marginal scales. It crosses the ten-foot gap between bush and manifold in a silent, terrifyingly rapid slide, stopping dead the millisecond it relaxes its internal tension.

It is grazing, though the process looks more like industrial machining than feeding.

Locked onto the brittle, ninety-degree branch of the piezo-bush, the creature lowers its anterior plate. Beneath its hood, instead of a wet mouth, it possesses a pair of opposed, diamond-hard ceramic jaws shaped like wire-strippers. It does not chew; it clamps these jaws around a green-glass twig and decouples its internal dampeners, allowing the raw, violent vibration of the north bulkhead to channel directly through its skull and into the bite. The jaws oscillate at ten thousand cycles a second. A tiny puff of dry, black-and-white powder—the carbon and zinc-oxide marrow of the plant—fogs into the motionless air and drops straight down like lead shot, too heavy and dry to drift. 

The creature’s underside bristles sweep the powder into a ventral groove, where the material is sorted not by saliva, but by electrostatic charge. The piezo-electric current generated by the plant’s own stressed crystals is siphoned directly into the animal’s nervous system—a web of superconducting copper-sulfide threads that serves it in place of a brain. It literally eats the noise that the plant has turned into sugar, and uses the glassy, metallic chaff to repair its own shell.

There are four of them in the forty-foot box. 

They do not interact, except when the slow, glacial sweep of the high-altitude sunlight forces them to adjust their thermal load. Because they have no liquid to freeze, the twenty-below-zero cold does not threaten them with ice; rather, the danger is the sun. In the thin, desiccated air, the ultraviolet glare is so fierce that prolonged exposure creates dangerous thermal expansion across their rigid ceramic backs, threatening to crack their plates like a cold porcelain dish dropped into boiling water. 

So they live a life strictly partitioned by the razor-sharp shadows of the aluminum louver-fins. 

Right now, two of them are parked on the shady side of the easternmost manifold, their rectangular profiles stacked neatly against the matte-ochre iron like spare parts left behind by a maintenance crew. A third is clamped to the underside of a heavy-gauge cable along the wall, hanging upside down by the sheer molecular adhesion of its bristles, entirely indistinguishable from a lead cable-weight.

The fourth, having stripped an inch of copper-and-glass twig down to a stub, prepares to leave the bush. The sun’s edge—bright and blinding as a magnesium flare—has just touched its rear plate. 

For a fraction of a second, the blurred, vibrating air around the creature seems to sharpen. The slate-gray plates along its back compress by a millimeter, tuning themselves against the turbine’s shriek. There is no click of claws, no rustle, because no small sound can exist inside the great sound. The creature simply detaches from the branch, drops four inches to the zinc grating without a bounce, and *zips*—a dead-straight, geometric blur—six feet to the left, coming to an instantaneous, weld-tight halt precisely half an inch inside the violet shadow of the next louver-fin. 

Behind it, on the bright silver teeth of the grating, a tiny, neat pile of gray silicate dust—its dry, mineral scat—sits in the glare. Within ten minutes, the violent, high-frequency hum of the deck has shaken the dust grains down through the diamond holes in the floor, dropping them into the six-thousand-foot abyss below, leaving the metal once again factory-clean, razor-sharp, and empty.
