If you watch the middle turbine shaft—the one that still turns at its lazy four revolutions per minute—you will eventually notice that the shadow on its leeward side does not move with the light. It moves against the rotation, inching sideways at the exact speed required to remain perpetually in the dead-water pocket behind the column.

At first, it looks like a sloughed mat of maintenance grease and harbor felt, or perhaps a discarded leather diver’s apron caught on the graphitic iron. It is roughly the size and shape of a hearth rug, flattened and broad, with a scalloped, muscular margin that ripples in a slow, continuous sine wave to hold its position against the drift. 

The harbor seal knows what it is. When the seal slips through the broken east grate during the three-minute calm, it will hunt the congers in the beam-sockets and scatter the smelt, but it gives the central shaft a wide, respectful berth. Its dark eyes roll back in its head to keep the rotating column in view as it passes.

This is the Sump-Back, though nothing with a voice has ever named it. If it has ancestors out in the cold Atlantic beyond the harbor mouth, they were likely skates or monkfish, or perhaps some deep-shelf octopod, though the Sump-Back has spent so many generations inside the condenser plant’s intake manifolds that the chamber has remodeled it entirely. It has no bone left in it, only a dense, heavy matrix of grisly cartilage that allows it to pour itself through the four-inch gaps of the sluice grates if it wants to—though it never does. The chamber provides everything.

Its skin is a masterwork of industrial mimicry. The dorsal side is thick, granular, and completely devoid of slime, matching the velvety, carbonized rind of the decayed cast-iron shafts. It is the exact non-color of wet graphite, mottled with rusty ochre patches and pale, calcified lumps that look indistinguishable from stunted barnacles and patches of dead red crustose algae. When it presses itself flat against the concrete tiers or the side of a shaft, its profile disappears completely; it becomes merely a slight thickening of the wall, a patch of bad masonry.

For three minutes out of every four, the Sump-Back sleeps—or enters the cold, slow-pulsing torpor that passes for sleep down here. It clings to the turning shaft using a specialized ventral surface that is less a belly and more a single, massive chiton’s foot, cross-hatched with pale, grey-pink suction grooves that grip the slick, graphitic iron and walk it slowly backward as the column turns forward. 

It does not have eyes in the traditional sense; the dim mustard cones from the ceiling bulbs are useless to it. Instead, running down the center of its broad back are two parallel rows of raised, fleshy nodules—fourteen in total, spaced as evenly as the rivets on the bulkheads. These are hypertrophied lateral-line organs, little domes of tight, translucent skin stretched over cups of dense acoustic jelly. Through them, the Sump-Back does not see the room; it *feels* the geometry of the chamber’s noise. The *thud-thud-thud* of the north piston is its sunlight. As the heavy acoustic waves bounce off the concrete walls, the schooling pollock, the strolling spider crabs, and the hunting seal cast distinct sonic shadows across the Sump-Back’s dorsal domes. It reads the basin the way a blind man reads braille, mapping every moving body by the way it interrupts the rhythm of the machine.

Then comes the stutter in the north wall. The piston speeds up. The rust-snow begins to fall from the catwalks.

As every other creature in the basin hunkers down, braces, or flees to the lee of the columns, the Sump-Back wakes. The sixty-second washing-machine cycle is its hunting day.

It releases its grip on the central shaft, peeling away from the iron like a wet plaster cast. Instead of fighting the roaring intake current, it spreads its wide, fleshy margins, stiffening the cartilage ribs along its edges until it becomes a hydrofoil, a heavy, living kite riding the violent forty-eight-degree surge. 

It does not swim; it planes. Using the screaming turbulence of the open sluices, it slides laterally across the dark water with terrifying, silent speed, banking off the rising curtains of compressed air bubbles, dropping forty feet to the grated floor in a single, controlled sheer. 

During the surge, the chamber is so loud—filled with the shriek of bearings and the thunder of tonnage—that the conger eels cannot hear it coming, and the spider crabs cannot feel its approach through the vibrating floor grates. The Sump-Back drops directly over a crab that has hooked its copper-pipe legs into the iron grid. The creature’s underside opens—a pale, circular maw lined not with teeth, but with three interlocking plates of black, mineralized horn, shaped like the jaws of a pipe-wrench. 

There is a dull, muffled *crunch* that is instantly swallowed by the roar of the water. The plates shear through the crab’s heavy carapace as easily as a bolt-cutter through soft brass. The Sump-Back does not chew; it engulfs the crushed mass into a muscular gizzard lined with swallowed gravel and broken bits of ceramic insulator from the ceiling fixtures, letting its own internal machinery grind the meal down over the next several hours.

Sometimes, instead of dropping to the floor, it rides the updraft of the pneumatic relief valve right up into the frantic, braided ribbons of juvenile pollock hiding behind the outer shafts. It wraps its broad, rubbery wings around a dozen fish at once, forming a sealed pouch, and lets itself sink like a stone to the bottom while it suffocates them against its pale underside.

On the rare occasions it comes near the top of the chamber—drawn by a dying smelt thrashing in the oily chop—it stays just beneath the three-foot air gap, where the mustard light from the brass cages is strongest. Seen from the catwalk, just below the grease-line, it is deeply unsettling. Around the rim of its mantle are several long, pale, worm-like lures that it uses when resting in the silt. They do not look like worms, however. Over generations of selective predation in this specific box of water, the lures have evolved to mimic the only small, detached things that ever move along the bottom: they are coiled and segmented, looking precisely like three-inch lengths of dropped brass screw-thread or pale, rusted cotter pins, twitching in the current to draw in curious scavengers.

Then the sixty seconds are up. The pneumatic valve blows its final, roaring sheet of silver bubbles toward the ceiling. The intake sluices slam shut with a booming *clank* that makes the water itself stiffen.

Before the ringing in the iron has even died away, the Sump-Back has already ridden the final dying eddy back to the center of the room. It folds its heavy, graphite-black wings against the turning turbine shaft, locks its suction grooves onto the soft metal, and begins its slow, four-revolution-per-minute walk backward, vanishing once more into the architecture of the machine.
