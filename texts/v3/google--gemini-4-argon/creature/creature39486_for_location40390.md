In a clockwork reef where nothing flees, the apex of the food chain does not hunt; it services. 

Moving across the sheer face of the obsidian wall at a speed of three feet per hour is the *Gantry-Crab*, though it is neither a true decapod nor an individual organism. It is a compound, mobile chassis the size of a billiard table, its flat carapace forged from overlapping plates of dull, gray pewter held together by flush-set rivets of solid zinc. It does not walk on pointed legs. Instead, its twelve limbs terminate in flanged, double-rimmed wheels of dense bone that slot neatly onto the vertical seams of the volcanic glass and the hardened, extruded brass ridges of the piston-clam beds. It rolls upward and sideways along the reef like an overhead crane traversing a bay, winching itself against the heavy gravity of the brine with braided tendons of high-tensile nickel-titanium.

The Gantry-Crab has no eyes. In the shadowless, magnified glare of the basin, light carries no useful information that vibration does not carry better. Instead, its underside is a suspended forest of acoustic tuning forks and trembling plumb-bobs of raw lead. As it rolls over the thundering colonies of bellows-worms, these sensory weights vibrate in sympathy with the reef below. It is listening for a mistimed stroke. 

When a piston-clam’s cast-iron shell develops a hairline fracture from the freezing thermal stress, or when a valve-sponge’s bronze check-valve becomes fouled with precipitated copper sulfate, the rhythm of its exhaust changes. A four-minute cycle lags to four minutes and twelve seconds; a clean *CLANK* degrades into a wet, grinding *shuck*. The Gantry-Crab feels the rhythmic dissonance through its flanged wheels, brakes itself with a screech of bone-on-stone that sends a bright ribbon of sparks into the cold water, and locks its chassis directly over the failing organism.

From its ventral carriage, it lowers the *Tap-Worms*. 

These are its true digestive organs—symbiotic, yard-long annelids with collars of knurled steel and mouths composed of four radially symmetrical, hardened-carbide chuck jaws. Dropped on thick, greased umbilicals of sinew, the Tap-Worms clamp onto the housing of the ailing clam or sponge. They do not tear. They rotate. Driven by hydraulic bladders inside the crab’s main hull that pump with the slow, rhythmic thud of a steam-donkey, the worms bore straight through the cast-iron shells with agonizing, machinist precision. Spiral ribbons of biological iron curl away from the bit, drifting down toward the slag dunes below. 

Once the shell is breached, a second set of proboscises—fluted reamers and suction hoses of vulcanized gut—are inserted into the tap-hole. The Gantry-Crab does not kill its prey if the prey can still work; it performs an overhaul. It vacuums out the necrotic, fouled tissues, flushes the biological cylinder with a jet of concentrated sulfuric acid produced in its own lead-lined stomach, and then withdraws, sealing the drill-hole behind it with a cold-welded plug of excreted tin. It takes the excised meat and the mineral sludge as its fee. Only if the tuning forks detect that the organism’s internal casing is cracked beyond repair does the crab lower the heavy, drop-forged shears from its abdomen to dismantle the creature entirely, snipping its copper byssal threads and dropping the dead engine block into the trenches below to be recycled by the auger-snails.

Yet even the Gantry-Crab is subject to the primary hazard of the basin: the cold-weld.

In water so pure of organic slime and so heavy with dissolved metals, two clean surfaces of similar alloy that press together for too long will simply fuse at the molecular level. To stop moving in the cleft is to become structural. The lower tiers of the reef are a solid mass of ancient, seized machinery—generations of piston-clams and ratchet-shrimp that missed a stroke, fused to their neighbors, and were slowly plated over with native copper until they became the bedrock upon which the newer generations bolted themselves. 

This is the domain of the *Lard-Eels*, the basin’s indispensable parasites. 

Five feet long, thick as a man’s thigh, and entirely boneless, the Lard-Eels are the only soft things in the canyon. Their skin is a pale, translucent tallow, slick and waxy, smelling faintly of rancid whale oil and sulfur. They have no teeth, no shells, and no metallic parts; in the deafening roar of the basin, they are completely silent, slipping through the narrow, vibrating gaps between the engine-block fauna like strips of pale felt.

They feed on the violent static electricity of the flywheel-medusae and the thermal vents of the auger-snails, using the energy to synthesize heavy, complex hydrocarbons from the dissolved minerals and carbon dioxide in the brine. As they press their flat,suction-cup mouths against the spinning bell of a medusa to siphon off its electrical charge, their skin weeps a thick, yellowish, lithium-based grease. 

The reef tolerates the theft of its electricity because without the Lard-Eels, the entire foundry would seize within a month. The eels worm their way into the hinge-joints of the piston-clams, wrap themselves around the axles of the Gantry-Crabs, and slide through the dense clouds of ratchet-shrimp, coating every grinding gear and sliding valve in a microscopic film of waterproof lubricant. When an eel dies, it does not sink to the slag heaps; its tallow body floats upward, caught in the slow, churning eddies of the medusae, until it is drawn into the intake siphon of a valve-sponge, which uses the rendered fat to keep its vulcanized rubber chambers from cracking in the freezing water.

And at the very bottom of the cleft, half-buried in the sparkling dunes of iron filings and shaved lead, sits the *Sump-Loom*.

There is only one in the basin. It is not an animal, nor a colony, but a sprawling, sessile mat of biological wiring and fluidic logic gates that stretches for thirty feet along the floor of the deepest trench. It looks like the backplate of a telephone exchange dipped in verdigris and drowned in crystal water. Thousands of hollow, extruded-copper tubules rise from its central mass, waving almost imperceptibly in the heavy brine.

The Sump-Loom eats the noise. 

Its entire upper surface is a mosaic of piezoelectric plates grown from quartz and Rochelle salt harvested by the auger-snails. The deafening, stern-shaking roar of the reef above—the *shhhhhh-CLANK* of the sponges, the million-fold clicking of the shrimp, the low-gear grinding of the crabs—strikes these crystal plates and is converted directly into a steady, low-voltage trickle of current. The Loom uses this acoustic energy to smelt the biological slag that rains down upon it.

In its slow, dark furnaces—the only places in the cleft where shadows exist, cast by the overhanging diorite boulders—the Loom takes the iron filings, the hexagonal shell-nuts, and the stripped gears of dead fauna, and re-extrudes them. It spins miles of fresh, hair-thin copper wire, which the young, free-swimming larvae of the piston-clams must harvest to anchor themselves to the obsidian walls. It presses out flat, blank washers of brass and drops them into the silt, where juvenile ratchet-shrimp will pick them up and incorporate them into their growing claws.

Nothing in the basin knows the Sump-Loom is there. The flywheel-medusae spin in the blinding sun; the Gantry-Crabs roll along their tracks; the valve-sponges compress and vent on their four-minute timers. None of them possess a brain larger than a pinhead, and none of them have any conception of the whole. Yet if the water temperature drops by half a degree, threatening to slow the endothermic reactions of the reef to a fatal stall, the Sump-Loom’s piezoelectric plates register the slight drop in the pitch of the basin’s roar. 

Within three hours, the Loom alters the alloy of the wire it extrudes, increasing the zinc content. Within a week, the new generation of ratchet-shrimp, incorporating the altered metal, are clicking at a slightly higher resistance, generating three percent more friction heat with every grind of their claws. A million tiny gears warm the water by a fraction of a degree; the pitch of the roar rises back to its proper key; the Loom settles back into its baseline hum.

Above, the mountain wind slices the flat mirror of the surface into blinding, geometric nets of light. Below, in the freezing, shadowless lens, the grease-slicked gears turn, the bronze valves slam shut, and the living factory manufactures itself, one revolution per hour, until the mountains themselves wear down to the level of the sea.
