If you stay on your knees until the damp soaks through the wool of your trousers and the cold stops feeling like a knife and starts feeling like an iron weight, your eyes finally adjust to the clockwork of the pit. You realize that the city is not merely inhabited by the slow, leathery quadrupeds; it is *maintained* by them, though the maintenance takes place across a timescale so stretched that a single task might outlive a dog.

They are called the *tett* by the few herders who cross the grey clay barrens, though the herders rarely stop to look at them, considering the basins bad luck—places where the earth has gone sour and sluggish. 

Look closely at one of the tett wedged into a four-inch alleyway. It is not quite a mammal and not quite a reptile, though it possesses the stubborn, heavy-boned density of both. Its skin is the exact color of wet slate, loose and folded deeply at the neck and groin like an oversized coat, textured with flat, hexagonal tubercles that hold the condensing moisture in miniature reservoirs along its spine. It has no eyes—not even the sunken, skin-covered pits of a cave creature. Where a face should be, there is only a blunt, calloused wedge of pale horn, split down the middle by a vertical sensory cleft that slowly opens and closes, tasting the thick, cold grease of the air.

Its blood is not red, and it is not warm. It is a clear, amber fluid heavy with glycerol, pumped by a two-chambered heart that beats once every three or four minutes. When a tett lifts one of its disproportionately heavy forefeet to take a step up a mossy stair, you can actually watch the hydraulic swell of the limb as the thick blood is forced into the leg, stiffening it before the horn-claw sets down. 

They do not build anymore because there is nowhere left to build, and no loose stone left to quarry. Every flake of slate and lump of white clay in the basin was stacked three thousand years ago, when the fissure at the bottom of the pit vented steam instead of a lukewarm sigh, and the tett moved at the speed of badgers. As the geothermal throat choked on its own mineral deposits and the pit cooled, the tett did not die out, and they did not leave. Their bodies simply wound down with the rock. 

Yet the instinct for masonry remains, locked in their tiny, dense brains like a fly in amber. 

If you watch the two that have just finished their ten-minute snout-press in the alley, you will see what they are doing. The meeting was not a greeting; it was an exchange of mortar. The larger one has been down in the cellar-warrens—the dark, waterlogged foundational honeycomb beneath the visible streets where the warm breath rises directly from the clay. Its mouth-parts are packed with a walnut-sized wad of fresh white tallow-clay, chewed and mixed with its own thick, slow-drying saliva until it has the consistency of glazier’s putty. 

During the ten minutes their snouts were pressed together, the first tett transferred half of this masticated plug into the jaws of the second. Now, having crawled over its sibling’s cold, ridged back—their heavy claws finding purchase in the skin-folds without breaking the leathery hide—the second tett continues up the stepped lane toward a tower where a translucent, cabbage-like rosette has wedged its root too deeply into a joint.

It will take the creature until tomorrow morning to reach the spot, six feet away. When it arrives, it will use its heavy, flat foreclaws to shear away the plant’s lower leaves—not eating them, just clearing the work site—and then it will spend the better part of a week packing the fresh clay into the widened gap with its blunt horn snout, tamping it down with microscopic, agonizingly deliberate head-butts. The plant will not die; it will simply grow around the new mortar over the next decade. The city is a stalemate between the blind masons and the antifreeze flora, fought at a speed of inches per century.

Inside the towers, where the surgical glare of the sky cannot reach, the geometry is even tighter. The rooms are no larger than teacup saucers, connected by vertical shafts worn smooth as soapstone by generations of rubbing backs. Here, there is no greenery. The walls are coated in a thick, tallow-like rime made of shed skin-scales, dried mucus, and dust, which serves as insulation. 

In the lowest, warmest chambers—where the temperature might reach a balmy forty degrees Fahrenheit—sit the mothers. A reproductive female tett does not walk the streets. Once she reaches a certain size, her pelvis calcifies and widens until she can no longer fit through the boot-heel doorways. She becomes a permanent fixture of the ground floor, wedged into her stone room like a pearl in an oyster. She is pale, almost white, her skin gone soft and doughy in the dark. 

Other tett bring her food: the ochre-striped slugs scraped from the outer walls, bundles of bitter liverwort, and the occasional frost-killed body of one of the thumb-sized, bat-like creatures that fell from the eaves. She chews with a grinding, sideways motion of her jaw that takes two minutes per cycle. Every ten or twelve years, she produces a single offspring—not in an egg, and not quite live-born, but encased in a leathery, fluid-filled sac that she tucks into the warm mortared corner behind her hind legs. When the young tett finally claws its way out after three winters of gestation, it is the size of a walnut, already blind, already slow, and its first instinctive act is to eat the calcified shell of its own sac to harden its soft horn claws.

And what of the others? The city is an ecosystem of parasites and tenants that have all agreed to the same truce with time.

The ochre-striped slugs—*limax vitreus*—are not merely grazers; they are the city’s windows and its sanitation. Their bodies are so clear that as they slide over the mortared slate, you can see the dark green paste of algae moving through their straight, simple guts. They secrete a slime that does not freeze, a slick, iridescent varnish that coats the exterior stone and actually protects the ancient white clay mortar from dissolving in the perpetual condensation. The tett tolerate them, walking right over their rubbery backs when necessary, only harvesting one when it grows too fat and begins to block a doorway.

The glass-legged spiders do not spin their webs to catch flies; there are no flying insects down here, as the air is too heavy and cold for wings to beat. Instead, their webs are strung across the bone lintels and narrow alleys to catch *water* and *spores*. The silk is hygroscopic, drawing the clammy fog out of the air until the web is a chandelier of heavy, clear drops. Within these suspended droplets, microscopic snow-fleas, rotifers, and wind-blown pollen from the plain above become trapped and concentrated. Once a day, the spider—whose body is no bigger than a coriander seed and just as hard—walks the tightrope of its web, drinking the droplets one by one, filtering out the invisible broth of life suspended in the water, before spinning a fresh thread to replace the old.

Then there are the sleepers under the eaves. They are the only things in the pit with eyes, and their eyes are terrible: huge, black, lidless spheres that take up half their tiny skulls, filmed over with a milky nictitating membrane against the blinding white glare of the heather-diffused sun. They are not bats, though they have webbing between their long, multi-jointed fingers. They are warm-blooded creatures that belong to the wider plain, trapped here by a seasonal rhythm that has gone wrong.

In the brief, six-week summer of the barrens, when the low hills turn brown and midges rise from the thaw-ponds, these little flyers hunt the open sky. When the frost returns, they seek out the deep slots and fissures in the clay to hibernate. Those that find a sterile crevice sleep until spring. Those that tumble through the silver greasewood canopy into the tett’s city, however, are caught in a metabolic trap. 

The air in the pit is just warm enough to keep them from freezing to death, yet too cold—and the canopy above too densely woven—for them to ever wake up enough to fly out. Their hearts drop to two beats a minute. Their wide, lidless eyes stare straight down into the four-inch streets, registering the glacial passage of the blind masons below not as movement, but as a shifting of the background, like the hands of a clock. They hang by their hooked thumbs for years, living off the brown fat packed between their shoulder blades, slowly mummifying while still alive, covered in a fine velvet of green algae that grows on their leathery wings. Every few months, the claws of one finally lose their grip on the slick slate, and it drops without a sound into a cushion of ice-fern, where, within three or four days, a passing tett will find it by smell and carry it down into the dark to feed a mother.

At the very center of the pit, where the towers are tallest—reaching perhaps four feet from the invisible floor—there is a clear space no larger than a washbasin. Here, the stones are not slate, but stacked rings of ancient, mineralized bone: the ribs and vertebrae of some immense, long-extinct beast that died in this mud-hole when the world was warmer, providing the original anchor around which the city was begun.

This is the chimney. From the dark throat at the center of the bone-ring rises the slow, warm breath of the earth. The air coming out of it smells of wet iron, old sulfur, and the musky, turnip-like scent of ten thousand cold bodies packed into stone. 

Around the lip of this bone well, the tett gather when they are ready to die. When an old mason’s horn claws are worn down to the quick, and its joints are so stiff with mineral deposits that its amber blood can no longer force the legs to bend, it crawls to the chimney. They sit there in a tight, inward-facing ring, twenty or thirty of them, their blunt snouts resting on the ancient ribs, soaking in the forty-five-degree warmth as if it were a roaring hearth. 

They do not fall into the pit. They simply stop moving. Over years, the condensation beads on their grey backs; the ochre slugs crawl over them, leaving trails of waterproof varnish; the pale rosettes wedge their roots between their stiffened legs; and the younger tett, coming up from the dark with mouths full of fresh white clay, mortar right over their shells, locking them into the load-bearing wall of the well, raising the chimney another half-inch toward the blinding white sky.
