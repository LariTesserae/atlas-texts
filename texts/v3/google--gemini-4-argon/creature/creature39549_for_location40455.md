To understand what lives in the Sunken Crown, one has to look past the noise. The plated fish with their buzzing bladders, the clicking amphibians, and the canvas-scraping slugs are not the citizens of this place. They are the broom, the chisel, and the bellows. They are autonomous tools left running in an empty workshop. 

The true inhabitants of the caldera are harder to separate from the water itself, and harder still to separate from the builders who supposedly vanished.

If you hang suspended below one of the great ribbon-bridges for long enough, letting your eyes adjust to the wet-chalk smear of the mortar-light, you will eventually notice that some of the currents are moving the wrong way. A mass of water will slide deliberately against the slow, circular spin of the basin. Then, you will feel a pressure wave hit your chest—a single, low-frequency thrum so dense it feels like being touched by a cold, flat hand.

Out of the bruised indigo gloom comes a shape like a dropped cloak. 

At first, they seem to be titanic cephalopods or rays, twenty to thirty feet from tip to tip, made of a heavy, gelatinous blubber the color of tallow. They have no eyes—not even the scarred, vestigial pits that the plated fish have—and no true bones. Their bodies consist of a central, gourd-shaped mantle flanked by four immense, trailing aprons of thick flesh that undulate with hypnotic, terrifying slowness. 

These are the *Cantors*, though they have no name for themselves that can be spoken in air. 

As one drifts closer to the chalk-grey seam of a root-pillar, its anatomy resolves into something far more unsettling than a fish or a squid. The creature’s skin is completely hairless and smooth, worn to the exact same hydrodynamic finish as the calcified kelp-wood of the buildings. Running down its dorsal ridge is a series of seven cavernous, muscular siphons, lined with stiff, calcified baffles that look remarkably like the gill-arches of the amphibians, or the vaulted ribs of the plazas below. 

When a Cantor inhales, it draws hundreds of gallons of thirty-eight-degree water into its mantle. When it exhales, it forces that water through the internal baffles at tremendous pressure. The sound it makes is not an animal cry. It is a chord—a complex, multi-tonal hydraulic organ blast that shoots through the dark water for miles. 

In that chord is a three-dimensional map of the immediate architecture, an instruction to the surrounding schools of fish, and a structural stress-test of the nearest viaduct. 

The Cantors are the architects’ descendants, or perhaps what the architects chose to become when the ice finally sealed the roof of the world and they realized that hands, fire, and eyes were no longer useful technologies. Over epochs of deliberate self-modification—the same biological engineering they used to weave the kelp-roots—they bred themselves out of legs, out of lungs, and out of linear time. They traded tools for acoustic organs.

Because the water is thirty-eight degrees, a Cantor’s metabolism is glacial. Their hearts—three slow, peristaltic pumps distributed through their blubber—beat roughly once every four minutes. Consequently, they do not experience the world at the frantic pace of warm-blooded things. To a Cantor, the frantic, telegraph-clicking amphibians are a high-pitched static, like the hiss of summer insects. The thunderous cracking of the ten-foot ice ceiling is just the rhythmic ticking of a clock. 

They do not build with hands anymore; they build—and maintain—with sound.

Every plant and animal in the Sunken Crown was bred over millennia to be phono-receptive. The lignified moss-masonry does not grow toward light; it grows toward specific frequencies of vibration and recoils from others. The shell-less mollusks do not graze at random; their simple nervous systems are hardwired to move away from high-frequency clicks and congregate where deep bass notes settle. 

Watch a Cantor at work: it hovers above a broad, sunken avenue where a hairline fracture has formed in the braided root-stone. The creature folds its four fleshy aprons around itself like a dead blossom, anchoring its bulk in the current by gripping the smooth floor with a row of boneless, muscular thumbs that ring its underside—the last, haunting vestige of the terrestrial hands its ancestors once possessed. 

It presses its resonant belly directly against the green-bone masonry and begins to sing.

To a human diver, the vibration would cause immediate internal hemorrhaging. To the city, it is a hormone. The stone-fish scatter from the avenue in a panicked, metallic-humming ribbon. The giant amphibians on the nearby pillars fall silent, then begin clicking in a frantic, synchronized counter-rhythm that acts as an acoustic lens, focusing the Cantor’s bass note directly into the crack. Stimulated by the sustained frequency, the dormant meristem cells deep within the calcified kelp wake up. Slowly—over days, over weeks, while the Cantor remains locked in place without taking a single breath of air or morsel of food—the wood-moss swells, foams, and knits itself back together. Then, the Cantor shifts its pitch upward by a fraction of a hertz. Across the plaza, thousands of canvas-scraping slugs turn in unison and march toward the fresh scar, rasping the new growth down until it matches the ancient, smooth curve of the floor.

How do they live in a world without sunlight? 

Deep at the bottom of the caldera, miles below the ice where the great root-piers sink into the dead volcanic throat of the mountain, the water is not thirty-eight degrees. Down in the absolute black, where even the grey mortar-lichen does not grow, the roots drink from warm, sulfur-rich geothermal seeps. The entire city is a single, monstrous vascular system. It draws thermal energy and dissolved minerals up from the earth’s crust through its piers, converting it into slow-growing plant sugars and channeling it into the pale fungal seams. The slugs eat the lichen; the plated fish eat the slugs’ nutrient-rich mucus; the amphibians eat the fish. 

And the Cantors, when they feed once or twice a decade, simply open their pleated undersides while drifting through a crowded archway, tuned to a specific, lulling hum that puts the stone-fish to sleep. The fish drift into the Cantor's digestive folds like falling leaves, dissolved over months by cold, patient acids.

Yet maintenance takes up very little of the Cantors' eternity. The city is nearly perfect; it rarely breaks. Most of the time, the dozens of massive, pale shapes drifting through the miles of open water are engaged in something else entirely.

They are remembering.

In a world with no paper, no carvings that can resist the smoothing currents, and no light to read by, memory cannot be stored in objects. It must be stored in motion and sound. 

If you float in the great central amphitheater, where the water is completely unobstructed for a mile in every direction, you will find six or seven Elders—Cantors grown so old and vast that their blubber has calcified in patches, making them look like drifting pieces of the masonry itself. They hang in a wide circle in the dark, miles above the floor, miles below the ice.

They are singing a song that started four thousand years ago. 

One Elder releases a complex, rippling cascade of sonar clicks and bass groans into the center of the circle. Because of the immaculate, curved acoustics of the surrounding viaducts and plazas, the sound does not die. It bounces off a smooth colonnade half a mile away, Caroms off the underside of the ice sheet, funnels through a braided archway, and returns to the center of the circle twelve seconds later, slightly altered by the geometry of the city. 

Before it can fade, another Elder catches the echo, adds a harmonic layer to it, and blasts it back out along a different architectural path. 

The city itself is the brain. The vaults, pillars, and avenues are not roads—nothing here walks. They are acoustic delay lines, resonant chambers, and logic gates. The Sunken Crown is a monumental, water-filled computer, and the song bouncing endlessly between its smooth walls is an archive.

In that deafening, overlapping wall of sound—which to an outsider sounds like the chaotic roaring of a subterranean factory—is the entire history of the world before the water rose, the genetic blueprints of every organism in the caldera, complex mathematical philosophies, and long, slow poems about the nature of cold and weight. A single stanza might take three hours to travel from the eastern rim of the caldera, ricochet through a thousand root-chambers, and return to the center. If an Elder dies—sinking slowly into the abyssal root-tangle at the bottom to be reabsorbed by the kelp—two younger Cantors will swim into its place, catching the returning echoes before a single syllable of the four-thousand-year-old thought can drop out of the water and be lost.

Nothing new is ever added to the song. To add a new note would require changing the shape of the buildings that reflect it, which would disrupt the currents, which would starve the lichen, which would break the biological lockstep that keeps them alive in the freezing dark. 

So they hang in the ink-black water, great ghost-masses of tallow and cartilage, gently waving their heavy aprons to stay in place. Around them, the blind fish hum like plucked wires, the ice booms like distant cannon fire, and the Elders endlessly pass the thoughts of their long-dead ancestors back and forth through the dark—prisoners of a masterpiece so finely tuned that to have a single new idea would bring the sky crashing down.
