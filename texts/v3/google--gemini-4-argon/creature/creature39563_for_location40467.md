To survive in the valley without being drunk dry by the floor, a thing must be sealed. 

The people who built the looms and laid the copper cables are, at their core, soft and wet—ancestral creatures of blood and brine—Holdouts from an older, soggier world. Down here, a single drop of sweat falling onto the xyloid plain will trigger a greedy, localized eruption of pale shoots within minutes, a wooden bur rising to seek the source of the moisture. If a man were to lie down bare-skinned on the avenue and sleep, the city would have its root-hairs through his pores by third-bell, gently, dryly siphoning him into a husk while braiding his ribs into a respectable wainscoting.

So the citizens go about in *shells*. 

At first glance, walking the wide, chalk-bright avenues, they look like the valley’s own fauna evolved to walk upright: tall, narrow-hipped figures encased in articulated plates of cured, vulcanized heartwood and vulcanite rubber, their joints sealed with heavy, dry-waxed bellows of bladder-skin. Their helmets are smooth, featureless gourds of dark, resin-impregnated timber, fitted with wide, wrap-around lenses of sliced lumin-gall crystal that polarization turns the color of smoked topaz. Every breath they take is cycled through a twin-canister condenser strapped between their shoulder blades, reclamation units that click softly with tiny brass valves, catching the moisture of their exhalations and returning it to a drinking-tube inside the chin-guard. 

They do not smell like animals. They smell like violin cases, beeswax, and hot copper.

Because their mouths are sealed behind resin and filter-charcoal, they have given up speaking aloud in the streets. Speech wastes dampness, and the open air is too vast and absorbent for unamplified voices anyway. Instead, they carry *stridulators*—small, fan-like instruments of brass and stiffened horn bolted to their forearms. By flicking their fingers against a set of tuned metal teeth, they send sharp, cricket-like *clicks*, *zips*, and *ratchets* skittering across the smooth bark plazas. Two neighbors meeting on an avenue will stand five paces apart, heads tilted, their hands blurring in a dry, percussive chatter that sounds like a telegraph office or a pair of beetles arguing in a wainscoting. For longer conversations, or intimate ones, they step up to one of the city’s millions of tension-wires, press the steel ferrule of their walking-staves against the cable, and let the vibrations carry through the municipal rigging into the floorboards of their listener’s boots.

To live in a house here is to be in a constant, polite wrestling match with the furniture.

Only the oldest, poorest districts live in "dead-wood"—buildings that have been girdled at the base and starved of gas until they solidify into dry, inert bone. These places are safe, still, and looked down upon as tombs. A prosperous family lives in *green-frame*, in a house that is still rising. 

Inside a green-frame tenement, the air is pressurized slightly higher than the street to keep the calcium fogs out, though the inhabitants still wear their indoor-skins—tight, high-collared body-gloves of oiled silk that leave only their faces and fingertips bare. The walls are warm to the touch, pulsing with a faint, rhythmic dry heat as the carbon-gas pumps through the vascular wainscoting. You do not buy a table or a bed in this city; you clamp a cast-iron *mold-box* to the floor before you go to work, open a brass petcock on the baseboard to let a trickle of mineral-gas into the floorboards, and come home at dusk to unbolt the iron plates and find a chair has extruded itself into the cavity, pale and waxy and warm, its grain tightly curled where it fought the cast-iron corners.

Of course, the house does not know when to stop. Every morning begins with the *paring*. Before breakfast—a dense, oily paste of crushed moth-thorax, nut-tallow, and recycled water sucked from a silver bladder—the household takes up their hook-knives and spokeshaves. They go from room to room, shaving back the window-sills that have crept inward overnight to choke the glass, planing down the floorboards that have swelled to jam the doors, and snapping off the blind, white "thief-shoots" that are forever trying to worm their way toward the kitchen’s sealed water-crocks. The shavings are swept into a dry-chute; they are the household's currency, weighed out at the municipal bins in exchange for gas-credits and refined grease.

This endless maintenance has given the citizenry a peculiar posture and temperament. They are a lean, stringy-muscled people with oversized, calloused hands and a twitchy, hyper-alert stillness. They never lean against a wall unless they have just checked its dormancy-gauge. They never leave a tool on the floor for more than ten minutes, lest a root-lip curl over the haft and claim it. Their eyes, visible only when they retreat into the humid, rubber-gasketed sanctums of their sleeping-pods, are huge and pale, ringed with dark circles from the constant, shadowless noon of the lumin-galls—for the ceiling never goes dark. It cannot; if the electricity were cut and the galls stopped feeding the upper canopy, the vault miles overhead would begin to contract, drying and splitting until whole acres of dead timber sheared off to crush the valley below. 

So they sleep in windowless coffins of leaded timber, and when they wake, they step back into the everlasting, chalky glare.

Among them, distinct by the heavy copper braiding on their shells and the long, insulated grounding-poles they carry, are the *Graft-Wrights* and the *Tensioners*. 

The Tensioners are the city’s aristocracy of nerve. They work in crews of six, suspended in leather harnesses from the underside of the rising viaducts or scaling the great, groaning iron jigs of a new tenement block. Their job is to listen to the wood. A Tensioner presses the bone-conduction horn of his helmet against a steel tie-rod, closes his eyes, and feels the deep, fibrous *straining* of ten thousand tons of blind timber trying to twist toward the static-lines. If a building grows crooked, the stresses build until the whole structure explodes in a lethal burst of ten-foot splinters—a "dry-shatter" that can level a block. The Tensioners walk the scaffolding like spiders, tightening a turnbuckle here, slacking a winch there, bleeding off a pocket of pressurized nitrogen with a long brass trocar that hisses like a stuck pig, coaxing the titanic, idiot vigor of the floor into straight lines and right angles.

Beneath them, down in the sunken plazas where the grates open into the vascular dark, move the *Saps*. 

They are not citizens, not anymore, or perhaps they are what happens to citizens whose seals fail too often and who decide not to die of it. They are the maintenance caste of the deep-taps—figures who have replaced so much of their outer gear with living, grafted timber-plate that they can no longer unbuckle their shells. They move slowly, with a dry, creaking gait, trailing thin umbilical hoses from their backs that plug directly into the street-manifolds. They have allowed a regulated symbiosis: the wood has grown into their lower spines and lymph-nodes, filtering their waste in exchange for a constant, metered drip of pressurized nutrient-paste. They are huge, lumpy, asymmetrical things, crusted with pale sugar-scale that they let the six-legged street-grazers nibble right off their shoulders. They do not use stridulators; they have no fingers left fast enough for the keys. They communicate by thumping their heavy, iron-shod heels against the hollow ground, sending low, booming bass notes rolling through the root-mat to their brothers miles below in the dark.

And at the very fringes of the avenues, where the city’s jigs end and the uncut, undulating plains of the raw floor roll out toward the distant, curving walls of the cavern-gourd, live the *Gleaners*.

They are the ones who cannot stand the city’s relentless geometry. They live in nomadic, wheeled pavilions pulled by teams of domesticated, heavy-bodied grazers—houses that never stay in one place long enough for the floor to get a purchase on their axles. They follow the nitrogen-fogs across the bone-white wastes, harvesting the wild, un-jiggled growths that burst from the floor after a vent-blow: spiral-horned timber-truffles, brittle blossoms of pure crystalized turpentine, and the shed, glassy skins of the great amber-eyed predators that drop dead from the ceiling trusses when they grow too heavy with static. 

When a Gleaner wagon rolls into the city’s outer plazas to trade, the city-folk gather at a respectful distance, their brass stridulators clicking in a low, nervous murmur. The Gleaners’ shells are not smooth and polished like those of the town-dwellers; they are shaggy with unplaned bark, festooned with copper wind-chimes that spark and hiss in the static air, and stained yellow with wild pollens. They move with a loose, rolling swagger that makes the Tensioners’ teeth ache, as if they have forgotten that the ground beneath their boots is a hungry thing, waiting only for them to stop moving so it can turn them into a wall.
