To see what lives here, you first have to stop looking for motion. In a landscape where the architecture is indistinguishable from geology, survival belongs to things that have learned to behave like stone, or like the water that slips between it.

Start in the joints. A dry-stone wall is not a solid barrier; it is a three-dimensional lattice of dark, humid caves. Because there is no mortar, the interior of every wall, foundation, and stepped terrace is a continuous, interconnected labyrinth that stretches for hundreds of miles beneath and between the visible streets. 

If you press your ear to a retaining wall, beneath the faint sibilance of the runoff, you will hear a dry, microscopic ticking. This is the sound of the **shale-crabs**. No larger than a human thumbnail, they have evolved flat, disc-like carapaces the exact blue-grey of wet slate, allowing them to slide sideways through horizontal crevices only millimeters wide. They are detritivores and tireless janitors, feeding on the dead moss, wind-blown spores, and the gelatinous biofilms that form where the mist condenses. They never emerge into the bright, shadowless light of the plazas unless a wall shifts. When they die, their shells—rich in calcium leached from the water—dissolve slowly back into the silt traps, feeding the pale sedges.

Deeper in the walls, where the drafts are blocked by thick accumulations of gravel and older, collapsed foundations, live the **plinth-newts**. They are cold-water amphibians, nearly blind, with translucent, rubbery skin the color of tallow. They move through the subterranean dark where the city’s plumbing meets the natural fractures of the basalt table. They have no lungs; they breathe entirely through their skin and the feathery, blood-dark gills that flare behind their jaws, drawing oxygen from the hyper-aerated water that sheets down the internal stone chutes. They are slow, living for decades in a state of chilly torpor, waiting at the bottom of dark cisterns for a shale-crab or a blind aquifer-shrimp to wash over the lip of a stone weir.

Out in the open, life must contend with the thin air, the constant damp, and the glaring ultraviolet light that pours through the high, thin clouds. 

What looks at first like a particularly thick cushion of rust-colored moss on the leeward side of a watchtower will, if watched for an hour, slightly change its shape. These are **lichen-hares**, though they are closer to pikas or marmots. They have no external ears or tails to lose heat to the high-altitude wind. Their bodies are low, hemispherical mounds of dense, hollow-shafted fur that traps warmth like a diving bell. Because their outer guard hairs are grooved and permanently damp, actual symbiotic algae and pale green crustose lichens grow in their coats. A hare sitting motionless on a tumbled boundary wall is completely invisible. They move not by hopping, which wastes precious calories in the thin air, but by a slow, creeping glide, their wide, padded feet gripping the wet basalt as they graze on the bitter, stunted tubers and the spore-caps of moss. 

They are hunted by the **stilts**—the long-legged wading birds you see standing in the overflow basins. Up close, a stilt is a creature of unsettling stillness and harsh geometry. Standing four feet tall on legs plated in dark, overlapping scales that resist the freezing water, its plumage is a masterclass in counter-shading: dark, oily slate above to match the wet roofs, and stark, blinding white beneath to vanish against the bright ceiling of the sky when viewed from below. Their bills are not spears, like a heron's, but long, slightly curved forceps with serrated tips. A stilt can stand in a rectangular basin for six hours without a tremor, letting the laminar flow of water part around its shins without a ripple, waiting either for a blind crustacean to wash out of a stone grate, or for a lichen-hare on the wall above to betray itself with a puff of condensed breath.

Then there are the grazers—the source of the coarse, greasy wool the human inhabitants wear. You rarely see them in the dense center of the city; they prefer the "fallow wards," entire square miles of the metropolis where the roofs have long since sagged in, turning the rooms into sunken, wind-sheltered pens of broken rock and deep moss. 

These are the **cairn-sheep**, though they bear little resemblance to lowland flocks. They are heavy-boned, barrel-chested animals with massive nasal cavities that warm the thin, freezing air before it hits their lungs. Their hooves are extraordinary: cleft wide and rimmed with hard, sharp keratin, the undersides are soft and spongy, acting like suction cups on the slick, pitched slate of the roofs. They do not bleat; in an acoustic environment that swallows sound, vocalizing is useless. Instead, they communicate through posture and a low, sub-audible clacking of their heavy, curved horns against the stone walls—a sharp *tock* that travels through the solid rock itself, warning the herd of a shift in the stones. Their wool is six inches thick, matted with lanolin until it sheds water like a pitched roof, and stained dark grey by the basalt dust.

And what of the people? 

To call them "builders" is perhaps a misunderstanding of their place in the ecology. They do not build the city any more than polyps build a whole reef in a single generation; they *Secrete* it, maintain it, and move through it like an immune system.

If you follow one of the solitary, grey-cloaked figures, you begin to notice how deeply the altitude and the stone have shaped them. They are barrel-chested like the sheep, with wide, flat feet that have never been cramped by rigid shoes. Their hands are large, with thick, calloused pads and heavy, spatulate nails worn flat from centuries of generations prying and settling slate. Their faces are broad, their skin weathered to the texture of smooth river-stone, with heavy epicanthic folds that shield their dark eyes from the relentless, shadowless glare of the wet flagstones. 

Their blood is thick with hemoglobin, turning their lips and tongues a deep, bruised plum color. Because the air is so thin, they never hurry. Every movement is a study in the conservation of momentum. They walk with a rolling, flat-footed gait, their centers of gravity kept low, never lifting their feet higher than necessary to clear a gutter. 

They live in small, dispersed kinship clusters—rarely more than five or six people—occupying the lower, double-walled rooms of the stone warren where the earth holds a steady, cool temperature just above freezing. There is no wood to burn, so their interiors are lit and warmed by shallow stone dishes filled with rendered fat from the cairn-sheep, burning with tiny, smokeless blue flames along wicks of braided sedge-pith. The air inside their dwellings smells of wet wool, mutton tallow, crushed mint-moss, and the clean, mineral tang of cold water.

They are nearly as silent as the stilts. Spoken language here has been stripped down to what is necessary, spoken in low, breathy chest-tones that do not waste heat or moisture. Much of their communication is left behind in the architecture itself. A traveler walking through an empty district can read the recent history of the ward in the placement of small, white quartz pebbles set on the rims of the sluice gates, or the angle at which a flat stone is leaned against a doorway. A stone tilted left means *the cistern below is silting up*; three flat shards stacked on a retaining wall means *the frost has bulged this course; walk wide*. 

They do not think of themselves as the masters of the plateau. In their quiet cosmology, the plateau is a single, tilting machine designed to slow the water down on its journey from the clouds to the distant edge of the world. If the water moves too fast, it tears the stone away; if it stops, it freezes and cracks the walls. Every living thing has a role in keeping the flow laminar. The moss holds the silt; the crabs clear the drains; the sheep fertilize the moss; the people balance the stones. 

When one of these solitary caretakers grows old and feels their thick blood slowing for the last time, they do not dig a grave—there is no soil to dig. Instead, they walk upward, against the tilt of the plateau, toward the older, higher wards where the wind is sharper and the mist never fully clears. There, they climb to the high, flat roof of a watchtower, wrap their grey cloak tightly around themselves, and lie down on the wet slate. 

Within an hour, the ten-foot shadow that races silently over the plazas will pause. The great scavenger—a **condor of the high thermals**, featherless from the neck up, its skin the pale blue of ice, its crop vast and patient—will drop out of the bright ceiling of the sky, landing on the roof with a heavy, muffled thud. And when the birds are finished, the rain and the mist wash the bones clean, and the next caretaker who passes that way will gather the ribs and the long femurs, carrying them down to wedging them neatly into the dry-stone walls—just another piece of pale, mineral weight holding the great, quiet reef together against the gravity of the earth.
