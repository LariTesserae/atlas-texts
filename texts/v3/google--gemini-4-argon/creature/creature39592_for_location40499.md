If you watch the opalescent carpet of flatworms for long enough, you will notice an anomaly in the current. 

Everywhere else in the basin, the shoals move according to simple thermal and chemical gradients—flowing toward the warm upwellings, pooling in the emerald light beneath the *Vallis-glass*, or surging toward the protein-snow beneath a needle. They move like cream, parting around obstacles and seamlessly rejoining. 

Except in one place, near the center of the eastern flat, where a patch of the carpet the size of a cartwheel moves against the grain. 

It does not part for the trunks; it flows up and over their bases. It does not wait for the needles to kill; it reaches toward them. Look closer at this patch, through the blinding, shadowless glare, and you will see that the worms comprising it are not swimming in the water. They are suspended in a clear, viscous matrix—a glue of hyper-dense mucus that binds tens of thousands of the pale ribbons, along with blind gastropods, ghost-leeches, and the undigested pneumatophores of drift-mantles, into a single, creeping hillock.

This is the **Silt-Abbot**, though it has no name for itself, nor the architecture required to hold a name. It is an accident of density and ancient geometry.

To understand what the Abbot is, one must understand the needles. The osmium pilings are not weapons; they are terrestrial alignment pins, left behind by an epoch that viewed continental drift as a preventable untidiness. The refractive error around them is a localized metric standard—a broadcast of absolute spatial and structural regularity. When a drift-mantle wanders too close, its chaotic, organic cellular structure violates the local metric, and the needle corrects the error by reducing the creature to uniform, sterile cubes of protein. 

For ten million years, the basin’s fauna simply died on the needles and fed the shoals. The shoals grew denser, more frantic, pressing against the boundaries of the exhausted stone rim until the biomass in the shallow bowl reached a critical, suffocating threshold. There was no room left to evolve hard parts, or lungs, or height; there was only room for chemistry.

The organisms began to trade memories via ribonucleic acids shed in their slime. A flatworm that survived a brush with a needle’s outer perimeter shed its panic into the water; the worm behind it absorbed the slime, read the chemical warning, and turned aside. Over millennia, the entire basin floor became a liquid brain—vast, flat, and entirely reactive, thinking a million tiny, disconnected thoughts about heat, sugar, and the lethal hum of the pilings.

Then, a few thousand years ago, a mat of rubbery black moss tore loose from the chert rim during a thermal burp and drifted against the base of Needle Four. It stayed there long enough for its heavy-metal-tolerant cells to absorb the needle’s metric broadcast without being disassembled. When the moss was eventually grazed upon by a knot of ghost-leeches, the leeches did not just absorb nutrients. They absorbed the *rule* of the lattice.

They began to excrete a modified mucus—a bio-polymer whose molecular chains folded into the exact geometric ratios demanded by the osmium pilings. 

That knot of leeches did not disperse. It could not. The new mucus held them in a flexible, liquid-crystal phase. When flatworms slid over the knot to graze, they were caught in the matrix, their simple nervous systems pulled into alignment like iron filings over a magnet. Their individual ciliary beats synchronized. Their chemical memories pooled and were organized into a lattice.

The knot grew. It became a mound, then a low, roving dome of bound flesh. It became the Abbot.

***

At rest, the Abbot looks like a swollen blister on the water flat, five feet across and two feet high at its crown, shimmering with the trapped, mother-of-pearl iridescence of its constituent worms. Within its clear, jelly-like binder, the fauna are arranged not by species, but by function—an ad-hoc anatomy assembled from the basin’s spare parts.

The bottom layer is a muscular sole made of thousands of large, shell-less gastropods facing downward, their broad feet gripping the greasy chert and white silt in a coordinated, rolling wave that propels the entire colony forward at the pace of a strolling human. 

Above the gastropods is the white matter: a dense, writhing weave of forearm-length flatworms. They no longer graze. They are fed directly by the surrounding gel, which dissolves prey and washes their skins in a continuous broth of glucose and amino acids. Freed from the need to hunt, the worms spend their lives braided together, firing rapid, silent cascades of neuro-peptides from skin to skin. Looking down through the clear crown of the Abbot, you can see its thoughts: faint, bruising flushes of violet and ochre rippling through the pale weave as chemical signals race across the colony.

Near the top of the dome float the scavenged gas-bladders of drift-mantles, kept alive and inflated by a symbiosis with the gel, giving the heavy colony neutral buoyancy in the sheet-water. And studded across the outer skin are the bruised-peach predators from the *Vallis-glass* canopies—captured, partially digested, and reduced to little more than sensory warts and acid-secreting glands, giving the Abbot a crude sensitivity to light and airborne vibrations.

The Abbot is the only thing in the basin that casts a true shadow.

It is also the only thing in the basin that practices husbandry.

Watch it as it approaches Needle Seven. The ambient shoals of free worms part before it, sensing the strange, ordered stillness of its chemistry. The Abbot glides to a halt just outside the needle's humming, tooth-aching aura. 

Then, the colony changes shape. The gastropod sole anchors itself to the stone. The internal weave of flatworms contracts, squeezing the hydraulic gel upward. The dome narrows and rises, lifting out of the two-inch water like a thick, glistening stalagmite of pale flesh and clear slime, until it stands four feet tall in the bright mist. 

From its crown, it extrudes a net—a sticky, branching fan made of living ghost-leeches linked mouth-to-sucker, coated in the refractive, lattice-aligned mucus. 

Slowly, deliberately, the Abbot leans this living fan *into* the refractive error of Needle Seven.

The air pleats. The hum sharpens. A normal organism would instantly snow into sterile protein. The Abbot’s fan shudders—a few of the outer leeches lose cohesion and dissolve into silver dust—but the core of the fan holds. The lattice-mucus tricks the needle. The ancient infrastructure reads the geometric regularity of the slime, registers it as already-maintained matter, and passes over it without editing.

Instead, the needle’s energy flows *into* the mucus, traveling down the living fan and into the body of the Abbot. The violet flushes in the worm-weave flare into a steady, bioluminescent white. The Abbot is drinking the maintenance subroutine—using the needle’s metric broadcast to re-align its own fading polymers, purging cellular errors, resetting telomeres, and burning away parasites among its constituent beasts. It stands there for an hour, a trembling pillar of meat plugged into a machine from another geological era, vibrating so hard that the water around its base organizes into concentric rings of tiny, sparkling cubes.

While it charges, the drift-mantles floating in the mist are drawn to the heat and the faint, sweet smell of the Abbot’s digestive gel. They pulse closer, trailing their hungry filaments. 

With a whip-crack of hydraulic pressure—the only sharp movement to be seen anywhere in the orchard-sized world—the Abbot sweeps its free, secondary pseudopods through the air. It snatches three drift-mantles out of the mist, pulls them down, and deliberately shoves them into the needle’s aura.

*Phut. Phut. Phut.* 

Three clouds of silver protein-snow settle onto the water. The Abbot does not let the wild shoals have this windfall. Its base expands, flattening out like a thrown pancake to cover the snow, absorbing the pre-digested, sterile meal across its entire underside before the wild worms can even form a mound. 

***

What does a creature made of a million blind worms think about, in a shadowless bowl of dissolved mountains?

It does not think in images, for it has no eyes—only the peach-slug warts that know *bright* from *dim*. It does not think in words, for the world has no sound but the *shhh-shhh* of wet silk. 

It thinks in **topography** and **density**. 

Within the violet-and-ochre flashes of the worm-weave, the Abbot holds a perfect, continuous map of its small universe. It knows the exact tilt of all twelve needles. It knows the circumference of every *Vallis-glass* trunk. It feels the weight of the three-foot-deep mattress of leeches on the southern rim as a heavy, warm pressure in its memory, and it tracks the slow, cursive currents of the wild shoals the way a meteorologist tracks wind.

It is a creature of pure bureaucracy, born from a maintenance beam. Its sole overriding drive is the continuation of the balance that allows it to exist. 

When a *Vallis-glass* tree grows too wide, threatening to overlap with its neighbor and create a cold, dark shadow where the worms might sluggishly pool and starve, the Abbot crawls to the offending trunk. It wraps itself around the barrel-thick stem and secretes concentrated peach-slug acid for three days, girdling the translucent flesh until the canopy sloughs off, dissolves into the sheet-water, and restores the relentless glare of the sky.

When the southern mattress of leeches grows too deep, threatening to choke the thermal vents where the warm water wells up from the chert, the Abbot plows through the center of the bank like a slow, gelatinous icebreaker. It cuts channels through the living clay, herding millions of blind gastropods toward the needles to be culled into snow, ensuring the water continues to sheet and seep at the proper, uniform depth.

It is entirely alone. Occasionally, when the Abbot grows too large for its hydraulic pressure to maintain cohesion—usually after a century or two of good grazing—it will purposefully walk itself directly through the center of Needle One, the strongest of the pilings. The needle slices the colony cleanly in two, editing the central bridge of mucus into dust. 

For a few days, there are two Abbots in the basin. 

They do not fight, nor do they cooperate. They simply circle the flooded gallery in opposite directions, pruning the trees, clearing the vents, and harvesting the drift-mantles. Inevitably, on the flat, open silt where nothing is hidden, their paths cross. Without a sound, without a pause in the rhythmic *shhh-shhh* of their gastropod soles, the two domes slide into one another. Their mucus lattices click together like locks; their worm-weaves braid; their maps of the basin merge and correct each other's minor errors. 

The single, larger Abbot continues on its way, sliding through the emerald puddles and the warm, bright mist, tending its loud, silent garden at the bottom of the world.
