You are mistaken about only one thing: the ruins are not ruins. The masonry is not preserved by the water’s sterility; it is maintained. If you were to swim down to one of the waist-high stone posts and slice the braided kelp-hide rope with a steel blade, and if you were to return three days later, you would find the severed ends spliced back together with fresh, milky-white fiber that has not yet cured to black in the cold.

The valley is not abandoned. You simply arrived between the currents, when the Orcharders are tucked into the stone.

Wait until the pressure drops. Wait until that sudden tightening around the ribs announces the mid-day meltwater surge coming down from the peaks, and watch the low, circular huts when the mint-green light of the central tower flares through the dark.

What emerges from the low doorways is not an animal. The water here truly cannot support blood, or nerves, or the fragile, salty chemistry of meat. What steps out into the roaring current is a triumph of grafting—a walking, purposeful consortium of the valley’s own loud flora, bound together by ancient habit and a slow, cold intelligence that thinks in tensions, weights, and levers.

In silhouette, it looks vaguely like a broad-shouldered human wearing a heavy, bell-shaped diving dress of layered slate and black leather. It stands roughly seven feet tall, though it walks with a permanent, forty-five-degree lean into the current, its head-end bowed toward the basalt floor. 

It has no face. Where a head should be, there is a tightly bound bundle of hollow tube-reeds and tensely strung sinew-vines—an acoustic organ that trembles and flexes in the water, reading the roaring bass-notes of the bladder-pines and the sharp *cracks* of the bone-kelp as a clear, three-dimensional map of the dark. Tucked beneath this sensory bundle, protected by a collar of stacked slate plates wired directly through its wooden collarbones, is its core: a heavy, glowing loaf of lantern-moss. As the creature strains against the water, the friction agitates the moss inside its chest, causing faint, rhythmic pulses of mint-green light to leak from the seams of its bark-like ribs. It looks as though it carries a banked coal fire in its breast.

Its body is an intricate, terrifyingly strong latticework of black bladder-pine timber and pale bone-kelp. It does not have muscles that burn sugar and oxygen; it has hydro-mechanical actuators. Thick, muscular stalks of chalk-weed are woven around hinged joints of petrified wood. To take a step, the creature opens a series of valve-leaves along its back, allowing the violent current to rush through internal channels in its torso. The water pressure inflates a series of internal bladders while draining others, winching its left leg forward with a loud, fibrous *creak-clack*. It plants a wide, root-knotted foot onto the slick basalt, locks the knee-ratchet with a sound like a dropped wrench, shifts its valves, and winches the right leg forward. 

In the calm water between surges, an Orcharder is nearly paralyzed, forced to creep at a glacial pace or sleep wedged into the dry-stone huts to conserve the hydraulic pressure stored in its tightly coiled abdominal springs. It *needs* the violent currents to live. The storm is its breath and its heartbeat. The harder the water rages down from the unseen peaks, the faster and more fluidly the Orcharders move.

Watch one now, in the ten-minute glare of the green tower, pulling itself along the guide-ropes. 

Its "hands" are split, three-pronged clamps of calcified root-wood, lined with coarse, Velcro-like burrs. It reaches out, locks a clamp onto the braided kelp-rope, and lets the screaming current take its weight, using the kinetic energy of the water to wind up the torsion-springs in its shoulders. *Click-click-click-click* goes its spine, ratcheting tight. 

It reaches a wicker trap where three detached pine-bladders—pale, translucent spheres the size of medicine balls—are straining against the woven stems. With a swift, economical motion that relies on releasing a locked elbow-spring, the Orcharder draws one of the oversized black-flint knives from its hip. The movement is as fast and lethal as a sprung bear trap. It slices the tether of the wicker basket, gathers the buoyant bladders into a net slung across its own hunched back, and secures them. The upward pull of the gas-spheres lightens the creature’s immense, stone-ballasted weight, allowing it to take long, bounding, ten-foot strides across the valley floor with the current at its back.

There are perhaps forty of them in the basin. They do not speak, because they have nothing to say that is not already being said by the work. When two of them meet along a guide-rope in the bruised indigo gloom, they pause, lean their reed-bundled heads together, and briefly vent water through their chest-valves. A short, discordant chord of whistles and low thumps passes between them—an exchange of water-pressure data, a report on a frayed rope down-valley, the location of a chalk-weed dish that has been torn loose from its stalk—and then they pass on.

They are the stewards of the noise. Their entire existence is dedicated to maintaining the spacing and geometry of the valley floor so that the currents are never baffled and the plants never starve for friction. 

If two bladder-pines grow too close together, fouling their spheres and muting their deep, thrumming bass-notes, three Orcharders will arrive with heavy stone mauls. Standing in a circle, driven into a frenzy of motion by the freezing surge, they will swing the mauls with the snapping, trebuchet-force of bone-kelp, shattering the offending trunk at its base. If a field of ribbon-fern rolls into a hollow where the water is dead and still, an Orcharder will wading in, hook the heavy stone-roots with a pale driftwood gaff, and haul them back out into the howling wind-tunnel of the main valley.

And when an Orcharder grows too old—when its wooden joints are worn smooth and can no longer hold a ratchet, when its internal bladders calcify and split, or when a freak boulder rolling down from the peaks crushes its slate-armored chest—it does not die in the way an animal dies. 

The others come for it during the quiet intervals. Moving in slow motion through the freezing dark, using the last of their stored torsion, they unlash the fallen one. Nothing is wasted. The flint tools are taken up. The slate armor is used to patch the nearest windbreak. The still-living lantern-moss from its chest is carried into a dark hut to serve as a hearth-light, or grafted into the chest of a newly woven frame that has been soaking and curing in the back of a stone cairn for fifty years. 

Finally, they take the old, iron-hard ribs of its torso and weave them into the roof of a shelter, or lash them to a stone post to splint a weakened guide-rope. 

If you reach out and touch the braided rope that leads into the dark—the one you thought was an artifact of a vanished race—you will feel a faint, rhythmic ticking traveling down the fiber, distinct from the deep hum of the pines. It is the ratchet-click of an Orcharder’s hand-clamp, half a mile away in the freezing black, pulling itself steadily toward you against the flood, checking the line.
