To find the inhabitants of Oru-Vane, you first have to stop looking for movement and start looking for changes in texture.

A foreigner walking the narrow, blistering alleys between the condenser-banks will think the city is entirely automated, or perhaps recently abandoned. You might pass what you take to be a bronze stanchion or a swollen junction box—something cast in the same greasy, dark-red alloy as the rest of the street, beaded with the same mineral sweat—only to realize, fifty paces later, that the stanchion has rotated ten degrees on its base to watch you go.

They are called the *Kettled*, though among themselves they use a word that translates more closely to *the Seated* or *the Well-Cased*. They are human, or were three generations ago when the basalt tooth first breached the stratosphere and the charter-companies winched the first foundry-kits up the unweathered cliffside. Now, they are as much a part of the thermal architecture as the copper boilers.

In an atmosphere that sits at ninety-eight degrees and presses on the eardrums like warm tallow, naked human skin is a liability. A bare hand brushed against an intake manifold will blister instantly; lungs breathing the raw, unconditioned air will slowly coat themselves in a fine rime of copper sulfate and salt until they stiffen like parchment. Furthermore, to move quickly in Oru-Vane is to cook oneself from the inside out. The air cannot carry away the heat of exertion. 

So the people of the cleft have surrendered speed, and they have surrendered their skin.

An Oru-Vane guild-worker goes abroad in a *carapace*—a heavy, articulated suit of vulcanized gutta-percha, boiled leather, and beaten brass plate, sealed at the joints with dense mutton-tallow and grey litharge paste. These suits are not sleek; they are swollen, squat, and deeply asymmetrical, built to accommodate the coiled copper cooling-tubes that wind around the wearer’s torso beneath the plates. Every citizen carries a personal condenser on their back—a brass hump ribbed like a radiator, which catches the mineral sweat of their own labor, distills it through a charcoal filter, and cycles it back to a sipping-tube near the chin. 

Because the rock and the iron are knife-sharp, the soles of their boots are three inches thick, laminated from alternating layers of felt and lead sheet. Lead does not spark against flinty basalt, and it does not slip on the oily biofilm of the gutters; instead, it deforms slightly over the razor-edges of the stone, gripping the mountain with a dull, malleable weight.

Watch a Well-Tender at work in the deep fissures where the six-hour pistons turn. She does not walk; she shifts her ballast. Her movement is a slow, deliberate rocking from heel to heel, her heavy brass-shod hands resting on the warm guide-rails not for balance, but to read the city. The Kettled have largely abandoned speech in the streets. Sound carries weirdly in the thick, soupy air, echoing off the raw obsidian in flat, dead claps that give no sense of distance. Instead, they communicate through the iron. 

Every guild-worker carries a *tuning-iron*—a short, octagonal rod of high-carbon steel wedged into a slot on their forearm. By pressing the rod against a municipal pipe and tapping it with a weighted brass thimble, they send sharp, coded *tinks* racing through the plumbing. If you put your ear to a brine-conduit in the lower tiers, you will hear the whole district talking beneath the slow hydraulic sighs of the valves: a dry, clicking chatter like beetles in a wall, relaying pressure readings, marriage contracts, salt-yields, and gossip across miles of riveted copper.

Up close, when they unseal their heavy, wide-rimmed helmets in the slightly cooler gloom of the subterranean cistern-halls, the people themselves look half-smelted. 

They are universally pale, with the yellowish, waxy pallor of tallow candles kept in a warm cellar, because nobody in Oru-Vane ever stands naked under the linen sky. Their hair is kept shaved to the scalp to prevent salt-crusts from matting it, and their skin is perpetually slick with the heavy, ozokerite mineral-grease they rub into their pores to keep the damp heat from blistering them. They smell of this grease—a thick, flat scent of hot paraffin and camphor—layered over the sharp, metallic tang of their own dense sweat.

Their eyes are extraordinary. Because the light in the canyon is a dull, sourceless glare off dark glass and wet metal, and the interiors are lit only by the deep, infrared-heavy cherry glow of the basalt walls, the people have developed wide, dark pupils that seem to swallow the iris entirely. They blink very rarely. A man will look at you for three full minutes without his eyelids twitching, his face entirely slack—not out of hostility, but because facial expressions use thirty-two muscles, and using muscles generates heat. 

To show affection, or agreement, or anger, they use their hands, which are kept bare inside the halls. Their fingers are long, thick-knuckled, and stained a permanent, indelible blue-green around the nails from the copper-salts in the water. A slight, slow rotation of the wrist means *yes*. Two fingers laid flat against the breastbone means *I am listening*. A thumb drawn slowly across the eyebrow means *you are moving too fast; you are wasting yourself*.

Speed is the great obscenity in Oru-Vane. To hurry is the mark of a child, an idiot, or a low-lander whose blood hasn't yet thickened to the altitude. Their language has four different words for impatience, all of which are also words for different kinds of mechanical failure—*seam-split*, *bolt-shear*, *vapor-lock*, *boil-over*.

Because they live at the speed of the engines, their social lives are glacial, dense, and immensely long-memoried. A conversation over a bowl of grey, lichen-thickened brine-broth can take an entire four-hour watch to complete four sentences. They sit on benches carved directly into the warm basalt, leaning back so the rock’s deep-core fever seeps through their quilted house-coats, easing joints stiffened by decades of heavy armor. 

Yet they are not dreary. They have a fierce, meticulous vanity that expresses itself in the only medium the city provides: accretion.

In the lower world, people wash things to keep them new. In Oru-Vane, where the air itself deposits a rind of crystalline carbonate on everything it touches, cleanliness is a different art. It is the art of *training the crust*. 

A wealthy fractionator-master does not polish his brass carapace. Instead, over thirty years of service, he carefully paints the plates with small, precise tracks of tallow and acid, guiding the mineral-heavy condensation as it drips down his shoulders. By encouraging the deposits in some places and scraping them away in others, he grows a bespoke relief-map of crystals across his own armor. The great elders of the city walk the catwalks looking like roving geodes. Their shoulder-plates are bloomed with three-inch spires of electric-blue chalcanthite; their chests are ribbed with braided ridges of bone-white salt and yellow sulfur, grown layer by microscopic layer from the city’s sweat, recording the exact alleys they have walked and the exact vents they have tended for half a century. To chip another man’s crust in a crowded passage is a blood-offense, equivalent to slicing off an ear.

And then there are the *Silt-Eaters*, the lowest and strangest tier of the city’s life, who live out on the great hawsers between the cliffs.

The house-sized slag-buckets take fourteen hours to cross the three-mile gulf. Someone must ride them to grease the traveler-wheels and knock the mineral-icicles from the main cables before the weight snaps the iron. The families who do this never come into the rock-city at all. They live inside the double-walled hulls of the slag-cars, suspended two miles above the salt flats, rocked by the imperceptible, majestic sway of the cables.

They are smaller than the street-dwellers, bowed and bandy-legged from a lifetime of clambering over curved iron plate. Because they are out in the open glare of the linen sky, their suits are not brass, but tiled with thousands of salvaged *vitrifex* shells—the dull, grey silicate plates of the dead glass-makers, wired together with copper twine like rough dragon-scale. The silicate reflects the brassy sun better than metal does.

If you stand at the edge of an unguarded drop at the end of a hot alley, looking out across the dizzying gulf at a slag-car that appears painted against the sky, you might see two or three of them clinging to the hawser fifty feet ahead of the bucket. They look like knots of grey slag themselves, indistinguishable from the mineral tumors on the wire. Only if you come back an hour later will you see that the knots have moved six feet further out over the abyss, their lead-soled boots wedged into the lay of the giant cable, their stone-tipped scrapers rising and falling once every two minutes—*tink... tink... tink*—pruning the mountain’s slow, crystalline growth so the city can keep breathing.
