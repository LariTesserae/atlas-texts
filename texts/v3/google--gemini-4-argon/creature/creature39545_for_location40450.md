To see them properly, you have to stop looking for movement and start looking for weight. 

At first glance, a balcony four tiers down appears to be nothing more than an entanglement of *strangle-fig* bolsters and a few slabs of grey hornblende wedged into an alcove. Then the noon light shifts three inches to the west, and you realize the bolster has six knuckles, a broad, velvety heel wedged against the lip of the drop, and a pair of wide, dark eyes that have been watching you for forty minutes without a single blink.

They do not blink as we do. An aqueous tear film would evaporate in five seconds in this chalk-dry chimney, leaving the cornea scarred and salt-rimmed. Instead, their eyes—large, round, and the color of dark rosin—are coated in a slow-moving, viscous lipid secreted from glands at the bridge of the nose. Every quarter of an hour or so, a thick horizontal membrane sweeps slowly from the inner corner to the outer, like a squeegee spreading fresh oil over warm glass. It gives their gaze a heavy, unhurried permanence.

Up close, an individual smells of cedar-dust, warm tallow, and the faint, sharp tang of iron—like two pieces of flint struck together. Their pelage is not hair in the mammalian sense, which grows in loose shafts that trap air, but a dense, two-millimeter pile of hollow scales split at the tips into microscopic down, very much like the wing-dust of a hawk moth. When one brushes against the pewter leaves of a *slate-agave*, the white mineral powder of the plant does not merely sit on their coat; it works its way into the velvet, where it mixes with their skin oils to form a supple, fireproof, moisture-sealing crust. The oldest among them—those who spend their decades down in the deep, hot roots of the pit—are so heavily mineralized that their shoulders and flanks have the matte, silvery sheen of graphite. When they bend their elbows, the skin at the crease parts in dry, geometric plates like the rind of a cantaloupe, revealing the pale, lavender-grey hide beneath before sealing shut again.

They have no names, because a name is a shape made out of air, and they do not spend air. 

Their mouths are small, tight-lipped seams just wide enough to slide a shaved strip of palm-rind or a crystal of beetle-wax between their flat, millstone molars. Their nostrils are not open pits, but long, baffled slits lined with stiff bristles that curl inward, trapping the eighty percent of breath-moisture that would otherwise be lost on the exhale. To speak aloud would require bypassing those baffles; to shout would be an act of self-harm, equivalent to opening a vein.

Instead, they have their ears and their wrists.

The ears are astonishing structures. Rising from behind the heavy jawbone, each one is the size and rough shape of a dried fig leaf, ribbed with four cartilaginous struts that allow the pinna to fold like a fan, flare wide, or twist hundred-and-eighty degrees independently of the other. Because the skin of the ear is paper-thin and hairless, the dense web of capillaries inside is entirely visible against the pale afternoon light. 

A visitor who stays long enough on the ledges begins to read the weather of their faces. It is a language of hydraulic pressure and geometry. When two masons are wedging a five-hundred-pound hexagonal prism of basalt out of the upper wall using seasoned root-levers, there is no "one, two, three, heave." One mason rests his flat, horn-spaded thumb against the rock; his left ear flares wide and flushes from the color of old bone to a deep, venous plum from the base upward, like wine rising in a thermometer. The second mason’s ears drop flat against his skull, turning a sudden, drained chalk-white—*comprehension, readiness, brace*. Their heels lock into the stone steps. The plum-colored ear tips forward three millimeters. Both lean at the exact same microsecond. The rock parts from the parent column with a single, dry *tink*, like a glass cracking in a cupboard, and the dust that puffs from the fresh seam hangs between them in the dead air, undisturbed by a single heavy pant.

This is the public voice—the street-signage of the pit. You can look down from the third-tier root-bridges into the narrow, dim courtyards and see the conversation rippling across thirty individuals at once without a sound louder than a fingernail scratching bedlinen. A juvenile perched on the high, fluted shell of a passing tortoise spots a ripe seed-pod splitting on a *tallow-vine* thirty feet up; his ears flare and pulse twice—*maroon, cream, maroon*. Six yards away, an elder sitting in the crook of a fig-buttress does not turn her head, only cocks her right ear backward at a sharp forty-five-degree angle and lets the rim darken to the color of dried cloves: *Leave it. Not ready.*

The private voice happens at the wrists.

Because their fingers end in thick, insensitive shovels of dark horn—tools that grow continuously from the nail-bed to compensate for the constant grinding against volcanic glass and pumice—their fingertips are nearly dead to touch. All the nerves that would have gone to the pads of the fingers have migrated three inches south, to the underside of the wrist. There, the ash-velvet parts to reveal an oval patch of naked skin, perhaps the size of a walnut shell, cross-hatched with thousands of raised dermal ridges and kept supple by a tiny, dedicated sebaceous gland.

When two of them meet on a stone stile too narrow to pass without brushing, they do not step aside. One locks his knees into the crevice of the wall; the other swings slowly around his torso, using the first’s shoulders and hips as handholds, moving with the deliberate, three-point security of a sloth. As their centers of gravity pass one another, they press the insides of their wrists together for three or four seconds.

In that press is everything that cannot be shouted across a courtyard with blood-flushes. There is the exact temperature of the individual’s core; the turgor of the skin, which tells how many days it has been since they ate a moisture-bearing root; the minute, rhythmic twitch of the tendons beneath the dermis—a tactile grammar as complex as Braille read at sixty words a minute; and the chemical smear of the wrist-gland, which leaves behind a greasy signature of hormones, age, lineage, and mood that the other will carry on their skin for the next six hours. 

A household is not a building; a household is a cluster of twelve or fifteen individuals whose wrist-oils have been rubbed together so many thousands of times over the decades that they share a single, unified scent-profile and bacterial flora. They sleep piled in the small, octagonal masonry cells like sacks of warm grain, legs braided together, chins resting on ribs, breathing through each other’s fur to recycle the trace humidity of their lungs. 

Their relationship with the rest of the chimney’s inhabitants is so lacking in boundaries that an outsider might mistake it for indifference, though it is actually a total integration of metabolisms. 

Nothing in the fumarole can afford to flee, and nothing can afford to chase. Predation, in the red-toothed sense, does not exist here; the caloric math simply forbids it. Instead, there is a continuous, polite harvesting of surpluses.

Take the tortoises—the great, high-domed reptiles whose shells look like tiered terracotta wedding cakes, each scute ridged to catch the falling sulfur-dust. A full-grown female weighs perhaps eighty pounds and takes four hours to cross a courtyard thirty paces wide. The ash-furred people do not pen them, herd them, or slaughter them. They use them as furniture, as slow-moving escalators along the broader stepped ramps, and, most importantly, as digesters. The tough, blackened lower leaves of the fan palms are too fibrous and bitter with alkaloids for the hominids’ short guts to process. The tortoises eat them by the pound, grinding them with horny beaks, and seven days later excrete dry, odorless boluses the size of ostrich eggs, composed of pre-broken cellulose and rich gut-yeasts. 

An elder will walk alongside a tortoise for twenty minutes—matching its six-inch-per-minute stride by shifting his weight from foot to foot while braiding a rope of vine-bast—waiting for the tail to lift. The bolus is caught on a piece of frond-parchment before it even hits the flagstones, carried up to the warm, dark lower tiers, and wedged into a hollow of the living *strangle-fig* wall. Within a month, a dense, cream-colored shelf fungus—the texture of warm brie and seventy percent bound oil by weight—blooms out of the pellet, fed by the geothermal heat of the stone behind it. 

In return, the hominids groom the tortoises. Every afternoon, during the three hours when the bleached mountain sun drops straight down the throat of the pit, you can see the juveniles sitting astride the high shells in the courtyards, using the sharp, spade-edges of their thumbnails to pry the dry, crystalline scale-mites out from the leathery folds of the reptiles' necks and armpits. The tortoises stretch their long, wrinkled throats out flat along the warm basalt, eyes half-shut, while the children work in complete silence, popping the salt-rich mites into their own mouths like roasted hemp seeds.

Even death in the chimney is dry, slow, and architectural.

Because there is no water to breed putrefying bacteria, things do not rot here; they cure. When an old lizard loses its grip on the sheer hexagonal columns and drops into a thicket of *cinder-moss*, it does not bloat or draw flies—there are no flies. Within four days, the warm sulfur-updrafts and the hygroscopic dust of the *slate-agaves* have drawn the last teaspoons of oil and water from its tissues, leaving a perfect, hollow leather envelope that rattles faintly when a tortoise brushes past. The juveniles collect these husks, pack them with the rendered fat of the walnut-sized beetles and powdered pumice, and use them as caulking wedges between the fresh basalt slabs of the upper-tier nurseries.

And when one of the people themselves reaches the end—usually after eighty or ninety years, when their joints have become so calcified with absorbed volcanic dust that they can no longer bend their knees to climb the stiles—they do not bury them. There is no soil to dig, and the stone is too hard.

Instead, the dying individual climbs down. 

Supported by the wrists of their house-kin, taking three days to descend the five stories of ledges, they go down to the very floor of the throat—the first level, where the light from the oval sky above is only a pale, smoky twilight filtered through five layers of frond-canopies and root-bridges. Down here, the basalt is hot enough to make a stranger’s boots smell of scorched rubber, and the *strangle-figs* are not vines anymore, but massive, fused walls of grey wood that have swallowed the original masonry of the ancestors three centuries ago.

There are small, three-sided niches left intentionally in the living wood down here, warm as bread ovens. The old one sits inside, knees drawn up to the chest, elbows tucked into the groin to conserve space, chin resting on the flat horn spades of their folded hands. The house-kin come by one last time, pressing their wrists against the old one’s temples, forehead, and throat—leaving a thick, glistening glaze of family tallow over the ash-grey fur.

Then they take a living runner of the *tallow-vine*—a pale, fleshy root-tip as thick as a man’s thumb—and pin it across the open front of the niche with two splinters of stone.

The old one does not starve; their metabolism simply downshifts into the heat of the rock, the heart beating four times a minute, then twice, then once every three minutes, the lipid-film over their dark eyes thickening until it hardens into a clear amber lens. By the time the breath stops entirely, the vine-runner has already split into four tendrils, drinking the last trace moisture from the nostrils and the wrist-glands, pouring its slow, woody latex over the knees and shoulders. Within five years, there is only a smooth, bulbous swelling in the lower wall—a buttress of living timber and bone-ash that takes the weight of the four stories of tenements, tortoises, agaves, and silent, flashing ears stacked directly above its head.
