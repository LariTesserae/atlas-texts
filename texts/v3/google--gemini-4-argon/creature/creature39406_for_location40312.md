To find the masters of this basin, you have to look for the places where the carpet of shells rises up into a hill that does not match the contours of the underlying slate. 

Nothing with soft skin or warm blood could last twenty minutes on the valley floor; the lithovores would mistake the heat for a bacterial bloom and rasp the flesh from the bone before the creature had finished dying of shock. Even the great, lobster-like hunters are merely middle management—fast, violent, and short-lived, burning through their energetic reserves in a few frantic seasons before their joints foul with mineral grit and the carpet reclaims them.

The true sovereigns of the valley are the **Shingle-Backs**, though it takes the eye a long time to separate them from the terrain they harvest. 

At first glance, a Shingle-Back appears to be a slow-moving landslide—a low, oval tumulus of broken carapaces, limestone flakes, and living filter-worms, roughly the size of an overturned skiff. It moves at a glacial pace, perhaps ten yards an hour, rising and falling slightly as it negotiates the fluted gullies. It does not part the living carpet the way the fast predators do; the carpet simply continues right up and over its back, grazing on the detritus caught in its armor. 

Beneath that self-renewing dome of glued-together rubble is an animal that has abandoned the frantic speed of the arthropods in favor of the slow, crushing permanence of the rock itself. It is a vast, radially asymmetrical mollusk—or something that arrived at the same conclusion as a mollusk from a very different ancestral starting point. Its core is a thick, muscular mantle of rubbery, slate-gray flesh that never touches the air, kept permanently slick by a heavy mucus that smells sharply of ammonia and old iron. 

It does not walk on a single foot like a snail. Instead, its underside is a terrifying, geometric grid of hundreds of muscular, sucking cylinders, arranged in concentric rings. As the creature advances, the outer rings reach forward, clearing away the smaller lithovores with a sweep of heavy, bristled skirts, and plant themselves directly onto the bare limestone. They create a vacuum seal so powerful that when the creature shifts its weight, it sometimes pulls loose whole sheets of rotten slate with a sound like a pistol shot. 

Between these rings of hydraulic feet lies its true nature: it is not a single animal, but a mobile reef. 

The Shingle-Back is a farmer, a kiln, and a fortress. In the dark, warm vault beneath its dome—the space between its belly and the stone—it maintains a private, pressurized atmosphere. As it glides over the swarms, it does not crush everything in its path. Its outer skirts sort the biomass with blinding, tactile precision. The heavy-shelled grazers are shunted aside or flipped onto the roof to be cemented into the armor; the soft-bodied midges, the egg-sacs, and the sulfur-scraping worms are swept inward, toward the center of the underside.

There, they are not immediately digested. They are held in deep, folded gill-pockets where the Shingle-Back’s own waste—rich in urea and dissolved lime—is pumped over them. In that warm, rhythmic dark, the captured bacteria and worms multiply at five times the rate they manage out in the cold fog. The Shingle-Back carries its pastures inside its own skirts, harvesting only the overflow with a ring of slow, circular radulae that grind the excess into a grey paste.

Because they are so heavy, and because they do not need to chase anything, the Shingle-Backs have developed a relationship with time and sound that the frantic insects around them cannot comprehend. 

They are the architects of the valley’s acoustic landscape. While the cicada-things and the bellows-snails on the hoodoos scream at high, tearing frequencies to be heard over the general roar, the Shingle-Backs communicate through the stone itself. A Shingle-Back will lock all four hundred of its ventral suckers onto a solid vein of limestone, draw its massive, shell-encrusted dome tight against the rock, and contract its core muscles in a slow, shuddering spasm. 

The sound does not travel through the air; it travels through the bedrock. It is a sub-audible, bone-shaking *thrum*—a ten-hertz pulse that runs for miles along the fault lines of the basin. To a human, it would feel like the sickening drop of an elevator that lasts for three seconds; to another Shingle-Back two miles away, reading the bedrock through the sensitive nerve-nets in its feet, it is a clear, richly detailed broadcast.

Through these seismic groans, they negotiate territory, announce the discovery of a rich sulfur seep, and arrange their decades-long courtships. 

When two of them meet to mate, the valley floor changes shape. They draw alongside one another and seal their outer skirts together, forming a single, massive mound thirty feet across. For months, they do not move. The drift of shell-sand piles up against their windward side, creating a temporary ridge; filter-worms colonize their backs in such numbers that the mound grows a swaying pelt of white feathers; even the bellowing snails will sometimes descend from the spires to glue themselves to the new, high ground. 

Inside the sealed dome, in total silence—for the three-foot-thick wall of cemented shells and calcified mud blocks out the abrasive roar of the valley entirely—the two creatures exchange not only genetic material, but starter-cultures of their internal pastures, ensuring the next generation will have the hardy strains of sulfur-bacteria needed to survive. 

They do not lay eggs. An egg in this valley is merely a snack waiting to be cracked. Instead, when the two adults finally unseal their skirts with a long, wet, tearing sigh and grind away in opposite directions, they leave behind a litter of *shards*. 

A juvenile Shingle-Back is born the size of a dinner plate, already wearing a thick, calcified wedge of its parents' own dorsal armor, broken off from the lower rim of the dome during the separation. It is flat, sharp-edged, and fast—at least compared to its parents. For the first ten years of its life, it does not farm; it acts like a living wedge. It slides *under* the carpet of lithovores, using its sharp, porcelain-hard edge to pry them right off the rock, stealing the bacterial mats they have uncovered before sliding away into the shell-hash to digest. 

Only when it has grown too heavy to slip beneath the swarm does it begin to secrete the slow-setting, mineral glue from its dorsal pores, catching the dead shells that rain down upon it, building its roof layer by layer, century by century, until it is no longer an animal moving through the valley, but a piece of the valley that has decided to walk.

And on the very oldest of them—the true ancients that have been grinding across the basin for five hundred years, whose domes have grown twelve feet high and are indistinguishable from the limestone hoodoos—something else lives. 

High up on the peak of a primeval Shingle-Back’s dome, above the reach of the lobster-predators and out of the suffocating slurry of the floor, sits the **Listener**. 

There is never more than one per dome. It looks like a cross between a starved mantle-piece clock and a white spider the size of a sheep. Its eight legs are not made for walking; they are fused directly into the calcified rubble of the Shingle-Back’s peak, grown right into the stone-glue like the roots of a tree. Its body is a hollow, parchment-thin drum of translucent chitin, stretched tight over a delicate frame of hollow bones. 

The Listener has no eyes, no mouth, and no stomach. Its roots tap directly into the vascular system of the giant beneath it, sipping a tiny, negligible fraction of the Shingle-Back’s rich, iron-heavy blood. In return, the Listener acts as the mountain’s ears. 

While the Shingle-Back can feel the bedrock, it is entirely deaf to the air. The Listener’s parchment body vibrates in sympathy with the deafening, brassy interference patterns of the screaming spires. It reads the acoustic shadows cast by the hoodoos, the tearing-silk sound of the wind-fanners on the ridges, and the dry clatter of the large predators plowing through the hollows. 

When a collapse occurs on the valley wall, or when a flash-flood of calcium-slurry begins to roar down a distant gully, the Listener’s drum-body shivers in a specific, complex rhythm. It sends a sharp, chemical spike down through its root-legs into the Shingle-Back’s nervous system. 

Minutes before the wall of grey mud and tangled centipedes arrives, the living hill simply stops, alters its course by thirty degrees, and grinds slowly up onto a high slate fin, letting the catastrophe slide harmlessly past in the dark below, while the pale, motionless silhouette on its peak continues to tremble in the mist, translating the roar of the world into the slow language of stone.
