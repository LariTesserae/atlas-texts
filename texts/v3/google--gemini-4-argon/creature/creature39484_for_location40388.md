If you stand in the jaundiced cone of a sodium lamp and watch the standing waves in a puddle, it takes a long time to realize that some of the geometry is moving against the current.

The things that live here do not have flesh, because flesh is essentially trapped water, and the sub-audible bass of the deep turbines would turn a cellular structure into froth within an hour. Instead, what crawls and roots and grazes across the slag-flats is built on the principle of the sponge, the gasket, and the leaf-spring. Everything is porous; everything lets the sound and the cold, poisonous water pass right through it. 

At the lowest level, barely distinguishable from the wet clinker itself, are the silt-weavers. At first glance, they look like spilled handfuls of rusted cotter pins and copper turnings that have washed into the shallow depressions. Watch one for twenty minutes, however, and you will see it unspool a single, hair-fine filament of drawn brass, hook it into a pore of vitrified brick three inches away, and winch its loose, rattling body forward. They have no central organs. They are colonies of heavy-metal bacteria that have learned to smelt at room temperature, binding iron filings and silicate grit together with a dense, grease-like mucus. They graze on the yellow sulfur-algae in the warm runnels, leaving behind neat, ribbon-like tracks of bare, scrubbed metal that look as though someone has taken a wire brush to the floor of the bog.

Where the silt-weavers gather, the larger things come to feed on them, though "feeding" in the iron-bog looks less like hunting and more like salvage.

Out in the absolute dark between the lamp-standards, moving through the ankle-high wire-scrub, are the things that might be called the bog-striders, or the calipers. They are the size of hunting dogs, though they weigh nearly four hundred pounds and have no mass to speak of—only frame. A strider consists of three or four long, bowed legs made of laminated bog-iron and petrified ash, meeting at a central, knuckle-like hinge that sits four feet off the ground. Beneath this hinge hangs a heavy, pendulous sac of vulcanized, rubbery lichen-hide, slick with machine oil. 

They do not walk so much as they measure the ground. Because the floor is constantly vibrating at twenty cycles per second, a strider does not need muscles to lift its legs; it simply alters the tension in its central hinge, catching the upward phase of the ground-tremor to vault a single leg forward, locking it into place with a dry *snick* as the vibration drops. They move across the flats in slow, deliberate triangles, like draftsman’s compasses walking across a blueprint. When a strider passes over a colony of silt-weavers, the heavy sac beneath its hinge drops on a braided wire tendon, presses into the wet slag with the weight of an anvil, and comes up coated in iron filings, bacteria, and crushed clinker. Inside the sac, there is no stomach—only a galvanic bath of concentrated copper sulfate and acid where the metals are slowly stripped of their bacterial bindings, generating the tiny trickle of millivolts the strider needs to tighten and loosen its hinges.

Because there are no eyes here—light is too scarce and the mist too thick—everything perceives the world through impedance. Every creature is an ear, or rather, an accelerometer. 

A strider knows where the sunken tarn is because the water deadens the 20-hertz thrum of the floor; it knows where the leaning lamp-standard is because the hollow iron pole rings like a tuning fork in the three-minute intervals when the distant relief-stacks shriek. They navigate by the shadows that solid objects cast in the vibration of the ground.

And then there are the things that follow the dredge-plows.

The plows themselves are only machines—blind, deaf hulls dragging their chains along the buried induction cables. But a plow that has been crawling the flats for ten thousand years is no longer a single object; it is a moving reef. 

If a plow passes near one of the sodium lamps, you can see that its cast-iron hull is buried six inches deep in a shivering coat of valve-limpets and bellows-worms. The limpets are flat, overlapping scales of layered rust and zinc that clamp onto the vibrating hull, using the kinetic shiver of the plow’s engine to pump water through their gills. Between them grow tall, swaying chimneys of soft lead and blackened rubber—organisms that look like perished radiator hoses, which lean toward the engine block to soak up the radiant heat.

Trailing behind the plows, riding in the trough of churned, wet slag turned up by the heavy chains, are the chain-leech and the harrow-crabs. 

The crabs are wide, flat things, shaped like manhole covers or broken gear-wheels, with six or eight multi-jointed legs tucked entirely beneath their rims so that the heavy chains cannot snag them. They do not eat the algae or the weavers; they eat the fresh, un-oxidized iron that the plows scrape bare from the deeper strata. They scuttle sideways in the wake of the chains, pressing broad, rasping plates of silicon carbide against the newly exposed metal, grinding it down into fresh filings before the cold rain can rust it, drinking the raw, galvanic spark of the oxidation process itself. 

If there is a master of this landscape—something that approaches a mind—it is not a beast that roams the flats, because nothing that roams can grow complex enough to think without being shaken apart by the deep turbines. The intelligence of the bog is sedentary, and it lives in the leaks.

Scattered across the miles of wet dark are the steam-wens: places where an ancient, high-pressure line fourtiers below has cracked, sending a permanent, scalding trickle of water and oil up through the compressed ash. Over the epochs, something has grown over these vents to cap them. 

From twenty yards away, in the red glare of the distant blast-pits, a wen looks like a slumped cairn of slag-blocks or a discarded boiler shell covered in those pale, dinner-plate lichens. Come closer, and you see that the cairn is breathing—not air, but pressure. 

The structure is a dense, six-foot-tall labyrinth of calcified mineral deposits, braided wire-scrub roots, and scavenged plates of vitrified brick, all cemented together with hardened grease. Inside, it is a cathedral of tiny, whistling pipes and fluttering leather valves. The organism has grown *around* the steam leak, routing the scalding water through thousands of narrow, self-extruded lead capillaries that wind through the cold mud around it, forming a vast, subterranean condenser coil. 

These are the Switch-Minds, though they have nothing to switch. By opening and closing thousands of tiny, sphincter-like mineral valves within their bodies, they play the escaping steam like an organ, stepping the massive, violent pressure of the deep pipes down into gentle, rhythmic pulses of heat and low-frequency clicks. 

They do not think in words or images; they think in hydraulic logic. A wen is a biological Babbage engine powered by condensation. Every time a distant sluice-gate opens and sucks a tarn dry, the drop in the water table alters the back-pressure in the wen’s cooling coils. The wen registers this; a hundred little lead flappers inside its dark, wet core clack shut; the pitch of its internal whistling drops by a third; a new pattern of warm water is pulsed out into the surrounding bog through its root-pipes.

Half a mile away, another wen feels that pulse of warmth arrive through the wet clinker four hours later, coupled with the three-minute shriek of the relief stack, and adjusts its own valves in answer.

Across the whole of the dark, roaring basin, these blind, steaming mounds are locked in a slow, millennial conversation conducted through water-hammer and ground-tremor. They have mapped the entire deeper geology by the way the turbine-bass echoes off the collapsed boiler shells miles down. They know three hours in advance when a flat will shudder and sink, and before the sluice-gate even cracks open below, the wens in that sector will tighten their vents, whistling a sharp, high-frequency warning into the wet floor that sends the caliper-striders vaulting away toward higher swells and causes the wire-scrub to draw its crystalline beads back into its thorns.

Nothing builds here. Nothing remembers what the sky was, or why the dredge-plows must drag their chains from the east to the west until the chains wear through and leave a long, rusting snake of iron links to be eaten by the crabs. There is only the slow, patient metabolism of the rust, the geometry of the standing waves, and the low, hydraulic ruminations of the mounds in the dark, calculating the exact weight of the rain.
