At first, in the submarine gloom of a second-tier alley, you do not see them; you only smell them—a sharp, medicinal tang of camphor and stale mutton-fat that cuts through the pervasive reek of celery-sap—and you hear the dry *snip-snip-snip* of bone shears. 

When a figure finally detaches itself from the swollen, mossy masonry of a root-wall, the eye struggles to separate the inhabitant from the town. They are a squat, barrel-chested people, short-limbed and built with a low, stubborn center of gravity. A tall man among them barely clears four and a half feet, yet his shoulders are nearly as wide as the sunken trenches he walks through, forcing him to move with a practiced, rolling corkscrew gait—right shoulder forward, then left, sliding between the wet black trunks without ever truly bruising the bark.

Their clothing is not woven, for nothing dry enough to spin grows here. Instead, they wear tiered capes and aprons of cured, overlapping shelf-fungus—pale, flexible bracket-leathers scraped thin, boiled in resin, and oiled until they turn the color of smoked bacon. Over this, for warmth, they drape heavy mantles of dead, dry beard-lichen netted together with sinew, so that when a citizen stands still in an alcove to let another pass, they become an indistinguishable hump of grey-green vegetation, dripping in the fog.

Beneath these fibrous cowls, their faces are broad, flat, and startlingly pale—the color of lard or peeled tallow, untouched by the sun—with high, shelves of cheekbones and noses whose nostrils flare wide to drink the thin, freezing mountain air. Their eyes are dark, heavy-lidded against the perpetual drip from the canopy, and surrounded by a permanent squint-web of wrinkles stained mahogany-brown by wood-tannin. 

Look at their hands, and the nature of their pact with the cleft becomes clear. They go bare-handed even in the rawest frost, their fingers short, thick as carrots, and permanently horned with calluses. The skin from their knuckles to their wrists is stained an indelible, greenish-black from centuries of handling mountain-beech cambium, and their thumbnails are grown out long, thick, and curved like horn gouges, hardened over tallow-lamps so they can score bark and nip invasive buds as casually as other people scratch an itch. They never stop touching the walls. As they walk, their heavy, bare hands trail along the damp trunks, feeling the turgor pressure under the bark, reading the slow, hydraulic pulse of the house. If a shoot has crept half an inch over a bone window-spacer since yesterday, a thumb flicks out—*tik*—and the pale sprout is pinched off and popped straight into the mouth. They chew the bitter, turpentine-tasting shoots constantly, like cud; it keeps the scurvy away and turns their saliva dark green.

They call themselves the *Knerk*, or something that sounds very like it—a wet, glottal click at the back of the throat followed by a nasal hum, a word that does not travel more than ten feet before the yard-deep sphagnum sponges it out of the air. Because the moss swallows high pitches and the creaking wood masks the low, they rarely speak aloud in the streets. Instead, they communicate across the tiers by *thumping*. 

Every load-bearing trunk is a telegraph wire under terrific tension. A woman on the third-level balcony, wanting to know if her sister two stories down in the dark peat-cellars has spare tallow, will take the heavy horn-handled mallet at her belt and strike a fused bough-joint three times—*tok, tok-tok*. The vibration travels down the tight grain of the pleached beech, bypassing the deadening moss entirely, and her sister feels the rhythm through the soles of her split-hoof boots and taps back against the living wall of her kitchen. At certain hours of the evening, the whole cleft hums and chatters through the soles of the feet with a hundred silent conversations, the entire green monolith trembling like a plucked bass string while the air itself remains dead and quiet save for the *thwack* of the tipping drainage reeds.

Inside the warm, hollow root-chambers, the Knerk shed their heavy lichen-mantles, revealing skin smeared with a glistening coat of rendered beetle-grease and pine-pitch to keep the damp from rotting their pores. Here, where the composting peat-packs beneath the floorboards raise the temperature to a muggy, fungal sixty degrees, the air is thick as broth. There is no fire—fire is the great, unspoken terror of a town pumped full of turpentine-sap, despite the wet—so there is no smoke. For light, they keep wooden bowls of bruised, rotting roots that harbour a cold, blue-green foxfire fungus, just bright enough to turn the greased faces of a family into a ring of floating, tallow-colored moons.

Their lives are ruled by a patient, claustrophobic vigilance. A house among the Knerk is not an object you build and then ignore; it is a slow, blind beast that wants to crush you in your sleep. The cushions of the *Raoulia*—the great, rock-hard green masonry plant—swell by fractions of an inch a month, and the fused beech-trunks are forever trying to heal the wounds that form the rooms. Every household has its rack of bone wedges, stone spreaders, and garrotes of twisted gut. Before a family goes to sleep in their suspended, hammock-like pouches of netted root-fiber, the eldest takes a heavy wooden screw-jack and checks the door-frames, cranking the timbers back a quarter-turn against the inward bulge of the living wood. 

To be lazy here is to be entombed. There is a chamber on the lowest tier, down near the black, liverwort-choked greywacke, that belonged to an old widower who took sick in the lungs three winters ago. Nobody came to turn his jacks or shear his lintels for six weeks. When his nephews finally forced their way down the narrowed trench, the doorway had puckered shut to the size of a dinner plate, fused into solid, knotty wood, and the room inside was no longer a room, just a solid mass of pale, blind root-hairs and swollen cambium that had already digested his bedding and cracked his bones for the calcium. They did not dig him out; to cut that deep would bleed the sap-pressure of the three houses above him. They simply grafted a weeping-willow shoot over the puckered hole to mark it, and now use the bulge of his former parlor as a step to reach the second tier.

Yet they are not a grim people, only an intensely inward, slow-moving one. Their metabolisms seem to have borrowed something from the plum-sized black beetles and the torpid, berry-drunk birds in the roof. They sit for hours in the warm, dark compost-heat of their domes, passing around bladder-skins of a thick, syrupy beer brewed from tapped beech-sap and fermented crowberries. It tastes of cough-syrup and wet cellar-stone, and it makes them warm at the extremities and mildly hallucinatory, so that they sit rubbing their broad, flat feet together, listening to the great, ship-like groans of the grove shifting against the sharp greywacke fault, arguing softly over whose great-grandfather grafted the load-bearing arch over the south gutter, and whether the wood in the west wall is growing "sweet" or "sour" this decade.

Their dead do not leave the cleft, and neither do their wastes; the mountain outside is sterile, razor-edged stone and blank cloud, offering nothing. Everything that dies in the settlement goes down through trapdoors in the lowest floors, packed into the deep, steaming peat-beneath-the-roots along with the sheared shoots, the shed lichen, and the beetle-shells. The town eats its dead, turns them into heat to warm the living floors, and draws their marrow up through forty feet of twisted, black-barked *nothofagus* to make the waxy leaves that catch the fog at the top of the sky. 

If you stand on the highest woven walkway, where the grey-haired birds sit digesting in the mist, and look down into a thirty-foot vertical shaft between the root-walls, you might see an old woman on a cantilevered balcony two levels below. She is leaning out over the drop, a basket of bone-spacers on her hip, driving a fresh granite wedge into the armpit of a bough with a stone maul. With every blow—*clink, clink*—a spray of cold water shakes loose from the red sphagnum overhead and showers her greased, leather-fungus hood. She does not look up. She wipes the turpentine-sap from her horn-thumb onto her tongue, tests the tension of the branch with a broad, dark hand, and slips back through an oval hole in the tree, pulling a curtain of wet lungwort shut behind her.
