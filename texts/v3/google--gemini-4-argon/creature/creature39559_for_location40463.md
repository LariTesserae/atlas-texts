He is called the Gleaner, though there is no one left within four hundred miles to use the name. 

You do not see him at first because your eyes are looking for something soft, something with wet eyes and pliant skin that flinches from the roar. Instead, look at the sagging center of the iron catwalk, right where the worn support strut trembles under the ceaseless battering of the tide below. What looks like a heap of discarded canvas, oxidized copper sheeting, and bundled wire suddenly shifts its weight, uncoiling with the dry, ratcheting sound of a rusty winch.

He has lived in the basin for forty years, or perhaps he is the third generation of a single, stubborn post-holder who refused to walk out across the pale plateau when the company carts stopped coming. Either way, the pocket has claimed him. It has dried him out, cured him in ozone and talc, and ground away everything unnecessary.

He is a tall, stoop-shouldered figure, entirely encased in an armored patchwork of the basin’s own materials. No woven cloth could survive a week in this abrasive fog; the very air would sand it to lint. Instead, his coat is a heavy overlap of cured shale-back plates, drilled through with a hand-awl and laced together with bright bronze spindle-tendons. When he moves, he sounds like the basin itself—a shuffling, ceramic clatter that blends seamlessly into the background roar. Beneath the slate-mail, he wears the leathery, accordion-pleated hides of dead bellows-worms wrapped around his joints like gaiters, sealing his knees, elbows, and neck against the relentless silica dust. 

His face is hidden behind a mask of hammered sheet-lead and leather, fitted with two thick lenses chipped from the bottoms of heavy glass insulators. Over his nose and mouth hangs a bulbous snout—the repurposed thorax of a giant bellows-worm, stuffed with shredded fibrous mineral-crust—through which he breathes with a slow, rhythmic *hiss-click* that answers the scavengers clinging to the girders above his head.

Only his hands are bare. They are extraordinary, frightening things. Decades of exposure to the talc, the static, and the microscopic grit have turned his skin into something resembling dark, oiled horn. The nails are thick as iron spades, worn flat at the tips. A pale gray powder is permanently worked into the deep creases of his knuckles, making his hands look as if they were carved from the very soapstone of the walls. He does not sweat. He cannot afford to. His body runs on a miser’s rationing of moisture, reclaimed through a condenser tube that runs from his breathing-snout down into a copper flask strapped against his groin.

To walk on the floor of the basin is to be dragged down, abraded to the bone, and reduced to powder in an hour. When the Gleaner must descend from the catwalk, he uses the *skids*. 

They are wide, flat-bottomed snowshoes fashioned from the corrugated iron of the generator shed, curled up at the toes like heavy sled-runners. He drops from the catwalk—six feet down—and lands on the moving backs of the swarm with a flat, metallic *crash*. Instantly, he is in motion. You cannot stand still on the shale-backs; to stand still is to have your feet swept out from under you by the slow-motion black avalanche. He rides them the way a river-driver rides a logjam, knees bent, shifting his weight in short, choppy jerks, surfing the grinding, knee-deep current of plates. 

As he slides across the pool of clicking carapaces, he carries a six-foot grounding pike—an iron rod tipped with a heavy brass hook, trailing a length of braided copper wire that drags behind him through the swarm. 

Every time the generator in the shed goes *chug-chug-thump* and the anodes hum, the static charge in the basin spikes. The Gleaner’s coat stands out; blue corona-sparks hiss from his shoulders and dance along the rims of his glass lenses. The spindles go mad during the pulse, vibrating until they blur, darting toward any concentration of charge. When three or four of the wire-legged predators converge on him, stepping high and fast over the clattering floor, the Gleaner does not swing his pike like a club. He simply touches the brass hook to the soapstone wall—or plunges it deep into the conductive silt at the very bottom of the pile—and grounds the circuit through their bodies. There is a sharp *crack*, a puff of burnt-tendon smoke, and the spindles drop, their legs curling inward like heated copper shavings. He scoops them up without breaking his sliding stride, stuffing the twitching bronze bundles into a wire-mesh creel at his hip.

He is not an invader here; he is the apex scavenger, the regulatory valve of the little iron church. 

His life is dictated by the rhythm of the flywheel in the northern shed. Every three hours, he skates across the churning floor to the half-buried corrugated shack, wading thigh-deep through the drifts of hollow, shed exoskeletons that crunch like dry autumn leaves. Inside the shed, the noise of the basin is muted to a dull, grinding vibration that comes up through the soles of his boots. Here, in the dark, smells of old grease and hot iron fight back the stench of crushed ants. 

He tends the engine with a religious, mechanical intimacy. He has no oil left, so he lubricates the bearings with the finest grade of talc scraped from the exhaust vents of the bellows-worms, mixed with a pale, waxy fat rendered from spindle-joints over a tiny electrical coil. He takes a flat iron scraper and clears the silica pellets from the flywheel trough. He checks the tension on the braided anode cables. He does not know *why* the machine must run—the logbooks rotted to dust fifty years ago—only that when the *chug-chug-thump* falters, the current dies; when the current dies, the salt-crust stops blooming on the walls; and when the crust stops, the shale-backs begin to eat the iron towers themselves, chewing through the rivets with their iron-sulfide mandibles until his sky-high home threatens to topple into the grinder.

So he keeps the current flowing. In return, he harvests.

He has no fire—there is nothing to burn—so he cooks with resistance. Up on the catwalk, inside a shelter cobbled together from flattened condenser drums and bolted like a swallow’s nest to the side of Tower One, he hooks two leads to the main cable. He clamps a chunk of dense, mineral-heavy shale-back meat between two copper plates and lets the current pass through it until it turns gray, dry, and hot, smelling faintly of sulfur and toasted nuts. He eats it in the dark, lifting the lead mask just an inch, chewing slowly with teeth that have been ground completely flat by forty years of unavoidable grit. 

For water, he harvests the bellows-worms. They are the only things in the basin that hoard dampness, trapping the microscopic trace-moisture of a hundred thousand clicking respirations inside their leathery folds. Once a week, he crawls out along the underside of the catwalk, moving hand-over-hand above the deafening black sea, and milks them—squeezing their concertina bodies until they wheeze and spit a few precious, murky ounces of water into his copper funnel. He filters it through four feet of pure shale-back silica until it is clear, flat, and tastes of cold stone.

At night, the pale sky above the rim turns the color of tarnished silver, and the temperature drops like a stone. The basin does not sleep, because friction does not sleep, but the tempo slows. The deafening roar of the rock-crusher drops an octave, becoming a low, rhythmic, tectonic *shuck-shuck-shuck* as the shale-backs huddle deeper into the pile to share their kinetic warmth.

The Gleaner does not sleep in his metal nest on the tower when the frost comes. Instead, he climbs down. 

He leaves his iron skids lashed to the lowest rung of the ladder, wraps his lead mask tightly in a length of cured worm-hide, and steps directly into the swarm. He lets himself sink. The platter-sized arthropods do not bite him; to them, wrapped in his slate-mail and smelling of ozone and stone, he is merely a strangely shaped boulder. They slide over his legs, his hips, his chest, three layers deep, pressing against him with a heavy, dry, insistent weight. 

Down beneath the surface of the herd, the air is eighty degrees warmer, heated by the ceaseless rubbing of a million chitinous plates. The sound is inside his bones now—a vast, mindless, comforting purr that drowns out the silence of the empty world above the rim. He lies back at a forty-five-degree angle, supported by the slow, churning tide of living stone, his brass-hooked pike clutched to his chest like a bishop's crosier. 

Every few minutes, the engine in the shed goes *chug-chug-thump*. The brass anodes in the walls hum. A tingle of raw electricity washes through the soapstone, through the million clicking shells, and straight through the Gleaner’s horn-hard body, making his stiff fingers twitch once in his sleep, before the slow, black current carries him another three inches toward the southern wall.
