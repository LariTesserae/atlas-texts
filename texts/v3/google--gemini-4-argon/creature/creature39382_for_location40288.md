In a world without dark, shelter is not a place; it is an angle of refraction, or the acoustic shadow behind a rivet head, or the six-inch boundary layer of slow water hugging a mile of poured concrete.

To survive in the Sump is to negotiate with two unyielding facts: the light that never shuts off, and the sound that never stops. Because the carbon-arc ceiling glares at a permanent, shadowless noon, every creature in the mid-water has been forced to solve the problem of its own silhouette. 

Look closely at the silver smelt, or the spindle-shaped hunters that harry them, and you will notice they have no eyes—or rather, their eyes have sunk beneath translucent plates of bone and skin, reduced to mere light-meters that register the flickers of passing hulls above them. Eyes are dense, dark, pigmented things; in the cerulean void, a pair of pupils looks like two buckshot pellets suspended in gin. A predator five hundred yards away can lock onto a pair of black retinas against the white ceiling and close the distance in six seconds. So the fish of the Sump have gone blind in order to go invisible. They navigate entirely by the *thrum-clack*. 

The fifty-mile intake valves are the sun and moon of the basin’s sensory world. Every creature with a spine has developed a hypertrophied lateral line—a stitch-mark of sensory pits running from gill to tail that reads the industrial racket the way a bat reads its own shriek. When the northern valves slam shut, the compression wave rolls south at five thousand feet per second. It strikes the fifty-yard Thermal Columns, rings the redwood-thick Tension Cables, and bounces off the mile-square expansion joints. To the spindle-hunters—which might be called **glass-jacks**, though there is no one here to name them—the entire basin lights up in crisp, acoustic relief twice every minute. They do not see the smelt; they feel the faint, foamy acoustic muffler that a million tiny swim-bladders cast against the roar of the pumps.

This reliance on the valve-pulse has bred its own quiet horrors. Near the Thermal Columns, where the water blurs with heat-shimmer, lives a thing that looks at first like a torn ribbon of maintenance vinyl or a peeling sheet of translucent lime-scale. It is a flatworm, cousin to the marine flukes, though here it has grown to the size of a banquet table. It has no skeleton to vibrate, no swim bladder to echo. To the sonar-sense of the glass-jacks, it has the exact acoustic signature of the turbulent, hot-and-cold water layers shearing against each other. It rides the vertical downdrafts like a falling bedsheet, wrapping itself around the jacks when they come to warm their pale, cold-cramped muscles near the boiler-plate. It does not bite; it simply wraps the fish in a slick, rubbery shroud and lets the thirty-knot current drag them both down into the twilight of the floor, digesting the hunter through its skin on the long fall down.

Closer to the structures, life stops trying to be invisible and starts trying to be architecture.

On the Thermal Columns themselves, where the riveted steel plates radiate a steady, trembling heat into the thirty-four-degree water, the food chain is a matter of millimeters. The green turf-mosses are grazed by creatures that have abandoned the open water entirely. If you were to hover ten feet off Column 14-North, you would swear the boiler-plate was clean save for the moss and the orange streaks of iron oxide. If you placed your palm against the metal, the rivets themselves would slide away under your fingers.

They are **rivet-limpets**—mollusks that have evolved shells with the exact hemisphere, diameter, and dull zinc-gray patina of the three-inch steel fasteners used by the basin's builders. They secrete a grey, calcareous shell heavily doped with iron and zinc leached from the water, and they arrange themselves in neat, vertical rows along the seams of the plates, mimicking the industrial grid. When the sluice-currents howl past at thirty knots, a creature with an irregular shape is plucked from the wall and flung ten miles south; the limpets, shaped with the precise hydrodynamic profile the engineers calculated for the rivets, sit untouched in the screaming slipstream.

They are hunted by the **spanner-worms**—segmented, armored polychaetes three feet long that live inside the overlaps of the boiler-plate. The worms have flat, wedge-shaped heads of dense chitin, black and greasy as molybdenum disulfide. They do not pry the limpets up; the suction of a rivet-limpet is strong enough to tear its own meat from its shell before the foot lets go. Instead, the worm slides its chisel-head flat against the steel, aligns itself with the current, and uses the thirty-knot water like a hammer, letting the hydraulic force drive its wedge-jaw under the limpet's lip in a single, sharp *tink* that vanishes into the ambient hiss of the oxygen manifolds.

Above them all, upside down, is the nursery.

Two hundred feet below the true sea, the armored glass ceiling is a flat, blazing sky. Because gravity pulls dead matter down, the ceiling is clean of debris, except for what rises: heat, stray grease from the distant intake pumps, and the endless, fizzing curtains of micro-bubbles from the oxygenation lines. The bubbles hit the three-foot glass panes and flatten into coalescing, quicksilver lakes of trapped air that skate across the glass until they are sucked into the ceiling's return vents.

Here, walking inverted on the boundary between water and air, is an entire inverted benthic zone. The microscopic crustacea are the thickness of curdled milk right against the hot glass, cooking in the unshielded ultraviolet of the carbon-arcs. Feeding on them are the **skimmers**—pale, flat-bellied fish that have flipped their orientation entirely. Their backs, which face down into the three-thousand-foot abyss, are mirrored silver to match the glare of the lights behind them; their bellies, pressed to the glass, are black with dense melanin to keep their internal organs from being broiled by the arc-lamps inches away. They have modified pectoral fins fringed with stiff, dry-looking bristles that reach up *through* the skin of the air-pockets, skimming the greasy, oxygen-rich foam that collects on the glass.

When a carbon-arc bulb the size of a locomotive finally burns out—an event that happens once every few years in a given sector—it leaves a sixty-foot square of sudden, bruised blue shadow on the ceiling. Within hours, that patch of dead glass becomes a slaughterhouse and a rookery. Creatures that have spent their whole lives squinting through milk-bone eyelids gather in the relative dimness to spawn. The water below the dead bulb thickens into a writhing, grey column of glass-jacks, smelt, and huge, pale **cable-eels** that unspool themselves from the braided wire of the Tension Cables a mile away. For days, until some automatic relay clicks over and a backup arc-lamp strikes with a blinding violet flash, the shadow is the only place in five hundred square miles where a creature can exist without being seen from a league away. When the backup light finally ignites, the sudden heat and glare shocks the spawning column; for an hour afterward, a snow of blinded, half-cooked fish drifts three thousand feet down toward the yellow stenciled letters on the floor.

Down there, on the aggregate plain of *B-04* and *B-05*, life is slow, loud, and geometric.

The six-foot spider-crabs are only the walkers; they are the nomadic herds. The true masters of the floor live in the expansion joints—those canal-wide trenches that slice the world into mile-square tiles. 

The joints are twenty feet wide and forty feet deep, filled at the bottom with a thick, rubbery mastic that was poured centuries ago to allow the concrete floor to flex under the weight of an inland sea. The water down in the trenches does not move at thirty knots; it rolls in a heavy, oiled vortex, trapping everything heavy that falls from the mid-water. The yellow paint flaking off the eighty-foot letters, the zinc scales of dead jacks, the iron-doped shells of rivet-limpets, and the rubbery ribbons of dead thermal-flukes all tumble across the seamless plain until they drop into the slot of the joint.

Down in that groove lives the **gasket-hag**—a descendant of the hagfish and the conger, evolved into something colonial and architectural. They are blind, thick as firehose, and translucent pink, their skin loose and puckered like wet parchment. Thousands of them tangle together in the corners of the trench, secreting a tough, mucilaginous slime that reacts with the lime in the concrete and the freezing water to cure into a grey, rubbery vulcanite. Over generations, they have built their own pipework along the expansion joints—long, ribbed tunnels of hardened slime and cemented crab-shell that run for dozens of miles in straight, ninety-degree grids, following the engineers' survey lines.

At the intersections—where the joint between *B-04* and *B-05* crosses the joint between *C-04* and *C-05*—there is always a creature that has outgrown the tunnels.

It is an octopus, or what became of one when trapped in a world with no rocks to squeeze under and no shells to hide in. Its mantle is the size of a cement mixer, flat and grey and pebbled with small, hard tubercles that precisely mimic the poured aggregate of the basin floor. When it rests on the plain, it does not look like an animal; it looks like a slightly raised patch of poorly troweled concrete. Even its siphon is squared off, angled like a drainage scupper.

It is entirely deaf in the conventional sense, for the *thrum-clack* of the northern valves would have ruptured a delicate cephalopod statocyst ten thousand generations ago. Instead, its eight arms—each thirty feet long and flattened like canvas fire-hose—are spread out flat across the concrete, their tips tucked into the expansion joints on four sides, or resting lightly against the base of a Tension Cable anchor block.

It uses the floor of the Sump as a spider uses its web. It feels the five-mile Tension Cables singing when a current hits them two thousand feet above. It feels the rhythmic *tick-tick-tick* of the spider-crabs' chitinous toes marching across the aggregate half a mile away, their steps amplified by the dense concrete. It knows the difference between the vibration of a crab herd and the heavy, rolling thud of a dead glass-jack hitting the floor.

And when the great northern sluices begin to open—five minutes before the thirty-knot gale of freezing water actually arrives at Sector B—the octopus feels the change in the concrete itself, the microscopic flexing of the continental slab as a billion tons of water shift their weight to the north.

Long before the spider-crabs begin their single-file march to the trenches, the grey patch of aggregate near the corner of the big yellow *B* peels itself up from the floor. It slides across the concrete like a spilled puddle of mercury, pours its thirty-foot arms down into the expansion joint on top of the frantic gasket-hags, and pulls its rough, pebble-dashed mantle flush with the crisp bevel of the concrete lip—sealing the trench like a manhole cover, locking its suckers into the poured stone, waiting for the water to scream.
