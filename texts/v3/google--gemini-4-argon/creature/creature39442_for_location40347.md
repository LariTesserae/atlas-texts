At first, you would take him for a particularly dense knot of timber and old canvas that had been caught between a donkey-engine’s cylinder and a six-sided tuber, and simply been grown around. 

It takes ten minutes of watching the milky light slide over the condensation on his back to realize that the canvas is a coat—or the ancestral memory of a coat, its linen weave long since invaded and reinforced by the pale, rubbery threads of the floor-mat—and that the timber is a spine. Another five minutes passes before a brass sight-glass beside his ear burps a bubble of green foam, and his hand moves.

The movement is not like the twitch of an animal. It has the slow, hydraulic certainty of the gourds swelling in the flywheel teeth. His arm, thick and bark-scarred from the wrist to the elbow, extends at the exact speed of a rising counterweight, guided by the creak of his own stiff, resin-sealed joints. His fingers—broad, spatulate, with nails as thick and curved as horn spoons—settle onto a square-headed brass packing nut. He does not grip it so much as mould around it. Then, leaning the immense, waterlogged weight of his shoulders into the lever of his own forearm, he turns the nut a sixteenth of an inch. A tiny, weeping leak of pressurized sap at the base of the piston rod stops hissing. 

He does not step back to admire the work. He simply stays where he is, his fingers still welded to the warm brass, waiting for the next vibration to travel through the iron and up into his wrist-bones to tell him where to go next.

If there was ever a name for him, it dissolved centuries ago in the penny-and-fennel sweat of the enclosure. He is the Oiler, or the Ballast-Man, or simply the Governor—the living, ambulatory part of the equilibrium. The plants and the iron have reached a truce, yes, but a truce between blind things is always edging toward catastrophe. Left entirely to itself, a ferro-ivy tendon will eventually pull an axle a millimeter out of true; a burst bladder-gourd will leave a stringy rind in a gear-trough that, over forty years, will pack hard enough to split a cast-iron housing. Someone has to clear the rinds. Someone has to bleed the air-locks. Someone has to shift his weight from one side of a sinking masonry plinth to the other so the level stays true while the bone-callus wood hardens underneath.

There is only one of him. The enclosure could not feed two, nor is there room for two to pass each other in the narrow, hexagonal alleys between the boiler-tubers. 

Up close, he smells entirely of the yard: bruised fennel, warm tallow, and the sweet, sharp tang of pickled copper. He has the squat, barrel-chested proportions of an old dock-walloper, built low to the ground to survive in a world of heavy, swinging things, though time and the conservatory glare have remade him into something native to the mat. His skin has the thick, poreless, translucent look of the canopy bladders overhead—a pale, waxy amber through which you can faintly see the slow, dark pulse of veins that carry something much thicker and cooler than common blood. He does not sweat water; he exudes a thin, natural lacquer that keeps the damp from rotting him, and where his knees and elbows regularly brace against the weeping brick or the iron frames, his hide has thickened into grey, laminated plates like the calluses on an old tree.

His face is almost entirely lost behind a heavy, drooping curtain of what looks like a beard, though it is not hair. It is a beard of pale root-hairs and fine, wire-thin brass turnings that have grown into his chin and down onto his chest, hanging like a bib. When he moves through the steam-warmed soup at the bottom of the trenches—where the mat dips down around the great submerged reduction gears—he dips this beard into the mineral water. The root-hairs drink the warm broth directly, bypassing a mouth that has not chewed solid food in generations, while the brass turnings catch the stray globules of amber gourd-mucilage floating on the surface. 

Earlier, it was said that nothing with eyes lives here, and in the strict sense, this remains true of him. Where his eyes should be, beneath heavy, scarred brows, there are two smooth, convex domes of clouded horn, very much like the verdigris-rimmed sight-glasses on the boilers. They do not dart or focus. In this shadowless, milky glare, where nothing moves faster than the hour hand of a clock, standard sight is useless. Instead, those horn domes only register the broad shifts in the canopy light above—telling him when the sun is high and the sap-pressure is peaking—and the faint, infrared glow of friction. If a bearing three alleys over begins to run dry, growing five degrees warmer than the skin-temperature damp around it, he perceives it through the press of the stalks as a dull, sullen ember in his milk-white vision.

Mostly, though, he lives by his ears and the soles of his feet.

He has no boots. His feet have spread over the decades into wide, flat, leathery pads that distribute his considerable weight across the bladder-moss so he never sinks more than an inch or two. Through them, he reads the floor-mat like a spider reads a web. Every engine in the three-hundred-pace yard is bolted to the calcified wood below, and every stroke of every sap-driven piston sends a distinct tremor through the wet sponge. He knows the voice of all twelve engines. He knows the forty-minute *chuff* of the corner donkey-engine; he knows the four-hour, grinding *clank* of the twin-screw press near the north wall; he knows the constant, sub-audible purr of the twelve-foot flywheel. 

He stands in the green, copper-smelling twilight between two flattened tubers, sometimes for three days at a stretch, his head cocked slightly, his hands hanging heavy at his sides. During these long pauses, the grease-slugs do not distinguish him from the machinery. A tallow-colored mollusk will spend a whole morning gliding up his shin, crossing the calloused cap of his knee to lick the dried amber sap from the seams of his ancient coat, and he will not brush it off. In fact, when a slug reaches his shoulder, he will sometimes lift a slow, horn-nailed finger, let the creature crawl onto the knuckle, and carry it over to a brass escapement that is beginning to chatter for want of tallow, depositing the slug directly onto the warm metal like a man applying a drop of oil from a can.

His tools are not carried in a belt; they are grown into him or cached in the living wood of the grove. His left thumb was lost to a double-helical gear a very long time ago, and in its place sits a heavy, drop-forged iron spanner-head, its tang sunk deep into the wrist-bone and sealed over with a smooth collar of pale, stony callus-wood. For clearing the burst skins of the lubricating gourds from the gear-troughs, he uses a hooked iron bar that he retrieves from the crotch of a liana—where the vine holds it tightly in a muscular loop for him, loosening its grip only when he taps the bark three times with his spanner-thumb. 

He is never hurried, because hurry is fatal here. To move fast in this density is to snag yourself on a tensioned ferro-ivy tendon or to misjudge the descent of a two-ton stone counterweight. He moves at the speed of the grove. When he needs to get from the central flywheel to a weeping pressure-vessel by the east wall, he does not walk around the crowding tubers. He hooks his iron thumb into a loop on a rising liana-hoist, puts his wide feet against the side of a boiler-trunk, and lets the hydraulic swell of the plant lift him twelve feet into the air over the course of an hour, riding the slow, groaning rigging through the hot, bright mist near the bladder-roof before stepping off onto the top of a flywheel housing.

At night—when the translucent bladders overhead go the color of dark horn, the heat drops by three degrees, and the sap-pressure subsides until the engines slow to a single stroke between midnight and dawn—he does not sleep on the mat. The mat is too busy in the dark, drinking back the day’s sweat. 

Instead, he climbs into the warm, concave hollow between the twin brass cylinders of the largest donkey-engine near the center of the yard. The cast iron retains the midday heat long after dark, radiating a dry, metallic warmth that smells of old lacquer. He folds his heavy, stiff legs against his chest, tucks his spanner-thumb beneath his beard of roots and brass shavings, and settles his back against the main steam-chest—now a sap-chest—where the slow, rhythmic *lub-dub* of the check-valves beats directly against his ribs. 

There, in the warm dark, his own pulse slows to match the engine’s nocturnal idle: one beat every three minutes. His clouded horn-eyes go entirely dark. Over his head, a broad, waxy leaf droops down as the turgor pressure falls, draping itself across his shoulders like a tarpaulin to keep the night-drip off his neck, and the machine, the plants, and the man hold each other at temperature until the sun hits the roof-bladders again and the pressure begins to rise.
