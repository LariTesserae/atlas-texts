At first, you would swear you are looking at a massive, waterlogged burl of strangler fig wedged into the iron valley between the two centrifugal pumps. It has the same wet, charcoal-colored skin as the rotted wood, the same slick coat of dark green liverwort, the same complete stillness. 

Then the north wall’s relief valve blows—*shhhhh-clack*—and as the fresh cloud of steam rolls down over the condensers, a heavy, gray-green membrane slides sideways across a pair of amber eyes the size of pocket watches.

What lives here does not have a name, because no one has looked down from the fourth-story windows in thirty years, and even if they had, they would only have seen the courtyard shifting its weight. There are three of them—or perhaps they are simply three concentrations of the same slow, patient presence, divided among the warm hollows of the machinery. 

The largest of them is built something like a giant mud-turtle that has forgotten its shell, and something like the root-ball of an upturned mangrove. It is roughly the size of an oil drum, low-slung and immensely heavy, its belly resting directly in the hot, oily peat between the twin intake pipes. Its hide is not scaled, but deeply furrowed and leathery, like the bark of an old cypress, so thoroughly colonized by velvety pillow-moss and pale, shelf-like bracket fungi that it carries its own miniature terrace of vegetation wherever it drags itself. 

It is an ectotherm, entirely married to the waste heat of the nineteenth-century ironwork. Its blood—thick, dark, and rich with copper—is warmed not by the distant, milky square of sky four stories up, but by the low-pressure steam jackets of the condensers. Its heart does not beat on its own authority; over decades of living pressed against the vibrating housings, its circulatory system has entrained itself to the machinery. *Chug-thump, chug-thump.* The iron piston falls, the creature’s three-chambered heart contracts, and warm blood pushes slowly out to the ends of its thick, blunt limbs. 

Look closely at those limbs and the boundary between the animal and the garden blurs even further. It has four legs, stumpy and columnar like the cast-iron pedestals of the boilers, ending not in claws, but in long, prehensile, root-like toes. These digits are multi-jointed and boneless at their tips, tapering into pale, sensitive tendrils that it buries six inches deep into the cinder-muck. Through them, it feels the courtyard. It does not use ears—ears would have been ruined fifty years ago by the deafening, boxed-in thunder of the pumps. Instead, it listens through the soles of its feet and the skin of its belly. It knows the exact harmonic rattle of every loose anchor bolt; it knows the heavy, liquid *slosh* of the water table three feet below the peat; it knows when a brick high up on the east facade is finally pried loose by a philodendron and begins its slow tilt toward the floor.

For four or five days at a time, the creature does not move an inch. It lies wedged against the chalky-green iron of the turbine casing, letting the hot condensate drip onto its back, where the water channels down the deep grooves of its skin to pool around its mouth. It drinks the broth of the yard—the boiled river water, the dissolved limestone, the iron-rich runoff, and the rainbow slicks of heavy cylinder oil. In its gut, a specialized flora of anaerobic bacteria breaks down the petroleum hydrocarbons and the rotting fern-peat alike, fermenting the industrial sludge into a slow, steady trickle of energy.

When it does move, it moves at the speed of a clock’s hour hand. 

A low groan—a sound so deep in the bass register that it is merely an extra syllable added to the *chug-thump* of the pumps—rolls out of its throat. The long, pale toes withdraw from the muck with a wet, quiet *shuck*. The creature rises an inch, maybe two, on its pillar-legs, shedding scales of red rust and black leaf-mold, and drags itself forward to the eight-foot flywheel.

Here, the relationship between the beast and the machine reveals its true nature. The machine keeps the creature warm; in return, the creature keeps the machine from choking to death. 

A strangler fig’s woody aerial root has dropped down from the overhead cable trays and begun to wind itself into the gap between the flywheel’s rim and the main bearing housing. In another month, the root would thicken, bind the wheel, stall the pump, and let the cold river water rise to drown the fireboxes and turn the courtyard into a dead, stagnant cistern. The creature reaches up with a heavy, club-like foreleg. Its jaw hinges open—wide, toothless, lined with two crushing plates of dark, horn-like keratin stained purple-black with tannins—and it clamps down on the wrist-thick root. 

It does not bite through it in a snap. It simply hangs its immense weight from the vine, closing its eyes, and lets the constant, bone-shaking vibration of the floor do the work. Over three hours, the keratin plates grind back and forth in time with the engine’s rhythm, sawing through the tough, fibrous wood until the root parts with a dull *pop*. Slowly, methodically, over the course of an afternoon, the creature chews the severed root into pulp, swallowing the bitter white sap and the bark, clearing the flywheel’s path.

Afterward, it turns its heavy head toward the brass oiler cups on the main shaft. Its tongue—long, grey, and muscular as an eel—unspools from its mouth and laps at the congealed tallow and axle grease around the rim, clearing away the dead fern-fronds and the drowned husks of rust-colored moths that would otherwise clog the drip-feed. From glands along its own broad, flat throat, it exudes a thick, mucilaginous slime, slick as wet soap and heavy with lipids filtered from its diet of oily peat. As it rubs its chin lazily against the warm, spinning steel of the drive shaft, it leaves a fresh, glistening coat of this biological grease behind, silencing a nascent squeak in the babbitt metal.

Up in the green suspension bridges of the overhead pipe-racks, twenty feet above the floor, live the younger ones—or the smaller ones, for it is impossible to tell if they grow into the great mud-thing below or are a different caste of the same quiet species. They are no larger than raccoons, flat-bodied and long-tailed, looking like patches of wet Spanish moss and staghorn fern that have detached themselves from the brick. They cling to the underside of the hot steam conduits with suction-padded bellies, absorbing the heat through their skin, creeping three feet a day along the pipe-runs. Where the insulation has rotted away and pinprick leaks of live steam hiss out into the leaves, these smaller ones gather, pressing their thick, calloused hides directly over the rusted holes like living gaskets, hoarding the fierce warmth for themselves and, in doing so, keeping the line pressure from dropping.

None of them ever look up at the rectangle of white coastal sky. To them, the sky is only the place where the cold rain comes from. Their entire universe is four stories high and forty feet wide, bounded by warm brick, pulsed by cast iron, and lit by a permanent, shadowless submarine green. As dusk begins to turn the green light to a thick, oily black, the largest one settles back into the hollow between the condensers. Its root-toes sink back into the warm, bubbling peat. A broad monstera leaf tips above it, spilling a cupful of warm water onto its mossy skull, and its heavy eyelids slide shut, locking back into the fifty-year dream of the pumps. *Chug-thump. Chug-thump.*
