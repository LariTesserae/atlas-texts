Down in the greased troughs, where the air smells sharpest of formic acid and sweet, rotting fig, something large is waiting for a column of ants to finish crossing the lip of a basin.

It is the size of a boar, and shaped vaguely like a badger that has been lengthened and flattened to fit through oval tunnels. It does not have eyes—only shallow, furred dimples behind its snout where eyes used to be, ten thousand generations ago, before its ancestors gave up on the sky. What it has instead is a muzzle broadened into a damp, cartilaginous shovel, fringed with forty or fifty pale, fleshy barbels that twitch and taste the masonry like the fingers of a blind potter.

The locals of the lowlands, who never come up here, have no name for it, because it never comes down. In the dialect of the valley’s own vibration, it is a *thrum-heavy*, a *slow- heat*, a *wall-cousin*. 

Its coat is not really fur anymore. The individual hairs have fused into thick, overlapping bristles the color of wet peat, grooved lengthwise so that condensation runs down them in neat channels toward the belly, keeping the skin dry while lubricating the creature’s passage through tight mud-chutes. Across its broad, arched back, a garden of pale grey bracket fungi and miniature ferns has taken root in the tallow-grease of its bristles. It never grooms them away. In the dark, when it presses itself into an alcove to sleep, its back becomes indistinguishable from the buttresses.

Right now, it is working. 

The creature is not a ruler of this city; the city has no rulers, only maintenance. It shifts its weight onto its hind legs—short, pillar-like limbs with five spatulate claws made of horn as dense as ironwood—and leans its heavy chest against the base of the parabolic arch. For three minutes, it does nothing but breathe, its ribs expanding and contracting against the warm clay, listening with its sternum. 

Through its breastbone, it feels the four-minute wave of the soldier termites' hammering pass underneath it, rolling west toward the horn of the saddle. It feels the hollow spot three feet inside the pier where a colony of sweat-bees died two winters ago, leaving an unvented pocket of sour comb that is softening the load-bearing mastic.

With a low, clicking sigh that is immediately swallowed by the katydid-roar, the beast opens its jaws. Its teeth are not for biting meat; the front incisors are wide, flat chisels, and the molars behind them are huge, ridged millstones that never stop growing. It bites into the softened wall—not tearing, but planing off a precise, curling ribbon of old, dead mortar. It chews. Its salivary glands, the size of gourds along its neck, flood the dry thousand-year-old clay and beetle-shell with a thick, alkaline mucilage that smells faintly of bitter almonds and ammonia.

While it chews, a small, frantic drama plays out on its shoulders. 

Three flightless ground-parrots, sleek as rats and the color of tarnished brass, have hopped out of the pandanus roots onto the beast’s rump. They do not fly; their wings are reduced to stubby, clawed paddles used for scrambling up vertical mud-flues. They move over the mammoth-badger’s back with the jerky, high-speed precision of clockwork toys, their heavy beaks snapping silently in the din. They are harvesting the pearl-white mites that live in the roots of the beast’s back-ferns, and in return, they are preening the wet clay-crumbs from the hinges of its legs where the paste might harden and cripple it. The beast does not acknowledge them, except to lower one shoulder slightly so the smallest bird can reach the crease behind its ear-slit.

The mortar in its mouth has reached the right consistency—warm, elastic, and sterile. The beast lowers its head and presses its broad, shovel-snout into the cavity it just excavated, extruding the re-chewed paste from the sides of its mouth while tamping it flat with the hard boss of its nose. Nose-pat, nose-pat, slide. The motion is identical to the amber ants working six feet away, only scaled up ten thousand times.

When the patch is flush with the flutes of the arch, the beast backs up two paces, turns around in the narrow trough with the fluid, boneless grace of an animal that has no collarbones, and sits.

Something else is coming down the trough.

You feel it first in your knees: a slow, rhythmic *thud-drag, thud-drag* that cuts beneath the high-pitched saw of the crickets. Out of the indigo gloom comes a pair of eyes—huge, liquid, and entirely black, set into a face that looks like a cross between a lemur and a tortoise. 

This one is vertical, or nearly so. It walks on its knuckles and the outsides of its curled feet, swinging a long, heavy abdomen between its forelegs like a pendulum. It is an *arch-weaver*, one of the valley’s true masons, though it looks less like an engineer than a bundle of wet rags hung on a frame of bamboo. Its limbs are absurdly long and multi-jointed, covered in loose, hairless skin the texture of mushroom caps, folded into deep wrinkles at the elbows and knees where white mold grows in hygienic, antibiotic mats.

The weaver is carrying a dead branch of mountain fig, four inches thick, which it has stripped of bark and dragged up from the lower terraces. Behind it, holding onto the greasy fold of skin at the base of its spine, ride two of its young—blind, pink-skinned things the size of fists, their oversized hands locked into their mother’s dermal ridges.

The badger-beast does not growl; there is no point in vocalizing here. Instead, it drops its chin to the floor of the trough and strikes the cured mud twice with its lower jaw. *Thock-thock.* 

Two yards away, the weaver stops. Its long, prehensile toes spread across the wet floor, feeling the double-tap through the ground. Slowly, with the syrupy deliberation that governs everything larger than an insect in this place, the weaver reaches out with one four-fingered, double-thumbed hand and touches the fresh, warm patch of mortar the badger has just laid into the arch.

Its fingers linger in the wet clay. It is reading the chemical signature in the badger’s spit—checking the pH, the moisture content, the diet of the beast over the last three days, the structural integrity of the wall behind the patch. Satisfied, the weaver lifts the heavy fig branch and wedges one end into the fresh mastic, angling the timber upward to brace a sagging lip of the ancient weaver-bird canopy overhead. 

Before the badger-beast has even turned away, the ants have found the seam where the new wood meets the wet clay. Five hundred of them break away from their hairline crack, flow up the badger’s tail, cross the weaver’s forearm without pausing, and begin pinning the timber in place with pellets of fern-pith.

Higher up—twenty feet overhead, where the heat from the fungal vaults collects under the arch’s crown in a pocket of ninety-eight-degree stagnant air—the ceiling itself begins to move.

What looked like a decorative frieze of dark, overlapping mud-shingles is actually a congregation of *mantle-slugs*, each the size of a man's forearm, flattened like soles. They do not eat the greenery; they are the mortar's final glaze. All day they have slept in a tight, geometric tessellation, their leathery backs hardened to keep their moisture in. Now, in the bruised twilight, they are separating with wet, sucking sounds that are instantly lost in the roar. 

They slide downward in a slow, synchronized sheet, leaving behind a ribbon of clear, silicate-rich slime that dries on contact with the warm air into a waterproof varnish as tough as horn. When they reach the new patch of badger-paste and the newly wedged fig branch, they do not go around it; they flow over it, sealing the fresh work against the night’s condensation, binding wood to mud to chitin beneath a fresh skin of biological glass.

Nothing here knows it is building a mountain. 

The badger-thing is only relieving the itch in its ever-growing teeth and the pressure in its throat-glands. The long-armed weaver is only following an inherited compulsion to wedge sticks into soft spots so the ceiling doesn't fall on its young. The ants are only following a pheromone trail laid down when the granite peak was still visible to the sun. 

Yet as the badger-thing shuffles away down the greasy trough, disappearing into the velvet dark five yards off, and the weaver climbs slowly back into the wet pandanus leaves, the valley is a quarter-inch narrower, a pound heavier, and a fraction of a degree warmer than it was ten minutes ago.
