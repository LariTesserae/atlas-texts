There are three of them in the basin right now, though you could stand at the rolled lip of the soapstone rim for an entire afternoon and swear the place was empty. 

To find them, you have to stop looking for movement and start looking for an error in the geometry of the rock. Look at the third green pillar from the western rim—the one with the deep, vertical scar where a lightning strike boiled its sap a century ago. Follow the pleated skirt of its basal leaves outward across the bruised-amber stone. Where the heavy, rubbery green edge meets the wet rock, there is a localized thickening. It looks, at first, like a shelf of precipitated talc, a pale yellow-grey slump of mineral rind left behind by a thousand years of creeping water. 

If you watch that slump for an hour—until your eyes ache from the shadowless, pearl-white glare of the sky—you will see the stone inhale. 

The seam between the yellow rock and the grey slump parts by the width of a thumbnail, drawing in a slow, viscous ribbon of water, and then seals shut again without a ripple. 

Whatever lives here does not belong to the quick, hot-blooded lineage of things that left the black flint axes behind. Those tool-makers were visitors from the lower valleys—creatures of hair and tendon and nervous fire who climbed up into the warm fog, worked furiously for a week or a seasonuntil their lungs filled with mineral damp or their patience broke against the weight of the red jasper, and then went back down the mountain, leaving their wood to rot. The basin is too spare for animals. Its calorie budget is measured in decades. To live here permanently, a creature must have the metabolism of a lichen and the patience of a stalagmite.

They are a kind of terrestrial, calcium-hoarding slug, though the word *slug* does no justice to their sheer, monumental stillness, nor to their weight. Fully extended—which happens only once every few weeks, usually when the geothermal draft runs slightly warmer than usual—the largest of the three is the size of a sleeping hog, and weighs closer to an anvil. 

Their mantles are not soft. Over centuries of grazing on the dissolved soapstone and the silica-rich mucilage of the giant plant-pillars, they have incorporated the basin’s minerals directly into their dorsal flesh. Their backs are a mosaic of overlapping, leathery plates, heavily impregnated with magnesium and talc, the exact color of wet, yellow-grey tallow. They feel like soapstone to the touch—greasy, warm, and dense. When they tuck their heads and skirts inward, settling into a shallow trough in the basin floor, they do not hide so much as they simply agree with the topography. The creeping sheets of water split around them, or slide right over their backs, just as they slide over the rock.

And yet, it was not valley-men who carved the two-finger-wide channels in the floor. And the project at the central pool is not abandoned.

If you wade out to where the black flint blades lie in the warm, skin-thin water, and you look closely at the dark stains where their wooden hafts supposedly rotted away, you will notice something strange. The stains are not fibrous. They are not the black smudges of decayed oak or ash. They are shallow, saucer-shaped depressions chemically etched into the soapstone, coated in a faint, iridescent residue that smells faintly of vinegar and chalk. 

The flint blades were never hafted to wood. They are hafted to the creatures.

Four days from now, the largest of the three—the one currently masquerading as a mineral slump beside the scarred pillar—will decide that the water table around a newly dropped plant-node six yards away is a fraction of a millimeter too low. It will begin to move. 

Its progress is invisible to the impatient eye, operating on the same clock as the sun-dial or the opening of a fern frond. Its broad, muscular foot, pale and translucent as beef suet, flows over the slick stone on a carpet of dense, clear slime. It will take the creature eleven hours to cross the six yards of bare rock to the flint tools. 

When it reaches the smaller of the two black blades, it does not pick it up with a hand; it has no limbs, no bones, no eyes other than four short, blunt horns on its head that can only distinguish between the bright glare of the sky and the dark bulk of the green pillars. Instead, the creature flows *over* the flint. 

Deep within the center of its muscular foot is a specialized, calloused pocket—a sort of muscular gizzard open to the underside, lined with tough, keratinous ridges. It engulfs the butt of the flint hand-axe into this pocket, gripping the stone with hundreds of pounds of hydraulic, hydrostatic pressure. Then, carrying the blade beneath its belly like the keel of a boat, it creeps toward the area of the floor that needs carving.

To dig a channel, the creature parks itself over the chosen line of soapstone. It secretes a puddle of weak, warm organic acid from its belly, softening the talc-heavy rock into a chalky paste. Then, over the course of three or four days, it rocks its heavy, armored body forward and back, forward and back, a few inches at a time, dragging the sharp black flint through the softened yellow stone. *Scrape. Pause. Scrape. Pause.* The sound is entirely muffled by the three hundred pounds of wet flesh pressing down from above, reduced to a faint, gritty shudder you could only feel through the soles of your feet. As the groove deepens, the creeping water washes the yellow slurry away toward the central pool, leaving a clean, curving vein in the rock.

They did not knap the flint themselves, of course. Eons ago, some wandering hunter dropped a stone tool here, or died here, and these slow, blind engineers—who had previously spent centuries etching their irrigation lines with nothing but their own acidic bellies and rough bits of gravel—found the sharp, foreign rock and incorporated it into their work. They have been passing those two black axes back and forth between the three of them for four hundred human generations, keeping them in the shallow water near the pool when not in use, slowly wearing the chipped edges smooth.

They are farmers, of a sort. They cannot eat the tough, rubbery stalks of the adult plant-columns, nor the bitter, pleated skirts of the basal leaves. They feed almost exclusively on the "milk" of the nursery: the sweet, starchy, translucent jelly that a newly dropped plant-node exudes during its first ten years of life as it uncurls and roots itself to the bare rock. 

To maximize this harvest, the three residents manage the basin like a slow-motion orchard. When a heavy green node drops from a crown and rolls down the slope, the creatures eventually converge on it. If it has landed in a dry spot, they spend the next two years carving a spiral channel to bring the warm seep to its base, bathing it, encouraging it to open and weep its rich mucilage into the water, where they can graze on it at their leisure. If two nodes land too close together, they will starve the weaker one by damming its water supply with a ridge of their own calcified dung, or by wedging a heavy piece of debris in its way, nudging it yard by yard back out toward the barren rim.

Which brings us to the red jasper boulder, and the three levers of dead, hardened plant-stalk.

Look again at the braided vine ropes lashing those levers together. They are not tied in knots. A knot requires fingers, a thumb, the concept of an overhand loop. Those vines are *grown* and *cemented* together—wrapped round and round in a slow, spiral overlap, likely by two of the creatures crawling in opposite circles over the course of a month, gluing the fibers into a solid mass with the same mineral-heavy mucus that hardens on their backs. 

They have been trying to move the red jasper boulder for eighty years. 

The boulder sits squarely over the warmest thermal vent in the central pool, partially plugging the up-welling of heat and mineral-rich water that feeds the entire basin. The three residents want it out of the socket. 

They do not use the levers by pressing down on them with a sudden jerk of weight; the dead plant-stalks would snap, and the creatures have no way to climb onto the high ends anyway. Instead, they are using the levers as wedges and swells. 

A century ago, when the three massive stalks fell from a dying pillar, the creatures spent a decade nudging them across the slick floor, inch by inch, floating them into the pool and jamming their tapered tips into the hairline gap beneath the red boulder's flank. Once the dry, woody stalks were wedged tight and lashed together with cemented vines to keep them from splay-slipping, the creatures simply let the water do the work. As the dead stalks absorbed the warm pool water over the years, they swelled with immense, microscopic force, prying the boulder upward by a fraction of an inch. 

Now, the second phase of the work is underway. 

If you step to the edge of the knee-deep pool and look down into the clear, warm water behind the raised end of the levers, you will see the other two inhabitants of the basin. 

They are smaller than the one by the pillar—perhaps the size of sheep—and they are wedged tightly between the underside of the wooden levers and the yellow soapstone floor, acting as living fulcrums. For the last eleven years, they have been sitting there in the warm bath, filtering the mineral water through their gills, drinking the dissolved stone, and slowly, deliberately growing their dorsal shells thicker. 

Year by year, millimeter by millimeter, as they accrete new layers of talc and calcium onto their backs, their bodies rise, pushing the waterlogged levers upward with the unstoppable, glacial force of a tree root splitting a flagstone. 

Already, the great red boulder has tilted three degrees to the east. The water beneath its western edge is noticeably warmer. In another twenty years, or thirty, the jasper erratic will finally pass its tipping point. It will roll out of its socket with a single, thunderous, echoing *thump* that will send a knee-high wave sloshing all the way to the basin's rim. 

The hot vent will clear. A fresh plume of rich, geothermal broth will flood the spiral channels. The great green pillars will drink, and swell, and drop a dozen fat, starchy nodes at once. And the three slow sovereigns of the bowl will un-wedge themselves from the mud, slide out across the mirror-bright stone, and begin the long, quiet feast.
