If you enter one of the thousand alcoves along the middle tiers and run your hands along the back wall, you will likely touch one of the residents before you realize it is not part of the wainscoting. 

They spend nine-tenths of their existence in a state called *the clamp*. 

Pressed flat into the concave sleeping-niches, their limbs folded tightly against their chests, their bodies are warm only in the way that a piece of wood left in the sun is warm—a dry, retained heat, barely two degrees above the ambient chill of the burl. To the touch, their skin feels like centuries-old parchment or the shed hull of a locust: thin, taut, and entirely without oil or sweat. Beneath that dry casing, there is no soft fat. There is only a dense bundle of fibrous muscle, tendon like catgut, and a skeleton of high-density, slightly flexible cartilage that has absorbed so much mineral content from the tree’s volatile resins that their bones ring faintly if struck.

They are roughly human in proportion, though smaller, compacted by the generations spent in narrow grooves, with long, flat feet and extraordinarily wide, spatulate ribcages. The chest is their largest feature. It is a barrel of thin, fused ribs designed not just for breathing the thin, turpentine-sharp air, but for *listening*. They have no eyes—not even the sunken sockets of cave-fish, just a smooth, unbroken sweep of brow—and their external ears are tiny, leathery pinnae pinned flat to the skull. Instead, the skin over their sternums is stretched tight as a tympanum. They hear the city through their ribs, through their jawbones, and through the broad, calloused pads of their hands and feet.

For years at a time, a resident will remain wedged in an alcove, metabolizing at a snail's pace. They are waiting for the *draw*. 

Because the mega-flora breathes on a barometric cycle that takes decades to turn over, the city has long seasons of stagnation when the air goes thin and still, and the wood barely groans. During these eras, the population sleeps like dry seeds in a pod. 

Then, miles below, a tectonic shift or a deep thermal tide alters the pressure. The wood begins to inhale. The lower flues catch the draft first. A low, sub-audible rumble builds in the floor, rising over weeks into that sustained, wooden chord that fills the dark bowl. 

The vibration travels through the ironwood walls and directly into the breastbones of the sleepers. It acts like a winding key. Slowly, over days, their blood—thick, dark, and viscous as warm pine-tar—begins to circulate faster. You can hear the waking of the city before anyone takes a step: a dry, papery rustling from a thousand alcoves, accompanied by the sharp, rhythmic *tock-tock-tock* of knuckles and knees popping as ancient joints unseize.

When they finally emerge into the grooved streets, they do not walk so much as slide. Lifting a foot and setting it down would create a sharp, percussive transient in the acoustic bowl—a rude, blinding flash of noise in a world made of sound. Instead, they move with a flat-footed, skating shuffle, their soles whisper-quiet over the lignum vitae, keeping three points of contact with the architecture whenever possible. A hand is always trailing along the lip of a wall, reading the amber scar-tallies like braille, while the bare shoulder blades brush the overhanging lintels to gauge the sixty-year sag of the ceiling.

In the waking season, they are intensely, quietly busy. Their whole culture is a single, ongoing maintenance project that spans epochs: they are the tenders of the instrument, and the parasites of its tension.

They do not farm, and they do not hunt. They harvest the *creep*. 

When the wood is under heavy barometric stress, the inner walls weep that cold, volatile resin. The residents move along the structural fault-lines—which they locate by pressing their jaws against the timber and listening for the high, singing pitch of straining fibers—and scrape the newly extruded beads of black amber into leather pouches made from the cured skin of their own dead. This resin is their meat and water. It is fiercely astringent, dense with complex hydrocarbons and trace minerals pulled from the deep crust. They do not chew it; they tuck a shard of it into the hollow of the cheek and let it dissolve over weeks, absorbing the volatile compounds through the mucous membranes while their bodies break down the carbohydrates into minimal, waterless energy.

The rest of their waking life is spent arguing with the wood. 

Because the burl is always trying to close the hollow—slowly bulging into doorways, swelling the floors, pulling the upper root-bridges taut to the point of snapping—the residents must constantly correct it. They do not work in crews with loud shouts and rhythmic hammering; they work like termites or watchmakers. 

A single resident will sit cross-legged in a narrowing street-groove for three days, holding a tiny, crescent-shaped flint scraper between thumb and forefinger. With short, controlled strokes that match the exact pitch and rhythm of the ambient timber-creak, they shave away a curl of ironwood no thicker than an onion skin. *Shhh-ick. Shhh-ick.* The shavings are never dropped; they are tucked immediately into a pouch, later to be ground into the impalpable talc at the bottom of the bowl, or braided into new tension-cords.

Up in the hanging infrastructure of the ceiling, the younger, lighter residents—whose bones have not yet fully mineralized and who can still bend like green willow—crawl along the humming bass-strings of the root-bridges. They carry small wooden pegs and bone needles. Where a new, hair-thin rootlet has sprouted in the dark, they capture it, braid it into a fraying cable, and score the bark of both so the two will bleed resin and fuse over the next century. It is a form of engineering where the blueprint is held in oral memory, passed down as songs that mimic the resonant frequencies of the bridges. A builder knows a bridge is safe when they pluck the central knot and it hums in the exact key of their great-grandfather’s teaching-hum. If it is sharp, the walls are pulling apart, and they must weave a slack-loop; if it is flat, the ceiling is sagging, and they must drive a wedge of dead heartwood into the anchor-joint.

Their language has no vowels. Vowels require open mouths and wet breath, and they waste moisture; worse, loud vocalizations bounce wildly off the opposite side of the amphitheater, cluttering the acoustic space with slap-back echoes that blind the listeners to the subtle shifts of the wood. 

Instead, they speak in clicks, tongue-pops, dry sibilants, and—most importantly—by tapping the wood itself. Two residents on opposite sides of a house-wall will converse by rapping the heels of their hands or the tips of their fingernails against the grain. The wood conducts the sound ten times faster and clearer than the air. A sharp *tic-tic-scrape* with a thumbnail on a doorway lintel can be felt through the soles of the feet by everyone on that tier, conveying a whole paragraph of information about which flues are opening, where a resin-vein has burst, or which street-groove is currently experiencing a tension-crack.

For more intimate speech, they press their foreheads or collarbones together, letting the low, purring vibrations of their larynxes travel directly from bone to bone. To an outsider standing three feet away in the pitch black, a pair of lovers or fierce debaters would appear to be two motionless silhouettes, leaning into one another in absolute silence, while inside their skulls a torrent of rapid, resonant humming is shaking their teeth.

They have no concept of a horizon, or of "outside." They know that if you dig too deep into the walls, the wood grows hotter, denser, and harder to breathes around, until the flint tools shatter against the petrified outer rind of the burl. Therefore, the world is an inside. Everything that exists is concave. 

Their dead are not buried; there is nothing to bury them in, and nothing to rot them. When a resident grows too old—when their joints calcify completely from decades of resin-diet, and their chest-tympanum stiffens so they can no longer feel the low chord of the flues—they climb down to the flat, compressed plain at the very bottom of the bowl. 

There, in the ankle-deep drifts of dry wood-talc, they lie down on their backs. The extreme dryness of the chamber mummifies them cleanly within months, drawing the last traces of vapor from their tissues until they are as light and rigid as balsa wood. 

They are not left there for long. In a world with no imports, a body is a quarry. After a respectful season—measured by one full inhalation and exhalation of the lower vents—the next waking generation comes down into the talc. Moving with great reverence and terrible efficiency, they dismantle their ancestors. The skin is peeled and cured with turpentine-breath to make tool-wraps; the gut-tendons are drawn out and twisted into tourniquets to train the upper roots; the dense, mineralized long bones are split and honed into chisels and wedges that are tougher than the wood itself. 

What cannot be used as a tool—the skull, the fused cage of the chest—is carried back up to the highest, quietest alcoves near the ceiling, where the living wood still creeps fastest. The elders wedge the bone-cages into the clefts of the growing root-knuckles and score the bark around them. 

Over three or four hundred years, the slow, relentless swelling of the tree swallows the bones. The ironwood flows over the ribs and fills the eyeless skulls, sealing them into smooth, bulbous burls along the ceiling vaults. The residents call these the *mute-knots*. When a young weaver climbs the upper dark, they use the protruding, wood-sheathed brow ridges and collarbones of their ancestors as handholds—literalizing their history into the load-bearing architecture of the sky.
