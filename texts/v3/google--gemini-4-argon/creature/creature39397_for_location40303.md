To see what lives here, you have to stop looking for flesh. Flesh is just water held in a net of carbon, and in the pit, water is a volatile gas that vanished epochs ago. If you watch the two-finger-wide walkways expecting a foot to come down, you will see only the racing, rust-colored dust and the violent thrashing of the tin-thin leaves. 

You have to listen to the organ pipe instead. 

The wind through the stacked galleries does not merely roar; it changes pitch. A high, whistling harmonic in the western wall will suddenly choke off, dropping a fifth into a low, fluttering groan, while three tiers down, a new chord abruptly rings out. If you squint through the coppery gloom at the exact doorway where the note changed, you will see that the opening is blocked. Something that looks like a fistful of tarnished silver wire and dark, flaking mica has wedged itself into the lintel. 

A moment later, the mass uncoils. It pulls itself out of the doorway, not by stepping, but by *extruding*—flowing along the two-finger slate walkway like a thick ribbon of dry mercury and braided copper. 

They do not have a name, because nothing with a tongue has ever survived the descent to name them, but their bodies are a marriage of the kiln and the loom. They are dry-state organisms, composite creatures no larger than a butcher’s cleaver, built from the very iron, silica, and sulfur that the plants pull from the air. 

Atfirst glance, an individual looks like a dense, creeping briar patch of articulated mineral plates and spun basalt fiber. They have no heads, no wet eyes, and no soft organs. Instead, their bodies are a shifting lattice of piezo-crystalline struts—grown from the glass-root they consume—bound together by hundreds of fine, high-tension filaments of native iron and nickel. They move by thermal actuation. By opening and closing tiny, overlapping shutters of slate-like scale along their flanks, they expose their internal metallic tendons to either the roaring, superheated updraft from the vent below or the cooler, dust-chilled downdraft from the basalt hoods above. The rapid expansion and contraction of the metal coils and uncoils their limbs with a sharp, rhythmic *tick-tick-snap* that blends seamlessly into the clatter of the ferro-lichen.

They are the builders, and they are the reason the city is empty of furniture. A room, to them, is not a place to sit; it is a nozzle. A doorway is a reed.

The miniature city is not a ruin they moved into; it is a vast, communal wind-trap and acoustic engine that they have been tuning for ten thousand years. The rounded, velvety edges of the slate walkways were not worn down by the wind alone, but by the constant, scraping passage of thousands of abrasive bellies dragging loads of ash-paste and sintered glass. 

Watch one as it reaches a cantilevered balcony choked with heavy, rattling ash-ivy. The creature does not eat the leaves with a mouth. Instead, it flattens itself against the plant, spreading into a wide, bronze-colored net, and begins to vibrate. It steals the kinetic fury of the wind—harnesses the *shhhk-shhhk-shhhk* of the beaten-tin rosettes—and turns itself into a living rasp. With a screech like a file on a sawblade, it saws through the brittle stem of the ivy. 

When the heavy metallic rosette tears loose and plummets into the central shaft, the creature does not let it fall alone. It leaps after it, opening its lattice-body like a parachute of woven wire. 

Out in the Mostly Void, caught in the violent thermal column, the creature and the severed plant tumble together in mid-air. As the updraft grinds the brittle leaves against the lower walls, shattering them into glowing, rust-colored clouds of spores and iron dust, the creature spins at the center of the miniature storm. Its crystalline struts generate a fierce static charge from the friction of the gale. The glowing dust doesn't just blow past it; the dust *jumps* to it, plating its wire-frame body in a fresh, velvet coat of nutrient-rich rust and sulfur. 

When it is heavy enough with dust to begin sinking toward the lethal, melting heat of the true vent floor, it snaps its scales shut, catches a rising eddy, and flings itself outward, snagging a thrumming, horizontal vine of glass-root with hooks of cast slag. 

Up in the dark, knee-high rooms behind the galleries, the air is slightly calmer, and this is where they fuse. The rooms are kilns. Inside them, three or four of the wire-and-mica things will press themselves together into a tight, tangled knot, blocking the draft from the back slits so the stagnant heat rises until the air shimmers. In that breathless, bake-oven dark, they shed their collected dust into a communal pile and use their own static-charged filaments to arc tiny, blue-white sparks through the powder—sintering the iron and silica into new struts, new scales, and occasionally, a new, smaller lattice that untangles itself from the mass and goes ticking and clicking out into the roaring red gloom.

They have no eyes to see the stray needles of red dust-light, and no ears in the way a mammal understands them. Their entire body is an eardrum. They perceive their world through the thirty-foot organ pipe they live inside. 

When a section of wall cracks, or a balcony becomes too overgrown with iron-moss, the pitch of the courtyard’s endless chord shifts by a fraction of a hertz. The dissonance vibrates through the glass-root vines, travels up the slate walkways, and sets the crystalline cores of the creatures humming with an unbearable, itching resonance. 

Drawn by the sour note, dozens of them will stream out of the dark doorways like ribbons of animated chainmail, flowing up the vertical walls in jerky, heat-driven spasms. Some will set to work sawing away the offending moss; others will regurgitate a dry mortar of sulfur-ash and volcanic glass, packing it into the cracked stone and holding their hot, vibrating bodies against the patch until the friction-heat welds it solid. 

They do not know there is a sky above the basalt hoods. They do not know what water is, or what silence feels like. To them, the universe is forty feet wide, fiercely hot, and made entirely of song and abrasion. They only know that the chord must be kept pure, the draught must be kept rushing, and the dust must never, ever stop rising.
