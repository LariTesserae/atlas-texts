At first, you only see the flowstone. 

Near the base of the western wall, where the fluted columns of tallow-colored stone swell out to meet the black humus, one of the amber bulges has the wrong kind of sheen. It is not the glassy, mineralized sweat of the limestone, nor the slick mucilage of the liverworts. It is matte, finely pebbled, and it rises and falls once every four or five minutes. 

When the thermal breaks—when the warm air chimneys upward and that ten-second rain of heavy condensation patters across the broad arum leaves—the bulge uncoils. 

She moves only during the shiver. The sudden, brief rush of the downdraft and the synchronized papery rustle of a thousand saucer-moths provide the only acoustic cover this chamber ever offers. In those ten seconds, she shifts her weight from one hip to the other, her joints making a dull, submerged *cluck* beneath her skin, and reaches a long, tallow-pale arm into the nearest of the carved stone basins. Her fingers—six on each hand, though the outermost two are fused nearly to the nail into a wide, fleshy paddle—scoop up three of the jet-black crabs, along with a thick ribbon of the mineral-rich biofilm that lines the stone. She brings them to her mouth before the draft dies. By the time the moths settle and the heavy, bruised silence re-seals the nave, she is stone again, chewing with slow, lateral grinds of a jaw that has no chin, only a soft, frog-like throat pouch that pulses faintly as she swallows the shells whole.

There are four of them left in the sinkhole, though you could stand in the center of the pitch-black mulch for an hour and never see more than one, and then only if you knew to look at the places where the roots of the strangler figs braid themselves into the dry-stacked cairns. 

They are what became of the people who wove the river-cane kilns on the ledges above, though the relationship now is only architectural, the way a hermit crab relates to the whelk that grew its shell. Those ancestors came down into the aven centuries ago—perhaps fleeing something in the bright, dry world above, or perhaps drawn by the winterless wet heat—and they never climbed back out. Over twenty or thirty generations in a bell-jar of hyper-saturated air, low oxygen, and total acoustic terror, the pit edited them. 

They are small. The largest of the four—an old male who spends his days wedged horizontally into the groin of a cavern-fig thirty feet up, draped in curtains of translucent air-roots—would barely reach a surface-man’s breastbone if he ever stood upright. He cannot stand upright. His spine has curved to match the rounded, dissolved geometry of the karst, and his pelvis has tilted so that he moves on all fours in a low, fluid spider-crawl, keeping his center of gravity pressed flat against the wet stone or the springy humus. 

Their skin is the exact color of the flowstone: a sour, translucent cream washed with the grey-violet of veins that run very close to the surface to dump heat into the stifling air. Because nothing here can afford to be sharp or dry, they have lost every hair on their bodies—no eyebrows to catch the incessant drip, no lashes, no thatch on the crown. Instead, the skin over their shoulders, spines, and the outer ridges of their forearms has thickened into soft, overlapping pleats, like the mantle of a slug or the caps of the bracket fungi. In the older two, small patches of that same velvet-green bryophyte that eats the stone walls have taken root in the damp creases behind their ears and along their collarbones, fed by their salty, oily sweat. They do not scrape it off; it breaks up their outline in the submarine green gloom.

If you were to catch one of them in a beam of real sunlight, the face would look unfinished, or partially melted, like the waxen pillars they mimic. The bridge of the nose is gone, replaced by two wide, upward-slanted slits ringed with a sphincter of pale muscle that can pinch shut when they push their faces into the warm, rotting mattress of the floor to hunt for blind snakes and white grubs. Their ears are huge, thin as bat-wings, and ribbed with cartilage that allows them to swivel independently, cupping the dead, pressurized air for the microscopic *tock* of a lemur’s suction-pad or the wet *snip* of a centipede’s mandibles. 

And their eyes: they have gone the way of the nocturnal primates in the canopy above, though not entirely. The irises are gone, swallowed by pupils so vast and hungry for the bruised green light that the orbs look like twin pools of crude oil set into deep, protective troughs of bone. A pale, milky third eyelid sweeps across them horizontally from the tear duct every half-minute, squeegeeing away the condensation and the drifting spores of the shelf fungi.

They do not live in the cane-and-mud cylinders on the ledges. Those were built by people who still remembered huts, who still thought of shelter as a dry box to keep the world out. The four who live here now know that a box is a trap, and dry things crack and make noise. Instead, they have given the cylinders over to the blind, ash-colored wasps, and have entered into a slow, parasitic arrangement with them. 

Once every few days, during the ten-second thermal rain, the youngest of the four—a female scarcely larger than a five-year-old child, though her flanks are heavy with the dense, pale fat they all carry—climbs the fluted wall to the ledges. Her hands and feet are extraordinary things: the palms and soles are deeply furrowed with soft, chevron-shaped ridges that swell with blood when she climbs, turning her extremities into wet, gripping pads that peel from the slick limestone without a sound. She reaches a cane cylinder, where the papery tiers of the wasp nests bulge out through the rotted roof. 

The wasps do not sting her. She smells of the cave floor, of crushed liverwort, old tallow, and the faint, ammoniacal musk of the blind snakes she rubs against her ribs for just this purpose. Moving with the glacial patience of a chameleon—a hand lifted, held through five minutes of dead air, then advanced six inches during the next thermal draft—she slips two fingers into the gray paper comb and breaks off a crescent of brood-cells. She does not flinch as the blind, wing-drifting wasps crawl over her knuckles, tasting her skin with their mandibles. She withdraws her hand over the course of twenty minutes, lowers it to her mouth, and lets the warm, buttery larvae dissolve on her tongue.

They have no tools because anything hard enough to be a tool makes a clicking sound against the karst, and in this abbey of ears and vibrations, a click brings the carmine centipedes pouring down from the crevices in the overhang, or wakes the pale, yard-long spiders that hang like clusters of dead roots beneath the philodendron leaves fifty feet up. Their only technology is maintenance, and it is practiced entirely with their bodies.

The carved basins at the base of the flowstone—the descending stair of bowls that catch the carbonic drip—are their hearth, though there is no fire. The ancestors carved them; the four merely keep them from disappearing. Over decades, the mineral-heavy water wants to fill its own bowls with fresh travertine, smoothing out the lips and choking the grooves. So, during the long, eventless hours between thermals, whichever of the four is resting at the base of the wall will curl beside the stair and place a thumb or a heel into the wet groove. For hours, while staring straight ahead with oil-black eyes into the violet-veined arums, they will work that thumb back and forth in a two-inch arc—*rub, pause, rub, pause*—using the grit of chewed crab-shell and their own weak, acidic saliva to keep the channels open and the water trickling silently from tier to tier. They do not know what the coiled spirals around the rims meant to the carvers; to them, the grooves are simply the places where their fingers fit.

They do not speak. The larynx in their soft throats has atrophied into a small, cartilaginous valve used only to seal the windpipe when they submerge their faces in the humus. If one of them were to scream, the sound would bounce off the curved limestone walls, amplified and distorted before being eaten by the moss, and for ten minutes afterward every predator in the dark would converge on the source of the vibration. 

Instead, they have the skin-talk, and they have the humus. 

When two of them meet at the base of the cairns, they press their broad, clammy flanks together, aligning the pleated skin of their shoulders. Through the contact, they pass long, low-frequency shivers—muscular fibrillations too faint to stir the air a millimeter away, a silent vocabulary of *full*, *empty*, *shedding*, *up*, *down*. When they are apart, they use the floor. The pitch-black mattress of decomposing leaves and roots is a drumhead that connects the entire oval of the nave. By pressing a six-fingered palm flat into the warm rot and flexing the tendons of the wrist, the old male at the base of a fig tree can send a single, dampened thrum through the mulch—a pulse indistinguishable from the drop of a heavy fruit or the burrowing of a snake to anything that isn’t tuned to its exact cadence. Thirty paces away, half-buried under a retaining wall, the young female feels the pulse in the bones of her jaw and knows not to come toward the eastern wall because the carmine ribbons are hunting there in force.

At the center of their lives are the cairns—those slumped, moss-furred mounds of dry-stacked karst tucked into the deep undercuts. They are not graves, or at least, they are not *only* graves. 

In a world where the floor is a furnace of bacterial rot that can strip a fallen lemur to red-stained teeth in forty-eight hours, calcium and phosphorus are too precious to let the ferns have. When one of the sinkhole people dies—as a fifth one did three seasons ago, her blood poisoned by the bite of a brass-colored frog she was too slow to catch—the others do not bury the body in the black mulch. Waiting for the ten-second intervals of the thermal rain, working in ten-second bursts spread across three days, they drag the corpse into the undercut and pull down the stones of a cairn. 

They lay the body inside and cover it back up with the grey karst blocks, leaving gaps just wide enough for the blind snakes, the carmine centipedes, and the tiny, cream-clawed crabs to enter, while keeping the larger scavengers and the strangler-fig roots out. Within a week, the small things have cleaned the bones in total silence. 

A month later, the survivors open the cairn again. They take the skull, the femurs, the ribs—already stained a pale, tallow yellow by the cave’s sweat—and they arrange them along the low, curving retaining walls, or wedge them into the forks of the cavern-figs. And then they eat them. Not all at once, and not with their teeth, which are small, peg-like, and suited only for soft grubs, wasp-comb, and brittle crab carapaces. They lick them. Over years, sitting motionless in the gloom while the saucer-moths pulse like gills around their heads, they hold the femurs of their mothers and brothers against their mouths, letting their acidic saliva slowly dissolve the calcium phosphate, drinking their ancestors back into their own soft, light-starved frames so their bones won't bend like rubber in the dark. The oldest skulls in the undercuts have been licked down until they are no thicker than the paper of the wasp nests, translucent domes of bone through which the bruised green light faintly shines.

Wait long enough—until the green stain overhead darkens toward the true black of surface-night, when the thermal drafts slow down because the jungle above has cooled—and you will see all four of them come together. 

They do not sleep on the floor; the night-shift in the humus belongs to the things with antennae three feet long and the blind burrowers that rise when the heat settles. One by one, between the slow drips of the stalactites, the four pale shapes flow up the side of the central flowstone pillar. They climb until they are twenty feet above the ferns, where a massive strangler-fig root loops out from the stone like a flying buttress, draped in a yard-deep pelt of Spanish moss and air-roots. 

They crawl inside the hanging curtain of roots and fold themselves together into a single, indistinguishable knot of tallow-colored limbs, their six-fingered hands gripping each other’s wrists, their flat, noseless faces tucked into the hollows of each other’s necks so their warm breath doesn't condense on the outside of the moss and give them away by the drip. The bryophytes on their backs blend with the epiphytes on the vine. A foot-long centipede pours over the bark six inches above their heads, its hundred legs dimpling the moss, and none of the four even blinks their milky inner eyelids. Their heartbeats slow to twelve times a minute. Their skin cools until it matches the wet limestone to a tenth of a degree. 

Above them, fifty feet up, a giant philodendron leaf shifts in the night wind of the world they no longer know exists, and a single drop of real rainwater falls all the way down the shaft, striking the crown of the old male’s head with a soft *plat*. He does not move. It is just water, and everything here is water, and there are still six hours until the green light comes back to wake the moths.
