To find what lives here, you have to stop looking for things with blood or eyes, and start looking for things that have learned how to be an interruption. 

The valley is a closed loop of mechanical habit. The ferro-brier remembers the refinery, so it builds the refinery. But a blueprint copied ten thousand times without an architect eventually develops smudges. Errors accumulate in the drafting. 

In the high, whistling narrows between two freshly braided fractionating towers, a three-foot segment of secondary creeper fails to weld. Instead of anchoring to the latticework, it curls back on itself, biting its own iron-plated tail to form a closed ring around a tension-cable. When the main cable winches upward, hauled by the shifting dunes below, the ring does not snap. Its inner scales, lubricated by a weeping fissure of graphite powder, slide along the rising vine. 

This is the simplest thing that lives here: the *grommet*, or the *collar*. It is a parasitic geometry. Once detached from the root-network, a collar has no metabolic purpose of its own, only an imperative to remain intact. As the great boiler-thick vines shoot across the dark chasms, hauling tons of deadweight, the collars ride them like beads on an abacus wire. They survive by fleeing the pinch-points. Because a collar is lined with nodes of piezoelectric quartz harvested from the deep silica ash, it can feel the electrical scream of building tension in the vine it rides. A split second before two scaffolding-trunks cross and shear each other into splinters, the collar contracts its horn-hooks, catches the outer bark of the intersecting vine, and Vaults across the junction with a dry, metallic *clack*. 

There are millions of them in the dark, clicking back and forth along the frantic loom of the valley, polished to a dull gunmetal sheen by centuries of friction, never touching the ground, living entirely on the kinetic hand-me-downs of the forest’s labor.

Where the collars gather in too great a number, jamming a junction, they attract the *fuses*. 

A fuse is not born; it is extruded. Deep in the three-hundred-foot mattress of the basin floor, where the weight of the slag-dunes crushes the shed iron-scales into a grinding, red-hot paste, the heat sometimes melts the silica ash into long, ropy curds of impure glass. The churning of the valley floor rolls these curds in graphite dust and iron filings, stretching them like taffy between the roots until they are as thin and flexible as whip-antennas, twenty or thirty feet long.

They have no heads, no mouths, and no organs. They are simply thermotropic filaments—long, shivering nerves of flexible obsidian and magnetized iron that slither up from the dunes into the dark scaffolding. They move by peristalsis, expanding and contracting their glassy rings as they absorb and shed heat. A fuse hunts for thermal bottlenecks. When two massive brier-cables rub together without enough graphite to lubricate them, the friction builds a localized hotspot that threatens to ignite the sulfur in their veins. The fuse smells this heat—or rather, its front tip expands toward the infrared bloom, dragging the rest of its whip-like body behind it through the shifting latticework. 

It slides directly into the grinding jaws of the two trunks, offering its own body as a bearing. The immense pressure that would shatter wood or iron merely flattens the semi-molten glass of the fuse. It lies in the pinch-point, glowing a bright, sudden tangerine in the dark, soaking up the destructive friction until its body is nearly white-hot—at which point it slips out the other side, slithers out onto a cool, slack suspension bridge, and drapes itself into the black air to radiate the stolen heat away, hardening back into black glass before it drops back into the tangle to feed again.

Longer, stranger things live in the spaces the brier forgets to fill.

Because the forest is constantly building and unbuilding manifolds, pipes, and containment drums, it inevitably traps pockets of its own exhaust. In the center of a newly woven tower, a seamless chamber of resin-welded bark might hold fifty thousand cubic feet of stagnant, oven-hot air, thick with suspended graphite dust, sulfur vapor, and the high-voltage static electricity thrown off by the grinding iron scales outside.

Something inhabits these temporary rooms. It has no body of its own; it is an organization of the dust.

When the static charge on the walls of a closed brier-chamber reaches a critical threshold, the floating graphite and iron particulates inside do not settle. They align. They link together along invisible lines of magnetic flux, forming a three-dimensional, cobweb-thin nervous system that fills the dark room like a cloud of black lace. 

This is the *Archivist*, though there may be only one of it, broken into a thousand pieces across the twelve square miles of the basin, or a thousand of them that occasionally merge when their rooms are torn open. 

The Archivist does not eat heat; it eats architecture. Suspended in the dead air of a hollow tower, its soot-filaments brush against the interior walls, reading the grain of the wood, the pitch of the resin, the exact tension of the braided horn-hooks. It is a dry, electrostatic intelligence, thinking in slow, crackling blue arcs that jump from one dust-cluster to the next. 

When the tension in the valley shifts and the tower begins to groan, preparing to snap apart and re-weave itself elsewhere, the Archivist does not wait to be dispersed into the dead sky. It condenses. It pulls its miles of floating, microscopic soot-chains down into the hollow, bone-white shell of an un-dropped tumble-gear hanging from the chamber roof, packing itself into the dry tendon-springs like gunpowder in a cartridge. 

When the wall rips open and the tower collapses in a dry roar of sliding scales, the heavy tumble-gear drops into the churning ruin. It rolls down the slag-slopes, clicking softly, carrying the compressed ghost of the demolished building inside it. 

Except this gear does not unwind in a random crevice. Guided by the magnetic weight of the dust packed inside its belly, it rolls selectively, tilting itself on the slopes to catch the pull of a rising gantry-vine. When its drill-root finally fires, it fires directly into the vascular main-line of a young, growing creeper. 

The Archivist injects itself into the plant’s dry, pumping stream of sulfur and graphite. And as that vine shoots across a fifty-foot gap to build a new suspension bridge, its growth becomes slightly warped. The hooks latch at a strange, unnecessary angle. The secondary creepers swarm across and weld themselves not into a refinery pipe, or a collector dish, or a warehouse roof—but into a seamless, dark, static-shielded chamber with no industrial purpose at all. A bedroom for a cloud of iron dust, which immediately bleeds out of the wood's pores to hang in the center of the new dark, humming quietly to itself, while outside, the continent of sandpaper whispers on.
