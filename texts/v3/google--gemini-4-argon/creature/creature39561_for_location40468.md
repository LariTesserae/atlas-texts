If you sit very still on one of the high, sun-bleached catwalks—tucking yourself into the shadow of a dry manifold so the ultraviolet glare doesn't blister your neck—you will eventually realize that the rust-colored primates are not the apex of this iron canopy. You will know this when a troop of them, mid-bark and mid-swing along a four-inch hawser cable, suddenly goes dead silent, drops into the wet fern-curtains below, and leaves the wire empty.

Wait another five minutes. Watch the junction where the main steam-riser meets the transverse girder. 

What detaches itself from the rivet-heads is not a beast, at least not at first glance. It looks like a maintenance bundle that has gone to seed. Only when a long, multi-jointed limb reaches out to test the vibration of a turnbuckle do you see the anatomy beneath the patina.

They are called the *Gasket-Weavers* by the few low-altitude dirigible crews who have ever drifted close enough with a spyglass to see them, though the name is too small for what they actually are. They are the descendants of the original crew—or the crew’s beasts of burden, or perhaps the automated bronze-and-leather maintenance rigs that were left running when the builders died out, though after four hundred years of wet heat and ferric spore-drift, the distinction between those three things has entirely dissolved.

A mature specimen stands seven feet at the shoulder, though it rarely stands. It is built for the three-dimensional geometry of the truss. It has four primary limbs, all of equal length, ending not in hands or paws, but in heavy, three-toed calipers of blackened horn. The inner curves of these calipers are grooved and calloused to the exact radius of a standard two-inch pipe. Its torso is barrel-chested, housing a tremendous, bellows-like lung capacity suited to the thin, violet air of the upper tiers, and its skin is a thick, segmented hide the color of boiled leather, perpetually slick with a secreted lanolin that smells faintly of hot tallow and crushed fern. 

Across its back and shoulders, the creature carries its "coat"—a living, symbiotic shawl of packed ochre rust-soil, wire-moss, and small, clicking brass gauges that it has wedged into the folds of its own scar tissue. When a Weaver curls up against the curve of a boiler housing to sleep through the freezing midday shadow or the cold night, it is completely indistinguishable from a burst lagging-jacket overgrown with weeds.

They do not have eyes in the way the soot-swifts do. The ultraviolet light at this altitude is too cruel for wet corneas. Instead, their broad, flat skulls are set with four deeply recessed thermal pits, lined with iridescent, heat-sensing scales like the belly of a copperhead, flanked by two wide, cup-shaped tympanic membranes stretched over bone rings on either side of the jaw.

A Weaver does not see the five-mile canyon of iron; it *hears* the chord of it, and it *sees* the heat of it. To a Weaver, the world is a glowing, vibrating lattice of magenta steam-arteries, white-hot friction points, and the cool, dark blue shadows of dead condensers, all thrumming in a three-part harmony of pressure, torque, and expansion.

And they are working. They are always working. 

Not with intelligence—not the way a human engineer reads a blueprint—but with the terrible, unhurried, genetic compulsion of a beaver building a dam or a termite mounding clay. The machine is their body’s outer shell. If the boilers go cold, the fifty-yard cloud forests freeze in a single night, the translucent frogs die in their grease-pans, and the Weavers freeze with them. So the Weavers tend the heat.

If you watch one moving along a catwalk, its gait is a marvel of heavy, swinging grace. It does not walk *on* the grating; it moves *through* the structure, using the slow, two-minute stroke of the cottage-sized pistons below as a metronome. It swings an arm out—*clack*—hooking a horn-caliper over a tie-rod just as the deck-plate heaves upward, using the engine’s own titanic momentum to loft its four-hundred-pound bulk twelve feet into the air, catching an overhead flange without looking, never breaking the rhythm.

When a steam pipe splits too wide, venting precious water vapor out into the dry, hungry gulf where the wind will steal it away from the ferns, the Weavers gather. 

Three or four of them will converge on the leak, drawn from half a mile away by the change in the acoustic chord of the truss. A bad leak whistles in a high, sharp key that clearly causes them physical pain; they will approach it with their tympanic membranes flattened back against their skulls, making low, rattling bass notes in their chests that sound like loose baffle-plates. 

They do not have wrenches. They have their mouths, their horn-hooks, and their stomachs. 

One Weaver will hang by its rear calipers from an overhead beam, lowering itself directly into the scalding white plume of the steam-jet—its tallow-slicked leather hide shedding the boiling water like duck feathers—and clamp its heavy, vise-like jaws onto the split iron lip of the pipe, using its own body weight as a lever to draw the seam narrower. The others will set to work with the jungle. They tear up great, fibrous mats of hawser-vine roots, chew them into a dense, pulpy oakum mixed with their own heavy, resinous saliva, and pack the mash into the screaming fissure. Over this, they smear mouthfuls of the ochre rust-mud dug from the girder-junctions, mixed with the thick, black cylinder-oil they scoop from the drip-pans of the lower tiers. 

Within an hour, they have built a bulbous, reinforced cast around the pipe—half termite-carton, half fibrous root-ball. Within a week, the heat of the pipe bakes the oil-and-rust mud into an iron-hard ceramic, while the chewed vine-pulp sprouts anew in the dampness, wrapping the repair in living, tightening green cables. 

The entire middle tier of the structure is held together by these grey-and-red carbuncles of Weaver-masonry.

Because they live by vibration, their social lives are conducted entirely through the metal. They have no vocal cords to speak of; the constant bronze roar of the dynamos would drown out a shout ten feet away. Instead, a Weaver communicates by dropping its heavy chest against a main load-bearing girder and striking the iron with a specialized, ossified hammer-plate on its wrist. 

*Clang-clack. Clack-clang-clang.*

The code travels through the riveted plates at five thousand feet per second. A Weaver striking a flange on the eastern distillation tower can be felt in the soles of another Weaver’s feet half a mile away across the central chasm, carried across the sagging suspension bridges through the braided steel cables. If you press your ear to a hand-rail at dusk, when the solar expansion joints stop creaking and the metal cools, the entire five-mile mountain range rings with their slow, patient telegraphy—a vast, hollow conversation of *here-is-heat*, *here-is-water*, *the-great-wheel-binds*, *come-and-chew-the-vine*.

They are not the only things up here that have adapted to the long maintenance, however. Every garden has its pests, and every machine has its parasites.

Down in the dark, grease-slicked bowels of the lower tiers, where the sunlight never penetrates the six layers of leaf-canopy and iron deck, live the *Sump-Eels*—six-foot, blind, legless amphibians, evolved from the tiny translucent puddle-frogs, that swim through the warm, waist-deep sludge of emulsified steam-water and lubricating oil that collects in the bilges of the foundation plates. They feed on the organic detritus that rains down from the hanging jungles above—dead beetles, rotted fern-fronds, monkey bones—and occasionally they work their way up into the low-pressure return pipes, clogging the valves until the pipes hammer and throb. When a pipe hammers, the Weavers come down from the sunlit tiers in silent, grim working-parties, locate the bulge in the lead piping with their thermal pits, and systematically crush the pipe flat with rocks or dropped counterweights until the blockage is pulverized and flushed through to the boilers to be burned as fuel.

And then there are the *Copper-Thieves*—which are not animals at all, but a species of woody, parasitic banyan that has learned to eat current. 

Unlike the gentle epiphytes that merely drink the sweat of the condenser coils, the Copper-Thief drives sharp, acidic root-hairs directly through the rubberized fabric insulation of the thick electrical conduits that span the chasm. It draws on the direct current of the dynamos to catalyze its own violent, accelerated growth, using electro-osmosis to pull mineral water up three hundred feet of vertical ironwork. Where a Copper-Thief takes hold, it grows in jagged, lightning-bolt angles, its bark plated with gray-green verdigris, its leaves stiff and sharp as tin shears, humming with a faint, ozone-smelling static charge that makes the hair on your arms stand up from twenty paces away. 

Left unchecked, a Thief-vine will short out a main bus-bar in a season, exploding in a shower of violet sparks and burning copper that can set a whole terrace of dry rust-ferns ablaze.

The Weavers hate the Thief-vines with a cold, ancient fury. You will see an old, scar-backed Weaver hanging upside down beneath a suspension bridge over three thousand feet of empty, cloud-floored air, methodically sawing through a wrist-thick electrical vine with a piece of salvaged steel strapping held in its horn-calipers. The sparks from the shorted plant shower down over the creature’s face and chest, singeing the moss on its back, disappearing into the abyss below like falling stars, while the Weaver simply closes its thermal pits, grips the swaying cable with its hind hooks, and saws away in time with the two-minute *thrum... chuff...* of the engines below.

When a Weaver finally grows too old—when its joints stiffen with mineral deposits from the hard water, and its tympanic membranes thicken until the great bronze chord of the turbines fades to a dull murmur—it does not fall into the gulf. Nothing is wasted in the trusswork. 

An old Weaver climbs down, away from the violet sky and the bright brass governors, down into the hot, dark, roaring core of the primary boiler-works where the heat is too intense for the ferns to grow. There, it finds a weak spot in the iron—a hairline fracture in a cast-iron pedestal, or a flange where the bolts have rusted down to toothpicks—and it wedges itself into the gap. 

It hooks its four horn-calipers into the rivet-holes above and below the crack, pulls its heavy, barrel-chested frame tight to take the shear-load off the metal, and locks its joints.

They have a tendon mechanism in their limbs, like the perching feet of a starling, that cannot be unbent once the weight settles on it. The Weaver dies there in the dark, baked dry within a week by the radiant heat of the fireboxes. Over the years, the dripping mineral water from the condensers above calcifies the corpse, turning the boiled-leather hide and the heavy bones into ironstone, while the strangler-figs send down exploratory roots to thread through its ribcage and bind it to the girder.

If you walk the lower catwalks with a torch, shining the beam into the wet, vibrating junctions where the great trusses meet, you will see dozens of them. They look like caryatids carved from red oxide and old brass, completely fused into the architecture, their horned hands still locked around the flanges, holding the mountain up in the dark so the gardens above can stay in the sun.
