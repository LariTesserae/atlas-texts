To find the thing that rules this room, you have to look at the floor for a very long time, until your eyes adjust to the standing grid of the Faraday waves.

The water is four inches deep, warm, and cross-hatched with a microscopic, diamond-shaped lattice of vibration. Everything in the puddle obeys this geometry. When a dead crane fly drops from the upper ferns, it does not drift; it is locked instantly into the acoustic grid, jiggling in place until the bacteria dismantle it.

Except there is a section of the floor near the base of the north wall—right where the fifty-hertz roar of the turbines is strongest—where the diamond grid is missing. 

Across an irregular patch about six feet wide, the water does not cross-hatch. It moves in slow, greasy concentric rings, like heavy oil being stirred from underneath by a very slow finger. The surface tension there is thick and slightly iridescent. If you look closely through the amber light, you realize that the pale, translucent travertine of the floor isn't floor at all in that spot. 

It is a mat of flesh, flattened to the thickness of a heavy wet blanket, and it has no edges that stay in the same place for more than an hour.

Ninety years ago, before the intake grates of the upper settling lagoons were fitted with micro-screens, the municipal river was much dirtier, and the city’s slaughterhouses and tanneries dumped straight into the main channel. During the great spring flood of 1934, the baffled spillways above the sump briefly choked and overflowed. What washed down into the newly sealed chamber was not a single animal, nor even a single species. It was a broth of river-bottom silt, freshwater sponge gemmules, bryozoan statoblasts, slime-mold plasmodia, tubifex worms, and the warm, tallowy runoff of the rendering plants. 

Locked inside the dark, booming oven of the sump, marinated in dissolved calcium and battered day and night by an acoustic pressure that never relented, something in that biological slurry failed to stay separate.

Today, it has no true taxonomic name, though it is built on the chassis of a colonial freshwater bryozoan that has forgotten how to make individual zooids, fused with a mutated, calcium-secreting myxomycete. It is a single, room-spanning sheet of pale, ochre-veined syncytium—a giant, multinucleated mass of protoplasm with the consistency of warm marshmallow and beef tallow, reinforced throughout with a flexible, gritty mesh of calcium carbonate spicules. 

It is the *Tapestry*, or the *Damp*. 

At rest, it is nearly indistinguishable from the slumped flowstone of the walls. It is the exact color of wet tallow, marbled with the rust-red grease of the dissolved iron tie-rods it has ingested for their minerals. It has no eyes, no mouth, no brain, and no digestive tract. Instead, it is an external stomach and a giant ear, rolled into one.

In a world where the sound is total, the Damp does not use nerves to coordinate itself; it uses the turbine’s roar. 

The calcium spicules suspended in its jelly are aligned like iron filings in a magnetic field, arranged along the standing acoustic waves of the room. The fifty-hertz hum passes through the creature’s flat body at a different speed than it passes through the water or the air. The Damp feels the entire forty-by-forty room as a single, continuous chord of vibration. When an amphipod walks across the moss thirty feet up the south wall, its tiny legs slightly dampen the vibration of the concrete beneath it. To the Damp, that microscopic deadening of the wall's hum registers as a cool shadow in the acoustic field.

When it feeds, it does not chase. Nothing in this room is fast enough to require chasing.

During the day, when the greenish-gold light pours down from the skylight, the largest portion of the Damp lies pooled on the floor, absorbing the ambient heat of the water and farming. Its upper surface is peppered with millions of microscopic, crater-like pores. From these pores, it exudes a sweet, mucilaginous slime rich in urea and dissolved lime. The *Bryum* moss and the liverworts do not just grow on the concrete; they grow *into* the margins of the Damp, drinking its nitrogenous sweat. The terrestrial amphipods wander down from the walls to graze on the rich bacterial velvet that blooms along the creature’s skin.

The Damp lets them graze. It lets ten, twenty, fifty of the pale little crustaceans crawl out onto its body. Its skin matches the fifty-hertz shiver of the stone so perfectly that the amphipods never realize they have stepped off the travertine and onto a living mantle. 

Then, locally, across a patch the size of a dinner plate, the Damp simply stops vibrating.

It relaxes the tension of its internal spicule-mesh, going acoustically dead. The sudden absence of the fifty-hertz tremor beneath their feet acts like a nerve-agent on the amphipods; bred for generations in a vibrating world, their proprioception fails instantly in the stillness. They freeze, confused, their antennae drooping. 

Slowly—over the course of twenty minutes—the pale, tallowy floor rises around their legs. It does not grab; it simply swells upward like rising dough, folding over the tiny creatures until they are sealed inside warm, translucent blisters of flesh. Inside those blisters, the pH drops rapidly. Within two hours, there is nothing left of the amphipods but their chitinous shells, which the Damp strips of calcium to build more internal spicules, before ejecting the empty, transparent husks back out into the puddle.

At night, when the skylight goes black and the green-gold block of light vanishes, the Damp changes its shape. 

Without the sun to warm the upper walls, the temperature gradients in the room flatten out, and the creature begins to climb. It moves by hydraulic peristalsis, pumping its thick, mineral-heavy protoplasm upward through veins as wide as garden hoses, sliding *underneath* the three-inch-thick pelt of moss and creeping fig. 

If you were suspended in the pitch-black center of the room at midnight with a flashlight, the walls would look like they were breathing. You would see the heavy curtains of maidenhair ferns rising and falling in slow, bulbous waves as the massive, flat body of the Damp slides between their roots and the concrete wall, lubricating its passage with ochre slime. 

It climbs all the way to the ceiling, sixty feet up, spreading out across the underside of the three-foot-thick wire-glass skylight. Pressed flat against the glass, it looks from below like a vast, pale placenta, veined with rust and dark green algal inclusions, thirty feet across. It hangs there in the dark to harvest the crane flies. 

The flies, hanging sluggishly from the highest fern tips in the dark, feel the air grow heavier and warmer as the massive sheet of protoplasm spreads above them. From its hanging surface, the Damp extrudes thousands of long, hair-thin filaments of clear mucus, weighted at the tips with tiny beads of liquid calcium. They look like spider silk, or the lures of cave-dwelling fungus gnats, hanging down two or three feet into the black air. 

These threads do not swing; there is no wind. Instead, tuned by the creature's body above, each sticky thread vibrates at a harmonic—a hundred hertz, or two hundred—humming silently in the dark like a plucked harp string. To the crane flies, whose long legs are tuned to the vibrations of fern fronds, these humming threads feel like the safest, sturdiest perches in the dark. They reach out, step onto the filaments, and are drawn slowly upward over the course of the night as the Damp reels its drool back into its body before the morning sun returns.

And then there is the crayfish.

Near the base of the west wall, in the shallow, ochre-stained basin, the thirty-year-old blind cave crayfish sits under its canopy of fern roots. The crayfish is very old—far older than its kind ever lives in the wild—and its shell is no longer white. It is encrusted with a thick, velvety layer of the Damp. 

They have reached an understanding, or rather, a biological stalemate that has turned into an organ system. The crayfish is too large and strong for the Damp to smother and dissolve; when it was younger, it simply tore through the protoplasmic sheets with its pincers whenever the creature tried to envelope it. 

Instead, the Damp has colonized the crayfish’s shell like a living, calcified overcoat, sealing the seams of its aging carapace so the old crustacean no longer has to molt—a process that would likely kill it now. In return, the Damp feeds the crayfish. Once or twice a week, a thin, pale pseudopod creeps into the west basin, carrying a concentrated pellet of half-digested amphipod meat and compressed algae, and places it directly between the crayfish's maxillipeds. 

Why the Damp keeps the old crustacean alive is a matter of engineering. Every few days, when the calcium carbonate precipitating out of the warm water threatens to cement the floor’s hidden weep-holes shut—which would flood the chamber and ruin the Damp's vertical pastures—the crayfish stirs. Guided by chemical cues pumped directly through its shell by its symbiote, the blind, heavy creature crawls out of its basin, walks across the vibrating diamond grid of the floor, and uses its heavy, calcified claws to mechanically scrape and pick the fresh travertine crust out of the drainage holes. When the drain is clear and the water level is safe, it lumbers back to its fern roots, settles down into the warm mud, and waits for its pale, faceless keeper to bring it another meal.

Nothing enters, and nothing leaves. The turbines roar at 1,200 RPM behind the north wall, shaking the world at fifty cycles a second. And inside the amber cube of light, the walls themselves slide slowly up and down behind the shivering ferns—a single, ton-heavy ear of warm grease and lime, listening to the engine that keeps it alive.
