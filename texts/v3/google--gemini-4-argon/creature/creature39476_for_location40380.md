To find the people of the lattice, you do not look with your eyes—the glare off the bleached wood and the fluttering wall of sails will blind you before you can pick a silhouette out of the scaffolding. You have to drop to your knees, press the heels of your hands against the warm, polished ironwood, and feel for the rhythm that does not belong to the wind.

The forest’s vibration is cyclical, heavy, and immense: the sixty-second buildup of the great tension-boughs, the *thwack-boom* of the release, the three-beat shudder of the ratchets resetting. Beneath that, if a clan is within five miles, you will feel a sharper, staccato tapping. *Two-three, pause, one-four.* It is the strike of heavy, horn-shod heels and brass-headed mallets against the grain, using the mile-long beams as telegraph wires.

They call themselves the *Kinet*, though the word is not spoken with the mouth. Nothing here is. In a world that roars like the inside of a dry turbine, vocal cords are vestigial. Even if you screamed into another’s ear, the gale would tear the sound away before it crossed the inch of air between you, and the effort would only waste the precious, warm moisture of the lungs. 

When they come into view, swinging down from an upper spar on braided cords of shed tree-tendon, they look at first like another piece of the forest’s own mechanical anatomy. They are tall, lean to the point of appearing skeletal, and wrapped tightly in wind-cured sail-membrane that has been oiled with axle-grease until it is translucent and supple as silk. Not an inch of skin is exposed to the desiccating air. Their faces are hidden behind masks of carved burl-wood, fitted with narrow horizontal slits—snow-goggles against the bone-white glare—and long, downward-curving snouts packed with sponge-horn filters to trap the swirling clouds of mineral dust and toasted resin before it can cake their lungs.

Their movement is terrifyingly fluid. A Kinet never fights the motion of the wood; to stand rigid on a primary bough is to have your ankles snapped the moment a tension-branch releases five miles away. They walk with a rolling, deep-knee gait, their joints constantly flexing to drink up the tremor of the floor. On their feet, they wear *crampon-claws*—split-toed boots of vulcanized sap and brass that can either grip the microscopic grooves of the wind-scoured ivory wood or, with a shift of the heel, lock into the greased channels where the great tendons run, allowing a person to be hauled a half-mile upslope in seconds by the tree’s own flexing muscles.

### The Architecture of the Pendulum

Nothing can be built *on* the boughs. The vibration would shake a stationary hut to splinters in an afternoon, and the shifting of the lattice during a wind-tack would crush anything wedged into a joint. 

Instead, a Kinet village hangs. 

Beneath a major arterial bough—perhaps three layers down from the blinding crown, where the light begins to soften into a dusty, luminous pearl—they suspend thirty-foot spindles of woven wicker and cured hide. These housings hang from gimbals of greased ironwood, tethered by long, elastic bungees of braided tendon. While the forest above and around them shrieks, bucks, and heaves fifty feet into the air during a gale-shift, the spindle-houses sway in slow, majestic arcs, entirely insulated from the bone-rattling shockwaves of the scaffolding.

Inside a spindle, the world is dim, warm, and smells heavily of roasted wood-sugar, tallow, and dry sweat. It is the only place where the masks come off. 

Unmasked, the Kinet are striking. Generations in the hyper-baric, high-altitude glare have given them skin the color of old parchment, tough and slightly leathery, with subcutaneous deposits of waxy fat around the cheekbones and eyes. Their eyes are huge, the irises a pale, reflective amber that can dilate to drink in the blue gloom of the lower levels or contract to pinpricks in the sun. Because they do not speak, their faces are hyper-mobile, capable of flickering through fifty distinct expressions in the space of five seconds, paired with the rapid, fluttering clack of finger-tallies—bone thimbles worn on the thumb and middle finger that they snap together like castanets to punctuate their sign-talk.

Even in the dampened quiet of the hanging houses, they never stop moving. Stillness makes them anxious; to a Kinet, when something stops vibrating, it is either dead or about to break. In their sleep, their feet twitch in time with the sixty-second recoil of the bough overhead.

### The Harvest of Friction

There is no soil to till, and no water to boil. The Kinet are parasites and engineers of the forest's kinetic metabolism.

Their hearths do not burn wood—fire is the ultimate horror here; a single spark in the bone-dry, oxygen-rich gale would turn five hundred square miles of ironwood lattice into a blast furnace in an hour. Instead, they cook and warm themselves with *friction-brakes*. A thick rope of green, fibrous vine is looped around a passing external tendon of the tree and run down into the spindle-house, where it wraps around a central drum of dense burl-stone. By tightening a lever, a householder increases the drag on the spinning drum. Within seconds, the stone block is radiating a fierce, dry heat, hot enough to bake cakes of compressed xylem-powder and roast the dense, oily meat of the six-legged chasm-runners.

Water is the true currency, and it is never found in a liquid state. The Kinet are masters of distillation. They harvest the *grease-slough*—the thick, hydrocarbon-rich emulsions that the trees secrete to keep their internal cams from catching fire—and the damp, pulpy marrow from the center of young tension-tendons. They pack this pulp into dark-stained bladder-sacks that are hung on the sunny side of the great sails. The relentless solar glare bakes the sacks, vaporizing the trapped moisture, which then trickles down through long, coiled tubes of hollow horn into shaded collection flasks in the cool, blue updrafts below. A single flask of clear, resin-bitter water is worth a man’s weight in brass chitin.

The hunters and gatherers of the clans are called *Line-Tappers*. They are the ones who go out into the white roar with long, hollow lances of brass-chitin. They know the anatomy of the wood the way a surgeon knows veins. By pressing a bone stethoscope to the bark, a Tapper can hear the high-pressure hiss of a xylem tube five feet deep in the timber, pumping its warm, snow-white storm of mineral flour and golden nitrogen-spores from the grinding mills in the trunks out to the growing tips of the sails. 

They drill in with hand-augers, timing their turns to the slack-phase of the tree’s sixty-second breath, and fit a valve into the wood. When the tension-bough snaps and the internal pressure spikes, the valve coughs a rich, warm dough of nutrient-paste directly into their collection bladders. Take too much, and the sail above goes slack, starves, and tears away in the wind, causing the surrounding lattice to warp and drop. A greedy clan literally saws off the branch they are hanging from.

### The Grease-Wrights and the Seizure

Because their survival depends on the smooth operation of the forest’s machinery, the Kinet act as its maintenance crew, much like the piston-necked scavengers, only with foresight.

Sometimes, the wind blows too hard from a single quarter for too many weeks, and a primary axle—a wooden cam the size of a cathedral—runs dry. You can feel a *Seizure* building from twenty leagues off. The rhythmic *thwack-boom* of that sector begins to drag. The pitch of the timber’s hum rises into an agonizing, discordant shriek that sets the teeth on edge across three dozen villages. Black smoke begins to curl from the seam of the joint as the friction-heat climbs toward ignition.

When a Seizure starts, every clan within felt-range drops their augers and mobilizes. 

Hundreds of Kinet converge on the screaming joint, riding the gale on personal kite-rigs of ribbed sail-horn, dropping down through the blinding lattice like spiders on silk. They carry heavy bladders of rendered beast-fat and wax-pulp Harvested from the deep, blue-lit rot-zones four miles down. Hanging by their ankles over six-mile drops of empty air, deafened by the shrieking timber and buffeted by burning-hot sawdust, the Grease-Wrights swing between the grinding, house-sized teeth of the wooden ratchets. 

They have forty seconds while the sail intakes its breath to smear the smoking channels with tallow and chip away the fused crusts of caramelized sap with long chisels. When the tension reaches its peak—marked by the sudden, terrifying silence of the wood locking up—the Wrights kick off into empty air, falling fifty feet on their bungee-tethers just as the cam breaks free with a thunderclap that can rupture an unpadded eardrum, sending the fifty-ton timber crashing past their noses.

### The Deep Drop

They have no graves. To leave a body in the upper lattice is to invite the brass-shelled gliders and the piston-necked scavengers to learn the taste of human grease. 

When a Kinet dies—whether from a frayed tether, a mistimed leap across a chasm, or the slow "white-lung" that eventually claims the old when the sponge-filters fail—the clan gathers on a Lowest Bough. This is seven or eight layers down, a mile beneath the sun-bleached surface, where the light is a permanent, twilight indigo and the roar of the wind overhead sounds like a distant, celestial surf. Here, the air is thicker, dusty with falling slough and dead spores, and the wood is dark, old, and rigid, forming the ancient compression-pillars that hold up the dynamic world above.

They strip the body of its moisture-suit, its goggles, and its brass tools. They do not wrap the corpse; instead, they lash the arms and legs to a wide, triangular frame of light sail-horn, turning the dead into a kite.

With a final, unison clatter of bone thimbles—a sharp, dry applause that echoes briefly off the colossal pillars—they cast the frame out into the blue abyss. 

Caught by the warm, rising thermal drafts that vent up from the sunless basalt crust five miles below, the dead do not fall at first. They wheel outward in great, slow spirals through the dim cathedral of the under-trunks, drifting for days among the pale, blind fungi and the slow-moving, spider-like bark-grazers of the deep layers, until the dry air drinks the last of their weight and the frame finally tips, sliding down through the violet gloom toward the stone floor that no living foot has ever touched.
