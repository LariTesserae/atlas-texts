You do not hear them before you see them, because there is no room left in the air for another sound. You become aware of them only as an interruption in the steam—a dark, upright shape standing fifty yards off beside a weeping brass manifold, motionless until the moment it is five yards closer.

They are called the Tenter-Men by the few records that survive from lower altitudes, though they are not exclusively men, and they no longer stretch cloth. What they are is the remainder of the city’s maintenance shift, four hundred generations after the blueprints were eaten by the walls.

At first glance, a Tenter looks like an oilskin coat hung on a frame of broomsticks and left out in the resin-mist to stiffen. They are tall—often six and a half feet—and impossibly spare, built of corded muscle and dry cartilage with not an ounce of tallow to soften their hinges. Fat is a liability on the Loom-Plateau; soft tissue liquefies or bruises under the unending, sixty-cycle-per-minute *thrum-clack* coming up through the soles of the feet. To survive the vibration, the Tenters have become fibrous. Their bones are dense and slightly flexible, heavily calcified by the mineral-leached xylem water they drink, and their joints are thick, knobby calluses that click like wooden toggles when they bend.

Their clothing is indistinguishable from their employment. They wear heavy, multi-layered smocks of felted bryophyte wool, cured in hot turpentine until it is as stiff and shiny as horsehide, shedding the lukewarm factory drizzle in greasy beads. Over this go aprons of flexible bark-leather and harnesses of salvaged copper wire, hung with tools that have no modern names: long, two-pronged tuning forks for testing the tension of underground root-valves; heavy brass spanners that have been grafted onto living ironwood handles; and bone scrapers used to clear away the rapid, milky cataracts of fast-growing cambium that constantly threaten to seal the factory vents shut.

They have no eardrums. The roaring cradle of the plateau ruptured the ears of their ancestors millennia ago, and evolution—or the slow, vegetative revision that passes for it here—simply sealed the canals over with smooth, pale scar-tissue. 

Instead, they hear with their heels and their teeth. 

A Tenter’s boots are their most sacred possession. The soles are cut from twelve-inch-thick slabs of *cork-burl*—a dead, spongy wood harvested from the deep underside of the plateau—laced onto the foot with rawhide and iron wire. When they walk, they roll across the silver moss with a high, rocking, flat-footed gait, like sailors on a pitching deck, absorbing the terrible upward punch of the subterranean pistons in the sponge of their soles. When a Tenter wants to listen to the city, they do not tilt their head. They drop to one knee, unlatch the heavy leather chin-guard of their cowl, and bite down hard on the brass head of a spanner whose other end is jammed against a root-rail or a sweating pipe. Through the jawbone, the deafening chaos of the plateau resolves into a crystalline map of working pressures: the wet *slap-hiss* of a failing lymphatic gasket three miles west; the dry, resin-starved shriek of a wooden flywheel overheating fifty fathoms down.

Their faces, when they lower their wraps of greased wool to breathe the thinner, cooler air between steam-vents, are grey-skinned and narrow, deeply grooved from nostril to chin like carved walnut. Their eyes are large, yellow-filmed against the glare of the high sky, and startlingly still. They do not blink vertically; they have developed a slow, horizontal nictitating membrane that wipes the turpentine condensation and wind-blown spores from the cornea without interrupting their sight. 

They cannot afford to blink when walking the seams.

Watch one of them cross an avenue where the geometry has sheared. A Tenter does not walk straight down a street; they tack across it like a ketch beating into the wind. Each carries a *plumb-wand*—a six-foot rod of flexible pewter-root tipped with a dried, hollow gourd containing three drops of mercury. As they walk, they whip the tip of the wand ahead of them in a low, continuous figure-eight. Where the air has hardened into an invisible pane, the wand rebounds with a dull *thwack* that travels up the Tenter’s wrist; where the plateau has slipped its moorings and a patch of turf is running four seconds behind the rest of the afternoon, the tip of the wand smears into a brief, fanshaped grey blur. 

Without breaking stride, the Tenter steps sideways, plants a cork-soled heel on the polished lip of a root-rail, and vaults cleanly through a window of air that is not there, disappearing from the left side of the street and emerging instantly thirty yards down the right, already reaching for a valve wheel.

Sometimes, the city’s repetitions catch them anyway. 

It is not uncommon, in the stuttering warehouse districts, to see a crew of five identical women in identical grease-blackened aprons working along the length of a single long brass main. They are not sisters; they are the same woman, caught in a quarter-mile pleat of the afternoon. The local custom for this is strict and unsentimental. The duplicates do not look at one another. They do not attempt to share tools. Each simply works her assigned forty yards of pipe, scraping the salt-crust from the brass flanges, until the underground engines shift their load, the air un-creases with a sound like a snapping cable, and four of her vanish in a puff of cold, dry ozone, leaving the remaining one with five times the work done and a splitting headache behind her eyes.

They do not live in the factories. The burls are too hot, too choked with scalding sap-fumes and the blind, crushing strokes of ten-ton timber cranks. Instead, the Tenters live in the *crotches*—the high, windy junctions where the massive exterior buttress-roots meet the vertical wooden walls of the mills, forty feet above the damp moss. There, out of reach of the ground-mist and the heaviest vibrations, they hollow out resin-lined burrows that smell fiercely of dried mountain moss, mutton-tallow from the six-legged canvas-elk, and old iron. 

Inside these small, trembling wooden caves, where the light enters through panes of shaved horn, their domestic life is entirely silent and entirely tactile. 

Because they cannot speak over the loom-roar and cannot hear if they tried, their language is a matter of pressure and carpentry. In the dark of a burrow, five or six of them will sit in a tight circle with their knees wedged firmly together and their bare, calloused hands resting on a *talk-board*—a thin, resonant disk of seasoned cedar balanced across their laps. They speak by tapping, scratching, and pressing the wood with their horn-thick fingernails. A heavy thumb-press in the center is an affirmation; a rapid, circular scrape with the side of the pinky is a warning of a spatial shear; a rhythmic, two-fingered drumming that mimics the *thrum-clack* of the floor, followed by a sudden flat palm that damps the board’s vibration, means *death*, or *engine-stop*, which to them are the same word.

Courtship, argument, and the passing down of the great, unwritten maintenance schedules of Oakhaven all happen through the wood between their knees. A child learns to read the board before it learns to walk the moss, its tiny fingers resting on its mother’s wrist to feel the grammar of the city’s pulse.

What are they maintaining? They no longer know, and the question has no shape on the talk-boards.

They only know that the plateau is six hundred feet thick and held aloft over an unthinkable void by the tension of its own working roots. If a factory-burl chokes on its own calluses and stops pumping, the root-mat for a mile around it cools, goes slack, and begins to rot. When the mat rots, the shears widen. Two winters ago, a forgotten refinery on the south rim seized its main beam; within a week, the three-mile valley of turf around it didn't collapse—it simply lost its friction with the present, drifted forty feet upward into the pale blue sky like a torn carpet, and blew away over the edge of the world in fragments the size of city blocks.

So at dawn, when the huge, cold sun clears the edge of the root-shelf and turns the steam-plumes to pillars of rose and brass, the burrows open. The leather curtains part. The Tenters drop down the rope ladders in their great cork boots, check the mercury in their wands, bite their iron wrenches to taste the morning’s rhythm, and walk out into the deafening, empty avenues to oil the hinges of the ground.
