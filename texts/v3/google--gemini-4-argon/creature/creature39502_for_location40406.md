If you walk the black pipeline for four miles down from the northern pass, your boots sliding on the iron where the spray has glazed it with pale algae, you will eventually see what looks like a grey basalt boulder resting in the notch between two of the great *Astelia* domes. 

Only when you are thirty paces away does the boulder heave itself upright, shedding a quart of cold water from the stiff, rubberized folds of its coat, and turn a flat disk of dark, smoked glass toward your face.

There are four of them in the basin at any given time, though you will almost never see two together. In the payroll ledgers down in the port towns—three days away by mule and narrow-gauge rail—they are listed as *Basin Line-Tenders*, or sometimes *Greasers*. Among themselves, when they are down out of the sky and drinking warm beer in the quiet, low-ceilinged taverns of the foothills, they do not call themselves anything at all, though the town-folk know them instantly by their walk and by the strange, deadened way they hold their heads.

Up here, a tender does not look entirely human. They are bulked out to the size of small draft-oxen by layer upon layer of boiled wool, oiled canvas, and heavy, vulcanized rubber waders that come up to the armpits. Because the glare comes from beneath their boots as fiercely as it comes from the sky, they cannot leave a single millimeter of skin exposed; even the undersides of their jaws and the insides of their nostrils would blister and peel by noon. They wear broad-brimmed sou'westers lashed tight under the chin with rawhide, and over their faces they wear leather masks with heavy brass-rimmed goggles, the lenses blackened with turpentine soot, and a long, hanging flap of double-thick canvas that covers the mouth and nose like a hound’s muzzle. 

They move at the pace of the valley. To hurry on the mat is to fight the ground itself; the stiff, waxy rosettes give way just enough to rob a fast stride of its spring, and the thin, freezing air punishes a quickened heart with a red spike of pain behind the eyes. So the tenders walk with a slow, rolling, flat-footed gait, planting a heavy hobnailed boot, waiting a beat for the *Donatia* to hiss and compress, and then swinging the other leg forward from the hip. From a mile off, moving across the blinding white-gold curve of a dome, a tender looks like a dark tick creeping through the bristles of a luminous hide.

Their ears are plugged with lamb’s wool soaked in tallow, and over the wool they wear thick pads of quilted horsehair. Even so, the roar of the scarps and the flat, dentist’s-drill scream of the turbines enter them through the soles of their boots and the cages of their ribs. A person who stays in the basin for a six-month hitch stops thinking in words after the third week. You cannot maintain an inner monologue when your teeth are vibrating in their sockets at sixty cycles a second. Instead, the tenders begin to think in pressures, in temperatures, and in rhythms. 

A tender’s work is simple, endless, and entirely physical. They are there to keep the valley from eating the iron.

Every day, each of the four walks a three-mile section of the pipeline. They carry a five-foot bar of octagonal tool-steel, flattened to a chisel at one end, and a heavy, short-handled machete with a belly like a meat cleaver. Where the cushion-plants are swelling up around the belly of the three-foot pipe—creeping over the rivets in hard, green, mineral-crusted lips that would eventually lever the iron right off its wooden saddles—the tender stops. They do not swing the machete; the mat would only bounce the blade back into their shins. Instead, they lean their whole two-hundred-pound weight onto the steel chisel, driving it down through the waxy rosettes with a slow, rhythmic grinding of their shoulders until they feel the gritty crunch of the woody peat-fibers giving way. They cut out great, rectangular sods of the living mat, each one weighing seventy pounds and dripping with iron-tasting water, and lever them away from the pipe. 

They do not throw the sods aside. If you leave a cut block of *Astelia* upside down on the surface, it will not die; its roots will simply drink the spray, and within a year it will have fused with the mat beneath it to form a hummock that changes the wind-flow and starts a new dome. Instead, the tenders haul the cut blocks to the edge of the nearest scarp and drop them into the roaring white curtains of the waterfalls, letting the hydraulic fury of the lower basin shred them into pulp.

To watch a tender check a pump-station is to see a creature communicating with a beast it has raised from birth. They do not use gauges; the glass dials on the turbines vibrate so violently that the needles are just grey blurs, and the moisture clouds them from the inside within a month of installation. 

Instead, the tender climbs the trembling iron ladder into the pale, corrugated box, pulls off one thick horsehair mitten, and lays a bare, grease-blackened palm flat against the housing of the main bearing. They close their eyes behind their dark goggles and lean their forehead against the screaming steel of the intake manifold, letting the bone of their skull take the pitch of the machine. They can feel a chipped tooth on a reduction gear three feet down the shaft; they can feel the slight, rhythmic hiccup of an auger-blade that has struck a buried rib of raw basalt fifty feet down in the dark under the peat; they can tell, by the warmth of the return-line against their thigh, whether the suction-head is choking on bladderwort and iron-bacterial slime.

When a station is sick, two tenders must work together. Since they cannot speak—even a scream directly into another man’s ear-pad is swallowed by the foundry-roar of the scarps—they have developed a language of flat-handed blows. One tender will strike the other twice on the point of the shoulder to ask for the four-foot wrench; a long, downward drag of the palm across the chest of a colleague's oilskin means *drop the pressure*; two sharp raps of a steel bar against the iron pipe itself will travel three hundred yards through the metal, felt through the soles of the other man’s boots as a distinct, sharp *clink-clink* beneath the steady hum of the water, calling him back from down the line.

They do not live in huts on the mat. If you build a hut on the cushion-sedge, the constant vibration of the earth and the slow, upward hydraulic heave of the plants will rack the frame apart in a single season, popping the nails from the beams and twisting the door-jambs until the roof collapses. 

Instead, the tenders live *inside* the pipeline’s support anchors. 

At three points along the twelve-mile valley, where the basalt steps are steepest, the company drove immense concrete caissons all the way through the fifty-foot mat to bolt the pipe to the living rock. Within the hollow cores of these concrete piers—twenty feet below the blinding surface of the bog, down where the living green gives way to the dense, brown, pickled corpse-matter of five-hundred-year-old roots—there are small, cylindrical rooms, eight feet across. 

To get home at dusk, a tender walks to a cast-iron manhole sticking up out of the *Donatia* like a ships-ventilator, unscrews the heavy brass dogs, drops down a vertical ladder into the dark, and pulls the lid shut over their head.

Down there, the sound changes. The high, tearing hiss of the spray and the scream of the turbines are cut off by twenty feet of saturated peat. What remains is the bass—a deep, monstrous, submarine thrum that never ceases, like living inside the chest of a giant who is running uphill. The concrete walls are always sweating cold beads of water that smell of sulfur and old celery, except along the eastern curve of the room, where the three-foot main pipeline passes directly through the chamber. The pipe is warm from the friction of the turbines and the pressure of the pumps—a steady sixty-five degrees.

The tender’s bunk is a curved wooden shelf bolted directly to the underside of that pipe. 

At night, stripped of his stiff, freezing rubber and his blinded goggles, the tender lies in the pitch dark in his damp woolens, his back pressed against the warm, trembling iron of the main line. His skin is pale as lard, except for a thin mask of leathery, purple-brown sunburn across the bridge of his nose where the goggles chafe, and his fingers are permanently curved into the shape of the chisel-bar, the nails thick and split from the cold water. 

He eats cold mutton fat, hard biscuits, and raw onions—food that requires no fire, because a stove down in the caisson would eat the thin oxygen and leave him gasping—and he drinks the rusty water tapped straight from a petcock on the pipe above his face. It tastes of basalt and old snow.

He is not the only thing down there. 

In the dark, warm two-inch gap between the sweating concrete of the caisson wall and the surrounding wall of compressed peat, something else has found a home. The tenders call them *pipe-lice*, though they are not lice at all, but an ancient, blind cousin of the surface weevils that has abandoned the sun entirely for the artificial summer around the company’s iron. They are the size of a man's thumb, completely translucent, their soft, wingless abdomens showing the dark, branching lines of their guts filled with digested root-fiber. They move even more slowly than the black weevils above; a cluster of twenty or thirty of them will hang on the warm concrete behind the tender’s head like a constellation of pale, greasy blisters, taking an entire week to shift three inches to the left as the warmth of the pipe fluctuates.

The tenders do not brush them away. When you have lived in the roar for four months, when the entire world above the manhole is a merciless white glare that wants to burn your retinas to ash and a green sponge that erases your footprints before you have walked fifty yards, there is a strange comfort in reaching up in the dark, touching the sweating stone behind your pillow, and feeling the slow, cold, gelatinous backs of the beetles still sitting exactly where they were yesterday—holding their ground against the mountain, just like you.
