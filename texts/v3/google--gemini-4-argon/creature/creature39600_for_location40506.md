If you stand on an upper terrace at dusk and watch a four-story tenement wall across the canal, you might see the wall breathe. Not the tiny puffs of rodent-steam leaking from the gaps, though those are there too, but a broader, slower shift—a section of the grey-white limestone courses sliding three inches to the left, a heavy lintel tilting up to let something wide and flat-backed pass beneath it, and then settling back into alignment with a muffled, gritty *thock*. 

The city is not abandoned. It is simply that its builders do not use the streets the way the water-voles do, nor the windows the way the wrens do. To an eye looking down from the rim, the city looks empty of people because the eye is looking for tiny vertical figures walking along the tops of the flagstones. 

Down in the flooded basements, where the cold black water sits ten feet deep over the sunken courtyard pavers, live the *Silt-Sleeved*. They are the primary architects of the lower courses, though to call them a single thing is to misunderstand how life works in the cold dark. A Silt-Sleeved begins as a frame—sometimes the ancient, waterlogged ribs of a drowned mountain goat, sometimes a clever lattice of bog-myrtle branches, and sometimes the dense, calcified bones of an older inhabitant that has finally stopped moving. Over this frame sits a colony of pale, cartilaginous tubes and heavy, translucent muscle, bound together by a gray, leathery mantle that looks remarkably like wet limestone. They are colonial, amphibious, and patient to a degree that borders on the geological. 

They do not have hands, yet they laid the foundations. When a building’s lower course begins to buckle under the weight of four stories of wet slate and alder-roots, three or four of the Silt-Sleeved will gather in the dark water of the cellar. They press their broad, muscular mantles against the slipping slab. Using hydraulic pressure—pumping the freezing aquifer water into their own interconnected bladders—they swell, wedging themselves into the structural gap. They will stay there for a week, or a winter, acting as living shims until they can maneuver a fresh piece of talus from the canal bed into the breach, sliding it home on a slick of secreted mucilage that hardens in the cold water like tough rubber. When you pole a rush-raft down the narrow slits between buildings, they are looking up at you through four feet of clear, dark water: wide, flat plates of pale grey flesh plastered against the submerged masonry, fringed with hundreds of tiny, dark eyes that track the shadow of your raft against the grey sky.

Above the waterline, the masonry belongs to the *Chink-Wives* and the *Rill-Wardens*, which are the closest things the basin has to landlords. 

If you pry away a six-inch pelt of emerald moss from a second-story wall and peer into the dry-stone matrix beneath, you will smell them before you see them: an odor like crushed fern-fronds, wet wool, and cold tallow. They are mammalian, or were once, before a few hundred generations in the dark, unmortared honeycomb of the city flattened their skulls and turned their collarbones into hinges. A Chink-Wife is the size of a large badger, though she can ribbon herself through a crack no wider than three fingers. Her pelt is a marvel of mimicry—not just in color, which is the exact blue-grey of fractured caprock, but in texture. Guard hairs grow in stiff, flat tufts that hold water and encourage the growth of tiny, pale lichens directly on her back. When she stops moving in a window-frame, she is simply another courses-stone with a bit of beard-moss hanging off the lip.

They are the ones who stuff the walls with peat. They have long, hairless, grey fingers with flat nails like mason’s trowels, constantly busy. A family of them—seven or eight individuals piled together for warmth in a chimney-less flue—will spend their days harvesting sphagnum from the roofs and jamming it into the drafty joints of their tower. They are fiercely territorial about acoustics. Noise in the basin draws the big predatory herons and the cliff-eagles from the rim; more importantly, vibrations travel through dry-stone masonry and shake the loose courses apart. If a shutter-stone begins to rattle in an updraft, or if a trickle of water starts to splash too loudly into a cistern, a Chink-Wife will emerge from the wall, her small, milky-filmed eyes blinking against the diffuse daylight, and she will silently, methodically wedge a tuft of moss or a flat shard of flint under the offending edge until the silence is restored.

They farm the voles and the blind crayfish, though "farm" is too grand a word. They rearrange the stones inside the walls to create dead-end runs and warm nesting pockets, encouraging the rodents to breed right next to the Chink-Wives' own sleeping dens, and then, when the frost bites deep, they simply reach through a gap in the bedroom wall and take one. 

And yet, neither the wet things in the cellars nor the flat things in the walls explain the city itself. They maintain it, they patch it, they grease its joints and stuff its cracks, but they did not quarry the talus into five-story tenements, and they do not weave the rush-rafts that sit tethered in the sedge-choked canals.

For that, you have to wait for the afternoon fog.

When the white wool rises from the fens and fills the stepped alleyways, the temperature drops another two degrees, and the stone-hoppers and wrens go quiet. That is when you hear the *clack-clack*—not the sharp, random clatter of a goat dislodging flint from the rim, but a slow, deliberate, two-beat cadence coming up the flooded stairs from the central marshes.

They have no name for themselves because they do not use their throats for speaking; the wet air ruins a voice in twenty years anyway, turning lungs into bogs. If an outsider saw one through the fog, they would think at first that a dwarf alder had uprooted itself from a parapet and was walking down the street, carrying a bundle of slate on its shoulders.

They are human, in the way that a cave-crayfish is still a lobster. Three hundred years in the pit—generations of inbreeding in a two-mile stone bowl with no sun, no fire, and water in their boots from birth to death—has pared them down and thickened them up. They are short, rarely over four feet tall, with barrel chests like deep-diving seals and legs bowed outward from a lifetime of bracing against slippery, pitched roofs and wading knee-deep in freezing peat-water. Their skin under their wrappings is the color of tallow, thick with a layer of subcutaneous fat that rounds off their jaws and swallows their cheekbones, leaving their dark eyes peering out from narrow, wind-proof slits. Their feet are bare—leather rots in a week down here, and wool stays wet forever—and the soles have grown three-quarters of an inch thick, horned and cleft like a goat's hoof, gray and utterly insensitive to the razor-sharp edges of freshly fractured limestone.

They wear the city. A coat is a woven mat of dead bog-myrtle and tough sedge-fibers, three inches thick, layered like thatch so the constant dripping from the lintels runs off their shoulders without touching their skin. Over this thatch, they encourage the rubbery liverworts and rust-colored mosses to grow, smearing their backs with canal-slime in the spring so that by autumn, a man standing still against a tenement wall is indistinguishable from a buttress.

They do not speak because sound is an enemy to dry-stone. Instead, they communicate with their fingernails—which are thick,ridged, and black with mineral deposits—against two small, flat pieces of dense flint they carry in their palms. *Tick-tock. Tick-tock-tick.* The sound is tiny, dry, and carries only twenty feet before the wet moss swallows it, just enough to tell a cousin on the roof above to lower the rush-rope, or to warn a sister in the canal below that a loose cornice is about to drop.

Their entire existence is a slow, obsessive war against gravity and ice. 

Because there is no mortar, the city is fluid. The water seeping down from the plains gets into the cracks between the stones; when the night temperature dips below freezing, the water expands into ice, jacking the heavy limestone slabs apart by a fraction of an inch. Left alone for fifty years, the five-story towers would simply belly out and roar down into the canals in a catastrophic avalanche of rubble, damming the water and drowning the whole basin.

So the people are constantly taking the city down and putting it back up again.

In the fog, you will see four of them on a fourth-story roof, moving with a slow, rehearsed grace that requires no words. They use no iron levers—only long, fire-hardened poles of waterlogged bog-alder, dense as lead, which they haul up from the sunken plaza pools where their grandfathers sank them to cure a century ago. Together, they lean their heavy, moss-pelted bodies against the poles, prying up a three-hundred-pound capstone that the frost has tilted out of true. While they hold the weight, a Chink-Wife—long accustomed to the partnership—darts her grey, trowel-fingered hands into the gap, scraping out the rotten ice and dead fern-roots, before the men ease the stone back down into its new, level bed with a muffled *thump*. 

They eat cold things. They pull the blind white crayfish from the lower windows; they gather the thousands of mottled newts in woven willow-traps; they harvest the sour, starchy tubers of the water-sedges and mash them into grey cakes that they dry under their own armpits, using their body heat since there is no sun and no fire. They drink the water that beads on the undersides of the lintels, licking it directly off the stone like the mountain goats, because the water in the canals is full of peat-tannin and newt-spawn.

When one of them dies—usually simply slowing down on a cold afternoon until they sit on a stepped alleyway and do not get back up—there is no burial, because there is no dirt. Instead, two cousins will come with a bound-rush raft. They will strip the dead man’s moss-coat off and lay it over a drafty roof where the thatch is thin. Then they will pole the heavy, pale body out to one of the flooded plazas in the center of the city, weight the ankles with two flat hearth-stones that have never known fire, and slide him over the side into the clear, black water.

Down he goes, past the submerged flagstones, through the drowned doorways of the cellar levels, into the dark where the Silt-Sleeved wait. Within a year, the flesh is gone into the bellies of the crayfish, and the heavy, calcified bones have been dragged into a foundation wall, wedged into a gap between two slipping limestone blocks, wrapped in a grey, muscular mantle of living cartilage, still holding up the house.
