At first, you would mistake them for the dredging machinery itself—another rusted winch or a slumped, grease-caked gearbox half-swallowed by the weeping mutton-fat stone. 

Wait long enough by one of the sodium-vapor lights, however, and a bulbous mass of yellow travertine at the base of a pillar will shudder. With a sharp, flat report—like a green branch snapping, though barely audible under the turbine-roar of the perimeter falls—a seam of calcified crust splits open. A gray-white limb, thick as a shovel handle and covered in a paste of wet calcium powder, draws itself out of the rock. 

They are called the *Tappers*, though there is no one left down here to use the word, and they do not have a word for themselves, because they have no voices. In an acoustic pressure-cooker where a shout is erased three inches from the mouth, language is dead weight. Their ears sealed over generations ago, reduced to dimpled scars of gristle behind their jawbones. Instead, they read the valley through the broad, calloused pads of their feet and the heavy, spade-like nails of their hands, feeling the low-frequency thrum of the ceiling faults and the sixty-cycle hum of the buried power grid through the stone.

Originally, they may have been the maintenance crews left behind when the company abandoned the dredging rigs—or perhaps they were down here before the iron was ever lowered through the fissures, and simply adopted the wreckage. Whatever they were, the basin has remade them in its own heavy, sluggish image. They are squat, barrel-chested, and monumentally dense. Because the air is a suffocating soup of carbonate mist and sulfur, their lungs have grown vast and slow, cycling once every two or three minutes with a deep, bellows-like heave of their ribs. Their skin is pale and thick as boiled pigskin, draped in heavy, tallowy folds at the joints that constantly slough off in flaky, mineral-caked sheets. This perpetual shedding is the only thing that keeps them alive; if a Tapper stops moving for more than forty-eight hours, the syrupy water sheets over their skin, the minerals precipitate into their pores, and the valley claims them. You can see their ancestors everywhere, if you know how to look: smooth, vaguely crouching lumps of bone-colored flowstone on the rims of the dams, their knees drawn up to their chins, now three feet thicker than any living creature could be.

Because the cavern is filling itself in, the Tappers live a life of relentless, sideways constriction. A hallway that a parent walked through upright must be crawled through by the child, and will be a solid wall of wet, copper-veined cauliflower rock before the grandchild is grown. Consequently, their joints have become wonderfully, terribly loose. Their collarbones are unmoored, floating in heavy muscle, allowing them to collapse their broad shoulders inward until their shoulder blades touch, greasing themselves with brown bacterial slime to slide sideways through six-inch gaps between the weeping pillars.

They move at the speed of an hour hand. Fast movement in eighty-five-degree broth leads to heatstroke, and there is nothing down here that requires a chase. When a Tapper feeds, it simply sits hip-deep in a warm, calcified pan, lets its heavy arms sink into the silt, and waits. Within minutes, the blind, hand-sized isopods and pale crayfish clack directly over its knuckles. A thick hand closes—slowly, hydraulically—and crushes the shell into a paste, which is lifted to a wide, lipless mouth lined with flat, molar-like plates built for grinding chitin and snail-shell alike. The calcium from the shells goes straight back into their own dense, overly heavy bones.

Yet they are not mere animals. They have an obsession, a singular, inherited drive that binds them to the rotting industrial bones of the valley: they are the keepers of the orange light.

The Tappers cannot see well—their eyes are small, sunken, and filmed over with a milky nictitating membrane that protects them from the alkaline drip—but they crave the concentrated heat of the sodium-vapor bulbs, and they love the bitter, tangy salads of translucent moss and leggy fern that only grow within the twenty-foot halos of the cages. More than that, the hum of the live cables is their compass. In a landscape where every pillar looks like every other pillar, and where landmarks are erased by new stone every five years, the buried electrical grid is the only permanent map.

So they tend it. 

Every Tapper carries a piece of the old operation. Usually, it is a three-foot length of hexagonal iron pry-bar, a heavy brass wrench, or a rusted scaling hammer, the metal handle long since fused to their palm by a combination of callous and mineral buildup, only broken free with a ritual twist every morning. They spend their waking hours shuffling along the heavy, insulated power cables that drape from column to column. Where the stone is creeping over a cable, threatening to pinch the rubber insulation or drag it down under five tons of wet travertine, a Tapper will plant its wide feet on the rim of a dam and begin to strike.

*Clink. Clink. Clink.* 

They do not swing with force; they swing with the tireless, pendulous regularity of a metronome, using the sheer weight of their iron-dense arms to chip away the embryonic stone before it can fully harden. They scrape the bulbous concretions off the iron junction boxes. They meticulously peel the wet, calcified crusts away from the glass globes of the floodlights, their thick fingers blistering against the hot glass so the bruised-orange glow can continue to bleed into the mist. 

When a bulb finally dies—its filament snapping after decades of under-volted burning—a small group of them will gather in the newly fallen dark beneath the dead fixture. They will stand together in the warm, cascading water for hours, their shoulders pressed together, feeling the absence of the sixty-cycle buzz in the iron post. Then, slowly, they will turn away, squeezing one by one through the narrowing gaps between the bloated stalagmites, following the heavy black cable through the roaring gloom toward the next orange halo, leaving the dead tower to be swallowed by the stone.
