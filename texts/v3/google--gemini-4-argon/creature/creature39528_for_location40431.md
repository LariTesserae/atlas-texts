If you watch the pale carpets of the knuckle-crabs for long enough, you will notice that they do not only part for the four-second blasts of the steam vents. Every so often, a wide, circular lane opens across the olive-green velvet, cleared three seconds before a heavy, slow-moving shadow passes over it. 

The crabs are reacting to a specific, low-frequency tremor in the floor—a deliberate, rolling gait that hits the wooden basin in the exact fractional silence between the *thud* of the third pump-node and the intake-gurgle of the fourth.

The creature that casts the shadow is called a **Sump-Warden**, though there is no one here to name it, and there are only two of them in the entire chamber. 

At first glance, a Warden looks like a boulder that has been tumbled in a river until it lost all its edges, then left in a greenhouse until it grew a pelt. It is roughly the size and shape of a crouching bear, though its anatomy is radial and ambiguously jointed, closer to a massive, terrestrial cuttlefish encased in the heavy, segmented plates of a chiton. Its shell is not made of chitin or bone, but of dense, mineralized wood-scale—layered accretions of the very same calcium and dissolved silica that the water leaves behind, laid down over decades until the animal’s back resembles dark, oiled walnut.

A Warden has no eyes. In a room where the light never changes and the walls never move, vision is an expensive vanity. Instead, its entire underside is a broad, muscular foot—pale, rubbery, and intensely innervated— flanked by six stubby, knuckle-padded limbs that it uses not so much for walking as for levering its immense bulk up the slick curves of the walls. Through this foot, the Warden reads the hollow with total clarity. The chamber is an acoustic drum, and the Warden’s nervous system is an oscilloscope. It knows where every crab-carpet is thickest by the faint, high-frequency scratch of twenty thousand tiny claws on moss. It knows the exact viscosity of the water in the overhead pipes by the pitch of the wooden valves slamming shut. It knows where the other Warden is at all times, even on the opposite side of the village-sized basin, by the tiny dampened dead-spot its ten-hundred-pound body creates in the floor’s resonance.

They are the uncommitted plumbers of this closed loop. While thegreat plant has achieved a near-perfect mechanical equilibrium, no system that pumps mineral-heavy water can entirely avoid calcification. Left entirely to itself, the root-sieve drains would eventually crust over with lime, and the steam-vents on the furnace-nodes would narrow until the pressure split the iron-hard bark.

The Wardens eat the blockage. 

When a Warden approaches one of the seven furnace-nodes, it waits for the vent-hiss to finish, then heaves its front half up against the slick, trembling tuber. Beneath its front mantle, where a head should be, is a circular collar of thick, grey muscular lips surrounding a rasping organ—a ribbon of serrated, iron-tipped teeth like the radula of a marine snail, but the size of a carpenter’s plane. Pressing its mouth-collar over the scalding rim of a steam-vent, the Warden seals the gasket with its own heavy flesh and scrapes. The sound is terrible—a harsh, metallic screech of iron teeth grinding away mineral scale, briefly cutting through the rhythmic *thud-hiss-gurgle* of the room. If the node fires while the Warden is working, the animal does not flinch; its mantle expands like a leather bellows, absorbing the jet of hot vapor into internal gill-chambers where it strips the steam of heat and suspended nutrients before exhaling a lazy, lukewarm drool down its flanks.

They do not compete, the two Wardens. Every few days, their grazing paths cross near the shallow, tepid pools at the center of the floor. When they meet, they do not fight or mate; they simply press their broad, blind flanks together for an hour or two, matching the slowperistaltic contractions of their mantles until their internal rhythms are synchronized again, sharing the chemical taste of the room's water through their skin, before peeling apart with a wet, heavy *schluck* to resume their rounds.

***

Because the Wardens are so heavy and move with such slow, industrial certainty, they have become a habitat in their own right. 

Riding in the deep, warm creases between a Warden’s walnut-like shell plates are the **Valve-Leeches**—though they are not worms, and they draw no blood. They are long, ribbon-flat creatures, the color of raw ginger, related to the suction-bellied frogs that slide down the wall-chutes, though the leeches have given up legs entirely. They are pure muscle and sensory nerve. 

They use the Wardens as elevators to reach the high, ribbed vault where the water-veins thin into translucent membranes. Once a Warden levers itself high up a curved wall to scrape the intake-joints of an overhead pipe, two or three ginger ribbons will slip from its shell and slap onto the pulsing wooden conduit.

The Valve-Leeches are heat-thieves and rhythm-parasites. They wrap themselves tightly around the thickest arteries of the ceiling, right over the internal wooden check-valves that clatter shut every four seconds. As the pipe expands with a surge of hot, mineral-rich water, the leech’s flat body is stretched tight; when the valve slams shut and the pipe contracts, the leech’s elastic skin snaps back. This constant, passive flexing pumps the leech’s own blood—it has no heart of its own, having outsourced the labor of its circulation to the plumbing of the plant. 

In return for this free heartbeat, the leeches perform a microscopic maintenance that the clumsy Wardens cannot. Their undersides secrete a slick, acidic mucus that seeps into the tiny hair-line fractures of the high conduits, dissolving the brittle crystals of salt that would otherwise stiffen the wood, keeping the great vascular struts supple and translucent so the flat, noon-like light can continue to filter through.

***

And then there are the **Tines**. 

If you look into the shallow, tepid pools at the very bottom of the basin, just above the root-sieve drains, you will see what looks like beds of pale, bone-white water-reeds swaying in the current. They are not plants. If a copper-shelled beetle loses its footing on the ceiling and drops into the pool, the reeds do not wait for it to drift into them; they lean *against* the current, converging on the ripple with the stiff, unified jerk of marionettes.

A Tine is a colonial organism, a stacked tower of tiny, calcified zooids that builds a hollow spire of calcium carbonate directly onto the bars of the drain-sieves. They look like three-foot-tall tuning forks made of wet ivory. 

In this deafening room, the Tines have found a way to eat the noise itself. 

Their spires are grown to a very specific thickness and height, tuned precisely to the acoustic resonance of the seven pump-nodes. As the booming *thuds* echo through the dense, waterlogged wood of the basin, the Tine-spires vibrate in sympathy. They hum—a high, continuous, glassy whine that you can only hear if you put your ear inches from the water. 

This constant, high-frequency vibration creates a localized acoustic trap in the water around the spire. Tiny particles of organic detritus—shed crab-gill, frog-mucus, the fine silt ground loose by the Wardens’ iron teeth—are caught in the microscopic standing waves radiating from the vibrating ivory forks. Instead of washing straight down the drain, the nutrient-slurry is shaken into neat, suspended clouds that hang inches from the Tine’s surface, where thousands of microscopic, hair-like mouths lick the water clean before letting it pass through the sieve.

When a Sump-Warden lumbers into the central pools to clean the drains, it is remarkably careful with the Tines. It will slide its broad, rubbery foot between the ivory tuning-forks with the delicacy of a hand moving through wine glasses, rasping the foul, clogged mats of dead moss from the wooden grates beneath while leaving the singing spires untouched. If a spire has grown too thick with age, losing its pitch and failing to vibrate in tune with the room, the Warden’s radula will dart out and nip a single, precise notch into the base of the ivory fork—*tink*—shaving away just enough mass to bring the colony back into resonance with the great engines before moving on through the warm, shadowless rain.
