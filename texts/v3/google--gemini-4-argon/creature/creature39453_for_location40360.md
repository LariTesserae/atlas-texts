If you stand inside one of the porcelain domes, in that dead, dry silence where your ears pop and the sweat instantly chills on your skin, and you look out through the torn membrane for long enough, you will eventually realize that the terrace thirty yards away is looking back at you.

At first, it is only a disturbance in the way the water moves. The lukewarm sheet-flow, instead of sluicing evenly over the black basalt, parts around an obstacle that has the exact color and wet-leather sheen of the rock. Then a curtain of boiler-moss dumps its ten gallons from the canopy above, and instead of splashing against stone, the water hits something yielding that shudders with a deep, subcutaneous ripple.

They are called the *Silt-Deacons* by the three people who have ever seen them on long-range surveyor drones, though the name makes them sound stiffer and more devout than they are. In truth, they are simply what became of the maintenance crew after four thousand generations of living in an over-pressurized soup where the machines did all the work and the air itself wanted to be chewed.

To survive in an atmosphere this thick and hyper-oxygenated, a body has to give up on the idea of being a sealed vessel. If a baseline human stayed out on the terraces for a week, the oxygen toxicity would burn their alveoli to scar tissue and the relentless mineral damp would rot the skin off their shins. The Deacons solved this by surrendering their borders. 

When one rises from a pool—a process that takes twenty minutes of slow, hydraulic uncoiling—it stands roughly seven feet tall, though it has no spine to speak of. Instead, it is built on the same principle as the oil-drum trees: a bundle of tough, fibrous muscle-columns inflated by immense internal blood pressure. They are terribly heavy, four or five hundred pounds of dense, waterlogged meat, shaped vaguely like a broad-shouldered human who has been left in a warm wax bath until all the sharp edges ran together. Their skin is two inches thick, grey-violet, and porous as river-tufa. It does not keep the valley out; it lets the valley through. Warm rock-sweat enters through broad, gill-like vents under their heavy ribs, circulates through a labyrinth of internal cooling-sacs to keep them from cooking in their own metabolic heat, and drains out through valved slits behind their knees and heels. 

They do not breathe so much as they steep. Their lungs have long since migrated outward, becoming thick, plum-colored ruffles of vascular tissue that drape over their collarbones and down their backs like heavy liturgical stoles. In the yellow-white glare, these ruffles pulse once every two or three minutes, lazily skimming all the oxygen they need from the broth of the air.

Because they have no bones to give their faces structure, their heads are wide, soft-featured domes that slope directly into their chests without the interruption of a neck. They have four eyes—a mutation borrowed or spliced from the six-legged birds epochs ago—set in a gentle crescent across the brow, each protected by a thick, yellowish nictitating membrane as tough as a watch-crystal. Beneath the eyes, there is no nose, only a wide, lipless mouth that stays slightly open, allowing the metallic snails to crawl in and out, scraping the bacterial film from their flat, fused plates of teeth. 

A Deacon does not walk. Walking is for dry worlds with crisp gravity. Here, they move with a slow, ponderous skating motion, riding on a thick cushion of heavy mucus secreted by their wide, saucer-like feet. They slide down the basalt steps with the stately momentum of harbor barges, using two tremendously long, three-jointed arms to punt themselves off the hydraulic tree-trunks or to hook onto the rigid, parabolic vines near the black needles. 

If you watch them for a day, they appear to be mere herd-beasts, grazing alongside the hound-sized drifters. They pluck the floating, golf-ball globules of water out of the air and press them directly into the thirsty pores of their chests. They use their heavy, spatulate fingers to peel sheets of algae from the rock, or they simply lie stacked with the flat-backed amphibians in the coriander-scented bogs, letting the geothermal water run through their vents while the six-legged birds walk across their faces.

Wait three days, however, and you will see the maintenance routines kick in.

The instinct is buried deeper than language—it sits in the basal ganglia, right next to the urge to mate and the urge to turn toward the warmth. A Deacon will be floating half-submerged in a terrace pool when one of the black needles two hundred yards upslope suffers a microscopic fluctuation in its local field. No instrument could catch it, and to an outsider, the copper-and-burnt-sugar smell of the air hasn't changed at all. Yet the Deacon’s gill-ruffles will flare a sudden, bruised crimson. 

Slowly, over the course of an afternoon, three or four of them will converge on the needle. As they enter the fifty-yard distortion zone, where gravity goes sideways and the rain falls in spirals, their bodies change. Their internal hydraulics shift; they pump dense, iron-rich lymph into their lower extremities to anchor themselves, while simultaneously inflating gas-bladders along their flanks—identical to those of the flying hexapods—to keep their upper bodies upright in the warped tension lines. 

They gather around the matte-black monument and press their broad, wet bellies directly against the post-physical material.

The needles have no seams and no controls, because they were never meant to be operated with wrenches or keyboards. They were built to interface with the biological signatures of their makers. The Deacons are the keys, swollen and gone feral, yet still carrying the correct chemical passwords in their slime. For hours, they hug the black pillar, secreting a thick, glowing paste of heavy metals—harvested from the shells of the millions of tiny snails they digest—mixed with specialized neuro-enzymes. This paste sinks into the unreflective black surface as if the monument were thirsty for it. While they work, the Deacons hum. They do not use throats; they vibrate their internal cooling-sacs, producing a low, cello-like drone that harmonizes with the turbine-thrum of the mountain’s water-fall, cancelling out the local acoustic pressure until a twenty-foot bubble of dead calm forms around the needle.

Within that bubble, the non-Euclidean geometry corrects itself for a few minutes. The horizontal vines droop; the floating water-globules splash to the mud; the air loses its copper tang and smells briefly, shockingly, of high-altitude ozone and cold snow.

Then the calibration is finished. The Deacons release their hold, slumping back into the mud with a wet, rubbery *slap*, and the heavy, yellow heat rushes back in to fill the gap.

Only one thing in the valley truly unsettles them, and that is the white porcelain domes. 

The Deacons remember the domes the way an oyster remembers the grain of sand that hurt it. Those dry, silent interiors are lethal to them; if a Deacon were to slide across the threshold into that zero-humidity dust, its vascular ruffles would parchment and crack in ten minutes, its hydraulic pressure would fail without the counter-push of the thick air, and it would collapse into a suffocating heap of blubber under its own immense weight. 

Yet they cannot leave the domes alone.

Every few weeks, an older Deacon—one whose skin has grown so thick with calcified mineral deposits and symbiotic fern-roots that it can barely bend its knees—will skate slowly up to one of the torn membranes. It will stop two inches from the invisible boundary where the roaring wet meets the dead dry. It will not reach inside. Instead, it will squat there for forty-eight hours in the pouring, lukewarm splash of the boiler-moss, staring into the dust-motes of the empty room with its four yellow-shielded eyes.

Sometimes, it will bring things and leave them right at the line of the threshold, arranged in neat, geometric rows that mimic the non-Euclidean curves of the vines: the iridescent shells of the largest snails, a twist of violet frond, or the skull of an eel bleached white by digestive acids. 

It isn't worship. It is more like a creature looking into an old, dried-out chrysalis and trying, with a brain that has become as slow and warm and plant-like as the rest of the mountain, to remember why it ever wanted to be that small, or that quiet, or that cold. After a day or two, a hound-drifter will *whuff* past its head, honking like a brass tuba in a swimming pool, and the old Deacon will blink all four eyes in slow sequence, turn its massive, dripping bulk back toward the steaming terraces, and slide back down into the soup.
