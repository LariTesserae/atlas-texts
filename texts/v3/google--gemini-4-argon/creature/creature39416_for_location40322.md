If you sit on one of the pale-green root knuckles for long enough—letting the wet, heavy heat soak into your clothes until you are the exact temperature of the air—you will eventually realize you are looking directly at one of the builders. 

You did not miss it because it was hiding. You missed it because your eyes are trained to look for life by looking for disturbance, and nothing here disturbs anything else. 

Thirty feet away, wedged into the fork of a fluted, silica-armored stalk, is a shape you took for a particularly large, resinous gall, or a cluster of overlapping tubers. Then, a bead of warm water rolls down a glass-vine above it, drops, and strikes the thing directly between its shoulders. The water does not splash; it beads and rolls down a back that is just as waxy and non-porous as the paddle-leaves. Two minutes after the impact—as if the thought had to travel through cold molasses to reach its limbs—a hand rises. 

It is a long, four-fingered hand, the joints thick and knobby like the ginger-root mattresses below, the skin a translucent, milky amber that lets the shadowless glare pass partway through the webbing between the digits. With agonizing, hydraulic slowness, one blunt finger intercepts the rolling bead of water, guides it to a lipless, horizontal slit of a mouth, and presses it inside. Then the arm takes four minutes to fold itself back into the exact geometric configuration it held before.

There are perhaps sixty of them in the entire three-mile basin. They have no name for themselves because they have no use for sound; in an acoustic environment where a falling drop of water sounds like a gunshot, vocalization would be an unbearable violence. 

They are vaguely hominid in their proportions, though foreshortened and dense, built for counterbalance rather than stride. Their skin is heavily infused with the same bio-silica that stiffens the canopy, giving their flanks and shoulders a faint, pebbled glitter in the yellow-white halos of the vines. On the soles of their broad feet and the palms of their hands, the skin thickens into dark, vulcanized pads—tough enough to step directly onto the razor-flutes of the black basalt without leaving a smear of blood, and tacky enough to adhere to vertical glass-stems without effort. 

To live in a place without decay is to live in a place without urgency. On the surface, creatures move fast because they are burning: their cells are sloughing off, their heat is escaping into the wind, their food is rotting on the ground, and their predators are starving. Down here, the metabolism of the builders is dialed down to a geological crawl. 

Their hearts beat once every four or five minutes—a slow, heavy contraction that pushes a thick, clear, maple-syrup-like hemolymph through their limbs. They do not breathe in a rhythm. Along their ribs are six vertical spiracles lined with pale, waxy baffles. These baffles stay shut for half an hour at a time to preserve moisture, opening with a faint, dry *click* to exchange gasses with the baking, pine-scented air, then sealing tight again. 

They do not eat the plants. To tear a leaf or break a root in this locked equilibrium would be to create an open wound that never rots away, an eternal scar on the architecture of their world. Instead, they are nectar-grazers and sap-tappers. When a turgid seed-pod pops in the heat to equalize its pressure, a builder within a quarter-mile will register the sharp transient wave through the stiff membranes of its knees. Over the next two hours, it will unspool itself from its perch and move toward the source. 

Their movement is extraordinary to watch. They never brush against a leaf if they can avoid it. They do not swing or leap; they shift their weight with the slow, deliberate majesty of a sundial’s shadow, placing one vulcanized foot precisely between two beads of mercury-like water without breaking the surface tension of either. When they reach the popped pod, they use a single, obsidian-hard thumbnail—grown thick and curved specifically for this—to scrape up the excess, sugar-heavy gum that has oozed from the seam. A thumb-sized dollop of this baked resin, combined with the warm condensation they lick from the leaf-cups, is enough to fuel one of them for a month.

This explains the huts, and the trails, and the strange, pristine emptiness of both.

The structures are not houses. The builders have no need for shelter from a sky that never rains, in a basin where the temperature never fluctuates by more than half a degree, safe from predators that do not exist. And the structures were not all built by the sixty individuals currently breathing in the basin—or rather, they *were* built by them, over the course of the last four thousand years.

Because nothing rots and nothing wears away, the builders themselves are functionally immortal, trapped in the same hot, bright stasis as the flora. They do not age in any way a surface creature would recognize; they simply accumulate. Over centuries, the silica in their diet slowly precipitates into their joints and dermal layers. 

The younger ones—those only a few hundred years old—are the pale amber ones, still relatively supple, taking only a day to cross a hundred yards of root-mat. It is they who chip the faint trail-marks into the bark and the basalt. The trails are not for navigation; every inhabitant of the basin knows every leaf-cup and basalt ripple by heart. The marks are records of thought. A builder will pause on a root-buttress, struck by a slow, three-year contemplation of the way two glass-vines refract a particular halo of light, and will score three neat, pale lines into the waxy bark to say: *Look here. The angles are good.* 

Because the wood never oxidizes and the bark never heals over, a mark made five hundred years ago looks identical to one made this morning. To a builder, the entire basin is a dense, blindingly bright library of spatial geometry, every score mark a sentence that is still being spoken.

The huts are the final punctuation of those sentences. 

As a builder reaches its second or third millennium, the silica buildup in its tissues begins to win out against the slow pump of its heart. Their amber skin turns milky, then opaque, taking on the fluted, glittering quality of the stalks around them. Their movements slow from hours to days, then from days to weeks. 

When an elder feels its joints beginning to lock into their final, crystalline permanence, it begins to build. 

It will spend eighty years selecting jagged, glass-sharp slabs of basalt from the floor below the roots, lifting them one by one with hands that no longer feel pain, stacking them without mortar into a small, perfectly balanced dome. Or it will spend a century in the mid-canopy, coaxing living, silica-stiffened branches into a hollow teardrop, tying the knots and holding them in place with its own hands for five years until the wood accepts the tension and locks. 

They do not use tools because time is their tool; if you can hold a branch in place for sixty months without getting tired, you do not need a clamp. If you can spend a week finding the exact center of gravity between two jagged volcanic rocks, you do not need mortar.

And yet, the huts are empty. 

They are empty because a hut is not a tomb; it is a frame. Once the structure is finished—once the stone is balanced or the branches are woven to perfection—the elder steps *out* of it. It climbs thirty or forty feet away, finds a fork in a giant column-stalk or a cleft in the black rock that offers a clear line of sight back to the empty doorway of the room it just made, and settles into the niche. 

It folds its limbs into the geometry of the plant. It drinks one last bead of warm water. And then, over the course of fifty years, it simply stops moving altogether. 

Its skin finishes hardening into biogenic glass. The pale-green root-buttresses slowly, imperceptibly swell around its ankles and hips, locking it into the mattress of the floor. The stiff, translucent vines drape over its shoulders, refracting the overhead glare through its milky, unblinking eyes. It does not rot. It does not slump into a skeleton. It remains whole, pristine, and immaculate, looking forever at the empty room it built—a room whose entire purpose is to hold a specific, carved-out volume of bright, hot, silent air.

If you look around you now, really look, past the copper beetle ticking across the vine and the pale lizard pulsing its throat in the shadows of the stone, you will see that the basin is much more crowded than you thought. 

That swollen, glittering burl at the base of the column ten feet to your left has five fingers, locked around the root it gripped before the surface world had empires. That pale, translucent arch in the canopy overhead is not a vine, but the spine and outstretched arms of an elder who decided, two thousand years ago, that the space between two paddle-leaves needed a bridge for the water-drops to cross. 

They are all still here. Every single one of them that ever lived in the basin is still in the basin, watching the light, holding up the branches, and waiting for the next drop of water to fall and break the quiet.
