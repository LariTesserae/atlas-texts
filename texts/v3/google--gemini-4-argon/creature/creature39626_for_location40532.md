If you stand on the middle tier of the cast-iron decking for forty minutes—two full breaths of the basin—your eyes eventually adjust to the amber soup of the light, and your ears learn to subtract the velvet roar of the steam. That is when you realize that the fat, creamy columns of lime and tallow bridging the cranes to the walkways are not all in the same places they were when you arrived.

One of them, six feet thick and ribbed like a melted candle, is four inches further down the railing than it was during the last high tide.

Nothing with blood or nerves can live in the Sump-Ward, because blood requires oxygen and nerves are shattered by the sub-audible battering of the western turbine. What lives here instead has no nerves to fray and no lungs to choke. It is a slow, mineral-fat pseudo-life, an accidental grafting of industrial chemistry onto the blind, creeping logic of a slime mold. It was born perhaps ninety years ago in the dark sump-pits beneath the bronze grates, where rendering-tallow from the old municipal lubrication lines mixed with copper-heavy silt, iron-reducing bacteria, and the rotting, acidic mulch of the pewter ferns. 

They look, at first, like the drippings of the place itself: mounds of grey-white cylinder grease, amber slicks of neat’s-foot oil, and crusts of lime scale that have sloughed off the boilers and coalesced on the perforated iron floors. 

In the ward’s eternal seventy-five-degree fever, these masses do not melt and they do not harden. They remain in a state of dense, buttery semi-solidity, like cold lard or modeling clay. Left to themselves in a dead room, they would simply sit. But the Sump-Ward is not dead; it is an immense transducer of kinetic and electrical energy. The low-frequency hum of the turbine vibrates the iron decking at nineteen cycles per second, and the dissolving brass grates and cast-iron piers sit in an electrolyte bath of acidic water, turning the entire basin into a vast, weak galvanic cell.

Held together by surface tension and laced internally with conductive filaments of pure, precipitated copper and iron oxide, the grease-masses respond to the current. They are piezoelectric and chemotactic. They have no brains, only a complex, shifting lattice of metallic salts suspended in fat that stiffens when a current passes through it and relaxes when the current drops. 

When the water rises on its twenty-minute cycle, submerging the lower tiers, the electrical potential between the bronze grates and the iron piers shifts. A tiny milli-voltage ripples through the warm water. In response, a thirty-pound mound of tallow-and-scale clinging to a submerged pier contracts along its metallic grain, pulls its trailing edge up out of the silt, and hauls itself three inches higher up the basalt wall.

This is the basis of the Sump-Ward’s ecology: a blind, silent, peristaltic husbandry.

The smallest of these things are no bigger than tea-saucers. They are flat, translucent blisters of paraffin and mineral oil that skate across the surface of the black water, driven not by wind, but by the subtle thermal gradients where hot condensate drips from overhead pipes. Inside their clear, oily skins, you can see concentric rings of green copper-carbonate and red rust, arranged like the agate lines in a marble. They drift until they bump against one of the copper buoys or a weeping gasket, where they cling, suck up a microscopic meal of fresh machine-oil or iron-eating moss, and then split in two with the slow, silent deliberation of a lava lamp.

The larger ones—the Tallow-Drifts—are the size of butcher’s blocks or drowned sheep. They live on the perforated walkways and the warm flanks of the inactive boilers. Over decades, they have absorbed so much lime-drip and soot that their hides have toughened into a leathery, tallowy rind the color of old bone or wet cement. They move like glaciers made of soap. A Drift will spend three days pouring itself over the lip of an iron girder, hanging down in a great, trembling teardrop five feet long until its tip touches the walkway below; then, over the next week, the upper half drains down into the lower half through the narrow neck of itself, until the whole mass has relocated to the lower tier without ever making a sound.

They graze on the orange, rust-eating bryophytes. When a Drift moves over the flank of a boiler, it smothers the glowing orange moss, digesting the plant’s iron-rich cell walls in an alkaline bath of saponified fat and lime, leaving behind a clean, startlingly bright track of bare, silvery-grey metal that immediately begins to rust anew in the wet air, seeding the next crop of moss.

Because they have no eyes, they navigate by heat and vibration. They congregate thickest around the western retaining wall, where the turbine’s hum is strongest, piling up in the corners like drifts of dirty snow, pulsing faintly in time with the machinery. 

And over the decades, the largest and oldest of them have begun to do something that looks terribly like work.

In the center of the court, where the eight-foot brass valve-wheel turns its single degree every hour, a massive, compound column of this living tallow has wrapped itself around the valve housing and the adjacent stone pier. It weighs perhaps two tons. Its exterior is petrified into a brittle crust of calcium carbonate and black silt, indistinguishable from the masonry, while its core remains warm, soft, and threaded with thick, braided veins of pure copper which it has refined out of the lock water over a century. 

It has grown into the gap where an ancient packing-gland blew out forty years ago. When the high-pressure steam hisses through the breach, the heat melts the creature’s inner fat just enough to make it flow into the gap; the cold lime in its skin then reacts with the minerals in the water to form a greasy cement, plugging the leak until the pressure builds enough to blow a small hole through it again. It is not trying to repair the machine; it is simply feeding on the thermal energy of the steam-jet, positioning its body to catch the heat the way a plant turns its leaves to the sun. 

Yet the result is that the Sump-Ward is kept in balance by its inhabitants.

Smaller, slug-like ribbons of gelatinous grease, striped with yellow sulfur, crawl eternally up and down the chains of the carriage-house counterweight in the corner shaft. They are drawn to the friction-heat of the links. As the great weight descends, groaning its metallic whale-song, it crushes dozens of them between the iron links—and in crushing them, smears their tallowy bodies across the metal, greasing the chain so it does not rust solid and snap. Twenty minutes later, the crushed ribbons simply flow back together along the warm iron, re-forming their sulfur-striped bodies out of the smear, and resume their slow crawl upward.

Another colony, dark red with ferric oxide and dense as vulcanized rubber, lives inside the teeth of the idle gears overhead. Every few months, when the accumulation of pale, water-filled bladder-vines grows so heavy on the upper girders that the ironwork begins to groan and bend under the tonnages of trapped water, the strain alters the acoustic resonance of the metal. The red colonies feel the pitch of the girder change. Drawn to the stress-fractures where the metal is warmest at a molecular level, they creep out along the undersides of the beams and engulf the roots of the vines. Their alkaline bodies burn through the plant tissues in a matter of days, severing the holdfasts. 

A hundred-pound bladder-vine breaks loose, plummets eighty feet through the pewter twilight, and explodes against the cast-iron decking with a sound like a dropped mattress, spilling warm water and shredded greenery across the grates.

Within an hour, the Tallow-Drifts have changed direction. Drawn by the chemical plume of ruptured plant-sugars in the rising tide, five or six of the pale, glistening mounds converge on the wreckage. They flow over the rubbery pewter ferns and the translucent skins of the vines, sealing them under a dome of translucent grey fat. There, insulated from the air, the plants are dissolved over weeks into a black, oily hydrocarbon sludge, which the Drifts absorb to replenish their own bulk.

Nothing here knows it is alive. Nothing here knows there is a city eighty feet overhead, or a sun above the city. There is only the twenty-minute inhalation of the black water, the twenty-minute exhalation through the bronze grates, the endless, velvet roar of the steam, and the slow, blind shifting of the grease-mounts as they creep from pipe to pier, keeping the iron greased, the leaks plugged, and the dead ferns cleared away, dreaming a slow, galvanic dream in the dark.
