If you stay by the condenser trap until the afternoon heat begins to slacken, the horizon will eventually detach itself and walk toward you. 

At first, they look like a low, shimmering mirage of the bark-plate itself, or a slow-moving drift of shed stridulator shells blown before the wind. There are never more than eight or ten of them together—a single *cord*, as they call a family unit. Any more than that, and the combined rhythm of their footsteps would draw a herd of the blind, rolling grazers from three valleys over. 

To live on a floor that listens, you must walk like the wind. 

They move in a strange, skating shuffle, their feet bound in thick, curved sled-soles made of spongy dead-pith belted with greased leather. They do not step; they glide, shifting their weight in a long, syncopated rhythm that mimics the irregular gusting of the plateau breeze. They are lean, stringy people, baked by the high-altitude sun and the rebounding glare of the wood into the color of cured resin. Even the youths have the faces of sixty-year-old sailors, their eyes permanently squinted behind slit-goggles carved from the knuckles of sky-gliders, the bone smeared around the inside with dark sap-soot to kill the blinding upward reflection of the world.

When they reach the shelter, they do not speak. No one speaks in the open on the bark-plate. To use your voice against the jet-engine roar of ten million stridulators and the sub-bass flute of the vents is to tear your throat to ribbons in a week. 

Instead, a boy at the front of the cord drops to one knee and strikes the wood twice with the butt of his staff. The staff is tipped with a resonator nodule harvested from a grazer’s acoustic organ. Twenty yards behind him, an older woman feels the sharp *tock-tock* through the soles of her pith-shoes, up her shins, and into her teeth. She answers with a single, sliding scrape of her heel. *Water is good. No teeth in the ridge.*

Up close, the people smell fiercely of the plateau, though they have curated the scent. Their quilted coats—stitched from bladder-skins stuffed with shredded pith to insulate them against both the noon furnace and the screaming acoustic pressure—are rubbed down with the sour, vinegar-musk of crushed stridulators. To the blind grazers and the insect swarms, the people do not smell like meat; they smell like a patch of annoyed bugs. Around their necks and wrists, where sweat gathers, they wear thick collars of absorbent, dried callus-sponge, so not a single drop of moisture falls to the ground to trigger the wood's hungry surface-roots or draw the attention of a sky-glider miles overhead.

Their ears are gone—or rather, they are hidden. From birth, a child of the plateau has their outer ears bound flat against the skull under tight leather caps stuffed with cured sap-rubber and waxed pith. Left unprotected, an infant’s eardrums would rupture before their first year was out, shredded by the unrelenting wall of sound. By thirty, most of them are entirely deaf to airborne noise. 

They do not miss it. They hear with their bones.

Watch them when they crawl inside the low, chitin-shingled dome of the acoustic shelter. Once the heavy, triple-layered bladder-door is pulled shut and toggled down, the relief is so sudden it feels like dropping into deep water. The screaming brass of the stridulators vanishes; the high whistling of the gliders is gone. All that remains inside the dark, turpentine-scented dome is the eternal, inescapable bass note of the continent: the *thrum-shuck, thrum-shuck* of the great vascular canyons miles beneath their hips, felt as a slow, warm heave in the floor.

Here, in the dark, they take off their goggles and their ear-caps, and they talk.

A woman wants to tell her brother about a scar-vein of prime, brittle amber she saw two coulees back. She leans forward and presses her forehead—just above the bridge of the nose, where the bone is thin and the sinus cavity acts as a sounding box—directly against his temple. Alternatively, in casual conversation among the whole group, they sit in a tight circle and lay their chins or their bare collarbones against a shared "talking-hoop"—a thin, flexible ring of glider-bone that rests across all their laps. 

She hums. It is a language without consonants, a rich, throat-singing cant of shifting pitches, glottal clicks, and nasal trills that travel through skull-bone and hollow marrow-strut directly into the inner ears of her listeners. To an outsider sitting a foot away, not touching the hoop, the family seems to be sitting in near-total silence, swaying slightly, making faint, purring buzzing noises in the back of their throats while their hands fly in complex, shadowy gestures. To those on the hoop, the brother’s skull is ringing with a clear, hilarious story about how a young glider misjudged a thermal and broke its neck on a burl. 

They are a culture of absolute material parsimony, living as gentle parasites on a host too vast to know they exist. Everything they have is borrowed from the titans around them and must eventually be given back before the floor swallows it.

In the center of the shelter, an old man—his collarbones calloused into thick, horny pads from fifty years of resting on the talking-hoop—is repairing a tool. He holds a core of deep red, petrified sap between his feet. Using a pressure-flaker made from a stridulator’s drill-proboscis, he leans his weight into the amber. *Tick.* A curved, translucent blade, sharper than surgical steel, drops into his leather apron. 

He does not use fire. Fire is the ancient terror, the unthinkable taboo, though in truth it is nearly impossible to start; the living bark-plate is too dense and sap-logged to burn, and if scorched, the plateau responds within minutes by flooding the area with gallons of suffocating, gluey wound-resin from emergency ducts, entombing the fire and anyone near it in a permanent hill of amber. 

Instead of fire, they cook with the breath of the world.

As dusk approaches and the light outside turns the bruised violet of the vents, two of the younger adults slip back out of the dome. They carry a mesh bag of *pith-grubs*—pale, thumb-sized larvae harvested from the rotting underside of a dead grazer’s shed shell—and three flat cakes of pounded stridulator meat mixed with dried sap-sugar. 

They skate down the slope to the lip of the stomatal vent. The wood here is slick and warm, pulsing faintly like an artery. They move with extreme care; the violet rim is as slippery as greased glass, and the drop down the fluted throat goes into pitch darkness, miles deep, where the air is scalding and lacks oxygen. Using long hooks of glider-bone, they hang their mesh bags just inside the throat of the vent, anchoring the cords to the ribs of the condenser trap.

Ten minutes later, the plateau exhales. 

You feel it before you see it: the deep *thrum-shuck* beneath your feet slows down, gathers itself, and then a long, shuddering sigh rises from the depths of the world. A thick column of steam, smelling overwhelmingly of sweet resin, hot iron, and deep-earth minerals, rolls lazily up the violet throat. It pours over the lip, instantly cooking the grubs and the meat-cakes in the bags to a dense, rubbery tenderness, before spilling out across the pale wood in knee-deep ribbons of fog. 

The two gatherers do not retreat. This is their bath, and their harvest. 

As the scalding fog rolls past them, cooling rapidly in the thin plateau air from lethal to merely hot, they strip off their quilted coats. They stand naked in the knee-deep ribbons of vapor, using flat scrapers of bone to squeegee the instant condensation off their own skin and off the broad, waxed wings of the condenser traps, funneling every precious, turpentine-flavored drop into the waiting bladders. For fifteen minutes, while the sun drops below the curved wooden rim of the world and the sky turns from hot white to a deep, star-pricked indigo, they stand in the warm breath of the continent, their lean, scarred bodies gleaming like polished ochre in the twilight.

Above them, the thermal-gliders have gone to roost—not by landing, for they cannot walk on the flat wood, but by locking their wing-joints and rising three miles up into the stratosphere to sleep on the high, steady jet-streams. 

Around them, as the heat of the day radiates away into space, the ten million stridulators in the valley below begin to slow their wing-cases. The deafening brass scream drops in pitch, sliding down from a jet-engine whine to a low, rattling purr, like pebbles rolling back and forth in an iron drum. 

The gatherers pull their steamed food from the vent, shoulder the heavy, sloshing water-bladders, and skate back up the ridge to the dome. Before crawling inside, the woman stops. She takes a newly flaked blade of red amber and cuts away the small, pale lip of callus-wood that has crept half an inch up the eastern wall of the shelter since their last visit four months ago. If they left it for five years, the shelter would be a burl; in twenty, it would be a smooth swell in the ridge, its chitin shingles digested and crushed into the grain of the floor. 

She pops the slice of fresh, sweet callus-cambium into her mouth, chewing it like tough, piney celery, and ducks through the door. 

Inside, in the dark, the family hooks their heels into the woven sinew straps anchored to the floor-pegs, curling onto their sides so the smallest possible surface area of their bodies touches the vibrating ground. They lay their heads on thick pillows of dead pith. Beneath them, through the soles of their feet and the curve of their hips, the continent pumps its slow, dark rivers through the night—*thrum-shuck, thrum-shuck*—a lullaby the size of a world, carrying them toward the morning.
