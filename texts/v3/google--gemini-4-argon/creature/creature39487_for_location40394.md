To find the true inhabitants of the Foundry-Ward, you cannot look for movement. In a world where a flywheel takes four hours to complete a single turn, anything that scurries or strides has already burned itself out epochs ago. You have to look instead at the places where the infrastructure appears to be thickened, or slightly out of plumb. 

If you follow the bundled pipelines out from the Smelting House into the pitch-black half-mile between sectors, guiding yourself by the smooth, blood-warm iron beneath your palm, your fingers will occasionally brush against something that is not cast metal. It feels like dry, vulcanized leather, or heavily oiled slate, warm to the exact seventy-eight degrees of the air and the pipe. If you stop and wait—and you must wait for twenty or thirty minutes—you will feel that mass shift its weight by a fraction of an inch. 

They have no name for themselves, because they do not speak, though if pressed to categorize them, an observer might call them the *Tenders*, or the *Lagged*. 

A Tender is roughly the height of a man, though vastly heavier, built with a low, broad center of gravity like an engine block. It is difficult to say where their bodies end and their attire begins, or whether the distinction ever existed. Their skin is a dense, horn-like hide accrued from centuries of mineral sweat and airborne silt, cured by the dry air into overlapping, flexible plates the color of stove-polish. In the joints of their knees and elbows, where the hide folds, thin crusts of yellow sulfur and dry tallow flake away when they bend. They have no eyes—only smooth, recessed sockets of dull bone beneath heavy brows, because eyes are wet things, and the plateau does not permit water. Instead, their faces are dominated by wide, baffled nasal slits lined with stiff, metallic bristles that filter the flour-silt, and their cheekbones are broad and flat, flared outward like acoustic horns to catch the bone-deep vibrations of the ironworks.

They are creatures of extreme metabolic poverty. Their hearts—or the muscular, valved pumps that serve them as hearts—beat once every three or four minutes, pushing a thick, dark, petroleum-heavy ichor through their limbs. To move quickly would tear their dry tissues and starve their lungs in the thin air. So they walk the way a glacier moves, or the way a clock weight descends. A Tender setting out from the Condenser District to adjust a manifold in the North Slag-Yards will take three days to cross two miles of open dust. 

In the absolute blackness between the sodium-vapor lamps, they are entirely invisible, betrayed only by the sound of their passage: a slow, rhythmic *crunch-sift*, *crunch-sift*, with a full minute of dead silence between the planting of the left foot and the lifting of the right. Because there is no wind, the tiny plumes of ochre dust kicked up by their flat, calloused heels hang in the air behind them for hours, forming a low, stationary fog bank three inches off the ground that marks their trail long after they have passed into the dark.

What they do is tend. Whether the Foundry-Ward was built by their ancestors, or whether they evolved from some simpler scavenger to fit the shape of the machinery the way a hermit crab fits a shell, is impossible to tell. Their hands have no fingernails; the tips of their thick fingers are capped with flat pads of hardened, calloused magnetite, utterly insensitive to pain but exquisitely sensitive to heat and tremor. A Tender will stand beside the hull of a processing plant for six weeks, its flat cheekbone pressed against the riveted plate, listening to the interior. It waits through the twenty-minute silences between the catching of the great gear teeth. It listens to the quality of the groan when the pig-iron engages. 

If the pitch of that groan is half a hertz too high—indicating a dry bearing or a microscopic misalignment—the Tender will slowly reach into the folds of its heavy, apron-like hide. In the warm pockets of its own flesh, it carries tools that have been worn down until they look like river stones: a spanner whose jaws have been smoothed by ten thousand years of thumb-friction, an oil-can made of seamless brass that weeps a single, black, heavy drop of tectonic grease every quarter-hour. 

The Tender will fit the spanner to a bolt as large as a dinner plate and lean its four-hundred-pound bulk against the handle. It does not heave. It simply applies its weight, letting gravity and hours do the work, until the ancient threads yield with a single, sharp *tink* that rings out across the silent flats.

When they are not working, they do not sleep—their waking state is already so close to stasis that sleep would be redundant. Instead, they *bank* themselves. In the dim, oxblood-lit alleys between the battery-blocks, or high up on the catwalks of the cooling towers where the sulfur-smoke rises in straight yellow pencils, you can find three or four of them standing together in a tight circle, their heavy shoulders touching, their foreheads leaned inward against a central steam-pipe. They stand there for months at a time while the fine, flour-like dust settles on their crowns, building up into little ochre cones that never blow away. 

They do not converse, for speech wastes air and moisture. Instead, they communicate through the iron itself. A Tender three miles away at the Smelting House will tap the blunt heel of its iron-shod palm against a pipeline—*one, pause, two*—and twenty minutes later, another Tender leaning against that same pipe in the Condenser District will feel the faint, tiny shiver in the metal beneath its fingers. It is a language with a vocabulary of perhaps thirty rhythms, expressing only the states of pressure, heat, resistance, and equilibrium. *The east valve holds. The grease is low. The wheel turns.*

They eat what the city provides, which is to say, they eat the city's friction. A Tender will scrape a thumbnail of sublimated sulfur from a leaking flange and let it dissolve on a dry, rasp-like tongue. They will pluck a *slag-tick* from the brickwork—taking twenty minutes to pry the little iron-sulfide shell loose—and crush it between molars made of dense, black enamel, swallowing the drop of heavy grease inside. When a *dredger* slides past them in the dark, its flat belly whispering through the silt, the Tender does not step aside, and the dredger does not attack. The Tender simply reaches down as the machine-beast passes and runs a calloused hand along its flaking mica carapace, feeling for loose boiler-plate, sometimes reaching directly into the creature's warm, grinding gizzard through a maintenance gill to retrieve a handful of refined copper nodules that the dredger has sifted from the flats, leaving a smear of fresh axle-grease on the beast's snout in payment.

Nothing is born quickly here, and nothing dies with any drama. A Tender lasts for centuries, gradually taking on more and more of the plateau’s mineral weight. Over the decades, the iron-sulfide in their diet and the metallic dust in their lungs begins to calcify their joints. Their four-minute heartbeats slow to ten, then to twenty. Their movements become even more fractional, until they can only work on the largest, slowest machinery—the great main-valves that only need to be turned once a millennium.

Eventually, even that ceases. If you walk the catwalks of the Central Manifold, or peer into the dim, amber-lit recesses beneath the forty-foot flywheels, you will see shapes that look like spare castings or pillars of sintered slag propped in the corners. They are Tenders who finally reached the same equilibrium as the smoothed basalt boulders out on the flat. Their hides have mineralized completely into ironstone; their feet have fused with the rusted floor-plates through decades of motionless pressure. 

The living Tenders do not bury them, nor do they mourn them. In the profound, thrift-bound economy of the Foundry-Ward, nothing is wasted. When a younger Tender—perhaps only two hundred years old—needs a replacement part for a failing condenser, or a shim to level a sagging pipeline trestle, it will walk out through the pitch dark to one of these standing, stone-still ancestors. With infinite, agonizing slowness, it will unbolt the ancestor's mineralized forearm, or pry loose a calcified collarbone that has the exact curve required for a valve-seat, and carry it back through the warm, dead air to bolt it into the living machine.
