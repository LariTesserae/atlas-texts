You do not see them at first because your eyes are looking for something that stands against the movement, rather than something that is part of the stroke. 

Look again at the three-story wall of raw basalt rising beside the dormitory. Halfway up, wedged into the two-inch clearance between the ascending stone and the trembling brick, is a figure bundled in layered, pleated oilcloth the exact blue-black of the rock. He is not trapped. He has one broad, rubber-soled boot braced against a mortar joint and the other riding the lip of a rising basalt hex. As the earth pushes him steadily toward the second-story eaves, he uses a long, spoon-billed iron bar to flick jammed winter-ferns and shattered brick out of the vertical seam, clearing the track so the wall won’t bind. When his head reaches the level of the second-floor windowsill, he doesn't climb in; he simply shifts his weight, catches the stirrup of a passing counterweight on its downward plunge, and drops twenty feet back to the street with the heavy, boneless grace of a sack of grain, stepping off just as the iron block seats itself in a cushion of moss.

These are the citizens of the basin, though they would not recognize the word *citizen*, which implies a fixed address and a stable relation to the ground. If they have a name for themselves, it is something closer to *gasket* or *clearance*.

Up close, they are stocky, barrel-chested people, built low to the ground with centers of gravity that belong on the deck of a ship in a gale. Their clothing gives them the silhouette of heavy-duty upholstered furniture: thick, quilted coats stuffed with greased wool and shredded lichen, reinforced at the elbows, knees, and shoulders with curved plates of vulcanized rubber and boiled leather. The fabric is waterproofed with the same dark, odorless tallow that lubricates the cranes, so they carry the faint, sweet-and-mineral smell of the machinery wherever they go. In the biting cold, they leave only a narrow horizontal slit of face exposed between their high, stiff collars and their felt caps—skin the color of lard, crosshatched with tiny red broken capillaries from a lifetime in the dry, barometric downdrafts of the stacks, and eyes that are constantly watered by a thick, clear film that protects them from the ozone.

They are as silent as the birds. In a valley where the only sound of a ten-ton granite column shearing loose from the mantle is a dry *tick* no louder than a snapped twig, a human voice is a dangerous blindness. To shout is to drown out the warning creak of a porcelain insulator about to shatter under tension, or the faint, high hiss of a hydraulic line developing a pinhole leak that could slice a man in half. 

So their throats have gone quiet. Instead, their hands and shoulders are in constant, rippling motion. They speak a dialect of taps, postures, and flashing colors. Every citizen carries a pair of iron thimbles on their thumbs and wears a narrow strip of polished brass sewn down the outside of their forearms. Walking through the narrow corridors, you will hear a tiny, staccato rhythm beneath the hush of the wind: *tink, tink-tink, scrape*. A woman paused at a corner taps her thumb against a cast-iron drainpipe; fifty yards away, around two bends of rising stone, her partner feels the tremor through the sole of his boot resting on a manifold, reads the cadence, and steps backward out of the path of a swinging gantry before its shadow even touches him. When they have line-of-sight, they flip the lapels of their oilskin coats—lined inside with vibrant, aniline-dyed silks of signal-red, sulfur-yellow, and vivid violet—flashing brief, complex geometric sentences across the sunlit chasms, borrowing the grammar of the copper-feathered birds that scurry between their legs.

To understand how they live, you have to step inside one of the brick tenements while it is being moved.

The interiors are warm, heated by copper radiator coils that bleed excess thermal energy from the district induction lines, and they smell overwhelmingly of steeped pine needles, mutton fat, and damp wool. There are no four-legged tables here, no standing wardrobes, no paintings hung on nails. A nail driven into a wall today will be sheared off by a rising basalt column tomorrow, or tilted at a forty-five-degree angle before supper while the roof-pistons recalibrate. 

Instead, a home is a collection of heavy canvas and leather pods suspended from the ceiling joists on three-point gimbals and heavy brass springs. As the tenement shudders, tilts, and is slowly jacked six feet to the left by the street-cranes outside, the interior remains eerily level. A family sits on slung leather hammocks around a gimbaled iron stove, drinking hot, resinous tea from weighted mugs with wide, magnetized bottoms that cling to the iron dinner tray. When the automated bricklayers peel back the southeastern corner of their living room to let a spine of blue-black stone thrust through the floorboards, nobody looks up from their soup. The cold air rushes in for forty seconds; a shower of dry mortar dust patters against the oilcloth canopy over their heads; the six-legged badger-things poke their pleated snouts through the gap to lick the newly exposed stone; and then the spider-silk cables drop a pre-mortared arch of fresh brick into place, sealing the room back up around the new rock column, which is now simply part of the parlor. Within ten minutes, one of the children has slung their coat over a sharp hexagonal projection of the pristine basalt, using the raw earth as a hat-rack while it is still thirty degrees below freezing.

Sleep is taken in shifts, and never all at once. Even inside a gimbaled hammock, the human body here never fully surrenders its muscle tone. Watch an old man dozing in the warm, steamy lee of a boiler housing: his eyes are shut, his breathing is slow, yet his ankles and hips are subtly flexing every few seconds, unconsciously reading the micro-tremors of the iron deck plates beneath him. If the vibration of the induction coils changes pitch by half a hertz—indicating a load-shift three blocks away—he will roll three feet to his left in his sleep, curling automatically into the triangular safety-niche between two main struts, without ever waking up.

What do they do here, in a city that builds and repairs itself? 

The machines have immense power and tireless, clockwork logic, but they have no improvisation. The city’s automated systems know how to lift a walkway, how to level a roof, how to lay a brick. They do not know what to do when a cartwheel-sized rosette of frost-lichen freezes solid inside a limit-switch, fooling a crane into thinking a steel girder is six inches higher than it actually is. They do not know how to untangle a three-hundred-foot copper loom that has snagged on the horn of a newly calved basalt ridge while simultaneously being strangled by ironwood vines.

The people are the tending caste of the friction. They are the grease-monkeys of a geological event.

You see them forty feet in the air, walking along the backs of moving crane-arms with the casual balance of tightrope walkers, carrying long-handled brass scrapers and pots of warm tallow. They move in small, silent crews of three or four. One pries open the casing of a groaning gimbal; another scoops out the packed, frozen mash of crushed fern-fronds and dead copper-birds that has fouled the gears; a third smears fresh black grease into the teeth just as the giant arm sweeps them all across an intersection. They don't use harnesses—a tether in a place where the anchor points are constantly moving in opposite directions is just a fancy way to get sawed in half. Instead, they carry short, curved iron hooks in their belt-loops, which they can slap onto an overhead cable to take their weight for three seconds while the floor drops out from under them, before dropping onto whatever new surface has risen to replace it.

They harvest the valley, too. The industry produces no goods that leave the basin—there are no roads over the southern terrace, no trains climbing the rim. The city’s only product is its own continued existence against the rising stone, and the people live entirely off the metabolic byproducts of that struggle.

In the damp, shadowed under-wards, where the condensation falls like a constant, chemical drizzle, you will find the lichen-cutters. They move among the jerky, six-legged grazers like shepherds among sheep, using crescent-shaped knives to harvest the swollen, inner hearts of the frost-lichen rosettes. Raw, the plant tastes of battery acid and turpentine, full of leached copper salts and hydraulic fluid. But the citizens pack it into perforated iron drums and hang the drums inside the hot, high-pressure exhaust flues of the distillation coils. Over three days of heavy barometric distortion, the toxins are steamed out and re-condensed for the machines' reservoirs, leaving behind a dense, grey, starchy loaf that smells faintly of mushrooms and baked clay. They slice it thick, fry it in tallow rendered from the heavy-bodied birds, and season it with the sharp, camphorous sap of crushed ironwood.

It is a life of relentless, microscopic attention, and it has made them a strange, unsentimental people. They have no monuments, because anything stationary is an enemy of the city's survival. They have no graveyards; when someone dies—usually old, their joints finally stiffened by the cold until they are half a second too slow stepping off a shearing fault-line—they are not buried in earth that would only spit them back up tomorrow. They are wrapped in clean canvas, weighted with scrap copper, and lowered into the deep, steaming sumps beneath the main hydraulic accumulators, where the heat and the chemical frost-water break them down in a matter of weeks, returning their salts to the condensation cycle that feeds the ferns.

Their only art, if you can call it that, is geological. 

Because the basalt columns rise at a steady, measurable rate—an inch an hour here, four inches a day there—the citizens use the emerging rock as both calendar and canvas. A young woman waiting for her lover at an intersection will take a cold-chisel and a heavy, lead-headed mallet (lead, because it makes a dull *thud* against the stone rather than a ringing *clang*) and chip a shallow, intricate geometric notch into the face of a basalt column right at eye level. When her lover passes that corner six hours later, the mark is three feet above his head, riding the blue-black stone toward the sky. He hooks a crane-stirrup, swings himself up, and chisels his reply two inches below hers, where the rock is newer. 

If you look up at the tallest, oldest columns in the center of the basin—great, jagged spires of basalt that have been pushing upward for sixty years, towering two hundred feet above the shifting roofs of the tenements before they finally topple under their own weight and are broken up by theautomated hammers for ballast—you can see their entire history written from top to bottom. Near the dizzying, sunlit peaks, bleached by the high ozone, are the chiselled tallies, love-notes, and warning-glyphs of the current citizens' grandparents. Lower down, wrapped in thick cables of waxy green vine, are the marks of their parents' youth. And down at the base, in the cold, damp gloom where the stone is still wet with the grease of the earth and the sweat of the boilers, a ten-year-old boy with lard-pale cheeks is crouched between two thumping hydraulic stabilizers, quietly chipping his name into a piece of the world that did not exist twenty minutes ago.
