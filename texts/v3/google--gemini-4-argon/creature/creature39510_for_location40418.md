If you sit very still on the stamped-steel grating of the middle tiers, letting the wet heat soak through your clothes until your skin matches the temperature of the iron, you will eventually notice that the shadows between the boilers are not entirely fixed. 

At first, it seems to be a trick of the bullseye lanterns—a slow refracting of orange light through the rising vapor. A bundle of lead-sheathed cables seems to sag a fraction of an inch where there is no weight upon it. A pale, waxy philodendron leaf trembles, though there is no draft. Then, you realize that what you took to be a rusted cast-iron manifold, or a knotted bolus of bone-white root wedged into the acute angle of a truss, has a pulse. It is a very slow pulse—perhaps four beats to the minute—pushing thick, cool blood through a body built to endure a place where briskness is fatal.

They are called the *Gaugemen* by nobody at all, because there is nobody left on the parched surface to name them, and they have no use for nouns among themselves. 

There are perhaps a dozen of them distributed throughout the three-hundred-foot vertical column of the sinkhole. They do not gather. The geometry of the pit is too tight for congregation, and the heat makes the proximity of another warm body intolerable. Each occupies a distinct stratum of the machinery, a territory measured not in square footage, but in plumbing circuits and thermal gradients.

Seen in the jaundiced glow of an oil-tube, an inhabitant of this place looks vaguely humanoid, though edited by generations of tight spaces and absolute darkness into something resembling a cross between a deep-sea crustacean and a grease-rag. They are small—no heavier than a child—with limbs that have lengthened and flattened to slip through the six-inch gaps between copper vats and the basalt wall. Their joints are loose, capable of painless subluxation; a shoulder can pop from its socket with a wet, muffled *cluck* to allow the torso to pass through a dry turbine intake, then slide back into place on the other side. 

Their skin is the color of tallow and entirely hairless, coated in a thick, sebaceous film that smells of paraffin and crushed sulfur. This grease is their primary defense. Without it, the constant, mineral-heavy condensation would macerate their flesh within a week, rotting it on the bone like damp leather. On their knuckles, elbows, knees, and the crowns of their skulls—the places that inevitably scrape against the razor-sharp basalt or the crisp, unrusted edges of the iron girders—the skin has thickened into heavy, horn-like calluses infused with iron scale, turning their extremities the dull, gunmetal gray of the foundry itself.

They have eyes, though they are vestigial and lidded over with a translucent nictitating membrane to keep out the caustic sweat of the rock. They do not need to see the shape of a catwalk; they know the whole sinkhole by heart. It is a static universe. Every rivet, every stamped serial number, every cold-shut in the cast iron is a permanent landmark mapped in their minds. The only things that change are the plants and the slow drift of the machinery, and both happen so gradually that a blind hand trailing along a pipe can read the difference from one day to the next like Braille.

They move at the speed of the pistons. In an atmosphere hovering at ninety-eight degrees with one hundred percent humidity, sudden exertion causes the body’s core temperature to spike instantly; to run or climb quickly is to cook one’s own brain inside the skull. So they drift. A limb is extended; a long-fingered hand, tipped with flat, spoon-shaped nails, finds purchase on a valve wheel; then, over the course of ten minutes, the weight of the body is transferred. They hang in the trusses like sloths, their pale, rubbery bellies resting against the sweating bricks of the boiler-houses, letting the masonry draw the excess heat from their organs.

Yet they are not feral animals merely sheltering in a ruin. They are maintenance. Whether they are the heavily mutated descendants of the original engineers who built the pressure-sink, or a purpose-bred biological component introduced when the last rivet was driven home, the directive of the place is in their marrow. They are the governors of the equilibrium.

Left entirely to itself, the jungle of thermal flora and the weeping mineral scale would choke the district in fifty years. A bone-vine would eventually wedge itself so tightly into the teeth of a brass reduction gear that the metal would shear; a beard of yellow silicate would seal a pressure-relief valve until a copper vat ruptured, parboiling the entire chasm in a single catastrophic breath of steam. 

The inhabitants prevent this, acting as a slow-motion immune system. 

One of them will spend an entire afternoon—if afternoon had any meaning here—curled around the housing of a locomotive-sized cylinder. With a shard of black basalt held in its gray, calloused fist, it gently, methodically scrapes the sulfur-crust from the brass lip of a seal. *Scrape. Pause. Scrape.* The rhythm matches the *plip-plip* of the condensation. When the brass is clean, it dips two fingers into a weep-hole where heavy, luminescent oil collects, and smears the grease across the piston rod just ahead of the descending collar.

Their relationship with the pale vegetation is one of meticulous, unhurried gardening. They know which vines are load-bearing—having replaced rusted-out tension rods centuries ago—and which are invasive. When a rubbery philodendron begins to smother a pilot light, threatening to starve the burner of oxygen, a pale hand reaches out of the dark. The long, spoon-shaped fingernails pinch the stem, not severing it, but crushing the vascular bundle on one side. Over the next three weeks, the plant will grow away from the trauma, curving neatly around the bullseye glass and leaving the sullen orange flame to burn in peace.

In return, the district feeds them. The inhabitants cannot eat the sulfur-snails or the toxic spore-moths; instead, they are parasites on the alchemy of the machines and the plants. 

They harvest the square, gear-molded tubers that grow in the drive-chains, slicing off small, translucent wafers that taste of alum and starch. They drink from specific, cupped rosettes of the cabbage-like plants—never from the stagnant puddles on the steel floors, which are foul with lead and grease, but from the high leaves that catch the pure, newly condensed steam before it touches the rock. And they consume the "butter" of the vats: in certain low-pressure distillation columns, the geothermal heat and the deep-earth gases combine to form a thick, yellowish paraffin that oozes from the gaskets. It is rich in complex hydrocarbons, which their altered livers can break down into glucose. A creature will sit beneath a leaking flange for hours, mouth slightly open, letting a single warm, waxy globule drop onto its tongue once every twenty minutes.

Because there is no wind, and because the machinery is nearly silent, the sinkhole acts as a giant acoustic resonator. A sound made at the bottom of the pit travels up through the three hundred feet of interlocked iron and stone with weird clarity. 

The inhabitants do not speak with their throats; the thick, ether-heavy air is too exhausting to push through vocal cords. Instead, they communicate through the infrastructure. Using a knuckle horned with iron-scale, or a small wrench salvaged from a centuries-old toolbox, one will tap a cast-iron riser pipe.

*Tink.* 

A pause of ten seconds. 

*Tink-tink.*

The vibration travels through the sweating metal, past the brick boilers, through the braided bundles of vine and cable, into the palms and bare feet of the others clinging to the machinery in the dark above and below. It is not a language of words, of past or future. It is a language of pressure and state. A single tap on a high-pressure steam line means *I am here, tending the upper valves.* A slow, rhythmic scratching against a copper vat means *the oil is rising in the third quadrant; come and eat.* 

When one of them dies—usually after a lifespan that stretches over a century, their metabolism having run so cool and slow—there is no burial. There is no dirt to dig into. Instead, the nearest neighbors will spend days slowly dragging the light, greasy body down through the dry turbines and the narrow catwalks, descending toward the very bottom of the pit where the heat is fiercest and the cast-iron floorboards rattle softly over the magma-heated groundwater. 

They wedge the corpse into the tightest, hottest crevice between the primary boilers and the jagged basalt wall. Within a month, the broad, silver-veined leaves of the thermal philodendrons have draped themselves over the body, their wax-like roots flattening against the cooling skin to drink the last of its heat. Within a year, the bone-vines have braided themselves through the ribcage, turning the skeleton into structural reinforcement for the lower steam-pipes, while the relentless, mineral-heavy condensation washes the flesh away into the dark below, returning its water to the cycle of the rising steam.
