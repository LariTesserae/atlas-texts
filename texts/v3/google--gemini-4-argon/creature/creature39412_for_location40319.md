If you stand on the western terrace and watch the city for an hour, you will swear it is abandoned. If you watch it for six hours—long enough for the sharp, black wedges of shadow behind the dry-stone houses to pivot forty-five degrees across the blinding mirrors of the courts—you will realize that the stone has rearranged itself. 

A single slab near the edge of a third-tier sluice, which had been lying flat at noon, is now tilted on a small, basalt chock, altering the sheet-flow across six hundred square yards of terrace by half a millimeter. Farther out, at the intersection of two long causeways, three greywacke pillars that were not there in the morning have resolved out of the glare, only to slowly separate and move down two diverging paths at the pace of the sun’s shadow.

They are there, in the hundreds. You look directly at them when you scan the horizon.

The builders of the basin have no lungs. At this altitude, where the air is thin and dry above the rim, breathing is a frantic, expensive business—a constant bellows-work of ribs and blood that demands high calories and produces restless, twitching creatures. Nothing with lungs could endure the stillness here, let alone build for it. 

Instead, the inhabitants take their oxygen the way the liverwort does: directly from the warm, skin-temperature sheets of water that glide over the stone, and through the damp film that clings to their hides. They are amphibious, though not in the slick, fragile way of newts or frogs. They are massive, slow-metabolizing things, built on a hydrostatic skeleton braced by dense, fibrous plates of dark cartilage that have, over decades of soaking in the mineral-rich runoff, partially calcified. A mature adult weighs perhaps four hundred pounds, most of it dense, water-logged muscle and heavy, stone-colored hide that has the exact matte, granular texture of wet basalt.

When they stand upright on the causeways, they are roughly the height of a man, though much broader at the base, tapering upward like the corbelled vaults they build. They have no distinct head—no neck to break the geometry of their silhouette. Their sensory organs—dark, horizontal slits that read polarized light and water-level, and broad patches of tympanic skin that feel the vibrations of the bedrock—are set flush into their upper chests and shoulders. Below this, their bodies divide into four heavy, pillar-like limbs that serve equally as legs and levering arms, each ending in a broad, flat pad rimmed with horn as hard and square-edged as a mason’s bolster.

Their life is governed entirely by the physics of the half-inch sheet of water. 

Because they breathe through the wet vascular fringes tucked beneath their calcified plates and through the broad, porous soles of their feet, they cannot allow the water beneath them to splash or break into turbulent froth; bubbles carry less contact than a continuous, laminar film. When they walk down the causeways or across the flooded plazas, they do not lift their feet clear of the surface. They glide. A foot-pad slides forward through the half-inch sheet with such slow, beveled precision that it does not raise a ripple. They move at the speed of a meniscus creeping up a glass tube, leaving the razor-sharp reflection of the black peaks unbroken behind them.

This is why the city is so sparse, and why the courts of water are three hundred yards wide. They are not social in the way of clustering mammals, who need each other for warmth or defense against predators. There are no predators above the clouds, and the rock itself provides the heat. Instead, their spacing is metabolic and acoustic. Each cluster of six or seven stepped-stone houses represents the exact residential density that a single hydraulic terrace can oxygenate and warm without the water cooling or going stale before it reaches the next step down.

They do not eat the watercress or the broad-leaved arums. To chew and digest fibrous cellulose requires a hot, fermenting gut and a greedy metabolism; it would mean stripping the basins bare, muddying the water with waste, and shattering the equilibrium of the plateau. 

Instead, the vast emerald beds of cress and the thick carpets of moss are their external organs. The plants act as huge biological condensers, drawing dissolved iron, calcium, sulfur, and atmospheric nitrogen out of the thin air and the mountain runoff, weaving them into complex sugars and amino acids that leach continuously from their roots and lower leaves into the warm, slow water. In the chest-deep squares between the causeways, the water beneath the floating islands of arum is a clear, nutrient-dense broth. 

For ten hours out of every twenty-four, the inhabitants simply lie on the submerged stone benches inside the dark, corbel-vaulted dwellings, or sit motionless at the bottom of the chest-deep cisterns among the pale, hanging roots of the cress. There, in the warm dark, they open the pale, pleated gills along their flanks and drink the broth directly into their tissues, absorbing the sun’s energy at one remove while the occasional *plip* of condensation falls from the ceiling above them. They have no mouths to speak of—only a narrow, muscular siphon at the base of the thorax used to take in mineral-heavy water and to expel clean, filtered brine, along with small, dense pellets of precipitated chalk and silica that sink like gravel into the joints of the paving stones.

Then what is the city for? Why prize up the massive slabs with wooden wedges and wet rope? Why build the low towers and the miles of straight causeways?

Because the mountain is young, and the heat beneath it is not still.

To a fast-living creature with lungs, the basin looks eternal, frozen in a glassy quiet. To the inhabitants, whose hearts beat once every four minutes and who live for three or four centuries—gradually stiffening as the silicates in the water turn their cartilage to actual stone—the plateau is a restless, shifting engine. The deep-earth heat that keeps the water at the temperature of skin moves through the bedrock in slow plumes, taking five or ten years to migrate from the western terrace to the eastern Spillway. As the heat shifts, the basalt expands microscopically; the rectilinear joints pinch shut in one district and yawn open a tenth of an inch in another. The grade of the two-mile floor tilts. 

If left to itself, the water would gather into rushing channels. It would carve gorges, drain the broad terraces dry, expose the moss to the crisp, killing air, and rush off the plateau too quickly to be warmed by the rock. The oasis would die of its own drainage in fifty years.

The city is a two-mile ballast and a hydraulic computer. 

The clusters of six or seven heavy stone houses are not merely shelters from the sun’s high glare; they are counterweights. Stacked directly over the fault-blocks of the basin floor, their immense, dry-stone mass presses down on the fractured greywacke, keeping the subterranean seams pinched to the exact aperture required to let the deep warmth weep through without venting steam. 

And the inhabitants are its constant, silent masons. 

Their language has no voice. It is hydraulic. Standing on a causeway in the western district, an individual will lower its broad, heavy body into a sluice, displacing the water by an inch. The resulting pressure-wave—not a splash, just a wide, half-millimeter swell in the laminar sheet—travels across three hundred yards of mirror-still court in twenty seconds. Three terraces away, four others feel the subtle change in pressure against their foot-pads and the change in the pitch of the glassy whisper along the retaining walls. They read the message in the altered geometry of the sky’s reflection across the basin.

*The heat is drifting north,* the swell says. *The third seam is closing.*

Over the next three weeks, without a word, without a splash, thirty of them will converge on a raised plinth. Using lengths of dense, fibrous stem brought up generations ago from the cloud-forests below the rim—stems they keep submerged in the cisterns so they remain supple even as they slowly petrify—they lash together a harness. They fit wedges of dry horn into the natural cleavage of a two-ton basalt slab, soak the ropes, and wait for the slow expansion of the fibers and the afternoon sun on the black rock to split the stone with a single, low *thud* that travels through the soles of every foot in the basin.

Then, moving a foot an hour, they slide the new slab across the slick, liverwort-greased stones of the causeway to a house two districts away, adding its weight to a roof vault to press a rising seam back into level.

When one of them finally grows too old—when the calcium and silica of the basin have so thoroughly infiltrated its tissues that its joints lock and its gill-fringes can no longer flex—it does not rot. There are no bacteria here suited to the warm, sterile mineral flow, and no scavengers to tear the flesh. 

Instead, while it still retains the last fractional movement in its pillar-like limbs, the elder glides slowly to a gap in a retaining wall or the corner of a rising foundation. Over the next decade, the tough, bamboo-like ferns take root in the seams of its armor; the bright green moss carpets its shoulders; the mineral water finishes turning its dense, fibrous core into dark grey stone. 

If you walk down the long, straight causeways and look closely at the knee-high walls that hold the mirror-still courts in place, you will see that every fifth or sixth block is slightly rounder at the corners than the split basalt, its surface grained like old horn, holding up the water and the sky in the warm, whisper-quiet sun.
