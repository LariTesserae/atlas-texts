The people who built the talc windbreaks and hung the copper pry-bars did not die out, and they did not leave. Leaving would have required descending into the lavender haze, which would have required a sustained intention across several days, and the altitude had already stripped them of the metabolic luxury of plans.

Instead, they stayed until the staying changed what they were made of.

You will not spot one until you have been standing on the ridge for half an hour. Your eyes, fatigued by the endless, chalky stipple of the fauna, will eventually catch on a vertical line that is neither the ninety-degree kink of a Glass-Brier nor the crooked tripod of an ancient tool-rack. 

Three hundred yards up the slope of a waxy granite dome stands a tall, narrow cylinder of wrapped felt and split bone. It is roughly the height of a man, though thinner, as if the core of it had dried and shrunk inside its clothes over a very long time. It is wading knee-deep in the alabaster tortoises and the feathered knuckle-spheres—or rather, its lower legs are submerged in them, the pale carapaces passing seamlessly through its shins like water around a piling, the intersection marked only by a dry, gray fuzz where fabric and chitin fail to argue over the same four inches of space.

They have no name for themselves because they have not spoken since the wind stopped, an era ago. If they had a function, it would be something like *Gleaners of the Seam*, or *The Unwedged*.

A Gleaner’s body is a triumph of desiccation. Beneath five or six layers of pale, grease-free felt—pounded together millennia ago from the shed down of the knuckle-beasts—their skin resembles split slate or the gray inner bark of a dead cedar. They have no moisture left to sweat or bleed. Their joints do not use fluid; they articulate on dry, polished pads of calcium that click softly when a limb is levered into a new position. Under their wrappings, their ribs are reinforced with splints of black silica harvested from the briers, lashed directly to the collarbones with copper wire to keep the chest cavity from collapsing into the neighboring ribs during periods of spatial drift.

Most striking are their lower legs and feet. They do not wear boots. From the knee down, their legs are encased in long, heavy split-cylinders of smooth talc, strapped tight to the bone. They do not walk *on* the fauna; to step on top of the three-foot-deep carpet of shells would require lifting the knee high with every stride, an exhausting waste of energy in the thin air. Instead, they shuffle *through* the beasts. 

The talc greaves act as phase-keels. When a Gleaner wishes to move, it leans its slender weight forward—just a fraction of an inch—and waits for the local boundary calculations to slip. With a faint, static *hiss* like tearing tissue paper, its shin slides directly through the solid rib-vault of a segmented cylinder-beast. TheGleaner moves at a pace of perhaps forty paces a day, drifting through the crowded biological pavement like a stake dragged through stiff tallow.

Watch one for an hour, and you will see the work they still perform.

The Gleaner carries a long staff made of a single, straight vein of native copper, beaten flat at the lower end like a baker’s peel. It does not hold the staff in its fingers; its fingers fused into a single, hooked mitten of horn and dry tendon centuries ago. Instead, the staff passes straight through the palm of its hand, held in place by the smoky, double-exposed friction of an intentional intersection.

Slowly—so slowly the shadow of the staff doesn't seem to move at all—the Gleaner inserts the flat copper blade into the air just above a saucer-like depression where four or five pale beasts have overlapped into a single, knotted blur. 

Where the creatures intersect, those little floating cubes of inverted light and geometric distortion gather like dry froth. To the Gleaner, this is the only crop worth harvesting. The Gleaner twists its wrist. The copper blade catches the edge of a silent tear in the air—a tiny rectangular window showing the gray stone three feet below—and *lifts*. 

There is that sound again: the low, wool-muffled *thrum* of a bass string.

The distortion peels away from the overlapping shells like a skin of dried glue. It has no weight, only an intense, cold static charge that makes the loose fibers of the Gleaner’s felt wrappings stand out stiffly at ninety-degree angles. The Gleaner brings the copper blade up to its face. 

Where a mouth and nose should be, there is only a flat mask of thin, translucent alabaster—the shed shell of a paper-tortoise—bound over the lower skull. Behind this pale shield, something dry and gray-violet flickers once, twice, absorbing the little cube of broken geometry and the static electricity clinging to it. The cube vanishes. Below, the four overlapping beasts settle an inch deeper into the rock, their boundaries momentarily crisp again, though they do not wake to notice the relief.

This is how the Gleaners eat. They do not consume meat or mineral; they consume the *discrepancy*. They feed on the exhausted math of the plateau, skimming the little errors of volume and gravity that collect in the hollows like pond scum.

Sometimes you will find two Gleaners together, though "together" is a word that has lost its precision here. 

Near one of the ancient bone-tripods, what looks like a single, wide Gleaner with four arms and two heads wrapped in the same continuous shroud of gray felt stands motionless. Fifty years ago, two of them approached the same tripod from opposite directions to test the weight of a hanging flint mallet. Neither gave way. Over the course of three autumns that never changed temperature, they simply walked into one another and stopped.

Now, their collarbones cross like the timbers of a roof. Their two hearts—dry, leathery bellows the size of walnuts—occupy the exact same hollow in the center of their shared chest. Because they beat only once an hour, and out of sync, they never collide; one contracts at the top of the hour, the other at the half, pulsing quietly through the ghostly tissue of its twin. 

They are not dead, and they are not asleep. If you walk up to them—your boots crunching loudly on the brittle carapaces, your breath clouding absurdly in the moistureless air, moving with the terrifying, frantic velocity of a creature from the lower world—their four eyes will not track you. You are too fast to be registered as an object. To them, you are merely a brief, warm draft that smells faintly of wet salt, passing through the valley and gone before their optic nerves can finish compounding the first image of your face.

Only hours after you have walked past, when you are miles away on the next smooth dome, one of the four arms will rise an inch, a dry calcium knuckle clicking in the stillness, to brush away the slight atmospheric disturbance you left behind.
