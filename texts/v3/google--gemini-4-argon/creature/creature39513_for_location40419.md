If you stand on the basin rim and squint into that shadowless glare, you will see nothing moving but the water. If you climb down into the roar and walk among the wet spires, you will still think yourself alone for the first hour, because your eyes will be looking for flesh, or fur, or the dark darting shadow of a fish. 

You have to stop looking at the stones and start looking *through* the water to find what lives here. 

The first thing you will notice is a slight thickening inside one of the horizontal streams—a knot of denser refraction sliding against the current. If you reach your hand into the freezing, thigh-thick conduit of airborne water, your fingers will brush against something cold, firm, and slick, like heavy ballistic gelatin braided around a core of flexible chalk. It will flinch—not with the panic of a trout, but with the sudden, hydraulic contraction of a muscle made of pressurized water—and shoot sideways out of the stream, riding a thin film of spray up the vertical face of a limestone fin.

They are the **Rill-Spindles**, though they have no single shape when relaxed. They are colonies of hydro-mineral zooids that have solved the problem of an environment with no soil and no sun-warmed food chain by eating the one thing the basin has in violent excess: kinetic energy and dissolved stone. 

A spindle’s body is a lattice of flexible, rubbery silica tubes filled with a clear, high-viscosity fluid that has the exact refractive index of the basin’s water. In the pools, they are completely invisible. You only see them when they move through the air-streams, or when they feed. To feed, a spindle anchors its calcified tail-hook into a crevice near a mid-air collision point—where two errant currents smash together into atomized mist—and unfurls itself like a ribbed translucent umbrella. As the fast-moving water hammers against its membrane, the creature’s piezocrystalline spine converts the sheer mechanical battering into a weak bio-electric current. It literally eats the crash of the water, using that stolen spark to fix dissolved calcium carbonate and carbon dioxide straight out of the spray, growing those rapid, geometric fans of "false lichen" directly upon its own back as an external storehouse of mineral fat. When the fan grows as large as a dinner plate and snaps off, it doesn't just dissolve; the spindle drops down into the pool after it, wrapping its clear, gelatinous mantle around the chalky shard to digest the bound energy at its leisure in the quiet dark of a pothole.

Because the water does not obey a single gravity, neither do the things that hunt the spindles.

Look up at the undersides of the stone arches, where the water hangs like wobbling mercury mirrors, twenty feet in the air. Clinging to the dry stone *just* outside the wet rim of those inverted pools are the **Plumb-Walkers**. 

At rest, a Plumb-Walker looks like a split piece of blue-black chert wedged into a fissure, flanked by eight long, hair-thin slivers of wet slate. They are lithic arthropods, their carapaces grown from layered calcium and dark flint, utterly blind because eyes are useless in a world where light comes from every direction at once and bends around corners. Instead, their long, stone-splinter legs are tipped with delicate, hollow bulbs of thin chitin that act as hydro-acoustic hydrophones. By resting two legs in a horizontal stream, one in a pool below, and one against the vibrating rock of a hoodoo, a Walker maps the entire three-dimensional web of the basin through the clatter and hum of the water. 

When a Walker moves, it is startlingly fast and deeply wrong to watch. It does not care which way is down. It steps off a rock face directly onto the outer skin of a vertical ribbon of water, skating along the surface tension of the ascending stream as easily as a water strider on a farm pond, its body sticking out at a ninety-degree angle to the valley floor. They use the warped gravity troughs as highways, dropping *sideways* through the air from one suspended lens of water to another, snapping up Rill-Spindles with heavy, knapped-flint chelicerae that click together with a sound indistinguishable from two river cobbles striking in the current.

And then there are the builders. 

The dry-stone walls, the spiral cisterns, and the needle-like cairns with their taut, vibrating cords look ancient and abandoned because they are half-dissolved, their slate edges worn smooth by the relentless water. A visitor assumes the architects died out centuries ago. 

They didn't. They are standing right there in the flooded flats, and they are the reason the cords are still taut.

If you watch a cluster of three or four smooth, grey hoodoos near a broken aqueduct for half a day, you will realize that one of the pillars is forty inches further to the left than it was at noon, and the water-channel at its base has been freshly chinked with blue sinkhole clay. 

The **Karst-Wrights** do not look like creatures; they look like the valley’s weather given a slow, deliberate shape. A Wright is a five-foot-tall, roughly cylindrical column of honeycombed limestone, pale grey and deeply fluted by vertical runnels. It has no head, no eyes, and no true limbs. Instead, the porous rock of its "body" is a living shell—a massive, mobile bryozoan reef inhabited by millions of microscopic, silica-secreting polyps and bound together internally by thick, muscular cables of that same rotting, mineralized cordage you see tied to the cairns. 

Inside the Wright’s hollow stone core, it holds a captive lens of the basin’s warped water. By squeezing and shifting this internal water-lens with its fibrous tendons, the Wright alters its own localized gravity and buoyancy. To move, it doesn't walk; it leans into a warped spatial trough, lets the upward-pulling water take ninety percent of its three-hundred-pound weight, and slides across the slick limestone floor on a Cushion of pressurized clay and mucus, grinding slowly over the cobbles like a miniature glacier. 

When it needs to build or repair, the Wright extrudes dozens of long, glistening, grey-white tendrils from the pores and vertical flutes of its stone hide. These tendrils—as thick as marine rope and coated in abrasive chert grit—can slither into a sinkhole to dredge up gummy clay, or wrap around a hundred-pound slab of split slate, hoisting it effortlessly by passing the stone through an upward-flowing ribbon of water.

The Wrights are not building houses. They have no need for shelter from the cold or the wet. They are building *looms* and *mills*.

To a Karst-Wright, the broken physics of the basin is a vast, tangled machine that must be constantly tuned. They build the chest-high aqueducts and wedge the slate splash-plates between the hoodoos to force the errant streams to collide at specific angles. They are farming the crashes. By steering two high-velocity currents into a spiral cistern, they create a roaring, atomized mist-chamber where the calcium-crust blooms ten times faster than it does in the wild, attracting hundreds of Rill-Spindles and the Plumb-Walkers that eat them. When the spindles and walkers die or shed their mineral plates at the bottom of the cistern, the Wright simply slides over the open top of the structure, seals the rim with its base, and floods the chamber with acidic digestive fluids, drinking the dissolved stone and biological slurry straight up through its porous foot.

And the cairns—those hundreds of needle-like spires in the flats with their tethered, perforated chert disks straining at impossible angles in the airborne streams—are their nervous system. 

The Wrights do not speak, and they cannot see across the mile-wide glare of the valley. Instead, they weave their own shed tendons—the tough, mineralized cordage—and tie the pierced chert rings into the high-velocity water bridges. When the water shoots through the hole in a suspended stone disk, it sets up a fierce, harmonic vibration that travels down the taut cord, into the stone cairn, and straight into the bedrock floor of the basin. 

The entire valley floor is a soundboard. By subtly shifting a splash-plate a quarter of an inch to the left, a Wright on the eastern rim changes the flow of a horizontal stream, which alters the pitch of three tethered chert disks a hundred yards away, sending a specific, resonant shudder through the limestone flats. Every Wright within a mile feels that shift in pitch through its stone base. They know instantly that a spatial trough has drifted, that a cistern is full of calcium-crust, or that something warm, soft, and two-legged has just stepped off the ridge trail and is ducking under the thigh-thick streams, disrupting the ancient, booming song of the water.

If you stay in the basin until the dark violet sky turns black—though the valley floor never truly goes dark, as the hanging water-lenses hold the twilight like fiber-optic cables, glowing with a faint, cold triboluminescence from the crashing mineral spray—you will hear the clatter of the water change. 

The random roar of millions of glass beads will begin to organize. As the Wrights slide out from among the hoodoos and adjust their slate baffles in the pale, sourceless glow, the slapping of water against stone falls into a slow, crushing, polyrhythmic cadence. The taut cords on the cairns hum in unison, a deep, bone-shaking bass note that makes the teeth ache in your jaw and causes the loose gravel at your feet to skitter sideways, uphill, toward the nearest dark pillar that wasn't standing behind you five minutes ago.
