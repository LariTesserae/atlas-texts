To see them, you must wait for a quartz vein to shear. 

When the blue triboluminescent flash briefly strips the gloom from a cliff face, you might catch three or four of them suspended fifty feet up a seventy-degree slab of gabbro, frozen mid-stride. When the rock cools back to black, they are gone—not because they have fled, but because their bodies are the exact matte, light-drinking charcoal of the basalt, dusted over with the grey-and-copper frost of the dead lichen they harvest.

They are built for edges. 

At a glance, they resemble tall, desperately attenuated hominids, though their proportions have been drastically revised by the altitude and the stone. Because the air is so thin, their ribcages are immense—deep, keeled barrels that jut forward like the prows of ships, housing lungs that take up nearly the entirety of their torsos. Below the ribs, their waists pinch in to almost nothing, and their limbs extend in long, dry bundles of tendon and bone that look less like flesh and more like cured hardwood wrapped in slate-colored hide.

You cannot walk here on soft feet. Anything with a fleshy pad would be ribboned to the bone in ten paces. Below the knee and the forearm, their skin hardens into thick, laminated plates of dark keratin. Their feet are not flat; they are split into two long, opposing digits—like the talons of an osprey or the cleft hooves of an ibex, but narrower, shaped like cold chisels. They do not step *on* the rock; they slot themselves into it. A toe wedges into a hairline fracture; a heel hooks over the razor-lip of an obsidian shelf. When they move across a talus field, they make the exact same sound as the settling mountain: a light, dry *tink-tink-clack*, like heavy stoneware plates being stacked in a dark cupboard.

They have no spoken language. In an atmosphere this thin, shouting is a waste of precious breath and carries only a few yards before dying in the warm vacuum. Instead, they listen through their shins. 

When a group of them rests inside one of the herringbone shelters, they do not sit on their haunches. They crouch with their cleft heels locked tightly against the largest, deepest-set foundation stones, their long arms wrapped around their knees, chins resting on their breastbones. They are feeling the valley. Their leg bones are dense and resonant, conducting the subterranean snaps of the bedrock directly to their inner ears. 

To communicate across a ridge, one of them will draw a heavy striker of dense hornblende from a belt of braided fungus-fiber and tap it against the spine of a monolith. *Tak-tak. Tak.* Three hundred yards away, across a gulf of dark air, another crouched figure feels the vibration travel up through the root of the spire and into its heels, reading the cadence, the pitch, and the intervening density of the stone.

Their eyes are very large, set deep beneath overhanging brows of horn to protect them from flying rock-splinters when a needle collapses. The irises are pale, milky amber, evolved to drink in the ten-second flares of cherry-red mantle light and the blue friction-flashes of splitting quartz. When a seam ruptures nearby, they do not flinch or look away; they turn their wide, pale eyes directly toward the glare, studying the newly exposed face in the brief window of illumination, memorizing the fresh geometry of cracks and overhangs before the darkness swallows it again.

They are nomads, though they never travel more than a few miles across the basin. They are farmers of a crop that lives for three hours.

Their entire existence is tethered to the braided tension-lines. They do not use the ropes as bridges—the pale fungus-fiber is too brittle to bear weight, sawed at constantly by the stone pillars. The ropes are traps for movement. 

A family unit—usually five or six individuals, genderless until the rare, brief seasons when a deep thermal vent stays open for weeks—will camp in a wedge-shelter at the hub of seven or eight radiating lines. They sleep in short, twenty-minute bursts, their bodies warm from the ambient kiln-heat of the stone, their great chests rising and falling in slow, silent bellows-strokes. 

Then, out in the dark, a line tightens. A sliver of pierced slate drags across a stone notch. *Shhhk.*

Instantly, the camp is awake. By the pitch of the scrape, they know which spire is tilting, which chasm is widening, and therefore where the bedrock is about to tear open and bleed heat. 

They move out into the gloom before the dust of the collapse has even settled. In the dark, navigating by memory and the micro-tremors in their soles, they scramble down the fresh chutes of scree toward the dull red glow of the new fissure. The air there is thick with hot, dry sulfur—a mixture that would sear soft pink lungs, but their respiratory tracts are lined with a thick, waxy mucus that traps the particulates and absorbs the trace moisture steamed out of the mantle rock.

As the copper-and-ash lichen blooms across the hot stone, fractalizing in the sulfurous breath, the harvesters go to work. They use long, curved scrapers knapped from black glass, peeling the living, metallic crust from the rock while it is still warm and pliable, before it can cool and calcify into useless dust. They move with a frantic, spider-like grace, balanced over freshly opened abysses, their keratinous hands working inches from rock faces that are still snapping and spitting shards of superheated quartz. They pack the warm, copper-colored mash into pouches woven from the same pale belly-fungus they harvest from the deep, unreachable abysses using thousand-foot drop-lines.

This crust is their bread, their water, and their salt. Their gut flora is entirely chemoautotrophic—a dense, symbiotic cauldron of bacteria that breaks down the sulfurous minerals and heavy metals of the lichen, yielding metabolic energy and extracting tiny, tightly hoarded droplets of metabolic water. 

Because they never drink liquid water, they never urinate, and they do not sweat. To lose moisture here is to die within an hour. Their nostrils are narrow, baffled slits packed with dry, spiral turbinates that recapture every molecule of dampness from their exhaled breath, condensing it back into their throats. When they exhale, the air leaving their bodies is as bone-dry as the kiln-wind around them.

They are not primitives, though they have no fire and no metal. Fire requires oxygen this altitude cannot spare, and wood that has never existed here; metal requires smelting, when the earth beneath them already provides volcanic glass sharper than any steel. Their intelligence is entirely spatial and dynamic. A human looks at a mountain and sees a permanent wall; these people look at the five-mile basin and see a slow-motion sea of colliding icebergs. 

They build their herringbone shelters not as homes, but as temporary eddies in a solid current. When a basalt dike rises through the center of a circular cairn, shearing it in half, the builders do not mourn the ruin or try to repair it. The next time they pass that ridge, they simply dismantle the lower half, carry the flat slabs ten feet up the new cliff face, and key them back into the surviving upper wall, adapting the house to the new altitude.

Sometimes, the mountain takes one of them. A spire drops without warning; a fault snaps shut on a wedged ankle; a pocket of carbon dioxide vents silently into a shelter and puts a family to sleep forever. 

There is no soil to bury the dead, and no wood to burn them. Instead, when one of their kind dies, the survivors strip the body of its hornblende strikers, its obsidian blades, and its belt of braided fiber. They carry the corpse to the highest, most unstable needle of rock they can climb—a pinnacle already groaning and ticking with rising pressure. They wedge the dead one upright into a high crevice, locking the cleft heels and the horned fingers into the stone just as they stood in life.

There are dozens of them up there in the dark, high above the tension-lines. With no moisture and no bacteria in the dry, warm air to rot them, they do not decay. They simply cure, drying out until their hide is as hard as the gabbro and their ribcages are hollow resonant chambers. They stand watch on the tilting spires for years, coated in drifting silver dust, until the afternoon when the pressure finally peaks, the base of the needle pulverizes, and the dead come clattering down into the basin with the fresh scree—a dry, hollow music announcing that a new seam has opened, and it is time for the living to come down and harvest the light.
