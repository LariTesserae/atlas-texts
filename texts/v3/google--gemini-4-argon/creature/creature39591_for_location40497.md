To find what truly rules the interior of the stone chime, you have to look past the frantic, clattering surface of the moss and the birds. The swifts and the raptors are loud, and the pale salamanders are numerous, but they are merely the current. Beneath them, and woven through the human leavings, is the thing that acts as the stone’s memory, and the people who come up from the clouds to bargain with it.

First, there is the Anchor. 

If you were to press your ear against the three-foot-thick cushions of moss—ignoring the freezing water soaking your cheek and the deafening shriek of the wind-horn—you would hear a sound beneath the basalt’s bass drone. It is a slow, rhythmic *schluck-schluck*, like a boot being pulled from deep mud. 

The moss is not held to the sheer, wet rock by its own roots alone. It is stitched there. Running through the dark, anoxic peat at the base of the greenery is a vast, continuous lattice of rubbery, translucent tubes, thick as a man's thumb. This is the body of the Anchor. It is neither worm nor fungus, though it shares the traits of both, and there is only one of it in the entire monolith. It is a colonial organism, a single, sprawling nervous system and digestive tract that has grown over centuries to match the exact shape of the chime’s interior, like a lining inside a glove.

The Anchor feeds on the vertical bog. When a swift is clumsy and breaks its neck against a rib, or when a salamander freezes during a sudden shift in the thermal draft, the moss does not simply rot them. Slender, blind polyps—pale violet and tipped with rings of horn-like teeth—extrude from the mossy cushions, draw the carcass down into the dark, wet mat, and dissolve it. The nutrients are pumped through the translucent tubes from the sunny, wind-scoured eastern rib to the freezing, shadowed northern rib, keeping the whole impossibly lush ecosystem in balance. In return, the Anchor’s tough, fibrous tubing binds the moss and the fern-roots to the slick ironstone, preventing the whole garden from peeling away under its own waterlogged weight and plummeting three thousand feet into the clouds.

The Anchor has no eyes, but it has an exquisite sense of vibration. It feels the sixty-mile-an-hour punch of the raptors tearing through the air; it feels the pop of every mist-bladder reed; and above all, it feels the chord of the stone. When the wind shifts and the monolith’s hum drops a quarter-tone, the Anchor contracts, squeezing gallons of Peaty water out of the moss at once to lighten the load on the leeward ribs.

And it feels the Bell-Tuners when they climb into the cage.

The people who leave the willow baskets and carve the tallies do not live in the chime—nothing with lungs built for the valleys could sleep here for more than a night without their blood thickening to sludge and their chest filling with fluid. They live four thousand feet below, just under the permanent cloud-deck, on shelved villages clinging to the mountain’s unseen shoulders. 

To them, the monolith is the *Throat*. They never see the sun; they live in a perpetual, dripping twilight of white fog. They only know what the sky is doing, and what the seasons are bringing from the far side of the range, by the sound the Throat makes above them. The deep basalt chord travels down the rock of the mountain itself, vibrating through the soles of their feet, while the high, reedy note of the suspended ram's horn cuts through the three-thousand-foot cloud layer like a lighthouse beam. A shift in the horn's pitch means the high-altitude jet stream is dipping; a stutter in the bass drone means the thermal chimney is cooling, and a killing frost is hours away from dropping into the valleys.

So, three or four times a year, a pair of Tuners climbs out of the white blindness and into the blinding blue abyss.

You would barely recognize them as human when they haul themselves up through the porous chimney at the base, emerging dripping wet and steaming from the warm updraft into the freezing gale. They are built like barrels, with chests expanded by generations of thin air, though they are barely five feet tall. They wear nothing that flaps—a loose cloak in this wind would snap a spine or tear a man from his holds. Instead, they are sewn into tight skins of oiled marmot fur and laminated fish-bladder, smeared over with a thick, grey rind of mutton tallow and pine pitch to shed the sheets of condensing water. 

On their hands and knees, they wear straps of rawhide studded with the hooked beaks of the alpine raptors, allowing them to climb the vertical bog the same way the birds do—by sinking their points deep into the sponge of the moss and the body of the Anchor beneath it. They wear goggles of beaten copper with mere pinholes punched in the center, to save their eyes from the violent glare of the cloud-floor below and the ultraviolet burn of the thin air.

Because the noise inside the ribs is an unbroken roar of wind, water, horn, and bird-shriek, the Tuners have no use for voices here. When they are inside the chime, they communicate by pressing their foreheads—or their teeth, through their leather masks—directly against the wet black basalt. They tap out complex rhythms with heavy copper rings worn on their thumbs, sending sharp *clacks* through the iron-rich stone that the other climber can feel yards away through their own knees and elbows.

Their relationship with the chime is one of frantic, terrifying maintenance. 

The river stones in the willow baskets are not for warmth; the Tuners bring them up from the deep valley riverbeds five thousand feet below, carrying fifty pounds of dead weight into the death-zone, to *ballast* the garden. Where the moss grows too thick and threatens to tear loose, or where a rib is vibrating at a dangerous harmonic that might crack the frost-wedged basalt, the Tuners weave a basket into the fern roots and load it with river-stone. They are tuning the rock itself, adding weight to dampen the vibration of one rib, or chipping away moss to sharpen the note of another.

The tallies carved into the stone are not a count of days or visits. They are a ledger of pitches—a musical notation of the mountain’s voice over centuries, recording how the chord has drifted as the rock slowly erodes.

And the grease on those stones—thick, rancid yak butter mixed with crushed lowland beetles and bone-meal—is payment to the Anchor. 

When a Tuner hammers a fresh copper piton into the rock, or drives a bone wedge into a crevice, they inevitably pierce the translucent, rubbery lattice of the Anchor beneath the moss. The entire garden shudders when this happens. Millions of dark red liverworts curl up at once, exposing the black stone like a sudden intake of breath, and the pale salamanders drop from the rills like hailstones, driven mad by the chemical distress signal the Anchor releases into the water. If the Tuners do not pay, the Anchor will slowly digest the willow baskets, corrode the copper pitons with acidic secretions, and slough off the patch of moss the human is clinging to, dropping them into the updraft—which is strong enough to slow a bird, but will only tumble a man outward until he clears the rock and falls for three silent minutes into the silver floor below.

So the Tuners smear the heavy river-stones with fat and lowland rot—nutrients rich in phosphorus and salt that the updrafts can never carry high enough. As soon as a stone is set into its basket, the violet, blind polyps of the Anchor rise out of the green cushion, wrapping around the willow weavers, coating the greased rock in a glistening, digestive film. The garden relaxes. The liverworts unfurl. The moss tightens its grip around the new piton, sealing it in place better than mortar.

Then the Tuners crawl out onto the web of bleached hair and sinew—hanging suspended between the ribs with twenty-two thousand feet of blue nothingness below their heels and the wet, shrieking green cathedral above their faces. They scrape the rime of ice from the inside of the ram’s horn. They tighten a sinew stay with a bone turnbuckle until the horn’s note hits the exact, piercing fifth that will carry down through the clouds to the unseen villages. 

Before they slide back down into the dark, steaming throat of the chimney, they always take two things: a leather sack stuffed with the fat, pale salamanders—whose suction-cupped bellies are boiled down in the valleys to make a glue that never lets go—and a handful of the burst, plum-sized bladders from the horizontal reeds. Down in the permanent fog, those bladders are dried and burned by the village elders; they smell of ozone, high-altitude lightning, and the terrifying, sterile blue of a sky the people below will never see with their own eyes.
