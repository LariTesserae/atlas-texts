They do not call themselves after a tribe or a bloodline, because down in the unmortared dark of the middle tiers, bloodlines tangle and knot like the roots of the grease-bush. They identify themselves by their slope-cant, by the particular mineral salt of their home cistern, and by the sound of their neighbors’ chisels through the wall. 

To look at one of the Shelf-born in the street is to look at a walking cairn. Because of the five layers of greased felt, the draped aprons of russet ox-pelt, and the broad, sloped shoulders built up by padding that sheds the afternoon wet, they have no waist, no neck, and very little silhouette. They move with the "terrace-roll"—a wide-stanced, lock-kneed gait where the whole torso pendulums slightly from side to side to lift the heavy, hair-wrapped boots over the slate risers without engaging the small muscles of the calf. It is a gait that can be maintained for fourteen hours down and up a forty-degree incline without ever breaking a sweat. 

Here, sweat is a kind of mortal sin. To sweat is to wet your inner felt; to wet your inner felt is to freeze when you stop walking. Therefore, the highest virtue among them is *stillness of the blood*. 

Beneath the bundle of hides, they are built like the mountain’s own mortar: short, extraordinarily broad through the chest, with ribs sprung wide like barrel-hoops to house lungs the size of leather bellows. Their skin is the color of old tallow, except across the bridge of the nose and the high, flat cheekbones, where the thin air and the cold brass sunlight have burst the tiny vessels into a permanent, webby map of deep violet and plum. Their blood is so thick with red cells that when a slip of a bone-wedge splits a thumb, the droplet does not run; it stands up on the skin like a bead of black currant jam, slow and heavy, before crusting in the dry air. 

Their hands are scarcely human to a lowlander’s eye. The fingers are short and thick as wooden pegs, the nails horn-yellow and worn flat from generations of feeling for the *cleave*—the subtle, hair-thin change in temperature along a vein of quartz that tells a mason where the slate wants to part. Their eyes have an extra fold of heavy, greasy lid at the corners to narrow the aperture against the blinding glare of the afternoon fog-glow, and their irises are almost universally pale—grey, straw-colored, or the milky white of quartz—watching the world through a squint that looks like hostility, though it is only conservation.

***

Life is lived in two distinct temperatures: the *Grit* and the *Stifling*.

The Grit is the open air—the wind-baffled alleys, the fog-wet terraces, the quarries on the raw upper shelves where the mountain is still wild and unstacked. Out in the Grit, a person is nearly mute. You do not open your mouth to speak in the zero-degree wind if a tilt of the chin or a tap of a horn-spoon against your thigh will suffice. An entire complex grammar of clicks, whistles, and heel-scuffs exists for the open terraces: a double-click of the tongue to warn a herd-bull you are passing behind his hocks; a low, two-note flute through the teeth that carries down a chimney-vent to tell the household below that the afternoon cloud has reached the third tier and it is time to unstop the cistern-pipes.

The Stifling is the indoors. 

To drop through a low lintel-hole from an alley into a residence is to leave the mineral world and enter the belly of a beast. There are no windows. The walls are four feet thick, double-stacked with dry slate and packed between with dead moss, old wool-clippings, and wood-ash. The floor is frequently just a grille of split stone slabs laid directly over the byre where six or eight of the huge, russet-skirted oxen—they call them *khor*—stand chewing the cud of dried lichen-mash. 

The heat rises through the floor-slits: a thick, damp, ammoniacal warmth of eighty degrees, smelling fiercely of lanolin, wet horn, fermented moss, and centuries of oil-smoke. 

Inside the Stifling, the five layers of felt come off instantly. The people sit naked to the waist, their barrel-chests slick with a protective smear of rancid butter and soot that keeps the dry winter air from cracking their skin when they go back out. Here, in the low, corbelled vaults lit by a single floating wick in a dish of fat, the silence of the outside world breaks into a constant, low-voiced hum. 

They talk with their heads close together, knees touching, voices pitched to a gravelly purr that doesn't carry past the next dry-stone partition. They are compulsive finger-workers. No one sits in the dark with empty hands. While they talk—discussing the slow creep of a bulge in the lower retaining wall three tiers down, or the marriage-trade of a bull-calf, or the precise thickness of the frost-rime on the western gutters—their blunt fingers are ceaselessly twisting horsehair into boot-cord, scraping the black belly-lichen into digestible flakes with shards of white quartz, or polishing bone needles against the side of their own noses.

***

Their society has no kings, because a king requires a surplus that can be carried to a palace, and on the Escarpment, nothing is carried far unless it walks on four legs. You cannot march an army up a forty-degree staircase of loose slate where a single pulled keystone will send three hundred tons of terrace roaring down into the cloud-basin, taking the road and the invaders with it.

Instead, they are ruled by the *Plumb* and the *Drop*.

The keepers of the Plumb are the old women and men whose knees have finally calcified from sixty years of the terrace-roll. Unable to climb the steep risers to the upper quarries, they live in the middle tiers, carried from vault to vault in wickerwork panniers slung between two steady *khor*. These elders possess the *Ear*. 

Because the entire five-hundred-mile city is dry-laid—held together entirely by gravity, friction, and the precise wedge-angle of a billion hand-split stones—the mountain is never still. It settles. It breathes with the afternoon frost-heave and the morning thaw. A householder lying awake in the dark will hear a faint *tick* inside his bedroom wall, different from the *tink* of a neighbor’s chisel. It is the sound of a load-bearing slate three layers deep shearing under the weight of the twelve terraces above it.

In the morning, the elders of the Plumb are brought in. They press their flat, scarred cheekbones directly against the cold dry-stone wall, closing their pale eyes, and listen for hours. They can hear the stresses running through the quartz veins for fifty yards in any direction. When they speak, their word is absolute. If an elder says, *"The groin-vault under the fourth cistern is pinching; take down the three houses above it and re-lay the batter at six-in-ten before the autumn freeze,"* the families do not argue. They move their felt bundles, their butter-crocks, and their beasts into their neighbors' pens, and by noon, twenty men and women with bone-shod crowbars are methodically dismantling their own bedrooms stone by stone, passing the slabs from hand to hand in total silence. 

To refuse the Plumb is to become a *Talus-maker*—the worst curse in their language. A Talus-maker is one who lets the mountain revert to rubble.

The second law is the Drop—the water-right. 

Water is not drawn from wells; it is harvested by surface area. A wealthy household is not one with gold or land, for there is no soil and no metal; a wealthy household is one that maintains five hundred yards of wide-flanged condensing gutter on a windward-facing buttress where the afternoon cloud hits thickest. 

There is a class of boys and young girls—the smallest, lightest-boned of the children, whose ribs have not yet thickened into the adult barrel—who serve as the *Rime-Sweepers*. Every morning, an hour before the brassy sun clears the eastern peaks, while the city is still grey and murderously cold, these children crawl out along the dizzying, unrailed outer edges of the gutter-courses. Before the sun can sublime the night's hoarfrost directly back into the dry air, the children sweep it with brushes of stiff rock-fowl wing-feathers into the stone chutes, sending rivers of dry, tinkling ice-powder sliding down into the dark clay cisterns below. They work on ledges four inches wide above drops of four thousand feet, unroped, because a rope snags on the rough slate and pulls your balance outward. When one falls—which happens every few weeks along the five-hundred-mile face—there is no scream. They are taught from infancy that to open the mouth while falling is to burst the lungs; they go down into the white belly of the morning cloud curled into a tight, silent ball, like a dropped stone.

***

And yet, for all its rigor, their life is not grim. It is dense with small, greasy comforts.

They have a passion for riddles that take three days to answer. They brew a thick, milky beer from fermented lichen-jelly and butter, served scalding hot in bowls carved from *khor*-horn, which leaves a ring of warm fat around the drinker's mouth. They are connoisseurs of bird-song, though their birds do not sing; they breed the heavy grey rock-fowl not just for eggs and feather-down, but for the pitch of their chest-mutters. A household will spend twenty years selectively breeding a line of chimney-fowl until the forty birds roosting along their roof-lintel produce a specific, three-chord harmonic drone when the evening fog rolls in—a low, vibrating *voo-oom* that resonates down through the stone ceiling and lulls the children to sleep.

And they have the *White Season*, when the clouds fail.

For six weeks in mid-winter, the air grows so cold that the afternoon fog-bank freezes solid three thousand feet below the city and cannot climb the buttresses. The sky turns the color of bruised iron. The gutters dry up; the black lichen shrivels into brittle scabs that crackle underfoot. 

Then, the city seals itself. The outer alley-doors are walled up with loose slate and caulked with dung. The millions of terraces go empty. Above, in the blue-black sky, the giant vultures sail over a seemingly dead mountain range, where nothing moves for five hundred miles except the thin, greasy threads of yellow smoke rising from a million tiny roof-holes. 

Down in the dark, the entire population lives in the Stifling. For forty days, the city becomes a single, continuous subterranean burrow. They open the *creep-holes*—low, crawl-space tunnels connecting house to house, tier to tier, across miles of the vertical slope. Carrying little stone lamps fueled with hyrax-fat, young men and women crawl for days through the warm, soot-blackened labyrinth to visit kin five miles away and two thousand feet higher up, never once seeing the sky. 

In the vaults, beside the steaming flanks of the great woolly oxen, the bone-carvers work by touch, turning out tiny, intricate figurines of beasts and mountains from old knucklebones. The grandmothers recite the *Cleave-Songs*—epic poems that are actually five-thousand-year-old geological surveys, cataloging the exact angle of the slate-grain and the depth of the clay-pockets in every buttress from the southern salt-pans to the northern ice-wall.

When the cold finally breaks and the first afternoon cloud comes boiling up from the unseen world below—smelling of lowland mud and distant, rotting forests none of them have ever seen—the walls are taken down in an hour. 

Out they come onto the ledges, blinking their pale eyes in the blinding brass light, five million bulky, barrel-chested figures in greased felt. They do not cheer. They simply stand along the five hundred miles of dry-stone parapets, shoulder to shoulder with their great russet beasts, listening to the first billion droplets of fog begin to tick against the thirsty slate, while already, somewhere three tiers up, a bone-headed hammer goes *tink... tink... tink* against a fresh vein of quartz.
