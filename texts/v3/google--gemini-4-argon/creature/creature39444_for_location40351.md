If you watch the spiral of square niches for three or four cycles of the vent, you will realize that one of the ledges is not rock. 

It is a shoulder. 

When the heat spikes and the *shhh-tik* of the walls rises to its twenty-minute roar, a portion of the calcified crust just below the lowest ironwood beam detaches itself from the basalt. It does not startle or shake itself free; to shake is to waste salt and tear the hooks off the neighbor-plates. Instead, it slides upward with the slow, hydraulic inevitability of the arachnids, allowing the river of bristle-millipedes to part around its shins like dry grain around a mill-post.

What lives in the Throat is called the Miller by the goat-herders who occasionally find its cast-off work in the high screes a mile below, though the herders have never seen it, and there is no grain within thirty leagues. 

There is only one of it now, though the river-cobble cairn on the floor is packed around the dry, disjointed pelvises of four others that came before it, or perhaps were parts of it before the chimney narrowed to its current choke. It is roughly the shape of a man who has been cured in a smokehouse for a century and then armoured in old shingle-iron, though its proportions have been corrected by the vertical dark. Its legs are short, bowed outward like callipers, with heels that turn half-backward so the broad, horn-soled feet can wedge sideways into the flutes of the tube-worm reefs. Its arms are immense—long enough that when it hangs from the ironwood beams by its thumbs, its ankles rest in the niches five feet below. 

It is hard to say where the Miller’s skin ends and the Throat’s masonry begins. Whatever soft hide it was born with was surrendered to the sulfur decades ago. Its back, shoulders, and the crown of its long, flat skull are plated with living *silt-limpets* and small, blind *skree*-mites that it has allowed—or encouraged—to anchor in its own dense, leathery calluses. In return for the steady heat of its slow blood and the salt that weeps in microscopic crystals from its pores, the mites keep it clean of the boring-beetles that would otherwise tunnel into the marrow of its hips. Two great slate-backed arachnids, old females whose carapaces have gone silver with calcium deposits, ride permanently over its shoulder blades like pauldrons, their twenty legs braided peacefully through the fibrous ropes of muscle at the base of the Miller’s neck. When the Miller reaches out to work, the spiders shift their weight to counterbalance the leverage, their mandibles ticking softly against its ears.

The Miller does not have lungs anymore, at least not the wet, bellows-kind that would parboil in the vent-drafts. Its ribs have fused into a rigid barrel-vault to protect its dry inner core from the crushing tonnage of the chimney walls during a heat-surge. Below the ribs, where a belly should be, there is a deep, pleated cavity lined with tough, yellow-ochre membranes that look remarkably like the gill-books of the platter-spiders. Every ten minutes, the Miller leans its chest against a weeping crack in the rhyolite, seals the rim of its torso against the stone with a thick, waxy grease it secretes from its wrists, and inhales a slow, silent draft of raw hydrogen sulfide and iron-rich gas, letting the symbiotic bacteria in its gut-lining turn the poison into the slow, flameless grease that fuels its muscles.

Its face is rarely uncovered. When it is resting between the breaths of the mountain, it tucks its chin into the hollow of its collarbone and draws the two shoulder-spiders forward until their flat shells meet like the visor of a helm. When it opens them to look at its work during the twenty minutes of the noon sun-blade, there are no wet eyes in the sockets—moisture would boil away in an afternoon—only two wide, recessed pits of dark, heat-sensitive membrane that see the chamber not in brown and yellow, but in shades of violet, dull cherry, and blinding white warmth. Its mouth is a narrow, vertical slot bordered by four heavy plates of yellowed nail, evolved or carved to snip through spun basalt and the calcified necks of tube-worms without letting the swirling snow of chitin-dust into its throat.

Yet for all that the chamber has remade it in the image of a beetle, the Miller’s hands remain stubbornly, terribly human. The fingers are twice as thick as a blacksmith’s, the nails grown out into curved, iron-hard gouges, the skin gray and greasy as rubbed graphite—but the thumbs still oppose. They still remember geometry.

The Miller is a tender of the choke. Left to itself, the Throat would kill itself in fifty years. The calcified tiers of the tube-worms grow inward four inches a decade; without the chisel, the central shaft would seal shut, the twenty-minute gas vents would find no release, and the building pressure would blow the entire top of the ridge into the sky in a cloud of pulverized slate-bugs and sulfur ash. 

So the Miller moves on the twenty-minute clock. 

When the gas pocket deep in the mountain sighs shut and the temperature drops back to its baking equilibrium, the Miller unhooks its heels from the niche. It does not drop; the air is too thick with falling dust and the walls too crowded for falling. It descends by *swimming* through the crust. It slips its long, flat hands beneath the three-deep shingles of the platter-spiders—never breaking a leg, never crushing a spiracle, knowing the exact hydraulic pressure required to make a forty-pound arachnid unhook its claws and step sideways—and wedges its body into the gap. The creatures do not fight it. To them, the Miller is simply a warm, slow-moving outcrop of basalt that smells of old grease and familiar sulfur. The pale, hand-shaped filter-things in the crevices brush their cartilaginous fingers across its knees as it passes, harvesting the flake-skin from its shins.

It carries one tool: a bar of meteoric iron three feet long, octagonal, worn so smooth by ten thousand years of palms that the middle is pinched thin like a waist. When not in use, the bar hangs from a loop of sinew between its shoulder blades, buried under the living cloak of mites. 

With this bar, the Miller prunes the masonry. It works in the dark, guided by the purple heat-glow of the rock beneath the worm-crust. Where the tube-worms have built a shelf that catches too much falling dung—threatening to dam the shaft and start a smoldering grease-fire in the dry litter—the Miller wedges the octagonal tip of the iron into the seam between the worm-lime and the primary basalt. It does not swing the bar; there is no room to swing, and the impact would shatter the brittle shells of a thousand bystanders, setting off a chain-reaction of orange bioluminescent panic that would bring the great hunting centipedes boiling up from the floor. Instead, the Miller hooks one foot into a carved niche, braces its fused ribcage against the opposite wall of the narrow shaft, and becomes a jack-screw. It straightens its short, thick legs over the course of three minutes. *Shhh-tik.* A three-hundred-pound slab of living, calcified reef parts from the wall with a sound like tearing canvas and slides down the central chute into the mattress of husks below, where the shoveling beetles are already spiraling inward to receive it before it hits the bottom.

Then the Miller reaches into the raw, smoking scar on the rock, picks out three or four pale, soft-bodied worm-queens that were crushed by the pry-bar, and tucks them into the pleated pouch under its ribs to dissolve.

The niches, the ironwood beams, the river-cobbles, the copper bell—they are not monuments. They are an engine for regulating the draft, and the Miller is its mechanic. 

The river-cobbles at the bottom are a heat-sink and a baffle. River-stone, dense and water-forged, holds the spike of the vent’s heat longer than the porous volcanic rhyolite. During the quiet intervals, the Miller descends into the five-foot mattress of shells at the floor—wading waist-deep through the blind, jawed rivers of centipedes that slide harmlessly off its greased, horn-plated thighs—and rearranges the cobbles. If the eastern wall is cooling too fast, causing the slate-spiders there to go torpid and drop from their holds, the Miller shifts six cobbles to the west side of the cairn, tilting the invisible plume of the thermal updraft two degrees east.

And the copper bell is the barometer. 

The pseudoscorpions that pack its hollow interior are more sensitive to the deep magma-gases than anything else in the chimney. Three minutes before the deep pocket vents, before the rock even begins to hum, a trace of heavy, metallic vapor rises through the floor. The pseudoscorpions feel it and begin to unhook their pincers from one another, their velvet bodies swelling as they prepare to ride the thermal. As the living ball expands inside the copper hood, the outermost scorpions are pushed past the rim. The Miller, hanging directly below the lowest beam with its heat-pits turned upward, feels the first three scorpions drop onto its chest like warm thimbles of lead. 

That is the signal. Before the heat arrives, the Miller climbs the spiral of niches with its strange, unhurried spider-gait, reaching the three ironwood beams. From a hollow carved into the eastern socket of the top beam, it takes a flat cake of pressed chitin-dust and tallow—rendered from the winter-die-off of the floor beetles—and smears it across the black wood of the beams and into the braided basalt rope. Without the grease, the superheated dry air of the vent-cycle would eventually splinter even petrified ironwood into dry tinder. 

When the noon light comes—that twenty-minute razor of white sky that cuts the chimney in half—the Miller is almost always in the same place: wedged into the third niche below the copper bell, just outside the blade of sun, which would blind its heat-pits instantly. 

It does not look up at the blue crack of the mountain world. There is nothing up there for it; the cold air would freeze the wax in its breathing-pleats in five minutes, and the emptiness of the sky would terrify a mind that has spent two hundred years with every inch of its skin touching something that moves. Instead, it reaches one long, gray-plated arm into the edge of the white light, holding up a piece of newly shed platter-spider shell. It tilts the shell by fractions of an inch, using it as a dull mirror to bounce a weak, yellow smudge of sunlight into the dark crevices under the ironwood sockets, checking the basalt rope for fraying strands, checking the square niches for hairline cracks in the stone, before the twenty minutes are up, the mountain exhales from the bottom, and the walls begin to climb over themselves again.
