Because the baked clay conducts impact like cast iron, the largest animals in the basin are also the softest. 

Moving along the high ridges of the vaulted causeways is the **Spongy Walker**, an herbivore with the bulk of a bison but the silhouette of a squat, broad-shouldered sloth. A single hard hoof-strike on a hollow dome would ring through the galleries below and bring a million stinging, acid-spraying soldiers boiling out of the vents within seconds. Consequently, the Walker’s feet have evolved into huge, round cushions of fibrous fat and gel, nearly two feet across, wrapped in tough, dusty hide that gives like an under-inflated tire. When it takes a step, the foot squishes down, molding silently over the sharp flanges and honeycombed ridges of the masonry, distributing its immense weight across dozens of square inches before peeling up again without a sound.

They do not graze; they tap. A Walker will lumber up to the swollen, silvery-green trunk of a giant caudiciform tree, brace its wide chest against the cool bark, and deploy its lower incisors. These two teeth are forward-pointing, heavily enameled gouges, hollow down the center like hypodermic needles. With a slow, hydraulic flex of its thick neck, the animal drives the teeth through the waxy rind of the tree—a dull, wet *crunch* that is the loudest sound it ever makes—and simply stands there for hours, eyes half-shut against the yellow glare, as the tree’s pressurized, mucilaginous sap wells up through the channels in its teeth and trickles down its throat. When it finally pulls away, mason wasps are already hovering at its chin to drink the droplets from its whiskers, while others swarm the bleeding wound on the tree, plastering the puncture with red clay to keep the moisture for their own hives.

To survive the masonry is to negotiate with its builders. Beneath the skin of the clay, where the light never reaches and the air is thick with carbon dioxide and the smell of fermenting wood-pulp, live the **Grout-Worms**. They are not worms at all, but legless, blind amphibians the length of a man's arm and the thickness of a wrist. Their skin is constantly slick with a specialized, sweet-smelling mucus laced with pheromones that mimic the scent of a termite queen’s egg-casing. 

The Grout-Worm does not dig its own burrows; it swims through the wet, freshly chewed clay of the active construction zones, or slides effortlessly along the polished floors of the oldest highways. When the blind worker termites encounter one, they do not attack. Compelled by the chemical mimicry, they pause to groom the amphibian, scraping the dead skin from its flanks and daubing it with nutrient-rich saliva, which the worm absorbs directly through its pores. In return, the worm eats the parasitic phorid flies and invasive beetle larvae that slip through the city’s defenses, plucking them from the darkness with a short, sticky tongue. They are living mortar-Joints; sometimes, when a major support pillar cracks under the shifting weight of the upper tiers, three or four large Grout-Worms will wedge themselves into the fault line, inflating their lungs to brace the masonry until the workers can pack new mud around them, eventually slipping free just before the clay sets hard.

In the vertical slums—the narrow, pitch-black canyons between the towers where the heat collects and the air barely moves—the hunters do not use eyes or ears. They use their ribs.

The **Stridulators** (though they make no sound) are flattened, arachnid-like predators the size of dinner plates, colored the exact matte, dusty ochre of century-old clay. They spend their lives pressed completely flat against the sheer walls of the chimneys. Their eight legs are splayed wide, the tips of their tarsi wedged into microscopic pores in the masonry. They are listening to the city’s bones. To a creature tuned to substrate vibrations, the metropolis is a roaring, three-dimensional map of telemetry: the rhythmic, gritty *scritch-scritch* of ten thousand mandibles expanding a fungal cavern forty feet down; the heavy, slow *thrum* of a Spongy Walker shifting its weight three towers away; the frantic, high-frequency flutter of a trapped moth in a ventilation shaft. 

The predator waits for the specific, irregular tremor of a solitary vertebrate—a gecko, a pocket-rodent, or one of the long-bodied viverrids—creeping along the canyon floor. When the prey passes directly beneath, the hunter does not pounce. It simply lets go of the wall, dropping like a flat piece of broken pottery through the still air, folding its heavily spined front legs around the victim’s neck before its own weight can hit the ground and alert the neighbors.

Even the birds have given up the sky. The rising thermal engine above the basin is too dry, too turbulent, and too full of choking dust and fungal spores for soaring. Instead, the **Flue-Swifts** live *inside* the breath of the city. They are tiny, nearly featherless birds, covered instead in a dense, velvety down that resists caking with mud and dust. Their wings are short, broad paddles of stiff cartilage and skin, more like the flippers of a penguin than the wings of a swallow. 

They nest in the high, blisteringly hot necks of the thirty-foot cooling towers, gluing their shallow saucers of plant fiber right above the main exhaust shafts. To feed, they drop headfirst into the darkness of the chimney, riding the steady, humid updrafts like a hawk on a ridge lift. Down in the pitch dark, where the air smells of mushrooms and formic acid, they navigate by a soft, tongue-clicking echolocation—a sound so high and dry it blends seamlessly into the background hiss of the insects' mandibles. They hover in the rising column of hot air, snapping up the winged reproductive alates—the young princes and princesses of the termites and ants—as the colonies launch them up the flues toward the sky. A swift might spend its entire life inside a single tower system, rising and falling on the colony’s exhalations, only poking its dusty head out of the lip of the chimney at dusk to blink at the burning rust-colored horizon.

And deeper still, down where the oldest, petrified mud of the first millennia has been compressed by the weight of the city into a kind of biological sedimentary rock, lies the **Basal Mat**. 

This is the true ruler of the basin, though no surface animal ever sees it. It is a continuous, subterranean web of symbiotic fungi—originally cultivated by the termites in million-year-old gardens, now grown together into a single, continent-spanning mycelium that threads through the foundational masonry. It is the Mat that regulates the city. When a district of towers grows too heavy and begins to sink toward the deep water table, the fungus thickens its hyphae, calcifying them with minerals drawn from the groundwater to form stonelike subterranean arches. When a quadrant becomes too hot, the fungus alters the chemical signals in the fruiting bodies it feeds to the termites, compelling the blind workers miles above to chew wider vents into the sun-facing walls, changing the angle of the draft. 

The insects believe they are farming the fungus. The mammals and reptiles believe they are hunting through a dead landscape of mud. In truth, the entire basin is a single, sedentary body—a vast, breathing carapace of baked red earth, continuously repaired by its own circulating cells, drinking from the deep dark below, and exhaling its dry, spore-laden breath into the yellow sky.
