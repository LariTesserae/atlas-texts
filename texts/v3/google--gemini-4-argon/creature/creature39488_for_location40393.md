At first, if you were to stand in the center of the dish, you would think the chamber’s largest inhabitants were simply parts of the floor that had not yet finished melting. 

There are three of them in the room. There have been three of them for several thousand years. If a fourth is ever born, it will be because one of the three has begun the decade-long process of dissolving back into the bryophyte mats. 

They have no name for themselves, because they have no language that separates *self* from *room*, though if they were to be categorized by an outside eye, they might be called the Listeners, or the Balancers. Once, in an epoch when the mountain still had jagged peaks and the artifacts were newly sealed into the rock, their ancestors were likely high-altitude burrowers—marmot-like or badger-like things that dug down toward the unnatural warmth as the ice ages came on. 

Now, they are immense, boneless-seeming mounds of pale, translucent tallow, each the size of an overturned dory. 

A Balancer has no eyes, not even the vestigial sunken pits of the ribbon-amphibians. Its head is merely the narrower end of its teardrop bulk, terminating in a wide, muscular pad of whiskered flesh that stays pressed to the wet soapstone floor. Its limbs have retreated into its flanks, reduced to four broad, cartilaginous paddles used to lever its seven hundred pounds through the millimeter-deep films of water at a pace of roughly six feet an hour. It moves like a glacier of warm lard, sliding over the black-and-ochre mosses without crushing them, lubricated by a thick, celery-scented mucus that it secretes from its belly.

What it has instead of eyes, and instead of legs, is a back covered in ears.

Not ears like a mammal’s pinnae, but forty or fifty deep, fluted craters arranged across its dorsal ridge, each lined with rings of erectile, velvet-skinned cartilage and taut, drum-like membranes. When a Balancer is resting—which is almost always—these craters dilate and contract independently, swiveling slightly in the absolute dark like a bank of blind anemones. 

They do not merely hear the roaring darkness of the abbey-nave; they *see* the room’s geometry in the interference patterns of the noise. 

To a Balancer, the chamber is not dark at all. It is a blindingly complex, constantly shifting sculpture of standing waves. When the melon-throated amphibians beneath the hovering torus boom, the low-frequency pulse rolls out across the dish, strikes the tallow-smooth walls, reflects off the heavy suede-like trunks of the heat-drinking trees, and returns to the Balancer’s dorsal craters. The creature’s brain—which is massive, flat, and spread out like a saddle beneath its acoustic craters—reads the phase-shifts and delays of every echo. It knows the exact thickness of the wet moss on the northern wall by the way the moss dampens the twenty-minute metallic trills of the pylon-insects. It knows where the soft-shelled, terrier-sized grazers are clustering by the shadow their heavy bodies cast through the clicking of their own mandibles.

The Balancers are the shepherds of this acoustic equilibrium, though their shepherding looks entirely like digestion and singing.

Because the room is small and sealed, the ecology is always in danger of a local excess. Left alone, a mat of bryophytes might grow too thick around the base of the needle-pylons, insulating the stone and starving the rest of the chamber of the thirty-degree bleed. Or a population of clicking grazers might strip a suede-tree bare, exposing the ancient rock and creating a hard acoustic "bright spot" that ruins the delicate echo-landscape the smaller predators rely on to lull their prey.

When an imbalance begins to form, the nearest Balancer feels it as an itch—a dissonance in the room’s chord. 

Over the course of three or four days, the creature will slide its great, pale bulk across the basin toward the dissonance. As it goes, the smaller life does not flee. The blind grazers simply climb up its slick, gently sloping flanks, scraping harmlessly at the old mucus on its skin, riding the slow mountain of flesh for an hour before sliding down the other side. Ribbon-amphibians slither beneath its belly to soak in the ninety-eight-degree heat of its underside. 

When the Balancer reaches the site of the trouble—say, a thicket of dish-leaved flora that has grown too greedily into the invisible force-lines of the hovering torus, blocking the sheet-water from splashing evenly onto the floor—it does not tear the plants down. It has no teeth for tearing. Instead, it settles its bulk against the fleshy trunks and opens its respiratory spiracles.

Internally, the Balancer is mostly lung and resonant cavity. It inhales the heavy, geosmin-thick air, holds it in three sequential bladders, and begins to hum.

It is a sound you would not hear with your ears so much as feel in the liquid of your eyeballs and the marrow of your shins. The Balancer matches the exact resonant frequency of the plant’s water-heavy cellular walls—and then shifts the pitch down by a fraction of a hertz. 

For forty hours, the pale mound sits in the dark, vibrating. The sound is completely swallowed by the general roar of the room, invisible to the untrained ear beneath the trill of the insects and the *plip-plop* of the torus-water. But within three feet of the Balancer’s snout, the air is a slow sonic hammer. Gradually, the over-ambitious branches of the suede-tree lose their turgor. Their cells lose the ability to drink the artifact’s field. The heavy, blind leaves droop, soften, and liquefy into a rich, warm slurry that the Balancer then sucks up through its wide, muscular mouth-pad, filtering out the nutrients and excreting a flat, sterile silt that smooths the floor back into its proper, dish-like curve.

Yet this is only their maintenance of the flesh. Their stranger relationship is with the artifacts themselves.

The five or six structures in the room are unimaginably durable, built by a physics that ignores time, yet they are not entirely independent of the medium they sit in. Their fields interact with the rock, the water, and the dense, wet biomass that crowds them. Every few months, the slow, unfathomable computation running between the hovering torus at the center and the leaning needles at the north wall hits a snag—a micro-fluctuation caused by the buildup of mineralized water on the stone pedestals, or the sheer dielectric density of millions of wet, crawling bodies interrupting the field-lines.

When this happens, the ozone smell sharpens. The thirty-degree warmth wavers by half a degree. The sheet of condensing water around the torus stutters, falling in jagged, irregular splashes rather than a smooth curtain.

In the dark, all three Balancers stop whatever they are doing.

They do not move toward each other, nor do they touch the machines. Instead, they position themselves at three precise points along the perimeter of the empty central vault, their locations determined to the millimeter by the geometry of the soapstone dome overhead. They press their whiskered snouts into the millimeter-deep water films, anchoring themselves, and they dilate every one of their dorsal craters to their widest extent.

First, they silence the room. 

They do this by emitting a sharp, phase-inverted pulse—an anti-sound tailored to the exact frequencies of the amphibians’ throat-sacs and the insects’ metallic wings. It takes ten minutes for the cancellation wave to build, bouncing off the tallow walls until the air itself seems to thicken and resist vibration. The insects inside the pylons go still, confused by the sudden deadness of the air. The amphibians deflate their melons and sink into the warm puddles. The terrier-grazers freeze, their mandibles half-open.

For the first time in months, the abbey-nave goes quiet. Only the *plip-plop* of the stuttering torus-water remains.

Then, the three Balancers become an acoustic lens.

They have no idea what the artifacts are computing, nor that there is an "outside" where the air is thin and freezing, nor that the sun exists. To them, the artifacts are simply the Hearts—the things that must beat smoothly if the Warm Wet is to continue. 

One Balancer—the oldest, whose skin is thickest with scar-tissue from centuries of grazing crustaceans—begins to sing a pure, sine-wave tone that mimics the healthy hum of the northern pylons. Across the eighty paces of empty, pitch-black air, the second Balancer catches that tone in its dorsal craters, shifts the harmonic to match the magnetic bleed of a half-sunk way-shrine in the western wall, and sends it back. The third Balancer catches both, adds a sub-bass thrum that vibrates the water films on the floor into a nationwide grid of crisp Faraday ripples, and focuses the combined chord directly at the invisible boundary field of the hovering torus.

In the center of the room, the empty air becomes solid with sound. If you were standing there, the pressure waves would gently push you out of the way, nudging you toward the walls like a cork on a swell. The water sheets falling from the torus’s force-field bend inward, vibrating into a fine, warm mist that hangs suspended in the intersecting acoustic beams. 

The sound-bridge holds for three hours, or six, or twelve. The three pale mounds sit in the absolute black, their bodies trembling with the effort of projecting such a massive, precise architecture of pressure across the vault, their mucus boiling slightly on their flanks from the metabolic heat. Through the water-mist and the ionized air, the acoustic density gives the artifacts’ faltering fields the conductancethey need to re-synchronize. 

There is a faint, non-physical *click*—a sudden drop in the ozone tang—and the torus’s field snaps back into frictionless perfection. The mist collapses at once into a smooth, continuous sheet of water. The temperature steadies. 

The Balancers let their dorsal craters slacken. The anti-sound drops away.

For a few minutes, the silence holds, broken only by the steady, soothing splash of the newly balanced water-curtain. Then, tentatively, a single ribbon-amphibian beneath the pedestal inflates its throat and lets out a wet, hollow *boom*. Inside the northern needles, a few thumb-sized insects test their wings with a rusty scrape that soon catches on among their neighbors, building back toward their twenty-minute crescendo. The terrier-grazers resume their clicking march across the suede-trees, and the three great, eyeless mounds slowly sink flat against the soapstone, letting the warm water creep over their snouts as they go back to listening to the dark.
