To live in Spindle-Hollow is to be constantly, intimately grease-proofed. 

The human inhabitants—who call themselves "Hollowers," though the word sounds more like a soft cough in their throat-heavy dialect—are built like the dwarf-birches they prune: barrel-chested, thick-jointed, and slightly bowed against the perpetual western draft. Generations at this altitude have given them blood as rich and dark as beet-broth. It shows in their faces, which are broad and pale as dough, save for a permanent, startlingly violet flush across the cheekbones and the bridge of the nose—a map of burst capillaries caused by the thin air and the relentless forty-degree damp.

You rarely see their skin, however. Outside the slate rowhouses, a Hollower is an upright bundle of tallow-soaked shearling, layered wool, and heavy canvas cured with boiled linseed oil and beeswax until it has the stiffness and dull amber sheen of an old fingernail. They do not walk so much as they lever themselves along the slick boardwalks, their knees slightly bent, their weight settled low into thick-soled clogs carved from iron-birch root and shod with strips of vulcanized rubber salvaged from old compressor gaskets. Iron nails in a boot-heel are forbidden on the upper gantries; a single spark can do strange things when the mist is thick with bog-gas and static.

Because the peat and the fog swallow sound ten feet from the mouth, the people of Spindle-Hollow have largely abandoned the habit of raising their voices. Shouting here feels obscene, like tearing a wet rag, and it accomplishes nothing—the mountain simply eats the noise. Instead, they communicate across the catwalks by rapping their brass pruning-hooks or bronze wrenches against the copper conduits and guy-wires. The metal grid of the town is an enormous, resonant spiderweb. A gantry-walker fifty feet up in the pewter murk can tap a three-beat cadence on a high-pressure air line, and the miller three terraces down will feel the *clink-clink-clack* in the palm of his glove where it rests on a brass intake valve. They have a whole vocabulary of knocks: warnings for a surge of violet ground-fire, calls for fresh tallow-pots, the rapid, stuttering rattle that means a Savonius rotor has thrown a pin and is about to shear its rocker-arm.

When they do speak face-to-face in the narrow alleys, they lean in until the rims of their stiff, basin-shaped sou’wester hats touch, forming a little shelter of oiled canvas to keep the mist off their chins. Their speech is low, rapid, and clicking, full of consonants that mimic the ratchet-wheels and valve- checks of the mills.

Their lives are governed by two imperatives: keeping the moss out of the gears, and keeping the static out of their bones.

Every Hollower wears a "grounding tail"—a six-inch braid of frayed copper wire bolted to the heel of their left clog, which drags and whispers behind them over the wet stones and iron grates. Without it, a person walking through the moving cloud-banks builds up a charge so heavy that reaching for a brass doorknob results in a blue snap strong enough to numb the arm to the shoulder. Even with the grounding tails, the people are mildly conductive. In the dark, cramped parlors of the rowhouses, when they peel off three layers of damp wool by the peat-stoves, their hair stands out in pale, floating halos, and their knuckles crackle with tiny violet needles that smell of crushed garlic and rain.

This constant exposure to the weak, cold current has given rise to the town’s most peculiar trade: the *Spark-Eaters*, or *Reed-Tuners*, who maintain the miles of condensers and pneumatic lines. They are usually older women whose fingers have lost their fat to age and cold, leaving dry, calloused bone-hooks. They do not use voltmeters; glass dials fog up and rust out within a month. Instead, a Tuner walks the lines with her left glove off, running the back of her scarred index finger an inch away from the sweating copper pipes. She reads the health of the municipal machine by the way the fine hairs on her knuckle pull toward the metal, and by the taste that rises in her back teeth—coppery for a clean flow, sour as buttermilk when a bladder-fern has tapped the line and is leeching the current three hundred yards away in the fog.

And then there are the things that live in the gaps between the iron and the moss, which are neither entirely animal nor entirely separate from the town.

The stilt-hounds that pick through the terraced gardens are only half-wild. They were bred centuries ago from lowland sighthounds, though anything resembling a dog has long since been winnowed out of them by the High Slump. Stand near a slaughter-shed or a fern-press, and three or four of them will materialize out of the grey vapor like walking crutches. They stand four feet at the shoulder on legs no thicker than broomsticks, their joints knobby and calloused, their skin a greased, hairless slate-blue that matches the wet rock. They do not bark; they have no lungs for it in the thin air. When they beg from the scraper-crews, they make a wet, rhythmic *clacking* with their long jaws and pivot their immense, bat-like ears toward the worker’s chest, listening to the beat of the human’s heart to judge their mood. The Hollowers tolerate them because a stilt-hound can hear the high-pitched whine of a dry bearing half a mile away through the muffling peat, and will stand beneath the failing tower, shivering and pointing its long snout straight up into the mist, waiting for a maintenance crew to come and reward it with a lump of rancid mutton tallow.

Higher up, inside the warm, oil-smelling dark of the compression sheds and the attic-lofts where the great leather bellows wheeze day and night, live the *bellows-newts*. They are blind, heavy-bodied amphibians the length of a man’s forearm, with skin that looks and feels like wet chamois leather. They hate the cold mist outside, congregating instead around the kinetic joints where friction makes the iron warm. A machinist opening a gearbox to clear out strangle-moss will often find six or seven of them curled in a slick, pale knot around the main housing, soaking up the forty-five-degree heat of the turning axle. The machinists never kill them; the newts secrete a thick, mucilaginous slime from their bellies that happens to be the finest anti-corrosive lubricant in the basin. A good mill-hand keeps a scrap of dry crust in his pocket to coax a newt onto a squealing brass bushing.

Behind the slate walls, domestic life is vertical, warm, and intensely crowded. Because the ground outside is three inches of freezing sponge, the ground floors of the rowhouses are given over entirely to storage, peat-drying, and the iron accumulator tanks that store compressed air for the household tools. The families live on the second, third, and fourth stories, connected by spiral iron stairs as narrow as chimney flues. 

A Hollower kitchen smells overwhelmingly of boiled roots, hot tallow, and the sharp, medicinal tang of steeping juniper needles. Everything in the house is driven by a thumb-thick brass air-pipe that comes through the wall from the street main. They have no open fires—chimneys do not draw well under the heavy lid of the basin’s mist, pushing smoke back down into the rooms—so they cook over enclosed catalytic grates fueled by bog-gas, and turn their roasting spits, butter-churns, and sewing-lathes with tiny, whistling pneumatic turbines attached to the wall-valves. Every home has a low, constant *hiss* in the floorboards, like a cat sleeping under the rug.

At night—which is merely when the luminous lead-grey of the sky deepens to the color of an iron skillet—the whole town draws inward. The gangs come down from the brass rotors, their oilskins glittering with beaded grease-damp, their faces violet and numb. They scrape the black, nitrous slime of dead bladder-ferns from their clogs at the threshold. Inside, behind thick shutters padded with felted strangle-moss to keep out the draft, they sit elbow-to-elbow on benches carved from warped iron-birch. They drink *sump-tea*—a black, salty infusion of roasted liverwort and dried mist-skate wing—that warms the belly like hot gravel. 

And while they sit in the amber glow of their gas-globes, mending copper mesh or carving new teeth for wooden cogs, the entire four-story house trembles almost imperceptibly around them. Through the slate walls, through the soles of their stockings, they can feel the slow, heavy *thump-shuck, thump-shuck* of the hillside rocker-arms fifty yards away, and the endless, patient vibration of ten thousand iron masts drinking the wind in the dark.
