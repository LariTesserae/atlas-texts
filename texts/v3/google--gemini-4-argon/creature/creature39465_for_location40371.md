If you stand by the rusted narrow-gauge tracks and look down the valley for twenty minutes, you will swear nothing has changed. If you look for three hours, you will realize that one of the green-upholstered boulders thirty yards to your left is four feet closer to the transformer shack than it used to be.

It is not a boulder. Or rather, it is no longer just a boulder. 

To find what lives here, you first have to understand what the sound does to the valley floor. The intake towers do not rattle the moss; the moss is too thick, too heavy with frozen water and compressed peat to flutter. Instead, the sound *organizes* it. Between the ten great silos, the low-frequency roar bounces off the parallel granite cliffs and sets up an invisible architecture of standing acoustic waves. There are troughs where the air pressure drops, and nodes where the vibration is so dense it makes your eyeballs itch and blurs your vision. If you look closely at the frost-tipped cushions of *Racomitrium* and *Sphagnum*, you can see the geometry of the noise printed directly into the velvet: faint, sweeping corduroy ridges and vast, thirty-yard concentric rings where the spore-capsules have been vibrated into alignment, like iron filings over a magnet.

Nothing with eardrums or a hollow lung can survive that pressure for long. The air inside a chest cavity turns into a battering ram. 

So the things that live here have neither lungs nor ears. They are solid all the way through, and they use the sound the way a sailboat uses the wind.

Down in the absolute dark at the bottom of the fifteen-foot moss mattress, where the dead lower layers compress into sour, freezing peat, the rock is not dry. The acoustic humming of the bedrock—transmitted straight down from the concrete pads of the silos—forces ground-moisture and heavy sulfite vapors up through the hairline fractures of the fault. Where those mineral seeps meet the bottom of the peat, something grows that is neither entirely fungus nor entirely animal. 

If you were to dig twelve feet down through the frozen green sponge—using a crowbar and a saw, because a shovel won’t cut the matted rhizoids—you would hit a layer that feels like cold, wet bacon rind. 

It is a colonial myxomycete, a massive, cold-adapted slime mold that has abandoned the microscopic scale. The miners who originally laid the narrow-gauge track seventy years ago called it *shaft-tallow*, when they found pale, greasy ribbons of it weeping out of the lower granite cleavages. Left alone in the dark, fed on sulfur-oxidizing bacteria, rotting peat, and the constant, cellular massage of the hundred-decibel hum, the tallow did not die off. It thickened. It cross-linked its proteins with the silica and iron dust settling out of the mine exhaust. It became gristle.

In its youth, a piece of this organism is no bigger than a boot, a flat, blind slug of dense grey-white lard that creeps through the sub-moss caves at the rate of an inch an hour, grazing on ice-crawlers, springtails, and white fungal threads. It has no brain, only a hydrostatic mesh of tough, fibrous tubes pulsing at the exact resonant frequency of the nearest intake tower. It doesn't need to pump its own fluids; the thirty-foot axial fans do the pumping for it. The roar of the turbines drives its metabolism. If the fans ever shut off, the thing would simply stop, freezing into inert rubber within the hour.

As it grows, however, it rises. And as it rises into the upper layers of the *Sphagnum*, it takes the valley with it.

The organism is sticky, extruding a slow, clear mucilage that freezes into an elastic glaze when it nears the cold surface. The moss does not die when the tallow pushes up beneath it; the moss simply roots into the tough, leathery rind of the creature’s back as easily as it roots into granite or Corten steel. Over thirty or forty years, a dome of live, frost-crisp *Polytrichum* and rust-colored liverworts forms over the mass, five feet high and eight feet wide, completely indistinguishable from the upholstered talus boulders that litter the trench. 

Only when it needs iron or heat does it show itself.

If you walk down the pencil-thin lines of the rail track, you will notice places where the moss seems to have been torn open in a slow, ragged furrow, exposing the dark brown peat underneath like a fresh plow-trench in frozen soil. Follow that furrow for fifty yards and you will find one of the great mounds resting against the base of a galvanized power pole, or pressed flat against the bolted steel plates of a transformer shack.

They are drawn to the sixty-hertz hum of the step-down transformers, which cuts across the wider acoustic roar of the fans like a bright, warm scent. Three or four of the boulders will congregate around a single steel shack, crowding it, leaning their massive, moss-velvet shoulders against the vibrating metal until the steel plates groan inward. From beneath the skirt of green moss at the base of the mound, a pale, translucent apron of dense gel—stiff as cartilage and cold as river ice—extrudes itself over the concrete pad and up the wall. 

It isn't eating the steel for calories; it is harvesting the oxidation and the heat. When a mound pulls away from a transformer box after a winter spent pressed against it, the rusted iron plates have been stripped down to bright, pitted grey metal, etched with thousands of microscopic, parallel grooves, and the moss on the creature's back will have flushed a deep, rusty crimson from the iron-rich exhaust of its host.

Sometimes, they follow the tracks. The narrow-gauge rail is a tuning fork five miles long. When the maintenance crew five miles up at the trench-head sends an automated battery-cart down the line once every six months to check the fan bearings, the vibration travels through the rusted steel rails twenty minutes ahead of the wheels. 

If you are standing on the valley floor when the rail-line begins to sing that secondary, metallic note, you will see a subtle, disturbing change in the landscape. 

Along a two-mile stretch of the track, twenty or thirty green-upholstered boulders will slowly tilt. A rim of frost snaps and tinkles—a tiny sound, instantly swallowed by the roar of the silos. The mounds do not have eyes, so they do not look toward the cart. Instead, long, stiff filaments of white cartilage, thick as telegraph wire and coated in clear rime, slowly telescope out from beneath the overhanging cushions of olive-green moss. These pale, frost-sheathed feelers reach out across the three-foot gap and lay their tips directly onto the steel rail, drinking the sharp, stuttering vibration of the approaching wheels.

If the cart hits one of the mounds that has crept too far over the iron ties, there is no crash. The cart simply bogs down, its small flanged wheels spinning uselessly in a bank of frozen green velvet and dense, rubbery tallow that yields just enough to swallow the axle and then holds it fast. 

There are four such carts along the five-mile line now. You can tell them from the real boulders because their shapes are still boxy, like horse-drawn carriages buried under twelve inches of bronze and emerald plush. The moss on them is very healthy. And if you put your gloved hand down on the green hump of an old cart, beneath the crisp, three-inch crust of frost, you can feel the tallow inside the battery housing—warmed by the long-dead lead-acid cells and the endless, chest-shaking thunder of the silos—slowly, rhythmically squeezing the iron frame in the dark.
