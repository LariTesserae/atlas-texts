If you stand very still at the edge of a gravel-sluice, letting the alkaline steam coat your eyelashes until you are squinting through milky tears, you will eventually realize that the pile of wet, gray-green mortar four paces to your left is breathing.

Wait longer, and it will stand up.

They are called the *trowel-backed*, or sometimes just the *setters*, though there is no one in the basin to use those names unless a man falls in from the rim. From a distance of ten yards—which is as far as the cresset-light reaches—they look like exceptionally tall, narrow men who have been boiled in tallow and then rolled in quarry-dust. They are six and a half feet from heel to crown, terribly thin through the ribs, with long, ropy arms that hang down past their knees. 

They do not wear clothes. Clothes would snag on the raw cedar bark or get sliced to ribbons on the fresh slate. Instead, they wear a rind. A setter’s skin exudes a thick, sebaceous oil that smells faintly of mutton-grease and bitter resin. As they work, the airborne lime-dust, coal-soot, and fine slate-grit settle into this oil, curing on their bodies into a flexible, segmented crust. Their knees, elbows, and shoulders are encased in heavy gray calluses as hard as horn, deeply scored with white scratches from rubbing against the narrow alley walls. 

Their hands are the worst thing about them. They have no fingernails, and their fingers have fused into two broad, flat paddles on each hand—bone-hard, spade-shaped mittens of gray cartilage. With the left paddle, they scoop up a ten-pound gob of wet mortar from a trough; with the right, they slap it into the dry-laid slate joints and draw the edge of their palm across it with a wet, ringing *shhhk* that leaves the seam flush and smooth. They never use tools. They *are* the tools. When a piece of slate is too wide for a gap, a setter does not reach for a hammer; he raises his elbow—where the joint-bone grows out into a blunt, calcified spike—and brings it down on the stone with a single, snapping whip of his torso, splitting the shale cleanly along the grain.

They have faces, though they have very little use for them. Their eyes are small, sunken beads the color of tarnished pewter, protected from the stinging lime-steam by three layers of translucent, sideways-blinking membranes. They have no external ears—ears would only be torn off in the narrows, and besides, the noise in the valley is too monstrous to be sorted out by an eardrum. Instead, they hear through their feet. Their soles are broad, flat pads of gray flesh, three inches thick and tough as vulcanized rubber, impervious to the razor-sharp slate. Through those soles, they feel the thrum of the treadwheels, the distant *thud* of a beast falling into a cistern, and the creak of a cedar trestle that is taking too much weight.

You will never hear a setter speak. Their throats are choked with soot and coated in dried lime-mucus. When four or five of them must lift a green cedar log into its notch on a corbelled dome, they coordinate by striking their chests with their flat, horn-plated wrists. *Thock. Thock. Thock-thock.* The sound is hollow and wooden, indistinguishable from the knocking of the scaffolding poles in the draft. 

They do not sleep in beds, or in huts, or even on the ground. To lie down in the basin is to be mortared over by a blind beast or buried under five tons of quarry-spoil when a chute opens in the dark. When a setter’s muscles finally seize from the cold and the labor, he climbs forty feet up into the wet cedar scaffolding, finds a joint where three poles cross, and hooks his long, prehensile toes over a rawhide lashing. Then he lets his body hang upside down, or folds himself in half over a beam like a wet sack of meal, his long arms dangling in the soot-greasy air. Up there, the six-legged leather-chewers scurry right over his back, rasping at the rawhide bindings between his feet, sometimes spitting a gob of bone-glue onto his calf by mistake. The setter never twitches. He hangs there in the chemical steam for three or four hours, his breathing slow and rattling, while the grease from the cressets drips onto his spine.

They have no children, or none that look like children. Beneath the current working-floor—down in the first tier of buried cellars, where the cressets have just recently choked out and the air is ninety degrees and thick with the stinging heat of ten thousand tons of curing quicklime—there are the *slake-cradles*. 

These are old cisterns that have been roofed over with slate slabs, leaving only a ten-inch breather hole at the top. The cisterns are half-full of warm, milky lime-sludge, rendered fat that has dripped down from the upper tiers, and the bodies of the pale beasts that broke their legs in the alleys and were shoved down the chutes to get them out of the way. 

In that warm, alkaline soup, in the pitch dark, things grow. They start as gristly, white knots the size of a fist, clinging to the submerged ribs of a dead ox-beast, drinking the calcium out of the water and the fat out of the broth to build their heavy, dense bones. When they are four feet long, they crawl out of the sludge, haul themselves up through the ten-inch breather hole with their soft, paddle-fingered hands, and stand shivering in the greasy twilight of the working-floor. Their skin is raw and pink for the first six hours, weeping clear fluid as the coal-smoke and lime-dust bite into it, forming their first gray crust. Within a day, they are standing at the troughs, scooping and slapping, *shhhk, shhhk, shhhk*, their pewter eyes filmed over against the steam.

And then there are the old ones. 

A setter does not die of old age; he simply cures. Over years of breathing the white steam and absorbing the wet mortar through the cracks in his horn-plated skin, the calcium begins to settle in his joints. First, his toes stiffen, so he can no longer climb the cedar poles to sleep. He stays on the ground then, shuffling slowly along the wooden sluices, his hips locking up, his spine fusing into a single, rigid bow of calcified gristle. He becomes slower and heavier. His gray rind thickens to two inches, then three, flaking off in hard, shaley plates when he brushes against a wall.

Eventually, a morning comes—though there is no morning, only the endless orange smudge of the cressets—when an old setter steps into a narrow gap between two retaining walls, or leans his shoulder against an unfinished pier to rest his fused knees, and simply cannot unbend.

The others do not mourn him. They do not even stop. If a setter has locked up in the middle of a gravel-sluice, two younger ones will come along, knock their wrists against their chests—*thock-thock*—and tip him over the edge into the foundation-trench below. 

If he has locked up against a wall, where he is not in the way, they simply include him. The next setter down the line scoops a heavy gob of gray-green mortar from the trough, slaps it across the old one’s chest, and lays a course of fresh-split slate right over his shoulder. The old one’s pewter eyes will stay open for a week or two, peering out from a three-inch gap between two courses of stone, watching the pale, blind beasts shove their snouts past in the gloom, watching the black mutton-grease drip from the baskets, until the next gang comes through with a load of rubble, and the floor rises again, and the dark closes the seam.
