To find the things that actually *live* here—in the sense of having a will, rather than merely functioning as a gasket or a bearing in the planet’s dark engine—you have to look at the places where the machinery stutters. 

Where a basalt piston catches on an outcropping of raw bismuth and hangs, shuddering, for three or four seconds before the sheer torque of the mantle forces it down; where a ridge repeats itself four times across the horizon and leaves three hollow, brittle afterimages of stone behind it; that is where the **Gleaners of the Slip** congregate.

At first glance, a Gleaner looks like a pile of industrial slag that has been caught in a localized gravity well. They are roughly the height of a tall man, though their proportions are constantly negotiating with the terrain. Their bodies are a composite of heavy, dark felt—grown from the compressed spores of the verdigris moss—and overlapping plates of terraced, rainbow-sheened bismuth that they flake off their own backs when they grow too heavy. They have no heads, only a central, barrel-like thorax wrapped in thick, vascular cables of copper-wood vine that pulse with a slow, turpentine-scented blood. 

Instead of hands, they terminate in five or six multi-jointed calipers of dense, horn-like bone. They move with a startling, syncopated grace. A Gleaner will stand completely motionless on a shearing shelf of obsidian until the exact microsecond the stone loses its structural memory; then, before the rock even knows it is falling, the Gleaner blurs—its joints clicking like a telegraph key—and steps across the falling debris as if walking up a flight of stairs, its brassy, caliper-limbs ringing softly against the stone to test its temper.

They are the custodians of the mountain’s errors. 

When a cliffside stutters like a skipped film frame, the "ghost" ridges it leaves behind are made of *paradox-stone*—matter that is only half-convinced it exists. It is grey, porous, and warm as fresh bread. The Gleaners descend upon these stuttered ridges in silent, frantic droves. Using their bone calipers, they pinch off chunks of the undecided rock before it can collapse into spores, shoving the warm, grey paste directly into the slits of their felt-lined thoraxes. They do not eat for calories; the heat and oil of the air provide all the energy anything here needs. They eat *potential*. They consume the un-collapsed possibilities of the mountain to keep their own bodies slightly out of phase with the crushing, mechanical certainty of the fault lines.

Because they are full of this half-real paste, a Gleaner is never entirely *there*. If a hundred-ton stamping press of basalt drops directly onto one, the creature does not get crushed. It simply smudges—stretching laterally like grease on a lens, its bismuth plates sliding through one another with a dry, metallic shucking sound—and reassembles itself two yards to the left, smelling faintly of ozone and scorched mint, already reaching with its calipers for the next seam of warm, oozing sap.

***

If you follow a band of Gleaners down into the mile-deep trenches—the ones that yawn open to reveal the slick, velvet-beetle-coated walls of the deep crust—you will eventually find what the Gleaners are working around. You will find a **Sump-Wright**.

The Sump-Wrights do not move. They are too vast, and too deeply plumbed into the four-stroke fumaroles, to ever leave the trenches where they settled as larvae. A mature Wright fills the bottom of a canyon the way an engine block fills the hood of a lorry. It is a sprawling, asymmetrical mass of translucent, blubbery oil-sacs, calcified exhaust pipes, and great, weeping bellows of dark leather that heave in counter-rhythm to the mountain’s own pistons. 

The Wrights are the only things in this bruised-plum darkness that possess eyes, though they do not use them to look at the world. Embedded deep within their translucent, jelly-like mantles are clusters of huge, cataract-white orbs, each the size of a carriage wheel, lit from behind by the same flickering, vacuum-tube static that powers the sky-drifters above. These eyes stare inward, watching the shadows of the Wright’s own dozens of hearts as they pump thick, black mineral sludge through miles of external, copper-sheathed arteries.

A Sump-Wright is a living refinery, and it is the closest thing this mountain range has to a memory. 

As the terrain above revises itself—shearing, melting, and stamping out new plateaus every hour—the runoff of that violence drains into the trenches. Torn copper-fern roots, crushed velvet beetles, liquefied gear-stone from the jellyfish-drifters, and the warm, minty blood of broken strata all sluice down into the dark. The Sump-Wright catches this broth in great, terraced catch-basins formed from its own calcified skin. 

Inside the Wright’s cavernous, multi-chambered gut, the chaotic slurry of the mountain is sorted, distilled, and *remembered*. The Wright does not merely digest; it reads the chemical and geometric history of everything that falls into it. In its silent, trembling belly, it reconstructs the shapes of peaks that existed for only ten minutes three centuries ago, casting miniature, three-inch replicas of extinct valleys out of pure, refined bismuth and iron-slag. 

It excretes these tiny, perfect landscapes in neat, terraced rows along the canyon floor—millions of intricate, metallic dioramas of a mountain range that no longer exists, all ticking softly as they cool in the dark. 

***

And then there are the **Tappers**, who live on the backs of the Sump-Wrights and walk among the miniature landscapes. 

Of everything here, the Tappers are the smallest, the rarest, and the most unsettlingly deliberate. They are no larger than a human hand, shaped vaguely like long-legged spiders made of braided copper wire, watch-springs, and wet, translucent parchment. They have no faces, only a single, tuning-fork-like prong of hardened steel protruding from their front ends.

While the rest of the ecology is locked in the frantic, silent rush of the assembly line, the Tappers are slow. They walk with a high-stepping, fastidious hesitation over the trembling bellows of the Sump-Wrights, stepping carefully over the miniature bismuth mountains. Every few minutes, a Tapper will stop, raise its steel prong, and strike a specific point on a Sump-Wright’s calcified pipe, or tap the edge of a newly excreted diorama, or lean out over an abyss to *clink* its head against a passing, million-ton slab of sliding basalt.

In a world packed in heavy wool—a world of muffled *shhh-chunks* and wet hisses—the sound a Tapper makes is a needle of pure, dry, silver resonance. *Ping.* 

The note hangs in the grease-thick air for a long time, cutting right through the hydraulic thrum of the planet. 

When that note rings out, everything within fifty yards pauses for half a beat. The origami-hounds freeze mid-ratchet. The copper ferns hold their shutter-clicking leaves open. Even the great basalt pistons seem to hesitate at the top of their stroke, trembling under an immense, invisible pressure. In that half-second of absolute stillness, the Tappers listen to the echo. By the way the sound warps through the warm fog and bounces off the shifting plates, they know where the next fault will tear, which trench is about to snap shut, and which ridge is about to forget its own solidity. 

Before the silver note has even fully faded into the wooly dark, the Tappers have already gathered their little wire legs beneath them, turned their backs on the doomed sector of the canyon, and begun their slow, fastidious march toward the one seam of rock that will still be solid in an hour’s time—followed, a moment later, by the silent, blurring rush of the Gleaners, and the slow, heavy leaning of the drifting vacuum-tubes overhead, all of them herding together into the safe angles of the dark before the mountain stamps down again.
