They are sitting four hundred yards out from the eastern wall, wedged into the dry V-shaped notch between two slabs of fallen limestone the size of barges. There are five of them. From twenty paces away, in the flat, shadowless pewter light, they do not look like people; they look like growths of pale lichen or slumped heaps of guano-streaked shale. 

This is because of their coats. Over heavy inner tunics of surface-deer hide, they wear ankle-length parkas sewn from hundreds of cliff-swift pelts, turned feather-side out. The slate-grey and chalk-white plumage is greased with rendered weasel tallow to shed the constant damp, and it is matted with the same floating down that coats the rocks. Even their hoods are trimmed with the stiff primary feathers of adult martins, breaking up the outline of their heads so completely that rolling swifts regularly bank within an inch of their faces, feeling the draft of their breath without registering a predator.

They are small, broad-shouldered people, built like badgers, with thick joints and short, bowed legs that have adapted over three generations to a world without a flat horizon. They do not walk so much as they lever themselves from angle to angle. Their feet are bare from the mid-calf down—leather rots too quickly in the seepage pools and slips on the slick liverwort—and the skin of their soles and ankles has thickened into a grey, horn-like callous, deeply fissured and permanently stained black by the wet basalt. Their big toes are noticeably splayed, gripping the sharp, unweathered edges of the broken rock like thumbs.

They do not speak. In the ninety-decibel roar of the basin—that unending sound of a shingle beach being dragged by the tide—the human voice is useless beyond two feet. Over the decades, their vocal cords have gone largely slack from disuse, used only for a few sharp, low-frequency grunts felt through the chest when huddled together in the sleeping crevices. Instead, they communicate across the boulder fields using two flattened disks of dense, black chert held in the palm. A sharp *clack-clack* of stone on stone cuts cleanly through the high-pitched dry chatter of the birds. 

Right now, an older woman with hair the color of wet salt—braided with bird-bones to keep it out of her eyes—taps two stones together once. *Clack.* 

Twenty yards away, a boy of about fourteen rises partway out of a bed of crushed shale. His eyes are striking: the pupils are huge, permanently dilated to drink in the shadowless fifty-degree gloom, surrounded by pale, watery-grey irises. When this group climbs the three-day vertical fault-chimney to the upper plains—which they do only twice a year to trade cured skins and prime black chert nodules for surface-wood and river cobbles—they have to wear slits of scraped willow-bark over their faces, weeping blindly in the violent yellow glare of the sun. Here, in the leaden noon that never changes, the boy sees every twitch of a rock-lizard’s throat forty feet away.

He does not use the throwing sticks lying back at the eastern overhang. Those are for the climb up to the rim, where the big prairie-hawks and coyotes circle the fault-line. Down here, hunting is a matter of harvesting a crop that does not know how to flee. 

The boy reaches out with a slow, liquid motion that matches the sluggishness of the cold-numbed lizards. In his hand is a four-foot wand of peeled willow, tipped with a noose of braided weasel-sinew. He slips the noose over the head of a roosting crag-martin packed tight between two of its neighbors on a basalt lip. He draws it tight and lifts the bird off the rock before the two flanking it even shift their weight to fill the gap. There is no flutter; his thumb presses the base of the skull with practiced, mechanical economy, and the warm body goes into the wide leather sack slung across his groin. 

They eat the breast meat raw while it is still ninety-eight degrees, prizing the animal heat in a valley that has no fuel for fire. The rest of the bird goes back to the limestone shelf to be rendered. 

Back in the V-shaped notch, the other four are not resting; they are sleeping. Because the light never dims, the temperature never drops, and the noise never abates, these people have lost the circadian rhythm of the surface world. They do not sleep in eight-hour stretches. Like the mustelids in the rocks below them, they drop into forty-minute stupors whenever their bellies are full, three or four at a time, piled together like litter-mates to share body heat while one keeps watch for the rare rockfall. 

They smell powerfully of the rift: a thick, musky odor of wet feathers, old tallow, crushed fern-root, and the sharp, ammoniacal tang of the guano-crust. Their teeth are worn down flat from chewing dried meat gritty with shale-dust and softening sinew with their jaws, and the corners of their mouths are permanently stained dark with the rich, oily fat they drink from the limestone mortar.

The older woman watches the boy fill his sack. Her breathing is slow—ten times a minute in the cool, heavy air. Her fingers, short and thick-knuckled, absentmindedly work a strip of willow bark into twine in her lap without her looking down. Above her, at forty feet, the grey ceiling of mist churns slightly as a dense column of hatching stoneflies punches into it, followed instantly by a black vortex of ten thousand swifts banking in unison. The air pressure drops; the roar of wings briefly presses against her eardrums, flattening the down on her hood.

She does not look up. To her, the mist is the sky, the roar is silence, and the four-mile box of jagged stone is the whole of the earth—a cold, deafening, inexhaustible larder that requires nothing of her people except that they stay small, stay greased, and keep moving when the chill settles into their bones.
