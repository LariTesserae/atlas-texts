To look at one of the rigging-dwellers is to look at something that has been cured in a kiln rather than born. There is no soft belly to them, no wet shine of mucous membrane, no heavy, sloshing sacks of blood and water. They are narrow, dense-boned, and drawn taut like rawhide dried over a frame of ironwood. 

Their skin is the color of unglazed terracotta or tarnished pewter, thickest at the forearms, knees, and breastbone, where it forms segmented, cornified plates that flake away in dry, mica-like scales whenever they scrape against the shatter-reed. They do not sweat. To lose fluid in the rift is to die within an hour; instead, their metabolism runs hot and dry, venting excess internal heat through a stiff, pleated ruff of cartilage and dry blood-vessels that flares from the back of the neck and down between the shoulder blades. When a worker has been hauling on the basalt lines in a rising thermal chimney, this dorsal crest flushes a dull, glowing brick-red, radiating dry heat into the updraft like the fins of an air-cooled engine.

They are built for a three-dimensional world that is constantly trying to shear them in two. Their hips are narrow, their legs slightly bowed and spring-loaded, terminating in feet that have abandoned the flat heel entirely. Each foot is a four-pronged grappling iron: two long, heavily calloused toes facing forward and two facing back, tipped not with flat nails, but with thick, curved wedges of dark keratin that can hook into the porous grain of pumice-burl or lock around a braided chrysotile cable. Their arms are disproportionately long, the tendons in their wrists standing out like bowstrings beneath the leather of their skin, capable of locking into place without muscular effort so they can hang from a vibrating rigging-line and sleep while the forest wars with itself below.

Their faces are flat and wedge-shaped, dominated by a heavy brow ridge that shields the eyes from falling glass-chaff. Each eye is protected by two sets of eyelids: a heavy leather outer lid for sleeping, and a tough, translucent inner membrane—yellowish and glassy, like oiled parchment—that stays snapped shut almost constantly while they are outside the huts. They look out at the amber-and-violet glare of their world through these yellow filters, squinting through the perpetual blitz of static sparks and abrasive silica dust. Their nostrils are downward-facing slits lined with stiff, dense bristles that trap the microscopic glass-dust before it can enter their dry, bellows-like lungs; every few minutes, a dweller will give a sharp, percussive snort, ejecting a tiny dry pellet of compacted dust and sulfur-ash from their nose.

They have no external ears. In the basin, an eardrum would be ruptured at birth. Where ears should be, there are only smooth, slightly concave disks of bone covered in tight skin, just behind the jaw. They do not hear the air; they hear the rigging. 

Because of this, they are a profoundly touch-bound people. A solitary person on a rope is half-deaf, aware only of the thrumming tension of the line under their feet—which tells them whether the anchor-tree is surging, splitting, or being sawed through by a neighbor—and the deep, seismic bass of the rift’s tectonic groaning in their chest cavity. To speak to one another, they must press together. 

When two of them meet on a swaying catwalk of braided reed, they do not wave or shout. They hook their knees around the guide-ropes, lean in, and press their collarbones, foreheads, or forearms firmly together. They speak through an internal click-and-hum organ set deep in the sternum—a resonance chamber of ribbed cartilage that vibrates like the flint-shells of the cicadas they eat, though at a much lower, tighter frequency. When they touch, they feel the rapid-fire *thrum-tick-thrum* of the other’s chest directly through their own skeleton. 

A family inside one of the teardrop huts does not sit in a circle; they sit in a tight, tangled knot, knees over shoulders, feet hooked into the woven ribs of the wall, a single multi-limbed mass of warm, dry leather and clicking bone. In the deadened, clay-muffled quiet of the hut—where the apocalyptic roar outside is reduced to a heavy, grinding shudder that makes the dust fall from the ceiling—they groom the glass-splinters from one another’s scales with flint tweezers and talk through their ribs. Conversations are not turn-based; five people pressed together can hum five overlapping lines of thought into the shared bone-pile at once, a complex, chorded vibration of gossip, tension-math, and memory.

Their lives are governed entirely by the *Spool-Watch*. 

A shelter is not a permanent place; it is a slow-motion vehicle falling upward through a solid medium. Because the five or six mega-stalks holding a hut are growing at wildly different rates—one shooting up ten feet in an hour as it hits a sulfur pocket, another twisting clockwise as it braids with a silica-vine, a third dying and turning to brittle black glass that could shatter at any second—the geometry of home is an unending crisis. 

There is always someone on the outside of the teardrop, clinging to the grease-slicked bone ratchets with a heavy flint-headed mallet hooked to their belt. These watchers do not look at the scenery; they read the ropes. By resting a chin or a wrist against a taut chrysotile cable, a seasoned rigger can feel the pitch of the fiber's vibration. A high, singing whine means the tree is pulling away too fast and the bone-spool is jammed with dust; a dull, ragged buzz means a strand has frayed against a fresh extrusion of rock-bark forty yards out in the violet glare. 

When a line goes critical, the watcher doesn't go inside to call for help. They strike the main framing-hoop of the hut with their mallet—three sharp, distinct *clacks* that travel instantly through the wicker and into the bones of everyone sleeping inside. Within seconds, the leather flap of the lower hatch splits open, and the household spills out into the roaring updraft like spiders from a disturbed sac.

They move through the rigging with terrifying, casual speed, never using safety lines—a trailing rope in this forest will be snagged by a whip-cracking tendril and yank its owner into the grinding mulch before they can draw a breath. They carry heavy, crescent-shaped blades of knapped obsidian bound into the jawbones of great grazers, and pots of grey, gritty tallow rendered from the kinetic-organs of the canopy fauna. While one person scrambles out along the vibrating hawser, kicking away the parasitic mineral-shoots trying to calcify around the rope, two others hang by their heels from the counterweight frame, knocking the wooden pins out of the bone ratchets to let the basalt blocks drop twenty feet, taking up the slack with a jolt that makes the whole house lurch and swing across the chasm.

Sometimes, a stalk cannot be saved. When a shatter-reed reaches its structural limit, the internal pressure of its rising mineral-pith builds until the trunk begins to sing—a piercing, crystalline harmonic that vibrates in the teeth of every creature within half a mile. The dwellers know they have perhaps three minutes before the tree explodes into a million razor-edged shivers of obsidian. 

Then you see the true nature of their society: the *Cut-and-Swing*. They do not panic. Moving in total, percussive coordination, the riggers hack through two of their five anchor lines with single, heavy blows of their stone axes. The teardrop hut drops like a stone into the rushing hot wind, pendulums wildly across a hundred-yard gap of blinding violet light and shrieking cicada-swarms, and slams into the tangled mattress of a neighboring pumice-burl cluster. Before the rebound can swing them back into the blast zone of the dying reed, the nimble youngest members of the family have already leaped from the hull, trailing new anchor-lines behind them, driving stone hooks into the soft, newly extruded pith of the fresh trees to tie the house down before the old trunk detonates behind them in a dry storm of black glass.

Because everything they eat is dry, their relationship with food is mechanical and loud. They harvest the flint-shelled cicadas from the long nets, hauling the kicking, shrieking masses into the lee of a giant burl, where they crush them whole in deep basalt mortars. The insects do not bleed; they shatter into a rich, oily paste of copper-tasting fat, ground flint, and static-charged pith. The dwellers mix this with the scraped, inner cambium of young silica-vines—which looks like spun fiberglass before it calcifies, and tastes of hot sulfur and iron—and press the mash into flat, dense cakes that are cured by hanging them in the hottest thermal vents. 

Eating requires jaws like nutcrackers. Their teeth are not separate incisors and molars, but continuous, ridged plates of dark gray enamel—powered by massive temporalis muscles that attach to the top of their skulls—that grow continuously from the jawbone to replace what is worn away by the stone-meal and silica-fiber. When a family eats, the sound inside the clay hut is like gravel being turned in a wooden barrel. From this dry mash, their bodies extract the trace moisture bound up in the chemical structure of the minerals, breaking down hydrous silicates with fiercely acidic, grinding gizzards lined with swallowed pumice-stones.

Even their fire is different. You do not light a campfire in a mattress of dry, gas-bloated kindling unless you want to burn five miles of forest into a white-hot slag-heap. Yet they need extreme heat to fire the acoustic clay that keeps their minds from being rattled to mush by the noise, and to cure the flexible reeds of their cages. 

So they practice *friction-kilning*. They build small, thick-walled spheres of stone and old clay, pack them with raw pottery or bundles of green reed, and lash the sphere tightly into the crotch of two massive, competing mega-stalks that are actively grinding against one another in the thermal draft. They let the forest do the work. As the two trunks saw back and forth with the force of tectonic plates, the stone sphere is caught in the vise; within an hour, the friction and piezo-electric discharge between the trees brings the interior of the kiln to a glowing, white heat without a single open flame. When the wind shifts or one of the trunks snaps, the riggers dart in with long poles of chrysotile-wrapped bone, hook the smoking sphere out of the cleft, and haul it back up to the hanging workshops to cool.

When one of them dies—usually crushed by a sudden counterweight drop, pierced by a whip-vine, or simply worn down until their gizzard can no longer grind the stone-bread and they stiffen into stillness—they are not buried, and they are not dropped into the floor. The floor is for trash, for broken wicker and dull flint; to drop a kinsman into the mulch is to let their rare, precious minerals be sucked up by the weeds.

Instead, the dead are kept in the rigging. The body is folded tightly—knees to chest, heels to hips, arms wrapped around the shins—and bound with old, retired tension-rope. They are hung just beneath the floor of the teardrop hut, among the notched basalt counterweights and the spare bone-spools. In the dry, blistering updrafts, the corpse does not rot; it desiccates within days into an indestructible, resonant bundle of rawhide and dense bone, lighter than stone but still heavy enough to serve the house. 

A dead grandmother becomes the trimmer-weight on the north-west stabilizer line; an uncle who lost his footing on the nets now hangs below the hatch, keeping the main plumb-line taut against the sixty-mile-an-hour sulfur gale. And when the living climb down into the beneath-rigging to grease the ratchets or check the knots, they will pause, reach out a leathery hand, and rest their palm against the dry, humming breastbone of the ancestor swinging in the violet light—feeling the vibration of the whole house, the tension of the five trees, and the deep, unbroken roar of the rift passing through the dead bones and into their own, making sure the line still holds.
