If you were to stare into the amber-lit tangle between the largest brass pressure-sphere and the north wall of the chimney for several hours, your eyes would eventually adjust to the high-frequency blur of the place. Everything in the cistern is shivering at roughly four hundred cycles per second, which gives the edges of the vines and the bolts a faint, ghostly double-outline, like a photograph nudged during exposure.

Except for a patch of what looks like dry, gray-brown grease wedged into the junction where a cable-vine throttles a cast-iron exhaust flange. 

This patch is in sharp focus. It has no double-outline. It is not vibrating with the machine; it is vibrating *against* it, oscillating in exact, micro-second counter-phase to the screaming dynamos, so that to the naked eye it appears dead still.

This is the first indication that the thing is there, though this is only its skin, or perhaps its heel.

To find the rest of it requires giving up on the idea of a body that displaces space. In a cistern packed so tightly that a dropped iron nut would never reach the floor, anything that requires room to turn around is already dead. What lives here—what has lived here since the brass spheres were still bright and the silica-kelp was no more than a crust of gray lichen on the bolt-heads—is not shaped like an animal. It is shaped like the remaining three percent of available volume.

It is an interstitial thing, a creature of the seams. Its substance is a dense, non-wetting amalgam of heavy metallic soaps, pulverized mica, and long-chain silicon polymers that it renders down from the shed cuticles of the kelp and the tallow-based lubricants bled from the ancient gear-casings. To the touch—if a hand could be forced between the granite and the vine-bark—it would feel like cold, hard cobbler’s wax or the leaden felt used to dampen piano strings. 

It is called *the shim*, though there is no one for fifty miles in any direction to call it anything.

At any given moment, the shim is spread across four or five cubic feet of the tangle, though if it were peeled out of the crevices and rolled into a ball, it would be no larger than a winter cabbage. It exists as a connected system of flat, muscular ribbons, knuckles of gray gristle, and wafer-thin sheets of tendon that occupy the microscopic fault-lines between the three great powers of the cistern: the granite, the iron, and the wood.

Where a massive, slate-barked vine presses against the curve of a cast-iron housing with four tons of static wooden force, the shim is the paper-thin gasket of lead-gray tissue between them. Where two silica-fronds are wedged flat against one another in the amber dark, the shim is the dry, talc-coated film that keeps their saw-toothed edges from locking.

It does not move by crawling. Crawling requires a gap to crawl *into*. Instead, it moves by the redistribution of pressure. 

The cistern is an engine of immense, frustrated torque. The locked flywheels still want to turn; their internal dynamos torque the iron casings continuously to the left. The great vines, grown in spirals, want to untwist to the right. The granite walls expand and contract by fractions of a millimeter as the sun swings around the spire outside, heating one face of the needle while the other stays buried in forty-below-zero shadow.

The shim is a master of these vectors. To travel from the brass pressure-sphere to the bed of silica-duff three feet below, it does not push the plants aside. It simply stiffens one of its horn-like wedges between a vine-knuckle and an iron rivet, stealing a fraction of the dynamo’s vibration and converting it into a minuscule jacking force. The vine gives by the width of a fingernail. Into that momentary slack, the shim flows—not like water, which has no strength, but like cold lead being extruded through a die. A slow, dry, waxy creep that takes forty minutes to advance an inch, accompanied by a sound that is entirely swallowed by the roar of the wind-pipe above: the *tink-tink-tink* of microscopic silica crystals in its hide ratcheting against the grain of the iron.

It has no eyes; in a place where nothing moves and the light never changes, vision is a waste of structure. It has no ears, because an ear is an instrument for detecting vibrations in a quiet medium, and here the medium is a solid wall of noise. Instead, the shim’s entire body is an acoustic lens. 

Spread out through the knot of the cistern like the lead cames in a stained-glass window, it reads the interior of the pocket by the way the dynamo-scream passes through it. It knows the exact density of the heartwood in the central vines by the way they muffle the 800-hertz harmonic of the lower flywheel. It knows when a deep crack is forming in the granite three feet behind the iron bolt-plates because the pitch of the stone’s ringing drops by a sixteenth of a tone. It knows the interior of the sealed iron casings better than the men who cast them; it has spent three hundred years pressed against their seams, feeling the gradual, century-long wear of the internal ceramic bearings, listening to the slow thinning of the ancient grease inside.

In fact, the shim is the only reason the cistern has not torn itself apart or ground itself into silent powder.

Left to themselves, the high-frequency rattle of the iron housings would have sawed through the silica-vines in a decade, and the expanding wedges of the roots would have cracked the cast-iron flanges from the wall a century ago. The plants and the machines are too hard, too unyielding, to survive their own intimacy. 

The shim is their buffer, their parasite, and their governor. 

It feeds on the boundary layer where the plant-flesh meets the metal. Where the silica-kelp presses against the humming brass and glows with that sickly amber light, the plant sweats a dry, crystalline exudate—oxalic salts, sulfur compounds, and tiny, fullerene cages of pure carbon shaken loose by the ultrasonic fury of the oscillators. The shim extrudes a broad, velvety tongue—thin as gold leaf and ribbed like a file—directly between the glowing leaf and the hot brass plate. It licks up the newly formed carbon-dust and the metallic salts before they can cake and insulate the leaf from its vibration-source. 

In return, it secretes a minuscule smear of its own substance—a dry, molybdenum-rich slick that keeps the brass from burning the plant and the plant’s silica teeth from scoring the brass.

It is an engineer’s animal. Its organs are not arranged in a cavity; they are distributed along its tension-lines like components on a belt. 

In the thicker pockets—such as the three-cornered shadow behind the broken pressure gauge—it gathers enough of its gray bulk to form what might be called a head, or at least a switching-yard. Here, the waxy flesh is folded over on itself twenty or thirty times, like puff pastry or the leaves of a heavy condenser. Between the folds are flat, crystalline plates of piezo-quartz that it has mined from the granite walls over the generations, grain by grain, using acid-tipped root-hairs stolen from the sulfur-moss.

When the wind outside shifts to the north-northwest and hits the upper chimney lip at the exact angle that causes the whole stone column to boom like an eighteen-foot organ pipe, the acoustic pressure in the pocket triples. The plants begin to over-vibrate; their amber veins flare toward a dangerous, brittle white. 

Then, the folded lump behind the pressure gauge tightens. The shim squeezes its internal quartz plates together with the slow, hydraulic power of its silicon muscles. Current bleeds through its leaden tissues. Its ribbons throughout the tangle stiffen, changing their density, altering their acoustic impedance. It becomes heavier, deadening the rattle, drinking the excess kinetic fury of the gale and turning it into a slow, dull warmth that radiates harmlessly into the cold granite of the wall. 

It has no face, yet it has habits that look suspiciously like preference.

It favors the lower left bolt of the central dynamo housing, which is slightly looser than the others and therefore sings a third higher—a bright, silvery *zeeee* that cuts through the grinding bass of the flywheels. The shim always keeps a small, knuckle-sized node of its gray flesh wrapped around the hexagonal head of this bolt, thumbing the worn iron corners, dampening the note and then letting it ring out again, over and over, across the weeks. Whether it is regulating the bolt to keep the thread from stripping, or whether it is playing the only note in the cistern that it can actually change, is impossible to say.

Once every few years, a piece of the outside world falls down the thirty-foot flue. Usually it is only a flake of frost-shattered granite from the lip above, which the shim slowly wraps in a cyst of gray gristle and works down through the tangle over the course of a winter until it is safely bedded in the motionless duff at the bottom, where it cannot rattle against the casings.

Once, forty years ago, it was a dead snow-finch, freeze-dried and weightless, blown over the peak from some lower valley. 

The bird wedged in the upper briars, six feet above the machines. It took the shim three months to reach it, extruding a single, wire-thin proboscis up the dry bark of a vertical stalk. When it touched the feathers, it recoiled for a week; the structure of the bird was baffling—full of hollow tubes that held nothing, built around a chemistry of frozen water that tasted like sharp, volatile poison to a creature made of grease and flint. 

In the end, the shim did not eat the bird. Instead, over the course of seven years, it slowly infiltrated the tiny, parchment-dry corpse, replacing the evaporated marrow in the hair-thin bones with its own gray silicon-wax, coating the feathers in a micro-layer of talc and oil until the dead finch was transformed into a solid, rubbery weight—an acoustic damper of a particularly complex and useful shape. 

It dragged the vulcanized bird down into the heart of the knot and wedged it tightly into a notorious gap between the primary intake-pipe and a cluster of sulfur-moss, where a troublesome 2,000-hertz whistle had been irritating the local root-system since the previous century. The whistle stopped. The amber light in the surrounding fronds steadied to a rich, buttery glow.

Now, the bird is still there, indistinguishable from the iron and the tubers, part of the shim’s extended skeleton. And the shim itself remains everywhere and nowhere in the cistern—the living mortar in the dry wall, holding the cracked glass of the pressure gauge together with a tendon that looks like a split vine, listening with its whole flat body to the endless, screaming chord of the mountain, waiting for a bolt to loosen by a millionth of an inch so that it may have somewhere new to go.
