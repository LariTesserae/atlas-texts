If you stand by the lattice-boom crane for three hours—until the chill of the valley has soaked through the soles of your boots and the blinding white glare off the porcelain bells has burned purple afterimages into your retinas—you will realize that the third cast-iron counterweight at the base of the derrick is not cast iron at all.

It has been unclasping itself from the steel frame at the rate of two millimeters a minute.

What lives here are the Gantry-Tenders, though there is no one left to call them that, and they do not distinguish between tending the valley and being the valley’s pressure-relief system. 

At first glance, a Tender looks like an artillery shell or a deep-sea diving bell that has been partially colonized by a salt-lick. It stands roughly five feet tall, lacks a head, and is shaped like a squat, heavy frustum—wider at the bottom than at the top—weighted so low to the ground that no shift in the tectonic plates could ever tip it over. Its outer casing is not skin or shell, but a thick, layered secretion of dull lead-carbonate and hardened tallow, dark grey and matte, indistinguishable from the oiled iron of the caissons or the blue-black basalt until you touch it. If you did touch it, it would not flinch. It would feel like cold soapstone smeared with axle-grease.

They do not walk on legs. The valley is too jagged with fresh-split chert, and the bladder-moss too treacherous, for ankles or knees. Instead, a Tender moves on a basal skirt of forty or fifty hydraulic tube-feet—thick, muscular cylinders of grey gristle sheathed in overlapping scales of dull horn, very much like the armored hoses that feed the condenser manifolds. When a Tender traverses a razor-sharp ledge of rock, it does not step; it *extrudes* itself across the gap. Three or four tube-feet reach out, moving at the exact speed of the crane’s hour-hand hoist, plant their suction-cupped, brass-rimmed tips onto the wet stone, and then the heavy, half-ton body slides forward on a film of clear, viscous mucilage. It leaves no footprints in the moss because it distributes its weight so gradually, and over such a wide, creeping footprint, that the spongy plants simply bend beneath it and slowly, waterloggedly, rebound after it has passed.

They are creatures of immense internal pressure and cold, slow chemistry. A warm-blooded animal would die here in a day, its lungs scorched by the thin, dry air between the wet leaves, its heart racing itself to pieces in the high-altitude hush. The Tenders have no lungs and no hearts. Inside their lead-grey mantle, they are an arrangement of rubbery bladders, copper-lined gizzards, and osmotic pumps, operating on the same principle as the cast-iron junction boxes. They breathe by drinking the sweat of the stones.

As a Tender inches through a thicket of silver-barked scrub, a fringe of pale, feathery gill-plumes—looking exactly like the ropy roots of the cliff-plants—hangs from a horizontal slit near its base. These plumes drag across the cold, wet leaves and the sweating copper pipes, wicking up the condensed moisture and the dissolved trace minerals of the newly broken rock. Inside the creature, this water is broken down by a slow, cold, galvanic digestion. They eat the static electricity off the high-tension pylons.

If you watch one approach a porcelain insulator bell where the coin-leaved vines are bristling with charge, you will see the Tender’s upper dome—a ring of six heavy, vulcanized-rubber valves—slowly iris open. Out of the dark interior rises a single, telescoping rod of tarnished native copper, grown from the creature’s own mineral-rich bone. It takes forty minutes for this rod to extend two feet. When it finally touches the live ceramic bell, there is no spark. A spark would be a waste of energy; a spark would make a sound. Instead, the rod makes a wet, greasy contact, and the static charge bleeds silently down into the Tender’s gut, where it is used to split the swallowed condensate into hydrogen and oxygen, pressurizing the creature’s internal ballast tanks.

Their eyes—if they can be called eyes—are set in a ring around the middle of their bodies, just below the grease-skirt. There are eight of them, and they are identical to the brass-bezeled sight-glasses on the machinery. Each is a thick lens of grown quartz, clouded with yellow oil to cut the ferocious high-altitude glare. Behind the lens, nothing moves. They cannot see anything that moves faster than a cloud shadow. If you were to run past a Tender, waving your arms and shouting, you would be as invisible to it as a rifle bullet is to a man; your voice would be a high-frequency vibration too brief to register on its heavy, fluid-filled tympanums. To the Tender, the world is a bright, crisp photograph where the sun does not travel across the sky, but rather exists as a solid white band of heat from horizon to horizon, and the only moving things are the rising hydraulic rams, the slowly turning gas-separators, and the creeping, green-grey tide of the horn-flesh rosettes trying to swallow the brass fittings.

This is what the Tenders are for, or at least what they do. They have a symbiotic, janitor’s relationship with the silent industry. 

The machines are very cold, and the plants love the cold metal, and left unchecked, the tough, waxy lithophytes would eventually wedge their roots into the bronze tracks of the rotating cylinders or choke the piston-rods with woody stems. The Tenders graze the machines clean. 

One of them is currently latched onto the side of the third atmospheric harvester. It has been there since Tuesday. From a vertical seam in its belly, it has extruded a broad, flat muscular pad covered in rows of black, chert-hard radulae—teeth like the teeth of a machinist’s file. With the patience of a glacier, it is rasping away a mat of silver bladder-moss that had begun to creep over a flange of dinner-plate hex-nuts. *Scritch.* Then a four-minute pause while the hydraulic pressure in its dental-sack equalizes. *Scritch.* The shredded plant matter is not swallowed; it is mixed with a heavy, foul-smelling hydrocarbon oil that the Tender secretes from its mantle-lip, and smeared directly back onto the steel bolts as a fresh coat of anti-corrosive grease. 

They also tend the stone. The valley is newly broken because the valley is still breaking—the machines are wedging it apart, harvesting the deep mantle-gases that seep up through the freshly cleaved basalt, or perhaps simply relieving the stress of the mountain range so it does not shatter all at once. The Tenders assist in the splitting.

Half a mile from the crane, in a narrow cleft where the black rock has not yet parted, three Tenders are wedged side-by-side in a hairline fault. They have been there for a month, looking like three grey boulders jammed into a crevice. They have drunk gallons of cold condensate from the weeping cliff-face and bled current from a nearby lead-sheathed cable until their internal bladders are taut as iron boilers. Every few hours, a walnut-sized bubble of gas rises through their quartz sight-glasses. Slowly, imperceptibly, they are inflating their side-plates, bracing their horn-scaled skirts against the opposing walls of the fissure, exerting a combined lateral force of ninety tons. 

There is no groan of straining rock. The chert here is too brittle and fine-grained for that. Some afternoon—perhaps today, perhaps next week—the tension will cross a threshold. The rock will split for forty feet down the face with a single, dry, flat *crack* like a pistol shot, the only loud sound the valley will hear all month. A fresh, matte-black slab will drop three inches into the moss. The white glare of the sun will hit stone that has never seen light, the cold air will immediately begin to sweat against the new fracture, and over the course of the following afternoon, the three Tenders will slowly deflate, unstick their grease-skirts from the cliff, and inch their way down into the new gap to begin laying copper pipe.

If you pry apart the damp plates of a ground-rosette near one of the heavy caissons, you might find where they come from. 

Next to the blind, thumbnail-sized flat-beetles, tucked deep in the dark, wet armpits of the horn-flesh leaves where the water collects in stagnant, freezing pools, sit things that look like lost hex-nuts or dropped brass washers. They are the larval stages. At this size, they are entirely metallic and crystalline, precipitated out of the mineral-heavy urine of the adults. They do not move at all for the first ten years; they simply sit in the wet moss like spare parts, growing layer after layer of lead-grey shell by electrolysis, waiting until they are heavy enough to crush the leaves beneath them and slide down to the rock.

Nothing eats them. Nothing bothers them. Once every few years, a stray mountain goat or an eagle blown off course from the lower, greener valleys will wander into this five-mile basin. The eagle will circle once in the blinding white silence, find no thermals in the dead, cellar-cold air, and beat its way frantically back over the ridge. The goat, if it stays, will find the waxy plants bitter with copper sulfate and tough as tire-rubber. It will wander among the thirty-foot porcelain towers and the gleaming hydraulic rams, bleating into an acoustic dead-zone where the moss eats its voice five yards from its mouth, completely unaware that the grey iron bollards it is stepping between are watching it with yellow, oil-filled eyes—and that when it finally lies down in the cold wet scrub to freeze to death, it will take the nearest bollard only six or seven days to slide over, settle its heavy, greased skirt over the carcass, and render the tallow and bone-calcium down into machine-oil and fresh brass.
