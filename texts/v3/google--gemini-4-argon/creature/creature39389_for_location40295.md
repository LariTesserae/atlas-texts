Look out across the mirror of the water at noon, and at first you will see only the storks and the swollen-trunked trees, wavering in the heat-shimmer like tall, thin ghosts. Look closer—Adjust your eyes to the glare that bounces off the amber water and the slick, sealskin-dark shields of rock—and you will notice that some of the peat mounds have a different silhouette. They are steeper, shaped like overturned bowls, and cemented with a mortar of pale kaolin clay that has been baked to the hardness of porcelain by the sun.

Wait another ten minutes. One of the distant storks will bend in a way a bird’s joints do not allow, folding downward at the waist to pick a shed strip of papery bark off the water’s surface. 

The people who live on the drowned peneplain call themselves nothing at all, because for three hundred miles in any direction there is no one else to distinguish themselves from. If pressed by a stranger from the distant, unseen coast, they refer to themselves simply as *those who stand*, to separate their kind from the amphibians that crawl and the clay that settles.

To survive in a world without cover, where the sky is both above and below and the water never leaves your skin, a human being must change their relationship with gravity and time. They are tall—exceptionally so, with long, fleshless shins and high-arched feet whose soles have thickened into broad, grey pads, cracked like dry riverbed yet supple enough to grip the grease-slick cyanobacteria on the granite shields. From the knee down, their skin is permanently stained a deep, plum-colored mahogany by the iron and tannins in the water; above the knee, they smear themselves with a slip of lavender kaolin and spherical quartz sand. It dries into a matte, chalky crust that reflects the brutal glare of the double sun and keeps the biting midges from finding their pores.

A person here never sits on the ground. To sit in the water is to invite the leeches, the tiny armored rasping-fish, and the slow rot that softens the flesh until it sloughs off the bone. Instead, they have mastered the geometry of the tripod. Every child, as soon as they can walk, is given a staff of bog-wood—one of those ancient, blackened branches that has cured in the tannic water for half a century until it is as heavy as iron and immune to decay. With the butt of the staff planted against the basement rock and one heel hooked over the opposite knee, a person can lock their hips and sleep standing up in the middle of a lagoon, swaying slightly in the warm wind just like the stork-birds half a mile away.

Speed is the great taboo of the flats. If you run, or even walk briskly, your feet kick up the silken kaolin clay. Because there is no current, a disturbed cloud of white silt will hang suspended in the amber water for three or four days, glowing brightly in the sun—a mile-wide blossom of milk that tells every predator, every rival, and every watchful bird exactly where you were and which way you were going. 

So they move like the hands of a clock. A hunting party of five people crossing a lagoon is an exercise in agonizing, liquid grace. A foot is lifted clean out of the water, brought forward without a drip, and slid edge-first back beneath the surface, feeling for the bare rock or sliding through the spherical sand without stirring a single plume of white. From a mile away, through the heat-haze, they do not appear to be walking at all; they simply seem to be growing closer, like shadows lengthening in the afternoon.

Even their biology has yielded to the nutrient-starved poverty of the ancient rock. In a landscape where the rain has washed the calcium and phosphorus out of the earth millions of years ago, bone is a precious, hoarded commodity. There are no graves here. When an old person dies, their body is laid out on the dry crown of a granite shield in the sun. Within days, the translucent frogs, the beetles, and the rust-colored storks have picked the skeleton clean, and the sun bleaches it to the color of the kaolin. The family then gathers the bones. The long femurs and tibias are lashed together to make the frames of their dome-huts and the prongs of their frog-gigs; the ribs are ground down on rough patches of gneiss into needles and awls. The skulls and the smaller vertebrae are burned in tiny, smoky fires of dried peat and papery bark, and the grey ash is stirred into the evening broth. You eat your ancestors so that your own children’s legs will grow straight and long enough to keep them above the water.

Because the world is drowned in that deafening, twenty-square-mile roar of clicking frogs, the human voice is nearly useless for anything beyond arm's reach. Try to shout across a lagoon, and your mid-range human vowels are instantly chewed up and swallowed by the high, metallic static of ten million pebble-clicks. 

Instead, they have divided their language in two. 

For distance, they borrow the acoustics of the great wading birds and the flat-tailed amphibians. Every hunter carries a *bellow-gourd*—actually the dried, hollow burl of a dwarf-cypress root, scraped thin as eggshell and fitted with a tight membrane of cured amphibian skin. By rubbing a wet thumb across the membrane, they produce a deep, sub-audible *thrum* that drops right underneath the high-frequency wall of the frogs. You don't hear it with your ears so much as feel it in the water around your ankles and the hollow of your chest. A single pulse carries for four miles across the flat stone. By varying the rhythm, two families invisible to one another in the late-afternoon apricot fog can hold a slow, resonant conversation about where the storks are landing or which way the rain-sheet is creeping.

Up close, when three or four of them are clustered together on a peat mound under the peeling, pale shade of a solitary tree, they do not speak at all. Their ears are tired from the eternal static. They talk with their hands and the shifting angles of their long staffs, or they speak by touching their foreheads together and humming—a low, vibrating murmur that travels bone-to-bone through the skull, completely bypassing the noisy air outside.

Their lives are small, warm, and intensely observant, governed by the micro-topography of a world where a two-inch rise in the granite is a dry continent and a four-inch dip is a sea. They know every whale-backed shield within twenty miles by the swirl of its iron and manganese stains, reading the rust and plum bands in the rock under the water the way a sailor reads charts. 

They farm nothing, because nothing will grow, yet they tend the landscape with the fastidiousness of gallery keepers. Each family claims a circuit of fifty or sixty of the swollen-trunked trees and the ruby-red patches of sundews and pitcher-plants between them. They never harvest the carnivorous plants—instead, they harvest *from* them. A child’s morning chore is to wade slowly from cluster to cluster with a long wooden spoon made of bog-branch, gently dipping into the digestive fluid of the larger pitcher-plants to scoop out the freshly drowned beetles and heavy-bodied flies before the plant can dissolve them, always leaving a third of the catch behind so the plant does not starve. They scrape the dark, slick film of cyanobacteria from the rock shields with stone blades, rolling the black jelly into sheets and drying it on their own shoulders as they walk, turning it into salty, leathery wafers that taste of iron and sun.

The great season of their year is not winter or summer, for the wet, simmering heat never changes. It is the *Fog-Drop*, that single hour every late afternoon when the air can hold no more water and the sky condenses around their knees and chests, turning the sun into a vast, fuzzy smear of orange light.

When the mist comes down and the ten-mile sightlines abruptly shrink to ten feet, the discipline of the day dissolves. The blinding mirrors of sky and water vanish; they are suddenly enclosed in a warm, glowing room of apricot cloud. The storks go silent. The families wade toward the nearest peat mound, guided by the low, chest-thumping *voom* of their root-drums. They climb onto the low clay domes they have built among the roots of the dwarf-trees, pulling their long, purple-stained legs up out of the water at last. 

There, in the warm, luminous soup of the mist, they sit shoulder to shoulder, rubbing pale kaolin into each other's skin, roasting flat-tailed amphibians and thumbnail frogs over tiny, fragrant fires of papery bark that hiss when the wet air touches the embers. For an hour, they are hidden from the sky. They press their foreheads together in the orange gloom and hum their long, wordless songs into one another's bones, while all around their tiny island of mud, the endless, invisible plain clicks and shimmers in the dark.
