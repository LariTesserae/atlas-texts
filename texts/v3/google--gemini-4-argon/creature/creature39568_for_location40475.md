At first glance, the thing clinging to the underside of the third condenser’s intake manifold looks like an ancient, hardened spill of industrial tallow, or perhaps a bulbous gasket of gray asbestos that has swollen with a century of absorbed grease. It is the size of a butcher’s block, pale and broadly domed, with a surface textured like dry, cracked heel-skin. 

Only if you watched it for the span of two or three of the chamber’s twenty-minute light-cycles would you realize that the crack between the creature’s rim and the iron plate is migrating. 

They have no name because no man has climbed down the oxblood limestone and returned with breath enough to name them, though if a boilermaker from the old world saw one, he might call it a *slag-caul* or a *walking blister*. They are the sole livestock of this scalding pasture. They are not animals in any way that matters to the surface world; they have no eyes to see the sour-yellow gloom, no lungs to be seared by the aerosolized rust, and no red blood to boil in their veins. They are something intermediate between a mollusk, a fungus, and a drop of hot solder.

Beneath its heavy, domed mantle of vulcanized hide—which is layered like slate and deadened to the point where a pickaxe would barely dent it—the creature consists of a single, massive foot of dense, sulfur-yellow gristle. Instead of water, its tissues are inflated with a thick, silicone-like fluid drawn from the old transformer oils and heavy machine greases that seep from the ruptured housings. It does not breathe oxygen; its metabolism is a slow, anaerobic smolder driven directly by the conductive heat of the iron it clings to. If you pried one off the hot metal and took it up into the cool, sweet air of the surface, it would not suffocate. It would simply cool down, stiffen like candle wax in a draft, and die of the chill at seventy degrees Fahrenheit.

A slag-caul moves by means of a hydraulic crawl so agonizingly deliberate it makes the creeping boil-cress look frantic. The forward edge of its gristly skirt softens in the heat, flows forward half an inch over the rusted rivets, and then firms up again as the rear edge detaches with a tacky, reluctant hiss. It leaves behind a faint, iridescent snail-track of refined mineral oil and stripped iron—the bare metal shining bright as a new coin for a few hours before the damp air rusts it over again.

They are grazers, though their grazing is an act of slow-motion industrial demolition. 

When a caul encounters a mat of boil-cress, it does not chew. It simply slides its heavy, scorching dome over the rubbery leaves and settles down for a week. From the underside of its foot, it secretes a thick, caustic lye mixed with iron-reducing bacteria. The terrible noise of the chamber masks the small, wet sizzle of the plant dissolving into a dark, metallic soup, which the creature then absorbs directly through the porous underside of its foot. What it cannot digest—the pure, heavy particulates of iron, copper, and lead—it pushes outward toward its back, where the metals precipitate into its hide. The oldest of the cauls, a massive, saddle-shaped patriarch currently wedged into the split seam of the second condenser, is nearly forty percent cast iron by weight. Its back is a jagged, terraced landscape of rusted scales and brassy nodules, indistinguishable from the scrap-heap it inhabits.

Yet they are not deaf to the room. In a place where sound is a physical bludgeon, the cauls have turned the din into a compass. 

Suspended within the thick, oily jelly of their bodies are three or four heavy spheres of pure, accreted magnetite, each the size of a musket ball. When the subterranean steam vents blow through the brass throats of the condensers, the low-frequency vibrations travel through the iron floor and set these internal stones trembling against stiff, cartilaginous plates inside the creature. They feel the topography of the sinkhole through its resonance. They know that a flat, brassy B-flat groan means a pipe is thinning and will soon rupture, offering a fresh, hot seep of oily sludge. They know the sharp, pistol-shot *crack* of a splitting organ-stalk means a shower of rich, rust-colored spores is about to settle on the east catwalk. 

When a stalk bursts, the three or four cauls within fifty feet will all, over the course of the next six hours, alter the bias of their imperceptible flow, turning their blind, heavy noses toward the fallout.

Once every few years, when the geothermal vein beneath the floor hits a multi-month peak and the chamber’s heat rises from a simmer to a genuine bake, the cauls congregate. They creep up the petrified root-gaskets toward the hottest crowns of the whale-skull condensers. They do not mate so much as they coalesce; two or three of the great, leathery domes will press their skirts together until their edges fuse, forming a single, sprawling scab across the hot iron. 

They stay like that for a month, trading genetic material and heavy-metal catalysts through their mingled oils, while the yellow light of the vines washes over them a thousand times. When they finally pull apart, they leave behind a cluster of small, black, rubbery cysts glued into the seams of the boilerplate. 

The cysts sit inert for seasons, waiting. They will not hatch in the mere warmth of the ambient air. They wait until the shifting scale in the pipes below redirects a jet of live, screaming steam directly across the plate they are glued to. Only then, bathed in a blisteringly loud, three-hundred-degree jet of vapor that would strip the flesh from a man’s bones in an instant, do the black husks split open—peeling back like tiny, charred flowers to let a pale, coin-sized dab of yellow gristle slide out into the grease, ready to begin its century-long crawl across the dark.
