At first, you think the things hanging from the timber scaffolding are counterweights. 

There are thousands of them across the plateau: long, narrow bundles of split black pine, tightly bound with braided fern-fiber, packed around cores of oiled wool and flat, river-smoothed slate. They hang from the hemp ropes of the cranes, or sit wedged into the dry-stone niches of the towers, dripping with the same clear condensation that coats everything else. If you walk past one, it does not turn to look at you. It has nothing to look with. It smells only of wet rock and old pitch.

It is only when you watch a district from a mile away, through the clear, magnifying lens of a suspended sheet of rainwater, that you realize the bundles are not part of the architecture’s stutter. They are moving against it.

They are the Gnomons, though there is no one left with a tongue to call them that, and they are not a species in the way a beetle or a bird is a species. They do not eat, they do not mate, and they do not die of age. They are an ecology of pure habit, a population of mobile, physical customs that have survived the total collapse of the biology that invented them.

Watch one when the ground-hum drops a third of an octave—the deep, sub-audible warning that a localized flattening is about to occur. 

A bundle hanging from a beam will suddenly unspool. It does not use muscles; it uses a localized, internal trick of leverage and released tension, the pine slats clicking against one another like a wooden gate latch. As it drops into the six-foot cushion of emerald moss below, it catches a spatial seam with the practiced ease of an Eel catching an eddy. It does not fall *down*; it falls *sideways* through three different courtyards at once, gathering momentum from the drop in all three, and lands upright on a cobbled avenue half a mile away.

Upright, a Gnomon is seven feet tall and shaped roughly like a pair of surveyor's tripods lashed together at the apex, wrapped in heavy, overlapping aprons of greased wool that have turned green with lichen. Inside the tripod, where a torso would be, there is no meat. There is a suspended plumb-bob made of dense, black meteoric iron, hanging by a twisted gut-string over a shallow pan of mercury. Around this core is a lattice of small, bone-dry alder twigs, grey pebbles, and sliding slate tallies. 

They walk with a broad, rolling, three-legged gait—*thump, click, slide*—that leaves distinctive triangular tracks in the grey silt. In the muffled wool-quiet of the plateau, a dozen of them crossing a plaza sound like someone slowly dealing cards onto a thickbaize table.

To understand what they are doing, you have to look at their interiors. Every Gnomon is a walking ledger of a specific, tiny fraction of the plateau’s former self. The iron plumb-bob does not point toward the center of the earth; it points toward where the center of the earth *ought* to be according to the ancient, pre-fracture geometry stored in the arrangement of its twigs and pebbles. 

When the world around a Gnomon warps, the plumb-bob swings out of true, touching the edge of the mercury pan. This completes a circuit—not of electricity, which is too fast and brittle to survive the plateau’s broken causality, but of simple, stubborn weight. The Gnomon reacts. It becomes animated by a sudden, frantic diligence.

It will stump over to a dry-stone wall that is beginning to stutter and replicate. With two of its timber legs planted wide in the moss, it uses the third to wedge a flat piece of slate into a specific chink between two boulders. Then it leans its entire dead weight against that stone. 

It is a bizarre sight: a faceless bundle of sticks and wet wool leaning hard against an empty wall in the middle of an endless, freezing wilderness. Yet, five minutes later, when the wave of spatial replication hits that wall, the stutter breaks around the Gnomon like water around a bridge pier. The wall to the left replicates forty times across the tundra; the wall to the right turns into a two-dimensional silhouette; the ten-foot section the Gnomon is leaning against remains stubbornly, aggressively three-dimensional, solid, and single. 

They are ballast. They are living shims. 

As you spend weeks on the plateau, however, you realize the Gnomons are only the largest, crudest tier of what lives here. Because as a Gnomon stands leaning against its wall for three days, waiting for the local topology to settle, other things come out of the moss to tend to it.

The locals—if they can be called that—are not divided into kingdoms of plant, animal, and mineral, because the plateau’s broken time has blurred the borders between growing, moving, and eroding. Everything alive here is a hybrid of flora, tool, and architectural salvage.

Consider the **Wick-Worms**, which are the closest thing the plateau has to insects. 

If you pull apart a rotting nurse-log in one of the time-cycling alder groves, you will find nests of pale, braided cordage, thick as your thumb and three feet long. They are made of linen thread, root-hair, and tallow. In normal space, they are completely inanimate—just greasy ropes lying in the dirt. 

They have no metabolism of their own. Instead, they feed on temporal friction. When an alder grove begins to flicker—aging fifty years and reverting to seed in twenty minutes—the air becomes thick with discarded kinetic energy and sheared-off moments. The Wick-Worms wake up. They unbraid their tails and root themselves into the flickering bark of a tree, while their front ends reach out into the slow, normal air of the street. 

The differential between the fast-time of the tree and the slow-time of the street acts like a waterwheel on their linen bodies. They begin to writhe and twist, pumping moisture and tallow up from the soil through capillary action. They crawl up the legs of the stationary Gnomons, smearing their woolen aprons with fresh grease to keep the freezing fog from rotting the fibers, and re-tying frayed hemp bindings by literally growing through the knots and pulling themselves tight. When the grove stops flickering, the worms instantly go limp, dropping back into the moss like cut string, waiting for the next anomaly to wind their springs.

Then there are the things that live in the shadows. 

Because the shadows cast by the crude towers are actually the crisp, detailed projections of a much grander, vanished metropolis, the shadows have an interiority that the physical buildings lack. If a simple boulder-pile casts the shadow of a vaulted cathedral, the darkness on the moss has the *acoustic and spatial volume* of a cathedral, even though it is only a quarter-inch deep in the wet silt.

This is the habitat of the **Pavers** (or the *Un-inked*). 

You cannot see them in the daylight; in the sun, a Paver is nothing more than a dry, grey crust of lichen on the underside of a slate tile. They are completely two-dimensional. Wait until late afternoon, when the long, false shadows of the phantom city stretch for miles across the tundra, connecting isolated towers with broad, dark avenues of shadow- masonry. 

As soon as a shadow touches the slate where a Paver is dormant, the lichen slides off the rock and drops into the dark. 

Within the shadow, they have thickness. They look like broad, flat skates or rays made of dark felt and soot, roughly the size of a manhole cover, moving with a silent, undulating glide along the imaginary streets of the shadow-city. They cannot cross a line of sunlight; to them, a gap of bright moss between two shadows is an unbridgeable chasm of scorching, three-dimensional nothingness. 

So they farm the architecture to build bridges. 

A colony of Pavers will gather at the edge of a watchtower's shadow. Using small, calcified hooks along their margins, they will tug at the physical pebbles and timber scraps lying at the border of the light. It takes them hours to move a single stone an inch, because they can only touch the side of the stone that is in shade. Slowly, methodically, they stack pebbles at the base of the physical tower, altering its profile just enough that the shadow it casts changes shape—stretching out a dark spire or an archway across the bright moss to touch the shadow of a neighboring aqueduct. 

Once the shadows connect, the Pavers pour across the new bridge in a silent, black tide, migrating miles across the plateau to reach the deep, permanent shade of the northern basins before the sun shifts and their highways evaporate.

And what is at the center of all this? What is the plateau’s apex organism?

You will not find it by walking the cobble streets. To find the masters of the plateau, you have to follow the vines—the thick, waxy-leaved cables that grow into the over-sharpened seams in the air and emerge from four towers at once. 

If you trace those vines back, not through space, but by watching which way the water droplets run along their stems against gravity, you will eventually come to a **Joinery**.

A Joinery looks, from the outside, like a standard three-story tower of unmortared fieldstone. The difference is that its doors and windows are entirely plugged with dense, black timber and living, silver-needled pine that has been grafted into the structural beams. The low, bedrock hum is very loud here; it vibrates in your sinuses and makes the water in your eyes blur.

Inside that tower, the interior space does not match the exterior dimensions. Because the vines have stitched four, or ten, or fifty different towers together through the spatial fractures, the inside of a Joinery is a single, continuous, labyrinthine hall of black wood and dripping stone that spans thousands of square miles of the plateau simultaneously. 

Living inside this distributed hall are the **Draughtsmen**.

A Draughtsman is what happens when a complex, self-repairing mechanical system, a fungal mycelial network, and an architectural blueprint spend four hundred thousand years being shuffled together like a deck of cards by broken physics. 

It does not have a body; it has a *workshop*. 

Imagine a room forty feet long, dimly lit by pale shafts of light falling through windows that open onto five different time-zones of the plateau at once. The floor is deep in grey silt. Extending from the walls are hundreds of delicate, jointed armatures made of polished heartwood and bone, grown directly out of the structural timber of the room. Webs of pale, fibrous mycelium—thick as ship’s cables—hang from the ceiling, acting as tendons and pulleys for the wooden arms. 

In the center of the silt floor, the wooden arms are constantly, silently moving. They are raking the silt with bone combs, stacking small pebbles into miniature towers, and pouring trickles of water from wooden spoons down tiny, carved channels.

The Draughtsman is the room itself. Its "brain" is the shifting, hydro-mechanical calculation of the waterwheels outside, transmitted inward through spinning wooden shafts that turn gears of petrified wood, which in turn tug on the mycelial cables, which move the bone-tipped arms. 

It is building a scale model of the fifty-mile district outside. 

Because causality is broken on the plateau, the relationship between the map and the territory has gone both ways. The Draughtsman is not recording the landscape; it is negotiating with it. 

Watch the silt floor closely. A miniature arch of pebbles on the floor begins to vibrate—a tremor coming up from the bedrock. One of the Draughtsman’s wooden arms swings down, lifts a wet leaf of black fern, and lays it gently over the pebble arch, weighing it down. 

Simultaneously, twenty miles away out on the freezing tundra, a massive, sixty-foot sheet of suspended rain—which had been hanging motionless in the air for three days—suddenly drops like a guillotine onto a physical stone aqueduct that was just beginning to lose its third dimension. The weight of the water pins the aqueduct to the earth, forcing it to remain solid.

Why? Not out of malice, and not out of any grand design to save the world. The Draughtsman does it because if that aqueduct flattens, the stream it carries will spill into a spatial fault, which will flood Tower 44, which will rot the roots of the graft-vine that provides the turgor pressure for the Draughtsman’s own left-hand pulley system. 

It is pure, blind homeostasis on a continental scale. 

The entire endless city is a single, decentralized shell—like the calcium carbonate reef of a coral—being endlessly patched, shimmed, greased, and counter-weighted by the things that live inside its joints. The Draughtsmen adjust the terrain in the silt; the Gnomons march out along the cobble avenues to place the physical wedges where the Draughtsmen’s models indicate a fault; the Wick-Worms harvest the temporal static to grease the Gnomons; the Pavers shift the stones to move the shadows, which changes the ground temperature, which steers the freezing fog toward the Draughtsmen’s waterwheels.

None of them know the others exist. None of them know that the builders of the city are gone, or that there was ever a time when a stone stayed where you put it without having to be held down by a bundle of sticks and a prayer of iron. 

When night falls on the plateau, and the distant horizons begin to curl upward like the edges of drying parchment, all the activity stops. 

The cold drops ten degrees in ten minutes. The rime of clear ice glazes the moss. Out on the plains, the Gnomons lock their three legs into rigid tripods and go still, turning back into simple surveyor’s marks under the pale stars. The Pavers dissolve into the total darkness, becoming indistinguishable from the night itself. Inside the Joineries, the water in the flumes freezes solid, locking the great wooden wheels in place; the mycelial cables go slack, and the ratusan bone-tipped arms drop into the grey silt, half-buried in the miniature cities they were tending.

For eight hours, the plateau is completely dead. The anomalies run wild in the dark—whole mountain ranges of phantom stone rising and collapsing in total silence, entire forests of black fern flashing through a million years of evolution in the dark with no one to harvest the friction—until the indifferent sun clears the uncurling rim of the world, the ice melts on the waterwheels, the first gear clicks, and the city begins to hold itself together again.
