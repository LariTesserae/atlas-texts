At first, you mistake them for cairns. 

Across the scree slopes and along the rims of the leaden tarns, there are upright stacks of grey slate, five or six feet high, draped in what looks like sodden, greasy felt. Because nothing in the valley moves unless it has to, they can stand like this for months. The heavy, stationary mist beads on their surfaces. The black, hovering lichen sometimes anchors a stray rhizome to their boots, humming faintly against their ankles until they finally shift their weight and snap the thread.

They are the Listeners, or the Wrights, though they have no use for names in a place where a shout is eaten instantly by the granite thunder at your elbow. There are perhaps a dozen of them in the twelve-mile basin—never more, never fewer. Since nothing here is born and nothing rots, it is likely they are the exact same dozen who have always been here, settled into the landscape like the boulders, having reached their own final, biological equilibrium.

Up close, their bodies are dense, barrel-chested, and squat, built to endure the relentless acoustic battering of the stone. If you were to touch one—and they will not stop you, only regard you with slow, watery indifference—you would find their flesh feels like cold tallow packed tightly around whalebone. The roar of the valley would shatter a normal skeleton over a few years, vibrating the marrow into soup and shaking the joints loose; the inhabitants have compensated by becoming partially non-newtonian. Under the constant, deafening hammer of the air, their muscles stiffen into the consistency of vulcanized rubber. Only when they enter the rare, dead-silent acoustic shadows inside the broken huts do they soften, sagging against the dry-stone walls like bags of wet meal, their breathing suddenly audible and wet.

They have no ears—any eardrum would have ruptured millennia ago—only smooth, concave depressions of thick cartilage behind their jaws. They do not hear with their heads; they listen with the broad, calloused pads of their feet and the flat palms of their five-jointed hands. 

Their eyes are huge, pale, and entirely cataract-white, yet they are not blind. Ordinary sight is treacherous here, where a horn-shrub can turn a distant ridge upside down and light refuses to cast an honest shadow. Instead, their milky eyes see the *sonority* of the basin. To them, the valley is not dark; it is blazing with fierce, overlapping auroras of sound. They see the standing waves in the tarns as lattices of pale gold; they see the shrieking tectonic fault-lines beneath the scree as blinding white ribbons; they see the acoustic dead-zones as pockets of velvet black.

They wear coats woven from the shed wool of the blinking goats, matted together with the oily, detached shadows that pool in the hollows of the rocks. You can watch one of them kneel beside a boulder, dip a cupped hand into a puddle of orphaned darkness, and smear it into the fibers of their cloak like pitch. It makes the garment utterly lightless and heavy, insulating them not against the cold—which is already inside them, permanent and accepted—but against the friction of the valley’s frayed reality. A cloak greased with raw shadow prevents a walker from accidentally stepping through a soft spot in distance and ending up two miles away with a broken leg.

Their days—though there are no days, only wakes and sleeps measured by the slow accumulation of fatigue—are spent tending to the leaks and snags of the basin. 

They are shepherds of broken rules. 

Because nothing grows and nothing dies, they do not eat food in any recognizable sense. Instead, they harvest the tiny, suspended increments of *potential* that the valley has trapped. You might see one standing waist-deep in a hovering sheet of rain, moving their long, flat-fingered hands through the suspended water with extreme delicacy. They are looking for drops that are still trying to fall—water that hasn't quite surrendered to the local gravity blind-spot, vibrating with kinetic energy that has nowhere to go. When they find such a drop, they pluck it from the air and swallow it. The trapped downward momentum hits their stomachs like a shot of warm spirits. 

Sometimes they harvest the horn-shrubs, not by cutting them, but by holding a flat piece of slate in the warped lens of space between the branches until the magnified, upside-down light of the distant sky scorches a dark, crusty rime onto the stone, which they scrape off with their thumbnails and chew like dried kelp.

When a goat skips fifty yards across a ridge, it leaves that lingering, dark afterimage in the air. If left alone, these tears in motion can fester, turning into permanent visual echoes or spatial ruts that trap the blind, air-swimming amphibians in endless loops. The inhabitants climb the slopes with long spindles of flexible horn and wind these afterimages up like cobwebs, spinning the leftover motion into dark, buzzing thread. 

Their relationship with the dry-stone structures is that of a crab to a shell it did not grow, or a mason to a ruin he cannot remember building. They do not live *in* the huts so much as they use them as tools and refuges. 

Because a six-foot hut can be forty paces wide on the inside while the door remains forever at your shoulder, the inhabitants use the interiors to store distance. If one of them needs to cross from the eastern scree to the western tarns, they will not walk the twelve miles through the screaming thunder of the valley floor. They will step into a hut, walk thirty paces into the dripping, cavernous gloom away from the door that never recedes, turn slightly to the left, and reach backward to step out of the doorway—which now opens onto a different hut four miles away, because all the broken spaces inside the stones share the same damp, subterranean lung of geometry.

When two of them meet on the flats, they do not speak. Nothing with lungs could compete with the cathedral-bell clangor and the iron-chute roar of the slate. 

Instead, they communicate by walking. 

They will step into a "bell-zone" together, standing ten paces apart. One will take half a step to the left, shifting their heavy mass onto a particular embedded boulder. The pressure of their weight slightly alters the acoustic pitch of the rock beneath them—dropping the endless shriek of the greywacke by a quarter-tone, or introducing a rhythmic stutter into the phantom avalanche. The other feels the alteration through the soles of their feet, reads the shift in the pale aurora of sound with their milky eyes, and steps two paces back onto a bed of humming black lichen, damping the vibration in reply. 

A conversation between them looks like a slow, deliberate, agonizingly ponderous dance across fifty yards of barren gravel, taking three hours to exchange four sentences, using the mountain itself as a throat and their own dense bodies as the tongue.

If you come here—if you manage to haul yourself up the last concave skirt of talus into the bruise-colored twilight, your teeth aching from the roar and your lungs burning with the thin, wet cold—they will not harm you. They have no weapons, because nothing here can be killed without an effort that the valley no longer supports. 

Instead, after a day or two, one of them will simply appear beside you. You will not hear them approach over the din; you will only notice that the pitch of the thunder around your head has dropped into a low, bearable hum, because their dense body is acting as a baffle between you and a screaming ridge of slate. 

They will smell of wet wool, cold iron, and the ozone-tang of old lightning. They will look at you with eyes like peeled eggs, studying the frantic, messy aura of noise your heartbeat and ragged breathing are making in their quiet, settled world. 

If you are dying of the cold or the thin air, they will take you by the wrist. Their grip is numb and heavy as a lead pipe. They will lead you to the low, three-foot wall on the barren flat—the one that shows an empty valley from the north and a ruined village from the south. They will not take you around the wall; they will push your head down and make you crawl *under* the top course of stones, through a gap that isn't there until your shoulders touch the slate.

And you will tumble out onto the cold grit of the ruined village that occupies no physical space in the valley. 

Here, the roar is gone, replaced by the dull, distant murmur of blood in your ears. Here, the others sit among the non-existent foundations, their rubbery bodies gone soft and broad in the silence, passing around a leather flask of heavy, mercury-thick water that never wets the throat. They will make room for you against a dry-stone wall that only exists if you look at it from the south. They will grease your coat with a handful of pooled shadow so you stop leaking heat into the sky, and they will wait, with infinite, stony patience, for the frantic noise inside your chest to slow down, settle, and find its permanent pitch.
