At first, you take them for the leather-lagging on a steam pipe that has split its copper bindings and begun to slough off in the warm current. You might look straight at one for ten minutes—which in the cistern-ward is barely the length of a sigh—and see only a bulge of dark, mottled hide wedged into the acute angle where a cast-iron stanchion meets the underside of a grating.

Then a relief valve two tiers down lets go its twenty-minute bubble. As the flattened lens of gas wobbles upward through the bladder-ferns, the bulge shifts an inch to let it pass. A pale, webbed hand, the color of tallow and the size of a dinner plate, reaches out from the fold, catches the trailing wake of warm sulfur-water, and tucks itself back in.

There are four of them in the nave, though they are so vast and so seldom in the same aisle that they feel less like a population and more like a single, slow condition of the water.

They are the Ward-Lumps—or the *Sump-Clerks*, as the last ditch-reeves called them before the upper sluices were bricked over a century ago. Whatever they were before they came down through the intake culverts—some giant strain of flat-skulled mud-salamander from the old fen-drains, or something older that lived in the flooded chalk-caverns below the limestone—the cistern-ward has remade them in its own heavy, mechanical image.

A full-grown Clerk is nine or ten feet long and weighs as much as a small boiler, though in the blood-warm buoyancy of the nave that bulk costs them nothing. They have no true shape of their own anymore; they have the shape of the gaps between the machinery. Their skeletons are predominantly cartilage, softened by generations of mineral-rich warmth, and their bodies are wrapped in a massive, loose mantle of velvety skin three inches thick. This mantle is the color of old gutta-percha and tarnished brass, so soft and heavy that when a Clerk settles onto a perforated catwalk to rest, its belly-flesh droops down through the iron holes below like Rising dough, hanging in little pale rows among the rust-colored ferns.

They never swim. There is no room for the thrust of a tail here, and besides, swimming requires an expenditure of blood and sugar that the slow galvanic trickle of the ward cannot support. Instead, they *pull*. They have four short, enormously thick limbs that end in five-fingered, knuckle-heavy hands. The palms are calloused with dark, horny pads grooved to match the herring-bone pattern of the iron deck-plates and the knurled rims of the brass valve-wheels. A Clerk moves through the nave the way a dry-goods clerk moves through a crowded attic: hand over slow hand, hooking two fingers into a flange bolt-hole, drawing its hundredweight of slippery, pleated bulk around the belly of a condenser vat, and then reaching blindly for the next vertical tie-rod. They can take half an hour to turn a corner, pouring their wide, boneless hips through a fourteen-inch gap between two high-pressure steam mains without knocking a single leaflet of blind-kelp loose.

Their heads are wide and flat as coal-shovels, almost entirely mouth and nostril, with two tiny, milky eyes set so far back behind the jaw-hinges that they are nearly in the creature’s armpits. Those eyes see nothing in the sharp, focused way of surface creatures; they are merely dim photo-receptors that register the tallowy amber glow of an inspection port or the sickly green smear of galvanic moss on a copper bus-bar.

Their real vision is in their chins and along their flanks. Running from the broad, shovel-snout all the way down the heavy, rudder-like tail is a double seam of deep, pit-like pores lined with golden cilia. These pores read the electrical weather of the nave. To a Clerk, the drowned borough is not dark; it is a blazing, three-dimensional scaffolding of currents and warmth. They feel the four-hour stroke of the great vertical pumps as a long, delicious tightening in the water’s grain. They taste the slow galvanic rot of a brass manifold thirty feet away through the dense brakes of vallisneria. They know which boilers are wholly dead and which still hold a warm, acidic heart of copper-sulfate liquor behind their three-inch glass ports.

Watch one when it reaches a live dynamo casing.

It does not graze like the blind crayfish. It drapes itself over the iron housing like a dropped tarpaulin, folding its broad, shovel-head down between its fore-legs until its throat-skin is pressed tight against the mineral rind of the metal. Along the sides of its neck, where a ditch-newt would have feathery external gills, the Clerk has three great, fleshy cowl-slits lined with pleated, purplish tissue that looks remarkably like the plates of a lead-acid battery.

As it lies there in the sub-audible hum that vibrates out of the deep strata, those gill-cowls open and close once every three or four minutes. It is not just drawing the scanty dissolved oxygen from the warm water; it is drinking the stray amperage and the sulfur-iron seepage that weeps from the gaskets. Their blood is not red with iron, nor blue with copper, but a thick, cloudy violet-brown that smells strongly of peat and bruised watercress. It circulates with agonizing slowness; their hearts beat once every two minutes, a low, hydraulic *thud* that you can feel through the soles of your boots if you are standing on the same tier of catwalk.

Because they stay still for days at a time while "charging" against a warm manifold, the flora of the ward makes no distinction between the Clerks and the cast iron. Every old Clerk carries its own small conservatory on its back. Along the ridges of their spines and in the deep, quiet creases behind their shoulders, rosettes of blind-kelp take root in the sebaceous, mineral-heavy grease of their skin. The pale, waxy creepers that love the power cables often jump the gap onto a sleeping Clerk’s tail, weaving a white, rubbery trellis across its hips before trailing off again into the gloom. The creatures never scratch this garden off; it weights them down to a comfortable neutral buoyancy, and the kelp-leaves leak a faint, sugary mucilage that the Clerks wipe from their own brows with a slow, greasy forearm and lick from their wrists.

Yet for all their vegetable torpor, they are not mindless. There is a heavy, deliberate housekeeping in their movements.

The cistern-ward is a closed equilibrium, and if too many pipes choke with scale, or if a relief valve seals itself shut under a rind of calcium-algal felt, the heat-flow from the deep limestone will falter, the water will cool by three degrees, and the chartreuse moss will die. The Clerks know this in the marrow of their cartilaginous bones.

When a brass relief valve becomes too encrusted to let go its twenty-minute bubble, the pressure behind the seat begins to sing—a tiny, high-pitched whine that no human ear could catch through the water, accompanied by a sharp spike in the local galvanic field. Within an afternoon, from three different levels of the drowned tenements, two or three of the great mottled lumps will begin to converge on the sound.

They do not hurry, and they do not quarrel when they meet in the narrow, leaf-choked alleys. When two Clerks must pass on a single eighteen-inch catwalk, one simply rolls onto its back, folding its broad hands across its pale, doughy belly, and lets the other haul itself directly over its chest, skin sliding against skin with a sound like wet chamois leather being wrung out in a bucket.

The first to reach the stuck valve will hook its hind feet into the grating, rear its heavy, garden-backed shoulders up into the amber-green gloom of an indicator dial, and wrap both broad hands around the calcified rim of the valve wheel. Its lower jaw drops open slightly—not in exertion, just in patience—showing a toothless gum-plate of hard, blackened horn. And then it leans. It uses its four-hundred-pound deadweight as a pendulum, hanging from the wheel for an hour, two hours, three, until the fifty-year rind of green scale gives way with a dull, underwater *crunch*. The brass spindle turns a quarter-inch. A gout of hot, rusty water and a silver globe of marsh gas belch out into the Clerk’s face.

The creature does not flinch. It closes its nostrils, lets the hot, sulfur-rich bubble wash up over its shovel-snout and through the purple pleats of its gill-cowls, and hangs there shivering slightly with pleasure as the warm cloud rises through the ferns overhead.

Once a month or so—perhaps when the barometric pressure far above in the surface world drops and the three-inch lens of stagnant air under the brick vaults grows thin and sour—a Clerk will make the long, labyrinthine climb to the ceiling.

It threads itself vertically through the four tiers of perforated plate, pulling its nine-foot bulk past the four-hour pistons and the five-foot gear wheels, until its broad, flat snout breaks the mercury-like skin of air between the masonry arches. In that three-inch gap of hot, copper-tasting darkness, it opens its mouth and lets go a single, ten-second exhalation of spent nitrogen and cold marsh-damp—a long, bubbling *plop-plop-plop* that echoes flatly along the wet brick groin-vaults—before taking in a vast mouthful of the stagnant air to roll around its belly as a fresh swim-ballast.

Then it lets go of the top-tier railing. It does not kick; it simply spreads its four wide, calloused hands and its heavy tail like a falling coat, drifting back down through the amber-green twilight at the speed of a sinking leaf, catching on a bundle of brass heat-exchangers halfway down, folding its limbs around the warm metal, and becoming part of the plumbing again before the ripples at the ceiling have even died away.
