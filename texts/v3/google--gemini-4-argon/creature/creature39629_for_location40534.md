They are noticed late, and usually from the waist down first, because from the waist down they are the color of the floor. 

A figure squats beside one of the stepped cisterns, his calves and thighs caked in a drying, cracking slip of red-orange clay that matches the terracotta beneath him. Above the waist, his skin is the dark, oiled brown of smoked river-wood, shining with a continuous, beaded sweat that never quite rolls off. He is broader in the chest and shorter in the leg than a modern man, built with the heavy, calm proportions of someone meant to carry wet earth in baskets all day without his knees giving out. His hair is black, coarse, and dressed with grey river-silt into stiff, square-ended locks that hang plumb-straight around his ears, motionless in the dead heat. Around his hips he wears a short, stiff kilt of unbleached linen, pleated so sharply that the ridges look as if they were creased with a hot stone and could draw blood if brushed against.

There are not many of them—perhaps three or four to a square mile of the basin—and they do not gather. They are spaced out among the green columns much like the pelicans, occupying the glaring, shadowless flats with the same dense, domestic unconcern. 

If you watch the man by the cistern, you see that he is not resting. He has a flat scraper of pale river-shell wedged between his calloused thumb and forefinger. With it, he reaches down into the quarter-inch sheet of lukewarm water and lifts a single turquoise snail from the seam between two bricks. He turns the snail over, scrapes a non-existent speck of silt from its operculum with a silent, practiced flick of the shell-blade, and sets it back down three bricks to the left, facing north instead of west. 

When the snail touches the clay, it makes that faint, dry *tick*—the only sound the air allows. The man’s chest rises and falls in the thick heat. His eyes are dark, slightly prominent, with heavy, sleepy lids that do not squint against the omnidirectional white glare. He stays crouched for twenty minutes, forty minutes, an hour, his heels flat on the sharp ninety-degree edge of the step, his reflection in the cistern water as crisp as an inlay of colored stone.

They are not the architects of this basin, though their thumbs are the Exact size of the prints fired into the clay. They are what remains of the labor once the work is finished and the time for completion has passed without anyone calling them home. They are the maintenance of a perfection that does not decay, which means their work has drifted sideways into pure arrangement.

A quarter-mile away, where the black flint walls step upward, two women are working a willow weir. One of them stands knee-deep in the channel; the other sits on the razor-sharp flint coping as comfortably as if it were a wool bolster, her bare skin taking no cut from the freshly knapped glass. The standing woman pulls a peeled willow wand from the weir. The wood comes free wet and yellow-white, smelling sharply of green aspirin and river mud. She hands it up. The sitting woman takes a small, crescent-shaped knife of cast bronze—the metal bright as a new penny, completely free of verdigris—and draws it down the slick wood, shaving off a curl of sap that isn't there. Then they trade places. The one who was sitting steps down into the water; the one who was standing climbs onto the flint, takes the knife, and shaves the wand again before wedging it back into the lattice. 

Their movements have the heavy, oiled precision of people working underwater, or of figures carved in low relief on a temple wall who have been granted the ability to exchange postures, provided they only use four or five postures in total. Sometimes, when the standing woman hands the willow stick up, her arm stutters—just like the scarlet birds in flight—her hand occupying the space by her hip and the space by her shoulder in the same instant, leaving a brief, solid double-exposure in the bright air before the gesture resolves. Neither woman looks at the stutter. It is simply the grain of the afternoon.

They do not speak to one another, because the air here eats voices the way it eats the clicking of the snails. If one of them parts their lips to sigh or to hum, the sound dies on the wet surface of their teeth. Instead, they have habits of touch and placement. When two of them cross paths on the open herringbone flats between the vine-pillars, they do not look up from the bricks, but as they pass, each reaches out and lays a flat, warm palm against the other’s breastbone for three heartbeats—*thump, thump, thump*—before continuing on. It is not a greeting; it is a way of checking the plumb, of making sure the other is still as solid and warm as the floor.

Their relationship to the built shadows is the closest thing they have to a private life.

Because the white sky presses down from every angle at once, the heat on the open terracotta is relentless, baking the top of the skull and the underside of the chin with equal, flat ferocity. The people do not sweat to cool themselves; they sweat the way the bricks seep—because they are full of warm water and there is nowhere else for it to go. When the glare becomes too thick in their eyes, they go to the reed mats.

Halfway between a column of elephant-ear vines and a lizard-stacked plinth, an old man—or a man built to look sixty, with tough, corded tendons in his neck and a belly like a taut waterskin—walks to a patch of woven reed matting. Woven directly into the yellow straw is the flat, jagged silhouette of a towering fig-column, twenty feet long and pitch-black, lying crookedly across the floor five yards away from the actual vine-rope it belongs to. 

The man does not lie down on the mat. Instead, he kneels at the edge of the woven darkness, takes a flat bone needle from behind his ear, and begins to unpick the black reeds at the shadow’s fringe. The black reeds are not dyed; they are made of an absence of light that has been dried, split, and soaked until pliable. They feel like cool cellar air against his fingers. With slow, methodical concentration, he re-weaves the edge of the shadow, widening a lobe of darkness until it matches the outline of his own broad shoulders and square-locked head. Then he lies down on his side, fitting his body precisely into the black patch he has just tailored, pulling the cool, flat dark up over his hip like a blanket. While he lies there, he is nearly invisible from above—just a flat, soot-black cutout of a sleeping man woven into the straw, his chest rising and falling with no depth at all.

When he rises an hour later, he does not leave the shadow distorted. He sits on his heels and weaves his own shape back out of it, returning the black patch to the exact outline of the fig leaves before walking away.

And then there are the ones in the ropes.

If you stand at the base of one of the colossal, braided hemp-and-reed cables that drop from the white sky, and you push aside the wax-heavy leaves of the monstera, you will sometimes find a pair of bare feet wedged into the lay of the rope, ten or twelve feet off the ground. 

The climbers are smaller, leaner, their skin dusted with the pale, powdery chafing of dry hemp. They hang in the vertical jungle of the vine-columns with loops of woven reed passed around their waists and the great central cable, leaning back into the harness with their knees braced against the wrist-thick aerial roots. They carry small pots of river silt slung from their necks and brushes made of chewed reed-ends. Leaf by leaf, they work their way up the towering pillars of greenery, painting a thin, invisible glaze of water and silt onto the waxy surfaces so the leaves will hold their fat, blinding droplets at the exact tip. 

They climb upward for days. From the floor, you can see the heavy leaves twitch slightly as a climber shifts their weight sixty, eighty, a hundred feet up, rising toward the place where the braided rope simply vanishes into the glaring white nothing of the zenith. 

They never fall, and they never seem to climb back down. Instead, if you are watching one of the wide, step-sided cisterns in the quiet middle of the afternoon, when the white light is at its heaviest and the lizards are stacked three deep on the altars, the glassy sheet of water at the bottom of the steps will suddenly part with a dry *tick*. 

A climber will step up out of the cistern—emerging from knee-deep water that is clearly resting on solid brick, though they have just risen out of it from the crown of their head to their soles—their hair slick, their reed-brush tucked behind their ear, their eyes calm and unblinking. They will pause on the top step to scrape the sole of one foot against the crisp, ninety-degree edge of a fresh terracotta brick, leaving a wet smudge that the heat takes three breaths to pale, and then walk out across the herringbone floor toward the nearest rope, ready to go up again.
