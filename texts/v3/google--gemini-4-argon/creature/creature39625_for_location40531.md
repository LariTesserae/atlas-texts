If you were to hang in the water at the thirty-fathom tier, wedged into the lee of a switch-house with your fingers hooked through the perforations of a cast-iron catwalk, you would at first think you were looking at the iron itself. 

Along the curve of a twelve-inch main, just where the boiling updraft splits against a bolster and curls back on itself in a tight, roaring eddy, the gunmetal surface of the pipe seems to breathe. A section of the smooth, silt-polished iron, roughly the size and shape of a cobblestoned paving slab, lifts three millimeters off the metal, shifts four inches to the left against a forty-knot shear, and settles back down with the heavy, dead-drop finality of a vault door closing.

It is not iron. It is a shell, or what became of a shell after forty generations of living inside a hydraulic cannon. 

The things that dominate the architecture of the surge chamber have no common name, because no one has lowered a net into this shaft since the administration of the third mayor, when the upper manhole was welded shut and paved over for a tram depot. If a municipal biologist from the surface were to pry one off the masonry with a crowbar—a task that would require two men and a block-and-tackle—they might trace its lineage back to the humble, fingernail-sized limpets or chitons of the river above, or perhaps the flatworms that rode in on the timber pilings a century ago. Whatever they were in the cold, slow mud of the surface river, the cylinder has hammered them into something else. It has forged them on the anvil of its own noise.

They are the *Schilder*—the Shield-backs, the Valve-plates, the Iron-skirts. 

At rest, an adult is a low, oval dome of overlapping plates, three feet long and two feet wide, risen barely two inches at its central ridge. Its profile is a work of pure fluid dynamics, identical to the streamlined cowls of silica that grow around the hot-water vents. The plates themselves are not merely calcium; they are heavily mineralized with iron oxide and sulfur drawn from the water, laid down in microscopic, cross-hatched laminae that give the creature’s back the exact dull, pewter-and-rust gleam of the scoured machinery around it. Silt and scalding grit have polished them until their edges are as thin and flush as a razor against a strop. When an individual clamps down over a riveted seam or an alcove bench, the water passes over it without a single stray vortex. It becomes a permanent part of the building’s relief.

Beneath that heavy, articulated carapace is a foot of solid, vulcanized gristle—a grey, bloodless muscle as tough as a vulcanized rubber tire. They do not creep on slime; slime would be stripped away in an instant by the ninety-degree current. Instead, their underside is divided into dozens of independent, piston-like suction chambers lined with microscopic hooks of black chitin. They walk the vertical walls the way a structural ironworker climbs a beam, locking three-quarters of their underside into the microscopic pores of the pillowy concrete while advancing the remaining quarter an inch at a time. 

They do not have eyes; there is nothing to see in the brown, vibrating twilight but the occasional orange smear of an under-volted bulb. They do not have ears, for an ear would be ruptured in its first second of life by the ripping-canvas shriek of cavitation. Instead, the creature’s entire body is an instrument of barometric and acoustic registration. Running through the seams between its eight dorsal plates are lines of tough, bristle-like sensors—stiffened with silica—that read the water’s violence the way a spider reads its web.

To a Shield-back, the dark cylinder is not dark at all. It is blazing with acoustic light. 

Every object in the forty-pace-wide shaft casts a sharp, three-dimensional shadow in the deafening roar of the check-valves. The sunken lampposts, the ceramic street signs reading *TIER 4 — NORTH MANIFOLD*, the hollow housings of the turbines, the fluttering ribbons of pale eels—all of them hum, ring, and deflect the locomotive *thrum* of the central geyser in distinct, unmistakable geometries. The creature feels the exact shape of an iron bench fifty feet away by the way the bench splits the sonic pressure wave of a slamming twenty-ton gate. It knows when a blind crab is scuttling three galleries down because the crab’s tiny, coin-flat shell interrupts the high-frequency hiss of the intake grates.

Most of the time, the Shield-backs are merely grazers of the vertical street. They move in slow, methodical herds of four or five across the brick facades, scraping up the tough, rubbery mats of mustard-colored sulfur bacteria with radulae whose teeth are tipped with actual magnetite—black iron ore precipitated out of their own blood. You can hear them feeding if you press your skull directly against the iron catwalk: a slow, rhythmic *scritch-scritch-grind* that cuts right through the low-end roar of the water, like a file being drawn across the edge of a spade.

Because the currents are so uniform in their violence, the creatures have developed habits that look eerily like municipal maintenance. They cannot tolerate turbulence around their margins; a stray eddy means the water is getting leverage under their skirts. Consequently, when a pipe joint begins to mineralize unevenly, throwing off a chaotic plume of bubbles, two or three of the big, pewter-backed plates will migrate toward the offending whistle. They will settle over the jagged calcium buildup and grind it down with their iron teeth until the pipe is smooth again and the water flows silent and laminar once more. Over eighty years, they have kept the sluice-tracks clear, the bronze seats polished, and the tiled signage free of scale, simply because a clean, streamlined wall is a wall that cannot tear them off and hurl them into the central abyss.

Yet the walls are only half their lives. The center of the cylinder—that empty, boiling core where an iron wrench would be tumbled like a leaf—is their commons, their highway, and their breeding ground.

Wait until the distant factory district dumps its reservoirs. 

Long before the water level actually rises, the pressure wave hits the bottom of the shaft at a mile a second. The amber filament-bulbs in their heavy glass cages dip to a bruised, dying red as the dynamos take the load. The great bronze check-valves at the base of the well groan on their trunnions.

In that three-second pause before the geyser arrives, every Shield-back on the middle tiers stops grazing. Along the submerged tenement galleries, across the rounded noses of the bollards, and up the bundled copper conduits, hundreds of sections of "iron" suddenly lift their leading edges. 

When the ninety-degree surge erupts up the center of the tank, they let go.

They do not fall; they *launch*. Tilting their heavy bodies at a forty-five-degree angle into the vertical hurricane, they catch the boiling updraft like flat stones skipped across the face of a gale. Instantly, the slow, ponderous grazers of the brickwork become high-speed gliders in a three-dimensional storm. They do not swim—no muscle could beat against that hydraulic head—they plane. By flexing the muscular hinges between their eight iron-plated segments, they warp their carapaces like the wings of a steel hawk, riding the invisible, spinning helices of the central shaft.

In the pitch-black, roaring heart of the well, where the rising geyser slams into the falling mass of the previous surge, hundreds of these heavy, three-foot plates banking and slicing through the scalding froth would shred a human body to ribbons in seconds. They miss each other by fractions of an inch, guided entirely by the screaming Doppler shifts of the cavitation bubbles collapsing on their neighbors' shells. 

This is how they cross from the North Manifold to the South Switch-House in two seconds flat. This is how they shed their old, pitted plates, letting the violent shear strip away the dead lamina while the new, soft iron-chitin hardens in the scalding heat. And this is how they mate—slamming together belly-to-belly at thirty miles an hour in the boiling central updraft, locked together by their suction-hooks for the six seconds it takes the surge to hurl them forty feet toward the brick dome of the ceiling, releasing bursts of heavy, hook-spined eggs that will only hatch if they are battered against warm iron within the hour.

At the top of the surge, just where the boiling silver surface slams into the pocket of compressed, steam-thick air beneath the weeping brick dome, the gliders peel off. Under the brief, dirty glare of the single sodium floodlight, you might see them for half a heartbeat: great, slick, gunmetal shields breaking the white froth like the backs of mechanical porpoises, shedding sheets of rusty water before they pitch over, dive down the perimeter backwash, and slam back onto the concrete walls of the upper galleries with a dull, muffled *thud* that vibrates through the masonry.

Then the surge ebbs. The water drops forty feet down the shaft, leaving the upper catwalks dripping and hissing in the pressurized brown twilight. The amber lights brighten a fraction. 

Down in the dark, thirty fathoms below the street that nobody walks, the gliders lock their skirts back over the rivets, sealing their edges against the stone. The iron benches are slightly thicker now; the flank of the switch-house has an extra layer of gray armor; the base of the sunken lamppost is flared with living pewter. The cylinder settles back into its locomotive *thrum*, and the slow, metallic *scritch-scritch* of iron teeth against warm brick begins again.
