Look at the flywheel on the number-three stamping engine, just after the twelve-minute blow finishes and the drizzle stops. Look right at the base where the iron-bark burl swells up to swallow the axle-housing. A piece of the burl will detach itself, wipe the pine-grease out of its eyes with a heavy, wooden thumb, and go back to scraping sulfur-fungus out of the governor gears. 

They call themselves the *Tappers*, when they use words at all, though mostly they don't bother. You cannot shout over the cicada-screech of the valve-lice and the bass-thump of the boilers without tearing your throat to ribbons, and anything whispered is eaten instantly by the bone-hard wood of the walls. Instead, they speak through the floor and the pipes. A Tapper standing by the north intake grate will rap the brass head of a wrench against a high-pressure sap-line—*two short, one dull, a scrape*—and four hundred feet away, inside the hollow, timbered shell of a third-floor tenement, three others will feel the cadence in the soles of their feet and come down to help pry a dead tread-crab out of a piston rod.

They are human, in the way that the tenements are still houses. The architecture of a man or woman is entirely there, just digested and re-extruded by the Sump. 

They are short, immensely broad through the barrel of the chest, and heavy-limbed, built to keep their footing when the twelve-minute gale hits. Their skin has the thick, poreless, gray-brown look of wet leather that has been cured in turpentine smoke. It is never clean, and it cannot be washed; from birth, their pores are packed with a protective mortar of tallow, machine-grease, and crushed sulfur-fungus that keeps the acidic condensation from eating them raw. Where their bodies take the most friction—the palms, the knees, the shoulders where they brace against stuck valves, the soles of their bare, flat feet—the skin has grown into horn-like calluses three-quarters of an inch thick, grooved and blackened until it is almost indistinguishable from the iron-strangler bark. 

When they grow old, the distinction fails entirely. You can find the grandfathers sitting in the warm, crooked elbows of the exhaust pipes up on the second-story gantries. They don't move much anymore. Their joints have seized with resin-gout, and the *Ficus ferrum*—which cannot tell the difference between a cast-iron bolt, a dead crab, and a man who sits still for three months—has slowly sent pale, velvety creeping-burls up over their ankles and thighs, anchoring them into the wood of the walls. They aren't dead. They sit there in the hot dark, breathing the turpentine mist, their eyes milky and blind, their thick fingers resting on the pulsing copper hoses. They are the ones who listen to the deep engines. When a valve-seat begins to wear thin a mile down in the root-bed, or the sap-pressure surges dangerously in the west wall, the old ones feel the arrhythmia in their grafted bones and rap their knuckles against the wood, passing the warning down to the plaza.

A moving Tapper wears very little, because cloth rots in a week down here. They wear aprons and gaiters stitched from the cured, wing-leather of grease-swifts, slick and shedding water like oilskin. Across their chests they sling bandoliers of tools that are half-forged, half-grown: iron pry-bars with handles of living root that have been trained to wrap around a wrist-bone; hammers headed with lumps of solid brass scavenged from old street-signs; hollow bone needles used for bleeding air-locks out of the braided copper hoses. 

Their faces are startling when the amber firebox-light catches them. Because the air is full of stinging mist and flying chitin, they wear goggles carved from the translucent carapaces of young tread-crabs, the edges sealed to their cheekbones with blobs of yellow tree-gum. Above the goggles, their foreheads and scalps are shaved bald and greased, though many of the younger ones cut shallow, geometric channels into the thick skin of their brows and rub the cuts with sweet-oil so the *lumen-grubs* will take up residence there. A gang of five or six Tappers working inside the dark belly of a stalled dynamo will light their own work with their faces, their brows and shoulders traced in squirming, chartreuse lines like the ghost-windows of the houses above them.

They do not view the engines as machines, nor the tree as a plant. To the Tappers, they live inside the ribcage of a single, cranky, immortal beast made of brass and timber, and they are its white blood cells. 

Watch a Tapper move across the open plaza between the ten-minute and twelve-minute marks. They do not walk like surface people. They have a low, rolling, flat-footed gait, knees bent, weight dropped into their hips, sliding their calloused soles along the polished drainage gullies rather than lifting them. If you lift your foot in the Sump, you step on a tread-crab, slip on its greased shell, and break your neck. If you slide your foot, you nudge the crabs aside like hockey pucks; the crabs don't even break formation, just click their mandibles and flow around the Tapper’s ankles like water around a piling. 

Similarly, a Tapper never waves their arms or moves suddenly. A sudden vertical motion interrupts the acoustic paths of the clack-moths, causing a thousand pounds of blind, frantic leather-and-chitin to crash into you like a falling brick wall. The Tappers move at the exact speed of a heavy piston on the return stroke—deliberate, rhythmic, oiled. They walk right *through* the ropy torrents of flying things, parting the living curtains with the slow, swimming sweep of a greased forearm.

Their whole lives are measured out in twelve-minute increments. 

Every child born in the wooden hollows of the third-story tenements learns the count before they learn to chew. They don't count in seconds; they count by the *chug-thump* of the nearest boiler. Four hundred and eighty strokes between the bells. 

At stroke four-hundred, the Tappers out in the plaza begin to drift toward the margins, or toward the deep erosion gullies. Those high up on the iron gantries check their harness-hooks. 

At four-hundred-and-fifty, the mothers in the tenements pull the heavy, grown-wood shutters across the window-holes—not to keep the steam out, for the houses have no backs and the steam gets everywhere, but to keep the flying swarms from being blown inside to thrash themselves to pieces against the walls.

Then the brass bell strikes. *Clang. Clang. Clang.*

A surface man caught in the plaza would panic, run, slip in the yellow runoff, and have the meat stripped from his bones by the boiling turpentine-fog before the forty seconds were up. The Tappers simply drop. If they are near a wall, they press their backs into the hollowed-out "shadow-niches" carved into the iron-bark for just this purpose. If they are caught in the open, they drop belly-down into the foot-deep runoff gullies right alongside the crabs. They pull their swift-leather hoods over their necks, tuck their thumbs into their armpits, and lock their knees and elbows against the sides of the groove. 

The tread-crabs scuttling for cover do not distinguish between the wood of the gully and the grease-caked back of a Tapper; within two seconds, the person is completely shingled over by three layers of dinner-plate-sized scavengers locking their legs together. The gale hits. The white-hot pine-fog roars over the plaza. Beneath the living armor of the crabs, the Tapper lies in a hot, dark pocket of stagnant air, listening to the scream of the steam and the massive, wet *sigh* of the wooden floor expanding around their ribs, drinking the heat.

When the valves slam shut and the warm drizzle falls, the crabs unlock and scuttle off. The Tapper stands up, shakes the hot, amber water from their ears like a dog, reaches down before the swarm can scatter, and casually breaks the legs off two of the larger crabs for supper.

They eat what the Sump makes. They boil the oil-stained crab meat in tin cans hung directly over the steam-leaks until the turpentine taste cooks out, leaving a stringy, sulfurous white flesh. They scrape the velvety white fungus from the brass pipes and mash it with the sweet, fat bodies of lumen-grubs into a glowing, greenish paste that tastes like sour cream and copper pennies. For drink, they never touch the amber runoff in the gullies—that will rot your gut in an hour—but instead tap the high, cool condensation-boughs near the roof, where the distilled steam collects in wooden cisterns before the bark can drink it all.

Their work is endless, petty, and vital. Left to itself, the *Ficus ferrum* would strangle the engines completely, growing over the fireboxes, choking the exhaust flues, and crushing the copper sap-lines until the boilers starved and went cold. And if the boilers went cold, the upper canopy would freeze, the carbon-steam would stop, the tree would go dormant, the fungus would die, the crabs would starve, and the Sump would become a silent, petrified tomb. 

So the Tappers prune. 

They go out in teams of three with heavy, curved chisels and iron mallets. While one watches the governor-clock, the other two chip away the woody calluses that are creeping too close to a flywheel's teeth. It is delicate work. If you cut too little, the wood jams the gear; if you cut too much, you breach the rust-rotten iron beneath, the boiler blows a gasket, and you are scalded to death by high-pressure sap. They are surgeons operating on a patient made of ship-plate and oak. When a copper hose splits, they don't replace it—there has been no new copper in the Sump for two hundred years. Instead, they bind the split with strips of swift-leather, smear it with irritant resin, and Deliberately coax a fast-growing runner of iron-bark to wrap around the leak, using the tree's own crushing strength as a hose-clamp.

At night—though there is no night, only the *Low-Stroke*, a four-hour period when the main governor drops the sap-pressure by twenty percent to let the iron cool—the Tappers retreat to the tenements. 

Inside, the buildings are like the chambers of a nautilus shell. The old brick rooms have been entirely replaced by smooth, muscular walls of gray wood that curve into the ceiling without corners. There is no furniture; the Tappers have used wedges and poultices over generations to irritate the floor-wood into growing low, smooth burls that serve as beds, tables, and stools. The air inside is thick with the smell of old sweat, roast crab, and hot pine. 

Here, where the bone-hard wood baffles the worst of the outside roar down to a dull, underwater throb, they actually speak. Their voices are astonishing—deep, gravelly, and rhythmic, pitched to the exact resonant frequency of the wooden rooms so that a single syllable hums in the walls. They speak in a clipped, four-beat cadence that matches the idle-stroke of the boilers outside:

*“West-line / weep-ing / near-the /third-joint.”*
*“Take-the / red-wedge / after / blow-time.”*

They do not tell stories about the surface. They do not know the surface is there. To a Tapper, the universe consists of the Root below, the Canopy above, and the great, roaring Belly in between where they live. They believe that if you climb too high into the braided iron girders of the ceiling, the air grows thin and cold and you simply turn into gray wood; if you dig too deep into the floor, you strike the big arterial sap-veins and are drowned in burning turpentine. 

When a Tapper dies—really dies, when the heart stops beating in time with the number-three stamping engine—they are not buried, and they are not burned in the fireboxes (human fat fouls the sap-injectors). Instead, during the four-hundredth stroke before the twelve-minute bell, the family carries the body out to the base of the great boundary walls where the arterial trunks rise into the dark. They wedge the body into a crotch between two massive, sweating roots, pack the joints with white sulfur-fungus, and walk away before the bell rings.

The twelve-minute gale hits. The pores of the great wall open to drink the mist. Within three days, the pale, creeping velvet of the *Ficus ferrum* has thrown a sheet of gray bark over the corpse. Within a year, there is only a smooth, muscular swelling in the wall, faintly human in its curve, warm to the touch, vibrating quietly as the high-pressure sap pumps through it toward the engines. The younger Tappers will sometimes lean their backs against these swellings while waiting for a valve to equalize, resting their greased heads against their ancestors, feeling the steady, violent equilibrium of the town square beating right through them.
