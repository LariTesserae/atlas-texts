To find what rules the mire, you have to stop looking for a silhouette against the pewter sky and start looking for an error in the perspective. 

Out between the humming boulders and the clicking meres, you will occasionally see what looks like a mound of drowned thatch—something the size of an overturned dory, draped in liverwort, grease-veined marigold leaves, and black silt. You might take it for one of the repeating shelves of peat, except that the rain-lattices never form directly above it, and the square plates of copper-colored moss always slide around its perimeter, never over it. 

If you watch it for an hour through the distorted, thick-glass air, the mound will resolve. It does not stand up so much as it unfolds through several missing feet of space. 

They are called the *Sluice-Draggers* by nobody at all, because there is nobody left to name them, though if a man were to see one, his mind would reach frantically for the vocabulary of old, heavy farm equipment and bog-iron. A Dragger is a massive, low-slung assemblage of biology and localized physics, roughly the bulk of a draft horse, though built on a wide, radially symmetrical plan that makes it impossible to tell whether it is coming or going. 

Its body is a dense core of wet, fibrous gristle and plated cartilage the color of split slate, slung beneath an overarching carapace of compressed peat and living stone that has been grown, rather than built, into its hide. It moves on six—sometimes seven, depending on the steepness of the spatial shelf it is climbing—jointed limbs that resemble thick bundles of peeled willow roots. These legs do not step smoothly. Like the neck of the gaunt heron, they stutter. A limb will lift from the black silt, blur slightly at the knee-joint, and simply exist four feet forward, already planted deep in the mire, bypassing the tangled, whipping rushes entirely. It leaves tracks that have no drag-marks between them—just deep, round post-holes in the mud that immediately fill with heavy, oil-thick water.

The Dragger has no head, because the valley offers nothing that needs to be looked at from a single direction. Instead, running around the upper rim of its peat-crusted mantle is a continuous, recessed seam of pale, blistered skin—a sensory organ that reads the barometric pressure, the phase-state of the surrounding rocks, and the electrical current of the soil. When a boulder nearby prepares to *bong* and shed its silicate slurry, the Dragger’s seam flushes a deep, bruised violet, and the creature braces its root-like legs, leaning into the acoustic wave before the sound even happens.

They are the grazers and the maintenance engines of this gone-to-seed machine, though they have no memory of being designed for it. They eat the errors that have grown too thick to pass through the valley’s throat.

A Dragger will wade into the edge of a tessellating mere, its heavy underside parting the clicking, hexagonal steps of water with a sound like a shovel pushed into gravel. Lowering its bulk, it extrudes a broad, muscular feeding-skirt from beneath its mantle—a wet, gray organ lined with thousands of calcified, comb-like plates. It presses this skirt against a patch of frantic, braiding rushes or a collision-ridge of dying moss. There is a sharp smell of scorched marsh-gas and ozone, and a sound like wet canvas tearing. The Dragger is not merely chewing the vegetation; it is discharging the built-up computational static in the plant’s right-angled veins, shorting out the bioluminescent grease and sucking the reduced, dead carbon into its gut. When it lifts its skirt, the rushes are no longer braiding themselves; they are just limp, ordinary weeds, boiled gray and lifeless in the cold mud.

They are slow, patient creatures, entirely deaf to the roar of the water and the thud of falling rain-columns, listening only to the deep, tectonic hum through their feet. 

Yet they are not the only things that have made a home in the rewritten rules. Every heavy creature in a swamp has its commensals, and the Draggers are hosts to the *Seam-Mites* and the *Joiners*.

The Mites are small—no larger than a man’s thumb—flattened, eight-legged things with shells like flakes of wet mica. They live in the spatial creases that the Dragger’s own heavy body creates as it stutters across the bog. When a Dragger’s leg omits the space between two steps, it leaves behind a microscopic, temporary fold in the air—a pocket of nothing that takes three or four seconds to snap shut. The Mites dart into these pockets to lay their eggs, hiding their brood inside the geometry itself where the chalk-colored eels cannot reach them. Seconds later, when the space corrects itself with a soft *pop* like a bursting soap bubble, the eggs are ejected back into the wet moss, already hatched into tiny, translucent larvae that feed on the silicate dust shed by the boulders.

The Joiners are larger, and much harder to look at without getting a headache. 

If you look up at the far cliffs where the waterfalls vanish into horizontal tears in the air, you will see dark, ropy masses hanging from the bottom lips of the spatial seams, swinging slowly in the cold updrafts. A Joiner looks at first like a tangle of drowned tree roots or a colony of giant, black tube-worms cemented together with dried mud. 

In truth, it is a colonial organism—part fungus, part muscular siphonophore, and part spatial scar-tissue. It does not live on one side of a tear or the other; it lives *through* them. 

A single Joiner colony might have its rooting-holdfasts anchored in the gray shale at the top of the cliff, its middle body dangling out of a seam fifty yards to the left over a plunge-pool, and its feeding-fronds emerging from a three-inch gap in the air right at ground level, two miles out in the middle of the mire. If you were to tug on the wet, rubbery black fronds waving in the sedge at your feet, a clump of moss would shake on the cliff face two miles away. 

They are the valley’s scavengers and its mortar. The spatial seams in the valley are ancient, and over millennia, they have a tendency to fray—widening under the weight of the water pouring through them, threatening to collapse a whole shelf of the basin into an infinite, repeating loop of falling mud. The Joiners prevent this. They secrete a thick, milky mucilage that hardens on contact with the ozone-rich air into a substance like black horn. They weave this horn across the edges of the spatial tears, stitching the broken air to the solid rock, holding the seams at a stable width so their own bodies are not sheared in half. 

From their low-hanging vents out in the marsh, the Joiners exhale the air they breathed in at the high cliffs. Because the atmospheric pressure at the rim is slightly different from the pressure in the foreshortened pockets of the valley floor, the Joiners whistle. It is a low, hollow, flute-like note, shifting in pitch whenever a curtain of rain stalls in the air nearby. 

When a Dragger finally dies—usually when its internal phase-organs calcify, leaving it unable to adjust its density when a boulder rings next to it, so that half its body turns to porous gray stone—it does not rot in the conventional way. The anaerobic mud and the forty-degree chill would preserve it for centuries if left alone. 

Instead, the nearest Joiner fronds smell the cessation of its electrical field. Over the course of three or four days, the air around the dead Dragger begins to wrinkle, like the skin on boiled milk. Tiny, hairline fractures in space open up in the mud beneath the carcass, coaxed wide by the black, muscular tips of the Joiner’s roots pushing through from a mile away. The black roots wrap around the petrified, peat-crusted hulk, secreting digestive acids that smell fiercely of vinegar and copper. They do not pull the carcass into the holes; they pull the holes over the carcass, slicing the dead Dragger into neat, foot-thick cross-sections that are distributed across five miles of the valley’s subcutaneous plumbing in an afternoon. 

By the next morning, there is nothing left on the surface of the mire but a flat, wet patch of black silt, a few scattered mica-flakes of crushed mites, and a faint, lingering distortion in the air that makes the sedges behind it look twice as tall as they ought to be. A square plate of copper moss slides slowly over the scar, the hexagonal water clicks in the wind, and a hundred yards away, a boulder rings like a bell, resetting the weight of the morning.
