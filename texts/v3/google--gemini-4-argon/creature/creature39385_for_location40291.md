To find the ones who hold the chisels, you must look past the traffic. 

Look at the fourth tier on the north wall, just beneath an overhang where the frigid runoff is diverted by a lip of scarred root-horn. What appears at first to be a bulging gall in the bryophyte wall—a fist-sized knot of bruised, grey-green tissue slick with condensate—suddenly shifts. A seam parts down its middle. Two long, flat limbs, the color and texture of peeled willow wands, unfold from the mass. 

The locals might call them the *Grafts*, or the *Rind-Wives*, though they have no voices to name themselves. There are three of them in this shaft. They are not insects, and they are not quite beasts; they are what happens when a burrowing vertebrate spends ten thousand generations negotiating a treaty with an aggressive subterranean flora.

A Graft is roughly the size of a winter stoat, though built entirely wrong for running. Its torso is short, wide, and dorsoventrally flattened, allowing it to press itself flush against the vertical terraces like a gecko or a patch of lichen. Its skin is hairless, thick, and cartilaginous, resembling the pale, translucent white of a turnip near the belly, shading into a tough, photosynthetic slate-green across the back and shoulders. In fact, the creature’s back is heavily colonized by the very bryophyte it carves; the sharp, crisp plates of the moss grow directly out of the tough hide along its spine, anchored into its bone-ridges like shingles on a roof. 

Its limbs are strange, levered things. The hind legs are short, bowed, and tipped with curved, hook-like nails made of blackened horn, designed for locking into the woven grass rigging or wedging into the crisp angles of the carved walls. The forelimbs, however, are disproportionately long and muscular, ending in three-fingered hands that have no soft pads at all—only calloused, shovel-shaped plates of yellow keratin. 

Right now, the Graft on the fourth tier is working. 

It does not move with the frantic, insectile scurrying of the swarm around it, nor does it have the zombie-like torpor of the toads. It moves with the slow, hydraulic tension of a root splitting a flagstone. Its metabolism is so cold that its heart beats perhaps four times a minute, pumping a thick, clear blood rich in natural antifreeze through its translucent belly. When it reaches out to adjust a split-reed pipe that has slipped its moorings, the motion takes thirty seconds to complete, yet there is a terrifying, mechanical certainty to the grip.

From a deep, vertical pouch in the loose skin of its throat, the Graft withdraws a tool. It is the femur of a long-dead bat, split lengthwise and ground against flint until the marrow-channel forms a wicked, curved gouge. 

The creature does not have eyes—only two flat, silvery tympanic membranes on the sides of its wedge-shaped head that twitch at the *plop* of water and the click of isopod shells, and a pair of fleshy, star-like nasal fronds on its snout that constantly uncurl to taste the cold air. It presses these wet fronds against the living wall. It isn't smelling for prey; it is tasting the *turgor pressure* of the plant matter. It can feel the sap rising in a rogue shoot behind the terrace wall; it can sense where the calcified root-horn is growing too brittle and where the green bryophyte is swelling out of alignment, threatening to warp the geometry of the aqueduct.

With a single, silent, levering push of its long arms, the Graft drives the bone gouge into the crisp plant-stone. A curl of pale-green shaving peels away, weeping cold, clear sap. 

Immediately, the city responds. Two plum-sized isopods alter their course, their antennae waving blindly as they converge on the Graft’s elbow to eat the freshly pared shaving right out of its hand. The Graft does not swat them away. Instead, it reaches down with its free hand, plucks one of the striped, agate-colored ribbon-worms from a nearby railing, and squeezes its tail. The worm secretes a thick drop of milky, astringent mucus. The Graft smears this mucus over the fresh cut in the wall. The chemical burn of the worm-slime instantly cauterizes the plant's wound, hardening the exposed cells so they will not sprout wild, ragged runners into the avenue.

This is the nature of the builders: they do not merely inhabit the ecosystem; they are its central nervous system, and the swarm is their toolkit.

Look higher up, near the ceiling plug where the violet bruise-light seeps through the translucent tubers. A second Graft is suspended upside down in a harness of braided sinew. It is older, its back so heavily overgrown with calcified root-horn that it looks like a hanging stalactite of pale jade. It is tending to the bats. 

The bats are not trespassers in the shrines; the alcoves were carved to the exact dimensions of their clustered bodies to trap them there. In this freezing box, warm blood is the rarest and most volatile resource. The old Graft moves along the edge of a sleeping cluster, using a tiny, serrated flint saw—lashed to its own thumb with wet gut-string—to trim back the sharp moss-thorns that threaten to poke the bats awake. Why? Because beneath the sleeping mammals, the Grafts have carved deep catch-basins filled with chewed pith. The rich, nitrogen-heavy guano of the bats drops into this pith, fermenting just enough to generate a tiny, localized breath of heat and a rich slurry of fertilizer. 

The old Graft scoops a handful of this dark, rich paste from the basin. It does not eat it. Instead, it leans out over the abyss and drops the ball into the waiting loop of a ribbon-worm pulley, sending the rich nutrient down to the lower tiers where a bridge was recently torn by a salamander, so the frayed grass-ends can be fed and coaxed into knitting back together by nightfall.

Down on the floor, among the geometric grid of sunken water basins, the third Graft is engaged in something darker. 

The basins are not just for drainage; they are rendering vats and nurseries. In the center of the deepest pool lies the pale, bloated carcass of a blind cave-rat that fell through the tuber-plug weeks ago. The cold water preserves the meat from rotting foully, turning the basin into a slow, icy broth. Here, the third Graft stands waist-deep in the freezing water, entirely unbothered by the chill. It is using a heavy bone chisel to pry the dead rat’s ribs apart, harvesting the long tendons and the tough, ivory curves of the bones. 

Around the Graft’s legs, in the nutrient-rich water at the edges of the pool, pale, gelatinous spheres are anchored to the moss-floor with threads of silk. They are the Grafts' own eggs—leathery, translucent sacs no bigger than gooseberries. Growing right through the outer membranes of the eggs are tiny, hair-thin green filaments of the wall-moss, already fusing with the pale, curled embryos floating inside. Before the young even hatch, the city is already growing into their spines.

Suddenly, a tremor runs through the root-horn walls. Somewhere in the earth outside this cistern, a heavy root has shifted, or a distant subterranean stream has surged. 

A hairline crack splits the third-tier aqueduct. Freezing water begins to spill over the lip, missing the reed pipes and splashing directly onto the main vertical avenue, threatening to wash a column of blind beetles into the central shaft and drown the lower terraces in ice.

None of the three Grafts make a sound. Their throats have no cords for screaming. Instead, the Graft on the fourth tier raises its flat, calloused hand and strikes the calcified wall three times with the butt of its bone gouge. 

*Tock. Tock. Tock.*

The sound travels instantly through the oak-hard plant matter, vibrating directly into the hook-claws of the other two builders, and into the sensitive feet of the twig-legged spiders, and through the bellies of the ribbon-worms. 

The entire blind city shifts its weight. 

The Graft in the water basins abandons the rat carcass and begins to climb, hauling a long strip of wet, freshly peeled sinew in its jaws. The twig-spiders immediately abandon their pruning on the third tier; they march in a silent, eight-legged phalanx toward the breach, their spinnerets already weeping thick, water-resistant silk to dam the crack. Even the broad-backed toad in the archway is used: the Graft descending from the fourth tier reaches out, grabs the massive amphibian by the loose skin of its shoulders, and shoves it bodily into the gap in the aqueduct. 

The toad does not fight back. It simply inflates its body reflexively, wedging its slick, slate-like bulk tightly into the V-shaped gutter, acting as a living plug to stem the freezing flood while the Grafts and the spiders go to work around it with bone-needles, sinew, and silk.

In ten minutes, the breach will be lashed tight, packed with chewed pith, and smeared with worm-mucus. By tomorrow, the bruised moss will have grown over the sinew stitches, hardening into a seamless scar as tough as bone. The toad will be pried loose and shoved back into its archway to resume eating beetles. The three silent masons will retreat into the recesses of the wall, folding their flat limbs against their chests, closing their seams until they look once more like nothing at all—just three wet, cold knots in the bark of a tireless, self-carving tree.
