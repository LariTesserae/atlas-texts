At first, you mistake them for a localized buckling of the basketry—a place where three or four secondary struts have pulled loose from a cornice and tangled together under tension. 

Only when a joint unbends, shifting its weight with the slow, hydraulic patience of a sunflower tracking a hidden sun, do you see the skin. It has the grain and matte dampness of soaked rawhide, vertically striated like inner bark, colored the dull, bruised plum of the surrounding gloom.

They have no vertical posture. In a world where "down" is merely the local prejudice of the nearest main-vine, a spine meant for standing upright is an encumbrance. Instead, they are radially symmetrical from the pelvis, like a pair of hands joined at the wrist. Four limbs, all of equal length and indeterminate function—neither strictly arms nor legs—branch from a compact, barrel-chested torso that houses lungs large enough to work the thin, desperate air like bellows. Each limb terminates in a long, flat, spatulate foot-hand featuring two opposing thumbs: one set for taking the broad curve of a timber, the other, smaller and tipped with a hooked horn nail, for picking at the tight interstices of a braid.

They move with a spider’s economy, never committing their weight to a new cane until they have tasted its gravity. Watch one crossing a junction where two ages of wood have been spliced together: a limb reaches out through the greasy chromatic haze of the seam, the skin on its forearm goose-pimpling as the temperature shifts. The fingers lightly pinch the bark, feeling for the direction of the sap-pull. Then, in a liquid, boneless inversion that makes the inner ear ache to witness, the creature lets go of its old gravity, swings its torso through a ninety-degree twist, and settles onto the new vine entirely upside-down relative to the path it just left, its heavy tool-pouches swinging up to slap against its ribs.

These are the Plaiters, though they have no word for themselves, because they have no words at all. 

Nothing in the lattice wastes breath on noise. The Plaiters’ faces are broad and nearly flat, dominated by wide, flaring nasal slits that drink the wet air and two enormous, oil-black eyes that lack sclera, giving them the unreadable, liquid gaze of deep-water fish or nocturnal lemurs. Where a mouth and chin should be, there is only a small, tucked masticatory orifice beneath a heavy brow of calloused bone, and below that, a broad, pale throat-sac crossed by veins that flush and pale in complex, rhythmic ripples.

When two of them meet on a narrow catwalk, they do not stop; they pass over and under one another like four-handed acrobats, their limbs interlocking briefly. Through the soles of their feet and the palms of their hands, they read the tremors of the wood. To communicate across a distance—say, from the crown of a sagging arch to a tension-crew working fifty yards down the rib—a Plaiter will take a heavy, horn-headed mallet from its belt, wedge its knees into a cleft, and strike a dried tendon-vine that has been pulled taut for the purpose. There is no sound in the air, not even a whisper, only the dull *thup* of the hide-wrapped head. But through the soles of your feet, you feel a sharp, three-note stutter pass through the wickerwork, followed by a low, shivering purr—a message traveling through the grain of the wood for half a mile before the damp moss drinks the vibration.

For centuries—or perhaps ten minutes, depending on which way one walks along the aqueduct—it was assumed by the few outsiders who fell into the lattice that the builders had died out, leaving their great work to the termites and the moss. 

The truth is that the builders simply ceased to be architects. 

Watch a family group—a cluster of six or seven, ranging from a vast, lichen-crusted matriarch whose joints creak like dry hickory to hairless, translucent-skinned infants clinging to her flank-hair—as they come upon a broken truss. They do not consult a plan. They do not gesture toward the twenty-mile gulf below them or squint through the mauve twilight at the distant pier where the bridge once landed. Instead, their nostrils flare. 

The severed wood is bleeding a slow, milky tear of stress-sap, rich with pheromones. To the Plaiters, that scent is an intolerable itch, an open scream in the chemical quiet of the world. 

They fall upon the break with a methodical, trance-like frenzy. They do not carry metal; their tools are harvested from the fauna and the ancient, iron-hard heartwood of dead zones. A young male unslings a flat blade made from the shed mandible of a constrictor-centipede and begins to pare back the ragged bark of the broken strut, his strokes long, shaving curls of pale cambium that drop into the abyss and drift sideways on the updrafts. Beside him, two others have anchored themselves to a neighbor-vine and are hauling on a cable of braided gut, winching a fresh, green shoot across the gap. Their chests heave silently, the skin of their throat-sacs flashing dark indigo with exertion and oxygen-debt.

They are no longer masters of the wood; they are its reproductive organs, its healing scar-tissue. The lattice has bred them over ten thousand generations just as surely as they once bred the vines. When a cistern-leaf begins to tear under the weight of thirty tons of warm sweat-water, the stress-hormones released by the straining petioles drift on the thermals. Three miles away, a dozen Plaiters sleeping in a woven hammock-nest will twitch in their sleep, their noses wrinkling. Without waking fully, they will unhook their thumbs from the ceiling, drop onto a slick causeway, and begin the long, loping, four-handed scuttle toward the scent, carrying bundles of six-inch black thorns and reels of stripped bark-twine.

Because the geometry of their home is rotten with time-slips and spatial pleats, the Plaiters have no concept of history, nor of completion. 

Sometimes a solitary Plaiter, gray-muzzled and stiff with the damp, will turn a corner on a narrow hurdle-walk and come face-to-face with a gleaming, wet split in a beam—a cut made by a flint adze that is, by the reckoning of the wood, still warm from the hand of his own ancestor three thousand years ago. The old Plaiter does not marvel at the ghost-work. He simply smells the raw latex, notes that the bone wedge driven into the fork has worked loose by a fraction of an inch, draws his maul, and gives it two solid, silent taps to seat it deeper before moving on into the mist.

They are not the only human-shaped things in the weave, however. The souring of scale has seen to that.

If you pry apart the coarse, hairy curtains of weeping moss that hang beneath the oldest, petrified junctions—places where the wood has turned as black and dense as anthracite—you will find the *Mites*. 

They are Plaiters, anatomically identical down to the horn-tipped secondary thumbs and the flat, obsidian eyes, and they carry the same bone wedges and flint scrapers at their waists. Yet a grown male could stand comfortably in the palm of your hand. They were lost in the perspective-drifts epochs ago, wandering down catwalks that widened until the world became gigantic around them, their bodies shrinking over generations—or perhaps in a single afternoon of bad walking—to match the tight, miniature weave of the root-hairs and club-mosses. 

They live in frantic, high-metabolism terror of the larger fauna, darting through the chinks in the basketry like woodlice. Where the great Plaiters weave the barges and the vaults, the Mites weave the skin of the world. They pleach the individual tendrils of creeping ivy, tying microscopic square knots with strands of spider-silk to bind the bark-scales together, sealing the wood against rot. If you hold very still against a guardrail, they will eventually emerge from the crevices, hundreds of them, smelling of crushed beetles and sour sweat, and attempt to weave *you* into the rail—Splitting the frayed hem of your trousers with tiny chips of chert, driving splint-wedges under your fingernails, and lashing your boots to the lichen with wet hair, working with the same solemn, glassy-eyed diligence as their giant cousins.

And then there are the ones who stopped moving altogether.

In the deep *kosong*, where the warm updrafts stagnate and the bruise-colored light is thickest, you will sometimes pass a junction where a great vine-knot seems to have grown an unusual number of burls. If you look closely through the sheen of warm condensation, you will see the curve of a collarbone, or a broad, flat foot whose toes have elongated, grown bark, and rooted directly into the cambium.

The Plaiters do not bury their dead, nor do they drop them into the void—in a world of circular gravity, a dropped corpse is likely to come drifting back through the mist fifty years later, bloated and covered in pale ferns, to lodge in the eaves above its grandchildren. 

Instead, when a Plaiter’s lungs finally thicken with the chronic wet-rot that claims them all, when they can no longer haul themselves across the greasy seams of mismatched space, they find a weak point in the architecture. They look for a hairline fracture in a load-bearing rib, or a place where a wattle-screen has begun to unravel. 

The dying Plaiter crawls into the gap. They wedge their pelvis against one strut, reach across the split with all four limbs, and lock their double-thumbs around the opposing cane, pulling the ruin closed with the last of their strength. Their kin come then, with stone knives and pots of stinging, gummy sap. They score the skin of the dying one’s forearms and calves, scrape the bark of the living wood raw, and bind the two together with tight wraps of parasitic runner-vine.

It does not take long in the warm, wet dark. Within a week, the pale rootlets have found the veins; within a year, the sap has replaced the blood, though the great dark eyes remain open and wet under a film of translucent bark for decades, still watching the gliders drift across the twenty-mile bays. The wood takes the bone and the muscle and uses the tension. The Plaiter becomes a living gusset, a warm, breathing knee-brace holding up a span of causeway, their heart beating three times a minute in time with the slow, hydraulic surge of the world’s sap, holding the void together with their own ribs.
