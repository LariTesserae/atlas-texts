If you stay in the chamber long enough for your ears to go numb and your eyes to adjust to the shadowless, milk-white glare, you begin to notice that the stone is not entirely stationary.

You will be watching one of the great parabolic ribs of limestone—watching the fist-sized spheres of water hurtling past it like shining cannonballs—and you will realize that a section of the wet, polished bone-grey rock is breathing. 

It is an ambush predator, though it has no eyes to see its prey and no teeth to tear it. Its shape is that of a thick, heavy mantle, roughly the size of a butcher’s apron, draped seamlessly over the curved stone. Its skin is a marvel of mimicry: a dense, fibrous epidermis that has calcified just enough to match the exact matte-gloss sheen of the eroded limestone. It is completely flat at the edges, tapering down to a translucent fringe only a few microns thick, creating a perfect, uninterrupted contour with the floor so the shearing gales and horizontal deluges find no lip to catch on, no seam to peel back.

It moves only during the *thrum*. 

To move in the silence between pulses would be suicide; the skitters would feel the microscopic tremor of its approach through the rock and vanish into their crevices. Instead, the mantle waits for the nearest spindle to click and drop the air pressure. In the two seconds of absolute acoustic chaos that follow—when the roar of displaced air and crashing water deafens every sensory organ in the cavern—the creature releases the vacuum-seal of its belly. It rides the grease-slick film of warm water, hydroplaning a few inches forward along the smooth trough, guided by a nervous system that reads the mathematical geometry of the carved rock like a braille map. THE *WHOOMP* hits, the water rips upward, and before the droplets have even reached the apex of their braided arcs, the mantle has slammed its edges back down, purging the water from beneath itself to lock onto the stone with ten thousand microscopic suction-pits. 

When a skitter finally miscalculates—when it darts out during a lull to scrape the glowing puddle-scum and runs directly over what it assumes is a slight swell in the limestone—the mantle does not snap or strike. It simply softens. The center of its body drops its muscular tension, becoming a sudden, viscous tar-pit of muscular folds that wraps upward around the crustacean’s alabaster shell, sealing it against the stone in a pocket of crushing digestives. It takes three days for the mantle to dissolve a skitter, during which time six hundred thousand machine-storms will pass harmlessly over its back.

Look up from the floor, however, into the braided streams themselves, and you will find the things that have abandoned the stone entirely.

You cannot see them while the water is on the ground. In the puddles, they are nothing—transparent, gelatinous ribbons no thicker than cellophane, tangled helplessly in the warm runoff like shed skin. They have no musculature to crawl and no rigid structure to hold a shape against normal gravity. 

Their life occurs only in the four-second intervals when the water is in flight.

When the spindles fire and the puddles shear sideways, tearing into millions of trembling, fist-sized spheres, these ribbons are lofted into the air. Caught inside the individual globes of hurtling water, they snap open like parachutes. They are hydro-sailors, creatures composed almost entirely of structured water and elastic cartilage, shaped like multi-winged arrowheads. Inside the flying sphere of water, they are suddenly apex organisms. 

They use the surface tension of the airborne water-ball as a wall to push against, darting from one side of the sphere to the other to gorge on the dense soup of photic nodes trapped inside with them. As they eat the glowing machinery, their own translucent guts ignite with a concentrated, blazing white fire, turning them into living filaments of lightning inside the hurtling drops. 

More remarkably, they can steer. By flicking their cartilaginous vanes at the exact frequency of the spindle’s bass-heavy *thrum*, they can alter the surface tension of the water sphere they inhabit, causing it to flatten or elongate in mid-air. They ride the acoustic waves, guiding their liquid vehicles to collide with other spheres in mid-flight, leaping from drop to drop across the roaring white void, mating and feeding in the three seconds before the spindle desynchronizes and the whole chaotic river slams back into the stone troughs, reducing them once more to helpless, invisible jelly waiting for the next gravity-shear.

And then, there are the things on the spindles.

It seems impossible that anything could live on the dark, non-reflective alloy of the needles themselves, given that they vibrate across a tiny margin of time and emit enough acoustic torque to liquify human organs at close range. Nothing *fleshy* lives there. 

Instead, around the central mass of each hovering needle, there is a faint, shimmering halo that looks at first like heat-haze or an optical illusion caused by the time-blur of the metal. It is neither. It is a swarm of *chime-mites*—organisms that have crossed the threshold between biology and solid-state physics.

Over millennia of exposure to the wire-scrub that dissolves the copper-rich limestone, and the relentless photic nodes drifting in the mist, a strain of extremophile bacteria began incorporating the dissolved metals and broken, microscopic hulls of dead light-nodes into their cell walls. Generation by generation, they grew heavier, harder, and more resonant, until they evolved into tiny, polyhedral flecks of bio-metallic crystal, no larger than grains of coarse salt. 

They do not crawl on the spindles; they orbit them. 

Locked in the violent gravitometric grip of the ancient machines, the chime-mites exist in a permanent, tight cloud around the dark alloy, suspended a few millimeters from the surface where the temporal vibration is sharpest. They have no metabolism as we understand it. They do not eat carbon; they feed directly on the waste-state of the spindles’ work. When the spindles manipulate local gravity, they bleed off tiny fractions of kinetic and thermal shear. The chime-mites absorb this raw physical stress through their crystalline lattices, using the violent energy to split water molecules from the mist directly into hydrogen and oxygen to fuel their microscopic, internal chemistries.

You only know they are alive because of how they respond to the cycle. 

Just before a spindle fires—a half-second before the mechanical *click* that warns the skitters to clamp down—the swarm of mites aligns. Millions of tiny, crystalline facets snap into uniform orientation around the dark needle, turning the blurry alloy into a sudden, razor-sharp mirror that catches the blinding white glare of the mist. As the bass-heavy *thrum* erupts, the mites are flung outward by the acoustic shockwave, expanding into a wide, shimmering ring like the rings of Saturn, vibrating against one another so rapidly that they add a high, singing, metallic *shiver* to the deep roar of the water—a sound like a million tiny brass bells being shaken in a hurricane. 

When the pulse ends, the gravity well snaps them back inward, tight against the dark needle, drinking the heat of the machine's exertion.

And beneath it all—beneath the skitters, the mantles, the sky-jellies, and the singing dust—there is the stone itself. 

If you were to chip away a piece of the smooth, wax-like limestone—a task that would require diamond-tipped tools, as the rock has been acoustically compacted into something approaching the density of steel—you would find that the grey-white color is not uniform. Two inches beneath the polished surface, running parallel to the sweeping troughs and parabolic ribs, is a thin, dark band of violet-black. 

It is a subterranean reef of endolithic slime-mold, a single, contiguous organism that spans the entire aircraft-hangar breadth of the bowl. It hates the light, and it hates the wind, so it lives *inside* the rock, moving through the microscopic pores of the limestone at a rate of millimeters per century. 

It is the true master of the basin’s geometry. As the flying water erodes the stone above it, the mold secretes a hardening agent—a biological cement—into the rock fibers directly beneath the areas of highest acoustic stress, while secreting mild acids into the areas where the water falls slack. Slowly, imperceptibly, over tens of thousands of years, the mold is editing the erosion. It is subtly reshaping the limestone ribs and runnels, tuning the cavern floor like the soundboard of a massive violin, directing the ricochets of the flying water and the echoes of the *thrum* to maximize the growth of the copper wire-scrub above it, whose deep, dissolving roots the mold quietly harvests from below. 

The machines make the storm, and the storm carves the stone, but the stone is alive, and it is teaching the storm where to land.
