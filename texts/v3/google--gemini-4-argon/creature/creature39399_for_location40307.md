They are not seen until they move, and they do not move unless the pitch is sliding.

To look at the wall of the Condenser is to see only the overlapping shields of the philodendrons, the weeping orange seams of rust, and the slow, plate-sized beetles. But occasionally, a section of the root-matting five feet high and wide as a doorframe will simply detach its upper edge from the iron, peel backward without falling, and reveal the pale, ribbed underside of a creature that has been pressed into the foliage like lead into a mold.

Before the people remain, there are the **Gasket-Worms**, though they are neither worms nor truly separate from the wall anymore. They are great, flat, colonial mantles of gristle and velvet-like skin, shaped like pelts nailed to a barn door. They have no eyes and no heads, only a perimeter of hundreds of tiny, hooking toes that grip the fibrous roots, and a body so thin and pliable that when they settle over a cluster of rivets, you can see the round heads of the iron bolts embossing their backs from underneath. They feed by pressing their pale undersides directly against the corroded seams of the boiler plate, secreting an oily lime that neutralizes the plant-acid, and licking up the warm, iron-rich butter that forms between metal and moss. They are living patches. When a breach in the hull grows too wide and the freezing, thin air of twenty thousand feet begins to whistle through the lichen-windows, a Gasket-Worm will crawl over the hole and seal it with its own body, drawn by the temperature differential, holding itself against the vacuum-suck of the outside gale for ten or twenty years until it dies and dries into a permanent, leathery horn that the strangler figs promptly weave into the wall.

And tending the worms, or perhaps tended by them, are the **Tamp-Men**.

To call them men is an archaeology of the word. Five hundred years ago, when the great dirigible-tethers or sky-foundries still had crews, a maintenance gang may have been stranded here when the lower boarding-stages fell away, or perhaps they were left behind intentionally as living self-repair mechanisms—cheaper than clockwork and able to reproduce in the grease-warmth. Whatever they were, the cylinder has flattened them, deafened them, and stretched them to fit the curvature of the boiler plate.

A Tamp-Man has no use for a spine that stacks vertically like a pile of coins; the constant, hammering acoustic pressure from the central shaft would snap a standing back or throw an upright creature off the catwalks into the shredding air. Instead, their skeletons have splayed outward like the ribs of an umbrella, or a frog pressed under a stone. Their hips and shoulders are double-jointed and wide, allowing their chests and bellies to lie completely flush against the wet moss while their limbs reach out to the sides like the legs of a brittle-star. Their fingers and toes have lengthened into four-inch, hook-jointed callipers, tipped not with flat nails, but with thick, curved wedges of horn the color of old tallow, grown specifically to slot into the half-inch gaps between overlapping iron plates.

They have no ears—not even the holes where ears should be. The skin over their temples is a smooth, drum-tight scar of fibrous cartilage, thick enough to keep the screaming F-sharp of the descending piston from liquefaction of the brain. Yet they are more creatures of sound than anything with an eardrum could ever be. Their entire ribcage is a resonator. The breastbone has widened into a flat, cartilaginous keel coated in a thick pad of yellow callous. When a Tamp-Man wishes to know what is happening sixty feet higher up the curve of the wall, he presses his sternum hard against the naked head of a rivet and closes his eyes. 

Through his chest, he reads the cylinder. He feels the dry, high-frequency chatter of a brass valve-seat wearing thin; he feels the wet, heavy thrum of a root-mat that has soaked up too much condensation and is about to tear loose under its own weight; he feels the tiny, rhythmic *tick-tick-tick* of another Tamp-Man three hundred feet away on the opposite wall, tapping a piece of scrap-iron against a girder.

They are hairless, because hair traps the acidic rust-slime and rots the skin. Instead, from the crown of their flattened skulls down to the base of their spines, they secreting a heavy, tallow-like sebum mixed with the paraffin-sweat they scrape from the gear-casings. They smear this over themselves until they gleam in the green stained-glass light like figures modeled from lard and copper-grease. The orange rust-paste sticks to their knees and elbows, giving them the pied, oxidized coloration of the hull itself.

Their lives are divided into two six-hour halves: the **Drop** and the **Draw**.

During the Draw—when the great central piston is rising, and the note of the cylinder slides down into the low, organ-pipe C-sharp—the air is thick and sluggish with bass. The vibration is wide and rocking, like the swell of a heavy sea. This is the waking time. The Tamp-Men unhook their toes from the sleeping-clefts under the catwalks and slide out across the vertical jungle. They do not walk; they *flow* like drops of mercury moving uphill, four limbs working in an opposition so smooth that their torsos never rise more than three inches from the leaves. 

They are farmers of the leak and shepherds of the rust. A Tamp-Man carries a single tool, passed down through ten generations until the handle is worn to the exact negative of a six-fingered hand: a heavy, bronze-headed caulking-chisel that cannot rust. With it, he moves along the seams. Where the strangler figs have pried two boiler plates apart, threatening to let the precious, warm internal steam escape into the stratosphere, the Tamp-Man wedges the root out, stuffs the gap with a mash of chewed yellow moss and black beetle-shell, and hammers it tight with the side of his fist. They do not do this out of duty to the forgotten builders of the cylinder; they do it because the warmth is their blood. If the cylinder loses pressure, the frost creeps in from the hull, the ferns blacken, and the world dies.

They harvest the dinner-plate beetles, not by killing them, but by prying them up gently with the bronze chisel to scrape the sweet, white fungal curd that grows in the warm darkness between the beetle’s flat belly and the moss. They catch the spider-rails with nooses made of braided fig-sinew, though they eat only the heavy, muscular thighs, tucking the bird’s iron-red feathers into the cracks of the hull to act as wicks that draw the condensation down toward their favorite drinking-leaves.

They have no voices. A throat that opened to make a sound in the Singing Well would have its vocal cords torn by the standing waves before a syllable could form. Their language is **The Calk**.

When two Tamp-Men meet on the narrow, half-digested ribbon of a spiral catwalk, they do not look at one another—their eyes are small, pale-amber things, recessed deep under shelf-like brows to keep the stinging rust-water out, useful only for seeing three feet ahead in the green gloom. Instead, they crawl over one another until their flat, calloused chests are pressed against the same iron plate, or they lay their palms flat against the top of the other’s skull. Then, with their horn-tipped thumbs, they click. 

It is a language of percussion and tension. A scrape of a nail across a rusted bolt-head means one thing; a double-tap of the bronze chisel against a hollow plate means another. They speak in the grammar of the cylinder’s stresses. To say *hunger*, a Tamp-Man taps the rhythm of a dry, un-greased bearing. To say *danger* or *death*, he mimics the sharp, high-pitched *ping* of a braided steel cable snapping a single wire up in the sky. Their poetry—and they have it, tapped out over hours while clinging to the warm iron in the dark—consists of long, complex counter-rhythms played against the six-hour slide of the piston’s note, finding the tiny mathematical pockets of silence inside the C-sharp and filling them with the story of the great valve-blowout of three generations ago, or the memory of a massive, dead bird from the upper sky that once fell through the top vents and lodged in the piston-collar, feeding the tribe on strange, saltless meat for a month.

Then, the piston reaches the top of the cylinder. There is a pause of four minutes where the pressure equalizes, and the entire three-hundred-foot drum shudders as the counterweights outside shift on their greased chains.

The **Drop** begins.

As the ten-foot column of oiled iron begins its descent through the dead-zone, the pitch starts its slow, agonizing climb toward the screaming F. The acoustic wall thickens. The air in the center of the shaft begins to shimmer like heat-haze as the sound waves cross and chop at one another, turning the empty space into an invisible blender that can strip the flesh from a hand extended too far outward from the leaves.

As the note climbs past D and into E-flat, the Tamp-Men retreat. The high frequency makes their teeth ache in their gums and turns the fluid in their joints warm and bubbly if they stay on the bare iron. They slide back toward the **Baffle-Nests**—deep, three-foot pockets hollowed out behind the thickest bolsters of the strangler-fig roots, where the dead, cork-like wood of ancient vines absorbs the worst of the scream.

Here, in families of four or five, they stack themselves like saucers in a cupboard. The oldest and toughest—the **Anvils**, whose backs have grown a half-inch-thick carapace of dead, grey horn from sixty years of rubbing against the hull—lie on the outside, facing the shaft, their broad backs acting as acoustic shields. Beneath them lie the younger adults, and pressed directly against the warm, sweating iron of the hull’s skin are the young—little, translucent, frog-limbed things no bigger than a man's hand, who cannot yet endure the F-sharp without their soft bones warping.

Sometimes, a Tamp-Man will pull a Gasket-Worm over the entrance of the nest like a blanket, hooking the creature’s toes into the roots around them so the family is sealed in a dark, airless pocket that smells intensely of warm paraffin, crushed fern, and iron-oxide.

For the last two hours of the Drop, when the cylinder’s scream is at its zenith and the pleated leaves outside are thrashing their warm, vaporized rain into the shredding air, nothing moves in the nest. They do not sleep, because the vibration is too violent for dreams; instead, they fall into the **Stupor of the Note**. Their hearts slow down until they beat in exact, fractional divisions of the cylinder’s frequency. Their blood moves not solely by the pumping of the heart, but by the acoustic peristalsis of the scream itself pushing through their veins.

And once in a great while—perhaps once in a lifetime—during the height of the F-sharp, an Anvil who has grown too heavy, whose horn-tipped fingers have grown too thick and arthritic to slot into the plate-seams, will not crawl into the Baffle-Nest. 

He will climb instead out onto the very lip of a broken catwalk, where the perforated steel ends in mid-air above the three-hundred-foot drop. He will turn his flat, greased belly toward the empty center of the shaft, toward the descending black pillar of the piston ten yards away. And as the scream hits its peak, when the air is solid with sound, he will simply unhook his toes and push off.

He does not fall straight down. The standing waves catch him instantly. In the violent, invisible geometry of the central dead-zone, the acoustic pressure holds the flat body suspended for ten, twenty, thirty seconds—spinning him slowly like a dry leaf in an eddy, while the intolerable vibration shakes the sixty years of caked rust, paraffin, and grey horn from his skin in a luminous orange cloud. The mist of him drifts outward, blown back into the walls by the pressure, settling over the philodendrons and the orchids as a fine, rich dust, before the piston drops another inch, the harmonic shifts, and what is left of the lightened bones drops silently through the bottom grating into the four miles of white cloud below.
