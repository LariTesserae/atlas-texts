To see a resident of the cleft on a rope-walk is to first mistake them for a bundle of the chasm’s own moss that has grown legs and decided to migrate. They are bulky, round-shouldered figures, padded to twice their natural width against a cold that never relents and a silence that must never be broken.

Because there is no fire, a human body is the only furnace it will ever know. Their clothing is an elaborate system of heat-trapping and sound-baffling. Against the skin, they wear a thick coating of rendered badger-tallow mixed with bone-ash, which smells faintly of wet chalk and old grease, sealing the pores against the creeping damp. Over this go three or four layers of tunic woven from the down of the velvet-walkers—a fabric so soft and light it has no rustle at all—stuffed between the seams with dried bladder-fern. Their outer garments are heavy, hooded ponchos made from overlapped rag-moth membranes, cured in alum until they are as supple as oiled kidskin, slick enough to shed the constant condensation without the sharp *tick-tick* of water hitting stiffer leather. 

Beneath these immense, muffler-like cowls, the people themselves are surprisingly slight, built like the bamboo frames of their houses: light-boned, long-levered, and immensely strong in the tendon. Centuries in the slot have stretched their fingers and toes into prehensile hooks. Even through the thick, split-toed wool socks they hook into the bindings of their willow tread-frames, their feet are constantly flexing, reading the shift of the wet bamboo and the squirming depth of the insect drifts beneath them. Their faces are pale with the greenish-gray undertone of cellar-sprouts, except for their cheeks and the tips of their noses, which are permanently cobwebbed with burst purple capillaries from the chill. 

Their eyes are huge, dark, and seemingly all pupil, adapted to the pewter gloom and the neon-violet flicker of the badger-runs. Because a dry cough or a sneeze can pull a heat-seeker down from the upper overhangs, the cleft-folk have developed an intense, reflexive control over their own autonomies. From infancy, a baby that cries is not scolded, nor is it smothered; its mother simply presses her wide, warm palm over its mouth and nose for three seconds, tapping a rapid, rhythmic heartbeat against its sternum until the infant learns that distress is answered only when expressed as a silent, full-body tremor. By adulthood, they do not sigh, they do not clear their throats, and they do not laugh aloud. Joy is a wide, crinkle-eyed baring of the teeth accompanied by a rapid flutter of the fingers against the collarbone; grief is a slow, rocking crouch with the hood drawn tight.

To watch two neighbors meet on a narrow platform is to witness a dance of extreme, peripheral courtesy. They do not look directly at one another’s faces for long—their eyes are always scanning up, tracking the dark smudges of the overhangs, or down, minding the flow of the herds. Instead, they read the *bafflers*. 

Every adult wears a collar-frame of steamed willow over their shoulders, from which hang dozens of small, flat paddles carved from horn, bleached crab-shell, and bog-oak. By shrugging a shoulder, tilting the chin, or twitching a thumb tied to a gut-string, they flip these paddles to expose different painted faces—indigo from crushed beetles, ochre from iron-seep, glowing green from cured badger-gland. It is a language of shifting mosaics. A person walking down a rope-avenue is constantly broadcasting a low-level chatter from their shoulders: *Passing on the left / carrying heavy water / mind the loose lashing / three rails in my basket / the mist is sweet today.* 

For more intimate talk, when the wooden fans are tucked into their belts, they step close and press their foreheads or wrists together, speaking in *hum-talk*—vibrating their vocal cords with their mouths tightly shut, sending pitches and cadences directly through the bone and cartilage of the listener’s skull. Lovers sit on the bulbous moss-bolsters for hours in the blue dark, knees locked around a support strut, their temples glued together, humming long, wordless epics into each other’s heads while the velvet-walkers flow over their ankles like warm black sand.

They are never still, and they are never alone, not even inside their own clothes. 

Because there is no fuel to heat water for washing, and because their heavy padding can only be stripped off inside the thick-walled, air-tight sleeping drums at the center of each hut, the cleft-folk cultivate a personal ecology. Beneath their outer ponchos, tucked into the warm, damp pockets around their ribs and groins, they keep five or six "shirt-toads"—flat, palm-sized cousins of the masonry-toads, bred to be toothless and dry-skinned. These creatures live their whole lives in the dark of a human’s woolens, radiating a slow, steady metabolic heat like living hot-water bottles and snapping up the lice, moss-mites, and stray bristle-worms that inevitably migrate inward from the walls. When two people embrace, they must do so slowly, allowing the bulging pockets of their tunics to shift and settle so they don't bruise each other's heaters.

Their lives are measured by the windlass and the drop. 

At the top of the social order—and the physical town—are the **Comb-Tenders** and the **Greasers**. They are the heaviest people in the settlement, fed extra rations of rich slug-liver to give them the bulk needed to lean against the great windlass bars. Their hands are permanently stained yellow from the vats of rendered fat they carry at their hips, their fingers always working a lump of tallow into a wooden peg or a rope-sheave before it can dry enough to squeak. You can tell a Greaser by their walk: a wide, rolling, pendulous gait developed from spending twelve hours a day suspended in a gut-harness out over the three-hundred-foot void, wiping down the horsehair lines.

Below them, occupying the dense middle tiers where the rag-moth blizzards are thickest, are the **Weavers** and **Vat-Minders**. Everything in the cleft that is not stone or bone is fermented, pickled, or cured. Without fire to roast or boil, the people cook with chemistry. Every cantilevered hut has a porch-rack of heavy clay jars glazed in iridescent beetle-green. In these jars, stripped rail-meat, blanket-worms, and fat pale grubs are layered with acidic moss-squeezings, alkaline bone-ash, and salty rock-scrapings. The Vat-Minders are masters of the slow rot; they have fingernails grown long and scooped like horn spoons to test the jelly-like curds of fermented slug, and their skin smells sharply of vinegar and ammonia. They eat their meals cold, sucking the rich, sour pastes from reed tubes so they don't have to expose their bare hands to the air for long.

And at the very bottom, living on the lowest bamboo grids just thirty feet above the churning, Pitch-black bone-glacier of the floor, are the **Dredgers**. 

They are a breed apart—smaller, paler, their eyes almost entirely milky because there is nothing down there to see but the faint, sickly bioluminescence of rotting shells. They rarely climb up into the twilight of the mid-tiers. They spend their lives lowering weighted wicker baskets on braided hair-lines into the unseen dark below, fishing for the calf-sized blind scavengers that plow the detritus. When they haul a scavenger up—four or five Dredgers straining in absolute, straining silence on a greased pulley, veins standing out like cords on their foreheads, sweat freezing on their brows—they kill it not with a blade, which might make it thrash against the bamboo, but by stuffing its breathing-spiracles with balls of tallow-soaked moss. From these deep-dwellers come the heavy ribs used for house-joists, the thick hides for the sleeping-drums, and the dense, oily marrow that is the closest thing the town has to gold.

Yet for all the grim discipline of their survival, they are not a dour people. Their art is simply small, tactile, and silent. 

Inside the sleeping-drums—spherical cages of woven split-bamboo plastered three feet thick with moss and bone-ash mortar, entered through a tight, fur-lined sleeve—five or six family members curl together in a pitch-black, stiflingly warm knot of limbs and shirt-toads. Here, where no sound can escape through the yard of wet insulation, they whisper. They tell jokes that rely on the rhythm of breath blown against an ear; they pass around "feel-stones"—pieces of slate carved with such microscopic, swirling intricacy that running a thumb over them in the dark tells a story of the great tectonic split, or the migration of the badgers, or the foolish girl who forgot to grease her windlass. 

When someone dies, there is no weeping, and there is no grave. The rock cannot be dug, and the bottom of the chasm belongs to the scavengers. Instead, the dead are stripped of their woolens and their toads, washed in vinegar, and carried up—higher than the Comb-Tenders, higher than the topmost huts, up to the razor-sharp lip of the fissure where the permanent plug of fog sits like a roof of wet wool. 

There, in the blinding, freezing white-out where the chasm meets the mountain’s skin, the walls are lined with the **Salt-Cradles**: thousands of small, hammock-like nets woven from horsehair, pinned to the sheer slate. The body is laid in a net, and the living retreat quickly before the cold takes their fingers. Up there, where the wind finally has room to move, the fog does not merely condense; it freezes in thick, rime-white crusts, and the blind, heat-seeking things that roost in the upper crags do not touch meat that has gone cold. Slowly, over seasons, the wind-driven mist and the tiny, specialized ice-worms of the upper lip strip the bones clean, bleachers them white, and glazes them in clear rime. 

Years later, when a house needs a new lintel-pin or a boy comes of age and needs a gaff-hook, a climber will go up into the white ceiling with a bone saw greased with slug-fat, find an ancestor whose ribs have cured to the density of ivory, and bring them back down into the green, shivering dark to help hold the town together.
