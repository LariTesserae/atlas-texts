If you reach out into the dark to steady yourself against what you think is the greased housing of a turbine, and the housing gives way beneath your palm with the slow, dense resistance of packed lard, you have found one of the Tenders.

At first touch, they seem merely an exaggeration of the surrounding carpets of fauna—another vast, pale accumulation of blind meat pressed against the warm iron for comfort. They have no distinct silhouette because there is no light to cast one, and because their anatomy refuses to hold a single shape for more than a week at a time. To feel one from end to end would take twenty minutes of careful, blind groping along the base of an engine. You would find a low, sprawling mound of cool, tallowy flesh, heavy as wet sand, smelling faintly of boiled linen and ozone. 

They do not have limbs in any permanent sense. When a Tender needs to move across the razor-keen basalt, it does not walk; it pours itself forward, extruding thick, calloused pads of gristle that flow over the obsidian edges without cutting. Rather than slicing the creature, the sharp stone leaves deep, temporary geometric impressions in its underside, like the waffle-mark of a grate on warm butter, which slowly smooth out once the weight shifts. 

Yet as your hands move higher up the creature's flank, the impression of a simple beast disappears. Buried just beneath the slick, mucous-coated skin are hard, right-angled structures that do not belong to any amphibian. You can feel the distinct hexagonal ridges of five-inch nuts, the long, ribbed spines of threaded iron rods, and the smooth, curving plates of brass pressure-casings, all suspended inside the creature’s blubber like fruit in aspic. 

The Tenders are the slow intermediaries of the basin. They are the reason the engines have no crew, and the reason they have not seized up over the uncounted centuries. 

A Tender does not operate a machine from the outside; it becomes the missing system. When a cast-iron boiler develops a hairline fracture from the shifting of the young stone, a Tender will haul its massive, segmented bulk up the sweating metal casing—a climb that takes days—and settle over the crack. Its pale belly parts in a long, toothless cleft, pressing directly against the weeping iron. Over the next month, the creature’s flesh vulcanizes around the leak, turning dark, leathery, and tough as vulcanized rubber, while internal ducts reroute the escaping steam through the Tender’s own sprawling, cavernous gut. It uses the machine’s stray pressure to circulate its own sluggish, lukewarm blood, venting the cooled exhaust weeks later through a series of fluttering, muscular sphincters along its back that sound, in the dark, like someone sighing through wet lips.

If you stay near one long enough, and keep your hands lightly resting on its upper hide, you realize that the creature is not entirely unitary. It is a parliament of borrowed parts. 

Along the crest of its back, where the skin is thinnest, there are dozens of small, warm hollows. Tucked inside these pouches are the smaller creatures of the basin—the soft-shelled crabs, the blind eels—living symbiotically half-inside the Tender’s flesh. The Tender uses them as sensory organs and fine manipulators. When an engine’s oil line clogs, the Tender does not reach in with a hand; it presses a fold of its body against the pipe union and releases a pale, ribbon-thin worm that is still umbilically tethered to the Tender’s own nervous system by a six-foot cord of pulsing sinew. The worm slides into the dark piping, dissolves the blockage with alkaline bile, and is slowly reeled back into the parent mass days later.

They have no faces, because they have nothing to look at and no need to speak, yet they possess something like a head—a heavy, pendulous sack of dense cartilage that they drag behind them rather than push in front, keeping it safe from the sharp shale. Inside this sack sits their only piece of true, un-rotted anatomy: a massive, three-chambered organ lined with plates of bone that rub together at the speed of a clock’s hour hand. 

This is how they navigate the broken geography of the basin. Because five paces north does not equal five paces south, the Tenders do not map space; they map resonance. Every few hours, the bone plates in a Tender’s drag-sack slip past one another with a dull, submerged *clack* that travels through the water and the basalt rather than the air. The Tender then lies completely still for an hour, feeling the vibration return through the soles of its gristle-pads. It reads the state of the world by how the hum of the dynamos bends around the floating lenses of water, and how the echoes dull when a patch of basalt turns temporarily to clay. 

Where time pools and goes bad—in those pockets where a second stretches into an afternoon—the Tenders are the only things that do not get trapped, because they are large enough to straddle the boundary. You might find a Tender forty feet long, its front half wedged between two warm condenser coils, respiring at a normal, sleepy pace, while its rear half trails back into a pocket of slow-time, completely rigid and cold to the touch, waiting for last Tuesday to finish passing through its tail. 

Despite their bulk, their integration with the heavy iron, and their complete alienation from anything resembling a human silhouette, there is something unsettlingly domestic about them. They have habits that are not strictly mechanical or biological. 

Occasionally, two Tenders will meet in the flat, flooded dark between engines, drawn together by the sub-audible hum of the copper cables they follow like blind men following a rope. They do not mate, and they do not fight. Instead, they press their vast, wet flanks together and engage in a slow, bureaucratic exchange of inventory. Over the course of forty-eight hours, their skins fuse temporarily at the point of contact. Through this fleshy bridge, they pass things back and forth: a gallon of rich, high-grade cylinder oil one has filtered from a dying turbine; a thirty-pound brass valve that was swallowed decades ago and kept polished in a stomach-sac until it was needed; a cluster of small, translucent amphibians that have bred too thickly on one Tender's back and are transferred to the other like livestock. 

And sometimes, buried deep in the warm, pulsing tallow of an old Tender—deeper than the brass fittings and the swallowed wrenches, down near the slow-grinding bone plates of its navigation organ—there is something smaller. Something that feels, if you were to push your arm shoulder-deep into the yielding, fever-warm fold of its side, remarkably like a human ribcage, or a pelvis, or a row of fused vertebrae, softened to the consistency of wet chalk and long since repurposed to anchor a ligament that pulls a lever that vents a pipe. 

They do not mind being touched by a stranger wandering through the dark. If you trip on the fresh basalt and fall against one, it will not flinch. It will simply register your heat and the frantic, bird-like hammering of your heart. Slowly—over the course of ten or fifteen minutes—a thick, warm fold of its mantle will creep up over your boots and shins, heavy and comforting as a heated quilt. It does not mean to harm you. It only feels a small, high-speed engine running far too hot and far too fast in the dark, leaking saltwater from its upper vents, and it is trying, with infinite, blind patience, to figure out where your grease-fittings are, and how to bolt you to the floor so you can finally slow down.
