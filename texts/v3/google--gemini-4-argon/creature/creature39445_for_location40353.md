To find the people of the basin, you do not look across the miles of bone-white stone. You look into the black, tin-cut shadows of the monoliths, or down into the bedrock itself. 

If you watch the base of a pillar long enough, a shadow detaches itself from the leeward rock. It does not stand upright. To stand upright in the ninety-mile-an-hour wall of air is to become a sail, to have one’s heat stripped away in forty seconds and one’s footing sheared from the satin-smooth limestone. Instead, the figure moves on four points of contact—two knees, one elbow, one hand—keeping its profile under eighteen inches, shaped like the streamlined prow of the rock it just left.

At first glance, they look like the giant slate-colored insects they harvest: low, segmented, and flush to the stone. They are dressed in overlapping plates of cured glider-wing and split horn, sewn onto an under-layer of densely felted hair. The plates are shingled from west to east, so the wind slides over their backs without finding a seam to catch and rip. When they must cross the open, sun-blasted expanses between the pillars, they do not walk; they skate and haul. Facing into the gale, they reach forward with short, hooked gaffs of laminated bone, snagging a microscopic lip or flute in the bedrock, and pull their bodies forward on thick leather knee-pads greased with insect tallow. Going east, with the wind at their backs, they simply tuck their limbs, lift their chests an inch, and let the gale slide them across the polished floor until they jam their gaffs into a fissure to brake.

They are small, dense people, built like river stones. Over generations at this altitude, their chests have grown deep and barrel-shaped to house lungs the size of bellows, necessary to pull oxygen from the thin, violet air. Their limbs are short to minimize the surface area exposed to the cold, and their fingers and toes are startlingly thick, with wide, flat nails backed by calluses as hard as horn, used for jamming into shallow rock slits. 

They have no ears—or rather, the external pinnae of their ears are bound flat against their skulls in infancy beneath tight leather caps, and their ear canals are permanently plugged with a dense, malleable wax rendered from insect fat and powdered silica. Unprotected, the planetary roar of the mountain would shatter their eardrums and liquefy their inner ears into permanent vertigo. Even deafened by the wax, they are never free of the sound. They feel the sub-bass rumble in their teeth, in the water of their eyes, and in the marrow of their ribs. 

Because the chord never changes, it has become their baseline for existence. A person brought down from the basin to the quiet valleys miles below goes mad within days; without the massive, vibrating pressure of the mountain’s note holding their bodies together, they feel as though they are dissolving into the silent air.

Up here, because the rock is always screaming, the people are entirely silent. They have no spoken names. Even inside the deep, subterranean chambers where they sleep, their voices are atrophied, reduced to low, chest-rattling hums pressed directly against another person’s sternum.

In the open basin, they speak like the cliff-runners do: with light. 

Every person carries a fan of polished bone blades or a wrist-cuff plated with the salvaged, iridescent crests of the lizards. Because the air is so thin and clear that distance loses its meaning, a person anchored in the lee of a pillar can flick their wrist into the ruthless sunlight and throw a sharp, blinding flash of ultraviolet and white light five miles across the basin floor. If you stand at the edge of the plateau and squint against the glare, you can see the conversations happening across the empty miles—a silent, geometric language of quick white sparks and long, tilting mirrors speaking from shadow to shadow, indistinguishable from the thousands of signaling lizards around them. 

Close up, in the razor-sharp shade behind the monoliths where the wind curls into a freezing eddy, they talk with their hands pressed against the stone or against each other. They tap complex rhythms onto the bedrock with their bone gaffs, reading the vibrations through their knees and palms, or trace rapid, shorthand shapes onto the horned plates of a companion’s forearm.

Their faces are completely hidden when they are on the surface. The light would blind an naked eye in an hour, and the wind would freeze the corneas. They wear heavy visors carved from the dark, translucent horn of mountain beasts from the lower slopes, with three hairline slits cut into them—not just horizontal, to block the snow-blindness of the white stone, but angled to cut the vertical glare of the sweating, mirror-wet west faces of the pillars. Beneath the visors, their faces are smeared with a thick paste of grey insect-fat and chalk dust, which hardens in the cold into a windproof rind. Only when they drop down into the stone do you see their skin: weathered the color of old ivory, netted with fine burst capillaries from the altitude and the acoustic vibration, with dark, startlingly wide eyes that never seem to blink.

They do not live *on* the basin; they live *in* it. The limestone is a sponge of horizontal tubes and deep, wind-scoured chimneys. A hundred yards away from a roaring, flute-like aperture that is generating a hundred-and-thirty-decibel shriek, another fissure might curve back on itself in just the right way to create an acoustic shadow—a pocket of strange, pressurized calm where the air is still and the noise drops to a heavy, rhythmic throb, like the inside of a giant’s chest.

These are their homes, known only by the specific harmonic vibration of their walls. Several families pack themselves into these narrow, horizontal slits like wintering insects. There is no wood for fire, and dried lichen burns too fast and smoky in the thin air. For warmth, they burn fat. They render the millions of heavy, slate-colored insects into a clear, odorless oil, which they burn in shallow limestone lamps wicked with twisted human hair. The ceilings of these crevices are thick with centuries of black, greasy soot, polished smooth by the backs of the people crawling through them. 

The interiors smell intensely of warm tallow, wet limestone, ozone, and the sharp, musky scent of thirty bodies packed together in wool and leather. Here, wedged into the rock so tightly they cannot roll over without waking the whole line, they sleep, mend their hair-and-gut nets, laminate new gaffs with glue boiled from glider-sinew, and chew the tough, silica-crusted scrub leaves to keep their teeth from loosening in their gums.

Their lives are governed by the harvest of the wind. Everything they have arrives from the west, blown up from the unseen world below. The wind brings the mist that waters their stone troughs; the wind brings the microscopic spores that feed the rust-colored lichens; the wind drives the fat, wingless insects into the lee crevices and slams the heavy gliders into the drum-tight nets stretched across the wind-tubes. 

To harvest a net is the most dangerous work in the world. Two people must crawl out to the lip of a roaring chimney where the wind is funneled to well over a hundred miles an hour. They anchor themselves by driving bone wedges into the cracks and clipping their leather harnesses to the rock with short, thick tethers of braided gut. Then, hanging over the abyss of sound, they haul the bellied net in, inch by inch, using hooked levers to pry the meshes over bone pegs. The acoustic pressure at the mouth of a tube is so intense that their vision blurs as their eyeballs vibrate in their sockets, and if a tether snaps, they do not fall—they are shot horizontally eastward like stones from a sling, skipping across the polished basin floor until they vanish over the eastern drop-off.

Because the mountain preserves everything, the people live among their ancestors in a literal way. When someone dies, there is no soil to bury them and no fuel to burn them. Instead, the dead are stripped of their horn armor, wrapped in an old net, and carried out to the farthest, loneliest monoliths on the eastern rim. There, they are wedged upright into high, narrow chimneys facing the west. 

Within twenty-four hours, the freezing wind and the zero-humidity air desiccate the body into light, unrotting leather. Over the decades, the wind-borne silica polishes their skin to the exact dull satin sheen of the surrounding limestone. And because a solid body placed into a wind-flute alters the airflow, every corpse slightly changes the pitch of the chimney it occupies. 

The people know the voices of these chimneys. As they crawl across the basin floor, feeling the immense, polyphonic chord of the massif vibrating through their palms and knees, they can pick out the tiny, specific overtones generated by the hollows where their dead are wedged. To them, the deafening, ten-thousand-year-old roar is not an empty sound. It is a choir. It is the accumulated breath of everyone who has ever lived on the stone, still singing into the violet sky.
