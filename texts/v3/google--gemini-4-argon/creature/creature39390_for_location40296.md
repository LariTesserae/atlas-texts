You do not see the occupant until the five-minute fog clears, and even then, you usually see the tallow-pot first, hanging by its brass bail from a flange three feet to his left.

He is called the Gauger, though there is no one within four vertical miles to call him anything at all. At first glance, hanging thirty feet up in the chartreuse gloom between two sweating columns, he looks like a heavy canvas mail-sack that has been caught on a tie-rod and left to soak. 

Then the sack shifts, a bare gray heel hooks over a half-inch copper pressure line, and a hand comes out of the leaves to wipe a gauge face clean with a thumb that has no nail, only a flat, tough pad of horn.

He is not an old man, because nothing in the room is old, though he has the dense, compacted look of something grown under high atmospheric pressure. He is short and astonishingly broad through the ribs—a barrel chest built for processing the thin, sharp air of the peaks, now repurposed for breathing warm soup. His skin has the pale, lard-like sheen of a deep-mine pony, waterproofed by a permanent thin film of the clear tallow he uses on the pistons and the lanolin that renders out of the slag-wool mats. During the two-minute rains, the warm water does not wet him; it beads on his shoulders and runs down the deep groove of his spine in neat, silver mercury-trails.

He wears only a long, split apron of heavy oilcloth tied around his waist with copper wire, and a pair of lineman’s climbing irons strapped to his bare, hairless calves. The soles of his feet have grown thick and cross-hatched, stamped with the permanent, dark-gray negative of the foundry-fresh floor grates, though he rarely comes down to the floor if he can help it. He prefers the middle canopy, where the heat from the lower coils meets the bright, cold radiation of the wired glass.

He is an arbiter of tolerances. The room is too new and too fast to be left to itself; its two halves—the cast iron and the chlorophyll—have not yet reached an agreement. Left alone for forty-eight hours, the blue ferns would choke the pneumatic bleed-valves, the vines would pull the brass louver-cables three degrees out of true, and the whole cube would either boil itself dry or freeze solid in a single alpine midnight.

So the Gauger moves through the vertical iron forest with the slow, deliberate economy of the counterweights. He never climbs when he can ride. If he needs to reach the upper trusses, he steps onto the piano-sized ballast block at the bottom of its stroke, hooks one thick forearm around the grease-slick guide rail, and lets the hydraulic cycle lift him silently into the rafters while he pare-cuts the callus on his thumb or chews on a sour, fibrous stalk of copper-vine.

From the brass rings of his apron hang his instruments, which never clink together because each is wrapped in a sleeve of pigskin:
*   A pair of massive, drop-forged pruning shears with blades kept so sharp and greased they can take a two-inch lignified creeper off a rivet head with a single, soft *snick*.
*   A long-spouted copper oil-can filled with rendered marmot fat.
*   A double-ended box wrench, sized for every bolt in the building (they are all the same three-quarter-inch hex).
*   A tuning fork.

He uses the tuning fork on the steam mains. Because the room is full of the patter of water and the *shhh-ock* of the leather gaskets, he cannot hear a blockage forming deep in the pipes. Instead, he strikes the fork against his climbing iron, presses the stem to his mastoid bone, and lays his front teeth directly against the scalding brass of a feeder line. He listens through his jaw. If the hum of the mountain’s deep boiler is flat by half a semitone, he knows a mat of aerial roots has worked its way through a gasket lower down.

He has no house inside the barn; a house would be redundant inside a radiator. Instead, he sleeps in a sling of braided brass wire and coconut fiber suspended directly over the main carbon-dioxide vent, where the air is heavy enough to induce a thick, dreamless stupor. He sleeps for forty minutes at a time, waking automatically whenever the big counterweight reaches the top of its track and the air pressure in the room shifts by a fraction of a pound as the louvers turn.

His relationship with the smaller inhabitants is entirely administrative. When the translucent crabs multiply too quickly and begin to crust the sight-glasses like white barnacles, he sweeps them into a canvas bag with the side of his hand and drops them through the grating to the eels in the bilge. When an eel grows too fat to turn around in the drain-trap, he reaches down through the inspection hatch with a blunt iron gaff, hauls it up into the green light, and kills it with a single tap of his box wrench behind the external gills. He stews chunks of the oily white meat in a tin pannikin balanced on the main steam manifold—this, along with the large, waxy, cabbage-scented buds he pinches off the philodendrons to keep them from going to seed, is what the room smells of.

What is most peculiar about the Gauger is that he is still learning the room, or perhaps the room is still finishing him. Like the trusses above his head, he seems to have been delivered from the valley in pieces and assembled on the peak. Across the broad, flat plane of his left shoulder blade, just under the film of tallow, there is a faint, bluish-purple mark under the skin—not a tattoo, precisely, but an inspection stamp, in the same block stenciling as the foundry marks on the iron columns: *LOT 4 — TESTED 300 LBS.*

When the five-minute fog comes on and the chartreuse light turns to a blinding, featureless white pearl, he stops working. He hooks his knees over a warm four-inch return pipe, lets his heavy hands hang down between his shins, and opens his mouth slightly to let the mist condense on his tongue. In the absolute whiteness, with the iron invisible and the leaves erased, you can hear him breathing—a slow, wet, rhythmic *shhh-ock* that is completely indistinguishable from the leather gaskets in the walls.
