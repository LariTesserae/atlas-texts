You can look straight at one for ten minutes and think it is only a sixth cairn of black rock, or a broken column of gabbro wedged beneath a silica-fern. 

Then the purple light from a spore-groove catches the crook of an elbow, and you see that the stone has tendons.

There are nine of them in the chamber. Two are inside the knife-walled domes, folded down onto the ash-kelp mats like jackknives closed for storage. Four sit upright at the bases of the hogshead tubers, their backs wedged directly into the razor-sharp flutes of the obsidian walls—not leaning gingerly, to avoid being cut, but pressed in hard, so the black glass slots neatly into the grooves between their shoulder blades. The last three are out in the open gravel among the copper harps. 

They are roughly the shape of thin, long-limbed humans, though their proportions have the drawn-out, economical look of wood-boring wasps or deep-trench crustacea. No upright man could stand in the domes, because the builders did not stand; their resting posture is a deep, tripodal crouch, knees flared wide like the legs of a developmental spider, pelvis dropped low to the gravel, one long-fingered hand always resting flat against the ground to take the bass-note of the locusts through the palm. 

Nothing about them is wet. If you took one of the flint blades from the cairns and drew it across the meat of their forearms, nothing would bead. The skin—if it can be called skin—is a dense, overlapping shingle of dark, keratinous plate, the color of stove-polish and old slate, dusted in the creases with the pale gold of sulfur meal and the gray talc of ground mica. Where a human body has fat to round out the cheek or the thigh, they have a dry, fibrous pith, like the inside of an old yucca stalk or a wasp’s nest. You can see the working of their joints—the ball of the hip, the twin pulleys of the ankle—through parchment-thin membranes at the bends of the limbs, dry and powdery as the hinges of a seashell. 

Their heads are narrow and tall-crowned, swept back slightly toward the vault of the roof. They have no external ears—no fleshy pinnae to catch and funnel the air—because in this room, funneling the sound would tear the mind apart. Instead, the sides of their skulls behind the cheekbones are wide, flat plates of naked, porous bone, faintly translucent in the ember-light, like cuttlebone or dry pumice. They do not hear with their heads alone; their ribs are broad and flat as barrel staves, resonating chambers stretched over a hollow chest where no wet lung needs to heave. 

They do not breathe the air so much as they let the sulfur drafts cure them. Down the center of the breastbone runs a double row of narrow, flanged slits—spiracles, lined with stiff, copper-colored bristles to comb out the floating silica dust. These slits do not open and close in a rhythm. They simply stand ajar, letting the heavy, hundred-and-two-degree gas drift through the dry sponge of their interiors, trading slow electrons with the blood-meal that creeps like warm mercury through their veins.

Because there is no water to lubricate an eyeball, their eyes are solid, faceted domes of dark, smoky quartz-chitin, flush with the socket and dry as pebbles. A ribbon-lizard can drop from a glass frond and land directly on the bridge of a builder’s nose—*chink-ring*—and the builder will not blink, because there is no lid to blink with. The lizard’s toes skitter across the mineral cornea, leaving not even a smudge, and the creature beneath does not flinch.

For a long time, watching them, you would swear they are dead. You would think they are the mummified remains of the crew that built the huts, preserved forever by the dry bake of the pocket.

Then the one beside the half-finished cairn moves.

It does not move the way wet things move. Wet things—things made of levered bone and hydraulic muscle—move in smooth, continuous arcs, powered by the burn of sugar and water. The builder moves like a dry watch-spring slipping a tooth. 

There is a sharp, wooden *tock* from its shoulder joint. Its arm is no longer resting on its knee; it is six inches above thestone mallet. A micro-spray of pale sulfur dust puffs from the crease of its elbow and hangs in the static air, pulled toward the nearest copper root by the electrical charge. 

They live at the speed of the stone they came from. 

To burn energy quickly is to make heat, and in a sealed blister that sits at a permanent hundred and two degrees, with no sweat to evaporate and no cool wind to carry the fever away, internal heat is death. The white beetles must rasp their legs all day and night just to throw off the warmth of their own small, frantic lives through acoustic vibration. The builders solved the problem of the furnace by letting their fires bank down until they were nearly indistinguishable from the rock. A single thought—*the fifth flint from the bottom is vibrating a quarter-tone flat*—takes twenty hours to travel along dry, mineral-sheathed nerves from the acoustic plate of the skull to the three-fingered hand.

After three days of your time, the hand closes around the petrified reed of the mallet. The dry lizard-hide bindings creak against the slate-shingled palm. 

They are not primitives who stumbled into a cave and made a religion out of noise. They are engineers of the equilibrium. 

Look closely at the one sitting by the copper harp. Its long, flat-nailed middle finger is hooked over the thickest wire. It has been hooked there for what you would measure as a week, pulled back half an inch against the pull of the magnetite weight. It is waiting. Up in the violet-lit canopy, three yards overhead, a blind mica-lizard is digesting a porcelain beetle; the lizard’s weight is bending a glass frond downward by a fraction of a millimeter an hour. When the frond finally snaps—tomorrow, or on Tuesday—the falling shard of silica will strike the third string of the harp. And at that exact instant, the builder’s finger will release the first string, so the two notes will meet in the air and cancel each other out before the resonance can shatter the brittle, century-old arch of the easternmost hut.

That is why the huts are built of knives. Soft, rounded stone swallows sound; sharp, fractured basalt splits the acoustic waves into harmless, high-frequency scatter. Inside those domes, under the woven ash-kelp, is the only relative quiet in the world—a pocket within the pocket where the roar of the beetles and the thrum of the locusts are baffled down to a dry, tolerable hiss. That is where they go when their hollow ribs ache from too much ringing, folding themselves into the jagged black teeth of the walls like cutlery into a lined box, letting the sharp stone points press into their insensitive, armored backs to ground out the static electricity that builds up on their skins.

And the glowing purple spirals pecked into the obsidian walls? They are not calendars, because nothing here has a season, and they are not maps, because the world is sixty paces wide.

Watch the third builder, the one standing facing the north mirror-wall, a quartz chisel pinched between its thumb and two opposing fingers. Its forehead is pressed flat against the obsidian. Through the bone plates of its temples, it is listening past the beetles, past the marimba-lizards, past the locusts in the gravel. It is listening to the eight hundred feet of dead basalt above the ceiling, and the miles of shifting mantle below the floor. 

The pocket was opened by a tectonic snap a few centuries ago. The rock is still settling, infinitesimally, grain by crystal grain, under the unthinkable weight of the plain above. Every few months, deep in the gabbro, a microscopic stress-fracture lengthens by the width of a hair, letting out a tiny, ultrasonic *ping* that is lost to any ear but theirs.

The builder hears the *ping*. Over the course of forty-eight hours, it raises the quartz chisel, places the point at the outer tail of the glowing spiral, and taps it once with the heel of its palm. *Chink.* A flake of black glass falls. With a thumb coated in the purple spore-dust of the ferns, it rubs the new groove until it glows. 

It is a diagram of the ceiling's remaining life. It is a stress-calculation written in light.

When the spiral reaches the edge of the obsidian slab, the builder will walk—three steps an hour, knees wide, kicking up no dust—over to the half-finished cairn of tuned flints. It will set the final blade on the pile, shifting the sympathetic frequency of the cairn by three hertz, just enough to rattle the burrowing locusts out of the northeast corner of the floor and force them to chew their mineral dust under the southwest wall instead, easing the pressure on the fault line overhead by four pounds per square inch.

The white beetle crawls off the mallet's head and onto the builder's knuckles. It pauses there, clamping its six porcelain feet into the cracks of the dark, shingled skin, and begins to grind its serrated legs against its own shell—*shhhk-shhhk-shhhk*—dumping its body heat into the air. 

Slowly, over the next hour, the mallet rises an inch from the gravel, carrying the beetle with it. The faceted quartz eyes do not turn toward the insect. The builders do not mind the beetles, any more than a cathedral minds the pigeons on its gargoyles. The beetle is part of the chord, and the chord must be held in tune, or the roof comes down.
