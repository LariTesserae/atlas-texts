In the third course of the eastern wall, a single block of limestone is missing. It was never dropped into place, or perhaps it was levered out on that last afternoon because of a flaw in the grain. Its absence leaves a square-jawed cavity two feet wide, two feet high, and nearly four feet deep, running back into the dry-stacked core of the pier. 

Inside this slot lives the Clerk.

At first glance, when the olive light shifts down through the fig-vault at noon, the front of the cavity appears to be sealed with a plug of dried gray mud. Only when a copper-tailed skink scrambles too close to the margin does the plug rearrange itself. A horizontal slit opens near the top of the mass—not an eye, which would gleam and betray the socket’s depth, but a breathing-valve bordered by short, stiff bristles dusted with limestone powder. 

The Clerk is not a single shape, or rather, its shape is largely borrowed from the room’s geometry. Its body is a low, broad turret of leathery mantle, the exact buff-gray of the quarry dust, flattened on top and squared off at the flanks so that it fits the mason’s recess like a drawer slid home. It is a cousin to the cellar-slug and the land-crab, though it has given up the wet slide of the former and the clattering armor of the latter. Instead, its hide is dry and finely pebbled, mimicking the raw-sugar grain of the split stone so closely that even the pale house-geckos will walk across its back, their toe-pads finding the same micro-purchase on its skin that they find on the rock.

To move—which it does primarily during the deep, velvety black of the middle night, though it shifts its stance throughout the day—it uses six jointed walking-arms tucked beneath its skirt. These limbs are not chitinous; they are muscular, boneless hydrostats sheathed in tough, velvety skin, ending in flat, square-cornered pads like a mason’s wooden floats. When it steps out onto the vertical face of the wall, it does not scramble. It extends two pads, presses them flat against the chisel-grooves to create a dry vacuum seal, and draws its bulk forward with the slow, greaseless precision of a block being winched into alignment.

It is the reason the chamber’s greenery looks pruned.

The Clerk cannot abide a blurred edge. This is not an aesthetic preference, nor is it tidymindedness; it is a requirement of its hunting. The creature is nearly blind, possessing only four pinprick light-sensors along its dorsal ridge that tell it when the sun-shafts are moving. Instead, it reads the chamber through the tension of the surfaces and the exact bounce of air-pressure inside the box. In a four-walled shaft of crisp, ninety-degree stone, every footstep of a beetle, every flutter of a moth against a root, sends a tiny, clean echo bounding off the flat planes. The pier is an acoustic mirror. If the creepers and ferns were allowed to mat across the stone, they would baffle the sound and ruin the lines of the wall.

So the Clerk maintains the margins.

Three hours after sundown, it eases itself out of the third-course cavity. As it clears the recess, its rear half uncoils—not a tail, but a heavy, counterbalancing anchor-foot that remains jammed into the joint while the front of the body pivots down the vertical face. Beneath its front cowl, tucked between the foremost walking-pads, is a pair of shearing plates made of dark, horn-like keratin, shaped like miniature billhooks. 

It moves to the four-foot gap of bare limestone between the *Monstera* and the hart’s-tongue fern. Since yesterday, the *Monstera* has sent a pale, wandering feeder-root four inches to the south, creeping across the point-chisel marks in search of a sweating joint. The Clerk stops beside it. Two thin, ribbon-like palps—the color of wet clay—unroll from beneath its cowl and tap the stone on either side of the root, measuring the intrusion against the parallel grooves of the mason’s tool. 

There is a single, crisp *snip*, no louder than a dry twig breaking between two fingers. 

The severed four-inch tip of the root does not fall to the floor. One of the Clerk’s square pads catches it before it drops, folds it inward, and passes it to the rasping mouthparts beneath. The creature chews silently, extracting the turgid, slightly bitter sap—the bulk of its water supply—before spitting a dry, neat pellet of pale fiber into its cheek-pouch. Later, it will press this pellet into the back of its sleeping-slot, gradually felting the interior of the stone hole with a smooth, sound-deadening wallpaper of chewed root-cellulose.

Having cleared the wall, the Clerk descends to the granite plinth at the base. Here, the giant pill-millipedes are grazing in a slow, armored phalanx along the damp line where the condensation pools. The Clerk does not eat the millipedes—their shells are too noisy to crack, and their defense secretions smell of bitter almonds, which fouls the still air of the jar—but it herds them. 

If the pioneer algae grows too thick, the stone stops sweating properly and begins to hold a slick, rotting slime that softens the sharp arris of the block. If the millipedes eat it all, they wander up into the ferns. So the Clerk lowers its flat front cowl like a snowplow and nudges the rearmost millipedes, rolling them gently along the plinth until they are concentrated on a patch of stone where the green film has grown too dark. The millipedes do not panic; they have grown up with this moving piece of wall. They unroll half a minute later and begin to rasp away at the exact square inch of granite they have been assigned.

For protein, the Clerk takes a toll from the middle air.

Because it never hurries, the faster hunters of the chamber treat it as topography. A translucent gecko, stalking a bark-louse along the fourth course of limestone, will use the Clerk’s squared-off shoulder as a blind, creeping right up to the creature’s dorsal ridge to peer over the edge. Ninety-nine times out of a hundred, the Clerk remains stone. The gecko leaps, takes the louse, and darts away.

On the hundredth time—usually when the Clerk’s shell-sac is low on calcium—the flat surface the gecko is standing on suddenly drops half an inch, breaking the lizard's footing, while two of the heavy, square-padded arms fold upward like the leaves of a hinge. There is no thrashing. The pads meet with a soft, muffled *thump*, like two leather-bound books clapped together. The gecko is caught in a dry press. Within seconds, it is drawn down beneath the skirt. The Clerk will spend the next four hours digesting the lizard entirely, saving only the tiny, chalky ear-stones and the crunch of the skull to harden its own shearing-plates, leaving not a single drop of blood or a shed tail to mar the quarry-dust on the block below.

Yet its strangest relationship is with the plumb-line roots of the figs and the horizontal webs of the orb-weavers.

In the center of the courtyard, away from the walls, the Clerk cannot climb the stone. Instead, it uses the hanging fig roots as a columned hall. Around midnight, when the upper canopy cools and the stone walls begin to sweat in earnest, the Clerk walks out across the dry clay floor, carefully stepping *between* the conical pits of the antlions. It knows where every pit is. If an antlion larva flips a grain of gray silt onto the Clerk’s foot-pad, the Clerk pauses, reaches down with a ribbon-palp, and drops a tiny, dried beetle-husk from yesterday’s meal into the pit—not out of charity, but to keep the antlion fat and stationary, so it will not dig a new funnel in the walking-lane.

Reaching the central cluster of thumb-thick aerial roots, the Clerk clasps two of them at once and hitches itself upward into the dark air.

It climbs until it is level with the great, horizontal saucers of the orb-webs. In the pitch black, it stops, anchored between two silver-skinned roots, and opens its breathing-valve wide. It exhales a slow, warm plume of air—air that smells faintly of bruised fig-sap, old fermentation, and damp limestone dust. 

In the utterly still atmosphere of the pier, this tiny thermal rises straight through the silk spirals above. Small midges and fungus-gnats, hanging dormant under the elephant-ear leaves, catch the warm draft and the scent of fermented sap and drift into the current, rising straight into the invisible horizontal nets of the spiders. 

The Clerk waits. Above it, the silk saucer trembles as the long-legged weaver darts out to harvest the catch. The spider is messy; as it wraps a moth or a crane-fly that has blundered into the thermal, it cuts away the dry, inedible wings, or accidentally shakes loose a bead of dew and a previously bundled packet of prey. 

Below the web, the Clerk tilts its flat, square back horizontally, forming a living shelf four feet above the floor. The discarded moth-wings, the dropped bundles of silk-wrapped gnats, and the shaken beads of water land silently on its leathery mantle. With its long, clay-colored palps, it reaches over its own back, gathers the fallen parcels and the water droplets, and draws them down into its mouth.

By the time the first gray dilution of dawn filters through the fig leaves thirty feet overhead, the Clerk has climbed back down the roots, crossed the floor between the antlion quarries, and scaled the eastern wall. 

It backs into the missing block’s cavity, fitting its squared sides flush with the neighboring stones. Before it settles, it uses one flat pad to brush away the faint, damp track its own belly left on the granite plinth below, patting a fresh pinch of loose quarry-powder over the spot until the raw-sugar sparkle of the split fossil shells is restored. 

Then it pulls its cowl in, aligns its flat face with the mason’s string-line, and becomes the stone that was never laid.
