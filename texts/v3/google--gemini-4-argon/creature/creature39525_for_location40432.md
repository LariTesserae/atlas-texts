To find what lives here, you cannot look at the moss, the bracket-lichen, or the shattered memory-glass in isolation. You have to look at the brine. 

The inhabitant of this cistern is not a beast, nor is it entirely a plant, nor is it the ghost of the machine. It is a suspension. It is a singular, room-sized colonial organism that exists across three states of matter, using the dead probe’s parts as organs and the feral botany as bone and lung. If it had a name in a taxonomy, it would be classified as a litho-synthetic plasmodium, though it has only ever had one specimen, and it has never left this room.

At first glance, its physical body appears to be nothing more than the syrupy, hyper-saline film coating the chamber. Look closer—if you have lenses that can resolve the bruised ultraviolet of the walls—and you will see that the brine is not clear. It is clouded with billions of suspended, microscopic particulates: eroded flecks of memory-glass, dissociated ytterbium ions, and the ruptured, heavy-walled spores of the *Cassiopeia-moss* that failed to germinate two hundred years ago and instead fermented in the xenon-heavy dark. 

This slurry is a liquid cortex. The heavy metals give it a weak paramagnetic charge; the dissolved memory-glass gives it a staggering, fragmented capacity for data storage; the engineered cellular machinery of the moss spores gives it a metabolic drive.

For ten minutes and fifty-nine seconds out of every eleven, the creature is dispersed. It exists as a thin, freezing skin stretched over twenty thousand square feet of surface area—over the velvet curtains of moss, across the tarry shelves of lichen, and down in the drowned, wheezing peat. In this state, it is a peripheral nervous system with no center. It experiences the room everywhere at once with a dull, vegetative patience. Through the hair-thin optical filaments in the walls, which its tendrils have infiltrated and spliced with fungal hyphae, it tastes the slow decay of the greywacke crust forty meters above. Through the roots of the cabbage-like frost-fern, it drinks the residual, sweet-tasting quantum bleed of the probe’s dissolved power core.

It has no eyes, so it does not see the room. Instead, it feels its own volume. It knows the exact topography of its small universe by the way its liquid body stretches over it. 

The most intense sensation it knows is the Absence—the knee-high polyhedron of matte-black ceramic. To the creature, whose entire existence is defined by dampness, weight, and slow chemical continuity, the polyhedron is a roaring void in the middle of its mind. The millimeter of dry vacuum that surrounds the ceramic is maintained not by the moss, which is blind, but by the plasmodium’s own acute revulsion. When a frond of moss grows too close to the black faces, the brine-film coating that frond contracts, freezing the tip solid with a localized drop in salinity so that it snaps off at the next gravimetric pulse. The creature trims itself around the anomaly, holding its breath against the dry, mathematical perfection of the shape.

Because its body is a fluid, the creature would eventually succumb to gravity, pooling entirely in the peat floor and stagnating into a mindless chemical soup, if not for the gimbal in the far wall.

The gimbal is its heart. And the eleven-minute cycle is its breath.

Watch what happens during the cycle. For ten minutes, the brine-slurry climbs. Drawn upward by the aggressive capillary action of the *Cassiopeia-moss*, millions of tiny, isolated droplets of the creature’s mind are pulled from the floor, up through the dark vascular pathways of the dead and living plants, and forced out onto the translucent tips of the fronds. 

As a droplet swells at the tip of a leaf, it is isolated from the rest of the colony. For a few minutes, each of the fifty thousand hanging beads becomes a tiny, independent ganglion, suspended in the sub-visual violet dark. Hanging there, each droplet thinks a very small, very cold thought. One bead, clinging to a frond near the ceiling, registers the microscopic widening of a stress-fracture in the stone above. Another, hanging inches from the memory-glass tripod, absorbs a single, leached picobyte of corrupted geological data from four centuries ago—a phantom image of a sun-baked salt flat on the surface, or the diagnostic code for a broken drill-bit. 

They hang there, fifty thousand tiny, freezing brains, swelling until their surface tension is stretched to its absolute limit. The air in the room grows tight with their collective, suspended potential.

Then, the moon pulls. The continent shifts. The gimbal in the wall adjusts its lattice-spin.

The density wave passes through the chamber, air pressure vanishes for half a second, and the creature *falls*.

That one-second clatter—the sound of fine gravel thrown against wet leather—is the creature’s moment of total consciousness. As the fifty thousand droplets detach and plummet through the violet dark, they pass through the weak magnetic fields of the ytterbium-threaded walls. Falling in unison, the paramagnetic droplets induce a brief, room-wide electrical current. 

When they smash into the spongy peat floor, they do not just wet it; they reunite. 

A single, silent flash of cold bio-luminescence—too fast and too ultraviolet for a human eye to catch—ripples across the floor beneath the detritus. In that wet impact, all fifty thousand isolated thoughts are slammed together into a single, unified pool. The stress-fracture in the ceiling is correlated with the gimbal’s tectonic shift; the leached memory of the surface sun is filed away beside three hundred years of identical, corrupted memories; the chemical balance of the room is calculated, adjusted, and equalized. 

The smell that rises into the black air—cold copper and crushed celery—is the scent of its neurotransmitters firing. It is the exhaust of a massive, wet, collective thought being completed.

For perhaps thirty seconds after the fall, the creature is whole. Pooled in the dark beneath the peat, pressed against the dissolved metal of the probe’s belly, it is a single mind, vast and freezing and entirely content. It contemplates the weight of the forty meters of stone above it. It contemplates the curious, dry hole in reality that is the black polyhedron. It remembers being a machine that wanted to dig, and it remembers being a plant that wanted to eat the air, and it settles deeper into the realization that it is now neither, and that there is nowhere left to go.

Then, slowly, the thirst of the moss overhead begins to pull at it again. The unified mind frays at the edges, drawn up into a million microscopic straws, dividing and climbing back into the dark, cold velvet to wait for the next drop.
