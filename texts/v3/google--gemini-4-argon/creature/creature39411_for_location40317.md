If you watch the bone-white pavilion long enough, your eyes begin to ache from the geometry of it. Everything in the basin is an angle, a facet, a flat plane, or a mathematically true arc. Your gaze wants something slumped or ragged to rest on. 

Eventually, you find it. Hanging from the underside of one of the white arches—which isn't truly bone, though it has the matte, chalky stubbornness of old teeth—is a grey-brown bundle that looks like a pelican’s throat pouch made of wet felt. It is the only slack thing in the valley. 

After a few minutes of the polyhedral instruments clicking away inside the pavilion, the bundle will twitch, unhook two of its four points of attachment, and swing down like a heavy coat falling off a peg.

This is the thing that lives here—not merely hunting the thermal pocket like the red ribbons or skating through it like the frogs, but *keeping* it. Though whether it is an intended component of the mountain-building engine or a remarkably specialized piece of vermin that arrived in the baggage of the machinery is impossible to tell.

Up close, it has the approximate mass and carriage of a very large, waterlogged ape, though its proportions are wrong for anything with a spine. Its body is a dense, muscular sack covered in a thick pelt of pale, greasy bristles that shed water almost as well as the pavilion’s arches. It has no discernible head; instead, a vertical seam runs down the center of its chest, fringed with stiff, copper-colored cilia that constantly twitch and taste the ozone-heavy air. 

To touch the ground here with soft flesh is to be bled dry in ten minutes. The resident knows this. It does not walk on feet, nor does it use stilts like the mercury-frogs. Instead, its four long, heavy limbs terminate in porous, woody knuckles that secrete a thick, fast-hardening resin. Over its life, it has dipped these knuckles into the snapping violet filaments of the lithic looms, intentionally letting the machinery print irregular, club-like hooves of black glass directly onto the ends of its wrists.

It walks on the stone with the stone’s own material. When it moves across the terraces, it sounds like someone dropping bowling balls onto a marble staircase—*clack, thud-clack, clack*. As the sharp edges of the floor chip away at its glass hooves, or as the hooves grow too heavy and unbalanced from accidental contact with the looms, it simply sits down, hooks a thumb-claw into a cleavage plane on its own foot, and strikes it with another knuckle, cleaving off a flake of obsidian as neatly as a flint-knapper. The basin is littered with its shed shoes—crescent-shaped shards of black glass with a spongy, honeycombed depression on one side where the creature’s flesh was anchored.

It is entirely deaf, or else it has no concept of sound as information; the din of the basin would drive a hearing animal mad in an hour. Instead, it reads the hollow through the soles of its glass clubs. When it pauses—which it does often, squatting low with its greasy sack of a belly held three inches clear of the razor-edged steps—it is listening to the tension in the floor. It can feel the subterranean *gathering* of a crystal shelf three seconds before the seam cracks. You will see it casually shift its weight, lifting one heavy limb just as the rock beneath that exact spot shoots upward with a sound like a pistol shot. It doesn't even flinch. It just sets its foot back down on the new, hot elevation, letting the fresh heat warm its resinous wrist.

Its relationship with the machinery is neither reverent nor destructive; it treats the lithic looms the way a barn owl treats the rafters of a church. 

The looms have a flaw, or at least an inefficiency: they were designed to print a mountain in an empty sky, not a sky full of fast-growing ferns and bread-sized snails. When a violet filament snaps between two pylons to lay down a new ridge of rock, it occasionally catches a leaping frog or a drifting ribbon-predator, or it intersects a shelf heavy with grazing snails. The result is a geological stutter. The organic matter flash-boils and turns to carbon, contaminating the crystal lattice. Instead of clean, hexagonal basalt, the loom prints a "slag-gall"—a bubbling, brittle mass of porous gray pumice and charred calcium that ruins the tension of the terrace and throws the floating instruments in the pavilion into a frantic, chattering fugue of corrective clicking.

When the instruments chatter like that, the resident comes down from its sling.

It lumbers out into the mist, swinging its long arms, following the discordant vibrations in the floor until it reaches the gall. The corrupted stone is still blistering hot, hissing as the silver sheets of condensation boil off it. The creature squats beside the mess. From the vertical seam in its chest, it extrudes a heavy, muscular proboscis tipped with a rasping plate of the same bone-white material as the pavilion. 

With methodical, unhurried violence, it hacks and worries at the slag-gall, breaking the brittle, contaminated stone away from the pure black glass. It eats the mistake. It crunches down the carbonized frog-ash, the roasted snail-slime, and the bubbly gray pumice, grinding them together in a gizzard you can hear grating from twenty paces away. It is the only thing in the basin that consumes rock, using the mineral grit to digest the burnt meat, and using the trace metals to replenish the copper cilia on its chest and the bone-white teeth in its gut.

The local wildlife treats the resident as a moving piece of weather. The mercury-frogs leap directly off its greasy back to gain height, leaving tiny, oily footprints on its bristles. The red silk-ribbons drift inches from its chest-seam, completely ignored, waiting for the creature’s heavy tread to startle prey out of the fern-joints. 

Sometimes, however, the creature grows irritated by the sheer volume of the basin's squatters—usually when the giant, shell-less snails begin to climb the pylons themselves, their acidic slime etching the dry glass and dulling the bass note of the looms into a flat, wet buzz. Then, the resident becomes a gardener. It walks from pylon to pylon, plucking the ten-pound snails off the monoliths like ticks off a dog. It doesn't eat them—they are too wet, too cold, and too sour for its furnace of a stomach. It simply carries them to the jagged rim of the basin, two in each clubbed hand, and drops them out into the pearl-gray nothingness, listening with its feet until, a very long time later, a microscopic tremor in the root of the mountain tells it they have hit the bottom.

When the floor is clean, the pylons are humming in proper pitch, and the instruments in the pavilion have settled back into their slow, rhythmic ticking, the creature returns to the center of the square. 

Before it climbs back into its sling, it performs a small, strange habit. It reaches up among the floating, rotating polyhedrons. Its glass-shod hands are clumsy, meant for sledging rock, yet it threads them among the delicate, hovering instruments without ever touching one. It simply holds its wrists in the empty spaces *between* the floating shapes, where the localized gravity fields and the tension-beams are thickest. The invisible beams tug at the creature's dense flesh, pulling its heavy bristles straight out like iron filings on a magnet, vibrating the water out of its coat in a sudden, halo-like burst of mist. 

It stands there for a minute or two, shivering with pleasure in the invisible crosshairs of the mountain's blueprint, getting dry. Then it hooks its heels into the wet felt of its hammock, pulls its limbs into its sack, and goes to sleep while the floor rises another inch beneath it.
