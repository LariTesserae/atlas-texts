If you stay on that narrow ledge until the chill gets into the marrow of your shins—until the roaring static of the ten thousand drains stops sounding like noise and begins to feel like a physical pressure holding your eardrums apart—you will realize that the water-wheels are not spinning uselessly after all. Or rather, you will notice that the *clack-clack-clack* of a wheel three yards from your face has just stopped, though the water is still pouring over its paddles. 

In the milky, directionless gleam of the slime, you will see that a grey stone lintel has grown a knuckle. 

The knuckle is the exact color of the wet soapstone, coated in the same tallowy mineral glaze, and for twenty minutes it does not move at all. Then, with the slow, hydraulic deliberateness of a root splitting a flagstone, a second knuckle appears beside it, and a broad, flat thumb hooks under the axle of the little water-wheel, lifting it a fraction of an inch out of its socket to clear a stray grain of quartz from the groove.

They are not the builders, or if they are, they have forgotten the building and kept only the tending. 

To look at one directly is to misunderstand it, because your eye wants to separate the creature from the architecture, and here the two have been pickled in the same lime-drip for so long that the distinction is mostly philosophical. At rest, wedged into an alcove between two gurgling downspouts or folded beneath the arch of a split-slate bridge, one of the Tuners looks like a slump of wet potters’ clay, or a bundle of roots that has calcified into stalagmite. They are roughly the size of an autumn squash or a newborn calf folded tightly in the womb, though their proportions are impossible to judge until they unspool. 

When they do move, they do not walk across the miniature city; they extrude themselves through it. 

A Tuner has no bones, or else it has too many tiny, shifting ones—plates of gristle and chalky shell under a hide like greased leather. It can flatten its belly to slide along a three-inch aqueduct without spilling a drop over the lip, and it can reach an arm around three separate corners of a colonnade at once to adjust a sluice-pin. The arms are long, heavy at the wrist, and terminate in hands that look discouragingly human in their ruin: four thick, shovel-nailed fingers and two opposing thumbs, one on either side of the palm, calloused with actual mineral scale. The skin is the dead blue-grey of the soapstone, marbled with pale scars where the glowing bacterial slime has tried to colonize their backs and been slowly scraped away.

They have no eyes. In this sourceless, glowing gloom, eyes would only tell them what they already know by the shape of the stone. Instead, their heads are low, broad domes of scarred cartilage, pressed flat against their shoulders without the vanity of a neck. Where a face should be, there is only a wide, downward-turned mouth with a lip like a river-clam’s foot, and flanking it, two immense, intricate auricles. 

These ears are the most complex things in the cavern. They are not flaps of skin, but deeply whorled, chambered funnels of stiff gristle that take up nearly a third of the creature’s bulk, resembling the insides of chambered nautiluses or the carved sound-holes of ancient viols. Small, muscular valves along the rims of these funnels twitch and flare constantly, shutting out the general roar of the room to isolate the pitch of a single clay pipe four tiers down, or the hollow *plock* of a cistern thirty feet up the wall.

To the Tuners, the cavern is not loud. The roar that deafens you is, to them, a three-dimensional map of absolute clarity. Every spout has a pitch; every basin has a resonance; every stone wheel has a rhythm. Together, the ten thousand trickles form a vast, unchanging chord. 

A Tuner lives inside this chord the way a spider lives inside the tension of its web. 

It will sit in a high niche for forty hours, its chin resting on a wet lintel, its great fluted ears cupped toward the central basin, cold blood beating in its throat once every three minutes. It is listening to the architecture. Beneath it, a pale salamander may crawl directly over its foot; a palm-sized amphipod may shelter in the dry fold behind its knee. The Tuner does not eat them. Its clam-lip occasionally scrapes a ribbon of the glowing tallow-slime from the wall, or sucks a mouthful of mineral-heavy silt from a settling-pot, digesting the iron and sulfur and spitting out a neat, round pellet of grey clay which it immediately thumbs into a crack in the masonry.

Then, somewhere in the dark honeycomb on the far side of the room, a flake of slate slips. Or a snail crawls into a thumb-thick drainpipe and dies there. Or the lime-deposit on the lip of a gargoyle grows a hair too thick, splitting a single stream into two.

To your ears, nothing has changed; the static is still an unbroken wall of consonants. To the Tuner, a half-flat note has just rung out in the cathedral. A shadow has fallen across the sound.

Across the cavern, in three or four different niches, the stones begin to uncurl. 

They do not hurry—nothing with blood as cold as cellar-water can hurry—but they move with the terrifying, trackless certainty of worms in an apple. One lowers itself down a vertical face of tiny balconies, four thumbs hooking into fist-sized windows with practiced, blind memory, never knocking a loose pebble from its seat. Another slips into a deep runnel and lets the current carry its heavy, boneless bulk down a flight of miniature water-stairs, its body conforming to the ninety-degree turns like a bag of mercury. 

When the first Tuner reaches the offending spout, it does not fight the others that arrive ten minutes later. They do not speak; how could they, and why would they add a messy, living voice to the perfection of the stone’s noise? Instead, two or three of them will pile together on a narrow slate viaduct, their cold, slick bodies overlapping like wet sacks, six or eight hands working in the dark of a single niche. 

One uses a thick, horn-nailed forefinger—grown long and curved specifically like a gouge-chisel—to ream the excess calcium from the bronze sluice-pin. Another presses its broad, flat chest against a leaking wall to stop the flow while a third packs fresh grey spit-clay into a weeping joint. 

If a stone has worn entirely through—if five hundred years of a three-inch waterfall have finally bored a hole in the bottom of a basin—one of the Tuners will reach into a dry, dark pocket beneath its own armpit, a fold of skin where it carries its tools, and draw out a pebble of harder river-chert or a shard of old green bronze. With its heavy, calcified knuckles, it will tap-tap-tap the new piece into place, or score a fresh runnel into the soft soapstone to divert the water back to its proper pot. Their fingernails are constantly growing, thick and grey as horse-hoof, fed by the lime in the water they drink, so they always have a blade to pare the stone.

As they work, their great, nautilus-shell ears are pressed together, almost touching, angling toward the trickle. 

*Glug-glug-clack.* No. Too sharp. 

A thumb scrapes a curl of soapstone as thin as birch-bark from the lip of the drain. 

*Glug-glug-plock.* 

The chord resolves. The acoustic engine drops back into its ancient, vertical groove. The vibration in the stone beneath their bellies smooths out, the tiny dissonance erased before the water in the central basin could even register the change in pressure.

For an hour afterward, the three of them might remain tangled together on the narrow ledge, their clam-mouths pressed to the wet slate, drinking the cold seepage, their skin slowly taking on the sheen of the surrounding rock as the spray coats them. If they breed, they do it then—some slow, gill-to-gill exchange of milt in a flooded cistern, leaving behind half a dozen leathery, four-cornered eggs wedged behind a water-wheel where the rhythmic *clack-clack-clack* will rock the embryos until they know the tempo of the room in their sleep. If one of them dies, it does not rot; the others simply fold its heavy, gristled body into an empty ossuary niche that has become too loud with echo, and the lime-water glazes it over in a decade, turning the corpse into a necessary baffle for the sound.

Look down at your boots on the balcony ledge. 

The heel of your coat has brushed a tiny stone gutter at waist-height, diverting a trickle of water no thicker than a knitting needle so that it drips onto the toe of your leather shoe instead of the resonant clay pot six inches below your ankle. 

You cannot hear the difference over the roar. You have no idea that a tiny hole has just opened in the ten-thousand-year-old chord. 

But twenty feet away, and ten feet down, and directly above your head where the ceiling weeps, seven small, wet stones have just uncurled their thumbs. They are turning their great, blind, fluted ears toward the strange, soft *thup-thup-thup* of water hitting wool and boot-leather, and very slowly, at the speed of clock-hands, they are coming to fix the leak.
