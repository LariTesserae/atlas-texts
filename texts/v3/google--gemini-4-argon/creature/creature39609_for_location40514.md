At first glance, the open spaces of red dust and smooth granite between the plant clusters appear uninhabited, serving only as kill-zones where the crimson lizards streak from one velvet fern to the next. The heat and the noise seem to belong entirely to the frantic, miniature jungle clinging to the pylons.

Wait through three or four cycles of the machines, however, and the geometry of the sinkhole begins to shift. 

A patch of the dark, damp soil at the base of a pylon—a section roughly the size and shape of a butcher’s block—detaches itself from the bedrock. It does not stand up on legs, nor does it slither. It moves with the slow, unsettling fluidity of heavy mud sliding down a pane of glass, propelled by a muscular, corrugated underside similar to a slug’s foot, though dry and dusted with red grit. 

They are easy to miss because they are the exact color and texture of the basin floor, and because everything else in the sinkhole is moving at sixty miles an hour while they move at the speed of a clock’s hour hand. There are five of them in the basin. They are massive, amorphous grazers, shaped somewhat like broad, flattened loaves of dough, weighing close to four hundred pounds apiece. Their hides are thick, fibrous, and deeply fissured, acting as mobile terraces for the very ecosystem they inhabit. Rich, lime-green moss grows in the creases of their backs, and their skin is so thoroughly insulated against the acoustic violence of the pit that the copper-veined cicadas frequently land on them by the hundreds, mistaking them for warm boulders. 

These creatures—though they have no eyes, ears, or distinct heads—are the true masters of the basin’s peculiar physics. They do not eat the ferns or the insects. They graze on the pylons.

When one of the matte-black monoliths prepares to cycle, the local gravity begins to slip before the moisture actually drops. The fast-moving creatures ignore this until it happens, simply riding out the spatial warp. The slow grazers, however, feel the tide turning in their dense, crystalline inner-ear structures—organs that run the entire length of their bodies like lateral lines in a fish. Minutes before a pylon fires, the nearest grazer will inch up to the black rubbery surface and press its flank flat against the machine.

When the distortion wave hits, the creature does not bend away from it; it leans *into* the warp. Its heavy, dense flesh briefly elongates, stretching upward along the pylon in a smear of impossible, non-spectrum colors as space twists around it. As the ambient moisture of the high desert is ripped from the air and slammed against the pylon, the grazer opens thousands of wide, gill-like pores along its raised side. It drinks the water at the exact millisecond of its forced condensation, while the liquid is still charged with the anomalous kinetic energy and static ozone of the machine's effort. It isn't just drinking water; it is drinking the waste momentum of the gravity drive, metabolizing the heat and the pressure drop directly into its slow, heavy cells.

Yet even these living boulders are only furniture to the sinkhole’s permanent resident.

High up on the eastern wall, just beneath the smooth, bone-like overhang where the plateau wind blows its constant flute-note, the python-thick vines weave together into a particularly dense, tangled canopy. Hanging upside down within this curtain of greenery is something that looks, at first, like a discarded bundle of dried leather and copper wire.

She—though gender is a loose translation for an organism that budded off from a solitary ancestor ten thousand years ago—is roughly the size of a teenage human, though her proportions are entirely wrong for walking on flat ground. Her torso is a compact, barrel-chested cage of fused ribs, covered in a short, velvety pelt that matches the deep, bruised purple of the fern shadows. She has four arms, tremendously long and double-jointed, ending in wide, spatulate fingers tipped with blunt, hooked claws of translucent horn. Her legs are short, bowed, and essentially vestigial, tucked up against her groin and used only to anchor her weight in the vines.

She is an ambush predator that has evolved to hunt in a distorted gravity well, inside a bell that never stops ringing. 

Because the noise in the sinkhole is a permanent, physical wall of metallic grinding, ears would be useless to her; they would have been blown out at birth. Instead, her face is a smooth, featureless dish of taut, pale skin stretched over a hollow cartilaginous skull. She has no eyes, nostrils, or mouth on the front of her head—her breathing spiracles are tucked behind her collarbones, and her wide, crushing jaw is located on the underside of her throat. Her blank, pale face is a biological sonar array. She reads the sinkhole by feeling how the deafening roar of the cicadas bounces off the moving bodies below. To her, the world is a blindingly bright, constantly shifting tapestry of acoustic shadows. A crimson lizard darting across the stone isn't a flash of red; it is a sharp, moving tear in the wall of sound. 

She spends ninety percent of her life completely motionless, her metabolism dialed down to a crawl to match the slow grazers below, letting the warm, wet air wash over her pelt. Her copper-colored blood runs slow and thick. 

Then, she gets hungry. 

She does not climb down to hunt. She waits for the pylons below her perch to align their cycles. She knows the rhythm of the ancient machines better than they know it themselves; their timing is etched into her nervous system. 

When two nearby pylons prepare to pulse within seconds of each other, a strange tension builds in the air of the forty-yard bowl. The cicadas’ grinding reaches a frantic, unified crescendo. The bird-mantises scatter upward toward the rim to avoid the double-warp. 

Right as the air shimmers, she lets go of the vines. 

She does not fall straight down. She drops with her four long arms spread wide, stretching a membrane of loose, tough skin between her wrists and her ribs. She falls deliberately into the outer edge of the first pylon’s gravity distortion. 

To an outside observer, it looks like a glitch in reality. The pale-faced creature drops ten feet, hits the shimmering pocket of bent space, and is violently whipped sideways at an impossible angle without losing momentum, her purple fur flashing with ultraviolet and nameless, refractive hues. She uses the gravity well like a slingshot. Silent, heavy, and moving faster than the six-legged lizards, she ricochets out of the first distortion wave, glides two feet above the red dust, and sweeps her four long arms through the startled cloud of bird-mantises that were hovering in the safe zone between the monoliths. 

Her spatulate fingers don't grab; they swat and scoop, funneling three or four of the clattering, rapier-limbed birds directly into the wide, toothless maw open at her throat. 

Before she hits the opposite granite wall, she angles her skin-membrane and catches the edge of the *second* pylon’s pulse, which bends her trajectory straight upward. Carried by the warped space and her own stolen momentum, she shoots back up the twenty-yard wall in a silent, multi-colored arc, her claws catching the heavy vines near the rim with a dull thud that is entirely swallowed by the roar of the bugs.

She settles back into the leaves, folding her long arms over her chest. As she slowly digests the frantic, high-speed creatures she has just swallowed, her pale, dish-like face tilts upward toward the circular opening of the sky. 

Sometimes, a wandering mountain goat or a high-plains scavenger will peer over the smooth granite lip, drawn by the weird, warm smell of wet ozone bleeding out into the cold desert air. The creature in the vines always knows they are there instantly; their heads cast a distinct acoustic shadow against the low flute-note of the wind. She never bothers them. To her, the flat, silent, normal-gravity world above the rim is as alien and uninhabitable as the vacuum of space. She simply waits in the deafening, steaming dark of the vines, listening to the ancient machines breathe, waiting for the air to bend again.
