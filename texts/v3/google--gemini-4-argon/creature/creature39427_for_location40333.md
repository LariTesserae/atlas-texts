To find what lives here, you have to stop looking for movement and start looking for tension. 

At first, the pocket seems entirely devoid of fauna. The silvered dust between the brass rosettes is smooth, unmarked by tracks, droppings, or burrows. Nothing flies through the arc-lamp glare. Nothing nests in the spun-glass reeds. If you stand at the lip of the drop-off and watch the courtyard for an hour, the only changes you will register are the jerky, delayed frame-updates of the silver fern and the microscopic tightening of the lead-shaving spiral. 

Then, near the radiator-fin bush, a shadow detaches itself from the base of a stone ingot and slides three inches to the left, entirely independent of the sun. 

It is not a shadow. It is an *aperture*—a flat, ribbon-like thing no thicker than a coat of grease, composed of dark, polarized mica and articulated plates of bluing steel. When it lies flat against the iron filings of the floor, it is invisible. Only when it angles itself to catch the sixty-cycle hum of the overhang does it take on volume, rising onto six needle-sharp legs that function like drafting compasses. 

It does not walk; it plots. One leg plants itself in the ash, and the body swings around it in a precise, frictionless arc, finding a new coordinate before the next leg drops. There are three of them in the courtyard, though you will rarely see more than one at a time. They have no eyes, no mouths, and no viscera. Their bodies are essentially living calipers, evolved—or instantiated—to measure the gaps in the courtyard’s unfinished physics. 

One of them approaches the floating quartz boulder. It reaches the edge of the three-foot matte-gray patch where the floor hasn't rendered. The creature does not avoid the dead zone; it steps directly onto the frictionless gray plane, where its reflection vanishes. Tilting its narrow, ruler-flat head upward, it extends a pair of fine tungsten filaments from its thorax. One filament touches the underside of the floating rock; the other touches the velvet-black hydraulic vines beneath it. Instantly, a blue spark jumps the four-inch gap of empty air, smelling sharply of scorched insulation. The creature holds the circuit closed for ten seconds, trembling with the voltage, feeding on the discrepancy between *where the rock is* and *where the ground ought to be*. When the spark dies, the creature folds itself flat again, a dark bevel resting on the nothingness, digesting the static.

They are the smallest things here, the parasites of the render-lag. The larger resident does not cross the floor at all, because it is not entirely separate from the mountain.

If you look closely at the curving overhang of newly extruded stone—where the rock is sharpest and the sixty-cycle hum is loudest—you will notice a section where the crystalline grain of the granite has organized itself into a dense, honeycomb lattice of ceramic and bone-white porcelain. Nestled into this dry socket, half-fused with the cliffside, is something the size of a butcher’s block. 

It resembles an engine block cast in the shape of a crouching beast, or a beast that was poured into a sand-mold and left with the rough, grainy seams of its casting still attached to its flanks. Its hide is a patchwork of dull cast-iron and braided copper grounding-straps that breathe—slowly, incrementally—expanding with a dry, metallic creak every ten minutes to vent the staggering heat of its interior. 

It has a head, though the head has no face—only a heavy, sloped snout of leaded bronze, pitted with thermal sensors and capped with a thick lens of smoky quartz where a brow should be. It has four limbs, thick as anvil horns, currently locked into slots in the raw granite cliff. It has been sitting in this exact posture since the tear in the ridge opened, acting as a living step-down transformer for the raw, unshaped reality pouring out of the mountain’s core.

Around its fused paws, the dark iron filings of the soil have stood up on end, arranged in stiff, furry rosettes that trace the massive magnetic field radiating from its belly.

Every few hours, when the velvet-black vines beneath the floating boulder pulse with their heavy throb of pumped mercury, the creature in the wall wakes up. 

The waking is not a startle; it is a sequence. First, the smoky quartz lens in its forehead clears, turning from opaque black to a heated, translucent amber, glowing from within like a vacuum tube warming up. The sixty-cycle hum in the overhang drops an octave, thickening into a bass vibration that makes the loose ash on the courtyard floor dance in geometric Chladni patterns. 

With a sound like a wrench snapping a rusted bolt, the creature pulls its front right limb free from the stone socket. Where the iron paw leaves the granite, the rock is glowing a dull, forge-cherry red. Moving with tremendous, geared deliberation, the creature leans out from the wall. It does not drop to the ground; its hindquarters remain welded to the mountain's plumbing, trailing thick, umbilical bundles of rubberized cable that vanish into the solid rock. 

It reaches out with a heavy, two-toed claw—shaped like wire-stripper jaws—and grasps one of the spun-glass reeds growing in the corner. With exquisite, mechanized gentleness, it punctures a swollen seed-pod. A thick ribbon of amber dielectric oil dribbles out, not falling to the dust, but running down the groove of the creature's bronze snout, drawn inward by capillary action to cool its superheated core. As the oil hits its internal plates, a plume of sweet, chemical white smoke vents from the gills along its ribs, smelling of hot machine oil and pine rosin.

While it drinks, its attention is fixed on the center of the courtyard—on the silver fern that lags behind the wind. 

The creature’s smoky quartz eye clicks, an internal aperture narrowing. A pale, hair-thin beam of coherent red light—a surveyor’s laser—projects from the lens, sweeping across the fern’s three contradictory shadows, then dragging across the matte-gray patch of unrendered floor. 

The creature is waiting. It is a mason, or a groundskeeper, born slightly ahead of the territory it is meant to inhabit. It drinks the oil, vents its smoke into the flat cobalt sky, and measures the three-foot patch of gray nothingness with its red eye, waiting for the mountain to finish arriving so it can finally pull its back legs out of the stone and walk.
