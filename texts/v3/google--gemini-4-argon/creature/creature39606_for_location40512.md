To find what lives here, you cannot look at the surfaces. A creature with a shell, a spine, or a nest would be ground into paste within an hour by the shifting carpentry. To survive in an architecture that is constantly tying and untying itself, an inhabitant cannot be an object at all. It must be a condition of the material.

Look closely at the great translucent conduits running along the avenues, where the boiling, copper-scented water surges uphill. Inside the foaming, shadowy rush of those ten-foot pipes, something is swimming against the peristaltic current. 

At first, they look like bundles of stripped phloem—pale, fibrous ribbons five or six feet long, frayed at the edges like wet linen. They have no eyes, no mouths, and no fixed anatomy. They are colonies of hydro-vascular filaments, existing somewhere between a siphonophore and a braided taproot. They live entirely inside the high-pressure plumbing of the city. When the wood-pumps contract with that deep, resonant *lub-dub*, these ribbon-things flare open like parachutes, catching the scalding surge to ride it three hundred feet up into a newly forming campanile, or collapsing into tight, needle-thin spindles to slip backward through the valves before a joint seals shut.

They are the city’s internal hitchhikers, feeding on the hyper-oxygenated, sugar-heavy sap that leaks into the water tubes where the fast-growing wood splits under its own tension. 

But they do not stay in the pipes forever. The architecture is too volatile; conduits are constantly being pinched off, drained, and unbraided. The inhabitants must be able to read the structural mood of the valley, and they do this through vibration. 

When a sector is about to lose its turgor pressure—minutes before a massive viaduct actually lets go and untangles into the reservoirs—the acoustic pitch of the wood changes. The tall-ship creaking rises by half an octave; the rifle-shot popping of the canopy slows down. Before the hydraulic drop even registers in the sap, the pale ribbons bunch together behind the thinning walls of the high-pressure joints. When the wall finally tears open in a geyser of steam and hot rain, they are blown out into the open plaza by the hundreds.

Hitting the ankle-deep runoff of the braided floor, they do not flop or writhe like stranded fish. They instantly unspool. 

What was a six-foot ribbon in the pipe becomes a translucent, glassy sheet three yards wide and no thicker than a soap bubble, spreading flat across the iron-hard weave of the roots. Moving with the fast, clear current of the floor-water, they glide across the open amphitheaters like living oil slicks, shimmering faintly with the iridescent copper and magnesium they’ve absorbed from the sap. If a whip-like shoot of blind-white timber lashes down from above, striking the floor with enough force to shatter bone, it simply slices straight through the gelatinous sheet. The perforated creature does not bleed; its edges immediately flow back together around the new pillar, re-fusing before the wood has even finished turning bronze.

And yet, as you watch them slide across the flooded courts, you realize they are not merely surviving the city’s hysteria. They are directing it.

Follow one of the shimmering sheets as it reaches the base of the four new obelisks in the center of the valley, where the green cables are currently whipping across the sky to weave a ceiling. The creature does not avoid the violent construction zone. Instead, it draws itself up out of the water, creeping vertically up the glossy, scar-tissue bark of the northeast trunk. 

As it climbs, it changes shape again. It pulls its flat membrane inward, rolling itself into a thick, muscular cord that looks identical to the plant’s own fibrous tendons. It slips into the seam where two load-bearing walls meet—right into the razor-sharp corner where the wood is hottest and the cellular expansion is most frantic. 

There, the creature sinks millions of microscopic, hair-like haustoria directly into the cambium of the obelisk, and it begins to inject a sharp, alcaloid venom into the wood.

The architecture reacts instantly. The venom is an irritant, a false hormone that mimics the plant’s own panic-growth signals. Where the creature stings the trunk, the internal pressure spikes violently. With a sound like a cannon shot, the obelisk splits, and a massive, unplanned cluster of barrel-thick shoots erupts outward at a forty-five-degree angle, completely altering the geometry of the ceiling being woven overhead. The new shoots catch the falling green cables mid-air, pulling them into a tight, domed vault rather than a flat terrace.

Within ten minutes, the creature’s chemical tampering has forced the blind algorithm of the mega-flora to build a shaded, high-pressure cistern three hundred feet in the air—a calm, flooded pocket of warm water shielded from the harsh violet sky and the drying mountain winds. 

As soon as the new vault seals itself in a cloud of hissing steam, the ribbon-creature detaches from the corner seam, drops into the freshly pooled water of the aerial basin it just tricked the city into making, and dissolves back into a dozen smaller, frayed filaments. 

The valley is not empty at all. Every arch, every sudden buttress, every collapsing bridge and newly woven hall is the scar of an argument between the blind, upward rush of the timber and the quiet, liquid architects sliding through its veins, stinging the wood into shapes that suit the water.
