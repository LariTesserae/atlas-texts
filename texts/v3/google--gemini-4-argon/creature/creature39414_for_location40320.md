If you look closely at a mason-whelk—past the clicking cluster of its stone-fitting limbs and the wet, bubbling ridge of its mantle—you will notice that its shell is not entirely its own. 

Sitting in a dry groove near the creature’s apex is a fist-sized clump of jointed, chalk-white twigs. It looks like a tangle of shed whelk-cuticle or a dead root ball wedged into the spiral of the shell. Occasionally, when the air pressure drops before a Sleight, the clump unfolds. It extends two impossibly long, brittle legs—thin as pine needles and multijointed like a spider’s—and taps the stone five feet ahead of the whelk’s blind snout. Then it reaches down with a much shorter limb and touches a specific cluster of nerve-nodules on the whelk’s neck. The great ox-sized mollusk halts, pivots forty degrees on its dry, leathery foot, and begins laying a rib of slotted stone out into the open air, building a buttress to catch a gravity tide that hasn't arrived yet.

The whelks are the muscle and the mortar of the chasm, driven by their simple, panic-stricken instinct to wall off the void. They would simply plaster over the cliff face in blind, tumour-like mounds if left to themselves. They do not know what a bridge is. They do not know what a gallery is. 

The things riding them do.

They are called the *Cadastrals*, though there is no one left with a throat wet enough to pronounce the word. They are the true citizens of the vertical metropolis, the beneficiaries of its million wind-keyholes, and the only minds capable of holding its fractured map together.

A Cadastral is not an animal you can see all at once. What rides the whelk is merely a node—a dry, chitinous ganglion the size of a walnut, protected by a basket of ribs. From the underside of this node trails a fine, silvery-grey thread of desiccated nervous tissue, no thicker than spider silk and coated in a powdery alkaline dust that makes it virtually invisible against the slate. This thread runs down the flank of the whelk, drops off its tail, and lays loosely along the perforated floor of the causeway. It runs through the wind-holes, turns corners, climbs a vertical chimney for six hundred feet, and joins another node sitting in an alcove, which in turn trails four more threads down into the residential honeycombs.

A single Cadastral is a web of dry nerve-cord and walking-stick limbs stretched across three or four square miles of vertical city.

In an environment where the air is too thin to carry a shout and where a solid body can be sheared in half by a sudden shift in perspective, the Cadastrals have abandoned the concept of a centralized anatomy. You cannot be marooned in a pocket of looped space if only your left hand is inside it. When a Sleight rolls through the gulf—when the temperature plunges and the bronze light splits into jagged panes—a Cadastral does not flee. Its threads simply pay out from tiny spools within the nodes, accommodating a three-foot gap that has suddenly become a two-mile chasm. A thread may stretch until it is a single molecule thick, passing straight through a spatial tear where a broken aqueduct pours dust into nothingness, humming faintly with slow, electrochemical pulses. 

If the geometry snaps back too violently and severs the cord, the isolated nodes do not die. They simply become smaller, simpler minds. They pull their severed threads back in, coil them up like dock-ropes, and wait. Days or years later, when another node of the same lineage wanders past on the back of a whelk or crawls by on its brittle stilts, they touch antennae, recognize the specific mineral taste of their shared memory, and splice themselves back together with a drop of resinous spit. 

Because of this, there are only about forty individuals in the entire city, though their bodies comprise millions of creeping, stick-like fragments. Some of them are older than the cliff face they cling to.

To watch a Cadastral at home in the residential spires is to watch a ghost play a harp. The hollow towers—which look to a human eye like abandoned dovecotes or empty honeycomb—are actually booming boards for the Cadastral minds. Because sound dies in the thin air, they communicate and perceive through vibration in the stone and tension in the silk. A node will anchor itself in the center of a vaulted gallery, extending thirty or forty hair-thin filaments to plug directly into the tiny apertures of the walls. As the tearing winds hiss through the perforations, the stone hums at microscopic frequencies. The Cadastral reads the weather of the gulf by the trembling of the architecture. It feels the weight of a parchment-glider landing on a parapet half a mile away; it feels the slow, rhythmic grinding of a thousand stone-borers chewing through a lower viaduct; most importantly, it feels the groan of the slate just before gravity uncouples.

They do not eat the lichen, nor do they hunt the gliders. Their metabolism is glacially slow, fueled almost entirely by the piezoelectric charge generated when the chasm’s shifting gravity squeezes the quartz-heavy slate of the walls, supplemented by the mineral-rich mucus they harvest from the mason-whelks. They are creatures of pure arrangement.

For a long time, the sparse travelers who glimpsed the city assumed the architecture was a relic of some vanished, bipedal race—that the whelks were merely feral livestock maintaining a dead master’s house. The truth is stranger: the city was never built for anyone who walks on two legs. The boulevards, the vaulted arches, the sweeping cantilevered plazas that step out over the three-mile drop—these are not roads. They are looms, and they are baffles.

The Cadastrals use the whelks to build structures that manipulate the Sleights. 

A Cadastral knows that if you stack slotted shale in a specific, towering parabola on the Whispering Terrace, it will catch the edge of a passing geometric anomaly and pin it in place for a week, like a cloak snagged on a nail. They do this deliberately. A pinned Sleight creates a localized distortion where a fifty-yard gap can be crossed in a single stride, allowing the Cadastral’s slow-moving whelk herds to reach a rich bed of salt-lichen on an otherwise inaccessible pinnacle without burning calories. They build the great hollow viaducts not to carry traffic, but to channel the heavy, alkaline dust-tides toward their blind builders, and to create wind-shadows where their fragile nerve-threads won't be snapped by the gales.

The entire metropolis is a vast, slow-motion machine for catching, bending, and surviving broken space, constantly torn apart by the chasm’s logic and constantly re-stitched by blind mollusks under the direction of a creature that is ninety percent string and memory.

Sometimes, however, a Sleight is too profound even for them. 

Deep in the lower tiers, down near the point where the bronze light gives up entirely, there are districts that have fallen out of the city’s shared time altogether. Here, the spatial loops have knotted themselves so tightly that they never untangle. If you were to descend the slotted ribs into that cold dark, you would find the *Snarls*. 

In a Snarl, a single archway might repeat itself forty times in the span of ten yards. And caught within it is a Cadastral that could not let go of itself in time. As the space folded and multiplied, the creature’s nodes were duplicated, inverted, and stacked upon one another, their connecting threads woven through four or five overlapping dimensions until they formed a dense, dry mat of silver-grey felt that completely chokes the gallery. 

These tangled minds have gone mad with geometry. Cut off from the upper city, they still command the few mason-whelks trapped in the loop with them. In the pitch dark, in the absolute silence of the thin air, the whelks click endlessly back and forth across a three-foot bridge that goes nowhere, building a wall, tearing it down, and building it again at four right angles to itself—guided by the frantic, twitching twigs of a mind trying to solve a maze that has no outside.
