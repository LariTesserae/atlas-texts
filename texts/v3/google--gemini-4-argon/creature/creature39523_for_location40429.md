At the base of the northern wall, where a fault line in the granodiorite forms a jagged, three-inch overhang, there is a cluster of small, clear domes that look entirely like frozen droplets of runoff. They are the exact refractive index of the syrupy water. When the municipal drone passes over them, its violet laser passes right through them, reading only the dark stone beneath. 

Only when the mist blooms—in that brief, three-second window before the electrostatic grid slams the moisture to the deck—do the domes soften and uncurl.

They are low-slung, eight-legged things, no larger than a thumbnail, with bodies made of a dense, translucent hydro-gel. They have no shell in the traditional sense; their exterior is a rubbery, super-hydrophobic membrane that holds their internal fluids under high pressure to keep them from freezing solid. When they move, they do not skitter like the lizards above. They glide on the surface tension of the wet stone, their eight paddle-like feet dimpling the water without breaking it, skating along the geometric channels in absolute silence.

They are grazers of the seam. When the mist falls with its soft *shhhk*, the creatures are knocked flat against the stone, absorbing the kinetic shock by flattening into coin-thin discs. Then, as the heavy water begins to sheet toward the southern wall, they ride the sluggish current down to the mineral-crust biofilm. They have no eyes, only a ring of barometric and thermal pits around their margins. Using mouthparts forged from the very silicates they ingest—hard, black scrapers of biological glass—they rasp away the outer edges of the sulfur-eating crust. They are meticulous and slow. Because the municipal drone expects the biofilm to grow in specific, mathematical fractals, the creatures only ever eat the dead, chalky interior of the pattern, leaving the growing edges intact so the city’s diagnostics never flag an anomaly.

Higher up, clinging to the smoked-ceramic facade, lives something that looks like an architectural flaw. 

It appears, at first glance, to be a smear of pale dielectric grease left behind by a maintenance rig, or perhaps a patch of stubborn rime that the rippling wall-scales have failed to shed. It is about the size of a dinner plate, translucent and veined with faint, milky filaments, pressed flat against the microscopic scales of the southern wall. 

In truth, it is a colonial organism, a cold-water myxomycete that has jumped the gap from rotting alpine timber to synthetic polymer. It does not fight the wall’s constant, frost-shedding ripple; it uses it. The creature has no muscles of its own. Instead, it adjusts the viscosity of its underside in micro-second intervals, stiffening one half of its body while liquefying the other, allowing the endless, microscopic wave-motion of the city’s smart-panels to peristaltically inch it across the facade. It surfs the building's skin at a rate of four feet a day.

It is a heat-thief and a static-eater. Behind the smoked ceramic, great trunks of data-conduits and thermal exchangers hum with the life of the metropolis. The colony feels the faint, warm signatures of these interior rooms through the insulation. It migrates endlessly across the wall, chasing the shifting heat loads—clustering over the warm patch of a server bank at midnight, then sliding twenty feet east by dawn to sit over the exhaust run of a residential block’s kitchen sector. 

Its diet is the electrostatic charge of the courtyard itself. When the grid beneath the paving fires to pull down the mist, a faint, residual charge bleeds up the wainscoting. The colony extends hair-thin, conductive tendrils down into the capillary drains, drinking the stray voltage along with the trace nitrates dissolved in the snowmelt. 

And then there is the thing in the notch.

Forty feet up the northern granite face, where the rock was cleaved by a prehistoric frost-heave, there is a vertical fissure no wider than a human hand.Wedged deep inside this crack, out of reach of the four-hour blade of midday sun, is a mass of grey, felt-like material. 

It is a nest, woven from the shed skins of the high-altitude lizards, threads of blown fiberglass insulation from the city's upper tiers, and the brittle, chitinous wings of a thousand dead midges. 

Inside sits a creature the size of a clenched fist, though when it unfolds itself in the dark, its reach is closer to two feet. It is a cousin to the cellar spider and the glacier harvestman, adapted to the crushing thinness of the eleven-thousand-foot air. Its body is a tiny, insulated capsule covered in dense, slate-grey down that traps a microscopic layer of warm air against its skin. Its legs, however, are absurdly long, hairless, and segmented like carbon-fiber whipping rods. 

It is an acoustic hunter in a world of tiny, precise sounds. 

All day, it sits in its felt pocket, its eight legs splayed out to touch the raw basalt on one side and the extruded polymer of the city on the other. It listens. It knows the exact, healthy *tick-tick-tick* of the cooling smart-materials. It knows the resonant frequency of the thimble-drone’s internal gyroscopes. It waits for a discordance.

When a lizard high on the cliffside grows sluggish as the four-hour sun blade slides away, its claws make a slightly duller *scritch* against the granite. When a large, high-altitude moth, blown over the peaks by the stratospheric winds, drops into the courtyard with ice on its wings and strikes the wet slate, the impact sends a minuscule tremor through the water film.

The harvestman does not run down the wall. It drops. 

It waits for the pressure change. Just as the air thickens and the dense mist blooms—blinding the courtyard’s optical sensors and muffling the ambient light—the creature lets go of the rock. It falls thirty feet through the whiteout, spreading its long, whip-like legs to catch the dense, wet air like a parachute, slowing its descent just before the electrostatic grid fires.

*Shhhk.*

The mist collapses into water, and the spider lands at the exact same millisecond as the millions of droplets, its arrival completely masked by the unified sigh of the artificial rain. 

In the three seconds it takes for the syrupy runoff to clear the deck, the creature has already pinned the half-frozen moth, injected it with a cold-active enzyme, and begun the long, patient climb back up the seam where the mountain meets the wall. It moves one leg at a time, matching its rhythm to the *tick-tick-tick* of the expanding ceramic, ascending into the dark cleft before the water has even finished draining into the black wainscoting below.
