To see the people of the Chimney clearly, you must first understand that they do not walk; they *perch*. 

A flat floor is an absurdity to them, a waste of precious horizontal space that could be catching mist or hanging tubers. Even inside their round, mud-and-guano pods, the floors are curved like the insides of bowls, lined with tightly woven mats of agave fiber. A resident of the shaft is never entirely at rest unless their bare heels are hooked over a rung, a root, or a lip of polished basalt, their knees drawn up nearly to their chests. 

Centuries of life inside a three-hundred-foot vertical tube have reshaped them. They are small, compact people, rarely clearing five feet if they were ever to stand fully upright—which they almost never do, as a fully extended spine feels vulnerable, an invitation to a long fall. Their shoulders are extraordinarily broad and heavily muscled, tapering down to narrow hips and short, bowed legs. Their feet are not made for striding; the big toe is widely splayed, opposable as a thumb, calloused to the hardness of horn on the underside from a lifetime of gripping rough lianas and braided copper cables. When a child learns to move, they do not crawl; they clamber, clinging to the shaggy curtains of beard-moss like monkey-spiders before they have even lost their milk teeth.

Because water is too precious to waste on washing, they clean themselves as the desert animals do: with dust and oil. 

Every household keeps a shallow stone trough of the finest, silk-soft grey granite flour brought down from the dead world outside, mixed with dried, powdered sage and the rendered fat of the giant geckos. In the evenings, families sit together on their porches, rubbing this paste into their skin and scraping it off with curved ribs of bone. Over a lifetime, this leaves them with a permanent, pearlescent grey-gold sheen, like greased slate. Their skin is thick, warm, and smells strongly of aromatic herbs and hot stone. Their hair—which grows thick and coarse—is never wasted; it is shorn twice a year and spun into the miles of mist-combing nets that hang at the rim, for human hair holds a bead of condensation better than any plant fiber. Consequently, everyone from the toddlers to the elders has a cropped, bristling skull, which they oil and pattern with ochre clay according to which tier of the shaft they were born in.

The altitude and the sulfur have remade their insides as well. Though the Chimney is warm, the air at three miles high is thin and poor in oxygen, laced with the volcanic breath of the deep earth. The people have massive, barrel-shaped ribcages to house lungs that are huge for their small frames. Their voices, when they speak close-up inside their huts, are surprisingly deep, breathy, and resonant, rumbling in their chests. 

But in the open shaft, against the relentless *chak-chak* of the geckos, the cooing of ten thousand birds, and the drone of the beetles, normal speech is useless. Across the gulf, they whistle. 

A man mending a net-garden in the amber mid-tiers can hold a complex philosophical argument with his cousin three hundred feet above in the blinding sun of the rim, entirely through a rapid, liquid series of trills, clicks, and piercing, hawk-like notes. It is a language of pitch and contour. Because the curved basalt walls create a bizarre acoustic echo-chamber, a skilled whistler can "bounce" a message off a specific polished rock lobe so that it lands directly on the porch of a house hidden behind a curtain of thirty-foot moss, audible to the intended listener and almost no one else. The town is constantly stitched together by these invisible threads of sound—warnings of a slipping counterweight, gossip, love songs, and the hourly call of the cistern-keepers announcing the dew-yield.

Their eyes tell you immediately where in the five-hundred-foot column they spend their days. 

Those of the Rim-Clans—the net-weavers, the dew-bailers, and the ash-gatherers who brave the freezing lip of the crater in heavy coats of stitched bird-skins—have eyes squinted into permanent slits, the whites yellowed and leathery from the violent ultraviolet glare of the violet sky. 

Those of the Sump—the fungal-tenders, the compost-turners, and the blind-millipede herders who live in the perpetual, smoky twilight at the bottom of the pit—have huge, dark, liquid eyes with pupils that scarcely contract, and skin pale as cave-crickets beneath their grease-and-ash coating. When a Sump-dweller climbs up to the mid-tiers to trade a basket of rich black soil for dried rail-meat, they wear goggles carved from flat plates of tortoise-shell or pumice, pierced with tiny pinholes to keep the bruised-amber light from blinding them.

They are a people obsessed with *weight* and *balance*. 

In a city suspended on vines and counterweights, an unannounced shift in mass is a civic hazard. You do not simply walk onto a neighbor’s bridge; you whistle your weight from the anchor-point, waiting until they shift a sandbag or step onto a counter-lever to receive you. Children play games with pebbles and small bone scales the way children elsewhere play with balls. Every adult has an instinctive, bodily sense of tension; sitting in a room with five other people, a Chimney-dweller can feel through the soles of their feet the exact moment a heavy-bodied rail lands on the guy-wire fifty feet away, and they will unconsciously lean three inches to the left to keep the room's suspension lines humming at the proper pitch.

Their relationship with the animals that cram the shaft is not one of farmer and livestock, nor of hunter and prey, but of dense, irritable cohabitation. There are no cages. The heavy, flightless rails wander through the kitchens like fat, feathered dogs, pecking at dropped scraps of tuber-rind until someone decides it is time to wring a neck for the stew-pot. The forearm-sized geckos are sacred nuisance-neighbors; no one kills a gecko while it is alive and hunting the beetles that would otherwise devour the rigging-vines. Instead, every house has a "lizard-ledge" near the warm clay stove where an ancient, scarred gecko lives like a household god, barking at strangers and eating the roaches, until it dies of old age and is respectfully rendered down for skin-oil and bone-needles.

At the very bottom of everything, in the warm, sweet-rotting dark of the Sump, sits the town’s memory and its future. 

Because nothing can be thrown away—not a scrap of fingernail, not a dead leaf, not a drop of urine—death in the Chimney is not an ending, but a descent. When an elder's grip finally fails and their lungs can no longer pull the thin air, they are not buried in the dead granite outside. To put a body outside in the freeze-dried vacuum is the ultimate punishment, reserved only for those who cut a main line or steal from the clay cisterns; out there, a corpse never rots, becoming a grey, leathery stone among stones, useless forever.

Instead, the dead are wrapped in worn-out hair-nets and lowered by the great, creaking bone-wheels down through the tiers. As the bundle descends through the three hundred feet of town, people step out onto their porches to dab a single, microscopic smear of water from their wet-thumbs onto the shroud—a gesture of extravagant grief and respect. 

At the bottom, in the warm, black muck where the blind millipedes churn, the body is laid beneath the roots of the great lianas. Within a month, in that hot, bacterial darkness, they are soil. Within a year, that soil is hauled up in wicker baskets by the wind-wheels to the hanging gardens. Within two, they are the waxy leaves of an agave, the meat of a bird, the fiber of a rope. 

When a person in the Chimney grips a thick, woody vine to swing themselves across the gulf, they do so with a strange, quiet intimacy. They know whose bones were ground into the mortar of the wall, and whose blood is currently holding up the bridge. They live inside a single, warm lung of stone, breathing the same air over and over, holding each other up in the dark so that none of them fall into the cold outside.
