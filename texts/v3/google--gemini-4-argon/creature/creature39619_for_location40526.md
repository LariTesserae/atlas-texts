The worker’s boots are carved from layers of dense, vulcanized rubber and boiled leather, the soles three inches thick to keep the heat of the iron grating from cooking the bottoms of his feet. Even on the elevated catwalks, the air is a wet, alkaline soup. His coat is heavy bleached canvas, stiffened with salt-crust around the hem, and beneath the wide brim of his hat, he wears a pair of spectacle-frames fitted not with glass, but with thin plates of dark horn, each pierced by a horizontal slit. Without them, the double-glare of the violet sky and the white stone would burn the retinas inside a week.

Around his neck hangs a wooden toggle on a leather thong. Right now, it is gripped firmly between his back teeth. 

To live and work in the basin—to be a Tender, as the syndicate calls them, or a "Sinker," as the people down in the timbered valleys say—is to abandon the ears and learn to listen with the jawbone. Because the heavy, vapor-choked air smothers sound at thirty paces, the workers use the infrastructure itself as their medium. When the man at the junction box finishes watching the gauge, he does not shout to his partner a quarter-mile down the line. He leans over the handrail, presses the flat of his wooden biting-wedge against the warm copper of a return-pipe, and taps the pipe three times with the brass heel of his wrench. 

Twelve hundred feet away, inside a dark, thick-walled brick switching-house, a woman sitting on a three-legged stool with her chin resting on a resonance-post feels the *clack-clack-clack* bloom squarely in the center of her skull. She reaches up, adjusts a counter-weighted valve by two notches, and taps twice in reply.

There are perhaps four hundred of them in the basin at any given time. They are a quiet, heavy-limbed people who move with the exact same deliberate, heat-conserving slowness as the clay-colored hexapods clinging to the distillation towers. Fast movement in the basin is a kind of madness; if you run, you sweat out your salts, your heart hammers against the thin mountain oxygen, and you drop on the travertine to be gently boiled in an inch of mineral runoff. So the Tenders walk with a rolling, flat-footed gait, never lifting their boots higher than necessary, gliding along the rusted iron walkways like clockwork figures on a track.

Over generations—for the contracts here are hereditary, passed down through families who have adapted to the strange, bright pressure of the bowl—they have developed their own physiological quirks. Their skin is universally cured to the texture of saddle leather, stained a faint, permanent ochre by the sulfur vapors, except for a stark white band across the eyes where the slit-goggles sit. Their voices, rarely used outside the damp, echoing interiors of the brick bunkhouses, are dry and raspy, barely above a whisper. In the glare of the flats, they speak to one another almost entirely in "Flange"—a crisp, angular sign language conducted with one hand kept squarely in front of the dark canvas of their chests, so the fingers can be read in silhouette rather than lost against the blinding white rock. 

They do not view the machinery and the wildlife as two separate things. In the basin, everything is part of the same slow, thermal digestion. 

A Tender’s job is three parts plumbing to one part husbandry. If the apricot bacterial mats grow too thick in a primary heating shelf, they insulate the stone and drop the temperature of the intake pipes by three degrees; if that happens, the great vapor-turbines half a mile away will lose their differential and stall. Therefore, the Tenders herd the plate-crabs. By raising and lowering the sluice gates, they steer millions of the translucent crustaceans into overgrown basins to graze the stone bare, then flush them out again with a sheet of hotter, deeper water from the sulfur vents when the rock is clean. 

They have an intimate, unsentimental truce with the larger animals. A worker checking the pressure seals on a spherical vat will routinely carry a long, flat-bladed scraper—not as a weapon, but to pry the six-legged clay-lizards off the sight-glasses. The lizards are heavy as soaked sandbags and entirely unafraid of humans; if you prod one with a boot, it will simply open a pale yellow eye, hiss without making a sound, and slide six inches to the left to continue licking distilled water from a weeping rivet. The Tenders tolerate them because the lizards eat the blinding-gnats that occasionally blow up from the lower mountain gorges, and because a cluster of lizards suddenly abandoning a pipe is the surest sign that the internal pressure is spiking toward a rupture.

With the copper-skinned waders, the relationship is more delicate. The birds—though they are not birds at all, having teeth inside their proboscises and giving birth to live, wriggling young in the warm mud of the dead-end pans—are fiercely territorial about the catwalks. They like the high iron because the breeze cools their undersides. To walk down a narrow trestle occupied by a bull wader requires etiquette. The worker must stop ten paces off, pull a rectangle of red-dyed oilcloth from his belt, and snap it once in the air. The wader will uncoil its throat-pouch, flashing a violent, vein-webbed purple in answer, and then step stiffly onto the parallel pipe to let the human pass. A green worker who forgets his cloth and tries to shoo a wader with a wrench will take a brass-hard kick to the chest that can knock him off the grating and down onto the scalding stone below.

The only creature the Tenders actively despise is the salt-otter—what they call the *grease-hound*. The hounds have twelve ribs on a side, every one of them hinged, allowing them to flatten their bodies like eels and slip through the grates of the cooling sumps. They don't care about the heat or the humans, and they have a ruinous habit of chewing through the soft lead gaskets around the base of the syphon-towers to get at the warm, mineral-sweet paraffins that condense inside. Every switching-house has two or three long, heavy-tined iron spears racked by the door, and a grease-hound’s pale pelt, cured in alum and nailed to a bunkhouse door, is a mark of high competence.

When the sun finally drops behind the western rim of the crater, the change is instantaneous and eerie. The furnace door slams shut. The bleached violet sky turns the color of a fresh bruise, then pitch-black, crowded with cold, hard stars that do not twinkle in the thin air. 

The glare vanishes, but the heat remains, rising from the miles of wet travertine in a thick, wooly fog that smells of boiled eggs and wet iron. 

This is when the Tenders come down from the catwalks. They gather in the switching-houses and the low, vaulted dormitories built directly into the limestone walls at the basin's edge. These rooms are cooled by running the cold, high-altitude night air through dry-pipes buried in the masonry, bringing the indoor temperature down to a merciful seventy degrees. 

Inside, the silence is finally broken, though never loudly. They take off their slit-goggles, revealing eyes that are unusually large and pale, adapted over generations to the permanent twilight behind the horn plates. They sit at long zinc-topped tables, eating flat-crab meat boiled in clean condenser-water, seasoned with the very mineral salts their machines refine. They drink dark, bitter tea imported from the lowlands, speaking in their low, dry murmurs, while the building vibrates gently around them—a continuous, sub-audible *thrummm* coming up through the floorboards from the great, greased flywheels turning out in the dark.

Later, one of the night-watch will step back out onto the porch to check the main manifold. He won't take a lantern; a flame just turns the night fog into a blinding white wall. Instead, he wears a pair of night-frames with wide, clear glass lenses to keep the sulfur mist out of his eyes, and navigates by the glow of the basin itself. 

In the dark, the bacterial mats have a faint, sour bioluminescence, outlining every terrace and overflow lip in bruised shades of green and orange. The great iron towers stand against the stars like the ribs of some colossal, half-buried beast. All across the glowing shelves, the waders sleep standing on one leg in the warm water, and the pale crabs shift in silent, phosphorescent tides. The watchman bites down on his wooden toggle, presses it to the main riser pipe, and closes his eyes. Through his teeth, he feels the whole three-mile bowl breathing: the slow, rhythmic *thump-slide* of the counter-weights half a mile north, the heavy, oily gurgle of the deep vents, and, faint as a spider crossing a web, the *tink-tink* of another watchman’s wrench at the far southern rim, signaling that the pressure is holding, the pans are full, and the night is clear.
