She is usually in the third puddle from the north wall, though it takes a minute for the eye to separate her from the obsidian gravel and the shifting caustics of the air-lenses. 

Her people do not have a word for the pins, just as an apple does not have a word for the branch, though in her private thoughts she refers to them as the *un-stones*, because they are the only things in the world that do not bleed, flake, or hold a shadow properly. She is the only one here. If there are other pockets notched into the high, freezing shoulders of the range, the mile of bare, roaring grey scree between them might as well be the vacuum between stars. 

Her name, if it were spoken, would sound like the soft, wet suction of a foot pulling free from warm silt, though she hasn't had reason to make the sound since the older one died five winters ago and was laid out at the base of the southern wall to be digested by the magenta bladder-weeds.

At first glance, she looks like an amphibious evolution of a human woman, or perhaps a very large, pale salamander that has decided to try out knees and elbows. She is small—barely four feet if she were to stand fully upright, which she rarely does because the stone is too sharp and the air is too heavy to waste effort fighting gravity when the water will hold half your weight for you. Her skin is entirely hairless, the color of smoked tallow, and slightly translucent over the ribcage and the inner thighs, showing the slow, indigo branching of her veins. Because the air pressure in the basin is twice what it ought to be, her lungs are small—just two neat, high bellows under her collarbones—and her chest barely moves when she breathes. Instead, she drinks oxygen through the damp, velvety skin of her back and shoulders, which are permanently slicked with a mixture of the basin’s mineral sweat and her own protective mucilage.

You have to look at her hands and feet to understand how long her kind has been trapped in the benevolence of the pins. Her fingers are long, flattened like wooden spatulas, and lack nails entirely. Between them stretches a thick, milky webbing. When she moves across the dry, razor-flaked basalt between the pools, she does not walk so much as unroll herself, laying down the broad, grey pads of her feet and heels—which are four inches thick with a dense, self-healing cartilage that accepts obsidian splinters the way a pincushion accepts needles, working them out days later in little pearls of grey jelly.

She is currently lying on her belly in three inches of hot, syrupy water, her chin propped on the meat of her forearms, watching the glass-orchid walker hunt. 

She is not hunting it; there isn't enough meat on the thing to justify chewing through its silica-stiffened joints. She is waiting for it to finish its meal. The walker’s proboscis is currently sunk into one of the thumb-sized, rainbow-slicked slugs. The slug is dissolving from the inside out, turning into a rich, pre-digested broth bolstered by the walker’s own enzymes. Just as the insect’s abdomen begins to swell with the pale gold fluid, she reaches out—not fast, because nothing in the thick air moves fast, but with an inevitable, hydraulic steadiness—and pinches the walker’s mid-leg between two flat fingers.

The walker does not thrash; it knows the rules here. It simply opens its mandibles, regurgitates half the golden broth onto the tough skin of the puddle—where it sits like a poached egg yolk without dispersing—and waits until she lets go. She dips her chin and slurps the warm, sour drop off the surface of the water without getting her nose wet.

She is a gardener, though her tools are only her thumbs, her teeth, and the heavy, cooperative physics of the water. 

After her small meal, she rises into a low squat and shuffles over to the central jade rosette. The plant is engorged, its four-inch-thick leaves trembling slightly with the internal pressure of the sap. If left alone, the leaves will eventually split along their margins, and the black rot from the high mountain air—the one thing the pins cannot entirely keep out—will get into the wound. 

She reaches into a fold of skin at her hip—a shallow, natural marsupial pocket that has no reproductive purpose anymore, used only as a tool-bag—and pulls out a five-inch flake of black glass. She has wrapped the gripping end in the dried, leathery skin of an old bladder-stalk, glued together with sap. With the delicacy of a surgeon, she scores a tiny chevron into the underside of the lowest jade leaf.

Because of the pins, the sap does not spurt. It extrudes in a thick, emerald-tinted rope, heavy as warm honey, curling down into her waiting palm. She lets about a pint of it spool into the cupped webbing between her hands before she presses a pinch of dry volcanic ash into the cut to seal it. She carries the wobbling, coherent globe of sap across the courtyard to the dry fissure where the magenta stalks grow, and dumps it over their exposed root-knuckles. They cannot draw the raw mineral sweat of the walls on their own; they need the jade plant to process the sulfur out of it first. And the jade plant, in turn, needs her to bleed it, and to scrape the iridescent biofilms away from its base before the bacterial mat suffocates its crown.

The three of them—the woman, the jade, the magenta stalks—are a closed circuit of fluids, presided over by the five white cylinders that do not care if the basin is full of gardens or boiled bones.

She spends the middle of the day tending to the water itself. This is her strangest habit, the one that marks her as something more than an animal grazing in a warm trap. 

Near the center pin, where the surface tension is thickest, she sits cross-legged on the wet gravel and begins to *build* with the puddle. Using the flat edges of her hands, she scoops and folds the water over itself, trapping pockets of the heavy, lensing vapor underneath. Because the water refuses to flatten quickly and the vapor refuses to diffuse, she can stack them. She pinches a dome of water, rolls a clear, jelly-like blob of hot mist into its hollow, and seals the bottom with a slap of her wrist. 

Within twenty minutes, she has constructed a transient, trembling cairn of liquid and dense air, eighteen inches high, sitting right beside the matte-white cylinder of the pin. 

She doesn't do this for food. She does it for the light. As the relentless high-altitude sun pours through her stacked lenses of water and air, the cairn splits the white glare into long, fat ribbons of violet, sulfur-yellow, and deep crimson, throwing them across the dead-white surface of the pin. It is the only way to make the un-stones hold color. She sits with her knees pulled up to her chin, her throat pulsing in synchronization with the little metallic-blue lizards up on the rim, watching the slow, syrupy collapse of her sculpture as the domes gradually give in to gravity—*bloop, bloop, bloop*—and the spectrum slides down the white pole into the black mud.

In the late afternoon, when the mountain shadow finally slices across the basin and the light turns the color of a bruise, she climbs. 

The rock is razor-sharp, but she knows every hold the way a blind man knows the furniture of his bedroom; they have not weathered a millimeter since she was born. She hauls her dense, slick body up the eastern chimney until she is perched on a ledge just below the boundary.

Here, she can feel the edge of the world. 

She sits on the warm, slimy basalt and raises one flat, grey hand above her head. Two inches above her brow, her fingertips pass through the roof of the pin's influence. 

Instantly, the mucilage on her skin freezes into a grey crust. The air up there is thin, howling, and tastes of iron and nothingness. The wind—which is completely invisible and silent down where her lungs are—tries to tear her fingers backward, roaring across her knuckles with the force of a river in spate, though she cannot hear it; she only feels the violent, alien buffeting in the tendons of her wrist.

She leaves her hand there for a three-count, letting the cold bite deep into the meat of her palm until the pain is bright and clear, a sharp silver line cutting through the thick, warm, soup-like stupor of her ninety-degree life. 

When she pulls her hand back down into the kettle, the rime ice on her fingers melts in a single second, dripping warm onto her knee. A stunned, grey-winged mountain moth, caught in the downdraft of her retreating arm, tumbles through the invisible membrane, its wings still locked with frost. Before the little blue lizard two feet away can flick out its tongue, she catches the insect on the back of her still-chilled hand, brings it to her mouth, and crunches it down—cold, dry, and tasting of the sky.
