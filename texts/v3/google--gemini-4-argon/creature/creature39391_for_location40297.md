To find the things that actually *live* here—not merely function as its valves and gaskets, like the vent-crabs and the gear-frogs, who have no more interiority than a carburetor—you have to stop looking at the surface and start listening to the knocks in the plumbing.

The surface is too loud and too cold for anything that thinks. Instead, fifty feet down, suspended in the middle layer of the three-hundred-foot mat, is the Gasket-Work. Here the roots are the thickness of ship hulls, slick with black tallow, and the air trapped between them is a pressurized, ninety-degree sauna of sulfur, bruised sedge, and copper-smelling steam. 

This is the territory of the **Gage-Men**, though they are neither men nor entirely separate from the basin itself. 

If you were to drag one up into the pewter twilight, you would think at first you were looking at a drowned deep-sea diver whose suit had been stitched together from vulcanized rubber, giant tarnished brass washers, and the shed carapaces of the manhole-crabs. When it moved, you would realize that the rubber was its hide, the brass was a calcified mineral crust secreted by its own pores, and the crab-shell was fused directly into its collarbones. 

A Gage-Man is roughly the bulk of a draft horse, though built low to the turf on six radial, multi-jointed limbs that can lock like tripod struts when the ground heaves. It has no head in the mammalian sense. Where a neck should be, there is a thick, muscular cowl of slick grey gristle housing three horizontal slits lined with stiff, brassy cilia—sensors that read barometric pressure, thermal gradients, and the crackling ozone buildup of an impending spatial skip. Beneath this cowl hangs a heavy, prehensile proboscis, ribbed like an elephant’s trunk and tipped with a ring of horny, shearing plates used for splicing into high-pressure sap mains.

They are amphibians that decided, some four million years ago, that metamorphosis was a bad trade, and instead grew heavy, callus-plated, and bureaucratic. 

A Gage-Man does not hunt, and it does not graze. It *balances*. 

If you watch one moving through the surface mire—which they do only when an upper-level main has ruptured or when a spatial stretch has tied a knot in the local root-grid—it moves with the heavy, preoccupied gait of a night-watchman making rounds in a boiler room. Every few yards, it stops, drives two of its rear struts four feet deep into the soaking moss to anchor itself against the wind, and presses its flat, drum-skinned belly against the turf. 

It is listening to the knocks. 

Beneath it, three hundred feet of hydraulic vegetation is constantly threatening to tear itself apart. If a bellows-root over-pressurizes, it will blow out a quarter-mile of turf, exposing the deep tubers to the scythe-wind; the tubers will freeze, the freeze will spread like gangrene, and within a week a five-mile dead-spot of black ice will form, threatening the entire basin’s thermal grid. Conversely, if the piezoelectric charge between two rubbing root-mats builds too high without bleeding off, the resulting spatial skip won’t just double-expose a ridge—it will shear the vascular system cleanly in half, leaving thousand-gallon arteries of boiling sap pumping into nowhere, bleeding the valley white into an adjacent Tuesday.

The Gage-Man’s belly-drum reads the rhythm of the deep *thump-thump-thump*. When it hears a knock—a valve-crab stuck sideways in a intake sphincter, or an embolus of congealed black resin threatening to burst a piston-tuber—it uncoils its heavy, bone-tipped proboscis and drives it into the mat like a pneumatic drill.

What follows looks less like predation and more like surgery performed by a very large, very patient crab. With its front limbs—which end not in claws, but in long, flat, greasy spatulas—it prizes apart the tangled sedges, reaches down into the scalding dark, and physically re-routes the plumbing. It will haul up a forty-pound lump of solidifying tallow-clot, chew it with slow, rhythmic grinding of its internal gizzard-plates until it is liquid again, and spit it back into a low-pressure vein. It will pluck a misfiring vent-crab from a blown gasket, turn it over in its spatula-hands to inspect the creature's grease-seals, and either toss it aside to freeze or wedge it back into a different hole with a heavy, wet *thwack* of its flat forearm.

For a long time, any observer would assume the Gage-Men are just another automatic mechanism of the valley, no different from the frogs. 

Then you see two of them meet on a vibrating ridge, and they begin to argue.

They do not use sound; the basin is too loud for ears. They communicate by tapping into the root-mat between them and sending hydraulic hammer-blows through the sap-lines—*water-hammering* the turf in complex, syncopated rhythms that can be felt through the soles of your boots fifty yards away. 

*Knock-knock-slap. Thump. Knock-knock.*

They argue about pressure. This is the only thing they have to think about, and over geologic time, hydraulic pressure has become their language, their philosophy, and their art. A young Gage-Man (only the size of a hogshead barrel, its hide still pale and un-vulcanized) wants to bleed a swollen ridge immediately by lancing it; an older one, its back encrusted with six inches of yellow sulfur-scale and its third left leg missing from a spatial shear-event forty years ago, insists on letting the pressure build until the ridge skips. 

Why? Because when the terrain skips—when the local physics fray and the ridge exists in two places at once for three seconds—something happens to the Gage-Men who stand inside the anomaly.

This is the secret of the basin, the thing that elevates the Gage-Men from biological machinery into *people*. 

They are addicts of the skip.

When a Gage-Man calculates that a section of the mat is about to suffers a spatial exhaustion, it does not flee like the frogs didn't, nor does it ignore it. It lumbers into the epicenter of the rising, groaning hill of lichen. It locks all six legs into the shuddering turf. It opens the brassy slits on its cowl wide, exposing the raw, violet-colored olfactory membranes inside to the freezing, ozone-stinking wind. 

And when the whip cracks—when the ridge is both *here* and *twenty feet to the left*, when the river unhooks from gravity and hangs in the air like a ribbon of black glass—the Gage-Man is briefly multiplied. 

For three seconds, or five, or ten, its nervous system—which is built of copper-rich, superconductive nerve-cord designed to survive the valley's piezoelectric storms—bridges the gap between the two overlapping spaces. In that stutter of reality, the Gage-Man remembers every other skip it has ever stood inside. Because time and distance in the basin are elastic when the roots rub hot enough, an old Gage-Man standing in a skip is simultaneously touching the skip it stood in three weeks ago, and the one four hundred years ago, and the one that will happen a mile to the east tomorrow afternoon.

They do not build monuments because the ground would eat them. They build their monuments inside the skips. 

Over thousands of generations, the Gage-Men have deliberately tuned the root-engine of the forty-mile basin—pinching a tube here, letting a sump boil over there, herding the manhole-crabs in great rattling circles—not merely to keep the valley from freezing, but to orchestrate the anomalies. They play the forty-mile mattress like a vast, wet pipe organ, building the heat and the friction toward specific, harmonic failures of physics.

Inside that three-second double-exposure, a dozen Gage-Men spaced miles apart across the heaving plain will all step into synchronized skips at the exact same moment. In the stutter, their minds overlap. They deposit memories into the electrical charge of the anomaly—the taste of a particularly rich vein of deep-iron sap from three centuries back; the exact barometric texture of a winter when the sky-lid dropped so low the clouds dragged through the sedge; the schematic of a root-junction six miles wide that took eighty years to balance. 

When the whip cracks and the ridge slams flat again, dropping tons of freezing black spray back onto the turf, the Gage-Men unlock their struts. They shake the ice from their rubbery cowls. They are alone again in the deafening, chugging twilight, miles apart, ankle-deep in oily sludge. 

They do not look at each other. They have no eyes to look with. Slowly, methodically, they turn back to the vents, uncoil their drills, and begin setting the valves for the next chord.
