At first, you would think it is just the mineral scale. 

Along the waterline of the tilted polygons, where the six-hour tide leaves its chalky white tracing, some of the deposits are thicker than others. They look like blistered stalagmites or weeping sores of calcium and silica, roughly the size and shape of an upturned boot. If you watched one for an hour, you wouldn’t see it move. If you watched it for four hours, you would realize it is two inches higher up the basalt slab than it was before the water rose, and that the chalky ring beneath it has been licked clean away.

There are perhaps forty of them in the basin. They have no eyes, no ears to be ruined by the turbine’s shriek, and no lungs to drown in the hundred-degree steam. To the eye, they are simply asymmetrical domes of rough, porcelaneous crust—a layered patchwork of white sulfate, rust-red iron oxide, and the dull, industrial green of paint chips scavenged from the water and cemented into their shells with secreted mucus. 

Beneath that heavy, rock-hard mantle is a foot of dense, pale-gray muscle, greasy to the touch and tough as vulcanized rubber. 

Before the collapse, they were smaller things—blind, thread-pale gastropods the size of a thumbnail, living two hundred feet down in the dark, crushed into the hairline fractures of the steam vault where they grazed on sparse filaments of sulfur-eating bacteria. They lived slow, starved lives under immense pressure. When the roof gave way and the sky fell in, bringing sunlight and the turbine, their world exploded into sudden, violent abundance. 

The sunlight woke the orange and yellow algae. The turbine brought the vibration. Between the two, the snails grew monstrous.

They are creatures of the hum now. The deep, sub-audible thrumming that shakes the basalt slabs is the heartbeat of their world. It passes right through their heavy shells and into their dense flesh, agitating their sluggish yellow blood, acting as an external metabolism. They don't need to waste energy circulating their own fluids; the iron box in the north corner does it for them, shaking their cells awake with a relentless, sixty-cycle-per-second massage. If you were to pluck one from the rock and carry it up the forty-foot cliff into the quiet grass, it would go cold and torpid within minutes, suffocating not from the air, but from the silence.

They move by riding the acoustic standing waves. A snail will slightly lift the center of its muscular foot, trapping a thin cushion of hot, oily water beneath itself, and let the micro-tremors in the stone vibrate it forward, sliding over the razor-sharp basalt edges without ever being cut. The heavy grease they leave behind—which smells remarkably like the petroleum lubricant sweating from the turbine's seams—coats the sharp corners of the rock, slowly polishing the fresh fractures dark and smooth.

When they feed, you can’t hear it over the scream of the housing, but if you laid your bare palm against the same slab of rock, you would feel a tiny, rhythmic *tick-tick-tick* interfering with the turbine's drone. Their radulae—the ribbon-like tongues beneath their shells—are plated with magnetite, literally teeth of black iron precipitated from the water. They scrape the thermophilic algae and the mineral scale right off the stone, grinding so hard they leave microscopic, parallel grooves in the volcanic glass.

As the afternoon wears on and the sun angles away from the pit, they begin their slow convergence. 

Drawn by the heat and the sheer density of the vibration, a dozen of the largest—some now the size of fire hydrants, their shells terraced like miniature volcanic cones—glide up out of the triangular pool in the north corner. They haul their heavy, dripping bulk directly onto the submerged flanks of the cast-iron turbine housing. 

They love the housing. To them, it is the sun and the earth combined, a colossal, screaming mother-rock that never stops trembling and weeps delicious, rust-heavy condensation. They plaster themselves over its blistered green flanks, clustering thickest around the inspection hatches and the intake seams where the whine is sharpest. Their iron teeth grate against the cast iron, stripping away the peeling paint, grazing on the thick mats of rust, and leaving shiny, silvery trails of bare metal that immediately begin to oxidize again in the wet air. 

One or two have even found the braided steel cables. Twenty feet up the sheer black wall, clinging to the humming wire like monstrous, crusted beads on a string, they inch toward the rim, scraping the zinc galvanization from the steel, turning the human machinery into shell, one microscopic rasp at a time. 

Down below, the gray electrical cabinet blinks its steady amber light. Around its stilts, just above the high-water mark, there is a ring of tiny, translucent capsules cemented into the bolted steel—eggs, the size of marbles, arranged in neat, geometric spirals. Inside each one, a pinprick of white shell is already spinning slowly in its clear fluid, keeping time with the shriek of the iron box.
