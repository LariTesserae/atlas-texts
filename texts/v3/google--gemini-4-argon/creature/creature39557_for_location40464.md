To find a Sump-Drawer, you do not look at the terraces; you look at the empty stone between them, or the undersides of the riveted trusses where the violet shadow is thickest. 

At first, they look like heavy canvas tool-bags left hanging from a Piton-spike, or perhaps a large, dormant Clinker that has grown an unusually angular shell of salvaged copper and greased felt. Then a counter-weight shifts, a brass cam clicks against a vanadium rail, and the bundle unspools itself downward, dropping sixty feet on a braided wire tether before catching the edge of a gantry with a single, long-toed foot.

They are human, or were once, though the horizontal world has been bred and levered out of them over a thousand generations of hanging by their heels. A Sump-Drawer on a flat floor is an awkward, distressed thing—their spines have lost the sharp lumbar curve required to balance a torso over a pelvis, straightening instead into a flexible, shock-absorbing rod meant for suspension. Their legs are not pistons for marching; they are calipers. The hips are wide-set and double-jointed, the calves bowed outward so the soles of the feet naturally face one another, allowing them to clamp onto a three-foot vanadium rail or a smooth stone rib with the effortless, passive friction of a wrench on a pipe. 

Because water is an industrial rumour locked away in the municipal copper vats, the Sump-Drawers do not sweat. To sweat in the dry updrafts of the Spindle Range would be to shrivel into leather before noon. Instead, they are creatures of oil and dry thermal regulation. Their skin is thick, matte, and grey-gold, largely hairless save for dense, coarse tufts inside the ears and nostrils that act as primary filters against the silica flour. To keep the fine, alkaline dust from scoring their epidermis, they coat themselves daily in *tallow-varnish*—a heavy, amber grease rendered from the lipid-rich tissues of harvested Vent-Limpets and mixed with fine graphite. It gives them the sheen of old, well-maintained machinery and a faint, warm smell of sulfur and hot brass. 

To shed heat, they rely on their clothing, which is half-garment, half-radiator. A Drawer’s rig is a complex carapace of pleated linen and thin, beaten copper fins that project from the shoulders and spine, designed to catch the constant vertical wind and wick warmth away from the blood vessels running close to the skin of the neck and back.

Everything about their lives is governed by the *Tare*—the strict accounting of mass and moisture. 

They are a small, dense-boned people, rarely exceeding five feet in height, yet surprisingly heavy for their size due to the high mineral content of their bodies. Generations of breathing trace metallic dust and drinking thrice-reclaimed condensate have given them teeth the color of tarnished pewter and fingernails so thick with keratin and iron-salts that they can be filed into flathead screwdrivers or stone-gouges. When a Sump-Drawer dies, they are not buried—there is nothing to dig—nor are they dropped into the amber abyss, which would be a catastrophic treason against the city’s closed loop. They are taken to the *Still-Houses*, where their seventy percent water is carefully baked out in solar retorts and returned to the municipal pipes, while their mineralized bones are ground down to flux the electro-smelters, and their tallow goes to grease the great bearings of the wind-screws. A citizen’s worth is measured by the purity of the water they leave behind and the richness of their ash.

Because the wind-hiss swallows high frequencies and the thirty-mile gulf of the canyon turns shouts into thin, useless ghosts, the Sump-Drawers rarely use their voices in the open air. Speaking requires opening the mouth, which dries the tongue and invites the silica dust into the lungs. Behind their copper-mesh respirators and yellow-tinted mica goggles, they are silent. 

Instead, they speak through the *Iron*. 

Every Drawer carries a *tine*—a short rod of tuned vanadium steel strapped to the wrist or built into the heel of their climbing boots. By striking a track-rail, a structural truss, or a high-tension cable, they send crisp, rhythmic vibrations singing through the bone-dry metal of the district. A child learns to read the hum of the wall through the soles of their feet before they learn to speak aloud. Sitting in a bracket-tower with one’s shoulder pressed against a main rivet-plate, a Drawer can feel the gossip of four vertical miles: the sharp, staccato *ting-ting-tock* of a Sifter-crew calling for more flywheel tension three thousand feet below; the slow, heavy *thrum* of a marriage contract being hammered out on an anvil-plate in the upper spires; the frantic, high-pitched trill of a warning when a forty-foot Kite gets its trailing-lines fouled in a funicular cable.

Inside the towers, life is arranged like the interior of a ship’s mast. There are no broad rooms, because horizontal floor-space requires heavy cantilevering; instead, there are *shaft-homes*—narrow cylinders six feet wide and eighty feet tall, shared by an entire vertical clan. There are no beds. To sleep flat feels to a Sump-Drawer like falling backward into the void; it induces a terrifying vertigo they call *the Unhooking*. They sleep upright or tilted at forty-five degrees, zipped into triangular canvas hammocks bolted to the interior masonry, stacked one above the other like chrysalises on a stem, gently swaying in the low, sub-audible vibration of the building’s trusses.

Their relationship with the cliff’s ecology is one of patient, slow-motion husbandry. They do not hunt; chasing anything across a vertical face is a fool's game that ends in a nine-mile plummet. They *farm the static*.

A family will lease a hundred yards of sun-baked cliff-face or the warm underside of a smelter flue. Using long, counter-balanced poles, they paint the stone and iron with grease-lines and weak acid washes, creating conductive pathways that attract the airborne mineral-dust. This artificial crust draws the Vent-Limpets and the Copper-Backs. The Drawers tend these herds like slow, metallic gardens. They know the lineage of a prize Copper-Back that has been grazing the same thirty feet of girder for forty years, periodically prying it loose with a brass bar to scrape the pure, electro-plated nodules of copper and zinc from the margins of its shell before gluing it back to the iron with resin so it can continue to grow.

Then there are the *Kite-Wranglers*, the closest thing the Sump-Drawers have to an aristocracy of daredevils. They work at the very edges of the translucent stone overhangs, where the thermals scream upward from the amber floor. Dressed in wing-suits of oiled Kite-leather that allow them to glide back to the wall if a tether snaps, they cast weighted lines out into the open air to snag the great, diamond-shaped Kites as they drift past. They do not kill the Kites—a big Kite takes fifty years to reach full span. Instead, they winch them in close to the gantries, lash their pneumatic wing-bones to the rails, and harvest their sticky trailing-lines, which are thick with the rich, fatty paste of ten thousand trapped mineral-moths. They comb the parasites from the Kite’s dry leather, patch tears in its membrane with vulcanized gum, and release it back into the thermal before the great creature’s dry lungs can suffocate for lack of airflow.

Time, for the Sump-Drawers, is not divided into days and nights, which are merely lighting changes, nor into seasons, which do not exist. It is divided into the *Up-Breath* and the *Down-Breath*, and the *Slack* between them.

During the Up-Breath—the long, blazing day when the abyss exhales its warm, dust-heavy air toward the plateau—the cities are engines of quiet, methodical strain. You see the Drawers moving across the pale travertine in long, diagonal chains, roped together with gleaming wire, scraping the electrostatic plates of the Sifters, funneling the pale waste-powder into the chutes to feed the permanent dust-rainbows below. They move with the same three-rpm cadence as the great canvas wind-screws—never jerking, never rushing, every movement balanced by an equal and opposite counterweight. To hurry is to sweat; to sweat is to die.

When the sun drops below the opposing canyon wall thirty miles away, and the violet shadows turn to plum, the *Slack* arrives. 

For ten minutes, the wind dies completely. The low, velvet *shhhhh* that has been pressed against their eardrums since birth suddenly vanishes. In the sudden, terrifying vacuum of sound, the entire vertical city freezes. Every Drawer hooks their safety-spur into the nearest eye-bolt. No one strikes the iron. The silence is so immense it feels like a physical pressure pushing them off the wall. In the Slack, you can hear the dry, hollow ticking of the Spire-Crabs’ claws on the corrugated roofs half a mile away, and the long, sighing groan of the mountain itself as the travertine cools by ten degrees. 

It is during these ten minutes of dead air that the Sump-Drawers unclip their respirators, lean their foreheads against the warm vanadium rails, and speak to whoever is hanging next to them in low, raspy whispers, sharing a sip of tepid, copper-tasting water from a brass nipple-flask. 

Then, a cold ribbon of air slides over the lip of the plateau above. A single canvas vane on a wind-screw shivers. The great bone-wood bearings let out a single, tectonic creak as they take the weight of the falling air, and the screws begin to turn backward. 

The sodium-orange lamps flicker on, turning the grease on the Drawers’ skin to the color of old amber. The Down-Breath has begun. The Kites fold themselves like dark laundry along the suspension cables; the Sifters switch their polarity to catch the fine, cold salt-grit sliding down from the high flats; and the Sump-Drawers unhook their spurs, kick off from the stone, and ride the counter-balanced wires down into the orange-lit dark of the lower terraces, dropping through the quiet air like spiders on threads of dark steel.
