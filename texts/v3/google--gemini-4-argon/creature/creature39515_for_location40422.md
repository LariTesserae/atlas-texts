They are not seen until one stops watching the hares and starts watching the machine’s shadow.

At first, they look like heavy, oil-soaked rags that have fallen from a mechanic's pocket and gotten wedged into the hot, vibrating crevices between the low-pressure cylinder and the condenser housing. Then a brass drip-cup runs dry, and one of the rags uncurls, reaches up with a hand that has two thumbs and no fingernails, and turns the knurled adjusting screw by a quarter-inch.

The syndicate records, if any still existed in a dry cellar five hundred miles south, would show that the camp never employed locals—there were no locals on the grey plain—and that the seven men who assembled the pump were all accounted for on the last mule-train out. The things living in the Kettle did not come from the syndicate, though they wear the syndicate’s cast-off shape the way a hermit crab wears a whelk shell, imperfectly and with modifications made for a cramped space.

There are perhaps eleven of them. It is hard to count them because they rarely leave the five-foot radius of the engine’s cast-iron skirt, and they move only on the *CLANK*. 

They have bred or whittled themselves down to the size of four-year-old children, though their proportions are entirely different: broad, flat-ribbed torsos that can slide between a moving eccentric rod and the bedplate with half an inch to spare, and thick, bowed legs with ankles as wide as their calves. Their skin has the color and texture of vulcanized rubber—a slick, poreless slate-grey that beads the lukewarm condensation right off their shoulders. They have no hair at all, not an eyelash or a brow, because hair catches in thirty-tooth governor gears. Instead, their scalps and forearms are calloused into smooth, horn-colored plates from decades of leaning against hot lagging and vibrating cast iron to sleep.

To see them move is to watch an extension of the valve gear. Because the forty-cycle rhythm of the pump is the only physical law in the Kettle, their nervous systems have been entirely colonized by it. They do not walk smoothly; they advance in three-second increments. *CHUG*—a crouch and a shift of weight. *Hiss*—the reach of an arm through the blurred spokes of the flywheel, timed to the millisecond when the counterbalance weight is at the bottom of its arc. *CLANK*—the strike of a flat, greasy palm against a sticking rocker pin, driving it home on the exact beat of the exhaust stroke so the impact disappears into the larger thunder of the machine. 

Between the beats, they are as still as foundry pig-iron. A brown hare will sit directly on the splayed, leathery foot of one of these mechanics, grooming its ears while the creature stands frozen with a ball-peen hammer raised six inches above a flange bolt, waiting for the *CLANK* to drop the head.

They are completely deaf, of course, or rather, their ears have closed over into smooth, dimpled scars of gristle behind their jawbones. They do not need to hear the air. They read the Kettle through the soles of their feet and the palms of their hands. By resting two fingers against the main bearing cap, one of them can feel a swallow strike the turf wall a hundred feet away, or tell which of the six underground root-siphons is beginning to choke with grey silt, or know that the big female hare under the firebox has just dropped a litter of four.

Every machine left to run for ninety years should have seized eighty-nine years ago, no matter how steady the gas or how tight the roots. Brass bearings wear into ovals; piston rings score their cylinders; packing rots out of stuffing boxes; oil turns to varnish and then to ash. The syndicate engineers believed the pump was abandoned. In truth, it was merely handed over to a crew that did not require wages, light, or silence.

Their life is the lubrication cycle. 

The sedge-willow mat does not just hold the iron; where its roots press against the hot cast-iron of the marsh-gas firebox, the woody cambium sweats a thick, amber resin to protect itself from scorching. The little mechanics harvest this gum with scrapers made from flattened swallow-bones. They boil it down in the concave hollows on top of the high-pressure cylinder head, mixing it with the rendered tallow of hares that have died of old age and the crushed, oily carapaces of ten thousand grey-backed beetles. The result is a dark, smelling tallow- pitch that never quite hardens in the fifty-degree air. 

All day under the pale, shadowless sky, and all night under the small wheeling stars, two or three of the grey figures are climbing the black iron like slow, deliberate tree-frogs. They carry wads of chewed sedge-fiber soaked in the beetle-grease. When the crosshead slides back—*CHUG*—a hand darts in, wipes the steel guide-bar clean of grit, and smears a fresh film of amber fat across the metal before the three-hundred-pound shoe comes slamming back down on the *CLANK*. When the stuffing-box begins to weep too much steam, they braided new packing from willow-root bast and the long, shed primary feathers of the swifts, driving it into the gland with wooden drifts while the piston rod flashes past their noses forty times a minute.

They have no language of mouth or hand. When two of them meet on the narrow catwalk of the condenser, they press their broad, flat foreheads together against the iron casing between them. For three, six, nine cycles of the pump, they stand skull-to-skull, letting the forty-hertz tremor of the engine pass through one cranium and into the other, reading the slight changes in how the other’s bone-mass dampens the vibration—a shared diagnostic of fatigue, hunger, or a loose cotter-pin on the eccentric strap.

When one of them grows too slow—when a knee stiffens by a tenth of a second and the flywheel rim takes an arm, or when their inner clock simply winds down after sixty years of three-second days—they do not rot on the turf any more than the hares do. The remaining ten carry the body up to the only place in the Kettle that is hotter than the boiler: the narrow, brick-lined throat beneath the grate where the raw marsh gas hisses out of the ancient clay and burns in a flat, blue-yellow sheet. What does not go up the stack in the three-second plumes of white weather settles into the ash-pit as grey lime and bone-char. The others rake it out, mix it with the acidic water weeping from the sump, and use the paste to polish the brass pressure gauges whose needles rusted against their stops before any of them were born.

They cannot see the numbers behind the green-streaked glass, and they have no idea what "pounds per square inch" might mean. They only know that the brass must be bright, the iron must be slick, the forty-beat measure must never falter into thirty-nine, and the white breath must go up into the flat sky every three seconds, forever, or the sky will fall in and the grey plain will come back to finish grinding the world to nothing.
