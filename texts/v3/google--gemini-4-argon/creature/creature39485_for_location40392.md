At first, you think he is merely an irregular lagging on the main return pipe, a bundle of tarred felt and canvas tied with twine that has come loose and slumped against the warm brickwork of the central condensing tower. Only when a wharf rat runs across his shin, and his hand comes up—not to strike it, but to absently scratch it behind the ear as it passes—do you realize the grease-stiffened canvas is a coat, and the twine is a belt.

The men on the day-shift inside the turbine house call him the Gauge-Man, or sometimes the Deacon, though most of them have only seen the crown of his hat through the wire-glass of the high transom windows. The municipality’s ledger lists a *Sluice-Tender & Sump-Watch (Grade IV)*, and every second Friday a brass token and a small brown envelope of coin are pushed through a slot in the iron door at the top of the eastern service stair. Someone always takes the envelope; by Monday morning, a bundle of tallow-soaked waste rags, four loaves of heavy rye bread, and a tin of lamp-carbons have been left in the iron basket at the top of the steps, and by Monday noon, the basket is empty again. 

He has been down in the soup for eleven years, or perhaps thirteen—ever since the city council bricked up the main haulage incline after the quarry was finished and the condensers were lit. Before the basin, he was a bilge-scraper on the coal-barges, or a bone-boiler’s second at the rendering works next door; whatever his name was on the street, the warm, roaring wet has steamed it off him like the label off a tin can.

He is not tall, and he walks with the flat-footed, wide-striding gait of a man who has forgotten what dry paving feels like. He wears no boots. Leather rots in a month down here, and wood-soled clogs slip on the oily basalt; instead, the soles of his feet have cured in the warm tallow-water and coal-grit until they are as thick and horn-black as an anvil’s heel. He can walk across a heap of freshly blasted traprock without looking down, his toes gripping the knife-sharp edges of the stone with slow, prehensile certainty. 

The ninety-eight-degree air has boiled every ounce of spare tallow out of his own frame while coating his outside in the tallow of the rendering vats. He is spare and stringy as dried salt-beef, his skin the color of old ivory that has been rubbed with machine-oil and soot. The pores of his forearms and neck are permanently stippled with fine coal-dust, which cannot be washed out, giving him a bruised, metallic cast under the violet-white buzz of the arc lamps. Because the sweat never dries on him, and the airborne grease never stops settling, he gleams faintly when he moves through the cones of light, like a seal or a well-oiled piston rod.

He does not wear a shirt—cloth against the ribs only breeds the red rot—just a pair of heavy, oil-tanned horsehide breeches cut off at the calf and held up by copper wire, and over his bare shoulders, a short cape he stitched together from vulcanized rubber belting scavenged from the turbine house trash-chute. The rubber sheds the hot, endless *clack-drip* from the catwalks above, so that as he moves, he makes his own small, duller percussion against the surrounding iron: *thup-thup, thup-thup.*

His work takes him twenty minutes out of every six hours, though without a clock or a sun, he measures the intervals by the slow thickening of the grease-scum around the central floor-drain and the slight, rhythmic drop in pitch of the number-three flue when the tallow-works blows down its boilers. When he feels that shift in the soles of his feet, he rises from his corner behind the manifold, takes up a six-foot iron spanner that is polished like silver at the grip and black with mutton-fat everywhere else, and walks out into the ankle-deep mire.

He does not hurry. Nobody hurries in ninety-eight degrees. He wades through the warm, rainbowed pools, and the dark, plated catfish do not scatter; they merely part around his ankles like heavy silk curtains, their barbels brushing his Achilles tendons. He leans his weight against the great cast-iron wheel of the primary sluice, giving it three quarters of a turn to the left until the vibration in the stem changes from a thrash to a purr, letting the belly of the sump clear itself into the municipal main. Then he walks to the eastern grate, hooks a long-handled iron rake from the brickwork, and draws three or four dead eels and a mat of green algae out of the bars, tossing them onto a flat shelf of basalt where the rats and the larger toads are already waiting in a polite, unblinking half-circle.

That is the whole of his trade. The rest of his life belongs to the basin.

Because there is no night, he does not sleep in a long trough the way men do up in the dry streets. He sleeps like a cat or a boiler-watch, in twenty-minute spells, sitting upright on a wooden grease-firkin wedged into the dry, hundred-degree pocket between the number-two condensing tower and the main return flange. His head tips back against the warm brick; his mouth falls slightly open; his throat pulses in the slow, heavy tempo of the toads lined up on the iron bolster beside his boots. When an arc lamp sputters—the carbon rods dropping a fraction of an inch in their brass jaws with a sharp *tink* and a flare of purple light—his eyes open, film-gray and unstartled, watch a starling dart through the steam-plume to take a beetle, and close again.

He has forgotten how to speak aloud. There is no use for a voice against the eighty-decibel wall of the turbines and the hog-head flues; a shout here is swallowed three inches from the teeth. Instead, his ears have grown extraordinarily acute to the small, sharp noises that live *inside* the roar. He can hear the dry rasp of a dying bearing in an exhaust fan forty feet up; he can hear the wet *plop* of a heavy toad hitting the mud thirty yards away; he can hear the tiny, frantic ticking of a stag-beetle’s claws when it lands upside down on an iron grating. 

Instead of speech, he has developed a set of signals with the iron itself. If a rat grows too boldaround his bread-tin, he does not hiss; he taps the heel of his spanner once against the flange—*tock*—and the vibration runs fifty feet down the catwalk through the soles of the rat’s feet, sending it trotting back toward the tallow-drains. When he wants the eels to rise so he can take one for his supper, he kneels by the warm pool and drums his knuckles in a slow,three-beat cadence against the submerged cast-iron lip of the sluice. Within a minute, the pale, blind things come writhing up out of the black silt, nosing at his fingers like suckling pigs, waiting to be chosen.

He cooks without fire. Fire is an insult in that air, and wood will not burn when it is wet with tallow-steam. Instead, he has a flat, oblong butter-tin with a hinged lid. He skins an eel with a thumb-length piece of ground hacksaw blade, drops the meat into the tin with a pinch of coarse grey salt and two bruised fronds of the bitter, coal-dusted fern from the upper weep-holes, and wires the tin tightly to the bare elbow-joint of the number-one exhaust flue. In forty minutes, the spent steam inside the cast iron has poached the meat in its own fat until it falls off the bone. He eats it with his fingers, sitting on the middle catwalk with his legs dangling over the void, dropping the vertebrae one by one between the iron bars to watch the small, armored catfish boil up in the violet light below.

He is not mad, though a man from the street who climbed down the iron ladder would think him so within five minutes. He has simply shed the habits of variable weather and variable time. Up above, in the town, men have wives, newspapers, bronchitis, Sunday trousers, and the fear of winter; down here, there is only the load on the municipal grid, which rises slightly when the tramways start and falls slightly when the theatres let out, felt only as a half-tone shift in the bass note of the north wall. 

In the pockets of his rubber cape, he keeps his collections: three dozen brass washers of varying gauges that have vibrated loose from the gantry cranes over the years; the dried, hollow shells of huge water-bugs, shiny as mahogany; and four short stubs of spent copper-coated carbon rod dropped by the lamp-trimmers, with which he draws on the smooth, unweathered planes of the basalt down near the water-line.

The drawings are never of women, or horses, or ships, or trees. He draws what is there, over and over, in greasy black lines that the water cannot wash away because the stone is too oily to take the wet. There are long, meticulous elevations of the manifold valves, every bolt-head and flange-joint counted and rendered; there are maps of the current-lines in the ankle-deep pools, showing how the rainbow scum whorls around the masonry piers; and there are hundreds of small, five-stroke tallies recording the fall of the starlings.

When a bird grows too heavy with the grease on its feathers, or old age overtakes it in its warm girder-joint, it misses its stroke in the vapor and drops like a stone into the mire. The Gauge-Man always hears the splash. Before the rats or the catfish can reach it, he wades out, picks the small, warm, oil-slick body from the mud, and carries it to the number-four flue, where the steam exits with the greatest pressure. He lays the bird on the iron lip, wipes its beak clean with his thumb, and then pushes it gently into the dark, roaring mouth of the pipe, where the current blows it back into the dry dark under the turbine floor, out of the reach of the scavengers. Then he goes back to the basalt wall, makes a small, greasy mark with his carbon stub, and sits back down beside the toads, his hands resting flat on the warm iron, feeling the city turn through his palms.
