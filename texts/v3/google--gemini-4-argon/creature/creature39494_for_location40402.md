You hear them before you see them, though at first you mistake the sound for the valley itself. It is a rhythmic, percussive *clack-clack-thrum*—the sound of long, fire-hardened tapping-poles striking the crust, testing the hollowness of the newborn stone before a foot is ever set down.

Then, through a tearing veil of sulfur-steam, a line of figures comes vaulting across a field of blistering black glass.

They are tall, stork-legged, and seemingly built of the same jagged materials as the trench floor. To walk on stone that is both razor-sharp and eighty degrees centigrade, they wear platform-stilts carved from the dense, petrified bases of giant fungal stalks, lashed to their shins with strips of dried gut. The stilts give them a high, lurching, mantis-like gait, keeping their vulnerable flesh two feet above the scalding brine-puddles and the rivers of clicking crabs. Their bodies are lean and wired with corded muscle, covered in a mosaic of translucent crab-carapaces and overlapping lizard scales sewn onto aprons of cured flyer-hide. The armor isn't meant to stop a blade; it is meant to turn the shrapnel of exploding rock domes and shed the splash of boiling mineral slush.

Because nothing in the Caldera-Trench stays still, they have become a walking building. 

A single individual—they call themselves the *Kett*, a word that sounds like two pieces of flint struck together—is a bristling scaffold of bone, copper, and basketry. Arched over their shoulders are tall wicker frames woven from mineralized reeds imported from the upper world, from which hang their entire lives: coils of braided sinew, stone-headed hammers, bundles of hollow bone whistles, and clusters of live, translucent crabs strung through the leg-joints to keep them fresh until supper. Their faces are entirely hidden behind masks of greased leather and hardened fungal-gum, fitted with narrow goggles of chipped obsidian to cut the blinding glare of the white aragonite terraces. To breathe the stinging, sulfurous air, they wear long, snout-like filters packed with damp charcoal and crushed, absorptive puffball-flesh, giving them the silhouette of giant, predatory weevils picking their way through the steam.

They do not walk so much as they ricochet. A Kett never stands on two feet if one will do, and never keeps a foot in one place for more than three seconds. Even when they pause to butcher a catch or haul in a flyer-net, they are doing a slow, rhythmic dance—shifting weight from left stilt to right, knees perpetually bent, their long tapping-poles vibrating in their scarred, four-fingered hands as they read the tremors under their heels.

To the Kett, the ground is not an object; it is a weather system. 

Watch a hunting party of six working a new fissure. A dome of pink salt and travertine has just ruptured, and the twenty-minute puffballs are already swelling in the cooling cracks, drawing a cyclone of four-winged flyers down into the updraft. The Kett move in with terrifying, synchronized speed, communicating without words. Their language has no use for shouting—the valley is too loud—so they speak in a combination of high-frequency bone-whistles and wide, semaphore-like gestures with their tapping-poles. 

Two of them—the *Listeners*—jam the butts of their poles into the groaning rock and press their masked cheekbones against the wood, feeling the deep, liquid burps of the magma blister thirty feet below. They are timing the eruption. The other four—the *Riggers*—move like spiders across the porcelain-thin ledges. They don't dig post-holes; instead, they splash ladlefuls of thick, mineral-heavy mud onto the bases of their slag-cairns. In the dry heat and chemical spray, the mud flash-cures into stone cement in ninety seconds. Up go the gut-nets, snapping taut in the hot wind. The flyers hit the mesh like hailstones, their leathery wings tangling. 

For ten minutes, the Kett are a whirlwind of obsidian knives and flying offal. They do not carry the whole carcasses away; weight is death when a shelf gives out. They strip the heavy breast-meat, the oil-sacs, and the long wing-tendons in three practiced slashes, tossing the rest to the seething carpet of crabs at their feet—which in turn fattens the crabs for the next harvest. 

Meanwhile, a child—already on miniature stilts at five years old, tethered to a Rigger’s waist by a twenty-foot cord of elastic gut—moves along the hissing steam vents, swapping out clogged bone-whistles for fresh ones. As the old bones are pulled from the fumaroles, their insides are lined with a quarter-inch of pure, precipitated sulfur and crusted salt, which the child knocks out into a copper bowl with a stone mallet. Nothing is wasted. Even the whistles that mark their trails are miniature mines.

Suddenly, one of the Listeners strikes the rock twice with a pole and throws an arm skyward. 

The Kett do not look back. They do not hesitate to grab a dropped scraper or a half-butchered flyer. One Rigger slices the main guy-line of the net, wrapping the meat-heavy mesh around a central pole like a giant spindle, hoists it between two sets of shoulders, and the entire party bounds off the terrace in unison. Four seconds later, the ground they were standing on sighs, turns to grey soup, and drops six feet into a spitting cauldron of violet mud.

Because there is no wood in the trench, the Kett have a complex, parasitic relationship with the rim three hundred feet above. You can see their "villages" if you look up at the sheer basalt cliffs: fifty feet above the valley floor, where the stone is old and cold and stable, hang clusters of bulbous, salt-crusted cocoons. These are the *Pendulums*—communal sleeping-sacks woven from leather and bone-ribs, suspended from iron-hard wooden spikes driven into the cliff crevices. 

At night, or when the entire basin floor enters a "boil"—a period every few weeks when the crust softens across hundreds of acres at once—the Kett retreat up the cliffs on knotted ropes, pulling their stilts up behind them. They sleep five or six to a sack, swinging gently in the hot updrafts while the valley floor roars and remakes itself in the dark below. Up there, hanging against the cold basalt, is the only place they remove their charcoal-snouts and obsidian goggles. Their skin, pale and permanently pruned from a lifetime of steam-baths, is tattooed with intricate, shifting maps of scars—each line marking a great eruption they survived, a rich vein of native copper they stripped before the mud swallowed it, or a family member who stepped on a hollow dome and was instantly cooked and mineralized into part of the landscape.

They do not bury their dead. When a Kett dies on the ropes, or grows too stiff-jointed to dance on the new stone, they are lowered down to the fastest-growing aragonite terraces at the center of the basin. The clan gathers on the surrounding spires, blowing a deafening, discordant dirge on fifty steam-whistles at once, and watches as the mineral spray goes to work. Within a day, the body is coated in a translucent glaze of white stone; within a week, it is a gleaming, abstract pillar of bone-white crystal, already covered in basking lizards and the scratch-marks of crab mandibles. 

Within a month, a young Rigger will lash a guy-line to the shoulder of that stone pillar to catch the morning flyers, before the earth shudders, cracks, and takes the ancestor down into the dark again.
