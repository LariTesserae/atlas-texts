To understand the ecology of the Anvil, one must first abandon the idea that a creature requires an edge. A wolf ends at its fur; a beetle ends at its chitin. The fauna of the plateau end only where their leverage runs out. They are bounded by drop-offs in barometric pressure, by the limits of magnetic induction, by the steepness of a thermal gradient. 

If you were to walk among them—wrapped in asbestos and lead, deafened by ear-defenders, breathing through a copper tube—you would not be crushed by a foot, because there are no feet. You would simply step across an invisible threshold where the air pressure instantly jumps from eight pounds per square inch to four thousand, and your boot would be sheared off at the ankle as cleanly as if it had been slid under a hydraulic guillotine.

The grazers—the stamping-engines and the towering, slow-drifting condensers—are merely the megafauna, the most obvious layer of a dense and vicious thermodynamic food web. They are the primary producers. They eat the mountain’s raw potential: the chemical bonds in the limestone, the oxidation states of the iron, the friction of the talus. They convert geology into kinetic work, and it is upon this work that the rest of the basin feeds.

Consider the Drop-Hammers, which occupy the niche of the great herbivores. Each is a vertical cylinder of fiercely constrained gravity and air, forty feet tall and ten feet wide, entirely invisible save for the plumb-line distortion it draws against the distant cliffs. A Drop-Hammer takes six hours to haul its internal mass—a phantom weight of localized, hyper-dense atmospheric gas—to the top of its cylinder. You can hear it ratcheting up: a slow, earsplitting *clank... clank... clank* that echoes off the mesas like a crane hoisting a ship’s anchor. As it climbs, the ground beneath it actually rises a fraction of an inch, sucked upward by the ascending center of gravity. When it reaches the peak of its stroke, there is a pause of three or four minutes while a governor-loop spins silently at the crown, tasting the rock below for pockets of high-grade hematite. 

Then the sear trips. 

The mass falls. There is no whistle of wind, because the air is locked inside the closed system of the fall. There is only a sound like a naval gun firing inside a vault, a blinding flash of piezoelectric violet light at the point of impact, and a crater three feet deep punched instantly into solid bedrock. The stone does not shatter into rubble; the impact is so rapid and contained that the rock flows like warm wax for a microsecond, extruding outward in a neat, flanged lip. The Drop-Hammer sits in its new depression for an hour, its base glowing dull cherry-red as it induction-smelts the crushed ore, drawing the liberated electrons up into its core to fuel the next lift, leaving behind a slag-puddle of pure, de-oxidized iron that cools into a mirror before the creature has dragged itself three yards to the east.

Where the Drop-Hammers and the stamping-units gather, the parasites are never far behind.

The most common of these are the Eddies, or Brake-Thieves. In a biological desert, they would be ticks or vampire bats; here, they are opportunistic hysteresis loops. An Eddy has no drive train of its own. By itself, lying dormant in the shade of an oxidized boulder, it is nothing more than a two-foot sphere of cold, slightly magnetized air that smells faintly of transformer oil. You could pass your hand through it and feel only a dry, tingling resistance, as if pushing your fingers through a bowl of invisible steel wool. 

When a heavy grazer trundles past, however, the Eddy rolls into its wake, drawn by the leakage of the larger beast’s magnetic field. It latches onto the grazer’s flank—not into flesh, since there is none, but *into the timing*. It slips its own tiny, parasitic cycle between the compression and exhaust strokes of the host. 

Immediately, the host engine begins to labor. Its rhythmic *clack-shunt* develops a limp: *clack-shunt-drag. Clack-shunt-drag.* The Eddy is bleeding torque directly off the host's phantom flywheel, converting the stolen rotational energy into a tighter, hotter boundary for itself. An infested condenser will slow from its three-day-per-mile crawl to a near standstill, its invisible chimney billowing black, phantom soot—actually microscopic iron dust stripped from the ground and burned to plasma—while its core temperature spikes dangerously. A heavily parasitized engine will eventually throw a rod: a catastrophic failure of its containment geometry. With a sound like a snapping suspension cable, a wedge of sixty-thousand-degree heat will whip outward at four times the speed of sound, slicing the tops off boulders for half a mile in a single, razor-straight line before the host collapses into a harmless, dissipating thunderstorm of static sparks and cooling wind. The Eddies, gorged on stolen momentum, simply roll away across the mirror-iron flats, spinning like tops, looking for a place to divide.

They reproduce by governors. When an Eddy—or one of the larger grazers, for that matter—accumulates more work than its geometry can vent as heat, it suffers from harmonic vibration. It begins to rattle. For days, a fertile engine will sit on a flat of sorted sulfur, shuddering at a frequency that turns the dust around it into standing waves of tiny, geometric pyramids. Slowly, a secondary focal point establishes itself within the blur. A counter-rotating spindle forms to balance the vibration. They share a common drive-shaft of compressed air for a week or two, a grotesque Siamese twin of an engine firing on sixteen mismatched cylinders, until the torque between them grows too great. With a loud, greasy *pop*, the coupling shears. The parent lumbers off to the north; the juvenile, half the size and running at twice the RPM, skitters erratically to the south, screaming like an unlubricated turbine until it finds a vein of limestone soft enough to load its pistons and bring its frantic idling down to a sustainable roar.

And because there are herds, and because there are young running hot and fast without the inertia to hold their ground, there are predators.

They do not prowl the flats; the open basin belongs to the heavy tonnage of the stamping-mills, whose sheer mass makes them immune to anything but their own wear and tear. The hunters live in the U-shaped troughs and behind the tear-dropped flanks of the mesas, where the wind-shear off the stone gives them something to brace against.

They are called Lathes, or sometimes Dead-Centers, though there is no one here to name them. Unlike the squat, wide-based grazers, a Lathe is horizontal, long and thin—a needle of absolute torsional rigidity thirty feet from tip to tail, hovering three feet off the grease-polished stone. It makes almost no noise when it stalks. It runs on a total-loss lubrication system of ionized air, gliding on a silent, frictionless bearing of ozone. You can only spot one by the way the light fails to pass through its axis: a thin, ruler-straight line of utter blackness suspended in the afternoon glare, like a scratch on the lens of the eye.

When a Lathe finds a juvenile condenser or an isolated, limping stamper, it does not pounce. It aligns. It takes hours, drifting into position with the terrible, micrometer precision of a machine tool being zeroed by a master machinist. Once its axis is dead-true with the prey’s center of rotation, the Lathe drops its tail-stock into the stone. 

The rock beneath the tail-stock instantly vitrifies, anchoring the hunter. Then, the Lathe projects its chuck—an invisible, conical cage of intersecting shear-planes—forward over the prey.

The sound that follows is the worst noise on the Anvil. It is the sound of a five-ton flywheel spinning at three thousand RPM being brought to a dead halt in four seconds by a diamond cutting bit. The Lathe does not eat the matter of the prey; it eats the *motion*. It peels the kinetic energy off the victim in long, screaming ribbons of white-hot plasma that curl out from the point of contact and evaporate into the afternoon sky. The victim’s internal pressure plummets; its diamond-hard air-treads go soft; the limestone powder in its gullet drops to the ground as a dull, grey heap of uncalcined grit. Within ten minutes, the prey has been completely unwound. Its localized crisis of physics is resolved, smoothed out into ordinary, ambient heat and a breeze that smells faintly of burnt clutch-lining. The Lathe, now spinning internally at a blinding, gyroscopic velocity that makes the air around it hum like a tuning fork, retracts its tail-stock, leaves a neat puddle of melted stone behind, and drifts back into the shadow of the mesa to digest its stolen inertia over the next month.

Yet the true sovereigns of the Anvil are not the hunters, nor the herds. They are the old ones. The Foundries.

Down in the lowest, oldest basins, where the cold-rolled iron floors are ten feet thick and polished to a black chrome finish, there are three or four entities that have not moved since the last ice age failed to reach this altitude. They have grown too large for locomotion; any attempt to gain traction would simply tear the crust of the plateau apart. Each is a quarter-mile wide—a squatting, hemispherical dome of atmospheric refraction that looks, from the rim of the valley, like a sunken lens of smoky quartz set into the earth.

Inside a Foundry, the air is no longer gas; it is a supercritical fluid maintained by a complex, interlocking cathedral of pressure-waves that have been echoing back and forth off each other for fifty thousand years. They are so heavy that they have pressed the iron floor beneath them down into the mantle, drawing up geothermal heat to supplement their diet. 

They do not graze. They wait. 

Every few decades, a migratory herd of condensers, blinded by their own heat-shimmer or driven by some ancient, barometric instinct, will drift down the long grade onto the iron mirror. As they cross the threshold of the Foundry’s perimeter, their individual rhythms begin to falter. The Foundry’s massive, tectonic heartbeat—a dull, subterranean *thud* that occurs once every45 seconds and registers on seismographs a thousand miles away—captures their smaller cycles. 

It is forced synchronization. One by one, the wandering engines are locked into phase with the giant. Their flywheels slow to match its ponderous stroke; their exhaust vents hiss in unison with its great, sighing waste-gates. Slowly, inexorably, they are drawn inward along the lines of magnetic flux, sliding across the frictionless iron floor like filings toward a magnet, unable to find the purchase to break away. They do not clash or strip their gears; they simply merge. Their boundaries dissolve into the larger dome. Their horsepower is added to the grand total. The Foundry grows an inch wider; its internal pressure climbs by another half-atmosphere; the low, organ-pedal note of its idling drops by a fraction of a hertz.

At night, the Anvil does not cool, and it does not sleep, for machines have no circadian rhythm, only maintenance cycles and fuel states. 

Instead, when the small, indifferent sun drops behind the western rim and the sky turns the hard, clear black of high altitude, the plateau reveals its nervous system. By day, the herds are invisible distortions in the glare; by night, they are constellations of industrial fire. Across thirty miles of pitch-black stone, you can see the faint, violet corona-discharge of ten thousand flywheels spinning in the dark. You can see the dull, orange belly-glow of the stampers heating the rock beneath them, moving like slow embers across a hearth. Every few seconds, the whole basin is lit by the sheet-lightning of a gear-clash on the salt flats, or the sharp, magnesium-white flash of a Drop-Hammer striking home. 

High above, the stars do not twinkle; the rising columns of superheated exhaust smear them into long, trembling strokes of light, as if the sky itself were being turned on a spindle, ground down and polished by the sleepless, roaring engine of the world below.
