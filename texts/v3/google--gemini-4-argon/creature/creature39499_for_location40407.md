To see what lives here, you cannot look for movement. You have to look for an error in the geometry of the light.

Across the floor of the basin, forty feet down, the sodium lamp casts its bright, polygonal web of refracted gold. Because the water’s surface never ripples, this grid of light is painted onto the grey chert slabs with the permanence of glaze on porcelain. Yet if you watched one particular intersection of those golden lines for an entire afternoon, you would notice that the light does not fall flat against the rock. It bends slightly upward, passing through a low, dome-like thickening on the stone that was not there the month before.

It has no name in any municipal survey, though its remote ancestors were once cataloged by nineteenth-century naturalists as freshwater sponges and bryozoans—things that lived in dark wells and spring-heads. When the basin was scoured fifty years ago, a microscopic trace of something ancient survived in a deep, unwashed suture of the dolomite. In the decades since, trapped in this hyper-oxygenated, heavily mineralized, unchanging lens of water, it has grown into something altogether different. It has shed the habits of an animal and taken on the patience of a crystal.

It is a colony, or perhaps a single, divided consciousness distributed across seven or eight sites on the basin floor. To the naked eye, an individual mass looks like nothing more than a pale, translucent blister on the smooth grey stone, roughly the size and shape of a turned-over soup bowl, or a low loaf of unbaked dough. Its body is a stiff, silicified gel, woven through with a lattice of calcium carbonate drawn directly from the hard, forty-eight-degree water. It is ninety-eight percent water itself, its refractive index so close to that of the pool that it is nearly invisible, betrayed only by the faint, milky ghost of its internal scaffolding and the way it warps the golden light passing through its flesh.

It does not eat in the way surface things eat. There is no detritus falling from above, no drift of dead plankton. Instead, it farms the minimal fallout of the upper wall. 

Twenty feet up, the velvet mats of green algae and the rigid tufts of water-moss grow in the perpetual noon of the sodium lamp. As their outermost cells age and die, they slough off—not in clumps, which would be too heavy, but in microscopic, disintegrating motes of cellulose and lipids. In a moving stream, this meager dust would be swept away instantly. Here, in the dead calm, the particles sink at a rate of inches per hour, drifting down through the mint-colored glass in unseen vertical columns.

The creatures on the bottom sit directly beneath the moss tufts, aligned with the plumb-line of gravity. Their upper surfaces are perforated with thousands of microscopic pores, though they do not pump water through themselves as marine sponges do; pumping requires energy, and the water here offers too few calories to waste on motion. Instead, they rely on the sixty-cycle hum. 

The continuous, low-frequency drone of the distant municipal pumps vibrates through the dense karst and into the rigid, calcareous lattice of the creature’s skeleton. The creature has grown its internal mineral struts to the exact thickness and tension required to resonate with that sixty-hertz tone. The entire gelatinous dome shivers at a microscopic scale, a standing wave captured in living tissue. This constant, sub-visible tremor creates a tiny, localized acoustic current just above its skin, drawing the sinking algal dust into its pores without the creature having to expend a single calorie of its own effort. It feeds by letting the waterworks shake its food into its mouth.

Because of this, its anatomy has become a direct transcription of the municipal water system. In its center, where the oldest spicules of lime are packed together, the lattice is dense and irregular—a record of the old iron piston-pumps that served the district in the nineteen-seventies. Out toward its margins, the mineral web is finer, strictly hexagonal and uniform, laid down after the county upgraded to high-efficiency centrifugal turbines twelve years ago. When a check-valve clinks miles away, sending a sharp spike of pressure through the rock, the creature’s outer gelatinous mantle flinches by a micron, and in the following weeks, a faint, denser ring of calcium—like the frost-ring in a tree trunk—forms along its outer edge, marking the event.

Higher up, in the five-foot air pocket beneath the stone dome, something else has claimed the aluminum housing of the lamp.

Where the braided steel cable enters the cast-aluminum cap, the metal is warm—the only warm thing for miles. The lamp burns at several hundred degrees inside its thick glass globe, creating a miniature thermal equator in the cold damp of the cave. Around the neoprene gasket, where the heat sweats out into the ninety-nine-percent humidity of the trapped air, grows a thick, dry crust the color of tarnished brass and old bone. 

It is a lichen, or the stripped-down cousin of one, arrived via a single spore stuck to the boot or coveralls of the electrician who hung the lamp forty years ago. Up here, starving for nitrogen, it lives on the trace nitric acid formed when the lamp’s intense ultraviolet bleed ionizes the damp air, and on the slow, galvanic corrosion of the aluminum housing itself. It grows outward over the thick glass lens in a ring of crusty, overlapping scales, advancing toward the center of the light until the heat of the glass becomes too fierce, creating a perfectly circular aperture—an iris of pale, mineral crust that frames the glare.

Between the crust on the lamp above and the glass-like domes on the stone below, there is a slow, decades-long dialogue. 

Every few years, as the lichen-crust on the lamp glass thickens, a tiny flake of it dries too much in the heat, curls, and detaches. It falls the five feet through the still air, hits the tight skin of the water, and remains there, floating on the surface tension for days like a speck of grey ash. Immediately, the golden web of light on the floor forty feet below is altered; a new shadow, the size of a coin, is cast onto the chert. 

If that shadow falls across one of the gelatinous domes on the bottom, the creature registers the sudden drop in radiation. Over the course of the next six months, the cells on its shaded side will slowly de-differentiate, liquefying their calcium struts and flowing outward at the speed of cold pitch, pulling the entire organism three or four inches across the smooth stone until it is back in the full, unobstructed glare of the sodium sun. Behind it, on the grey limestone, it leaves a faint, etched footprint where its acidic undersides dissolved the rock to build its bones—a pale, smooth scar shaped like a crescent moon.

Eventually, the flake on the surface becomes waterlogged, breaks through the surface tension, and sinks. If it lands on one of the domes below, it is a feast—a massive, rare meteor of carbon, aluminum oxide, and fungal chitin. The creature’s clear flesh will slowly mound up over the speck, entombing it in clear gel, digesting it over the course of a winter until nothing is left but a tiny, dark smudge of insoluble metal locked forever inside its crystalline skeleton.

Nothing else happens. The pinpoint bubbles of oxygen rise from the green velvet on the ledges, ascending like inverse rain, passing through the forty feet of winter-mint light to vanish at the ceiling. The water flexes by a sixteenth of an inch as a neighborhood three miles away wakes up and turns on its taps. And at the bottom of the lens, in the golden polygons, the clear domes sit on the grey stone, humming softly with the voice of the distant pumps, digesting the light.
