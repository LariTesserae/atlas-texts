They are called the Sump-Keepers by the municipal clerks who still, out of bureaucratic inertia, drop crates of tallow and graphite down the basalt hoist-shaft on the first Tuesday of every third month. The clerks never wait to see the crates unloaded. They release the cable, hear the heavy *clack* of the iron hook freeing itself at the bottom, and wind it back up.

Down on the flanged walkways, what approaches the crate is a posture before it is a body. In the violet-white stutter of the arc lamps, you first see the oilskins: immense, bell-shaped cowls of vulcanized canvas that have been re-tarred so many times over the decades they no longer crease when the wearer moves, but hinge and flex like the plates of a beetle. They are stiff with blackened pine-pitch, mineral wax, and the dried, iridescent slime of the marsh. Beneath the skirts of these coats, their boots are not leather—leather rots here in a week—but galoshes of poured gutta-percha cast directly over heavy wooden clogs, banded in copper to keep the soles from splitting on the hot iron grates.

There are perhaps eleven of them left across the four blocks, though you will never see more than two together, and then only when a main trunk-valve seizes and requires four hands on the wheel-wrench. 

They were men once, or the grandsons of men assigned to the basin when the municipal condenser was still a marvel of the lower wards, before the city above switched to the river-turbines and forgot the sump existed. Now, they are creatures of ninety-eight-degree water and copper-salt. If one pulls back its cowl in the lee of a brick tower, out of the warm rain, what looks out is pale and strangely hairless, the skin having taken on the tallowy, translucent quality of the boiler-ferns. Their pores are permanently dilated. Because the air is at body temperature and one hundred percent humidity, sweat does not evaporate; it simply rolls off them in slow, oily sheets, indistinguishable from the condensate dripping from the copper lattices above. Their fingers are permanently pruned into deep, tough ridges—pale grey at the tips where the skin has absorbed a century of lead-packing and graphite grease—and the nails have grown thick and curved as horn, stained a faint, verdigris green at the quick.

They do not speak. The four-minute shriek of the relief valves and the chest-rattling *thud-shuck* of the subterranean rams killed language here eighty years ago. Instead, they communicate through the iron. 

Every Keeper carries a three-foot rod of hexagonal brass—a tapping-bar, worn bright where the hand grips it. To stand on the causeways is to feel their conversations through the soles of your feet. A triple-rap against a main flange travels two hundred yards through the bolted plates in a second; a rhythmic scraping along the ribs of a high-pressure steam line tells a Keeper three towers over that the western sluice is choking on bladder-moss. When they are close enough to see one another through the sluggish fog, they speak with their lanterns—heavy, bull's-eye lamps fueled by pressed lard-oil—tilting the shutters open and closed in slow, languid winks of yellow light that cut beneath the violet glare of the overheads.

They are completely deaf to the air, their eardrums having calcified into thick, protective pearls before they reached adolescence, yet their skeletons are exquisitely tuned to the basin’s vibration. A Keeper asleep in a hammock slung between two sweating return-pipes will wake instantly if a counterweight cable three blocks away drops a single strand of wire and begins to hum in a flat key. They know the health of the subterranean pumps by the way the water teeth-marks the edges of the grease-lotus leaves.

Watch one on its rounds. It moves with a slow, rolling, flat-footed gait, conserving heat. In this air, moving too fast causes the blood to boil in the ears; everything must be done at the speed of a cooling boiler. The Keeper stops beside a masonry pier where a colony of bladder-moss has swollen too close to an expansion joint. It does not hack at the plant in anger. It uses a long, curved flensing knife to neatly lance the glowing, hogshead-sized sac. As the moss deflates with a wet hiss, the Keeper catches the thick, lukewarm mucilage in a copper pan. 

Nothing in the basin is wasted, because nothing new comes down the shaft except grease and carbon rods. The mucilage from the moss is boiled down in the lower tiers of the brick towers to make a slick, non-corrosive salve the Keepers rub into their skin to keep the sulfate-mist from blistering their necks. The six-foot fronds of the boiler-fern are harvested when they turn brittle, braided into heavy ropes, and stuffed into the weeping joints of the brickwork as oakum. The starchy, tuberous roots of the grease-lotus—which tastes strongly of coal-tar and iron—are fished out of the two-foot mire with long hooks, sliced thin, and baked directly on the uninsulated shoulders of the main steam-domes until they are black and crisp as slate.

Their homes are the upper three stories of the windowless purple-brick towers. You enter them not from the walkways, but by climbing thirty feet up the iron rungs mortared into the brickwork, passing through the thermal layers until you reach a heavy ironhatch that opens like an oven door. Inside, there are no floors, only spiral grates circling the massive, central standpipes that rise toward the copper canopies. 

It is drier here, baking and dark, smelling of roasted lotus-root, old tallow, and ozone. The walls are lined with the sheddings of the precinct’s long life: hundreds of blown glass pressure-gauges stacked like skulls; coils of braided copper wire; the brass nameplates of foundry companies that went bankrupt before the Keepers' grandfathers were born. Here, in the warm vertical caves between the pipes, they hang their oilskins to drip onto the grates below. 

In the highest tiers, just beneath the rafters where the counterweight cables groan through their sheaves, they keep the things they do not understand. There are porcelain insulators from the arc lamps arranged in neat, spiraled shrines on ledges of mineral wax; dried, pressed fronds of mutated fern that grew in the shape of human hands; and four-foot icicles of pure, blue-green copper sulfate that they have coaxed into growing from specific leaks in the roof, tending them for forty years like bonsai trees.

They have no concept of the city above them, nor any curiosity about the pewter smear beyond the condenser awnings. To a Sump-Keeper, the world is an closed circuit of pressure and release. Water rises as steam, strikes the copper sky, falls as rain, gathers in the two-foot dark, and is swallowed by the unseen rams to be heated again. 

And when the four-minute valve clears—when the horizontal pillar of white steam blasts across the plaza with a roar that flattens the duckweed and makes the arc-lamps swing wildly on their chains—the Keepers do not flinch or cover their ears. If one happens to be standing on the walkway ten yards from the vent, it will simply turn its masked face toward the blinding white column, lean its weight slightly against the blast, and let the scalding back-eddies wash over its tarred hood, standing quite still with its brass bar resting on the iron floor, feeling the pressure drop three pounds per square inch through the bones of its heels, satisfied that the world has breathed out, and is already gathering itself to breathe in again.
