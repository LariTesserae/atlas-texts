To survive in a medium that is constantly trying to grind you into paste, you have to choose one of three options: become harder than the stone, become softer than the mud, or find a way to stand in the narrow cracks where the world forgets what it is doing. 

The things that live in the Grist have tried all three.

***

### The Tallow-Skins (The Gleaners)

At first glance, you would take one for a bloated sack of grey suet or a drowned seal that has been tumbled in the surf until its bones turned to jelly. 

They are vaguely hominid, if a human were redesigned by a hydraulic press. A Tallow-Skin stands about five feet tall, though "stands" is the wrong word; they *slump*, wide-hipped and splayed-footed, their center of gravity sitting somewhere around their groin. Beneath their thick, hairless, lard-white hide lies a six-inch layer of dense, viscoelastic blubber that absorbs kinetic shock like ballistic gel. When a ten-ton mound of black shingle collapses onto a Tallow-Skin, it doesn't break them. Their ribs—which are not bone, but flexible hoops of calcified cartilage—simply flatten out. The creature squishes down into the interstices between the cannonball-sized stones, deforming like a water balloon, and then slowly, methodically squeezes itself back up to the surface through the gaps.

They have no external ears—the planetary roar would have ruptured their drums epochs ago—and their eyes are tiny, recessed pig-beads covered by permanently closed, translucent nictitating membranes. They see only shifts in the amber murk. Instead, they navigate entirely through the *Soles*. Their feet are immense, fleshy pads, three feet across, fringed with fat, worm-like toes that constantly knead the rolling gravel. Through these pads, they read the bass-notes of the basin. They can feel the distinct, greasy tremor of a Silt-Plough swimming three hundred yards down; they can feel the dense, dead *thud* of a jade monolith rising toward the surface miles away; they can feel the sharp, prickly static of a reality-stutter forming in the trough of an oncoming wave.

They are nomads of the Anchor-Groves. A family pod of four or five Tallow-Skins will haul themselves into the lashing, black-vine interior of a rolling thicket, letting the plant carry them across the flats. They move through the interior of the sphere with a slow, boneless grace, hooking their thick, two-clawed hands into the rubbery bark as the whole world tumbles end-over-end around them. 

They are farmers of a sort, though what they farm is catastrophe. They wait for the Anchor-Grove to hit a time-shear. When half the thicket suddenly accelerates, blooming into those pale, fleshy flowers that instantly rot into glowing slime, the Tallow-Skins drop from the vines like heavy ticks, scrambling over the groaning, lopsided wood to scoop the luminescent rot into pouches formed from their own loose belly-folds. This "shear-honey" is calorie-dense and humming with residual chronal static; eating it keeps their metabolism running hot enough to survive the endless, leeching wet.

To watch two Tallow-Skins communicate is deeply unsettling. Because the air is a solid wall of noise, they must press their bodies together to speak. Two of them will wade into the warm slurry, clasp each other in a slick, blubbery embrace, and press their broad, flat foreheads together. Then, they hum—deep, pharyngeal vibrations that travel directly from skull to skull, bypassing the air entirely. To an outsider, it looks like two faceless mounds of wet dough wrestling in the mud, weeping warm grey water as they trade stories of gravel-tides and sunken arches through the bones of their faces.

### The Chime-Lice and the Knotted

Below the Tallow-Skins, in the direct path of the grinding stones, life abandons flesh altogether. 

If you scoop up a handful of the peppercorn-sized Grist, about one in every thousand spheres isn't stone at all. It is a **Chime-Louse**. They are crustaceans that have evolved into perfect, seamless ball bearings. Their shells are composed of biogenic iron-sulfide, polished to a dull, oily mirror-finish by the very friction they live in. 

A Chime-Louse spends ninety percent of its life locked in a state of spherical rigor, allowing itself to be rolled, crushed, and hurled across the basin by the gravel-tides. It takes no energy to move when the world moves for you. Only when they are swept into a warm, stagnant pocket of iridescent microbial broth do they unroll—just a fraction of an inch—extending feathery, metallic cirri to sweep the muck into their mouths before snapping shut again as the next wave of stone collapses over them. You can tell when a dense swarm of them is caught in a gravel-surge because the sound of the roar changes: the dull, wet *shhh-krrrrk* of the stones acquires a high, frantic, metallic *ringing*, like millions of brass sleigh-bells being ground together in a cement mixer.

Then there are the things that live in the *ghosts*.

Where the air goes solid with the memory of a four-hundred-million-year-old granite peak, or where gravity tilts sideways and pins the rain to the dunes, you will find **The Knotted**. They are not animals; they are more like an opportunistic infection of the basin’s frayed physics. 

To the eye, a Knot looks like a tangle of greasy, iridescent hair or dark copper wire, roughly the size of a basket, suspended in the soup. They have no mass to speak of. In normal space, the churning gravel would tear them to shreds in seconds. So they do not live in normal space. They have evolved to hook their barbed, filamentary bodies into the shear-stress anomalies. 

When a wave of rust-colored water stutters—repeating the same half-second of splash five times—a Knot is already there, wedged into the skipping frame of time like a crowbar in a doorjamb. They feed on the concussive potential energy that builds up before local physics snap back into place. Through some frantic, metabolic alchemy, their filaments bleed off the anomaly’s tension, prolonging the stutter. A wave that should have skipped for three seconds might stay locked in a stutter for three days because a massive, breeding mat of the Knotted has woven itself through the water droplets, holding the splash suspended in mid-air like a ceiling of quivering amber glass.

Beneath these suspended, stuttering roofs of water, temporary dry pockets form on the gravel beds. The Tallow-Skins call these pockets *Halt-Holes*, and they will crawl inside them to sleep, curled up on the steaming, backward-flowing stones while six feet above their heads, a hundred tons of water repeats the first half-second of falling over and over again, held aloft by a humming, glowing web of greasy wire.

### The Architects of the Clove-Domes

And then there are the Cairns of the Deaf. They are not ruins. They are shells.

The things inside them are called **Masons** by the Tallow-Skins, though no Tallow-Skin has ever seen a whole one alive. Millions of years ago, the Masons may have been the ones who carved the milky jade archways when the world still had bedrock. When the mountains began to lose their grip and the great grinding started, the Masons did not adapt their bodies to the mud like the Tallow-Skins. They refused the mud. They chose to take the old sky with them.

A Mason is a colonial organism now—a fused, sessile tangle of pale, gangly limbs, oversized lungs, and hyper-developed secretory glands, all grown together into the interior walls of their bobbing stone huts. There may be six or seven individuals melted into the lining of a single dome, their nervous systems braided together, their ribs fused directly into the clove-smelling blood-cement that holds the dark pebbles of the roof in place.

The interior of a Cairn does not exist in the Grist. 

By secreting precise, rhythmic layers of heavy minerals and psychoactive resins onto the inside of the stones, and by maintaining a continuous, sub-audible chorus of throat-clicking within the sealed chamber, the fused Masons maintain a localized, permanent reality-shear inside their dome. 

If you were to crack open a Cairn—which takes the collision of two massive gravel-waves—you would not find a dark, cramped room full of wet meat. As the shell breaks, the interior spills out: a sudden, violent decompression of bone-dry, freezing desert air that smells of ancient sagebrush and ozone. In the half-second before the warm swamp rushes in to drown it, you would see that the inside of the ten-foot dome is somehow three miles wide. You would see a flat expanse of pale, unbroken limestone under a pitch-black, silent sky prickling with hard stars. And sitting in the center of that impossible, arid plain, you would see the Mason-colony: a weeping, fleshy heap of fused limbs and blind, upward-turned faces, utterly deaf, breathing the cold air of a world that died half a billion years ago, singing the song that keeps the ceiling from turning back into mud.

When two Cairns collide in the surf with that dull, booming *thud*, they are not crashing by accident. They are mating, or perhaps trading memories. In the fraction of a second where the space between them tears open, revealing that slice of cold, starry night, the two colonies exchange airborne spores across the bridge of ancient sky—tiny, dry seeds of memory and genetic code that drift from one impossible desert to the other before the warm, brown slurry slams shut again, and the domes bob apart into the roaring amber dark.
