To find the thing that actually rules this basin—or rather, the thing that *is* the basin in a way the snails and the liverworts are not—you have to stop looking at the surfaces and look at the joints. 

At first, the eye refuses to assemble it. You see what looks like a seam of translucent caulk wedged between two pillows of black basalt, or a pale, glistening collar wrapped around the base of a giant hornwort, half-buried under a shingle of sleeping amphibians. You might step on it, thinking it is just another rubbery holdfast of the flora, except that when your weight comes down, the pressure does not stop right under your boot. Six feet away, a sluggish pool of mineral water ripples in answer, and the pile of segmented creatures beside it tilts slightly, as if a mattress spring had given way beneath them all.

They have no common name because no one stays in the brass-colored gloom long enough to need shorthand for them, though the few surveyors who have stood on the jagged rim and watched the valley through binoculars refer to them as *the grouting*, or sometimes *the cistern-lungs*. 

They are animals, technically, though they have long since abandoned the exhausting project of having a discrete body. A single individual might cover forty square yards, though it is rarely more than four inches thick at any point—except where it drops a massive, taproot-like stomach down into a deep cooling crack in the lava. They are evolved from some ancient, opportunistic chordate—a lungfish or a caecilian that wandered into this thermal trap epochs ago and realized that in a world of ninety-eight-degree soup where food rains down continuously and nothing has teeth, a spine is an unnecessary expense. Bones require calcium, which is scarce in the fresh basalt; muscles require ATP, which requires hunting. The cistern-lungs gave up both. 

Instead, they have become sheets of tough, gelatinous mesoglea, pale amber and slick as wet soap, spreading beneath the vegetation and directly over the volcanic glass. The sharp edges of the slag do not cut them. When a cistern-lung grows over a razor-ridge of obsidian, it does not flinch or pull back; it simply extrudes a dense, calloused sheath of dead protein over the blade, sealing the sharpness away like an oyster turning grit into pearl. Over centuries, they have padded the entire floor of the valley. It is their bodies, more than the plants, that keep the basalt from weathering. They are the shock absorbers between the geological violence of the crust and the soft, pulsing weight of the biomass above.

If you peel back a layer of the giant liverworts—which takes considerable effort, like pulling up wet linoleum—you will expose the creature’s upper surface. It looks like the roof of a vast, pale mouth. Ribbons of dark purple blood vessels meanders just beneath the skin, branching and re-branching without a central heart to drive them. Instead, the creature relies on the ecosystem above to pump its blood. Every time a stack of amphibians shifts its weight, every time a heavy hornwort sways and presses down on its holdfasts, the pressure squeezes the cistern-lung’s vessels, pushing the sluggish, copper-rich blood a few inches further along its network. It has outsourced its circulation to the restless, peristaltic heaving of the millions of creatures that use it as a floor.

In return, it breathes for them. 

The water in the basalt hollows is ninety-eight degrees, and water that hot holds almost no dissolved oxygen. By all rights, the trenches packed with gilled amphibians should be suffocating dead zones. They aren't, because the bottom of every puddle is lined with the fluted, velvety skin of a cistern-lung. The creature takes in the carbon dioxide and the thick, ammonia-heavy waste of the packed amphibians directly through its skin, and—through a dense interior forest of symbiotic algae and heat-loving sulfur bacteria that live inside its translucent flesh—cracks the waste apart, venting pure, microscopic bubbles of oxygen straight up into the water column. 

If you look closely at the stacked amphibians, you will see that their synchronized gill-pulsing isn't just a rhythm they share with each other; they are keeping time with the slow, chemical exhale of the living liner beneath them. A milky cloud of oxygen-rich mucus rises from the bottom of the trench; ten thousand feathery gills flare at once to drink it in; the amphibians settle back down, their heavy bodies pressing the cistern-lung flat, squeezing its veins, pumping its blood to the next puddle over, where the cycle repeats. 

It does not eat in the way the snails eat. It has no radula, no jaws, not even a single dedicated mouth. Instead, its upper surface is dotted with thousands of sphincter-like pores, each about the size of a teacup, kept tightly puckered against the wet air. 

When a soft-bodied insect drops from the ochre sky, exhausted from mating, or when one of the giant gastropods finally reaches the end of its slow, scraping life, it doesn't just dissolve into the plants. The weight of the dead thing stops shifting. Living things in the basin have a tiny, constant tremor to them—the buzz of a wing, the scrape of a tongue, the throb of a gill. When that micro-vibration stops against the skin of the cistern-lung, the skin notices. 

Slowly, over the course of twenty minutes, the flesh beneath the corpse softens and dimples. One of the teacup pores migrates through the gelatinous body, sliding along like an oiled ring until it is directly beneath the dead snail or thespent amphibian. The sphincter relaxes, yawns wide, and the corpse simply sinks downward, greased by a sudden secretion of warm, digestive slime. The pore closes above it with a wet, silent *plop* that is entirely lost in the roaring white noise of the valley. 

Inside the translucent amber sheet of the creature, you can see the ghosts of the basin’s recent dead suspended as they are broken down: the cloudy silhouette of a four-inch lacewing slowly losing its outlines; the spiral of a protein-shell unwinding like a curl of smoke; a pale, segmented leg dissolving into a bloom of dark red nutrients that immediately flushes outward through the vein-network to feed the hornworts’ suction cups.

For a long time, it seems as though these creatures are merely biological plumbing—smart moss with a circulatory system, a series of disconnected fleshy ponds. 

Then you watch the clearing of a vent.

Because the crust here is young and thin, the floor occasionally shifts, or a new fumarole cracks open in the basalt, spitting a jet of scalding steam and hydrochloric gas into the bottom of the mat. In a normal forest, this would burn a dead circle in the greenery. Here, the response is immediate, and it travels across the five-mile basin faster than anything else in this slow-motion world.

A tremor starts at the scalding vent—not a seismic tremor, but a muscular one, the only fast movement the cistern-lung is capable of. A wave of milky opacity races through the translucent flesh beneath the plants, traveling at a brisk walking pace, radiating outward for miles. As the wave passes beneath the puddles, the cistern-lungs stop venting oxygen and instead release a sharp, astringent pheromone into the water. 

Instantly, the deafening roar of the basin changes pitch. The amphibians in the path of the chemical signal stop humming. They do not panic—they don't have the neurological hardware for panic—but they unstack themselves, slithering heavily over the liverworts to pile up three yards to the left or right, leaving a bare lane over the hot spot. The grazing snails drop their suction, rolling off the hornwort stalks like heavy hail, tumbling into the safe trenches.

Within ten minutes, a patch of the basin twenty feet wide is completely bare of small animals. The giant plants above the new fumarole wilt as the cistern-lung beneath them intentionally drains the water from their hollow stalks, sucking their internal pressure down into its own body so the rubbery stems collapse flat, out of the way of the rising acid steam. 

Then, from the surrounding forty yards, the cistern-lung inflows. It pulls its substance out from under the neighboring rocks and puddles, flowing toward the wound in the earth like warm wax pouring into a mold. It heaps itself around the hissing crack in the basalt, building a thick,chimney-like collar of dense, sacrificial cartilage that channels the toxic steam straight up past the ten-foot canopy of the plants, releasing it into the open headspace above where it can disperse harmlessly into the ochre clouds. The flesh nearest the heat cooks into a black, leathery crust, insulating the living gel behind it. 

Once the chimney is built and the steam is contained, the milky opacity fades from the floor. The hornworts are pumped full of water again, inflating back to their six-foot height with a slow, squeaking groan of wet tissue. The oxygen bubbles resume in the puddles. The snails crawl back up the stalks; the amphibians slide back into the warm trenches. The great, droning roar of ten million throats settles back into its flat, vibrating equilibrium.

If you stand on the rim of the basin and look out through the tarnished-brass light at those chimneys—there are hundreds of them scattered across the five-mile floor, looking like dark, stubby stalagmites rising just above the green mat, each one trailing a thin wisp of white vapor into the murk—you begin to realize that there are not thousands of cistern-lungs down there. 

The plants fuse where they touch; the grouting beneath them has had even less reason to maintain boundaries. Over the millennia since the lava cooled, the sheets of amber flesh have met in the cracks, dissolved their dividing skins, and joined their vein-networks. 

There is only one of it. It fills the five-mile dish from rim to rim, fitted to the blistered basalt like a lead liner in a sink. It has no brain, because there is nothing in the valley to outwit, and no eyes, because there is nothing above it but the unchanging ochre ceiling. Yet as you watch the slow, miles-wide peristaltic heave of the valley floor, it becomes very difficult to tell whether the millions of creatures on top are shifting their weight and pumping the giant’s blood, or whether the giant is shifting its own weight in its sleep, rocking its ten million noisy parasites just enough to keep them warm, wet, and breathing.
