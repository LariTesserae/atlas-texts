If you look at the primary pressure manifold for twenty minutes without blinking—allowing the occasional violet snap of static from the copper busbars to print and reprint the scene on your retinas—you will realize that the bulging, three-foot saddle of yellow-gray mineral crust straddling the main intake pipe is not an accumulation of anhydrite. 

Wait for three cycles of the relief valve. Thirty-six seconds. Over that span, the left flank of the mass will have shifted a quarter of an inch downward, shearing a fragile lacework of sulfur needles off the cast iron with the slow, inexorable force of a glacier.

At first, it registers only as a local thickening of the arthropod mat, a place where the rust-colored bacterial crust and the layered harvestmen have simply heaped themselves over an irregularity in the machinery. This is because its back is a garden. The creature moves so little, and at such a microscopic pace, that the lithotrophic bacteria have colonized its upper plates, and the blind, pale crickets hang from the heavy ledges of its margin as readily as they hang from the basalt ceiling. Only when the static arcs twice in rapid succession do you see the seam: a dry, horizontal cleft tucked beneath the overhang of its shell, sealed against the parched air by a mortar of its own dried, gummy secretions and powdered alum.

It has no eyes—not even the vestigial, sunken pits that the cave-crickets retain. Its ancestor might have been something akin to a giant, desert-dwelling whip-scorpion or a primitive, segmented myriapod that existed in the deep volcanic fissures miles down before the drills ever broke the ridge, drawn upward six months ago by the sudden, roaring concentrated heat of the tap. Or perhaps it came down from the surface in the greased tracks of the crane cables as a microscopic instar, and the hundred-and-thirty-degree furnace, the metallic ionizing radiation of the ungrounded busbars, and the sheer, force-fed abundance of the pocket bloated and warped it through six rapid, monstrous molts into something that belongs exclusively to this six-by-eight-foot engine room.

It is roughly the size and shape of a deflated leather bellows, or a heavy blacksmith’s apron dropped over the hot pipe. Its chitin is not sleek; it is porous, cinder-like, and heavily calcified, drinking in the ambient mineral sublimate until its carapace is chemically indistinguishable from the orthorhombic sulfur and iron oxide flaking off the turbine housing. It is heavy—unnaturally heavy for an invertebrate—because its shell is forty percent stone.

Beneath that stone-crusted saddle, however, it is startlingly, unpleasantly hydraulic. 

In an environment with zero liquid water, where the air will crack human lips in four minutes, the creature is a sealed bladder of recycled moisture. Every shred of chitin it consumes, every pale scorpion and fat, sluggish isopod it grinds into paste, is broken down metabolically to extract the hydrogen and oxygen locked in the proteins. It does not urinate; it does not transpire. Its underside, pressed tight against the scorching, matte-black fins of the heat sink, is a pale, translucent, tough membrane through which dark, viscous hemolymph pulses at a rate of once every two minutes. It uses the machine as its heart. The forty-revolution-per-minute *thrum* of the screw-expander vibrates through its belly, mechanically agitating its internal fluids, saving it the metabolic cost of a rapid pulse.

It does not hunt, and it does not even wait with open pincers like the mica-colored scorpions. It farms, and it grazes.

Every twelve seconds, when the choked pressure-relief valve begins its four-second, deafening *shriek*, the creature reacts. It is entirely deaf to air-conducted sound, possessing no tympanum, which would only be shredded by the acoustic violence of the chamber. Instead, it feels the preliminary build-up of torque through the iron casing—a subtle, high-frequency shudder that precedes the valve's release by half a second. 

As the shriek begins, a seam opens along the creature’s lower front margin, facing the valve’s exhaust port. From this cleft, it extrudes a pair of modified appendages that no longer resemble legs or pedipalps. They are broad, flattened paddles of fibrous, honeycombed bone-chitin, coated in a thick, non-drying mucilage synthesized from sulfur-fixing bacteria and the rendered fats of ten thousand crickets. It holds these paddles out into the roaring, violent gale of the valve’s release.

The four-second blast of superheated gas shakes the entire chamber, tearing loose a blizzard of live harvestmen, bacterial flakes, molted skins, and tiny, blind silverfish from the ceiling and walls. This organic shrapnel is driven through the air in a dense vortex, and a fraction of it slams directly into the creature’s sticky paddles. 

When the valve slams shut with its sledgehammer *bang*, the silence—or rather, the return to the grinding, bass-heavy *thrum*—finds the paddles already retracting. They fold back into the horizontal cleft like the blades of a pocketknife. Inside that sealed, humid dark, secondary mouthparts—rows of slow, reciprocating comb-plates that make a sound like two blocks of sandpaper being rubbed together—scrape the mucilage clean, rolling the dust, the sulfur crystals, the crushed scorpions, and the grease-vapor into a single, dense bolus, before coating the paddles anew and waiting eight more seconds for the machine to scream again.

Because the space is closing in, the creature is becoming the room’s architecture. 

Look at its rear segments, where its body tapers down into the V-shaped junction between the cast-iron housing and the razor-edged basalt wall. It has not moved its hindquarters in at least two months. The powdery white anhydrite and yellow sulfur frost have grown up over its rear legs, cementing them directly into the volcanic rock on one side and the bolted flange of the turbine on the other. It is no longer capable of turning around. If the machine were to suddenly shut down and the pocket were to cool, the creature would die bolted in place, locked in a cast of its own mineral accumulation.

In fact, this has already happened to its predecessor—or its earlier self. 

Just beneath the creature’s belly, wedged between two braided steel hoses, is a rigid, hollow arch of yellow stone that still retains the vague, segmented ribs of a smaller carapace. It is a six-week-old molt, now entirely petrified by the fumarole’s breath, serving as a bridge and a windbreak for a dense colony of tiny, rust-red pseudoscorpions. 

The living creature’s only concession to mobility is its anterior half, which swings in a slow, pendulous arc of about fourteen inches over the course of a day, grazing the bacterial mat from the copper busbars when the static isn't arcing, and positioning its paddles for the twelve-second gale. When the violet static *does* build up on the ungrounded copper, you can see the creature’s one remaining sensory apparatus: a fringe of short, brittle, metallic bristles along its front rim—hairs doped with conductive iron sulfide—that rise and stiffen in the electrical field, guiding its blind mouth away from the busbar three seconds before the violet snap discharges into the basalt.

It has no eyes, no ears, no voice, and no name. It is a three-pound sack of self-contained, grease-flavored brine, armored in sulfur and driven by the forty-RPM heartbeat of an industrial screw-expander. It is slowly growing into the shape of the negative space between the iron and the mountain. In another four months, when the mineral crusts finally bridge the remaining gap and choke the chimney shut for good, its carapace will touch the ceiling, its underside will weld to the pipe, and it will simply sit there in the pitch-black, roaring furnace—a living gasket of chitin and stone, chewing the dust of the dark every twelve seconds until the mountain itself goes cold.
