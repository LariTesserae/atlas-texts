To see them, you must wait until the morning cloud has fully cleared the basin and the slate slabs have begun to dry from the top down. Then, a wooden shutter—pale, unvarnished cedar bleached to the exact silver-grey of the limestone—swings inward at one of the three dwellings, and someone steps out onto the flagstones.

There are eleven of them at present. There have never been more than fourteen, and rarely fewer than nine; the three cisterns and the square footage of the slate orchard dictate the number with arithmetic finality.

They are built like the walls: low, dense, and wide-hipped, with the deep, barrel-shaped ribcages of people whose blood has spent generations thickening to catch the thin oxygen of the upper air. Their skin is the color of dry putty, weathered at the knuckles and cheekbones to a dull, wind-chapped russet, though it is soft and pale as tallow beneath their clothes. Because the light in the bowl has no glare, their eyes are rarely squinted; they have wide, calm gazes with dark irises that seem slightly oversized, taking in the whole width of a plaza without turning their heads. 

They move across the vast spaces between their houses with a peculiar, rolling gait—what a lowlander would recognize as a mountaineer’s rest-step, though here the ground is entirely flat. They place a whole foot down, lock the knee for a fraction of a second to let the skeleton bear the weight rather than the muscle, and only then shift forward. No one hurries. To hurry is to breathe hard, and to breathe hard in the dry afternoon is to exhale a thimbleful of water into the sky that the sky will not give back until tomorrow.

Their clothing is heavy, wind-proof, and nearly rigid. They wear tunics and trousers of grey wool felt, beaten and shrunk until it is half an inch thick and has no visible weave, cut in straight, boxy planes that stand slightly away from their bodies. Over their shoulders they drape cloaks made from the pelts of mountain marmots and rock-hares, sewn together with sinew so neatly that the garment looks like a single coat of dense, smoky fur. On their feet they wear soft boots of oiled ibex hide, soled with four layers of stitched leather. When two of them cross the main plaza together, they make no sound at all—not the slap of a sole nor the rustle of fabric—only the faint, dry creak of thick wool bending at the knee.

For hours at a time, their work appears to consist entirely of small adjustments to the geometry of the bowl. 

An old woman—though it is difficult to tell their ages, as the grey hair and leathery skin arrive early and then remain unchanged for forty years—walks the length of the water channels with a sheep’s shoulder-blade in one hand and a tuft of greasy raw wool in the other. She crouches by a runnel where a gust of night wind has deposited a pinch of limestone dust. With the bone scraper, she lifts the grit out of the groove so it will not dam the morning’s trickle; with the greasy wool, she wipes the lip of the cistern corbel, leaving a microscopic film of lanolin that forces the water to bead and drop cleanly into the dark below rather than clinging to the underside of the rock and evaporating.

Fifty yards away, a man and a boy are turning one of the heavy stone rollers across the northern pavement. They are not smoothing the stone. Over the winter, frost gets under the flagstones and heaves them by the thickness of a thumbnail; if a slab tilts the wrong way, the morning dew that condenses on the plaza floor will run toward the retaining wall instead of the third cistern. So they roll the three-ton cylinder back and forth over a single ten-foot square for an entire afternoon, leaning their thick shoulders into the wooden frame, pausing for five minutes between passes to stand utterly still, hands tucked into the opposite sleeves of their felt tunics, looking at the sky or at nothing at all.

They do not speak while they do this. The basin is an acoustic bowl; a word spoken at a normal pitch by the granary arrives at the gatehouse fifty paces away with every consonant intact, rebounding faintly off the retaining walls. Speech feels bulky here, like dropping a cobble into a still bucket. When they are out in the plazas, they communicate with small, economical shifts of posture. A chin lifted toward the northern notch means the wind is shifting and the dew-nets should be Taken down before the dry gusts shred them; two fingers laid flat against the thigh means *wait, let the cloud settle before you cross the slate-yard, or your body heat will spoil the condensation on the lower stones.*

They save their voices for the interiors.

Inside the three dwellings, the scale of the world reverses. Where the plazas are vast and open to the pale sky, the rooms are small, dark, and crowded with warmth. The walls are four feet thick, lined on the inside with woven mats of dried sedge to keep the stone from drawing the heat out of the inhabitants' bones. The ceilings are low enough that a standing man can lay his palm flat against the blackened juniper beams. 

Here, the faint woodsmoke that scents the basin originates. In the center of each room sits a brazier no larger than a soup bowl, burning a single twist of that ancient, wrist-thick shrub-wood harvested from the outer cliffs—wood so dense it sinks in water and burns without flame, holding a core of white-hot ember under a blanket of grey ash for twelve hours. Over this tiny heat, they boil their tea: water from the cisterns infused with dried saxifrage leaves and melted goat tallow.

In the dark of the houses, pressed shoulder-to-shoulder on low stone benches, they talk. Their voices are surprisingly low and resonant, a chest-register hum that vibrates in the timbers but never pierces the thick doorway into the plaza. They talk about the taste of the water in the second cistern compared to the first (the second has a sweeter, flinty note from a vein of quartz in its feeder-channel; the third tastes faintly of iron and old moss). They recite the histories of the individual slate monoliths in the orchard—which ones were dragged up the southern scree-chute by their great-grandfathers, which one has a hidden flaw that will split if a winter is too wet, which one catches the very first bead of moisture when the cloud-bank is thin.

They are not prisoners of the bowl, nor are they ascetics waiting for an end. The gatehouse that straddles the avenue to the sheer drop is not a ruin or a folly; it is a working winch-house. 

Inside its shadow sits a drum of hornbeam wood wound with three hundred fathoms of braided horsehair rope. Twice a year, when the high haze thickens into the grey wool of autumn and spring, four of them go to the gatehouse, swing the heavy timber crane out over the void, and haul up wicker baskets hooked to the line by people from the unseen valleys a mile below. The baskets come up heavy with hard-kerneled highland barley, blocks of rock salt, raw wool, and dried mutton fat. 

In return, the people of the bowl lower down small, stoppered horn vials of the thing they harvest from the margins of their world: a paste ground from the silver-blue saxifrage and the slow, hundred-year lichens that only grow where the air is thin and the light has no shadow. In the hot, damp valleys below, this paste stops a fever in an hour and seals a festering wound overnight. To the people below, the eleven figures at the top of the rope are half-mythical—the Silent Apothecaries, the Cloud-Herds—who never descend because the heavy air of the lowlands would drown their lungs like water.

To the eleven themselves, the trade is simply how the granary stays full.

Toward evening, the light does not redden; the linen sky merely darkens through shades of pewter and wet iron until the flagstones and the air are the same color. Before the dark is complete, a boy walks out to the stone orchard one last time. He carries a wooden mallet. He moves down the rows of ten-foot slate slabs and taps each one lightly near its base—*tock, tock, tock*—listening to the pitch of the stone to be sure the day's mild warmth hasn't loosened its seating in the socket. 

A crown-finch, roosting in a dry-stone joint high in the retaining wall, shifts its weight and tucks its head beneath a grey wing. The boy returns to the middle dwelling. The cedar shutter pulls closed and is barred from within against the night wind that is already beginning to whistle softly through the notched parapet. For the next ten hours, the plazas are entirely empty, sixty yards of swept stone waiting in the dark for the morning cloud to come over the rim and find everything in its proper place.
