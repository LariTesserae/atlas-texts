They are not inside the black needles, and they are not inside the gray spheres. To look for a hatch or a cockpit is to misunderstand what the structures are doing. The pillars and the buoys are merely the tuning pegs. The valley itself is the instrument, and what lives here is currently playing it, though to human eyes it mostly looks like an old woman trying to pull a drowned goat out of a bog.

You will not see them until you have walked four or five miles in from the tide-line, your knees bruised black by hidden basalt edges, your ears ringing from the sheer, percussive violence of the wind. When you finally spot one, you will think at first that a large, oil-slicked boulder has rolled into a cleft of dwarf spruce, or that a clump of horizontal willow has grown particularly thick and dark with winter rot. 

Then the boulder stands up, joints popping with a sound like dry spruce branches snapping in the cold, and shakes two gallons of freezing brown peat-water out of its sleeves.

Local subsistence hunters from the coast fifty miles south call them *K’eł-t’a*—the Silt-Heavy Ones, or the Listeners-in-the-Brush—though the hunters do not come into this basin anymore, not since the ice went back and the black needles rose. If you were to drag one of the Listeners out of the valley and stand them under a fluorescent light, they would look almost human, in the way a deep-sea chimera looks almost like a trout. The architecture is there—two legs, two arms, a torso, a head—but the proportions have been rearranged by a radically different set of requirements.

They are short, rarely clearing five feet, and extraordinarily wide. Their chests are barrel-like, thick with a layer of dense, fibrous brown fat that smells faintly of iodine and crushed cedar. Their skin is the color of wet slate, tough and slightly pebbled, utterly hairless except for a thick, coarse mane of hollow, translucent bristles that grows from the brow-ridge, sweeps back over the ears, and mats down the spine like the rain-cape of a marsh-harrier. Water does not soak into them; it beads and rolls off their skin as if they were coated in whale tallow.

Their legs are short and bowed, ending in broad, splayed feet that they never put into boots. The soles are three inches thick—a tough, horn-like pad of segmented gray callus that can step directly onto a fresh, razor-sharp flake of fractured basalt without dimpling, while the five long, prehensile toes wrap over the wet stone like tree-roots to anchor them against an eighty-knot gust.

They wear clothes, though it is difficult to tell where their skin ends and the garments begin. They wrap themselves in heavy, quilted mantles woven from black sedge-root, strips of rubbery bull-kelp, and the oiled skins of eider ducks, stitched together with what looks like fine, tarnished silver wire. The garments are stiff with dried glacial flour and old fish-grease, weighted at the hems with smooth, hexagonal pebbles of pure magnetite so the gale cannot whip them into their faces.

What makes them deeply unsettling to look at is their stillness, and their eyes.

A human in this valley is in constant, miserable motion—shivering, blinking away sleet, hunching shoulders against the gale, flailing for balance as the moss gives way. A Listener moves through the waist-high jungle of Sitka spruce with the slow, hydraulic certainty of a crab on the sea floor. When they stop, they stop completely. They do not sway in the wind. They do not blink when the sleet rakes across their faces. Their eyes are large, set deep beneath heavy, projecting shelves of bone, and they have no visible whites—only a dark, amber-colored iris that dilates until it fills the entire socket, covered by a thick, clear nictitating membrane that clicks sideways every few minutes to squeegee the freezing mud from the cornea. 

They do not use the black pillars for shelter. If you watch a pillar through night-vision glass from the ridge, you will see the foxes and the murres crowding into that three-meter radius of calm, sleeping in the dry, vertical rain while their feathers steam around the hexagonal puddles. You will never see a Listener step inside that circle. To them, the quiet zone around a needle is as blinding and obnoxious as a welding arc. 

Instead, they live in the roar. They *eat* the roar, or at least, they think with it.

The nodes and the spheres are not theirs, in the sense that they did not manufacture them in a factory and carry them here on ships. Or perhaps their ancestors did, ten thousand years ago, before they decided that metal and glass were less reliable than bone and blubber, and intentionally bred themselves down to fit the trough. Whatever the history, the relationship now is entirely biological. The machinery is an externalized nervous system; the basin is a gigantic, forty-kilometer battery of kinetic and acoustic violence; and the Listeners are the mind that wakes up when the tide hits the wind.

Watch one of them working the alder-ravine down near the gray sphere. 

It is a male, old by the look of the white lichen-scars crusting his knuckles, squatting on a slick ledge of weeping black rock just outside the sphere’s twenty-foot bubble of absolute silence. The flash flood is tearing past his toes, black with silt, smelling of ground-up granite and ancient ice. 

He has his left hand plunged wrist-deep into the freezing mud of the bank. His fingers are long, seven-jointed, and paddle-shaped, the nails thick and black like horn spoons. He is not hunting for smelt. His eyes are rolled back slightly, the clear membranes twitching as the gray buoy four feet away drinks the thunder of six-ton boulders smashing together in the dark. 

Every time two boulders collide in the silence beneath the sphere, the old male’s throat-sac inflates slightly, and his jaw shifts. He is *feeling* the fracture-lines of the valley floor through the soles of his feet and the tips of his buried fingers, reading the way the stolen acoustic energy is being routed down through the bedrock toward the nearest obsidian needle half a mile away.

They do not speak to one another with voices. Trying to push air over vocal cords in a ninety-mile-an-hour gale is for birds and fools. When two Listeners meet in the dark brush—wading waist-deep through the thrashing horse-tail ferns, while petrels drop like wet cannonballs into the branches around their knees—they communicate through the ground and the water.

One will strike the heel of its palm against a wet basalt plate: a dull, heavy *thud* that doesn't carry three feet in the howling air, but rings through the fresh, unweathered stone for fifty yards. Two seconds later, from down in a frost-heaved trench choked with sponge-moss, an answer comes back—not a sound, but a sudden, localized stiffening of the water in the rill between them. The Listener in the trench has slapped a root-bundle with three fingers, sending a coded ripple of hydro-static pressure upstream, straight through the thrashing bodies of a thousand migrating smelt, to tap against the first Listener’s ankle.

What are they talking about? Mostly, they are gardening, though their idea of a garden includes tectonic plates and barometric pressure.

Wait until the ozone-click comes from the nearest black pillar. 

Two seconds before the pulse hits, every Listener within half a mile will suddenly tilt its head at the exact same forty-five-degree angle, their translucent brow-bristles standing straight up against the wind, glowing for a fraction of a second with a dull, greenish St. Elmo’s fire. They have felt the charge building in the basalt seams beneath the moss. 

*Click.* 

The pressure hits your teeth. The rain freezes in mid-air for that single, uncanny microsecond—millions of drops of salt-spray and sleet turning into a suspended three-dimensional matrix of solid glass across a hundred-yard arc.

To a human, that microsecond is just a disorienting stutter in the storm, a momentary glitch before the water drops with a heavy *thwack* into the sedge.

To the Listeners, that microsecond is a wide-open room. 

In the instant the rain hangs solid, the acoustic properties of the entire hundred-yard arc change completely. For one thousandth of a second, the air is no longer a chaotic soup of wind and water; it is a rigid, crystalline lattice of ice-beads suspended at precise intervals. The old male by the ravine snaps his thick, horn-plated jaws together once—*clack*—at the exact instant the rain stops falling.

That single *clack* bounces off ten million suspended raindrops simultaneously. It turns the hundred-yard arc of frozen rain into a phased-array sonar dish of unimaginable resolution. In that microsecond, the reflected click maps everything inside the arc down to the millimeter: the exact depth of the new fractures in the cooling basalt fifty feet underground; the fat-reserves of every sea-mink and vole hiding under the liverwort; the trajectory of every smelt in the slush-runs; the root-tension of every dwarf spruce clinging to the stone.

The rain falls. *Thwack.* The gale roars back in. 

The old male blinks his clear membranes, reaches out with his right hand—moving with sudden, terrifying speed through the dark—and plucks a single, heavy-bodied murre right out of the air as it tumbles past his shoulder on an updraft. He does not look at the bird. He knew it would be there, down to the millimeter, three seconds before it arrived, because the frozen rain told him how the wind was curling over the rock-spur fifty yards upwind.

He breaks its neck with a thumb-press that makes no sound at all, tucks the warm, oily body into a fold of his kelp-mantle, and goes back to listening.

They do not have villages. A roof is just a sail waiting for the wind to tear it off, and a wall is just a dam waiting for the mud-flood to knock it down. When they need to sleep, or when the rare, terrible days of calm come in late summer—when the wind dies, the fog lifts, the sun beats down on the black rock, and the quiet makes them nauseous and half-blind—they go down into the chunnels.

Beneath the two-foot mat of sponge-moss and the labyrinth of broken basalt, the meltwater torrents have carved out long, flooded galleries in the raw stone. The water down there is thirty-three degrees and moving like a mill-race, pitch-black and roaring against the rock ceilings. The Listeners slide feet-first through narrow crevices in the willow-roots and drop into the subterranean current. 

They do not drown. They wedge their wide, heavy bodies into the jagged angles where the basalt plates meet, locking their horn-soled feet against one wall and their broad shoulders against the other, letting the freezing torrent pour directly over their heads. Their metabolic rate drops by eighty percent in three minutes. The brown fat in their chests keeps their core just warm enough to prevent ice crystals from forming in their blood, while the roaring water rushes past their eardrums, vibrating their heavy skulls with the continuous, comforting song of the basin grinding itself down into soil.

You can walk right over a sleeping family of four—your boot sinking to the knee in ice-water through the bright-green liverwort—and think you have only stepped into a particularly cold bog-hole. Beneath your heel, wedged into the knife-edged dark under the roots, four pairs of amber eyes will open behind their clear shields, watching the pressure-shadow of your boot-sole distort the standing waves rolling down from the nearest obsidian needle.

They will not grab your ankle. You are too warm, too noisy in the wrong ways, and you have no business with the basalt. They know that within six hours, the wind or the ankle-breaking stone will drive you back out to the coast, or else the cold will stop your heart and the foxes and the sedge-roots will render you down into something the valley can actually use. 

Above them, the black needle clicks again. The rain hangs in the dark. The old male by the ravine shifts his grip on a wet willow-root, pries a three-hundred-pound slab of fractured stone two inches to the left with his heel so the meltwater will hit a lower pitch as it drops into the gorge, and settles back into the mud, tuning the storm until it sounds right.
