If you are invited inside a Rigger’s cocoon—which requires sliding feet-first through a puckered sphincter of greased hide—the first thing that strikes you is the smell. It is a thick, suffocating miasma of scorching pine-sugar, wet fur, ammonia, and the sharp, turpentine tang of the Truss’s blood. The second is that nobody is sitting down. 

The floor of a cocoon is a taut trampoline of braided tendon, and the people inside do not rest on their spines. They hang. They squat in low, frog-like postures with their heels tucked into woven stirrups, or they lean back into leather belly-slings suspended from the ceiling ribs, swaying in unconscious, fluid counter-rhythm to the constant, terrifying shudder of the wood outside.

Look at a Rigger’s hands and feet, and you will see what the Truss has made of them. Their boots have no soles; they are thick, fingerless mittens of rough-out hide that leave the heel and the great toe bare—though the toe is usually capped with a carved horn thimble, cinched tight around the ankle with rawhide. Their hands are huge, the fingers permanently curved like grapple-hooks, the skin of the palms thickened into yellow plates of split callus from a lifetime of sliding down high-tension lines. 

Even their faces have been reshaped by the altitude and the gale. They are barrel-chested and short-necked, with wide, flat noses that warm the thin air before it hits their lungs. They smear their cheeks and foreheads with a paste of charcoal and sticky resin to prevent wind-burn, and over their eyes they wear slotted goggles carved from the translucent horn of young shoot-caps, shielding their corneas from the stinging crystals of the ice-fog. 

Because the wind outside never stops its titanic, jug-lip howling, the Riggers have largely abandoned the middle registers of the human voice. A normal speaking tone is swallowed instantly by the ambient roar of the timber. Instead, they communicate in two ways: a sharp, piercing whistle-speech that cuts right through the shriek of the gale when they are working out on the spans, and a deep, chest-rumbling murmur used inside the cocoons. 

More important than either is *the knock*. 

Every Rigger carries a tapping-iron—actually a dense rod of cured heartwood weighted with a precious knuckle of ancient stone—hung from their belt. Before a Rigger steps onto a new strut, they hit it. They don't listen with their ears; they press their jawbone or their collarbone directly against the freezing, waxy slate of the bark and feel the return wave. A tight, high *tink* means the beam is under massive tensile load—safe to cross, though dangerous to cut into, lest the wood split explosively under the blade. A dull, hollow *thud* means the vascular bundles inside have gone necrotic and dry; the span is deadweight, waiting for the next big structural adjustment to snap it off and send it tumbling into the planetary abyss. 

They talk to each other through the wood the same way. A Rigger family is never all in one cocoon; to put twelve people on a single node is to invite extinction when a shoot punches through from below and changes the geometry. A clan is spread out over three or four miles of intersecting triangles. At night—or what passes for night in a world where the indigo dark merely thickens into absolute pitch—an elder will press their forehead to the interior timber wall of the shelter and beat out the day’s news with a mallet. *Three gliders taken. The lower west strut is weeping sour sap; do not drink. A pale-quill was seen stalking the cistern.* Miles away, leaning against the same continuous vein of ironwood, their cousins feel the rhythm in their teeth and tap back.

Living with the constant threat of the fall has given the Riggers a peculiar relationship with weight and possession. Among them, hoarding is a form of madness, a slow suicide. Every ounce of gear must be justified against the strength of the cords holding the camp to the flute-ridges. If a Rigger acquires a new bone-spigot or a sharper adze, something else must be cast into the dark. 

The only exceptions to this rule are the Drop-Stones. 

In the center of the cocoon, lashed to the main structural post with twenty-ply braided gut, sits a piece of actual rock—usually granite or basalt, no bigger than a child’s fist, worn smooth as an egg by centuries of thumbs. The Riggers do not know what the bottom of the world looks like, only that their ancestors climbed out of it ten thousand generations ago, following the burgeoning growth of the Truss upward as the lower depths choked on stagnant, crushing poison-air. The stones are the only proof that a "bottom" ever existed. They are used to strike sparks against iron-hard dried sap-crusts, and to sharpen the horn-adzes, and when a Rigger marries into another node-clan, a flake is chipped off the family stone with immense ceremony and sewn into the bride or groom’s harness. To drop a stone is the only crime for which a Rigger will be cut loose.

Out on the spans, the Riggers move in groups of three, roped together at the waist by hundred-foot lengths of cured, elastic glider-intestine. They never walk three abreast; they move in a staggered triangle, one person on the knife-edge apex of the beam, and one on each of the thirty-degree angled flanks below. If the person on the ridge is blown off by a sudden downdraft, they drop to the left; the partner on the right flank immediately throws themselves off the opposite side. The rope catches on the sharp, obsidian-like ridge of the timber, and the two dangling Riggers counterbalance each other over the three-quarter-mile drop while the third anchors the line and hauls them back up. They do this without screaming, often without even pausing their whistled conversation. Falling is not an emergency; falling is simply how one travels when the wind is up. Only *unroped* falling is fatal.

Their lives are dictated by the great, blind metabolism of the tree-continent. They are parasites, yes, tapping the high-pressure sap veins for fuel and boiling the bitter, tannin-black water of the pitcher-galls with hot stones to make it drinkable. They harvest the rubbery, plate-sized scale-insects that crust over the warm vascular junctions, roasting them in their shells until they pop and leak a nutty, yellow oil. 

Yet they are also something like an immune system for the local scaffolding. 

The great terror of Rigger life is not the six-limbed quill-hunters—though every child is taught to sleep with a barbed spear in their hand in case a pale, slender neck comes snaking through the cocoon’s entrance-valve—nor is it the howling gales. It is the *Choke-Shoots*. 

When a new shoot, thick as a watchtower and driven by hydraulic fury, comes grinding up through the dark, it does not care what is above it. If it strikes the underside of a camp’s node, it will fuse with the wood, and the sudden release of pressure will cause the surrounding spans to shed their load—dropping fifty feet in a second, snapping anchor-lines like cobwebs, and shaking the cistern-galls loose to spill their scalding, bitter water into the void. 

When the seismic harps outside a camp begin to hum with the deep, sub-audible vibration of a rising shoot, the entire clan mobilizes. They rappel down into the freezing, roaring black beneath their home, swinging out on pendulums of braided gut to land on the rising column itself. It is a nightmare of a job: hanging onto the vertical side of a timber tower that is actively shuddering upward at a foot an hour, slick with boiling sap and freezing fog. With heavy horn wedges and bone-saws, the Riggers score the side of the shoot, bleeding off its hydraulic pressure on one side. A fountain of steaming, sugary antifreeze-sap sprays out into the gale, freezing into long, yellow icicles that shatter and chime as they fall into the abyss. Deprived of pressure on its cut flank, the titanic shoot slowly bends as it grows, curving away from the camp’s node to lock safely into a higher, uninhabited span fifty yards away.

And when someone finally dies—when their heart gives out from the thin air, or a pale-quill takes them in the fog, or age simply makes their grip too weak to hold the adze—the Riggers do not drop them into the deep. To fall to the bottom is to be lost to the cold and the ancient dark. 

Instead, they wait for a Choke-Shoot. They lash the dead Rigger, still in their harness, minus only their stone-chips and their horn-goggles, directly to the black, horny cap of the upward-driving timber. And then they watch from the knife-edge of their span, their grease-lamps flickering in the gale, as the forest carries their kin upward—driving them straight into the starless, indigo roof of the world, toward the mythic, impossible canopy that no living eye has ever seen.
