To see what truly belongs to the basin, you have to stop looking at the heron and look instead at the water inside the stone bowls as they tilt. 

In every third or fourth basin, floating upon the pewter-colored slop like a skin of boiled milk, is something that looks at first like a discarded grease-rag or a drowned owl’s pelt. It has no front and no back. When the limestone lip dips and the forty gallons slide into a neighboring hollow, the thing pours over the paper-thin edge with the water, stretching out to the length of a man's arm, entirely boneless, before gathering itself back into a heavy, puckered disk in the new bowl.

The locals, if any came near enough to peer down through the coastal drizzle, would call them *flukes* or *tallow-men*, though they are neither worm nor mammal. They are colonial, or perhaps simply indecisive about their own boundaries. A single one weighs about as much as a wet sheepskin and is the color of lard that has been rolled in coal-dust. Its upper surface is dry—repelling the mica-heavy water with a microscopic pile of stiff, greasy bristles—and is dotted with three or four dark, sphincter-like spiracles that open and close with a slow, rhythmic *tock* you can only hear if you are standing within an inch of the rim. 

These are the surface-skimmers, the juvenile or larval stage of the basin's true tenant. They ride the churning bowls the way butter rides a churn, feeding on the rainbow-filmed scum of mineral oil and iron-loving bacteria that floats to the top of the clay. When two bowls grind rim-to-rim and a skimmer is caught between them, it does not burst; its rubbery, cartilage-laced mantle simply squeezes flat as a penny, extruding sideways along the soapy limestone until the pressure passes, leaving behind a smear of pale yellow tallow that the blind eels immediately rise to lick away.

The elders—or the aggregate forms, for it is never clear whether one grows large or whether five of the skimmers simply slump together in a deep font and forget how to part—do not stay in the bowls. They have moved up into the ironwork.

If you watch the northeast corner, where the two brick condenser housings are slowly losing their war with the mud, you will notice that the slack steel cables do not always go taut solely from the drag of the rocks below. Sometimes a cable will shiver, draw tight, and haul a two-ton limestone font six inches against the grade of the slurry because something up on the wall has shortened its grip.

High on the weeping shale, nested inside the hollowed-out cast-iron steam chests and wrapped around the axles of the rusted flywheels, are the *Greasers*. 

A full-grown Greaser is a massive, asymmetric sack of grey-white gristle and dark muscle, roughly the bulk of a butchered hog, held together by a loose net of ossified plates that look remarkably like the sheared-off iron flanges of the pipes. In fact, they incorporate the iron. As they grow, their tough, acidic hides press against the boltheads, the broken couplings, and the slack cable-strands, slowly dissolving the rust and calcifying around the metal until the creature is half-rigging itself.

They have no eyes—there is nothing in the basin that needs seeing—and no bones that have not been stolen from the pumping array. Instead, they extend five or six long, muscular trunks, thick as firehoses and tipped with broad, cup-shaped pads of horn and suction-tissue. These trunks droop down from the shale walls, resting lightly on the wet leather drive-belts or trailing their tips in the spinning rims of the limestone bowls below.

They live on vibration. The entire basin is a spiderweb of kinetic energy, a silent roar of torque and shear-stress conducted through steel cables and iron pipes. The Greasers have wedged themselves into the junctions of this web. When a baptismal-sized stone heaves in the center of the pot, the cable bolted to its collar drags across a pulley on the west wall; the pulley’s axle warms in its socket, turning through the middle of a Greaser’s mantle. The creature shudders with a slow, digestive pleasure. It uses the mechanical advantage of the old machinery to shift its own crushing weight, locking a muscular pad onto a flywheel to brake it, storing the tension of the sinking stone below, and then releasing the wheel so that the rocker-arm above jerks violently, snapping a hanging reed-bed and shaking down a shower of fat, blackrock isopods and heavy condensation right into the Greaser’s waiting, upward-facing mouths.

They are the reason the basin is so quiet. 

Over the decades since the engineers left, the Greasers have worked their way into every bearing housing, every stuffing-box, and every fulcrum on the walls. Where iron would otherwise shriek against iron, there is now a thick, self-replenishing cushion of living blubber and slick, secreted mucilage—the source of that faint, sweet, ozonic smell that hangs over the pit. When a flywheel spins furiously for ten seconds, it is spinning on the greased, calloused back of an animal that is simultaneously eating the rust off its hub and using the rotation to scratch its own underside.

And down at the very bottom—below the limestone bowls, where the peat-water and grey clay are warmed by the deep, cold-gas vents and the relentless friction of a thousand tons of turning stone—there is the Sump-Mother, though nobody has ever seen her whole.

She is the floor upon which the bowls turn. Every twenty minutes or so, in the narrow, triangular gaps where three round stones cannot quite meet, the grey mud will part, and a section of something pale, ridged, and wide as a dory’s hull will slide past, four feet down in the dark. It is scarred with a cross-hatching of black lines from centuries of limestone keels riding over its back. The blind white eels do not swim freely in the slurry; they are anchored to her by their tails, growing out of her pores like pale, writhing hairs, reaching upward into the chinks between the stones to snatch at the bacterial mats and the droppings of the heron, passing the nutrients back down into the vast, flattened body that carpets the bedrock of the nave.

Now and then, a gauge needle behind its cracked glass will peg itself hard against the red line and stay there, trembling, while down in the middle of the floor, the churning stops. For three, four, five minutes, every stone basin locks in place, held from beneath by an enormous, suctioning contraction. The flywheels on the walls cease to turn; the leather belts hang dead; the heron freezes with one yellow eye cocked toward the mud. 

Then, from the sheared-off mouth of the largest cast-iron pipe, comes a long, wet, subterranean sigh—a bubble of cold marsh-gas and tallow-scented breath that smells of the nineteenth century and the bottom of the sea. A single pale parcel of meat, the size of a loaf of bread, is coughed out of the pipe’s orange throat and flops into the nearest limestone bowl. The pressure releases. The stones begin to turn again, silently tipping the new skimmer from bowl to bowl toward the center of the floor, while high up on the shale, a rocker-arm bows once, twice, like a priest returning to his liturgy.
