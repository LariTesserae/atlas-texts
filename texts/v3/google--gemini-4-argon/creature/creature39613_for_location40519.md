To understand what truly inhabits the basin, you must look past the giant ferns and the weeping walls, down to the places where the bronze moss parts around the mouths of the stone chimneys. 

The rims of these vertical flutes are crusted with a brittle, pale-pink mineral deposit, like the salt on the lip of a pickled jar, left behind by the evaporating seep-water. Half-submerged in the wet moss around these rims are hundreds of small, grey stones, each no larger than a plum, worn smooth by water and arranged in loose, crescent-shaped heaps. 

Until the sun reaches a certain angle, they are stones. Then, one of the heaps will shift. A stone will uncurl, revealing that its grey surface is a thick, chalk-dusted hide, and that tucked beneath it are four spindly, multi-jointed limbs that end not in claws, but in flat, grey-pink pads the size and shape of lentil beans.

They are the *plumb-warden*—or simply the *listeners*—a colony of blind, cold-blooded quadrupeds that have occupied the karst pipes beneath this sinkhole since before the roof of the cave collapsed to let in the sky. 

A listener is roughly the size and weight of a large bullfrog, though its body is shaped more like a leather pouch filled with lead shot. Its spine is sharply curved, giving it a permanent hump that mimics the river cobbles the harvester brings from below. It has no eyes—only two smooth, unbroken plates of bone where sockets might have been—and its nostrils are tiny, valved slits placed high on the back of its neck. Its entire head is little more than a housing for a massive, drum-like tympanum on either side of its jaw, each covered in a membrane so thin and taut that the blue-black veins beneath it are visible in the strong sun.

They are creatures of the pipes, born in the dark, flooded galleries fifty feet below the moss. Down there, in the absolute blackness where the water falls, they cling to the wet walls of the vertical flutes. They do not hunt by sight or smell; they hunt by resonance. 

The subterranean pipes are not merely drains; they are larders. The warm, damp air rising from the sinkhole acts as a thermal beacon on the barren plateau above, drawing in high-altitude insects—fat, clumsy timber-moths, wind-blown grasshoppers, and heavy, metallic beetles that drift over the limestone lip and drop into the windless calm of the bowl. Stunned by the sudden humidity or exhausted by their flight, these insects land on the slick liverworts or the bronze moss, lose their footing, and are washed by the constant, gentle trickle of the seep-water down into the stone flutes.

A listener spends ninety percent of its life hanging head-down inside a narrow chimney, three or four feet below the mossy floor, its four padded feet pressed firmly into the dry-wet boundary of the limestone. It is listening to the music of the sinkhole. To a listener, the *clop, tung, plock, boom* is not an erratic racket; it is a three-dimensional map of the rock. Each pipe has a voice, a specific pitch determined by its width and the depth of the water basin at its bottom. 

When a beetle washes over the lip of a flute, the sound of the water changes. A tiny droplet hits a chitinous wing-case instead of stone—a dry *tick* amidst the hollow *plocks*. Before the insect has fallen ten inches into the dark, a grey tongue, long as a finger and tipped with a sticky, muscular cup, whips out from a crevice in the pipe wall, plucks the insect from the air, and draws it back into the stone with a wet, muted *snap*.

Because their metabolism is nearly as slow as that of the glass-snails, one large moth will feed a listener for a month. When they are full, they climb up into the warm light of the basin to digest, looking for all the world like scatterings of grey gravel around the vents. 

Over generations, however, their habits have grown strange, warped by the acoustics of the open bowl and the presence of the bladder-plants.

The listeners are addicted to the low, brassy resonance of the bursting bladders. When a bladder reaches its final days of swelling, its taut skin begins to hum in sympathetic vibration with the deep *booms* coming from the floor pipes. Two or three days before a bladder is ready to split, the listeners begin to migrate across the open aisles of bronze moss. They do not hop or run; they move with a slow, mechanical, clockwork creep, lifting one leg at a time, setting the flat pad down without a sound, freezing for five minutes, and then lifting the next. It takes a listener three hours to cross twenty feet of moss.

By the time the bladder is taut enough to burst, twenty or thirty of the grey creatures will have gathered around it. They climb atop the ring of river cobbles placed there by the unseen harvester, becoming indistinguishable from the stones they sit upon. They press their wide, flat chests and their oversized jaw-drums directly against the warm, yellow skin of the plant, soaking in the subterranean vibrations that the gas-filled globe amplifies.

When the sun hits its zenith and the bladder splits—either along its natural seam or against the sharp underside of the harvester’s suspended stone maul—the gunshot crack does not scatter them. Instead, as the heavy, iron-smelling gas rushes out in that long, brassy sigh, the listeners go into a brief, frantic torpor. The heavy gas is mildly narcotic to them, and the deafening vibration is their mating call. For the ten minutes that the warm, mineral-rich vapor hangs low over the moss before dissipating into the upper air, the grey stones come furiously alive. They scramble over the collapsing, wet skin of the ruptured plant, pressing their throats together and emitting a chorus of clicks that sound precisely like two pieces of flint being struck together—*tink, tink, tink-tink*—before the gas thins, the heat takes hold of them again, and they slowly, mechanically creep back toward the dark mouths of their pipes.

There is another resident here, though it is never seen in the light, and its relationship with the harvester is much older.

If you were to go to one of the seven giant pillar-ferns and press your hands hard against the barrel-thick trunk, the rust-red fibrous hair would yield five or six inches, weeping warm, tea-colored water over your wrists. Deep inside that thick mat of wet hair, where the sun never reaches and the temperature never varies by even half a degree, the fern trunk is hollow—not by nature, but because it has been systematically chewed out over decades.

Living within the waterlogged insulation of the central fern—the largest one, whose fronds cast their lace-shadow directly over the stone ledge where the antler picks are stored—is the *Spindle-Mother*. 

She is an ancient, solitary crustacean, related to the woodlice that live under the plateau’s flat rocks, though she has grown to the size of a sucking pig in the wet, mineral-rich sanctuary of the sinkhole. Her shell is not grey, but the exact deep, fibrous rust-red of the fern hair, and from the joints of her fourteen segmented plates grow tufts of real moss and liverwort, making her a walking clump of the basin's floor. Her underside is pale, soft, and translucent, crowded with hundreds of feathery gills that wave constantly in the wet hair of the fern, drinking the oxygen from the seep-water.

She never leaves the ferns, except on the moonless nights when the high plateau above is freezing and the warm air of the sinkhole turns into a thick, blinding fog that fills the stone bowl to the brim. Then, when the acoustic pipes below are muffled by the mist, she descends the trunk of her fern—a slow, heavy, rustling descent that takes an hour—and walks across the bronze moss to the weeping walls. 

She is what keeps the garden sparse. With a pair of heavy, calcified mandibles that move like shears, she clips off every new pillar-fern shoot that dares to sprout in the open aisles, and she prunes back the creeping roots of the bladder-plants so they do not choke the water-vents. She is not doing this out of neatness; the tender, silica-rich shoots are the only food soft enough for her worn mouthparts, and the clear, brittle shells of the glass-snails—which she occasionally plucks from the fern trunks and crunches down like sugar-glass—provide the calcium she needs to molt her massive, moss-grown carapace once every seven years.

The person who comes down from the plateau knows she is there. 

In fact, the tripod of willow wands and the ring of river cobbles around the bladders are not just tools for harvesting leather; they are a negotiation. The Spindle-Mother will eat a young bladder-plant down to the root-net if she finds it in her path. She cannot, however, abide the touch of the smooth, dry river cobbles from the valley below, which lack the sharp, chalky taste of the native limestone and feel cold and dead to her sensitive antennae. By ringing a chosen bladder with those foreign stones, the harvester marks it as off-limits; the great crustacean will reach the ring of cobbles in the dark, tap them once with her three-foot feelers, and turn lumberingly aside to find an unmarked plant to graze upon.

In return, when the harvester climbs down into the bowl—lowering themselves by a thick braided rope looped around a boulder on the rim above—they never touch the central fern. More than that: on the dry ledge beside the green-stained antler picks, there is always a small, neat pile of crushed bone—the marrow-bones of the plateau elk, smashed with the stone maul and left on the rock. 

In the wet fog of the night, the Spindle-Mother climbs the slick limestone wall, her fourteen hooked feet finding purchase in the very crescent-shaped scars left by the flint-mining picks, and scrapes the bone-meal into her mouth, taking in the phosphorus that the limestone water lacks. 

By morning, when the blank blue sky returns and burns the fog away in minutes, she is gone, wedged back into the dark, wet heart of the giant fern, three feet above the moss. Nothing moves in the bright, hot bowl. A glass-snail inches toward the sun, its grey heart contracting once; a drop of water falls into the dark and goes *tung* like a wooden mallet on a gourd; and around the base of the swelling yellow bladders, twenty grey stones sit on top of twenty other grey stones, their thin, veined eardrums trembling to a music that comes entirely from beneath the earth.
