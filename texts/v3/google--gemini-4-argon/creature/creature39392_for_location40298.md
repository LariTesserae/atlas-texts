To find them, you have to stop looking for silhouettes. Nothing with a silhouette lasts more than twenty minutes on the stone. 

Instead, wait for the Harp to drop two octaves into the flat, buzzing minor third that signals a "dead-eye"—one of those terrifying, four-minute vacuums where two cyclonic walls cancel each other out. The eighty-foot whip-ferns drop to the gneiss with a wet, heavy *slap*. The sudden silence hits the inner ear like a blow, humming and pressurized. 

Then, the greased-hide hull in the glacial groove unseals from the bottom. 

What comes out does not stand up. Even in the dead calm, their posture is an acknowledgement of the ceiling that is about to fall back down on them. They move like river otters or heavy lizards—chest to the rock, elbows and knees splayed wide to the sides, pelvis tilted so their center of gravity never rises more than ten inches off the soapy gneiss. 

They are human, though their proportions have been renegotiated by ten thousand years of horizontal gravity. They have the dense, bowed limb-bones and massive collarbones of dwarves, wrapped in thick, smooth layers of subcutaneous fat that pads their ribs and organs against the constant, low-frequency battering of the rock. Their joints are loose, almost double-jointed at the hip and shoulder, allowing them to flatten completely against the stone like a dropped hide. A child of eight has the grip strength of a lowland blacksmith; their fingers and toes are short, broad, and permanently curved inward like climbing hooks, the nails horn-thick and worn flat at the tips from scrabbling at hairline fractures in the quartzite.

They are completely hairless. Hair in the spill-forests is a snare; a braided queue caught in a *sail-vine* during a directional snap will yank a neck out of its socket before a knife can be drawn. From scalp to heel, their skin is polished to a slick, copper-brown sheen with the rendered mucilage of the bladder-kelp mixed with skink-crab oil. It smells sharply of pennyroyal and old fat, and it makes them nearly impossible to grab. Even their ears are modified—not cut away, which would invite infection in the warm, wet air, but bound flat against the mastoid bone in infancy with tight strips of gut until the cartilage fuses to the skull, leaving only a narrow, downward-facing slit plugged deep with a malleable paste of tallow, ground mica, and heavy pith.

When they move across the open stone during the lull, they go in "chains." Three or four of them, linked waist-to-waist by thick, braided umbilical cords of cured sail-vine core, skittering at a startling, crab-like gallop. 

They do not speak. The plateau took their voices epochs ago; to open your mouth in a six-knot thermal shear is to have your lungs over-inflated with hot, misty air until the alveoli rupture, or to have the wind tear the words from your lips before the person six inches from your face can hear them. Their faces have grown quiet and wide-jawed, their noses flat and equipped with heavy, muscular nostrils that can seal shut like a seal’s when the wind reverses and sucks the breath from the throat. 

Instead, they talk through the cords, and through the rock. 

Watch the lead runner—an older woman whose sternum and kneecaps are calloused into grey, insensitive pads of horn the thickness of boot-sole. She stops at the edge of a root-fissure, her splayed fingers pressed flat against the vibrating gneiss. She isn't listening with her ears; she is reading the Harp through her palms and her jawbone. The stumpy stone monoliths are wedged directly into the deep fault-lines of the plateau; when the wind plays them, the stone itself hums with the specific frequency of that aperture. She feels the *C-sharp* of an incoming eastern shear-wall shivering through the quartzite half a mile before the air actually arrives. 

She gives two sharp, rhythmic tugs on the waist-cord—*anchor, east-by-southeast*—and the chain drops. 

They do not run back to the bunker. There isn't time. Each runner pulls a *bight-wedge* from their belt—a curved, asymmetrical cam carved from the dense, iron-hard jawbone of a lowland whale or ancient timber hauled up the cliffs five generations ago. With one practiced, fluid motion, they jam the wedge into a water-worn dimple or a microscopic vein of softer spar in the gneiss, twist it until the wood bites and swells in the dampness, clip their waist-harness to the thong, and tuck their limbs underneath their bellies. They pull a heavy, semi-rigid cowl of layered kelp-leather over their backs, hooking the front edge under their chins and sealing the rim to the greasy rock with a smear of heavy sap from a pouch at their hip.

They become bumps on the stone. 

Thirty seconds later, the world ends again. The air returns with the sound of a mountain splitting in half. Three hundred tons of green whip-fern and thorny cable-moss lift fifty feet into the sunny sky, hang for one terrible heartbeat against the pale blue dome, and then slam down across the backs of the crouched chain with the force of a falling building. 

The vines shriek over their greased cowls, tearing at them, burying them ten feet deep in a writhing, emerald dark that smells of bruised mint and ozone. Inside their personal cocoons, the people do not tense; tensing breaks bones. They go limp, breathing the small pocket of warm air trapped between their bellies and the stone, letting their ribcages flex and roll as the two-inch-thick stems of the sail-vines hammer across their spines. 

This is where their real work happens. Not in the lulls, which are merely for shifting position, but *underneath* the thrashing canopy.

Once the wind settles into its new direction—blowing hard and steady from the east for the next twenty minutes—a strange, roaring calm establishes itself in the bottom six inches of the world. The heavy mat of foliage, pinned down by the gale passing over its back, forms a turbulent, emerald roof. Beneath it, among the wrist-thick stems and the sliding, woody knuckles of the roots, is the *under-drift*. 

The people unseal their cowls and crawl through this green, screaming twilight like ticks through fur. 

Here, the light is a frantic, strobe-lit green as the canopy gaps and slams shut overhead. The noise is no longer a sharp crack, but a deafening, oceanic rumble that vibrates in the teeth. Moving on bellies and elbows, dragging short, hook-billed knives made of cold-hammered native copper or dense dark horn, they harvest the forest from its underside. 

They do not farm; you cannot tell a spill-forest what to do. They *prune* and they *trap*. 

They slice the swollen, translucent catching-sacs where they hang in the lee of the main stems, sucking the warm, copper-tasting jelly straight into leather bladders before the wind can atomize it. They follow the grooves in the rock where the skink-crabs shelter, flipping the coin-flat creatures out of the boundary layer with bone pries and snapping their cartilage legs into net-bags. 

More importantly, they set the *snatch-lines*. 

Because the kites—those great, ribless sheets of muscle and skin riding the upper shears—never land, the people must fish for the sky. During a steady blow, two hunters will crawl to the very edge of a whip-fern mat, where the eighty-foot fronds are streaming out horizontally over an empty expanse of polished stone. Using heavy rawhide lashings, they bind a three-pronged gorge of fire-hardened ironwood to the tip of a massive, thrashing fern-frond, baiting it with a fist-sized, iridescent beetle whose shell they have cracked just enough to leak scent into the updraft. 

Then they crawl back under the mat, holding a braided tether line that runs out to the frond-tip, and wait, their chins pressed to the stone. 

When the wind reverses again, the eighty-foot fern does not just slide across the rock; it *whips* upward in a hundred-mile-an-hour arc, flinging the baited gorge eighty feet into the chaotic shear-zone where the kites hunt. If a kite drops to skim the canopy and takes the beetle at the apex of the snap, the downward crash of the five-ton plant mat drags the creature out of the upper currents and slams it onto the bare gneiss before its six-yard wingspan can catch a thermal. 

The hunters feel the convulsive *thrump-thrump* on the tether line. They do not go out into the open gale to get it—that would be suicide. They simply lock the tether around a stone piton and let the plateau finish the job. Within ten minutes, the shifting wind and the thrashing vines have rolled the kite into a tight, bruised cylinder of meat and membrane, dragging it right up to the lip of the fissure where the hunters can haul it into the under-drift and butcher it by touch.

Inside the hulls—the overturned-boat shelters—life has the close, greasy, rhythmic intimacy of a whale’s belly. 

A hull holds six to eight people, usually an older matriarch whose joints have finally stiffened from decades of barometric bruising, two or three adults of both sexes, and a litter of small, round-bellied children. There is no fire; fire requires smoke-holes, and a smoke-hole in a shelter on the plateau would turn the interior into a vacuum-pump that would suck the lungs out of a sleeping infant, or else fill the bunker with choking soot in a downdraft. 

Instead, they have *rot-pots*. Recessed deep into the glacial groove beneath the center of the floor is a stone-lined pit packed with chopped bladder-kelp, crushed beetle-carapaces, and kite-offal. The bacterial fermentation in the warm, damp air is intense and rapid, generating a steady, wet heat that keeps the hull at ninety degrees even when the high-altitude night drops the sky above the thermal chimney to freezing. The smell inside is staggering to an outsider—a thick, ammoniacal soup of fermenting greens, rancid plant-grease, copper-sap, and unwashed skin—but to them, it is the smell of stillness. 

In the pitch dark of the hull, while the whip-ferns beat a relentless, frantic tattoo against the greased ironwood ribs three inches above their heads, the people finally unplug their ears. 

They do not speak with voices here, either; their vocal cords are atrophied, capable only of wet clicks, low grunts of effort, and a high, nasal humming used to soothe infants. Instead, they lie in a tight, tangled pile of greased limbs, skin-to-skin, and *tap*. 

Their language is tactile, percussive, and immensely complex. A thumb-strike against the collarbone is a noun; a three-fingered roll across the ribs is a tense; a sharp pinch to the deltoid modifies the direction of the wind being discussed. An entire four-hour story about a hunting chain that drifted three miles south during a five-day tempest can be told across the dark hull without a single sound being made, passing from the storyteller’s calloused palms to the shins and shoulders of the two people touching her, who simultaneously tap it onward to the bellies and backs of the others, until the whole pile of bodies is shivering with the narrative like a single organism thinking to itself.

They have no concept of "up" or "down" in their cosmology, only *Hold* and *Let-Go*. 

To be good, to be moral, to be sane, is to have *weight*—to be dense, well-greased, securely wedged into the crack, offering no purchase to the invisible devil that rules the world. Insanity, which strikes them occasionally when the barometric pressure drops too fast for too many days in a row, is called *ballooning*. A person who goes mad on the plateau does not rave; they simply begin to unhook their tethers. They wipe the grease from their skin with dry moss so the vines will catch them, and during a directional shift, they do not drop their chests to the rock. They stand up. 

It is the only time a human being ever stands fully upright on the plateau. The others watching from the narrow, horn-paned slits of the hulls describe it on each other’s ribs with a mixture of horror and secret, terrible envy: how strange and tall a man looks when he unfolds his bowed legs and raises his arms above his head; how white the soles of his feet are as the eighty-mile-an-hour thermal catches him under the armpits; and how quickly he becomes small against the clear, sunny blue of the sky, tumbling eastward over the thrashing green sea like a scrap of dried kelp, never touching the stone again.
