If you look closely at one of the high, sunny colonnades—perhaps fifty feet down, where the water is like chilled yellow wine and the booming of the hound-toads vibrates in the teeth—you will eventually notice that one of the smooth, slate-colored buttresses has two small, dark eyes. 

After a few minutes, the buttress will slowly unpeel itself from the wall.

The builders of the city have not vanished; they have simply decelerated to match the tempo of their timber. They are the *Vell*, though they have no vocal cords to speak a name that uses the tongue or lips. They are a heavy, broad-shouldered people, roughly the stature of terrestrial humans, though their proportions have been entirely revised by ten thousand generations of four-degree water. There is nothing lean or sinewy about them. Beneath a dense, velvety epidermis the exact olive-charcoal of the polished cedar bark, they are sheathed in a four-inch layer of firm, sculptural blubber. It rounds their joints, fills in the hollows of their collarbones, and gives their faces a serene, heavy-featured fullness, like statues carved from dark tallow. 

They do not swim through the great empty spaces between the towers. To swim in snowmelt is to burn calories that the spare, vertical ecosystem can ill afford to replace. Instead, the Vell are walkers and climbers of the vertical grain. Their hands and feet are broad, spatulate paddles with tough, ridged pads on the palms and soles, tipped with flat, horn-like nails shaped like carpenter’s gouges. They move along the carved avenues and straight up the sheer faces of the hollow watchtowers with a slow, deliberate, three-point gait, gripping the micro-fissures in the wood with the patient purchase of starfish. 

Because they are slightly negatively buoyant, a Vell who wishes to cross from an upper terrace on the eastern rim to a lower plaza on the western wall does not kick across the gulf. They simply walk to the edge of the balcony, fold their heavy arms across their chest, and step off. They fall through the bright, golden gulf at a gentle, diagonal drift, turning slightly in the faint thermal current like a dropped wrench, their wide, slit-pupiled eyes taking in the whole panorama of the town over a two-minute descent before they catch a passing handrail sixty feet below with one meaty, unhurried hand.

To live in water this cold and this loud requires a particular kind of body, and a particular kind of mind.

A Vell’s torso is dominated by a massive, barrel-like ribcage that houses not lungs, but a pair of cavernous, heavily vascularized gill-chambers. They have no external frills—delicate filaments would freeze or tear against the iron-hard wood. Instead, they draw water in through wide, valved spiracles just behind their jawlines and expel it through two long, muscular vents running down their flanks beneath their arms. A stationary Vell breathes once every three or four minutes: a slow, hydraulic intake that swells their chest, followed by a thirty-second pause while their dark, copper-based blood strips the high-altitude snowmelt of its fiercely concentrated oxygen, and then a long, warm exhalation that rises through the cold water in a faint, shimmering ribbon of distortion.

They have no outer ears. In the relentless acoustic battery of the caldera—where the bronze-bell hammering of the amphibians and the million-fold *clack-clack* of the grazing snails would pulp a delicate eardrum—the Vell hear entirely through their bones. In the center of their broad foreheads, and running down the crests of their shins and forearms, the blubber thins out over wide plates of porous, resonant cartilage. 

To a Vell, sound is not a separate sense from touch; it is architecture. They do not need to look behind them to know a shoal of silver char is threading through an unglazed window; they feel the acoustic shadow the fish cast against the constant, clicking backdrop of the snails. When the afternoon sun hits the upper lake and the great braided-root suspension bridges begin their long, groaning shift, a Vell sitting three hundred feet away on the benthic floor feels the exact pitch of the creak in their shinbones, reading the structural tension of the town the way a spider reads its web.

If you watch a group of them in one of the sunken courtyards, you might think at first that they are asleep, or dead, or merely statues left behind by someone else.

Four or five of them will sit in a circle on the worn timber cobblestones, knees drawn up, for three hours at a stretch. The giant, mottled amphibians crawl right over their feet and settle on their broad shoulders, inflating their throats and letting loose booming metallic notes that make the water itself shiver. The Vell do not flinch. In fact, they lean into the amphibians, resting their heavy cheeks against the creatures’ cold, leathery flanks. The amphibians are their livestock, their hunting hounds, and their voices. 

A Vell’s own voice consists only of two heavy, flat plates of bone in the throat that can be clacked together to produce a sharp, directional *tock*—like two river stones struck underwater. By varying the tension of their throat muscles, they can change the resonance of the click, speaking to one another in a spare, rhythmic morse code that cuts cleanly underneath the deep, brassy chorus of the frogs. When they want to project a message across the entire town—to announce a structural split in a western buttress, or the culling of a char shoal—a Vell will press their thumbs into specific nerve-clusters behind a hound-toad’s tympanum, playing the animal like a living bagpipe, forcing it to drop its pitch by a third or stutter its rhythm until the echo chamber of the caldera carries the altered chord to every balcony in the basin.

Their work is so slow that a terrestrial eye cannot recognize it as labor. 

A Vell carpenter—and they are all carpenters—carries a kit of tools lashed directly to the tough skin of their thighs with cords of braided kelp: three or four wedges of dense, fossilized char-bone, a heavy stone maul without a handle, and a bundle of living benthic cedar shoots no thicker than knitting needles. 

The carpenter will climb to a joint where a causeway meets a hollow pillar. They will press their forehead against the wood and wait for ten minutes, waiting for the afternoon thermal groan. When the groan comes, the carpenter’s skull reads the microscopic gap where the mortise is pulling away from the tenon. They do not hammer a nail. Instead, they take a bone wedge, set it into the living bark three inches *above* the joint, and strike it once with the stone in their fist. *Tock.* The sound rings out, sharp and dry. The wedge stays there. It will stay there for four years, diverting the downward flow of the tree’s phloem, forcing the olive-green wood to swell and callous around the weakness, sealing the joint from the inside out. The carpenter will come back next month to tap the wedge a quarter-inch deeper. 

A house is planned when a grandmother is born, shaped while she is a child, and roofed when she is old. Because nothing rots, nothing is ever built from scratch; it is only adjusted. Every Vell knows the biography of every pillar. Through the soles of their feet, they can feel the slight, rhythmic thrum of the town’s plumbing—for the hollow cores of the watchtower-trees are not empty. By opening and closing carved wooden sluice-gates at the bottom of the lake and at the sun-warmed top, the Vell use the natural thermal convection to turn the hollow trunks into slow, vertical chimneys of circulating water, drawing nutrient-rich silt up from the volcanic floor to feed the high arches, and pulling slightly warmer surface water down into the deep, dark apartments where the infants are kept.

The infants are the only things in the caldera that move quickly, and they are rarely seen. 

For the first six years of life, a Vell is too small to retain heat in the open avenues. They live inside the great bladder-fern lanterns—or rather, in a specialized variety of the pod that the Vell cultivate inside the sheltered, windowless interiors of the largest hollow trunks. These nursery-pods are the size of carriages, tethered in clusters like translucent grapes, filled not with air, but with a dense, mucilaginous sap secreted by the cedar roots, which holds the faint warmth of the sun-chimneys long into the freezing mountain night. Inside the amber jelly of the pods, pale, tadpole-limbed children wriggle and wrestle in total silence, insulated from the deafening roar of the lake outside, watched over by elders who press their broad, flat foreheads against the outer membrane of the pods, humming low, bone-rattling lullabies into the gel.

To eat, the Vell do not chase. 

In the late afternoon, when the sun begins to slip behind the high mountain rim and the golden block of water turns a deep, heavy bottle-green, two or three Vell will walk out onto the central suspension bridges. They carry no nets or spears. They simply stand along the braided railings, spaced twenty feet apart, and open the long gill-vents along their ribs, releasing a slow, musky cloud of fermentation—a paste made from crushed translucent snails and their own shed skin, which they store in hollowed-out burls along the parapet. 

The lazy, silver-scaled char, which have hung motionless in the avenues all day, drift upward toward the scent, congregating in a dense, shimmering cylinder between the bridges. 

The Vell wait until the fish are so thick they brush against their ankles. Then, simultaneously, six carpenters strike their throat-bones together at a precise, disharmonic interval. *Tock-tock-tock.* 

The sound bounces between the closely spaced wooden walls of the avenue, creating a standing wave—an acoustic knot of sudden, sharp pressure right in the center of the shoal. A dozen heavy char simply go rigid, their swim bladders stunned by the focused concussion, and begin to sink like silver ingots toward the bottom of the street, where other Vell are already waiting on the cobblestones to gather them into baskets of woven root, never once breaking their slow, deliberate stride.

At night, the caldera goes black, and the sound changes.

Without the sun, the thermal currents die. The great wooden towers stop groaning; the mortise-and-tenon joints settle into an iron-hard lock. The cold deepens, pressing down from the star-Lit surface like a physical weight. The millions of tiny snails stop clicking and seal themselves to the wood with plugs of mucus. Even the hound-toads quiet down, only one or two booming out a lone, hollow note every quarter-hour, like a night watchman calling the time.

The Vell do not go inside to sleep in beds; they have no use for horizontal surfaces when resting. Instead, they gather in the deep, recessed alcoves of the lower pillars, fifty or a hundred of them together, wedging themselves into the carved fluted channels of the living wood. They press their thick, tallow-smooth bodies tight against one another like tiles in a mosaic, sharing their sluggish warmth, hooking their gouge-like nails into the bark so the slight night-drift won't pluck them away.

If you were to drift down through the black, freezing water with a light, you would pass whole avenues of empty, spotless timber, the great bladder-lanterns straining silently upward into the dark above you. And then your light would catch the lower colonnades, and you would see the walls themselves breathing—hundreds of broad, dark, olive-skinned backs rising and falling once every five minutes in a single, synchronized hydraulic sigh, waiting for the sun to strike the water, for the bridges to groan, and for the bells of the frogs to start again.
