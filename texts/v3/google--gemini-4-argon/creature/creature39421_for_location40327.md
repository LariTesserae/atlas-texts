If you follow one of the yellow crawlers for an afternoon—walking in its lee where the wind is broken and the air smells faintly of warm hydraulic fluid and wet iron—you will eventually notice that the shadow beneath its belly is too thick. 

When the machine pauses, lowers its chassis, and fires its blue core-laser into the diorite, the flare of light does not scatter evenly across the water beneath it. It hits a pair of boots.

They are not mountaineering boots, which would have their rubber lugs chewed to ribbons by the cast-iron scree in forty-eight hours. They are clumsy, bulbous things, bound in overlapping shingles of vulcanized rubber salvaged from peeled crawler-pads, tied on with braided wire and strips of greasy, un-tanned goat hide. Above the boots are legs wrapped in thick, sodden puttees of felted wool, and above the wool is a coat made from the grey-green, rubberized tarpaulins that the supply drones occasionally drop and lose in the high winds. 

The people who wear these coats have no official name in the company registries down in the valley, because the company registries do not acknowledge that anyone is up here. The survey is autonomous. The altitude is lethal to the unacclimatized. Yet there are perhaps forty or fifty of them scattered across the five-mile tilted table, living in the blind spots of the machines.

To survive at five thousand meters without wood, without soil, and without shelter, a human being must become a tick on the belly of something larger. For these people, the crawlers are weather, hearth, and herd.

Look closely at the undercarriage of Crawler 09 as it lifts its abdomen and vents its plume of fuel-cell exhaust. Before the white steam can be torn away by the freezing wind, two figures step directly into the scalding cloud. They stand there for ten seconds with their eyes closed and their mouths slightly open, letting the pure, warm, distilled water condense on their faces, their lashed-together goggles, and the thick, greasy wool collars around their necks. One of them holds up a wide, flat bladder made of stitched leather and lined with tin foil; it catches the hot drippings from the exhaust manifold. When the crawler’s pneumatic valves hiss—the three-second warning before a hydraulic limb lifts and swings forward—the two figures step smoothly to the left, matching the cadence of the machine’s gait with the unthinking grace of cattle egrets walking beside a plow ox.

They are short, barrel-chested people, drawn mostly from the old mining villages two thousand meters below that went bust when the automated rigs took over the high country. You can tell how long one of them has been in the basin by their hands and their eyes. Their hands are stained a permanent, deep mahogany from the tannins in the red water-algae and the black grease of the crawlers' ball-joints. Their faces, where they show between wool wraps and smeared layers of goat-tallow, are cured to the texture of smoked leather by the relentless ultraviolet glare that filters through the thin, wet clouds, and the violent flashes of the core-lasers. Every one of them has the slow, deliberate movements of people whose blood has thickened to the consistency of dark syrup to carry enough oxygen through the thin air. They never run. If a man runs up here, his lungs fill with fluid by nightfall and he drowns in his own chest before morning.

Instead, they drift. 

Because there is no wood to burn and no dry dung, fire is a myth in the basin. Heat is stolen. Every crawler has a bank of radiator fins along its rear quarter where the waste heat from the hydrogen cells is bled off into the mist. At night, when the crawlers switch to low-power sonar mapping and slow their pace to three steps an hour, the people climb up the slick, hydraulic knees like spiders climbing a wall. They have iron hooks lashed to their wrists. They sling hammocks of braided wire and goat-hide directly beneath the radiator fins, suspended four feet above the freezing black water. Four or five of them will wedge themselves together into the warm, humming recess behind the rear legs, breathing the smell of ozone and hot paint, listening to the deep, rhythmic *thrum-click* of the internal gyroscopes through the steel plating against their backs. As the crawler takes a slow step in the pitch dark, the hammocks swing gently in unison, rockabye- fashion, over the razor-sharp basalt below.

They do not hunt the way people in the valleys hunt. Arrows and bullets are useless in a twenty-knot crosswind when you cannot afford the calories to chase a wounded animal over glass-sharp stone. Instead, they use the geometry of the rock and the routine of the machines.

When a crawler stops to drill, the laser bores a neat, two-inch cylinder into the stone, vaporizing the rock to test its spectrographic signature, then sucks the dust up into a collection tube. Sometimes, however, the laser hits a pocket of quartz or high-grade iron sulfide that fractures rather than vaporizes, and the machine's mechanical claw drops the core-sample after scanning it. 

The people wait for these. A core-sample of dense basalt, two inches thick and eight inches long, still blistering hot from the laser, is a treasure. They catch it in a scrap of wet hide before it hits the water and cracks. Tucked inside a coat, that stone will keep a woman’s ribs warm for three hours. Once it cools, it is a weapon. 

A hunter will take three or four of these heavy, perfectly cylindrical stone rods and walk out along the top of a fault-scarp where the grey mountain cats slink through the mist. The hunter does not stalk; he simply sits on a cushion of hard moss campion, pulls his grey tarp over his knees, and becomes another block of diorite. He waits until a flock of sandpipers comes fluttering in behind a crawler to feed on the stunned midge larvae. He waits until the flat-headed cat comes sliding down the stone trough to take a bird. When the cat is directly below him, he does not throw the core-sample—throwing takes too much shoulder, too much breath. He simply drops it. Eight pounds of laser-cut basalt falling twelve feet is enough to break a spine without making a sound louder than the clack of a goat's horn.

Nothing of the cat is wasted, nor of the heavy, wooly ungulates they take by stringing salvaged steel sensor-cables across the narrow fault-steps at knee height. The meat is eaten raw, sliced paper-thin with flakes of sheared basalt that are sharper than any steel knife, and warmed for twenty minutes against the crawler's exhaust pipes until the fat turns translucent. The grease is the most important part. They drink it warm, and they rub it into every square inch of their skin, their clothes, and their boots. Without the grease, the perpetual weeping mist gets in, and once the water touches the skin at five thousand meters, the body starts a countdown it cannot stop.

To fill their bellies between kills, they eat the same thing the birds eat, though they let the birds do the work, or they use the wool. A child—there are a few children here, twelve or thirteen years old, silent and wide-eyed as owls, who have forgotten the color of trees—will take a freshly sheared fleece from one of the pale mountain goats and peg it down in a shallow sheet of runoff downstream from a crawler. When the sonic probe fires and stuns the billions of black midge larvae in the pool, the current carries the twitching, protein-rich mass down the flat step of rock and right into the fleece. In ten minutes, the wool is black and heavy as lead with trapped larvae. The child rolls the fleece up like a carpet, wrings the water out, and scrapes the paste into leather pouches with a piece of split horn. Mixed with bitter, crushed saxifrage leaves to stave off the scurvy and dried on the warm top-plates of a machine, it makes a black, crumbly cake that tastes like pond-bottom and iron, and keeps the heart beating.

They have a strange, lopsided religion, or at least a set of manners, regarding the crawlers. They know the machines are not alive—most of these people used to repair heavy equipment, or their fathers did—yet it is impossible to live inside the warmth of a thing for three winters without giving it a personality. 

Crawler 04 has a slightly bent front-right actuator that makes it pause a fraction of a second longer on its down-step; they call it the Deacon, and the older people prefer to follow it because its pace is forgiving on arthritic knees. Crawler 12 runs two degrees hotter than the others because of a fouled coolant pump; that one belongs to the women with the youngest kids, and no one else crowds its radiator fins at night. 

They take care of the machines, in a quiet, invisible way that the telemetry engineers down in the capital never quite understand. If a sharp slab of diorite shifts under a crawler’s foot and threatens to pinch a hydraulic line, four men with iron pry-bars will materialize out of the fog, heave the stone clear while the leg is lifted, and vanish before the belly-cameras can pan around to register an obstruction. If the optical lenses on a machine’s abdomen become crusted with mineral scale from the steamed sulfur, a small, grease-shiny hand will reach up during the five-second cooldown cycle and wipe the glass clean with a rag of soft belly-fur. 

They do not do this out of gratitude. They do it because if a crawler breaks down, the heavy-lift helicopters will come up from the world below to retrieve it. And if the helicopters come, with their thermal cameras and their company security guards, the people will be seen.

There is a reason they are up here, after all, breathing thin mist and eating midge-paste in the blue flare of the lasers. Every one of them has a name written on a piece of paper down in the warm, oxygen-thick valleys—a debt-bond, a conscription notice, a warrant from when the mines closed and the towns burned. Down there, the air is warm, the soil is soft, and a person belongs to whoever owns the land they stand on. 

Up here, the rock is too young to belong to anyone. It has not even figured out how to be a mountain yet. 

So when the afternoon darkens toward evening, and the wind picks up, driving the ankle-deep water into tiny, freezing whitecaps across the black stone, you will see them gathering. A man steps out from behind a sheer wall of diorite, his coat glistening with mist, a brace of dead sandpipers hanging from his belt by their necks. He does not wave or call out to the two women already walking beside Crawler 09; sound is a waste of breath, and the wind would take it anyway. He simply falls into step behind the massive, yellow rear leg, his rubber-shingled boots splashing softly in the stunned, milky water left behind by the probe. 

One of the women reaches into her coat, pulls out a still-warm cylinder of laser-cut stone, and presses it into his wet hands without looking at him. He nods once, tucks the stone against his chest, and reaches up with his iron wrist-hook to catch the maintenance rung as the machine heaves itself up the next jagged step of the fault line, climbing slowly and blindly into the cloud.
