To find the rest of the Shelf’s inhabitants, you have to stop looking for animals and start looking for components. In a system where the environment has solved the problem of energy distribution, the line between an organism and a spare part has had ten million years to dissolve. 

Look first at the black vulcanized housings of the turbines themselves. Every pump-nodule has four intake vents and four exhaust ports, each three feet wide, guarded by grates of cross-hatched bone to keep larger debris from fouling the impellers. Yet the bearings inside those chambers—spinning four hundred times a minute under immense lateral drag—sometimes develop micro-fissures before the tree is ready to shed them. When a bearing begins to wobble, the pitch of the nodule’s thirty-hertz hum shifts upward by a fraction of a semitone.

Within minutes of that tonal shift, the **Lappers** arrive. 

From a distance, a Lapper in transit looks like a strip of wet buckskin torn loose from a hide, tumbling end-over-end down the four-knot current. They are boneless, blind, radially flattened worms—closer to a tongue or a heavy muscular gasket than anything segmented—growing up to seven feet long and less than an inch thick. They have no eyes because there is nothing they need to see; their entire skin is an acoustic tympanum tuned exclusively to the dissonance of a failing turbine.

When a Lapper catches the harmonic wobble of a bad bearing, it steers itself by curling its trailing edge like a rudder, planing across the laminar flow until it slams against the black cellulose housing of the faulty junction. It does not fight the suction of the intake vent; it pours itself through the bone grate like heavy grease.

Inside the roaring dark of the turbine chamber, where the hydrostatic pressure and sheer velocity would shred a fish in half a second, the Lapper is home. Its ventral surface is lined with thousands of microscopic, hexagonal suction cups that lock onto the stationary inner wall of the housing. Its dorsal surface—the side facing the spinning, bone-white impeller—is a dense shag of flexible, diamond-hard denticles lubricated by a thick mucus rich in metallic salts. 

The worm wraps itself around the wobbling shaft like a living brake pad. As the impeller spins against its back at forty miles an hour, the Lapper does not get torn apart; it *grinds*. It takes the kinetic shock of the unbalanced rotor into its own dense, rubbery musculature, turning the destructive vibration into heat, while its denticles methodically abrade the high spots off the bone bearing. It stays there for weeks, dining on the microscopic slurry of ground bone-meal and warm, leaking sap that washes over its oral grooves, until the shaft is true again and the thirty-hertz chord drops back into tune. When the vibration ceases, the Lapper’s suction cups automatically relax, and it is blown out the exhaust port like a spent casing, tumbling eastward in the blue void until another turbine five miles down-current begins to sing out of key.

***

Further out, in the three-mile spans of empty water between the trunks, hang the **Kites**. 

You rarely see a Kite whole at first; because of the terrifying clarity of the water, you see its tether miles before you see the beast. Drawn taut across the current from the vertical columns are single, glistening threads of spun collagen, no thicker than piano wire, stretching downstream for half a mile or more. 

At the end of each wire floats a creature that has turned the four-knot current into a stationary pantry. A Kite is a colonial siphonophore that has abandoned the soft, gelatinous architecture of the deep ocean for something closer to aerospace engineering. Its main body is a rigid, delta-winged hydroplane of translucent cartilage, twelve feet across, cambered precisely so that the eastward flow pushes it neither up nor down, holding it suspended at an exact depth with zero muscular effort. 

The Kite does not hunt. In water this clean, there is nothing to catch except what the forest itself sheds: the microscopic dust of worn turbine bearings, flakes of copper-rich bark, the occasional dead silica-crab tumbling toward the abyssal floor, and the steady mist of glucose-lubricant washed off the runners. To harvest this thin broth, the Kite drops a curtain of fine, feathered gill-curtains twenty feet below its belly. 

Because the water is so clear and the current so rapid, a standard filter-feeder would have its gills clogged or torn away. The Kite solves this with electricity. Its half-mile tether isn't anchored to the neutral deadwood of the columns; it is spliced directly into the violet corona of a silver nerve-trunk. The collagen wire has a core of liquid mercury-amalgam, scavenged from the mineral scale of the deep vents. The Kite runs a continuous, low-amperage charge down the length of its tether and out into its gill-curtains. 

As the water sweeps past, the ionized gills don't strain the water; they electrostatically precipitate the dissolved minerals and stray sugar-molecules out of the flow, plating them onto the feathery filaments like chrome onto a bumper. Every six hours, the Kite briefly shorts its own circuit. A tiny blue spark runs down the gill-curtains, reversing the polarity; the accumulated crust of nutrient-scale flakes off in a neat sheet and is sucked into the ventral siphon resting directly behind the wing’s trailing edge. 

In the deep blue rooms between the columns, hundreds of these pale delta-wings hang at staggered depths on their invisible wires, motionless as gliders frozen in a wind tunnel, flashing once every six hours with a quiet, rhythmic *pop* of blue light as they swallow their catch.

***

And then there are the **Linemen**. 

If you look up toward the horizontal stolons—those freight-train-thick cables of wood and copper that lock the grid together every three hundred yards—you will occasionally see what looks like a second, smaller cable braided loosely around the silver nerve-sheath on the underside, inching westward against the current.

The Linemen are the closest thing the Shelf has to a tragedy, because they are the only things here that appear to have a memory, though they have no brains to keep it in. 

Biologically, a Lineman is a social assemblage of segmented, polychaete-like bristles, each individual no larger than a human finger, covered in overlapping plates of tarnished copper and horn. Alone, an individual bristle is helpless—if it loses its grip on the bark, the current takes it, and it has no way to swim back. So they do not live alone. Five or six hundred thousand of them hook their barbed parapodia together to form a single, braided rope forty feet long and thick as a hawser. 

This super-organism moves like a caterpillar tractor, the individuals on the outside rotating inward against the bark to pull the whole mass forward, inch by agonizing inch, against the four-knot drag. They are the grid’s insulators and splicers.

Sometimes, when a high-voltage nerve-trunk arcs across a scarred node with that twenty-mile metallic *crack*, the blast chars the wood and vaporizes a six-inch gap in the silver-sheathed nerve. The circuit breaks. Downstream from the break, the turbines begin to lose their synchronization; the local magnetic field collapses; the chandelier-shoals of zinc fish lose their induction-lock and begin to tumble away in silver clouds; the Kites go dark. 

The Lineman-rope feels the drop in the local magnetic field from miles away. Slowly, fighting the relentless eastward push of the ocean, the braided colony hauls itself along the stolon toward the dead zone. When it reaches the charred gap in the nerve-cable, it does not repair the wood. Instead, the colony stretches itself across the burn. 

The leading edge of the rope anchors into the live silver bark on the western side; the trailing edge anchors into the dead stub on the eastern side. And then the fifty thousand individuals in the middle clamp down their copper dorsal plates, locking into a solid, metallic conduit, and *take the load*.

The violet corona leaps into them. Ten thousand volts of botanical current slam through the braided bodies of the worms. 

They do not burn. Their blood is an electrolyte slurry engineered to carry the amperage with minimal resistance, and the forty-four-degree water rushing past them at four knots strips away the ferocious Joule heating before their proteins can denature. For the next fifty years—or a hundred, however long it takes the glacial metabolism of the *Baryxylon* to grow a new bridge of silver tissue underneath them—the Linemen simply stay there, fused into a living jumper cable. 

The individuals on the outside of the braid, their copper plates glowing with a faint, eerie Cherenkov violet, open tiny gill-plumes to catch the glucose lubricant drifting down from the runner-pores upstream, passing the sugars inward through cell-to-cell junctions to feed the brothers locked in the white-hot core of the braid. When the tree finally completes its own repair and the current bypasses them, the magnetic lock releases. The colony unbraids itself from the scar, sheds the dead, fused husks of the few thousand individuals at the center who took the worst of the arc, and crawls slowly back into the current, feeling through the thrumming water for the next break in the line.

***

Only at the very top, in that twelve-foot band of blinding, turbulent water between the slick green tables of the tree-crowns and the hammered-tin ceiling of the sky, does the clinical order of the Shelf fray into something more recognizable.

Here live the **Skimmers**—the only creatures on the plateau that know there is an outside.

Down in the hypostyle halls, the flow is laminar and silent save for the thirty-hertz hum. Up here, the water is a roaring white hell of standing waves, cavitation bubbles, and blinding ultraviolet light pouring through the thin mountain air. The green photosynthetic tables of the *Baryxylon* are slick as wet glass, coated in a dense hydrogel to keep the churning water from tearing the light-gathering tissues apart.

Across these green tables dart things that look like three-foot-wide iron skillets with tails. The Skimmers are heavily armored placoderms, flattened like rays, their dorsal shields made of dense, dark bone pitted like meteorites to break up the surface drag. While everything below lives on electricity and mineral scale, the Skimmers live on oxygen and raw sugar.

Where the sunlight hits the green tables, the trees produce oxygen in massive, supersaturated sheets of micro-bubbles that are immediately swept eastward by the current. The Skimmers clamp themselves to the slick edges of the crowns using ventral suction-disks, their wide, shovel-like mouths gaping into the wash to funnel the oxygen-froth over their gills. This hyper-oxygenation gives them something nothing else on the Shelf possesses: explosive, muscular speed.

They are the gardeners of the roof. Occasionally, a wind-borne spore from the dry mountains thousands of miles away, or a stray filament of wild algae, will drop out of the thin sky, punch through the surface, and try to take root on the warm, sunlit tables of the *Baryxylon*. If left alone, a mat of foreign algae would shade the tree’s photosynthetic tissue, dropping the osmotic pressure in the column below, slowing the turbines, and throwing the local grid out of phase.

The moment a microscopic patch of foreign green or brown mars the uniform jade of a crown-table, a Skimmer releases its suction lock. It does not drift; it *shoots* across the table with a whip-crack of its muscular tail, moving at thirty knots through the twelve-foot shallows, hydroplaning inches beneath the tin ceiling of the sky. It slams its iron-hard chin down onto the foreign growth, planing it off the hydrogel in a single pass with a lower jaw edged in self-sharpening flint, and locks back down onto the downstream rim before the current can sweep it off the table.

Sometimes, when a Skimmer hits a standing wave at thirty knots, its flat body catches the angle wrong and it punches right through the mirror of the surface. 

For two seconds, the fish arcs through the freezing, thin air of the twelve-thousand-foot plateau—a black iron crescent sailing beneath an empty, colorless sky, looking out over an endless, flat expanse of white-capped water that stretches from horizon to horizon without a single island, a single bird, or a single cloud. Then it slams back through the hammered tin, drops twelve feet onto the slick green roof of the engine, clamps its belly down against the four-knot flow, and goes back to work.
