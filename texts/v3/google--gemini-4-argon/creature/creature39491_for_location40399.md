To find what actually *lives* here—as opposed to what is merely grown, extruded, or hung in the air like an equation—you have to look at the seams where the architecture is about to fold. 

The tram-grubs are just batteries with faces, and the creatures suspended in the aerial lattice are less like animals and more like a single, distributed nervous system keeping its synapses three feet apart so the room doesn’t short-circuit. The true residents of the hollow are the things that have learned to navigate the *flattening*.

They are called the **Girdlers**, though they have no name for themselves because they do not use their mouths for speaking. 

At first glance, you might mistake one for a bundled-up surveyor or a nineteenth-century night-watchman who has been dropped into a barrel of starch. A Girdler stands about five feet tall, shaped roughly like a stooped man in a heavy, double-breasted Mackinaw coat and a flat cap. If you get close enough—while dodging the three-foot exclusion zones of the floating, terrier-faced eels—you realize the coat is not wool, and the Girdler is not wearing it. The creature is an upright, ambulatory gall. Its "clothing" is a dense, layered carapace of corky bark and compressed beige lichen, grown in the exact, parodic shape of working-class winterwear, complete with horn buttons that are actually the protruding, blind eyes of symbiotic snails embedded in its chest.

A Girdler has no bones. Inside that stiff, coat-shaped husk is a hydraulic tangle of yellow taproots and muscle-fibers that smell strongly of horseradish and old brass. Where a face should be, tucked beneath the brim of its woody cap, there is only a recessed, moist aperture lined with three concentric rings of grafting-knives—chitinous, self-sharpening blades that click against one another with the sound of a watchmaker’s tweezers (a sound which, due to the room's broken acoustics, usually echoes out of a drainpipe thirty yards away).

They are the only things in the cathedral that are not bound to the invisible abacus of the air or the tracks of the floor. They walk up the sheer walls, their flat, boot-shaped feet secreting a quick-setting resin that lets them stroll casually across the underside of a tenement lintel forty feet overhead.

Watch one at work. It is currently hunched on the fire escape of an ironwood foundry that is four minutes old. In its hands—which are split, calloused mittens of heavy root-stock—it holds a bundle of copper-veined ivy ripped from the baseboards. The Girdler is *splicing*. With a frantic, trembling precision, it jams the live copper-ivy into the mortar-lines between the square tubers of the brickwork. 

It is not maintaining the town; it is sabotaging its logic. 

Left to itself, the turnip-pith would simply grow, ripen, and invert back into flat leaf-veins in a closed, meaningless loop. The Girdlers are parasites of form. By grafting the wrong veins into a budding building, the Girdler forces the architecture to *mispronounce* itself. Instead of a chimney, the roofline suddenly blisters and pushes out a massive, pale cabbage the size of a hogshead, which immediately splits open to reveal a litter of damp, twitching clockwork—gears made of hardened walnut shell, springs of coiled bramble, and three or four newborn Girdlers, already dressed in tiny, dripping waistcoats of fresh cambium.

The elder Girdler does not help them out. It is already running. 

It has to run, because the textile mill ten feet to its left has reached its climax and is beginning to invert. You can tell when a Girdler has miscalculated the timing of the room by looking closely at the "wallpaper"—those sections of the wall where old factories have collapsed back into two-dimensional leaf-surfaces. Among the flat, dark green venation that used to be windowpanes and brick-courses, you will frequently see the pressed, botanical silhouette of a man in a flat cap, flattened to the thickness of gold leaf, its snail-button eyes reduced to tiny, spiral stains in the rind, waiting for the wall to cycle back into three dimensions in an hour or a week so it can pop back out like a wet sponge and resume its work.

***

Down on the floor, in the shadow of the gourd-pistons, lives something else. Something that does not climb.

If you stand very still while one of those inch-thick vertical curtains of water condenses near you, you will see a silhouette step *out* of the water-wall just before the radiator-grilles suck the moisture away. 

This is the **Sump-Bailiff**. There is only one of him at any given time, though it is never the same one for more than an hour.

He is huge—nearly eight feet tall, built with the top-heavy, barrel-chested proportions of a Victorian ironmaster, though he is made entirely of clear, stiffened mucilage and gray river-silt held together by a dense internal basketry of horsehair worms. Because he is translucent, you can see what he is carrying inside his torso: a floating, suspended collection of things that *do not belong* to the vegetable hollow. A genuine iron bolt, rusted nearly through; the jawbone of a real dog; a sodden, unreadable ledger from 1874; three brass waistcoat buttons; a lump of actual coal. 

These are the irritants—the grains of sand around which this entire subterranean pearl of a mill-town is frantically, endlessly secreting itself. 

The Sump-Bailiff moves with a slow, agonizing dignity, his gait completely out of sync with the frantic, sped-up metabolism of the room. While the smokestacks shoot upward and the cane-trams blur past with their cargo of vibrating diesel-grubs, the Bailiff takes four full seconds to lift one heavy, jelly-like foot and plant it on the bark floor. When his foot comes down, the sound is not delayed or displaced; it is the only thing in the hollow that makes a noise right where it happens—a heavy, authoritative *thud* that makes the nearest three hundred floating animals in the aerial lattice shudder in their invisible slots.

He does not eat, and he does not build. He patrols the tram-lines, bending down with a wet, creaking sigh of his internal basketry to press a massive, cold thumb against the floor wherever a spore is threatening to inflate into a water-tower or a gasometer in the middle of the tracks. When he presses the spore, he doesn't crush it; he absorbs the growth-impulse into his own gelatinous wrist, which swells briefly into the shape of a tiny, translucent mansard roof or a miniature girder before smoothing out again.

He is looking for the Girdlers. When he catches one—usually one that has just peeled itself off a wall after being flattened, still groggy and two-dimensional around the edges—he does not kill it. He simply picks it up by the scruff of its corky coat and shoves it, kicking and clicking its mouth-knives, straight into his own translucent belly, right alongside the rusted bolt and the dog’s jaw. 

There, suspended in the cold mucilage, the Girdler stops struggling. It curls up around the lump of real coal, its root-hands clutching the mineral like an icon, and slowly dissolves over forty minutes, feeding its stolen, grafted memories of the town’s layout into the Bailiff’s silt-darkened core.

***

And yet, neither the Girdlers nor the Bailiff are the apex of this place. 

If you look up—past the forty-foot tenements, past the locust-boiler things snapping voles in the dark, right up into the high, gothic vaulting of the ceiling where the foxfire arc-lamps are strung—you will notice that the three-dimensional lattice of floating animals has a hole in it.

Right at the apex of the arch, there is a sphere of empty air about twenty feet across where no eel swims and no leather-moth hangs. The abacus-beads slide around it, refusing to enter.

Hanging in the center of that gap, suspended by nothing at all, is something that looks like an overturned surveyor's theodolite tangled in a bridal veil of white root-hairs. 

It is the **Clerk of the Works**. 

It is tiny—no larger than a cat—and it is the only warm-blooded thing in the entire turnip-cathedral. You can see the heat of it rising in a faint, greasy plume that immediately condenses into a miniature vertical sheet of water above its head. Beneath the veil of white roots, it has a body like a skinned rabbit, pulsing with a terrifyingly fast heartbeat, and six long, spider-like fingers made of yellow ivory that are constantly, furiously *measuring* the dark.

It pinches the empty air between thumb and forefinger, squinting through a single, milky eye that has grown into the brass tube of the theodolite. Every time it pinches the air, a violet spark of static jumps from its ivory knuckles to the walls, and down below, a building inverts. 

The Clerk is starving. It is always starving, because nothing that grows here can nourish a thing with red blood and calcium bones. It survives entirely on the *sound* that gets lost in the room. 

Those missing decibels—the roar of the three-hundred-RPM flywheel that only made the sound of a dry leaf; the thunder of the gourd-pistons that vanished into the damp; the screams of the floating voles that only arrived at your ankles as a tiny *tink*—they do not disappear. They rise. The decoupled acoustics of the chamber float upward like marsh gas, pooling in the twenty-foot gap at the top of the vault.

The Clerk of the Works hangs in that invisible reservoir of delayed noise, its little rabbit-ribcage heaving as it inhales the un-heard racket of the mill district. If you watch it through binoculars, you can see its throat bulge as it swallows the five-minute-old roar of a loom, digesting the sheer kinetic violence of the sound to keep its blood hot. Occasionally, a drop of its sweat falls—a tiny, scalding bead of real salt water that plummets down through the cold lattice of animals. 

If that drop of sweat hits the floor before a radiator-grille can suck it sideways, the turnip-pith beneath it goes mad. Within thirty seconds, an entire cobblestone alley—complete with gas-lamps that weep glowing sap, a butcher’s shop with rows of gourd-mutton hanging on thorn-hooks, and a tiny, blind horse made of braided parsnip—erupts from the spot, fully formed, violently geometric, and completely silent, before the Sump-Bailiff comes lumbering through the dark to press his cold thumb against the horse’s forehead and fold the whole street back into the floor.
