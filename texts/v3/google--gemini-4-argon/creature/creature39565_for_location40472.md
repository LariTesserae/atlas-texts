If you stand in one of the leached trenches and wait—not for an hour, for nothing here happens in an hour, but for the fourteen-hour smear of amber light to slide across the southern sky—you will begin to notice that the silence is not empty. It is merely pitched below the threshold of the ear. The plateau is a single, three-thousand-mile sounding board of dense, uniform stone, and through the soles of your boots, you can feel the slow, planetary grind of the convoys. 

To find what else lives here, you have to stop looking at the horizon and look instead at the margins of the work. The ecology of the plateau has no room for the frantic, burning metabolism of warm-blooded things. Everything that survives in the thin, freezing air is either part of the great milling machine or a parasite upon its patience.

Look first at the dead hulls. A hollowed-out Dredger sits on the plain for millennia before another line intersects it. In a world without trees, caves, or cliffs, an empty iron-chitin shell the size of a locomotive is a mountain range. Its interior, ribbed with heavy arches of non-rusting bio-steel, is the only shelter from the shearing, static-charged wind.

Inside these hulls live the **Tappers**. 

At first glance, a Tapper looks like a spill of dark, vulcanized rubber that has frozen halfway down the interior ribs of the dead hull. They are roughly the size of a crouching dog, radially symmetrical, with six low-slung limbs that end not in claws, but in broad, cup-like pads of dense cartilage. They have no eyes—there is nothing to see in the dark of a hull, and very little worth looking at outside it—and no mouths. Instead, their undersides are fitted with a three-inch stylet of hardened, copper-tipped horn.

For twenty hours of the cycle, a Tapper does not move at all. It clamps itself to the interior ceiling of a dead hull, conserving the meager heat of its own slow, sulfuric blood. It waits for a vibration through the stone. 

Because the rock is so dense and homogenous, the heavy, hydraulic tread of a living Dredger can be felt through the slate from three miles away. The Tapper reads the intersections of these vibrations the way a spider reads its web. When a living engine passes within a few hundred yards of the dead hull, the Tapper unpeels itself from the iron ribs. It drops to the stone with a dull, rubbery thud and creeps out into the grey twilight. 

Its gait is strange, almost mechanical—a low, fluid scuttling that keeps its flat body pressed within an inch of the slick rock, beneath the bite of the wind. It intercepts the living Dredger not from the rear, where the Sifters march in their millions, but from the side. Moving at ten feet an hour, the massive engine is no harder to board than a wall. The Tapper climbs the weeping, oxidized plates of the Dredger’s flank, finds the soft, leathery seam between the dorsal armor segments, and drives its copper stylet straight into the galvanic veins beneath.

It does not drink blood. The Dredgers have no blood to speak of, only a thick, acidic electrolyte slurry and the low-voltage current trickling down from the Condenser floating above. The Tapper drinks the current. It hooks itself into the Dredger’s circuit like a living battery on a charger. For three days, while the engine creeps forward a quarter of a mile, the Tapper hangs from its flank, completely inert, its dark rubbery hide swelling slightly as its internal chemical cells recharge, simultaneously drawing off a few ounces of warm, mineral-rich fluid to replace what it has lost to the dry morning air. The Dredger does not shake it off; it has no nerves in its armor, and the loss of a few milliwatts of power is less to it than a mosquito bite to a whale. When the Tapper is full, it withdraws its needle, drops into the carpet of lead-grey Sifters below—who simply part around it like water around a stone and close ranks again—and walks the long, straight trench back to its hull.

Where there are Tappers, there are **Gleaners**, though you might look straight at one for an hour and think it is only a shadow in a trench, or a dead strip of Condenser silk.

The Gleaners are the closest thing the plateau has to a true predator, though they do not hunt by chasing. Chasing requires oxygen that the air does not have and calories the stone cannot provide. Instead, they hunt by geometry.

A Gleaner is a flat, ribbon-like ambush organism, sometimes twelve feet long and three feet wide, yet scarcely thicker than a heavy winter coat. Its dorsal side is crusted with a mosaic of grey and black mineral scales that perfectly mimic the wet, leached basalt of a Dredger’s track. When it lies flat in a trench, it is invisible. Even the afternoon Sweat pools on its back and freezes into rime at nightfall, just as it does on the stone around it.

The Gleaner’s entire life is spent at the intersections—the smooth, geometric crossroads where one Dredger’s century-old path cuts across another’s. It lies at the junction for years at a time, its metabolism dropped so low that its heart beats perhaps once an hour, a cold, viscous pump moving antifreeze-heavy ichor through a flat lattice of veins. 

It waits for the Sifters. When a Dredger’s convoy finally grinds over the intersection, the engine itself passes right over the Gleaner; the creature’s flat body is tough and compressible enough to survive the hydraulic feet, and it secretes a waxy mucus that resists the ventral acid-grate. But once the engine has passed, the Sifters come—millions of them, marching blindly in their neat, three-meter-wide column, rolling their spheres of slag. 

As the column passes over the Gleaner, the creature’s edges curl upward by half an inch, forming a shallow chute. It does not snap or thrash. It simply opens a row of horizontal slits along its midline. Hundreds of Sifters, marching in their unthinking lockstep, walk directly into the slits and are drawn down by slow, muscular ripples into the Gleaner’s flat digestive sacks. The column above never breaks stride; the gap closes instantly. Over the course of the ten hours it takes the Sifter train to pass, the Gleaner might swallow four thousand of the lead-shelled crustaceans. 

When the convoy is gone and the silence settles back over the trench, the Gleaner remains where it is. Over the next six months, it dissolves the Sifters’ soft interiors and extrudes their empty lead-chitin shells out through its lateral pores, arranging them neatly along its own edges so that it looks even more like the ball-bearing-strewn floor of the trench. 

Yet the strangest life here does not live on the stone at all. It lives thirty feet up, in the permanent, freezing draft where the Condensers float.

If you watch the pale, translucent bladders leaning in the wind like a field of lamps, you will occasionally see a dark knot halfway up one of the black silk tether-cables, or sitting directly on the crown of a bladder itself. These are the **Spindles**.

They are arachnid in geometry, though their lineage is entirely separate from anything that ever spun a web in a warm forest. A Spindle’s body is no larger than a walnut, insulated in a dense mat of pale, greasy wool harvested from the shedding fibrous skirts of the Foundries. Its eight legs, however, are four feet long, hollow, and thinner than knitting needles, forged from the same dark, copper-iron chitin as the Dredgers’ plates. 

The Spindles never touch the ground. To touch the ground during the afternoon Sweat is to risk being frozen into the rime by nightfall, and their stilt-like legs are too brittle for the stone. Instead, they spend their lives in the rigging.

A Spindle stands atop a Condenser, its eight long legs spread wide to distribute its negligible weight across the thin, pulsing membrane of the bladder. It is a creature of pure static. Fine, feathery filaments along its legs comb the shearing, high-altitude wind, gathering the ambient electrical charge just as the Condenser does. 

When the wind gusts and the great field of bladders tilts at the exact same angle across a fifty-mile basin, the Spindles release reels of ultra-fine, conductive silk from their abdomens. The wind carries these threads horizontally for half a mile, a mile, two miles, until the drifting, charged tip of the thread is naturally drawn to the opposite charge of a neighboring Condenser. 

In the dim, pewter twilight, you cannot see the threads unless the low amber bruise of the sun catches them from directly behind; then, for a few minutes, the air thirty feet above the plateau is revealed to be laced with a vast, shimmering cat’s-cradle of black wire, linking the convoy together across the miles. 

Along these high wires, the Spindles walk. They step delicately through the freezing air between the engines, their long metal legs clicking softly against the silk—the only sharp sound in a world of low rumbles. They feed by piercing the veins of the Condensers to sip the distilled fog-water and the low-voltage current, and they mate in the middle of the half-mile spans, hanging suspended over the empty slate while the wind hums a faint, sub-audible note through the taut wires around them.

Sometimes, when two Dredgers on intersecting courses pass close to one another, their tethers and the Spindles’ bridging lines become fouled. Because nothing here moves fast enough to snap a cable, the two fifty-foot bladders are slowly, inexorably drawn together over the course of a week as the engines beneath them diverge. The Spindles do not panic. They scurry along the tightening lines, cutting the silk with sharp, insulated mandibles, salvaging and eating the thread as they retreat to their home bladders before the final tension is reached.

And at the center of it all, inside the weeping yellow mounds of the Foundries, lives the only thing on the plateau that never feels the wind.

The Foundry itself is blind and brainless, a colonial furnace of muscle and smelting-guts, but within its warm, sulfuric core—where the temperature stays a constant, tropical ninety degrees while the plain outside freezes—swim the **Grafts**.

They are pale, blind, eel-like things, three feet long, with flat, paddle-shaped heads and rows of muscular suckers along their bellies. They live in the boiling, mineral-heavy baths where the larval Dredgers and Sifters are grown from fertilized buds. The Grafts are the mechanics of the blind colony. They have no intelligence of their own, only an ancient, hard-wired instinct for symmetry and repair.

When a forty-year-old Dredger docks with the Foundry, sealing its ragged, acid-worn skirts against the mound’s base, the Grafts slither out of the Foundry’s pores and into the dark, cavernous interior of the engine through its ventral grates. For the months that the Dredger sits docked, the Grafts swim through its peristaltic mills and galvanic chambers. 

Where a basalt tooth has cracked from forty years of grinding rock, a Graft clamps itself over the gap, dissolves its own tail into a calcified, mineral cement, and hardens in place, becoming the new tooth. Where an electrical vein has shorted or corroded, another Graft stretches itself across the burned gap; its nervous system, heavy with copper and silver salts, fuses with the walls of the chamber, and the creature dies into a permanent, living wire, restoring the circuit.

When the exchange is finished, the Foundry’s skirts unseal with a slow, wet sigh that freezes instantly into grey frost around the base of the mound. The rejuvenated Dredger—carrying a dozen dead Grafts fused into its stomach and wiring, a new Condenser budding from its back, and a fresh brood of ten thousand juvenile Sifters spilling from its rear vents to join the column—alters its course by three degrees. 

It drops its grate. The acid hisses softly against the damp slate. Ten feet an hour, it begins its fifty-year march toward the next ridge, leaving a fresh, pale ribbon across the dark curve of the earth, while thirty feet above, a Spindle steps out onto a single strand of black wire to test the wind.
