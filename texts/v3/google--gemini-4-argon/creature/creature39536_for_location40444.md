If you sit by the western wall and let your eyes ache until they adjust to the shadowless milk of the air, the first thing you will notice is that one of the shell-stacks is breathing.

It is six feet away from the flint basin, tucked into a shallow scallop where the wall meets the floor. Like the pile in the corner, it appears to be five or six of the broad, chalky gastropod shells nested neatly together. Look closer, and the edges do not quite align; they are laced together with dry, sinewy strands of dead wire-fern, forming a low, articulated dome. 

Beneath this borrowed carapace, folded into a knot that would break a human’s hip-sockets, is the cistern-keeper.

She—though there are three of them that share this circuit, an elder and two younger siblings, and they are all small enough to be mistaken for ten-year-old children from a distance—is asleep, waiting out the noon fever. When the light overhead thins from incandescent white to the bruised, watery violet of dusk on the scrub-plain above, the shells will give a single, dry clatter. She will unhook the toggles of horn and fern-wire, lift the cap of shells off her back, and set it down with extreme care so as not to chip the brittle rims.

What unfolds from the corner is hominid, mostly, though whittled down by thirty thousand years of dry-country parsimony into something like a leather-bound cricket. 

She stands scarcely four feet tall, her torso compact and barrel-shaped to hold moisture, her limbs long, flat-muscled, and seemingly double-jointed at the hip and shoulder. Her skin is the color of unbaked potter’s clay—a pale, dusty grey-ochre—and lacks a single pore for sweating. To sweat down here is to die in an afternoon; instead, she radiates heat through oversized, parchment-thin ears ribbed with a pale web of blood vessels, which twitch and pivot independently toward the *plock* of the water spout and the dry ticking of the crabs.

Her face is broad and flat, dominated by eyes that look entirely solid and silver-grey. She has no dark pupils to be seared by the omnidirectional glare; instead, a permanent, milky nictitating membrane stays drawn across each eye like a pair of snow-goggles, slit horizontally with a hairline aperture no wider than a grass-blade. Only when she turns her face into the dimness of a wall-crevice do the membranes slide back, revealing dark, watery eyes as huge and startled as a tarsier’s.

She does not walk upright across the travertine. Even at dusk, the center of the chamber is a heat-trap, and the stone floor remembers the sun. Instead, she moves along the perimeter in a low, fluid four-limbed gait, her weight resting on her knuckles and the outer blades of her feet. Her palms and soles are shod in thick, amber-colored horn—natural calluses grown so deep and dense they clack against the limestone like hooves, though she places them with such deliberate, slow-motion precision that she makes less noise than the crabs.

Watch her at the flint bowl. She does not pick it up and drink; lifting it might spill three drops, and three drops is twenty minutes of the wall’s labor. Instead, she lowers her flat face to the stone and extrudes long, mobile lips—tough and prehensile like a camel’s—to siphon the water cleanly from the bottom of the nodule without disturbing the sediment of gypsum dust at the rim. As she drinks, her throat clicks softly, counting swallows. She leaves a third of an inch at the bottom. Not for the next day, and not out of forgetfulness, but because a dry flint bowl cracks in the noon heat, and a wet one stays whole.

When she has drunk, she picks up the scrub-oak stick and one of the grey chert scrapers. Her hands have five fingers, though the thumb and the little finger are nearly identical in length and oppose each other across a narrow, grooved palm, giving her a grip like a pair of pliers. The slick polish on the oak stick is not from sweat or oil, which her skin does not make, but from a fine, waxy powder her skin sheds constantly—a dry talc that keeps her joints from chafing in the chalky dust.

She approaches a sleeping gastropod four feet up the curve of the wall. She does not pry it loose and kill it. That shell pile in the corner is the accumulation of forty years of natural deaths, gathered and stacked because her kind cannot bear clutter; a cluttered floor confuses the air-currents that bring the wall-sweat, and an untidy pile casts dark, hot pockets where scorpions from the surface might take shelter if they fall through a vent. 

Instead, she taps the rim of the living snail’s shell with the wooden stick—*tick, tick-tick*—mimicking the sound of another snail bumping against it. The creature reflexively retracts a fraction of an inch to seal its border, and in doing so, squeezes out a thick, clear bead of mucus and stored water along its muscular foot. With the chert scraper, she deftly slices half of this gelatinous rind away, along with a shaving of the algal crust beneath it, and pops the rubbery ribbon into her mouth. Then she taps the shell once more—a softer, duller thud with the heel of her hand—and leaves the animal alive to re-seal itself and keep grazing. She is not a hunter here; she is a milker of stones and snails.

She will spend two hours tending the trough. Using the heavy river-cobble—which her grandmother carried down from an ancient dry riverbed seven miles to the north, sixty years ago—she taps the bone wedge into a fresh seam of selenite, extending the moisture-channel by half an inch. *Tink. Tink.* She pauses for five minutes between strikes, listening to the dome above. The quartz ceiling is thin; a heavy blow at the wrong harmonic could spiderweb the lens and bring a ton of translucent sharpnel down into the basin, ruining the greenhouse forever.

Before the moon rises and the scrub-plain above grows freezing cold, she will leave. 

There is no tunnel large enough for a human to walk through, which is why the chamber looks sealed. Thirty feet up the eastern curve of the dome, hidden behind a cluster of seven giant snails that she never harvests, is a dissolution tube no wider than a badger’s sett, spiraling upward through the limestone like a corkscrew. 

She will tuck three cushions of glass-wort into a mesh bag woven from her own shed hair and wire-fern—pale, salty water-bladders for her brother and her mother, who are waiting two miles away in a cooler, dark-rock crevice where the chert is knapped. She will stack her shell-cloak back onto the pile in the corner, aligning the rims until the stack looks untouched, ancient, and slightly mournful in its neatness. She will lay the scraper and the oak stick parallel to one another beside the flint bowl, three inches apart, exactly as they were.

Then she will climb the concave wall, hooking her horny toes into microscopic pits in the limestone that no booted foot could ever find, moving at the exact, imperceptible speed of the snails around her, until she slips into the ceiling pipe and the great white room is left to the midges, the crabs, and the three-minute drop of water.
