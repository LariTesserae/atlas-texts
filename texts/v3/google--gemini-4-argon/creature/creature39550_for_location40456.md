You do not see them at first because your eyes are looking for an animal, and an animal has edges. An animal is a discrete interruption of the background. 

What lives here begins as a discrepancy in the drainage. 

You might be looking at the underside of a hogshead main, where the *Sphagnum* hangs in sodden, yard-long beards, and notice that the water isn’t dripping from the lowest point of the curve. It is running uphill, very slowly, along a glistening, amber-colored ridge that wasn't there twenty minutes ago. Or you might watch a three-foot *Monstera* leaf, trembling in the draft of a flange-leak, and realize that the heavy, rhythmic bowing of its stem is slightly out of phase with the steam-jet—that it is being drawn downward against the updraft to dip its tip into a pocket of black peat, held there for three or four minutes while something pale and gelatinous unspools from its underside into the mud, and then released with a wet, leafy *flack*.

If you wait—and you must wait for a long time, breathing through a handkerchief to cut the copper-and-peat stench of the carbonic mist, letting your boots sink to the ankles in the warm, mulched liverwort—you will begin to see the sheets.

They are roughly the color of boiled mutton tallow, though thinner, and shot through with a fine, reticulated lacework of rust-red and Verdigris-green veins. At rest, one of these sheets might cover twenty square feet of iron pipe and aerial root, draped over the topography like a wet handkerchief dropped onto a bramble bush. It has no skin, only a firm, slick meniscus that repels the falling condensation so that water droplets skitter across it like mercury on glass. Within that translucent film, things are constantly in motion: tiny flecks of flake-rust, black granules of rotted fern, and pale, milky globules travel along the red and green threads in rhythmic, tidal surges. Five minutes one way, pause, five minutes back. 

The city’s maintenance manuals from sixty years ago, if any survived in the municipal archives upstairs, would have a footnote about a "fouling organism" native to the river-silt intakes—a freshwater sponge or colonial bryozoan that occasionally got past the gravel filters and clogged the low-pressure condensers. That was the grandfather. What is in the vault now has spent four decades marinating in sixty-cycle magnetic fields, boiling mineral spirits, and carbonic acid, with no predators and nothing to do but solve the geometry of the room.

It is not a single creature, nor is it entirely a colony. It is a shifting commonwealth of four or five great "drapes," each weighing perhaps two hundred pounds, which divide, fuse, and trade material across the two-hundred-pace length of the cistern. 

Watch the northern manifold, where the six-inch feeder pipes drop down from the street-level conduits. A drape is feeding there. It has gathered itself out of its thin, resting sheet-form into a thick, braided cable of tallow-colored jelly, big around as a man’s thigh, and hauled itself up the vertical iron by sheer hydraulic turgor—pumping its own fluid into its upper end, anchoring with a gluey secretion, and drawing the trailing mass behind it. It moves at the speed of the minute hand on a large clock. 

Where it reaches the canopy, it doesn't eat the leaves. The leaves are its roof; they keep the heat in and the tallow-colored daylight out. Instead, the drape spreads itself over the *Monstera* and the philodendrons like a glazier’s putty, sealing the gaps between their overlapping edges. It is building a baffle. For three days it has been cementing six large fronds together with a hardening, chitinous mucus that dries the color of old horn. By tipping the angle of this green awning just four degrees, it has redirected the scalding, white-noise jet of a pinhole steam-leak away from a patch of dying *Ficus* roots and straight onto a rusted cast-iron stanchion. 

Why the stanchion? Look at the base of the iron pillar, down where it meets the flooded brick sluice. 

Another section of the drape—or its sister, they were the same piece of jelly last Tuesday—is wrapped around the foot of that stanchion in a dense, muscular collar. Inside the translucent collar, pressed directly against the flaking, red-oxide metal, the creature’s veins are knotted into dark, violet-black nodules the size of plums. The steam-jet above heats the top of the stanchion; the sixty-degree water in the sluice cools the bottom. Across that twenty-foot length of cast iron, a thermal differential is established. It is a crude thermocouple, and the plum-colored nodules at the base are sizzling softly, drinking the weak, steady trickle of stray galvanic current that bleeds down the metal from the humming transformer boxes on the wall. 

They do not have eyes, because there is nothing to see at twenty feet that isn't the same at two inches. Instead, they have the iron. 

Every drape in the vault has at least one long, attenuated tendril—sometimes no thicker than parcel string—dropped down through the peat mattress and welded directly to the rusted wrought-iron grating of the catwalk. Through the catwalk, they feel the whole room. When the great thermal expansion collar high in the dark reaches its threshold and *groans*, sending that low bass shiver through the metalwork, every drape in the vault flinches simultaneously. For three seconds, their slick surfaces go matte and opaque, stiffening like warm wax dropped in cold water, before relaxing back into greasy translucence as the shower of shaken droplets patters down.

They are farming the room, though they do not know they are farming. They only know that when a steam-leak blows too hard for too long, the green canopy burns away, the cold tallow-light gets in from the street shafts, and the dry air makes their meniscuses crack and bleed milky fluid. 

And so, when a flange-joint begins to fail in earnest—whistling with a high, tearing shriek that vibrates the porcelain insulators at the wrong frequency—the commonwealth convenes. 

Over the course of a week, they come out of the sluices and down from the groin-vaults. They abandon the dead leaves they were digesting in the black mud; they unhook their violet nodules from the transformer grounds. They flow along the pipes like warm, veined tallow, converging on the shrieking joint. They cannot touch the live steam—it cooks them into white, rubbery ribbons that peel off and drop into the peat—so they work from behind the pipe, in the lee of the iron. 

They bring trash with them, rolled along inside their bodies like stones in a glacier: flakes of shed *Ficus* bark, rusted-out hex nuts dropped by dead mechanics fifty years ago, mats of dead *Sphagnum*, the empty, calcified shells of river-snails that came through the sluices during the administration of three mayors ago. 

Pushing from the cold side of the pipe, three hundred pounds of blind, hydraulic jelly compresses this aggregate against the whistling flange. The outer layers of the drape are scalded white by the escaping vapor; they die, vulcanize into a tough, leathery gasket, and the living jelly behind them simply detaches from the dead skin, backs off half an inch, and secretes another layer of horn-colored mucus to seal the patch. The high, tearing shriek drops to a hiss, then to a wet, rhythmic *putt-putt-putt*, and finally to silence. The room’s temperature drops two degrees. The trembling *Monstera* leaves above the joint go still.

In the quiet that follows—which is not quiet at all, only the return of the tremendous, unhurried *shuck-shuck-plop* of the million dripping leaves and the *clonk-slosh* of the southern float-trap—the great mass on the pipe begins to thin out. 

It does not walk away; it simply loses its height, slumping outward in twenty directions at once, flowing down the braided aerial roots, slipping through the gaps in the catwalk grating to bathe in the dark, slow water of the brick channels. Within six hours, there is nothing on the mended pipe but a gray, fibrous scab the size of a dinner plate, already sprouting the first pale-green threads of *Leucobryum* moss. 

Down in the peat, three feet below the soles of your boots, a pale sheet ten yards wide settles over the silt of the sluice-floor, opens a thousand microscopic mouths on its underside, and begins to digest the black mud, waiting for the next pipe to groan.
