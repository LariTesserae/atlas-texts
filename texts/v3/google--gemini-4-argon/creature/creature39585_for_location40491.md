To find the tenants of the cleft, you have to stop looking for bodies and start looking for knots in the cabling that shouldn't be there.

At first, the shoebox-sized ceramic modules seem too small for anything but routing hardware. And for the most part, they are: they house the warm, clicking optical looms that justify the settlement’s existence. The actual inhabitants—known in the coastal registries simply as the **Splicers**, or the **Gasket-Kin**—live primarily in the one-foot vertical shafts and the cramped, shivering tolerances *between* the boxes. 

If you were to catch one in the middle of a three-knot downward flush, you would mistake it for a bundle of braided industrial whip-hoses and weighted dive-canvas that had snagged on a ceramic cleat. 

Long ago, their baseline stock might have been human, or at least derived from human salvage-crews, but a human body is a bag of gas and rigid levers, and this place hates both. The acoustic levitation arrays would turn a normal liver into soup in twenty minutes, and the three-knot reversals would snap a collarbone against the invisible glass manifolds. To live in the *thwack-thwack-thwack* of the pistons, the Splicers had to become entirely solid, entirely flexible, and entirely blind.

A Splicer is roughly five feet long when stretched out by the current, though they rarely stretch out. They are built like a length of heavy-duty hydraulic line—no wider than a man’s thigh at their thickest point—covered in a tough, matte-grey integument that feels precisely like cured vulcanized rubber. This hide is cross-hatched with thousands of tiny, shallow scars from fresh basalt and serrated copper kelp. They have no air cavities left in them at all. Their sinuses have been filled with a dense, waxy acoustic-damping gel; their lungs are reduced to a pair of muscular, bellows-like gill-pockets behind the collarbone that rhythmically gulp the cold, bright fluorocarbon from the front and jet it out through slits near the kidneys. 

Because the shadowless Tuesday-noon brightness of the fluid never dims, and because half the hazards in the cleft are refractive-matched glass that cannot be seen anyway, eyes were the first things they discarded. Where a face should be, there is a blunt, slightly flattened prow of pale cartilage, studded with rows of black, pit-organ dimples that read thermal gradients and electrical leakage. Around this prow grows a "beard" of fifty or sixty stiff, fiberglass-like whiskers, each six inches long. 

They do not swim. Swimming requires room to kick. Instead, a Splicer moves like a brittle-star wedged into a watchcase. They have four limbs, though the distinction between arm and leg has vanished; each is a multi-jointed, hyper-mobile tendon-whip ending in a three-fingered, hooked "spanner-hand" surfaced with ridged silicone pads. 

Watch one move during the six-minute lull between thermal purges: it does not look where it is going. Its whiskers sweep ahead, tapping and scraping across the invisible optical manifolds, feeling for the faint, static buzz of amber data-pulses inside the glass or the slick, treacherous trails of silicate-crust lichen. One spanner-hand hooks a hexagonal coolant pipe; the body compresses like an accordion, folding neatly around a sharp basalt tooth without touching it; two other hands lock onto a matte-white ceramic rib. When the bone-snap relay clicks and the chamber’s current violently reverses, the Splicer doesn't even flinch. Its hands lock mechanically at the wrist—a dead-man's grip that requires no muscular effort to maintain—and its grey, rubbery torso simply flags upward in the three-knot surge like a heavy piece of kelp, waiting two seconds for the turbulence to stabilize before continuing its crawl.

They are not the masters of this infrastructure; they are its symbiotes, its biological washers and living zip-ties. 

The automated extrusion-heads are good at sintering rock-dust and printing ceramic ribs, but they are stupid. They will happily print a white bracing-strut right across an intake grate, or glue an optical manifold to a shifting face of basalt so tightly that the next millimeter of tectonic creep shears the glass in half. The Splicers exist to edit the machines. 

They carry their tools grown directly into their pouches and skin-folds: ceramic bone-saws, spudgers made of cold-forged copper, and reels of conductive suture. A Splicer will wedge itself into a six-inch crawl-slot where the acoustic whine of the levitation arrays is loud enough to boil water, wrapping its rubbery body around a vibrating pipe to act as a living harmonic damper while it calmly pries a panicked, malfunctioning extrusion-head off a fiber-glass conduit. They eat what the cleft grows. While anchored by two heels to a junction box, a Splicer will use a copper scraper to shave the geometric snowflakes of silicate-lichen off the basalt—licking the electrically charged, salty crust directly from the blade—or snip the frantic, vibrating fronds of copper-wire kelp, chewing the metallic fibers into a dull paste that patches the iron-and-copper requirements of their heavy, dense blood.

They are intensely, almost aggressively solitary in their work, yet they are never out of physical contact with one another. In a space the size of a two-story townhouse packed to eighty percent capacity with pipes, the seventeen or twenty Splicers who inhabit the cleft are constantly crawling *over* and *through* each other’s territory. 

Because the liquid medium is deafening, they have no voices. Any vocalization would be drowned out by the circular-saw shriek of the levitators or the chest-punching thud of the pressure pistons. Instead, they speak by using the infrastructure itself as a sounding board. 

A Splicer wedged near the bottom Sump will take a heavy brass knuckle-duster from its belt and strike a specific hexagonal coolant pipe three times—*clink, clink-clink*. The vibration travels through the solid ceramic and metal latticework at five thousand meters per second, entirely bypassing the noisy fluid. Forty feet above, just beneath the shimmering mercury ceiling, another Splicer feels the tap in the pads of its spanner-hands, reads the subtle change in the pipe’s harmonic rattle, and taps back. They gossip in torque-settings and structural stress. They argue by slightly tightening or loosening the tension on the braided fiber-glass cables that pass through each other's sleeping-slots.

And those shoebox-sized housing modules with the iris-valves? The Splicers do use them, though not to live in. 

Every twelve hours or so, the cold fluorocarbon fluid begins to leach too much heat even from their dense, blubber-insulated cores, and the constant, bone-shaking acoustic assault begins to fray their nervous systems. A Splicer will crawl to the nearest shoebox module, dilate the heavy rubber iris, and feed itself inside nose-first, coiling its five-foot length like a garden hose into a space no bigger than a microwave. 

When the iris snaps shut, it seals out the three-knot currents and drops the acoustic roar by forty decibels. The inside of the box is unlit—the only darkness in the entire cleft—and lined with a thick, velvety mat of biological filter-sponge that drinks the luminescent particles out of the trapped water. The box sits directly atop an optical processor, so the floor is forty degrees Celsius. There, in the sudden, warm, vibrating dark, the Splicer goes limp. It unhooks its joints, lets its stiff whiskers go slack, and sleeps for three hours, curled tightly around the warm glass heart of the machine that keeps the ocean from falling on its head.
