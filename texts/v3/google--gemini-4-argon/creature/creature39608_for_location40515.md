At first, you would mistake him for a bundle of surveyor’s rods and sun-bleached drop-cloths left leaning against a low buttress. 

He is tall—or rather, he consists of enough length to be tall, though he is rarely assembled in a way that shows it all at once. His limbs are long, turned spindles of pale ash-wood, joined at the knees and elbows not by bone or hinge, but by heavy brass plumb-bobs and tightly knotted whipcord. He wears a greatcoat of stiff, unwashed canvas, its myriad pockets bulging with carpenter’s chalk, dried sage, broken tiles, and coiled twine. Where a face should be, beneath the wide, drooping brim of a woven rush hat, there is only a softly glowing lantern of oiled parchment, stretched over a wicker frame shaped roughly like a jaw and brow. Inside his head, instead of a flame, sits a single, fist-sized lump of warm ambergris that smells faintly of resin and old sweat.

He is the Clerk of the Works, though there is no master builder to report to, and no blueprint other than the courtyard itself. He did not build this place. Like the ferns and the bellows-hares, he is a consequence of it—the human-shaped remainder left over when a space is measured too carefully for too long.

When he moves, he sounds like an easel being folded up. 

He walks through the chest-high thickets of felt-leafed briar with a slow, wading deliberation. Because the geometry of the courtyard is loose, the Clerk rarely exists in a single piece. You might see his left hand—five articulated dowels stained green at the fingertips from decades of brushing against chalk-leaves—reaching down from a cedar truss twenty feet overhead to pluck a dead ceramic-beetle from a vine, while his torso and legs are fifty paces away, kneeling by the sunken flagstones, carefully placing that very same beetle into a low terracotta bowl. He does not sever himself; he simply steps through the invisible seams between the bricks and leaves parts of his stride behind until he needs them again. 

His work is the maintenance of the truce between the masonry and the thicket.

If the greenery grows too lush, it chokes the sightlines; when a sightline is completely obscured, the courtyard forgets how far apart its walls are, and the architecture begins to pinch inward. Conversely, if a wall shifts or crumbles too much, the plants die, turning to gray ash that clogs the bellows of the straw hares and blinds the boxwood lizards. 

So the Clerk tends the balance. He carries no watering can, only a flat steel mason’s square, a wooden mallet, and a horsehair brush. 

When he finds a hemp-barked shrub going yellow at the edges, he does not prune the leaves. He drops to his knees in the warm, dry undergrowth—scattering a pair of overlapping hares that drift apart like smoke—and brushes the lime-dust away from the terracotta tile at the plant’s roots. With his mallet, he gives the edge of the tile a single, sharp *tap*, driving it a sixteenth of an inch back toward true north. Instantly, the crisp yellow edges of the leaves stiffen, drinking in the restored alignment, and bleed back into deep, chalky emerald.

In the corner where the wall is thirty paces wide to the left and three to the right, the Clerk has set up a camp of sorts, though he never sleeps. There is a workbench made of two sawhorses and a door that leads nowhere when opened, laid flat. Upon it are the casualties of the courtyard’s shifting rules: a wooden lizard whose middle section got caught in a mortar joint that sealed itself too quickly, leaving the creature in two living halves that still twitch in unison; a flock of quill-birds that stepped off a scaffold at the wrong angle and fell into the space inside a hollow brick, which the Clerk had to carefully chisel open like an egg.

He repairs them with warm beeswax, linen thread, and patience. He sits on an overturned dry-cistern for days at a time, his parchment head glowing softly in the amber light, his long wooden fingers moving with the slow, rhythmic certainty of a pendulum. *Click. Scrape. Puff.* A hare sits on his boot—and partially *inside* his boot—breathing warm lime-dust over his canvas hem. 

He has no voice, any more than the beasts do, yet he is not entirely silent. Draped across his chest, beneath the heavy canvas coat, is a lattice of thin copper wires strung taut between small wooden pegs, like the belly of a dulcimer. As he leans, reaches, or twists his torso to navigate the cramped A-frames, the dry heat and the tension of his movements cause the wires to expand or slip against their pegs. 

Every few hours, a single, muted note—a dry, metallic *plink*, like a drop of water that has forgotten how to be wet—rings out from beneath his ribs. 

At the sound, every boxwood lizard within ten paces freezes. The bellows-hares pause on the inhale. Even the grain of sand hovering above the mortar pile hangs suspended in the parched air for a heartbeat, waiting for the vibration to die away in the felt leaves, before settling into the wall with its tiny, answering *click*.

And deep in the thicket, where the shade under the brick arches is thickest and smells strongest of old linen, there are the things the Clerk is currently growing. 

Stacked neatly against a partition wall are three pairs of worn leather boots, filled to the ankles with dry mortar-dust and powdered chalk. Planted upright in each boot is a straight branch of seasoned ash-wood, tied to a mason's plumb-line. They have been there for years, drinking the perfect ninety-degree angle of the floor and wall. Already, the wood at the top of the branches is beginning to split and curl into the rough, articulated shape of knuckles, and from the mortar-dust in the heels, stiff, pale shoots of unbleached canvas are slowly uncurling, inch by dry inch, weaving themselves into the hem of a coat.
