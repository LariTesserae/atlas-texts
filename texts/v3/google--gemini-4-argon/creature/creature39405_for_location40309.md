The tracks did not lead out. 

Because the basin has no ramp, no collapse of talus offering a stair to the surface fifty feet above, anything that enters must drop, and anything that leaves must climb the smooth, inward-leaning bell of the overhang. The dimples in the cocoa-colored silt—sixteen of them, spaced short and wide apart, suggesting a low center of gravity and knees bent under weight—simply stop beside the third boulder. 

What looks at first like the third boulder is the one who brought the basalt.

In the ember-dark, there is no visual difference between the stone, the purple boulder-plants, and the occupant. The distinction is entirely a matter of texture, and even that requires a close, squinting inspection that the dull twilight barely allows. The limestone is matte and dry; the succulent is waxy and taut; the third shape is covered in a thick, stratified rind of the basin’s own silt, bound together by layers of the plant-wax that were smeared over the skin decades ago, over and over, until the creature inside was entirely sealed in an earthen caul.

It is roughly the size of a crouched human, though folded according to a different geometry. The spine is long and profoundly curved, allowing the pelvis and the ribcage to stack neatly upon one another like flat stones. The limbs are tucked wholly inside the mortar of wax and dust, the shins drawn up against the chest, the forearms crossed over the throat, the chin resting in the notch between the ankles. There are no ears to lose moisture, and no nose projecting from the flat plane of the face—only two narrow, diagonal slits above the jaw that have been plugged from the inside with hardened balls of mucus and gray plant-fuzz.

It is not dead, and it is not quite asleep. It is keeping time with the basin.

A mammal’s metabolism is a fire that demands constant stoking; it would burn through the meager calories of this room in a week and die of thirst in three days. The creature beside the chert anvil operates not like a fire, but like a slowly oxidizing mineral. Its blood is a dark, viscous syrup, heavy with glycerols and dissolved salts, moving not by the sharp pump of a heart, but by a sluggish, peristaltic squeeze of the main vessels that occurs once every forty or fifty minutes. 

If you were to press an ear hard against the waxy, silt-crusted curve of its back, you would hear nothing for nearly an hour. Then, a faint, hydraulic *slurp*, thick as cooling tar, as the dorsal vessel contracts, pushing a thimbleful of dark fluid toward the brain. 

It does not breathe the way surface things breathe. A pair of lungs pumping the dry, eighty-five-degree air would evaporate the creature’s internal water in a single afternoon. Instead, it relies on passive diffusion through the damp, pleated lining of its throat, drawing in the trace oxygen through the porous plugs in its nostrils without ever heaving its chest. It is cold-blooded, or rather, ambient-blooded; its core is the exact bathwater warmth of the dolostone walls, requiring zero energy to maintain.

The tools in the silt are not relics of a vanished visitor. They are in current use. The user is simply between strokes.

To understand the creature’s life is to stretch a single afternoon across a century. 

Approximately once every twelve or fifteen years—when the slow, chemical clock of its liver registers that its blood has thickened to the point of stalling—the mineral stillness breaks. It takes three days for the creature to wake. First, a slight, dry cracking appears along the seams of its wax shell, producing a sound indistinguishable from the *tick* of the iron-wire bushes on the ledges above. 

Over the course of forty hours, the syrup in its veins warms by half a degree through tiny, isometric shivers of its back muscles. The plugs in its nasal slits soften and drop into the dust. The eyes do not open—they are vestigial, covered in a permanent scale of translucent horn that registers only the difference between the black of the alcoves and the deep, clotting red of the ceiling aperture—but the heavy, three-fingered hands break free of their crusty mittens.

The skin underneath is the color of wet slate, loose and pleated like an accordion, tough enough to turn a flint edge. 

When it moves, it does not stand upright. It uncoils low to the ground, shifting its weight onto wide, calloused knuckles and the flat pads of its heels, moving with the agonizing, deliberate slowness of a chameleon on a cold branch. It makes no sound in the ash-silt. It does not disturb the air. It drags its tail—a thick, blunt organ shaped like a beaver's, depleted and shrunken into harsh wrinkles—behind it, erasing the sharp ridges of its own footprints and leaving only those broad, unreadable dimples.

It goes to the wire-bushes first, or rather, to the fissures beneath them. Taking the charred digging stick from its cleft, it pries at the stone lip where the subterranean vapor rises, scraping away the gray, damp-trapping fuzz of the roots and pressing the dry sponge of its tongue directly to the weeping rock. It stays like that for a week, motionless, drinking the rising humidity molecule by molecule until its wrinkled tail plumps slightly.

Then it turns to the purple boulder-plants. 

It does not eat them; to kill a plant that grows an inch a century would be suicide in a closed room. Instead, it harvests their armor. Using the flint flakes it struck generations ago, it scrapes the chalky, water-sealing wax from the purple hide, catching the shavings in the soapstone bowls. It never takes more than a third of the wax from any single plant, leaving the succulent enough coating to survive the decades until the next harvest. 

Some of the wax it chews—a slow, rhythmic grinding of flat, pavement-like molars that extracts the heavy lipids and trace sugars locked in the cuticle. The rest it mixes with its own thick, sparse saliva and the cocoa-colored silt, smearing the paste back over its slate-gray skin, sealing the pores, rebuilding the shell. 

Only once in five or six wakes does a flint edge dull enough to require the hammerstones. When it does, the creature sits before the chert anvil. It raises the black basalt stone to the height of its shoulder. It holds it there for three minutes, gathering the slow electrical charge in its nerves. 

*Clack.* 

The sound is swallowed instantly by the silt and the kidney-shaped pockets of the walls. A single flake drops. The creature lowers the basalt. It rests for an hour before inspecting the edge with a blunt, gray thumb. 

When the water is absorbed, the wax eaten, and the skin resealed, it backs into the space beside the two boulders. It tucks its shins into its chest. It folds its arms over its throat. It pushes two fresh wads of chewed wire-bush fuzz into its breathing slits. The last thing it does, before the blood slows to its hourly crawl, is reach out with one heavy hand and sweep a thin veil of dry silt over its own head, waiting for the gypsum dust from the ceiling to fill in the rest.

From the rim fifty feet above, looking down through the red gloom, there are three boulders around a stone workspace, a handful of abandoned tools, and the quiet, empty proof that whoever lived here is long gone. 

Down on the floor, inside the third boulder, the dorsal vessel contracts. A drop of dark blood moves an inch toward the brain. In eleven years, it will need the digging stick again, and it knows right where it left it.
