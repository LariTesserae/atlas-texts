You do not see them at first because your eyes are trying to adjust to the violence of the contrast—the blinding white of the quartz-schist in the sun, the ink-blue of the shadows—and because you are looking for something that moves at the speed of meat and blood. 

Look instead at the blue collars of ice bulging around the waists of the pillars. Look at one that is slightly greyer than the others, slightly less translucent, as if it has trapped a handful of the silver scree within its freezing matrix.

As the razor-edge of a sunbeam slides across the stone and touches this grey collar, it does not melt into water. It unfolds. 

What looked like a solid band of mineral-clouded rime separates into a cluster of long, brittle limbs, flat and translucent as blades of selenite gypsum. They are arthropods, of a sort—cousins to the harvestman or the whip-scorpion, though so severely edited by the altitude that they resemble nothing so much as animated frost-fractures. An adult is the size of a splayed human hand, though it weighs less than a dried leaf. Their bodies are a mere knot of pale, calcified plates, no larger than a thumbnail, from which eight ridiculously long, hinged legs radiate.

They have no pigment. Against the ultraviolet scouring of the high sky, pigment is a liability; instead, their shells are composed of layered, crystalline chitin that refracts the fierce light rather than absorbing it. When they stand in the shadow, they are the color of wet slate. When a shaft of sunlight strikes them, they flash with an internal, milky fire, scattering the glare like a prism so their tiny organs do not cook inside their shells.

For twenty-two hours of the day, they are indistinguishable from the geology. In the deep cold of the shadow, their body fluids—saturated with natural glycols and dissolved lime—thicken into a stiff, motionless gel. They cling to the flutes of the stone, their flat legs tucked in, allowing the rising breath of the vents to frost them over completely. They become armatures for the ice. White fern-fronds of rime grow right off the ridges of their backs. 

They are waiting for the noon clockwork.

The builders of the slate cages—whoever they were, perhaps monks who mistook hypoxia for holiness, or miners who went mad listening to the ringing stone—did not abandon their contraptions to the empty air. Whether by design or by the slow opportunistic creep of centuries, the cages have become the engine of the creatures’ brief, frantic day.

When the sun spears down into the amphitheater and the ice-lenses inside the copper hoops catch the glare, the focused beams strike the dark, soot-stained stones beneath them. In seconds, those dark stones grow hot. 

Before the first lead bowl has even filled with meltwater, the grey collars on the nearby pillars shatter. The creatures break out of their rime-crusts with a dry, faint *crick-crick* of flexing joints, dropping down the fluted stone not with the smooth scurry of a spider, but in jerking, incremental ticks, like the hands of a cheap watch. They gather around the slate cages, crowding at the edges of the focused light, holding their flat, crystalline legs out toward the hot black stones like miniature panels soaking up the radiant heat.

As their internal gel thaws to liquid, they become ravenous. 

Nothing green grows here, so they eat the rock, and they eat the traps. They are mineral-grazers. With mouthparts hardened by heavy metals—literally tipped with dark, dull iron and copper—they scrape at the white mineral salts left behind on the petrified sinew lashings. They scale the stacked slate shingles and harvest the microscopic crusts of calcium and potash deposited by the endlessly evaporating mist. Most of all, they crave the verdigris on the copper hoops and the sweet, white oxide crusting the lead bowls.

This is the hidden half of the basin’s afternoon music. The lead bowls do not tip merely from the weight of the melting ice. As the water trickles into a bowl, a dozen of these pale, refractive weavers clamber onto its rim to drink and scrape at the oxidized metal. Their collective weight, minuscule as it is, is often the final gram that overcomes the bone pivot. 

The bowl tips. The water spills down the rock flute. The creatures scatter upward in a glittering, silent explosion of pale legs just as the bowl rights itself with its dull *thunk*.

Where the spilled water runs down into the deep, perpetual shadows at the base of the needles, it feeds the saucer-shaped puddles beneath their invisible skins of ice. And it is here that the second half of their life takes place.

If you kneel on the ringing slate—careful not to crush the brittle, grey-white carcasses of the old ones, which do not rot in this sterile air, but simply dry out and shatter like spun glass among the scree—and peer through the clear ice of a puddle, you will see the silver gas bubbles migrating across the ceiling of the water.

Clinging to the underside of the ice skin, hanging upside down in the frigid, liquid lens, are the nymphs. 

They are soft-bodied, worm-like things, entirely transparent except for the dark line of mineral silt in their guts. They cannot survive the dry, sublimating air above, and the adults cannot survive the wet below. The nymphs live in the thin film of gas that collects between the water and the ice roof. As the silver bubbles from the thermal vents roll slowly across the underside of the ice like mercury in a level, the nymphs ride inside them, breathing the warm, damp exhalation of the mountain’s deep throat, grazing on the invisible, chemotrophic bacteria that skin the interface between water and gas.

When a lead bowl tips high above and a sudden, measured torrent of sun-warmed meltwater sluices down a pillar’s flute, it hits the puddle and melts a hole the size of a coin right through the geometric ice skin. 

For ten minutes at midday, the ceiling opens. 

Down from the sunlit pillars come the gravid females, lowered on guidelines of silk that is not sticky, but stiff and metallic, spun from the copper and lime they have grazed from the cages. They do not touch the water; they hang in the shaft of light just above the melted hole, dip their abdomens through the surface tension, and drop their eggs—tiny, heavy spheres of dull grey lead-protein that sink like shot to the warm slate bottom. 

At the same time, the oldest nymphs, sensing the sudden spike of ultraviolet light and the rush of oxygenated meltwater, haul themselves up onto the rim of the melting hole. There, in the chaotic boundary where the warm vent-gas meets the freezing shadow, their skins split. They pull their long, flat, crystalline legs out of their larval sheaths, stepping out onto the ice skin while they are still soft as wax, hurrying to fold themselves into the posture of a frost-collar before the shadow slides back and their blood seizes into glass.

Sometimes they are too slow. If you look closely at the thick blue ice collars that have sheared off the pillars and shattered on the floor, you will find them inside the splinters: a leg half-drawn from a moult, or two adults locked together in the slow, brittle geometry of their mating, caught when a cloud passed over the furnace-door of the sun and the water froze them solid in a heartbeat.

Even the cages themselves are no longer entirely the work of the long-gone builders. Over the centuries, as the ancient sinew lashings have frayed under the assault of the sun and the scraping of iron-tipped jaws, the creatures have repaired them. They cannot stack slate, and they do not understand the optics of the ice-lenses, but they know that when a copper hoop falls, the hot stone beneath it goes cold, and the noon-thaw stops. 

So they bind the failing joints. If you look at the bone splints and the slate shingles, you will see they are wrapped in thousands of layers of that stiff, mineral silk, spun exclusively in the frantic twenty minutes of high noon, year after year, century after century. The silk catches the mist, the mist leaves its salt, and the salt petrifies the silk into a new stone. 

The creatures have taken over the maintenance of the clock that wakes them. When the shadows slide eastward by an inch, and the last lead bowl knocks against its slate housing, they are already back on the vertical pillars, their legs tucked in, their faceted eyes dimmed under a fresh glaze of rime, waiting for their own repairs to catch the sun tomorrow.
