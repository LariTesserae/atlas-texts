If you were to strip one of the builders down—which never happens in the open, only inside the suffocating, tallow-stinking warmth of the sleeping-bladders hung beneath the main aqueducts—you would find a body that has been completely renegotiated by salt, glare, and gravity.

They are shaped like pestle-stones. Everything about them is low-centered and dense. Their legs are bowed and astonishingly short, the thigh bones thick as wagon-axles, ending in feet that have lost the arch entirely, spreading out inside their greased felt boots like wide, meaty paddles to distribute their weight over rotten slush-ice. Above the waist, they balloon into tremendous, barrel-vaulted ribcages housing lungs the size of blacksmiths' bellows, necessary to suck oxygen from air so thin and winter-dried it feels like inhaling powdered glass. 

Their skin, where it is not armored in yellow calluses an inch thick, has the cured, translucent look of smoked bladder-parchment. The water does this. Because every man, woman, and child spends sixteen hours a day wading through or being sprayed by water heavy with lithium salts and dissolved mountain-alkali, they are essentially pickled alive. Their knuckles are swollen to the size of walnuts with mineral deposits that have calcified in the joints, making their fingers permanently curved into the exact hook-shape required to grip a bone lever or a wet rawhide line. They cannot lay their hands flat on a table; there are no tables here anyway.

Because of the lithium in the brine—which enters through their cracked cuticles, through the mist they breathe, and through the meat of the silt-hares they boil in the vents—their nervous systems are marinating in a natural, perpetual sedative. 

This is the secret to their frantic, insectile industry. An outsider dropped into this freezing, blinding hell would go mad within three hours from the glare and the frantic precariousness of life; they would scream until their windpipes froze, or sit down in a warm boil and weep until the shifting vent-water parboiled them. The basin-folk feel none of that. Beneath their hyperactive scrambling, their minds are as flat, heavy, and ripples-free as a pool of mercury. When a wall of salt-ice calves off the northern face and crushes a three-tier rookery—along with the two uncles who were re-lashing its struts—there is no panic. No one drops a tool. The survivors simply pivot, their blood pulsing at a slow, steady fifty beats a minute, and begin digging through the slush for the reusable mammoth-ribs, pulling the warm bodies of their kin out of the wreck to be rendered for tallow before the meat can freeze. It is not cruelty; it is a profound, mineral-induced equanimity. They are chemically incapable of despair.

To look one in the face—when the horn snow-goggles are lifted in the dim, brown light of a gut-skin tent—is jarring. Their faces are broad and flat, the noses sunken almost flush with the cheekbones to keep the cartilage from freezing in the gale-force downdrafts. Because the sun hits them from below, bouncing off the grease-ice at ten thousand lumens, their eyebrows do not grow above their eyes, but *beneath* them, sprouting in thick, oily fringes along the lower lids and the tops of the cheekbones to catch the upward glare, while their upper brows are bald, scarred ridges of bone. 

The eyes themselves are small, black-irised, and covered by a thick, yellowish nictitating membrane that slides horizontally from the tear-duct, wiping away the freezing salt-rime every few seconds with a wet, inaudible *schlick*. By the time a builder reaches thirty, the corneas are so scarred by salt-crystals and ultraviolet light that they are milky blue at the edges, leaving only a tiny, central pinhole of clear vision—an organic mirror of the bone goggles they wear outside.

They have no names. Names are an acoustic luxury, meant for people who can call across a valley or whisper in the dark. Here, where the air eats sound and the wind tears the breath from your lips, a name is useless. Instead, they have *weights* and *rhythms*. 

Every person is known by the specific cadence of their stone hammer against the scaffolding—a signature rhythm tapped out in bursts of three or four strikes that travels through the wet bone-and-slate lattice of the town and up into the sensitive, nerve-rich soles of everyone standing on that section of the web. A child learns to feel its mother approaching fifty feet away through the soles of its boots: *tap-tap-slap, tap-tap-slap*—the heavy, dragging beat of a woman carrying two buckets of render-fat with a healed fracture in her left fibula. 

Up close, where the catwalks are so narrow they must slide past one another chest-to-chest, they speak with their chins and tongues. With their hands occupied holding heavy slate tiles or steadying themselves against the slick rock, two builders passing on a ledge will press their greased leather shoulders together to form a circuit against the wind, tilt their heads back slightly, and flick their tongues against their upper lips in a rapid, silent semaphore, or knock their heavy, horn-sheathed chins against one another’s collarbones. *Two-knocks, slide-left, tongue-out:* the boil under the third tier is cooling; bring the big pry-bars.

They smell of hot wet wool, rancid seal-oil, and the metallic, turnip-like stench of the deep-earth vents. 

Nothing goes to waste in the teacup, least of all the builders themselves. There is no soil to bury the dead, and no wood to burn them. When a worker’s heart finally stops—usually from the "heavy lung," when the salt-mist finally coats the alveoli in a crust too thick to breathe through—the body is taken down to the lowest, hottest mud-boils where the pig-sized gill-beasts wallow. The corpse is lashed into a wooden frame and submerged in the sixty-degree alkali sludge. Within four days, the chemistry of the water and the rasping tongues of the blind, blubbery amphibians strip the bones cleaner than any vulture could. 

The family then retrieves the frame. The skull is split and carved into snow-goggles or drinking bowls; the femurs become chisel-handles; the ribs are lashed into the lattice of the bird-cages; the teeth are inlaid into the soles of boots to give traction on the black ice. A young man levering a boulder out of a choked sluice is very likely using his grandfather’s shinbone as a fulcrum, while wearing his great-aunt’s pelvis as a shoulder-guard against the dripping brine-curtains.

They do not worship the mountain, nor do they curse it. They have no concept of an "outside." On the rare occasions when a hunting party must climb to the upper rim to scavenge the wind-scoured passes for ancient mammoth-ivory, the builders become violently, debilitatingly agoraphobic. Up there, where the horizon drops away into a hundred miles of empty blue peaks and the wind actually *howls* in the ears, they fall to their bellies, vomiting from vertigo and sensory overload, terrified of a sky that doesn't bounce the light back up at them, and horrified by the great, roaring noise of the world. They scramble back down into the blinding, silent, steaming pit as fast as their bowed legs will carry them, back into the grease and the crowding and the endless, lithium-numbed labor of keeping their world from freezing solid or washing away.
