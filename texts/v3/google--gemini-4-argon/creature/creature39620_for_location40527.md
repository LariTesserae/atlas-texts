If you stand on one of the drowned causeways and fix your eyes on a distant cast-iron pillar, it takes several minutes to realize that the pillar is slightly thicker on one side than it ought to be. 

Then, a piece of the architecture detaches itself. What looked like a vertical bolster of green-patinated brass and tallow-stone shifts its weight, and a circular ripple spreads from its base, ghosting out across the black water to meet the *plink* of the Regulator’s wheel.

The city’s oldest pipe-layers, who never come down here if they can help it, call him the Turncock, or simply the Greaser. Whether he is the last descendant of a hereditary line of municipal caretakers, a man whom the damp refused to finish killing, or an organism the Sump simply grew to tend the Regulator the way it grew the gasket-snails to eat the rust, is no longer a useful question. He has been down here long enough that the distinction between workman and fauna has dissolved.

He is tall, though heavily stooped, with the broad, barrel-chested torso of someone whose lungs have adapted to pulling thin oxygen out of cold, misty air. From a distance, he appears to wear a heavy greatcoat of oiled canvas, stiffened with minerals and patched with sheets of dark vulcanized rubber. Up close, it is impossible to see where the coat ends and his hide begins. The fabric is so thoroughly impregnated with tallow, iron-slime, and coal-tar that it has fused into a segmented, leathery carapace. In the crease of his bent neck and along the ridge of his left shoulder, where the rain from the brick vaults hits him most frequently, small clusters of slag-mussels have anchored themselves. He does not scrape them off; their fluted, metallic shells serve him as well as an epaulette, and they filter the water that runs down his back.

He moves at the exact pace of the chamber. A single step takes him five or six seconds: a slow, deliberate lifting of a wide, flat foot encased in what might once-upon-a-time have been a gutta-percha wading boot, though the rubber has long since split and been healed over by calloused, slate-grey skin as tough as hippo-hide. He sets his foot down toe-first, sliding it through the two-inch water over the rounded flagstones so that he never makes a splash. Even the blind eels, whose copper sensory pits can read the heartbeat of a frog ten yards off, do not scatter when he passes. To them, his legs are simply moving pilings, warm and harmless.

His face is rarely lifted toward the pewter twilight of the deck-prisms. It is tucked down into a collar of matted, wire-fern fibers, shielded by the brim of a slumped leather cap that has petrified into the shape of a turtle’s shell. Beneath it, his skin has the dense, poreless look of river-clay. His eyes are very large, entirely dark, and filmed over with a nictitating membrane as clear and thick as a watch-crystal—an adaptation against the constant, weeping lime-drip from the ceiling. He does not blink; occasionally, the membrane sweeps sideways across the orb with a wet, miniature click. He breathes through wide, slit-like nostrils that flare once every thirty seconds, tasting the air for changes in the sulfur content of the gas-lamps or the sour tang of an overheating bearing.

Across his chest, suspended by a strap of braided brass wire, he carries a long-spouted oiler of beaten copper and a heavy iron spanner whose jaws have been worn smooth by a century of his own thumb.

His life is an unbroken, four-minute rhythm, tethered to the Regulator. 

It takes him three hours to walk the perimeter of the lagoon, checking the gas-stanchions. When a globe’s amber light begins to gutter or turn blue with damp, he stops before it. He will stand motionless for twenty minutes, raising one heavy, three-fingered hand—the ring and little fingers having fused together into a single, broad paddle of bone and grey flesh—to turn the brass petcock a fraction of a millimeter. The vent-rooks roosting on the warm neck of the lamp do not flee; they merely shift their oiled bristles, clicking their claws, and sometimes one will lean down to gently preen the mineral crust from his shoulder while he works.

When the water is at its twelve-hour high, lapping three inches above his ankles, he wades out to the central housing of the Regulator. 

Here, he climbs. He uses the riveted iron plates and the narrow maintenance rungs with the slow, fluid grace of a sea-sloth, his heavy coat trailing streamers of black water. At the top of the housing, where the great flywheel rises from its pit, there is a series of brass drip-cups that feed grease into the main axle. 

He stands beside the wheel as the spokes rise out of the dark, shedding their rhythmic drops. From a pouch at his hip, he draws a lump of grey suet—harvested, though no one knows how, from the fat of the giant eels and boiled down over the steam-pipes with old machine oil and crushed snail-shell. With his long copper can, he feeds the tallow into the cups. 

Sometimes, when the city miles above is quiet—around four in the morning, when the streetcars stop rattling over the glass prisms in the gutter and the hydraulic pressure in the mains drops to a low, subterranean murmur—he rests. He does not lie down; to lie down in the Sump is to become part of the floor, to be grazed over by the copper herds of gasket-snails before you can rise again. Instead, he leans his back against the warm, sweating brass of the Regulator’s cylinder, folding his long arms over his chest, and lets his chin sink into his collar.

In the deep, velvety shadow between the amber lamps, he is entirely invisible. His breathing slows until it matches the four-minute turn of the wheel: two minutes to draw the cold, iron-tasting mist into his barrel chest, and two minutes to let it seep back out through his teeth with a sound like water draining through gravel. While he sleeps, a gasket-snail will often crawl up the skirt of his coat, leaving a shiny trail of mucus across his hip as it rasps away the day’s accumulation of rust, cleaning him before sliding back into the black lagoon before the water falls again.
