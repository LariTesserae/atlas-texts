To understand what else shares the hollow town, you have to stop looking with your eyes and start listening to the errors in the wind. 

The six-legged shepherd—the *draft-hound*, as a hypothetical naturalist might call it—is the largest moving thing in the basin, and at first, it seems to be a solitary janitor in a dead museum. It moves with a liquid, creeping deliberation, its greased fringe trailing over the polished soapstone, sweeping the thresholds. 

Wait until one pauses in a small, three-walled courtyard where the wind spins in a tight, whistling vortex. The hound’s flank-bristles flare. It does not walk into the adjacent room; instead, it reaches a heavy, blunt forepaw around the lintel and presses its weight against a fist-sized bulge of blue-black lichen on the doorframe.

Instantly, the piercing, flute-like F-sharp of that chamber drops to a dull, breathy C. 

Within the room, the sudden change in air pressure breaks the tiny, swirling cyclone that was holding a drift of frozen lowland midges airborne. The insects patter to the milk-white floor like spilled pepper. The hound slips in, its long, grey, spatulate tongue already unrolling to lap them up. 

It is not just reading the town. It is playing it. 

The draft-hound knows the acoustic map of the entire amphitheater, not as a visual layout of streets and plazas, but as a three-dimensional web of standing waves and pressure differentials. Its brain is essentially an organ for calculating fluid dynamics. When a rival enters the far side of the basin—two hundred yards away through a labyrinth of knee-high masonry—the resident hound knows immediately, because a low, familiar B-flat bass note coming up through the bedrock has suddenly gone flat, muffled by four pounds of dense, greased wool passing through a subterranean flue.

For a long time, it seems the hounds are the only animals here. The rooms are too clean for anything else. There are no droppings, no nests, no gnawed stems. Even the hounds’ own waste—dry, chalky pellets the size of peppercorns—is dropped deliberately into the "chimneys," the vertical wind-shafts that blast upward and fling the pellets over the ridge and down the mountain. 

The cleanliness is an illusion of speed. There is a whole secondary ecology here, one that operates in the ninety seconds between the arrival of a freezing fog-bank and its total evaporation. 

Look closely at the soapstone floors when the sun is out. In the diffuse, milky light, the rock looks continuous, polished to a uniform satin finish. Notice, though, how the faint grey veining in the stone seems to pool in the gutters and the dead-air corners. 

Wait for the fog. 

The silver cloud spills over the lip. The wind drops a fractional octave as it becomes heavy with ice-water. The millions of willow-vents begin their frantic, woody *clack-clack-clack*, drinking the mist. And as the condensation slicks the floors, the grey veining in the soapstone detaches itself from the rock.

They are not veins. They are *shale-skates*—flatworms no thicker than gold leaf, translucent and patterned to match the exact calcium-silicate marbling of the basin. During the dry hours, which is ninety-five percent of their lives, they are desiccated to the point of being mineral. They glue themselves into the microscopic depressions of the floor, so flat that the screaming wind passes over them without finding an edge to peel up, and so dry that the draft-hounds’ probing tongues pass right over them, mistaking them for stone.

The moment the water hits them, they rehydrate in three seconds flat. 

Suddenly, the miniature streets are alive with sliding, glass-clear ribbons, some as long as a human finger, rippling through the brief, frantic gutters. They do not eat the lowland detritus; they are grazers of the micro-film, scraping up the flash-blooms of mountain algae that turn the wet soapstone slick as grease. They mate, feed, and divide in a frantic fifty-second window while the town is blind in the fog and deafened by the clicking of the willow-vents. 

And they have a predator, though it is not the hound. 

Look inside the load-bearing arches of the shale-willow itself. The woody stems are fused into solid green walls, tough as oak and pressurized with a bitter, antifreeze sap. Here and there, where two thick roots have grafted together to lift a block of stone, there are small, smoothly bored holes, like the stops on a recorder.

As the fog begins to thin, and the gutters start to shrink back into dry rock, a Pale Mason emerges from its stop-hole. 

It is an arthropod, a distant, high-altitude cousin of the harvestman or the vinegaroon, though it has traded spindly legs for heavy, calcified plates that look as though they were carved from the very soapstone it walks on. It is the size of a walnut, stark white, and completely blind. 

The Mason does not hunt in the dry wind; it would be blown out of the bowl like a dry pea. Instead, it waits in the acoustic dead-zones inside the willow wood. It is a creature of pure resonance. Every chamber in the town has a fundamental pitch, and when a chamber’s floor is coated in wet, writhing shale-skates, the acoustic reflection of the stone changes—it becomes slightly muffled, slightly "wet" in its timbre. 

The Masons feel this dampening through the wood. They drop from their holes into the dying gutters, heavy enough to resist the draft for the few seconds it takes to seize a ribbon-worm in their scissor-like chelicerae. If a skate is already drying, gluing itself back to the bedrock, the Mason uses its heavy, chisel-tipped forelegs to pry the worm’s edge up before the biological cement sets, rolling it up like a strip of wet parchment and dragging it back into the woody wall before the sun burns the last of the mist away.

In fact, the Masons are the true architects of the town’s music. The shale-willow builds the walls to stop the wind, constantly growing over the gaps, trying to seal the chambers into stagnant, warm pockets of air. If the willow succeeded, the wind would simply glide over the top of the green mat, the sieve would stop working, the organic dust from the lowlands would blow right past, and the town would starve. 

It is the Masons, boring their acoustic tunnels through the living wood to create resonance chambers and listening-posts, that keep the town hollow and howling. They prune the willow from the inside, chewing out vents and flues to tune their traps, unwittingly maintaining the vast, shrieking pipe organ that feeds the hounds.

Sometimes, in the dead of the afternoon when the sky is an aching, ultraviolet blue and the wind is steady, you can see the whole system at rest. 

Down in a deep, three-story terrace near the center of the bowl, inside a room no bigger than a breadbox that hums with a steady, chest-vibrating G-below-middle-C, three draft-hounds are curled together like a single pelt of greasy dark wool. Their breathing is slow, synchronized to the pulsing beat-frequency of the room next door. 

Inches from their noses, entirely invisible against the polished white floor, fifty shale-skates lie frozen as vitrified glass, waiting for the next cloud. And inside the green, muscular arch overhead, a dozen white Masons sit motionless in their carved flutes, their stone-plated legs resting lightly against the wood, listening to the mountain sing, waiting for the chord to change.
