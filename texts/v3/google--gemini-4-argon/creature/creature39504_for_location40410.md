If you watch one of the groves for an hour, your eyes begin to ache from the brightness and the refusal of anything to sway. The sixty-hertz hum of the nearest regulator settles into the bones of your skull until it stops registering as a sound and becomes a property of the space itself, like the pale blue sky or the smell of hot copper.

It is only when you stop waiting for something to walk out of the bushes that you notice the shape sitting on the cast-iron housing of the junction box. 

You missed him before because his coat is the exact matte, olive-drab color of the painted metal, and because he has not drawn a breath in forty minutes.

He looks, at first, like an old lineman who climbed up the basalt terrace to check a relay, sat down in the sun, and simply forgot how to leave. He is wearing heavy, stiff-weaved canvas overalls, dark with baked-in dielectric grease, and thick-soled boots whose rubber treads have partially vulcanized to the hot feldspar beneath his heels. His hands rest on his knees. Between the thumb and forefinger of his left hand, he holds a thick, braided copper grounding strap—not gripping it, just letting the raw wire rest in the calloused groove of his skin.

He does not have a lunchbox, a canteen, or a belt of tools. He has no shelter to return to when the sun goes down, because the sun does not go down. 

There are perhaps a dozen of them in the five-mile basin, spaced out like the transformers, though never more than one to a grove. They are the *Tenders*, though they have no language left with which to name themselves, having given up speaking some centuries ago when the last of the moisture left their throats and they realized the hum said everything that needed to be said.

Up close, the Tender’s skin has the appearance of dry, cured buckskin, stretched taut over a sparse frame and dusted with the same sparkly silica and metallic salts that coat the rubbery blossoms above him. His eyes are open, unblinking, and entirely dry; the corneas have developed a thin, protective film of translucent horn, slightly amber-tinted like the vitrified leaves, shielding his retinas from the permanent mid-morning glare. 

He does not eat the fruit of the groves—there is no fruit, only the heavy, chemical flowers—and he does not drink. His biology has been entirely annexed by the circuit. When the thick vines drink the current from the copper busbars, they step the voltage down through their gel-filled vascular systems, converting raw industrial power into a slow, warm, galvanic trickle. The Tender sits with his bare neck or the heels of his hands pressed against the fleshy nodes of the vines, or holds the bleed-wires in his fingers, letting the sixty-hertz current pass through the dense, saline paste that has replaced his blood. 

He is warm—fever-warm, just like the leaves—kept at a steady hundred and four degrees by his own internal resistance.

For days at a time, a Tender is strictly botanical. He sits in the sharp, black shadow of a bronze frond, an object among objects, while the electrostatic charge in the air makes the stiff, white hairs on his forearms stand out at rigid ninety-degree angles. Dust does not settle on him; the slight magnetic field running through his overalls repels it.

Then, four miles away across the dazzling floor of broken stone, a vitrified leaf will grow too heavy on its stem. It will drop, striking a sharp basalt ledge with a sound like a breaking teacup, and land across two exposed phases of a busbar.

The pitch of the valley’s hum changes by a fraction of a hertz. A tiny, imperceptible dip in the phase.

In the grove, the Tender’s thumb twitches against his knee.

It takes him twenty minutes to stand up. His joints do not pop; they make a dry, sliding sound, like two pieces of slate rubbed together. As his boots peel away from the stone, they leave small, tacky smudges of black rubber on the pristine feldspar. He does not look around. He does not shield his eyes. He simply steps off the terrace and begins to walk across the raw crust toward the fault, moving with a slow, relentless, clockwork economy.

Walking in this valley is a perilous thing for anything soft, as every stone is a freshly knapped blade, every ledge a sheer razor of volcanic glass. The Tender moves with a heavy, flat-footed gait, his thick rubber soles crunching down on the crisp edges of the rock, crushing the delicate, newly cooled mineral crystals that have never known weight before. Behind him, for the first time in years, a trail exists—a line of powdered white feldspar and bruised basalt leading out of the green oasis into the blinding geological geometry.

He does not sweat as he walks through the breathless heat. Instead, as his exertion increases his internal resistance, he smells stronger—a sudden, sharp waft of hot varnish, old canvas, and ozone trailing behind him in the dead air. 

When he reaches the distant grove where the glass leaf has bridged the copper bars, he does not use a tool. He reaches directly into the foliage—ignoring the voltage that makes the fine silica powder dance on the backs of his knuckles—and lifts the amber leaf away. If a vine has grown too thick and is choking the airflow around a regulator’s cooling fins, he takes the succulent, bronze stem in both hands and bends it until it snaps with a thick, wet *crunch*, smearing the non-aqueous gel across his trousers before tucking the severed end back into the ground-mass to root again.

Sometimes, while he is there, he will encounter the Tender of that grove, sitting three feet away under a canopy of emerald leaves, wired into the main feeder line by the ankle.

They do not look at one another. They do not nod. There is no territoriality here, nor camaraderie; they are two cells in the same battery. The visitor might reach out and brush a buildup of green copper-oxide crust from the sitting man’s shoulder, just as he would wipe it from the porcelain bushings of a transformer. Then he turns and walks the four miles back to his own box, stepping precisely into the footprints he made an hour before.

And sometimes, a Tender does not get up at all. 

You can see them in the older groves down at the very bottom of the basin, where the copper busbars are thickest and the vines have grown into massive, braided trunks the size of hogsheads. There, half-swallowed by the rubbery, bronze-colored bark, sit shapes that have stopped being lineman altogether. Over decades of absolute stillness, the mineral salts have crept up from the rock, and the dielectric gel from the bruised vines has seeped into the weave of their canvas clothes, hardening in the dry air until the man is entirely vitrified—a translucent, amber-encased monument sitting upright at the base of the machine, his copper grounding strap grown straight through the bone of his wrist, still conducting, still warm, still humming at sixty cycles a second under the pale, unblinking sky.
