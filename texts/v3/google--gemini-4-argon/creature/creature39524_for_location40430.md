At first, you do not see them at all. You see only the wood, and the blinding, shadowless glare of it. 

If you look for the makers of the hanging baskets, your eyes will slide right over them, mistaking a crouched figure for a dark knot of compression-wood, or a cluster of frost-blackened lichen clinging to a vertical face. They want it that way. In the Hollow, anything that stands upright on two legs like a tree is subject to the same laws as a tree: it builds tension, it catches the freezing wind, and eventually, it snaps.

So they crouch. They cling. They move with the low, flat, scuttle-and-pause gait of spiders on a ceiling, keeping their centers of gravity pressed tight against the grain.

When one finally detaches itself from the pale wall of a buttress, the first thing you notice is the coat. It looks enormous, ragged, and strangely geometric—a heavy, tiered mantle made not of fur, but of thousands of overlapping scales of shaved, translucent horn-wood and stiffened lichen, greased with rendered fat and bound with sinew. When they are still, they pull these cloaks over their heads and draw their limbs in, becoming an aerodynamic bump on the bark, shedding the freezing downdrafts and the stinging flurries of frozen sap-dust. 

Beneath the mantle, they are human, or were once, though the Hollow has pared them down to wire, gristle, and cartilage. They are small—scarcely five feet tall if they ever stood completely straight, which they rarely do—with long, bowed forearms and heavily muscled shoulders. Their legs are short, bowed at the shin, and permanently flexed at the knee, built like shock absorbers to take the sudden, bone-jarring *thump* of a floor-beam dropping six inches beneath their feet. 

Their skin is the color of dark, smoked tallow, leathery and deeply creased around the eyes and mouth from a lifetime of squinting into a world that shines from below as brightly as it does from above. None of them have bare eyes. Over their faces they wear broad, convex masks carved from dark, close-grained heartwood, fitted tightly to the cheekbones with strips of soft inner-bark. There are no eyeholes—only two razor-thin horizontal slits, sometimes cross-hatched with a vertical notch, charred black on the inside to drink the glare. Through these slits, their eyes are wet, dark, and darting, the pupils permanently pinpricked against the relentless actinic white of the sap-veins.

To touch one of them is like touching a pine-knot. They smell powerfully of turpentine, dried sweat, rancid browser-tallow, and the sweet, green, cucumber-scent of fresh cambium. Their hands and feet are rarely fully wrapped; they need the purchase. Instead, they coat their palms and the soles of their split-toed boots with a gummy, black pitch boiled down from old callus-wood, mixed with gritty ash so it doesn't freeze slick. Their fingers are thick, crooked at the joints from old fractures, with nails worn down to the quick and replaced by strapped-on thimbles of carved iron-burl—curved, sharp-edged claws they use to hook into the microscopic fissures of the bark as they climb.

They call themselves the *Tethered*, or sometimes simply the *Ticks*, depending on how sour their mood is. They have no concept of land ownership, because there is no land. There is only the *Grain*, the *Load*, and the *Slough*. 

If you watch a band of them moving across a splinter-field, you see instantly how their minds work. They never walk abreast, and they never walk in a straight line. They move in a loose, staggered chain, connected at the waist by twenty-foot lengths of braided whip-vine—not so rigid that one falling person will drag the next into a chasm, but slack, threaded through wooden friction-brakes at their hips. The leader—usually the oldest woman, whose joints can feel the high-frequency hum of straining cellulose before it becomes audible—does not look ahead. She looks at the grain under her feet, and she listens.

Every ten paces, she stops and drives a *listening-peg* into the wood. It is a simple tool: a dense spindle of black iron-burl, flared at one end like a horn. She presses her teeth—not her ear, which is muffled against the frost—to the flared end of the stick, letting the vibrations of the mega-root travel up through her jawbone into her skull. 

She is reading the tension. To the Tethered, the world is not solid; it is an ocean of frozen violence, a billion tons of wood pulling against itself. Through her teeth, she can hear the deep, slow *glug-glug* of a pressurized sap-artery three hundred feet down; she can feel the rapid, dry *chittering* of bark about to delaminate; she can sense the terrible, gathering silence of a column that has locked up and is preparing to shear. If she taps her jaw twice with a clawed finger, the entire line freezes. If she slashes her hand downward, they drop flat, spreading their arms and legs to distribute their weight, pressing their bellies into the cold, glowing timber while, half a mile away, a ridge the size of a cathedral explodes into a cloud of white splinters and boiling mist.

They are quiet people. Speaking out loud in the cold air wastes heat and moisture, and more importantly, it deafens you to the *ping* of a failing joint. Instead, they talk through the wood. 

Within a cluster of hanging baskets—a village, if five or six swaying pods roped to the same weeping callus-ridge can be called a village—they communicate by rapping their burl-thimbles against the structural ribs of the root they hang from. The dense, pressurized wood carries the clicks cleanly for hundreds of yards. Sitting inside one of their pods is like sitting inside the body of a drum. The walls, woven tightly of flexible vines and caulked with gray moss and sap-paste, are warm and dark, lit only by a small clay-like bowl of hollowed fungus holding a chunk of *bleed-wood*—a hunk of severed vascular tissue that continues to glow with a dying, greenish-white light for days after being cut. The pod sways constantly in the slow, freezing updrafts. Outside, the void groans. Inside, four or five people huddle together under greased hides, knees drawn up to their chins, tapping lazily against the main suspension rope that runs through the center of the basket, gossiping with the pod fifty feet below them without ever opening their mouths.

Their whole lives are a race against healing and shattering. 

Unlike stone, the world they live in is alive, and it hates to be pierced. When a family drives a thick wedge of black iron-burl into a glowing vein to tap the hot, sugary sap—their main source of both water and calories—the great root immediately begins to fight back. Within hours, the wound weeps a thick, clear resin that hardens on contact with the freezing air into an amber-like glass, sealing the tap. Within days, the surrounding bark swells into a bulbous, angry callus, trying to swallow the wedge or spit it out. 

A tap-tender’s job is relentless. He hangs in a harness of braided browser-hide over a two-mile drop, hacking away the encroaching scar-tissue with an adze made of horn-wood, his breath freezing into a thick white beard on the outside of his glare-mask. He collects the sap in bladders made from the stomachs of the bristle-furred browsers, catching it while it is still warm and liquid, before the abyssal cold turns it into brittle, glowing ice that tastes of turpentine and iron.

Because nothing stays still, they keep nothing that is heavy. Their wealth is measured in cordage and wedges. A person’s most prized possession is their *bite*—a set of three or four iron-burl pitons, carved with deep, spiral threads like augers, which have been tempered by being boiled in mineral-heavy runoff water and charred in tallow-fires until they are nearly as hard as steel. When a pod-cluster has to move—when the great column they cling to begins the slow, agonizing twist that precedes a total fracture—they do not pack furniture. They unlash the suspension lines, cut the baskets loose to fall twisting into the bright abyss, and Spider-walk away across the ceiling, carrying only their ropes, their augers, their glare-masks, and bundles of dried, sugary cambium strips.

They have a strange relationship with gravity. To fall, to them, is not a sudden accident; it is the ultimate, inevitable destination of all things. Everything goes down into the *Slough* eventually—the shattered bridges, the dead browsers, the snapped miles of root, the empty baskets. 

When one of the Tethered dies in their pod, or is crushed by a kicking slab of bark, they do not bury them. You cannot dig a grave in hyper-pressurized wood; the ground would simply squeeze the body back out, or incorporate it into a burl. Instead, they strip the corpse of its mask, its cloak, and its augers. They take a sharp splinter of fresh, glowing white-wood and drive it through the dead person's tongue, pinning the jaw shut so the spirit cannot cry out and startle the living on the way down. 

Then they take the body to the edge of a shelf, where the warm, moist air rising from a deep sap-rupture meets the freezing gale of the open void. They do not throw the dead straight down. They wait for a flock of the pale, leaf-thin manta-moths to come riding up the thermal. They bind the body’s wrists and ankles to the tough, leathery wings of three or four of the largest flyers, smearing the dead skin with sweet sap to draw more of the creatures in. 

And then they push them off into the glare. 

The dead do not plummet. Buoyed by the frantic, silent flapping of the pale wings and caught by the rising plumes of diamond dust, they drift outward, miles out into the blinding white emptiness between the columns, turning slowly in the freezing gyres of mist, shrinking to a dark speck against the luminous walls until the cold makes them brittle, the ties snap, and the deep trenches finally take them in.
