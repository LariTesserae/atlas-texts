To live in a room that never stops redecorating itself requires a certain looseness in the knees. 

You can spot a native of the basin before you ever see their face, purely by the way they stand. They have the wide, perpetually adjusting stance of deckhands on a rolling ship, their weight shifting automatically as the copper-colored root-mat inflates and deflates beneath their heels. They never lock their joints. If one of them stops in the middle of the uncluttered plaza to have a conversation, their body remains in a state of micro-motion, rising three inches on a sudden bolus of municipal water-pressure, tilting slightly to let a newly extruded buttress slide past their hip, stepping sideways without looking to avoid the splash-zone of a venting relief-stoma. 

They are human, though heavily edited for the climate. Their skin has a dense, matte quality, rich in specialized lipids that cause the constant, mineral-heavy runoff to bead and roll off them like mercury off glass. They smell faintly of crushed fern, ozone, and warm tallow. Because the air is maintained at the exact temperature of blood, clothing is treated not as shelter, but as a portable toolkit and an interface layer. They wear heavy, open-weave aprons, kilts, and wraps grown from pale, symbiotic fungi—garments that drink the nutrient-broth out of the air to stay supple, and which have no pockets. If a resident needs to carry a splicing-knife, a sample vial, or a cluster of memory-galls, they simply press the object against their thigh; the fabric’s mycelium instantly unweaves, swallows the tool to the hilt, and knits tight around it until they yank it free with that same wet, Velcro *rip* that characterizes the town’s doors.

They are called the Arborists by the sparse, nomadic traders who occasionally cross the fifty-million-year-old gray flats to trade raw silicon and heavy salts at the enzyme moat, though among themselves they use a title that translates closer to *The Prompts*. 

For all its ferocious, steaming velocity, the settlement has no imagination. The hyper-accelerated timber and the photosynthetic matrices in the canopy are monumental engines of bandwidth and metabolic reaction, capable of calculating atmospheric variables or re-routing megawatts of glucose-power in seconds, but they are entirely reactive. Left to itself, the basin would descend into a frantic, cancerous stutter—growing and dissolving the same three bridges forty times an hour in a feedback loop of pure optimization. 

The residents are the city’s handlebars. They live in a state of continuous, casual choreography with the architecture. 

Watch an older woman walking from an atmospheric distillation tower toward her dwelling on the western edge. She does not follow a path; paths do not last long enough to be named. Instead, she walks a trajectory of intent. As her bare feet—soled in natural, bluish calluses as thick as tire treads—slap against the wet lignum, the pressure of her stride and the specific pheromonal signature of her sweat are read by the surface nerves of the root-mat. Six paces ahead of her, the standing puddles jitter with geometric ripples as the floor calculates her vector. A low, blistering ridge of wood erupts from the mat, hardening in two seconds into a dry, textured stepping-stone just as her left foot comes down. By the time her right foot lifts, the stone behind her is already softening into gray mush, its structural calcium being pumped forward under the floor to build the next step three yards ahead. She is literally walking on a wave of solid matter that the town is rolling out beneath her feet.

To speak in the plaza is to compete with the relentless, cathedral-scale clamor of snapping footbridges, rushing water, and the deep *thud-whoosh* of the xylem valves. Consequently, the residents rarely use their voices for complex matters. Their vocalizations are brief, sharp, and percussive—clicks, two-note whistles, and chest-deep hums designed to cut through the acoustic murk like foghorns, used mostly to call children or warn a neighbor that a spire is about to shear off overhead. 

Their actual language is conducted on their skin, and through the wildlife.

Every resident’s forearms, shoulders, and collarbones are laced with sub-dermal colonies of engineered chromatophores and bioluminescent nodes, borrowed from the same genetic library that produced the town’s fauna. When two residents meet in the wide, empty gallery between the towers, they stand three feet apart in the warm drizzle and *flash*. Conversations play out across their skin in cascading ripples of indigo, bruised violet, and sharp, sulfur yellow—a silent, high-speed grammar of shifting lattices and bars that looks remarkably like the QR-patterned skins of the giant, manhole-sized amphibians plastered to the nearby trunks. 

In fact, the amphibians are often part of the discussion. If an argument requires deep historical context or heavy computation, a resident will casually walk over to the nearest tower, slap a damp palm against the flank of a basking, four-foot toad, and use the creature’s neural plexus as a local terminal to query the canopy’s memory-banks. The toad doesn't mind; it gets a rush of warm glucose from the tree for acting as a modem, letting out a single, floor-shaking bass croak of satisfaction as the data transfers into the resident’s palm.

This total integration with the local fauna makes the humans look, to an outsider, like walking ecosystems. The otter-lemurs—which the locals call *fetch-kin*—use human shoulders as mid-air springboards when the gaps between growing branches prove two yards too wide. A resident won't even flinch when an eight-pound, slick-furred mammal drops out of the milky gold light, hits their collarbone with four webbed feet, and kicks off again into the void, spraying warm puddle-water into their eyes. And those who work as network-tenders walk through the plaza wearing thick smears of pungent, sugary resin behind their ears; they move enveloped in a buzzing, emerald halo of data-midges, their heads inside a spherical cloud of air-gapped information that they edit by sweeping their fingers through the swarm like conductors shaping a chord.

Inside the dwellings, life is warm, peristaltic, and entirely devoid of heirlooms. 

Because a house might need to dissolve its eastern wall to make room for a municipal steam-vent, or drop its floor by four feet to let a massive fiber-optic tuber pass underneath, nobody owns anything that cannot survive being dropped in a warm bog. There are no books, no framed pictures, no ceramics, no dry textiles. Furniture is temporary and grown on demand. When a family gathers to eat, the host scores a shallow mark in the living floor with a bone stylus and drops a pinch of hormone-salt into the wound; within ninety seconds, a table and a ring of cupped, ergonomic stools bulge upward from the lignum, steaming slightly, their surfaces smooth and warm as skin. When the meal—usually a rich, savory paste of fermented tuber-starch, boiled midges, and spiced marsh-crabs—is finished, the leftover scraps are simply pushed onto the center of the table. The wood liquefies at the center, swallows the compost with a slow, gulping contraction, and the entire dining set subsides back into the flat floor, leaving the room empty for sleeping.

They sleep in hammocks of living phloem that rock gently with the pumping of the house’s vascular system, listening to the walls breathe. 

Yet, for all their adaptation to this wet, roaring velocity, the residents have a strange, deeply ingrained habit that betrays the psychological weight of living in a place where nothing lasts twenty minutes.

In the late afternoon, when the milky gold light in the high canopy-opening begins to bruise into twilight and the town’s metabolic rate peaks in a deafening frenzy of evening revisions, you will find groups of residents gathered at the very lip of the basin. They sit on the low rim of ancient, soap-slick stone, their legs dangling just inches above the bubbling, enzyme-rich moat that marks the absolute boundary of their world. 

They turn their backs on the steaming, clicking, dissolving plaza. They sit in total silence—even their skin-lights damped down to a dull, uniform gray—and they stare out at the flat, featureless plain beyond the ring. 

Sometimes, a resident will reach over the moat and pick up a single, unremarkable pebble from the lowland—a piece of gray chert that has not changed its shape, its weight, or its chemical composition in fifty million years. They cannot bring it into the basin; the city’s digestive enzymes would dissolve it for mineral salts within an hour. So they simply hold it in their wet palms for twenty minutes, passing it from hand to hand along the stone lip, rubbing their thumbs over its cold, dead, glorious permanence, before setting it carefully back down in the dust and walking back into the roar of the living wood.
