To find what lives here, you have to stop looking for things with legs, or stomachs, or the fragile, watery cells of animals that bruise when they are pinched. A creature made of meat would be homogenized into paste within four minutes of entering the avenues. 

Yet a city, even a deluded one, demands a citizenry. And the basin has one, though it is currently locked in a fierce disagreement with itself about what a citizen is supposed to be.

Look first at the avenues, down in the mucilaginous slick where the nerve-vine tumbleweeds snap and roll. If you watch the geometric puddles—the ones vibrating in rigid standing waves from the roar of the surrounding timber—you will notice that the grease does not always flow downhill into the fibrous gutters. Some of it slides *across* the camber of the road.

These are the **Lignin-Skimmers**, the closest thing the city has to vermin. A Skimmer has no skin and no organs; it is a flat, dinner-plate-sized lens of dense, non-Newtonian sap and silvered wax, secreted by the friction-vents of the great towers. The acoustic fury of the basin is their metabolism. Driven by the deafening, rhythmic *thwack-boom* of the turgor blowholes and the screech of the canopies, the cymatic vibrations in the ground organize these waxy lenses into frantic, geometrical constellations. They skate across the iron-hard seed-pavement at thirty miles an hour, banking off curbs, splitting in two when they hit a freshly sprouted bollard, and fusing back together on the other side. They feed on the dry lignin dust that snows down from the stuttering, glitched bridges in the central plaza. When a Skimmer catches a drift of that hyper-dense wood-flour, its waxy body flash-hardens into a brittle, six-inch mockery of a curbstone or a clay roof-tile, which sits vibrating on the road for twenty seconds before the heat melts it back into grease and it skates away again.

They are the lowest tier of life here: pure fluid dynamics borrowing a silhouette from the pavement.

Higher up, inside the blind, bark-shuttered rowhouses and the dark rooms of woven thorns that only exist for three hours before noon, live the **Tenants**.

Because the city is constantly building rooms, and because the broken logic of the valley insists that a room is defined by the presence of an occupant, the walls are compelled to birth something to fill the void. When a second-story parlor of hollow bamboo seals itself off from the strobing sunlight, the interior walls immediately begin to weep a thick, pale latex from thousands of tiny pores. Before the latex can drip to the floor, long hair-like tendrils of phloem reach out from opposite walls, catch the droplets, and spin them into the center of the room like a spider bundling a fly. 

Within six minutes, a Tenant hangs suspended in the dead center of the dark room, held aloft by forty taut, thrumming umbilical threads attached to the floorboards, the ceiling, and the wainscoting. 

They are roughly the size of a butchered hog, though their shape is an unstable, bubbling compromise between a chandelier, a dressmaker’s dummy, and a tuber. They have no eyes—there is nothing to see in the dark rooms—and no mouths. Instead, they have dozens of hollow, rigid wooden reeds sticking out of their rubbery torsos like organ pipes. As the neighboring buildings crush against the exterior walls of the room, the air pressure inside spikes and drops violently. The Tenant breathes this pressure. As the room contracts, air is forced through the Tenant’s reed-pipes, and it lets out a long, resonant, multi-tonal honk—a brassy, mournful chord that vibrates at the exact resonant frequency required to stiffen the surrounding walls. 

For two or three hours, the Tenant hangs in the dark, screaming a single continuous structural chord that keeps its own apartment from collapsing under the weight of the boulevard growing on the roof. It is a symbiosis of pure stress: the room feeds the Tenant high-pressure sap through its tethers, and the Tenant’s agonizing, bagpipe-like roar hardens the cellulose of the walls against the neighbors. When the subterranean root-artery inevitably shifts and the building is slated for demolition, the umbilical threads snap. The Tenant drops to the floor, deflates into a flat puddle of starch, and is immediately paved over by the hexagonal tiles of a new foyer.

Then there are the things that actually move through the streets—not rolling blindly like the nerve-vines, and not skating like the grease, but *commuting*.

You will hear them before you see them, because their sound cuts right through the low-frequency roar of the bending ship-timbers. It is a rapid, dry, metallic *clack-clack-clack-clack*, like a boy running a thick iron stick along a mile of wrought-iron fencing.

These are the **Surveyors**, though they might just as easily be called the Masons, or the Bailiffs. They are the only truly independent organisms in the basin, and they are terrifyingly fast. 

A Surveyor is an insectoid frame made entirely of dead, fire-hardened heartwood, ironwood, and braided vascular wire, standing about seven feet tall at the shoulder, though its proportions are all wrong. It has six legs, each one a long, curved leaf-spring of black hornbeam that has been stripped of bark and polished by friction until it gleams like gunmetal. It has no head; where a head should be, it carries a massive, asymmetrical burl—a bowling-ball-sized knot of hyper-dense, tumorous wood that hums with the same spatial sickness as the central plaza. 

Inside the burl, there is no brain, only a labyrinth of hollow acoustic chambers and calcified seeds rattling in dry sockets. The Surveyor navigates by the city’s noise. It feels the hydraulic pressure building under an avenue three blocks away through the soles of its wooden spring-legs; it hears the pitch of a straining Gothic arch and knows the arch will snap in forty seconds.

They do not walk; they ricochet. Because they have no soft tissues to rupture and no blood to pool, they move by throwing themselves into the violence of the architecture. A Surveyor will stand at the bottom of a courtyard, wait for a ten-story terrace to split and telescope upward, and deliberately hook its front claws into the rising shoot-flesh—riding the explosive growth ten stories into the air in three seconds, before kicking off at the apex and crashing through a rose-window of woven briar on the opposite side of the street.

What do they do? They are editors. They are infected with the same architectural delusion as the valley, but theirs is sectarian and manic. 

A pack of four Surveyors will land on a newly erupted gable roof of slate-colored palm fronds. Using heavy, piston-driven mandibles made of flinty seed-coat, they will immediately begin to *prune*. They do not eat what they cut. With blinding, mechanical speed—*clack-clack-snap-thwack*—they shear off the growing tips of the fronds, crimp the high-pressure sap veins with their vices to create deliberate aneurysms in the wood, and jam iron-hard thorns into the cambium layer of the roof. 

The thorns are grafts. They carry a localized, viral instruction set. Within thirty seconds of being stung by a Surveyor, a perfectly ordinary bamboo market-stall will stop trying to be a market-stall and will violently burst into a thirty-foot spiral staircase that leads nowhere, or a row of twenty identical, non-functional dormer windows that immediately begin slamming their shutters in the heat. Having ruined the roof to their exact, incomprehensible specifications, the Surveyors leap off the eaves, catching a rising column of vented mineral steam on broad, folding fans of dry parchment-rind, and glide down into the next district.

Often, two different clades of Surveyors—one carrying the viral instructions for *Colonnade*, the other carrying the instructions for *Buttress*—will meet on the narrow ledge of a third-story bracket-fungus balcony. They do not roar; they have no lungs. They simply crash into one another like dry pine trees falling in a storm. They lock mandibles, their leaf-spring legs kicking out with enough force to punch holes in the mahogany walls behind them, trying to jam their grafting-thorns into the grain of the rival’s central burl. When one succeeds, the loser does not bleed out. Instead, its polished hornbeam legs suddenly sprout pale, frantic roots that nail it to the balcony floor; its polished joints fuse into solid timber; its central burl splits open and blossoms into a massive, weeping chimney of bruised purple flesh that vents hot sap onto the street below. The victor wrenches its mandibles free, leaving one of its own legs behind in the fused wood, and hops away on five springs without a moment's pause, already growing a replacement limb from a bud at its hip.

And finally, at the very center of the basin—where the geometry curdles and the thirty-foot kiosks contain miles of sunlit, internal avenues—there is the **Crowd**.

The city knows that a cathedral requires a congregation, and that a capitol requires a public. Out on the periphery, it tries to solve this with the tumbling balls of nerve-vine, which is a clumsy, suburban guess at traffic. At the center, where the biological ambition is thickest, the city gets much closer to the mark, and the result is far worse.

If you were to peer through one of those dilating briar-sphincters into the impossible, miles-long avenue inside a hollow trunk, you would see them standing beneath the skeletal trees as the solid wood leaves clang like iron plates around their ankles.

From a distance of a hundred yards, in the strobing, high-contrast light, they look like people. Hundreds of them. They stand five to six feet tall, arranged in tight, conversational clusters of three or four on the street corners, or queued up in long, patient lines outside doorways that open onto solid walls of wet pith. They appear to be wearing heavy, sweeping coats and broad-brimmed hats of dark olive and russet.

If you get closer—if you could somehow survive the acoustic hammer of the clicking square buds on the walls—you would see that they are not separate from the street. Each figure is a single, monstrously complex taproot that has grown *upward* out of the hexagonal pavement before peeling back its own bark to mimic the silhouette of a draped coat, a slouching shoulder, a tilted head. 

They are hollow husks of stiff, fibrous gourd-rind, filled with hot, circulating water that keeps them upright through sheer turgor pressure, the way a garden hose stiffens when the tap is turned on. They have no faces under their broad, leafy brims—only a smooth, blank curve of pale yellow wood that is constantly turning toward whichever neighbor is closest.

They cannot walk, because their boots are fused into the road-bed. Instead, they *propagate*. 

To cross the street, a member of the Crowd leans forward until its hollow gourd-chest splits down the seam, releasing a cloud of heavy, grey spores and a pressurized jet of mucilage that splashes three feet away on the pavement. Within four seconds, the pavement where the splash landed blisters, heaves upward, and inflates into an identical coat-and-hat silhouette, while the original husk behind it loses pressure, shrivels like a burnt paper bag, and is sucked back down between the hexagonal tiles. An entire crowd of fifty "people" can cross an avenue in a stuttering, stop-motion wave of inflating and deflating gourds, advancing three feet every four seconds, leaving a wake of vanishing husks behind them.

They stand in the impossible avenues, turning their blank, yellow-wood faces toward one another. As the hot water circulates through the convolute pipes of their hollow chests, it gurgles and hisses through perforated baffles in their throats. To an ear not yet deafened by the valley, it sounds *exactly* like the murmur of a busy train station—a vast, rising and falling wash of consonant-heavy gibberish, full of *shh* and *ch* and low, woody vowels, devoid of a single word, rising to a polite roar as the million square buds on the cathedral walls click open in unison to applaud them, and dropping to a wet, hissing whisper just before the root-artery shifts beneath the floor and the entire impossible avenue is crushed flat to make room for a fresh row of lime-green tenements.
