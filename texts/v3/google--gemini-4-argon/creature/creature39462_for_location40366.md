If you stand on one of the lower, flowstone-encrusted catwalks and hold your breath until the blood stops drumming in your ears, you will eventually realize that the Sump-Cathedral’s acoustic map is changing. 

The dripping of the alkaline sweat and the low, digesting rumble of the six-foot arterials are constant, yet the way those sounds travel through the dark is not. An echo off a distant turbine housing will suddenly dull, as if a heavy blanket has been hung between you and the source. A high, resonant ping from an upper manifold will lower its pitch by half a step, indicating that a mass has just settled onto the pipe, damping its vibration.

Something large is moving through the iron forest, and it is moving by temperature and tension.

They have no name for themselves because they have no voices, and they have no voices because in an environment of competing, continuous frequencies, the air is already full. To speak would be to waste heat. If an outsider were to bring a lamp down into the stifle and catch one in the beam, the immediate impression would not be of an animal, nor of a man, but of an upholstery of the machinery itself that had detached and begun to perambulate.

One might call them the *Lagging*, or the *Tallow-Men*, though their shape is only coincidentally anthropomorphic—a result of needing to reach across the spans designed by long-dead municipal engineers.

At first glance, an individual appears to be a slumped, bulky accumulation of industrial detritus, roughly the mass of a large boar or a crouched human, clinging to the curvature of a warm pipe. They have no skin in the conventional sense; they have an integument. Their outer layer is a dense, stratified coat of felted slag-wool, grease-soaked fuller’s earth, and living *felt-vine* that they have encouraged to root directly into their dorsal fat. This coat is patched and reinforced with flattened plates of botryoidal rust and wide strips of perished vulcanized rubber, all cemented together with a waxy, sebaceous secretion that smells strongly of hot mutton fat and machine oil. This heavy mantle serves a dual purpose: it insulates them against the blistering contact-heat of the unlagged steam-traps, and it renders them acoustically dead. When a fat drop of mineral water falls from the vaulting and hits one of them, it does not *plink* or *patter*; it makes no sound at all.

Beneath this heavy skirt of mineral-felt, their anatomy is low-slung, asymmetrical, and radially stubborn, like the root-ball of a tree pulled from a swamp. They possess four limbs, though there is no clear distinction between arm and leg; each is a thick, multi-jointed column of pale, tallow-colored tendon and slow-twitch muscle, terminating in a broad, calloused pad flanked by two opposed hooks of dense, black keratin. These hooks are not sharp; they are shaped like spanners or pipe-wrenches, curved specifically to mate with the standardized hex-nuts, rivet-heads, and flanged lips of the chamber’s ironwork. They do not walk upon the grated floor if they can help it. They bridge. They hang between two vertical conduits, or brace themselves in the warm V-junctions where the great arterials cross, locking their keratin hooks into the rust-nodules and letting their cartilage-heavy joints go on the latch, so they can hang for days without expending a single calorie of muscular effort.

They have no heads. A head is an expensive radiator of heat and a fragile extremity in a place of low-hanging iron. Instead, their sensory apparatus is distributed across the broad, sloped ridge of their shoulders and the flat underbelly that presses against the pipes. 

Where a face might be, there is only a recessed, vertical cleft lined with moist, pleated membranes the color of pickled walnut. They breathe through this cleft with agonizing slowness—one inhalation every three or four minutes, drawing the thick, sulfurous air over a labyrinth of internal gill-arches that extract both oxygen and airborne tallow-lipids. Flanking this respiratory slit are their primary organs of perception: two wide, shallow pits covered by a taut drumhead of translucent, yellow cartilage. These are not ears, nor are they eyes; they are thermal-acoustic tympanums. 

To a creature of the Sump, the pitch of a pipe and its heat are the same sensation. They perceive the chamber as a three-dimensional architecture of glowing, humming chords. A high-pressure steam line is a bright, tight, singing wire in their minds; a cold, lime-choked drainage sump is a vast, silent hole. They can feel the viscosity of the invisible fluid pulsing through a six-foot arterial through the soles of their gripping-pads, reading the health of the deep earth-pumps miles away by the micro-tremors in the cast iron.

They are creatures of an extremely low metabolic furnace. A human’s quick, nervous movements would seem to them like the frantic buzzing of a wasp before it dies. The Lagging move like cold molasses or creeping pitch. When one decides to relocate from a cooling condenser to a warmer exhaust flue, it first extends a long, whip-like probe from a sheath in its chest—a boneless, muscular tongue tipped with a sensitive cluster of chemo-receptors. This probe snakes out two or three feet into the absolute dark, tasting the iron for copper-salts, testing the air currents for the dry, stinging heat of a steam-leak, and mapping the geography of the rust. Once the path is tasted, a limb unlatches with a dull, woody *clack*, reaches out through the greased dark, finds a flange, and locks on. An hour later, the torso follows.

Because they live in a sealed system of finite energy, they are fastidious, almost monastic husbands of the chamber’s ecology. They do not merely inhabit the plumbing; they tend it, though without any conscious understanding of what the machinery is for. To them, the Sump-Cathedral is a colossal, benevolent beast whose interior they share, and whose fevers must be regulated so that all may continue to simmer in peace.

Their diet is a slow rotation of the chamber’s yields. With their spanner-like claws, they gently pry the *clinker-crabs* from the vertical conduits, not killing them, but holding them upside down to scrape the rich, sulfur-reducing bacterial paste from the crabs' under-carapaces with rasping pads of bone inside their feeding clefts, before setting the crusty arthropods back onto the iron to graze again. They harvest the brittle, blue-green fronds of the *verdigris-ferns*, chewing them into a mineral poultice which they smear into the creases of their joints to prevent rot in the 100-percent humidity. 

Most importantly, they are the keepers of the gaskets. The red *gasket-worms* are a delicacy, fat and rich with iron, yet the Lagging rarely eat them. They understand, through some deep, inherited instinct, that if a flange-joint blows out completely, the resulting lance of high-pressure steam will cook everything within thirty yards and alter the precious thermal balance of the room. Therefore, when a joint begins to hiss and weep excessively, two or three of the creatures will converge upon it over the course of a week. They will pack the leaking seam with clay-like balls of chewed felt-vine, lime-scale, and their own waxy dung, binding it all together with copper wire they have patiently unspooled from the decaying blankets of the arterials. They are the reason the Cathedral has not torn itself apart in the centuries since the surface district was abandoned; they have replaced the failed lead and rubber seals with living, calcified bolsters of their own making.

In the long years between the dropping of the counterweights, their social life is minimal and largely sedentary. They are solitary grazers of the middle tiers, though during the cooler cycles, three or four may gather in the hollow cradle between two sister-pipes, locking their limbs together to form a single, undifferentiated mound of warm, breathing felt. In these huddles, they communicate by pressing their cartilaginous tympanums against one another’s flanks and grinding their internal gizzard-stones—swallowed hex-nuts and ball-bearings—producing low, purring vibrations that travel directly through their bones without ever entering the air. They share memories of heat: the location of a new, warm blister on a deep piling, or the slow death of a tertiary manifold that has finally choked solid with grey lime.

They have only one true ritual, and it is dictated by the telluric gauges and the great chains.

As the years pass and a central counterweight nears the bottom of its pit, the Lagging begin to migrate inward from the brick perimeter. They do not need the violet-amber light of the gauges to know the time is near, yet they are drawn to the brass cages all the same. The phosphorescent oil inside the thick glass tubes has a unique thermal signature—a cold, heavy density that fascinates them. In the weeks before the trip-lever is thrown, dozens of the creatures will hang in the iron rigging around the central pit, their faceless shoulders turned toward the four-inch halos of bruised purple light. The violet glow catches the wet, mother-of-pearl sheen of their greased coats and the pale blue dust of crushed verdigris-fern tucked into their folds, making them look like sagging, grotesque vestments hung in a ruined sacristy.

When the weight finally bottoms out and the hydraulic trip-lever slams home beneath the floor, the sudden, quarter-tone drop in the chamber’s fundamental hum goes through the assembled creatures like an electric shock. For the only time in their lives, they move quickly—a heavy, synchronized shudder that runs through the rigging as fifty massive bodies shift their grip at once.

As the secondary engine wheezes to life and the great chains begin to clank and shudder, hauling the weight back toward the roof, the three-day rain of dislodged condensation begins. This is their spring, their harvest, and their mating season. 

The warm, mineral-heavy deluge washes the accumulated soot and dead skin from their felted backs. The creatures climb into the upper vaulting, where the air is hottest and thickest with the sulfurous exhaust of the waking relief-valves. There, suspended upside down from the ceiling- trusses like slothful, grease-slicked bags, they release clouds of buoyant, waxy spores from pores along their flanks. These spores drift on the violent convective updrafts created by the laboring ballast-pumps, mingling in the dark until they settle into the warm, wet blankets of slag-wool wrapping the six-foot arterials.

There, in the fever-heat of the main pipes, the fertilized nodes will take root like fungi, drawing warmth directly from the iron for three or four cycles of the weights, growing a shell of rust and felt before they ever develop a bone or a claw, until the day they are heavy enough to peel themselves away from the pipe and drop, with a soft, greasy thud, onto the catwalks below.

When the counterweight reaches the ceiling and the secondary engine dies away with a long, bubbling sigh, the rain stops. The violet oil in the gauges begins itsimperceptible, multi-year descent. The adults slowly unhook themselves from the upper trusses and descend back into the middle dark, dispersing along the warm arteries to inspect the joints loosened by the vibration, leaving the upper reaches to the blind crabs, the slow drip of the lime, and the absolute, humming quiet.
