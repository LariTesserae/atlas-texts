At first, if you stand in one of the narrow, shoulder-width slots between two silicate conduits, you will think you are entirely alone, save for the occasional parchment-and-bone hare phasing slowly through a plinth. Your eyes, trained by a lifetime of Euclidean habits, will look for silhouettes that stand *in front* of the matte-black modules. You will look for things that displace air, cast shadows, and walk *around* the buttresses.

You will only notice the inhabitants when your gaze rests for too long on one of the shimmering fault lines—where a gray ceramic block and a frosted metal pillar are locked in their slow-motion, double-exposed argument—and you realize there is a *third* exposure in the overlap. 

They are called the *Intercalated*, or simply the Wedged, though they have no vocal apparatus to name themselves. They are not visitors to the sinkhole; they are a phenomenon of its congestion, native to the overlap. 

At a glance, an individual looks like a tall, gaunt parcel of geometry that has been folded once or twice too often. If you could somehow extract one from the architecture and stand it in an open field, it might faintly resemble a human figure wrapped in heavy, stiff, wet felt, or a tall cairn of flat, greasy slates balanced on two articulated stilts. Its "skin" has the exact texture of the local condensation: a semi-solid, tallow-like film of grey-white grease that holds the biting cold at bay. Beneath this slick epidermis, its structure is a shifting armature of flat, spatula-shaped plates of dark bone and calcified cartilage, arranged like the blades of a heavy shutter.

But you will never see one in an open field. You will never even see the entirety of one at the same time. 

The Wedged do not live *in* the alleys; they live in the *intersections*. They are creatures of high spatial density. To a Wedged, an open alley—even one barely two feet wide—is an uncomfortable, thin-aired vacuum. They require the pressure of at least two, and preferably three, colliding solids to feel structurally sound. A typical specimen will be found standing half-submerged in the seam where an unfolding slate staircase is driving diagonally through a ribbed conduit. Its left side is inside the stone; its right side is inside the spun glass; its central axis is the shimmering, humming fault line itself. 

Because they inhabit multiple planes of poorly compiled matter simultaneously, their anatomy is discontinuous to an outside observer. You might see a pale, flat-jointed forearm and a three-fingered hand—resembling a mason’s trowel made of wet ivory—protruding directly from the solid face of a basalt hex-plate, slowly scraping a ribbon of translucent lichen into a vertical slit of a mouth that is hovering three inches to the left, entirely inside the ghost-image of a passing metal beam. The rest of the body isn't missing; it is simply rendered in an adjacent coordinate set that is currently losing the argument for visual dominance. As the buildings flicker in their slow battle for space, the creature flickers too. For four seconds you see the hunched, laminated plates of its back; then the metal buttress asserts itself, and you see only the creature's internal organs—which look like clusters of dark, mercury-filled bladders and copper-colored fibrous roots—suspended in the frosted steel like insects in amber; then the ceramic block wins, and you see the creature's narrow, eyeless head, slick with greasy moisture, tilted as it listens to the stone.

They move at the speed of the sinkhole’s geology, or just slightly faster. Their locomotion is not walking, which requires friction against a surface, but a form of *index-shifting*. 

To travel from one cluster of machinery to another, a Wedged leans its mass into a solid wall, adjusts the angles of its internal bone-plates until they match the grain of the material’s extrusion, and allows the rising or shifting architecture to carry it. When a stairway telescopes out of a plinth at millimeters per hour, it often brings a Wedged up with it, the creature riding within the stone like a knot rising in a growing tree. When they must cross an open gap between two towers, they do not step across; they wait where a cantilevered beam is slowly growing toward an opposite wall, or they follow the sideways-falling arcs of heavy water droplets, using the warped, horizontal gravity wells near the denser machinery to slide across the alley in a slow, stiff-limbed drift, catching themselves on the opposite lintel with a soft, greasy *smack* that sounds just like one of the giant drops of water hitting the floor.

Their metabolism is entirely tied to the thermodynamic friction of the colliding architecture. Like the wire-thin reeds, they are cold-blooded until they find a glitch. They press their flat, wide torsos directly into the humming seams where solid pushes through solid. The sub-audible thrumming vibrates through their shutter-like ribs, warming their mercury-dense fluids. In return, they act as a living lubricant for the broken math of the settlement. 

Left entirely to itself, a severe spatial collision—say, three conduits and a pillar trying to occupy the same cubic meter—can occasionally lock up, building a dangerous, silent pressure of uncompiled mass that threatens to shatter the local geometry into sharp, non-existent shards. The Wedged are drawn to these high-pressure knots from across the basin. Four or five of them will converge on the site, sliding through the walls from different angles until they are all overlapping in the same cramped, shimmering node. 

This is the closest they come to social behavior. They press into one another, a tangle of slick, gray-white limbs and clicking bone-plates occupying the exact same coordinates. They do not mate or speak; they simply *align*. By shifting their own internal geometries, acting as biological shims and wedges, they redistribute the spatial load. They drink the excess heat, lick away the heavy, sideways-dripping condensation, and graze on the thick mats of moss that choke the fault line. Over the course of several hours, the frantic flickering of the colliding walls slows to a gentle, stable pulse. The knot is eased. The architecture continues its blind, upward extrusion, now seamlessly incorporating the repairs, and the creatures slowly phase apart, drifting back into the walls down separate vectors.

They have no eyes, as the flat, pearl-gray light above never changes and offers no information that cannot be felt. Instead, the skin across their skulls is stretched tight over hollow, fluid-filled sinuses that act as seismographs and barometers for spatial density. They can feel the "weight" of a solid wall twenty feet away; they can sense the birth of a new hexagonal plinth rising from the floor by the subtle shift in the local gravity gradient; they can hear the slow, agonizing *tick* of cooling metal as if it were a struck bell.

Their relationship with the parchment-and-bone hares is one of patient, terrifyingly slow predation—or perhaps simply reabsorption. A hare will be pausing on a stepped conduit, its body folded to mimic a corner. It will not notice that the corner it is mimicking is already occupied. Slowly, over the course of twenty minutes, the flat, trowel-like hands of a Wedged will emerge from the stone *inside* the hare's own volume. There is no struggle, no blood, and no sound. The predator simply adjusts the angle of its bone-plates to match the hare’s joints, and then shifts its own spatial priority. When the wall flickers next, the hare is no longer phased with the air of the alley; it has been pulled into the intersection, wedged permanently between the ceramic and the steel, where the larger creature will slowly, methodically assimilate its folded parchment skin and calcium over the course of a week.

Nothing rots in the sinkhole, and so the Wedged do not die in any way we would recognize. 

As an individual grows very old—and in the shadowless, unmoving Tuesday of the basin, "old" is a measure of mass rather than time—it absorbs more and more of the silicate dust, the dissolved slate in the greasy water, and the sheer mathematical rigidity of its environment. Its internal shutter-plates fuse. Its movements slow from millimeters per minute to millimeters per hour, matching the exact rate of the settlement’s extrusion. 

Eventually, an ancient Wedged will find a particularly complex intersection deep at the bottom of the sinkhole—a place where a bridge, a blind window, a staircase, and a pillar are all knotted together—and it will simply stop adjusting its angle. The rising basalt plates will pass through its legs; the silicate pipes will rib themselves through its chest. The creature’s pale, greasy skin will harden into matte-black module casing or gray ceramic. 

If you look very closely at the oldest, lowest structures in the basin, where the spatial overlapping is at its thickest and the humming is strong enough to make your teeth ache, you can see them: faint, curves of bone-like stone and calcified, three-fingered buttresses, completely fused into the architecture, holding up a lintel that leads nowhere, still slightly warm to the touch, sweating heavy, viscous drops of water that take minutes to fall.
