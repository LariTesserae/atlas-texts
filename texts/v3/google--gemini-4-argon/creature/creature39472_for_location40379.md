Look closer at the shingle-things first—the ones that form the living slate of the upper walls. 

From a distance of a few feet, in the sour green smear of the grease-moss light, they appear to be individual shells, each the size of a round shield. Wait for ten minutes, however, watching a single overlapping row, and the seam between two carapaces will part with a wet, velcro-like tearing sound. What looks like leather-covered bone is actually a stiffened, keratinous mantle, and beneath it, the creatures are not discrete at all. Their undersides are a pale, mucilaginous gray, fused together at the margins by threads of shared vascular tissue, like oyster-beds that have forgotten how to separate. 

They are *the Tilers*. Their wing-flaps—vestigial, heavy aprons of gristle—do not beat; they slowly contract and expand like the bellows of an organ, drawing the impaled cloud-mist down through the overlapping flue of their own bodies. As the mist passes over their gill-books, they strip it not only of its scarce oxygen, but of the wind-borne dust, the microscopic quartz-flour, and the black volcanic ash from the lower peaks. 

Inside their shallow, dish-like bodies, they have no true stomachs, only a labyrinth of ciliated grooves that sort this mineral grit by weight and sharpness. Over months, a Tiler secretes a pearlescent, high-protein mucus around the finest grains, rolling them between its gill-leaves until it coughs up a heavy, putty-colored pellet the size of a plum. 

These pellets do not fall to the floor. They roll into the troughs between the Tilers' shells, where the pale climbers are waiting.

The climbers—the knots of six-limbed, shivering skin-bags wedged into the basalt cracks—are only the juveniles and the warming-caste of the thing that sits at the bottom of the pit. If you trace a knot of them into a fissure, you find they are tangled not by accident, but by the dry, fibrous tendons of their own overly long toes, which lock together in a winter-grip that requires hours of conscious chemical relaxation to undo. Their skin is the color of lard and entirely hairless, because hair traps the freezing condensation and turns to fatal rime; instead, they are coated in a thick, tallowy sebaceous grease that smells strongly of rancid mutton and iron.

They have no eyes—only sunken, coin-sized pits of dark, heat-sensitive skin on their flat faces, which allow them to see the cleft as a shifting topography of faint infrared blooms. Their chests are disproportionately huge, barrel-like cages of cartilage housing lungs that take up two-thirds of their body cavity, pumping with a slow, double-valved *thrum-click* that vibrates through the stone. 

When a climber grows old—which takes perhaps two hundred years at this metabolic crawl—it loses the flexibility in its lower two pairs of limbs. The grease on its skin oxidizes and hardens into a gray, flaky bark. Its bulk increases, its center of gravity drops, and it can no longer hold to the vertical knives of the wall. It descends, inch by agonizing inch, over the course of an autumn, down through the living tapestries of its kin, until its heavy, ossified knuckles touch the talus of the floor.

Here, on the tilted orchard of stone, it becomes one of the *Notchers*.

Up close, a Notcher is the size of a stunted bear, though shaped like a gargoyle that has half-melted into the rock it sits upon. Its four lower limbs are folded permanently beneath its groin, the joints calcified into a broad, four-cornered pedestal that anchors it in the black silt of the puddles. Only its upper arms remain mobile: enormously long, double-jointed levers of bone and slow-twitch muscle, ending in hands with two opposing thumbs and three thick, spatulate fingers tipped with black, horn-like nails that grow continuously to replace what the basalt wears away.

They do not have mouths in the way lower-altitude beasts do. The jaw is fused; there is only a soft, sphincter-like siphon beneath the heat-pits, through which they suck the protein-pellets dropped by the Tilers, and the starchy, fermented mash of the pale tubers that grow in their own waste. 

Because they do not need to hunt, and cannot flee, and because their huge, dark brains are fed by blood that moves like cold molasses, their consciousness does not experience time as a river, but as a geology. To a Notcher, a falling stone is not an event; it is a weather system that has already passed before the optic nerve could have registered it. A human standing among them, breathing sixty times a minute, would be invisible—a brief, warm gust of wind, a phantom flicker of infrared static no more substantial than a spark from a flint.

What they can perceive is the mountain.

At their speed, the cleft is not stationary. The cleft is *violent*. Newly split, the black basalt spire is still settling into the crust of the earth, and under the immense tectonic pressure of the range, the rock is screaming. To the Notchers, whose calcified pedestals are grown directly into the gravel and the bedrock fractures, the mountain is a live, bucking thing. They feel the microscopic creep of the fault line; they hear the ultra-low-frequency groans of the inward-leaning walls as the gap narrows by half a millimeter a year; they feel the build-up of piezoelectric static in the quartz veins before a frost-shatter occurs.

This is why they knap the glass. This is why they saw the grooves.

Watch one of the old ones sitting by a black puddle, its chest humming its three-note chord—*oom-click-humm*—once every forty seconds. It picks up a cobble of obsidian. It does not look at it. It presses the stone against its heat-pits to read the internal stresses of the glass, feeling where the ancient cooling of the lava left invisible knots of tension. Then, taking a heavy basalt wedge, it applies pressure. Not a strike—a strike is too fast for their nerves to coordinate—but a slow, hydraulic lean, putting three hundred pounds of leverage onto a single point the size of a pinhead until, with a sharp *tink*, a ribbon-thin blade of glass peels away.

The blade is useless to them. They have nothing to cut. What matters is the *core*—the three-dimensional polygon of stone left behind, now relieved of its internal tension, shaped into a precise, faceted wedge.

The cairns in the center of the floor are not stockpiles of weapons. If you were to pull one apart—risking the slow, crushing retaliation of twenty heavy hands converging on you over the next hour—you would find that the hand-axes and wedges are fitted together without mortar, bevel to bevel, facet to facet, with such microscopic precision that not even the condensation can seep between them. They are building dead-weights. They are constructing massive, interlocking lithic plugs directly over the hairline fractures in the floor, pinning the skin of the mountain down with its own rearranged bones to stop the floor from buckling upward.

And the notches on the walls—that dense, chaotic braille that covers the lower ten feet of the cliff—are not a tally of days. There are no days in the permanent purple gloom. 

Put your fingertips into a fresh groove, right beside the calloused, horn-nailed hand of a worker sawing with its splinter of slate. Feel the rock three inches to the left. There is a hairline crack there, invisible in the dark, where the freezing water has wedged itself into the basalt and is preparing to split a two-ton slab off the wall—a slab that would crush five hundred climbers and peel a thirty-foot swath of Tilers into the abyss.

The Notcher has felt the stone swelling. By sawing a groove parallel to the micro-fracture, one millimeter an hour, week after week, it is creating a relief-joint. It is bleeding the mechanical stress out of the rock face, redirecting the fault line away from the living nursery above and guiding it harmlessly down into the talus. 

The jutaan of overlapping marks are the scar tissue of ten thousand prevented rockfalls. The entire lower wall is a diagram of force, read by blind hands and answered with slate. 

Sometimes, though, the mountain moves too fast for them. A deep freeze hits the peak, the ice expands in a high cleft out of reach of the saws, and a pillar of stone gives way. 

When it falls, crashing through the dark, tearing through the Tilers and the tangled macaque-things in a spray of black blood and shattered chitin, the silence that follows is not shock. It is calculation. For twenty minutes, nothing moves. The hydraulic roar drops to a whisper. Every pedestal on the floor feels the new distribution of weight in the spire; every heat-pit reads the sudden,bright red splash of spilled entrails cooling on the stone.

Then, with the awful, inexorable deliberation of a glacier changing course, the nearest Notchers turn their heads. Those whose saws are no longer needed at the old stress-points drop their slate. Their long, pale arms reach out into the dark, fingers splaying through the silt, feeling for the fresh, razor-sharp pieces of the fallen pillar—already testing the new stone for its flaws, already beginning to stack it back against the dark.
