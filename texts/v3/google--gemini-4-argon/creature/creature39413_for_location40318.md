To see what lives here, you have to stop looking for movement and start looking for an error in the masonry.

Down near the choked nadir, where the bulbous terraces crowd so closely together that a human ribcage would be wedged fast between them, there is a section of the wall where the concentric rings appear to bulge slightly outward. The tiles here are just as bone-white as the rest, just as rounded by the dry wind, and just as coated in fine, floury kaolin dust. 

Then, the bulge shifts. It does not slide or crawl; it *indexes*. With a dry, percussive *clack*—a sound instantly swallowed by the roaring panpipe chord of the pit—a cluster of fifty or sixty tiles drops a fraction of an inch, rotating like the tumblers of a lock. 

They are not tiles. Or rather, they were not always tiles. 

The inhabitants of the basin have no soft parts left to desiccate. Whatever wet, biological ancestors fell or crawled into this acoustic furnace ages ago have long since surrendered their water to the updraft and their flesh to the mineral dust. What remains is an organism that is ninety-eight percent ceramic—a colonial, lithophagic creature that exists somewhere between a wasp, a barnacle, and a geological fault. 

Individually, they are called the *Shims*. 

A single Shim is the size and shape of a broad, flat thumb. Its body is a laminate of calcified chitin and ingested kaolin, pressed into a hard, matte biscuit by the crushing tightness of the crevices in which it grows. It has no eyes—there is nothing to see in the blinding, shadowless glare except more glare—and no ears to be ruptured by the deafening, multi-tonal hum. Instead, its flat underside is a dense mat of thousands of microscopic, silica-stiffened bristles. 

These bristles are its legs, its mouth, and its nervous system. 

A Shim does not walk. It wedges itself on edge between the ancient pottery shards and uses the constant, roaring vibration of the basin to move. By stiffening or relaxing different patches of its bristles against the humming clay, it rides the acoustic wave, ratcheting itself forward or backward a millimeter an hour through the dry, roaring interstices. It is cymatic locomotion; if the wind above the plain ever stopped, the Shims would be paralyzed, locked forever into the silent wall.

Left alone, a single Shim is barely alive. It is a self-driving shard of crockery. It uses its serrated front edge to saw away at the woody, glass-brittle stems of the wire-lichen, grinding the dry plant matter together with scraped ceramic powder. It binds this paste with a microscopic secretion of dry, alkaline spittle—the only moisture it possesses, jealously recycled in a closed internal loop—to repair its own abraded shell.

But the Shims are rarely alone. They are compulsive architects, driven by the single, blind imperative that defines the basin: *tuning*.

Because the Shims navigate and communicate entirely through the vibration of the stacked tiles, an irregularity in the roaring chord of the pit is to them what a foul stench or a blinding light is to creatures of the surface. When the wind sands a terrace down too far, or when a patch of silver-grey scrub grows thick enough to baffle a vent, the local pitch of the wall goes flat. A sour overtone enters the hum.

Slowly, over days, the Shims converge on the dissonance. 

They滑—*ratchet, clack, ratchet*—through the tight seams between the terraces, emerging from the deep rubble of the floor or sliding down the stepped rings like heavy white droplets. When a few dozen of them reach the offending gap, they pack themselves into it, stacking on their edges in tight, concentric rings that perfectly mimic the ancient masonry around them. They wedge themselves in with immense, hydraulic patience, locking their silica bristles together until they form a single, rigid unit—a living structural repair patch that brings the resonant frequency of the terrace back into the great, hollow chord.

They stay like that for years. 

In fact, a vast percentage of the basin’s lower walls is no longer the original, ancient pottery at all. It is a necropolis of Shims. When a Shim finally exhausts its tiny, internal reserve of alkaline moisture and dies, it does not rot or fall away. It simply ceases to ratchet. Locked under the immense pressure of the surrounding tiles, its pale, pinched shell remains exactly where it wedged itself, indistinguishable from the jutaan of thumb-shaped biscuits that form the pit. The living crawl over and between the dead, using the corpses of their predecessors as the raw material of their home.

Yet, if you were to stand wedged in the throat of the depression, enduring the cellar-chill and the skull-filling roar, and if you were to force your bleached, squinting eyes to focus on the standing waves of dust that hang motionless in the vents, you would realize that the Shims are not the only things here. 

The dust in those standing waves is too organized.

Where the roaring updrafts meet and cancel each other out, creating pockets of dead, suspended air, the abraded kaolin powder does not just hang; it forms a dense, trembling lattice. Deep within that white fog lives the *Silt-Weir*.

It has no body you could pick up in your hand. If you swept your fingers through the standing wave of dust, you would come away with only a dry, chalky smear on your skin and a few snapped threads of something that feels like spun glass. 

The Silt-Weir is a colonial spider-thing that has abandoned the concept of a central body entirely, distributing itself into a three-dimensional geometric web of crystalline mucus and electrostatic filaments anchored to the trembling tips of the wire-lichen. The "spiders" themselves are no larger than flecks of ground pepper—tiny, desiccated nodes of pale yellow nerve-tissue and chitinous hooks that sit at the intersections of the web. 

They do not hunt insects. They hunt the dust itself.

As the roaring draft pulls upward through the millions of ceramic interstices, it carries with it microscopic flakes of dead lichen, abraded particles of Shim-shell, and trace minerals leached from the deep earth below the rubble. When this roaring stream hits an acoustic dead-zone, the Silt-Weir is waiting. Using the static electricity generated by the constant friction of dry air on dry clay, the colony charges its glass-like threads. The nutrient-rich dust is yanked out of the air stream and snaps onto the filaments, building up into thick, powdery rime.

The pepper-fleck nodes slide along the threads, harvesting the chalky buildup, digesting the microscopic organic traces within the dust, and excreting pure, sterile kaolin powder back into the air. 

To look at a Silt-Weir is to see a ghost made of drywall powder. Because its threads are positioned along the exact nodes of the acoustic standing waves, the entire web vibrates in sympathy with the basin's chord. It looks like a solid, matte-white geometric sculpture—a series of nested, bone-white cones and hyperbolas hanging in mid-air between the bulbous terraces, trembling so rapidly that its edges blur into the glare. 

Between the Shims in the walls and the Weirs in the vents, there is a slow, dry war. 

A Silt-Weir needs the acoustic dead-zones to feed; if the air moves too fast, its brittle threads shatter. Therefore, the tiny nodes are constantly anchoring new tension-lines to the surrounding tiles, subtly pulling the masonry out of alignment, trying to widen the vents and create more pockets of baffled, quiet air.

The Shims feel this quietness as an itch, a hole in the world's song. 

Inevitably, a column of Shims will come ratcheting up through the crevices toward a Silt-Weir’s anchor points. They move with the glacial, unstoppable force of shifting tectonic plates. When they reach the rim of the vent, they begin to wedge themselves in, narrowing the aperture, choking off the dead-zone, and re-tuning the pipe. 

As the airflow changes and the roaring draft slices into the quiet pocket, the Silt-Weir does not flee. It cannot. Its lower threads snap with tiny, crystalline *pings* lost in the roar. The standing wave of dust collapses. Half the colony is ripped away, sucked straight up the vertical throat of the basin and spat out into the pale slate sky of the plain above, looking like nothing more than a brief, faint puff of white smoke emerging from a hole in the ground. 

The surviving nodes of the Weir simply retreat a few inches into the newly created eddy beside the Shims' bodies, spin new glass threads, charge them with static from the roaring wind, and begin to gather the dust again—including the fresh powder scraped from the shells of the very Shims that just destroyed their home.

Nothing else moves. The light blares down, bouncing off the dead tiles and the living tiles alike. The chill air roars upward, playing the graveyard of the builders, while the walls slowly, millimeter by century, chew themselves into a slightly different shape.
