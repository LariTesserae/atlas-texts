If you stand twenty yards downwind of Rig Four-Four—the one they call the Cougher because of the hitch in its secondary exhaust valve—and you hold your breath until the blood hammers in your ears, you will eventually hear a sound that does not belong to the iron or the mire.

It is the sound of brass touching bone. *Tink. Tink. Tink-tink.*

A pause, while the massive piston ten yards away slams down into the limestone sumps—*THUMP-sssshh*—and then the little sound resumes, riding in the trough of the machine’s acoustic wake. *Tink-tink-tink.*

That is a Greaser checking the tension on a stay-wire with the side of his thumbnail.

They do not call themselves Greasers, of course. They do not call themselves anything that can be rendered in a mouth built for dry air and open skies. Their language has no nouns for things that stand still, because in the dark, a thing that does not vibrate, drip, hum, or smell does not exist. To name a thing is to mimic its resonance. A man is his gait and the specific whistle of his nostrils; a woman is the weight of her tread in the sponge-mire and the pitch of her throat-click when she is pleased.

If you could strike a magnesium flare—an act of violence here that would leave nothing but bewildered confusion in the things around you, though the sudden, biting smell of burnt metal would send them scrambling—you would see them frozen on the lower catwalks and the slick limestone aprons around the rig’s pilings.

At first, you would mistake them for growths upon the architecture, or massive, pale grubs that have crawled out of the fern-mattresses to warm their bellies against the boiler-casings. They are squat, immensely broad at the hip and shoulder, built with the low, unshakeable center of gravity required by creatures who spend their lives walking on wet clay and greased iron in pitch blackness. Their skin has the color and heavy, translucent sheen of tallow, though it is never clean; from birth, it is cured in a mixture of peat-slime, rendered slug-fat, and the heavy, sulfur-sweet mineral oil that weeps from the rig’s packing glands. The grease keeps the perpetual rain from rotting their flesh, and it makes them slick enough to slide out of the jaws of the great, blind mud-eels that hunt the terrace basins.

They have no eyes. Where an orbital socket ought to be, there is only a smooth, continuous sweep of thick brow-bone covered in fine, velvety skin, extraordinarily rich in nerves. If you bring your open palm within six inches of a Greaser’s face, they will flinch—not because they see the hand, but because that flat sweep of brow feels the radiant heat of your blood and the microscopic compression of the humid air between your skin and theirs.

Their heads are carried low, canted slightly forward on necks as thick as fire-plugs, so that their ears are always aimed at the ground or the iron. And what ears they are: great, ribbed, cartilaginous sails that flare out from the sides of the skull, mobile as the ears of a mule, constantly twitching, cupping, and furling to filter the deafening wall of the Sump’s noise. They can fold those ears flat against their skulls with a wet *slap* when a high-pressure steam pipe ruptures ten feet away, or flare them wide to catch the sub-audible *whump* of a spore-bladder bursting half a mile out in the fern-beds.

Wait long enough by the Cougher, and a family unit will come down the service ladder from the upper cooling decks.

They do not climb like men; they descend like heavy, methodical apes, four-limbed and sure. Their feet are wide as spades, the big toe diverged nearly at a right angle from the others, calloused into horn so thick they can walk across hot manifold pipes without blistering. Even before they reach the bottom rung, their hands are out, lightly brushing the wet iron.

Watch the grandmother—the *Deep-Bell*, they call her, through a low, guttural hum that mimics the resonance of her broad chest. She is seventy years old, scarred across the flank from an encounter forty years ago with a snapping flywheel belt, and she knows the Cougher better than she knows the shape of her own skull.

As she steps off the ladder onto the trembling stone plinth, she does not hesitate. She walks straight toward the whirling, thirty-foot blur of the primary flywheel. The rim of that wheel is moving at sixty miles an hour, flinging scalding water and gobs of black grease into the dark. A step too far to the left, and the cast-iron spokes would turn her into red mist before the sound of the impact could reach the top of the tower.

She does not take that step. She stops with her broad, flat nose three inches from the gale of the wheel’s slipstream. She lets the hot wind part the coarse, sparse bristles on her scalp. She opens her mouth—wide, showing teeth worn flat by a diet of gritty tubers and tough crawler-meat—and she *tastes* the wind coming off the iron.

Her tongue is thick, deeply fissured, and sensitive to the finest chemical shifts in the mist. In that gale, she tastes the temperature of the main bearing. She tastes a microscopic scorching of tallow that tells her the oil-wick on the eastern axle is running dry. Beneath the thunder of the wheel, her splayed feet read the stone plinth, feeling a tiny, jagged harmonic in the foundation—a bolt three feet down in the mud has backed off half a turn from its nut.

She makes a sharp, double-clicking sound against the roof of her mouth—*tchk-tchk*—and her grandsons, two thick-bodied youths naked except for harness-straps of cured bladder-skin, move instantly through the dark. They do not bump into one another. Each is humming a continuous, low-breath note through his nose—*mmmm-uh, mmmm-ah*—a personal sonar beacon that lets every member of the clan track every other member’s position to the inch within the roar of the rig.

One boy swings himself down into the sump-pit beneath the piston rod, timing his drop between the downward strokes of the two-ton crosshead with the casual grace of a child jumping into a turning rope. *THUMP-sssshh.* Down he goes into the hot, oily water. Four seconds. He wedges a broad wooden shim against the loose foundation nut, hits it twice with a brass maul he carries looped to his wrist—brass, because iron sparks, and sometimes the marsh-gas pockets around the rigs are thick enough to catch—and pulls himself out just as the air compresses above his head and the piston plunges back down. *THUMP-sssshh.*

They are not mechanics. They do not know why the pistons plunge, or where the great, sweating cast-iron pipes take the brine after it is pumped into the stone aqueducts that run toward the plateau's rim. If you told them that once, three hundred years ago, men with eyes built these engines to leech lithium and heavy salts from the deep crust for a sky-borne empire that has since forgotten this world, the words would have no meaning.

To the people of the Sump, the rigs are simply the *Old Loud Ones*. They are massive, stationary beasts of iron and brass that drink the deep soup of the earth, sweat sweet water, and breathe out the heat that keeps the world alive. And like any great beast, the Old Loud Ones must be groomed by the small things that live in their fur, or else they sicken, their rhythms falter, they grow cold, and the creeping mire chokes them to death.

A dead rig is a tragedy that haunts their songs for ten generations.

Thirty miles west of the Cougher stands a silent rig—a cold mountain of rust that threw its main rod two centuries ago and tore its own boiler open. The clans call that place the *Hole-in-the-Drum*. Because it makes no sound, it is an acoustic abyss. To walk near it is to go deaf and blind at once; there is no rhythmic beat to bounce off the trees, no vibration in the mud to map the sinkholes, no thermal rain to wash the spores from the air. Only the silent, creeping things live there now—the cold-bellied spiders and the pale, soft worms that eat dead iron. The clans will walk ten miles out of their way through waist-deep mire just to keep a living rig’s heartbeat between them and that terrible, quiet place.

So they groom the iron.

Come up into the lower latticework of the Cougher’s cooling tower, where the warm brine cascades down through forty feet of slick, mineral-encrusted redwood slats. Here, out of the reach of the mud-eels and the heavy, shuffling predators of the fern-mats, the clan builds its rookery.

It is a marvel of blind weaving. Using cured strips of tough fern-leather and the fibrous roots of the sponge-mire, they have slung great, communal hammocks and bulbous, basket-like pods between the vibrating iron struts directly beneath the hot-water troughs. The constant, ninety-degree waterfall pours over the roofs of their woven pods, keeping them at a steady, womb-like fever-heat.

Inside a pod, the space is cramped, slick with body-oil, and smells overwhelmingly of crushed mint—which they bruise and rub into the wickerwork to drive off ticks—mixed with wood-rot, musk, and wet iron. There is no furniture. There are no possessions that cannot be hung from a belt or wedged into the weave of the walls.

Seven or eight of them sleep here at a time, piled together like amphibians in a winter burrow, limbs draped over bellies, chins resting on shoulders. In the absolute dark, touch is not an event; it is the baseline of existence. To be out of reach of another body for more than an hour makes a Greaser anxious, causing them to click their teeth and sway until their shoulder finds a wall or a kinsman.

Even in sleep, their bodies are tuned to the Cougher. Their breathing rises and falls with the sixteen-stroke cycle of the main condenser. If the rig’s governor slips a tooth and the engine speeds up by three revolutions a minute, every sleeping body in the rookery will groan, shift, and wake before the first check-valve has finished clattering.

Right now, two elders are awake in the pod, sitting knee-to-knee in the wet heat, talking.

They do not speak out loud—why waste breath competing with the waterfall? Instead, they have their hands clasped around each other’s forearms, thumbs pressed firmly against the pulse-points at the wrist, fingers splayed across the tendons. They speak by flexing their fingers in complex, rapid-fire chords, tapping out rhythms against skin and bone. It is a silent, muscular gossip—who found a clutch of soft-shelled crawler eggs in the northern condenser trough; how the grease-trough on the third level is beginning to taste of copper; how a young male from the *Three-Hiss* rig, six miles south, was heard singing in the fern-beds last night, his voice booming out across the mire in the half-second gaps between the steam-hammers.

Outside the pod, perched on a slick cross-beam, a girl of twelve is hunting.

She has wedged her heels into a rivet-seam and leans out into the warm deluge of the cooling tower, holding a three-pronged bone leister in both hands. Her great ears are cupped upward toward the wooden slats above her head.

Up there in the dark, a colony of six-limbed crawlers is clinging to the timber, clicking their mandibles in time with the rig’s check-valves: *clack-clack, clack-clack*.

The girl listens. The rig goes *clack-clack*. The crawlers go *clack-clack*. To an outsider, it is a single, metallic rattle.

To the girl, one of those clicks is three milliseconds late, and its tone is slightly dampened by the fat belly of a gravid female crawler pressing against wet redwood.

She does not aim. Aiming is for eyes. She simply aligns the geometry of her shoulders with the geometry of her ears, feels the tension of her own muscles, and thrusts the bone spear upward into the falling water.

A wet crunch. A frantic, scraping shriek of chitin against wood that breaks the rhythm of the tower. She hauls the spear back down, whipping the heavy, terrier-sized beast against the iron strut to crack its head before its mandibles can find her wrist, and then slides it into her harvesting sack without ever breaking her stance.

When the clans meet, they do not meet at the rigs. The rigs are too loud for diplomacy or courtship; the iron drowns out subtlety.

Instead, every forty or fifty cycles of the great rim-thunder—time is measured by the periodic collapse of the distant peat-mesas or the long, slow blow-off cycles of the deep-fault geysers—three or four clans will walk out into the middle of the mire, to a neutral basin equidistant from their home engines.

They call these places the *Still-Bowls*, though they are anything but still. They are shallow, mile-wide amphitheaters of weeping limestone filled with knee-deep, blood-warm water and carpeted with the humming, bass-droning heaps of velvet amphibians.

Here, the three rigs can be heard at once, distant and softened by five miles of wet fern-forest. From the north comes the *thump-hiss* of the Cougher; from the south-east, the rapid, triple-beat *chug-chug-chug* of the Three-Hiss; from the west, the long, mournful, metallic *whoooo-clank* of the Deep-Sinker.

In the center of the flooded basin, the three rhythms cross, interfere, and weave into a vast, complex polyrhythm.

Two hundred Greasers wade into the warm water. As they enter the basin, they slap the flat of their hands against the water’s surface, sending sharp, hydraulic cracks through the pool to announce their weight and number. The amphibians scatter, their bass drone breaking into a chorus of startled, bubbling trills.

And then the dancing begins.

It is a terrifyingly heavy, splashes-and-slaps affair in the pitch black. The men and women form great, concentric rings in the knee-deep water, their hands linked at the neck and shoulder, and begin to stomp. They stomp in the exact cross-rhythm where the three distant engines meet—*boom-splash, boom-splash, boom-boom-splash*—turning the entire limestone basin into a single, miles-wide timpani.

They throw their heads back, opening their throats to produce sounds you would never believe could come from a vaguely human chest. They have vestigial vocal pouches under their jaws—not as large as the amphibians’, but resonant enough—and they sing in split-toned chords, one note humming low in the chest like a brass boiler-tube, the other whistling high and piercing through the nasal cavities like escaping steam.

A young man looking for a mate will break from his clan’s ring and dance solo in the dark water between the groups. He does not show off his face; he shows off his *presence*. He leaps and drops his squat, heavy bulk into the mud so hard the shockwave travels through the ankles of every woman within fifty yards. He drums on his own greased chest, his wet belly, his thick thighs, rattling the bone-and-brass ornaments woven into his belt to prove his agility, his wind, and the density of his bones.

If a woman from another rig likes the weight of his splash and the smell of his sweat—which carries the distinct chemical signature of the deep fault his home-engine taps—she will wade out into the center and try to catch him.

He will try to evade her, moving only during the thunder-claps of the distant steam-hammers to mask the swirl of water around his legs. She hunts him by ear and by the heat-wake he leaves in the damp air. It is a violent, laughing, breathless game of blind tag played in the mud; when she finally tackles him, they go under the warm water in a tangle of slick, tallow-pale limbs, biting gently at each other’s thick shoulders and pressing their brows together to read the hammering pulse beneath the skin.

When the gathering is over, he will go back with her to her rig. It will take him three months of sleepless misery to re-tune his nervous system to the beat of her engine. For weeks, he will flinch when the strange valves blow off, and he will misstep on the unfamiliar catwalks, his hands patting nervously at rivets that aren't where his childhood taught him they should be. The clan will feed him extra slug-fat and let him sleep in the center of the pile until his breathing finally locks into the new machine’s sixteen-stroke cycle.

And when a Greaser dies—whether crushed by a slipping flywheel, boiled by a sudden blowout, or simply worn down by seventy years of wet heat and heavy gravity—they do not bury them in the mire. The mire is full of teeth and roots; it is undignified.

Instead, the clan carries the body up to the very top of the rig, higher than the cooling towers, higher than the rookeries, up onto the slick, curving back of the primary boiler-drum where the heat is fiercest and the rain turns instantly to hissing steam upon the iron.

Every member of the clan places their hands on the corpse’s chest one last time. Then, together, they press their foreheads against the hot steel of the boiler casing and sing the dead person’s name-rhythm—the exact cadence of their limp, the whistle of their nose, the favored chord of their voice—screaming it directly into the vibrating iron so the metal itself rings with the note.

Then they slide the body down the chute into the deep brine-well, straight down between the plunging pistons, three thousand feet into the boiling, sulfurous dark of the fault-line.

They believe—if "belief" is the word for something felt in the soles of the feet—that the iron remembers the song. And two days later, when the Cougher’s secondary exhaust valve hitches—*thump-hiss, thump-hiss, k-chug*—the grandmother on the lower catwalk will pause with her hand on a greased stay-wire, tilt her great, ribbed ear toward the roaring dark above, and smile a wide, flat-toothed smile, hearing her brother’s voice still working in the machine.
