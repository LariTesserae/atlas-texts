If you stand in one of the wide, pale courts for twenty minutes without moving, your eyes begin to adjust to the secondary motions of the basin. First, you notice the thirty-second stroke of the vine-pistons, then the three-minute glide of the overhead buckets. Only after an hour do you notice the things that move at the speed of an hour hand—and the rarer things that move with the quick, silent efficiency of an adjustment.

The basin is not unpopulated, though a census taker from the surface would find nothing to write down in a ledger. What lives here is divided between the minuscule, which facilitates the friction of the town, and the singular, which remembers its alignment.

### The Felt-Works (The Ticks and the Mold-Worms)

In the dark, warm crevices where the brass housings meet the cork-sedge floor, the *grease-worms* live in pale, braided colonies. They are not true worms, but a communal stage of a wingless tipulid fly that gave up flight when the canopy closed centuries ago. Individually the length of a sewing needle and translucent as boiled onion skin, they twist themselves into ropes as thick as a man’s wrist. 

These ropes crawl with imperceptible slowness along the undersides of the cast-iron frames, following the warmth of friction. When a bearing begins to run dry and heat up, the colony senses the thermal spike from ten feet away. They migrate up the housing and pack themselves directly into the journal box. Their bodies secrete a heavy, tallow-like lipid derived from eating the paraffin-rich leaves that drop from the upper canopy; those at the center of the bearing are crushed by the turning axle, their bodies rendering down into a slick, pale grease that cools the metal, while the survivors on the fringes feed on the iron-oxide paste left behind. They are the reason the twenty-foot flywheel has not seized in three hundred years.

Above them, in the unglazed brick weaving-houses, live the *bobbins*. 

To a casual glance, a bobbin is merely a lump of grey felt and copper wire clinging to the side of a brass loom. In truth, it is a soft-bodied, six-legged crustacean—a specialized terrestrial cousin of the woodlouse, grown to the size of a teacup. Instead of growing a heavy chitinous shell in a place where calcium is hoarded by the root-mat, the bobbin clothes itself. Using a pair of long, blunt-tipped forelegs that click like knitting needles, it harvests dropped lint from the vine-cables, shed moth scales, and strands of dry moss, felting them together with its own sticky saliva into a dense, oil-proof winter coat that covers its back.

They live in the hundreds inside the vertical looms, moving through the rhythmic clatter of the brass armatures with absolute timing. A bobbin will sit motionless on a stationary bracket for four hours while an iron cam swings past its nose at sixty beats a minute; then, between one beat and the next, it will scuttle three inches to the left, snip a frayed tangent of vine-fiber with its mandibles so the line doesn't foul the gears, tuck the loose thread into its own felt shell, and freeze again. When a bobbin dies, it does not fall to the floor. Its hooked feet lock onto the brickwork, and its dense felt coat slowly petrifies under the mineral drip of the condensers, becoming a permanent bumper or gasket that cushions the vibration of the building.

### The Acoustic Galls

Higher up, where the iron I-beams are swallowed by the grey collars of the banyans, the tree-flesh itself has developed a secondary resident. 

The constant, low-frequency hum of the taut steel cables and the rhythmic *shuck-clack* of the turgor-engines create standing acoustic waves within the wood. Over generations, a species of gall-wasp—now entirely blind and no larger than a grain of black pepper—has learned to lay its eggs exclusively at the nodal points of these vibrations, where the wood is under the most mechanical stress.

The resulting galls do not look like the round, woody apples of surface oaks. Because they grow within the vibration of the ironworks, they develop as hollow, fluted horns of bone-hard callous wood, some as large as a cabbage, clustered around the bolt-collars like rows of organ pipes. Inside the labyrinthine chambers of these galls, the blind wasps live out their entire lives without ever emerging into the cool air of the nave. They farm a black, velvety fungus that grows on the iron-rich sap weeping from the buried bolts. 

When a heavy ore-bucket glides past on the cable, the change in tension compresses the woody horns ever so slightly. The galls exhale a tiny, collective sigh—a chord of dry, woody notes that drifts down to the moss courts below, sounding remarkably like someone blowing across the tops of empty bottles in an attic.

### The Gauge-Keeper

And then there is the thing that walks the troughs.

There is only one, or perhaps there are three that never enter the same court at the same time; it is impossible to tell, because they are built from interchangeable pieces of the basin’s own history.

It stands roughly the height of a stooped man, though its proportions belong to a heavy balance-scale. Its core is an ancient, split-willow pannier—the kind the original quarrymen once used to haul chalk on their backs—which has been completely calcified by a century of sitting under a limestone drip, turning the basketwork into a ribcage of pale, porous stone. 

Inside this stone basket lives a dense, pulsing knot of the same muscular turgor-vines that drive the central flywheel, though this knot has detached itself from the floor. It is a roving heart of vascular tissue, dark purple and slick with condensation, which has threaded its tendrils out through the gaps in the stone wicker to animate a collection of salvaged scrap.

Its legs are three mismatched wooden surveyor’s tripods and a brass leveling rod, lashed together with braided cable-tendrils and shod at the bottom with thick pads of compressed cork-sedge, so that its gait across the floor makes no sound louder than a wet sponge being squeezed. For arms, it carries two long, articulated brass armatures taken from one of the fallen looms, ending not in hands, but in a pair of worn iron calipers and a hook-bill pruning shear.

The Gauge-Keeper has no head, and no eyes in any animal sense. Instead, mounted to the top of its stone basket by a collar of tough grey bark, it carries an old, round-faced copper pressure gauge, the glass long since broken out, and a cluster of three Fist-Snails whose shells have fused to the rim of its wicker shoulder. As it moves, two long, whip-like root-hairs extend from its chest, trailing lightly along the ground or brushing the flanks of the copper condensers to read the temperature, the moisture, and the tremble of the ground.

It does not eat. When its internal turgor-pressure drops and its movements become jerky and slow, it walks to one of the rhythmic hydraulic vents near the central flywheel, lowers its stone basket over the exhaust pipe, and sits for an hour in the ghostly puff of mineral mist, drinking the water through the grey pores of its bark until its vine-muscles are taut and round again.

Its life is entirely occupied by the *tolerance*. 

The equilibrium of the quarry is durable, but it is not perfect; left entirely to itself, a vine would eventually choke a pulley, or a rotting spool would jam a rocker arm. The Gauge-Keeper is the reflex that prevents this. It wanders the wide, shadowless bays between the banyans on a circuit that takes four days to complete, moving with a slow, tilting, tripod stride—*step, step, pause, pivot*.

When it reaches a condenser where the green patina has grown too thick, insulating the copper so that it no longer sweats properly, the Keeper stops. Its trailing root-hairs taste the dry metal. Slowly, the brass loom-arm rises, and with the side of its iron calipers, it scrapes a long, vertical ribbon of verdigris from the cylinder, letting the green flakes fall onto the waiting root-flanges below until the bright, raw copper gleams in the yellow-green light and immediately begins to bead with fresh water.

When it finds a wooden cable-spool in the brick houses that has rotted unevenly, threatening to tip an axle out of true, it reaches in with its hook-bill, snips the anchoring roots on the high side, and wedges a flat chip of slate under the low side until the hum of the spinning brass returns to the specific, quiet pitch the basin requires.

It has no memory of the men who built the mills, nor any concept that the ore-buckets sliding overhead have been empty for two hundred years. To the Gauge-Keeper, the bucket *must* cross the sky every four minutes because when the bucket crosses, the banyan limb dips by half an inch; and when the limb dips, the bark-collar squeezes the acoustic galls; and when the galls sigh, the slate-moths wake from the warm transformers and scatter their iron-grey scales onto the moss below. 

When the adjustment is made and the hum of the brass is flat and true again, the Keeper lowers its calipers, tucks them against its stone ribs, and stands completely still in the middle of the green court for an hour or two—just another upright housing of brass and wood in the cool, damp light, waiting for the next thing to wear down by a fraction of an inch.
