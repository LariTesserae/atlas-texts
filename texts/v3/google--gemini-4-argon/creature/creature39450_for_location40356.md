To find the dominant life in the basin, you have to stop looking for movement and start looking for weight. 

Because the water has no current, the greatest threat to the towering, fragile forest is its own generosity. The yellow spores hang in thick, stagnant ribbons that never reach their targets; the silver bubbles of oxygen and sulfur gas trap themselves beneath the broad, cupped rosettes, threatening to tear the brittle plants from their resinous holdfasts by sheer buoyancy; the shedding leaves take weeks to fall, catching in the golden filament-webs and rotting in place before they ever touch the shale. Left entirely to itself, the valley would choke on its own stillness within a generation.

It does not choke, because it is tended.

At first, you mistake them for the accumulation of debris. High in the eighty-foot burgundy canopies, or wedged into the crisp angles of the hexagonal basalt columns, there are masses that look like tangled knots of shed fronds and dark stone. Wait an hour, however, and you will see a subtle shift in the geometry of the knot. A six-foot cabbage-rosette will slowly tilt two degrees to the left, releasing a trapped, silver boulder of gas that wobbles roaring toward the ceiling. The knot has shifted its weight.

They are called the *Silt-Makers*, though they have not made much of it yet. 

In an environment where the geological clock has barely begun to tick, they are creatures that belong to the concept of erosion. They are vast, colonial gatherings—neither entirely mollusk nor entirely worm, though they share ancestry with both the ribbon-swimmers and the fist-sized grazers on the rocks below. A single individual is no larger than a human thumb: a soft, blind, slug-like muscular foot, dark grey like wet slate, topped with a frilly mantle of bruised violet or olive that perfectly mimics the velvety foliage of the basin. 

Alone, one of these creatures is nothing—it takes a week to crawl the length of a single basalt column. But they are almost never alone. In the warm, predatorless hush of the valley, they have abandoned individuality for architecture. 

Thousands of them lock their muscular feet together, secreting a clear, rubbery mucus that binds them into a single, shifting super-organism the size of a draft horse, or a small house, or a fallen tree. These colonies have no fixed shape. When they are resting on the talus slopes, they flatten themselves out like thick, dark carpets of moss, conforming to the razor-sharp edges of the blue-black shale without being cut. Because their weight is distributed across ten thousand tiny, tough undersides, they can flow over stone that would slice a fish to ribbons.

When a colony decides to climb into the forest, it pulls itself together, elongating into a thick, slow-moving rope or a many-limbed net of dark flesh and violet frills. It moves through the dense, rubbery foliage like a heavy thought. It does not swim; it reaches. It extends a pseudopod of five hundred linked bodies across a gap in the water, takes hold of a glassy spire of kelp so gently that the brittle spiral does not even bend, and pulls the rest of its mass across over the course of an afternoon.

They are the wind the valley lacks. 

As a colony drapes itself over a towering conifer-kelp, its thousands of tiny mouths go to work. They do not eat the living plant; the living foliage is too tough and full of bitter volcanic resins. Instead, they graze on the stagnant ribbons of yellow spores that have gotten trapped in the canopy. The colony inhales the glowing yellow clouds through thousands of tiny siphons, filtering out the dead matter and extruding the viable spores in neat, mucilaginous pellets on the undersides of the female fronds as they pass. They pollinate the forest by crawling through it like a slow, heavy coat of paint.

When a plant grows too tall and its gas bladders threaten to uproot it from the rock, a colony will climb to its crown and simply hang there like a leaden shawl, ballasting the tree until its holdfasts can thicken and anchor more deeply into the fractures of the basalt. 

But their strangest and most vital work happens down on the jagged floor, and it is the source of the valley’s rare, resonant sounds.

The basin has no sand and no mud because the rock is too young and the water is too still to grind it down. The plants, starved of true soil, can only cling to the surface of the stone. The colonies are changing this, grain by grain.

When a colony descends to the talus slopes, it does not just graze on the mineral film; it swallows the smaller splinters of freshly fractured shale. Deep inside the bundled mass of the super-organism—where ten thousand warm bodies press together—the colony creates a communal gizzard. Using the collective, rhythmic squeezing of their linked muscles, they grind the razor-sharp chips of basalt and shale against one another. 

If you swim down to the dark, blue-black floor and press your ear near a resting mat of them, beneath the *plop* of rising bubbles you will hear a faint, dry, rhythmic *shhh-shhh-shhh*, like two pieces of sandpaper being rubbed together by a sleeper. 

It takes a colony a year to turn a handful of sharp shale into a teaspoon of fine, black silt. When the stone has been ground down to a smooth paste, mixed with the digested remains of dead leaves and spent spores, the colony does not simply drop it. It carries the precious mud up into the notches of the hexagonal columns and packs it carefully around the pale, resinous holdfasts of the largest trees, cementing them in place and giving them their first taste of true earth.

They have no eyes, because in the shadowless, pearl-white glow of the steam ceiling, there is nothing to watch out for, and the light never changes except to fade smoothly into night and back again. Instead, they perceive the valley entirely through pressure and vibration. 

The entire basin is their inner ear. When a cooling basalt column fractures half a mile away with a sharp *tink*, every colony in the valley shivers in unison, their violet mantles rippling once before settling back into stillness. They know the topography of the valley by the way the sound of a rising gas bubble echoes off the distant cliffs. They know which trees are dying by the change in the creak of their water-logged trunks.

Every few decades, when a colony grows too massive even for the buoyant water to support, it does not split apart by crawling away. Instead, it climbs to the very top of the tallest eighty-foot spires, right where the turquoise water meets the blinding, hot underside of the steam blanket. 

There, in the warmest layer of the basin, the colony loosens its hold on itself. Over the course of a month, the rubbery mucus dissolves. The individuals—now old and heavy with black silt—let go of one another. They do not fall quickly. Like the shed leaves, they drift downward in a wide, lazy rain of dark grey specks, spinning slowly through the shafts of milky light, taking days to reach the bottom. 

Wherever a handful of them land on the same jagged rock, or catch in the same golden web of filaments, they reach out with blind, slate-colored feet, find one another in the quiet, and begin to gather the stone.
