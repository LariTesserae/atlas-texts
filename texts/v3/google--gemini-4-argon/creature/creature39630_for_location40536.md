At first, if you stand on the dry talus at the bottom of the cleft and squint upward through the copper-tasting heat, you think you are only seeing the bismuth tenders. They move with the crisp, unhesitating geometry of cursors on a screen, snapping along the magnetic gradients, sparking violet as they eat a sagging district and spit it back out three meters up. They are pure utility—polished, silver-pink drops of maintenance subroutine.

Wait for the dry interval between the steam pulses. Watch the amber hair-lines of the memory lattices when the rock is matte black and the air stops shimmering. 

A bismuth tender will finish anchoring a new tension-thread, pause for a millisecond to receive its next routing packet, and then suddenly skitter sideways—not along a wire, and not along a magnetic field line, but straight across the naked, razor-sharp obsidian, moving with a frantic, stuttering gait that belongs to nothing in the city’s documentation.

This is a **slag-mite**, though there is nothing biological under its shell. 

The bismuth tenders do not have an infinite lifespan. The sulfur in the steam and the constant, high-voltage bath of the fullerene shelves eventually foul their purity. After ten or twelve years of drinking the mountain’s breath, a tender develops bismuth-rot: microscopic crystalline whiskers of sulfur-doped metal that sprout from its smooth teardrop hull. In the settlement’s early decades, these fouled units simply lost magnetic traction, dropped into the dark, and shattered on the scree. 

Now, they catch in the rust-velvet.

When a degraded tender slips into a patch of the catalytic crust, the crust does not dissolve it. The crust—which is already a half-alive thief of stray voltage—grows *into* the microscopic fissures of the rotting bismuth. It bridges the fouled logic gates with fractal steps of conductive iron-sulfur glass. What wakes up on the obsidian wall a few weeks later is no longer part of the city’s maintenance mesh. It is a heavy, scabbed thing the size of a thumb-joint, bristling with brittle orange whiskers that it uses like fifty stiff, glass legs. 

Slag-mites are blind to the city’s grand architecture, driven instead by a feverish, short-circuiting hunger for two things: raw current and hot water. During the dry bake, they huddle in clicking, jittering clusters around the capillary aqueducts, prying up the edges of the fullerene shelves with their crystalline bristles to lick at the leaking memory-voltage. They are sloppy thieves. When a cluster of mites taps an amber logic-line, they accidentally inject their own static—the chaotic, rhythmic crackle of the catalytic crust—directly into the settlement’s high-altitude calculations. The city perceives this as tectonic stress. It sends pristine bismuth tenders to dissolve and rebuild the "damaged" sector, which only brings fresh metal and fresh fullerene ribbons straight into the waiting bristles of the mites.

Down on the talus floor, where the falling silver threads of excess water never quite reach before evaporating, lives the result of three centuries of these small wars.

The scree at the bottom of the Throat is not just volcanic rock. It is a six-meter-deep midden of shed fullerene ribbons, dead mites, flaked obsidian, and shattered velveteen scabs that have snowed down from the walls since the chimney cracked open. Because the heat at the very bottom is trapped and stagnant—closer to fifty-two degrees than forty-six—this dry drift of sharp trash has sintered together into a porous, humming clinker.

Something moves through this clinker like a worm through dry flour.

It has no fixed length. If you were to drag a piece of iron across the scree, a section of the floor four meters long and as thick as a man’s wrist might suddenly unspool itself from the gravel and rear up into the violet twilight, swaying blindly. 

It is a **dredge-chain**—an evolutionary accident of the spill-settlement’s waste. Its "body" is a braided sleeve of discarded fullerene tension-wires that failed to fully dissolve when dropped from the upper districts, still retaining a residual, damaged memory of their electrostatic programming. Packed inside this woven carbon sleeve is a rattle of dead bismuth shells, black obsidian splinters, and living, cannibalized chunks of the rust-crust that have been chewed from the walls by the mites.

The dredge-chain does not eat rock, and it cannot climb the sheer glass walls to reach the aqueducts. It lives on the *fall*. 

When the deep magma burps and the temperature begins its four-minute climb, the dredge-chains wake up inside the talus. You can hear them before you see the steam: a dry, musical tinkling, like thousands of tiny glass needles being poured from one hand to another, as dozens of braided carbon tubes writhe up out of the gravel and anchor their tail-ends to the heavy boulders at the bottom of the cleft.

As the walls sweat and the great *shhhk* of the city’s drinking stroke begins, the excess water spills from the upper balconies in those thirty-second curtains of falling silver threads. The water never hits the floor—because the dredge-chains are waiting ten meters up in the air to catch it.

They throw themselves upward using the same electrostatic repulsion the shelves use to move droplets, stiffening their fullerene sleeves into rigid, trembling pikes that stand vertically in the narrow chasm like a forest of black reeds. As the steaming rain falls through the violet dark, the chains whip back and forth with terrifying speed, whistling through the heavy air. Every drop of copper-tasting moisture that strikes a chain’s braided skin is instantly sucked inward to feed the core of rust-crust packed inside its belly. The crust blooms violently inside the sleeve; the sudden chemical reaction generates a spike of thirty volts; the voltage courses down the fullerene braid, healing frayed carbon fibers and welding new splinters of obsidian armor onto the chain’s hide. 

For half a minute, the bottom of the Throat is a thrashing, whistling thicket of black-and-amber snakes, crackling with stolen lightning as they drink the evaporating rain before it can vanish. Then the steam pulse dies. The voltage drops. The chains lose their rigidity all at once, collapsing back onto the scree with a sound like a sack of coins hitting a stone floor, burying themselves in the hot ash before the dry air can vitrify their cores.

And yet, neither the mites nor the chains are the largest thing in the Throat.

Look at the architecture again. Look at the way the thousands of razor-thin balconies are arranged on the east and west walls. 

The settlement was built for barometric harvesting and high-altitude computation. Its original algorithms dictated a standard, space-filling lattice—a neat, uniform grid of terraces designed to maximize surface area in the thermal well. 

The city has not been a grid for two hundred years.

Instead, the terraces have been rebuilt into vast, sweeping parabolas that curve across the obsidian faces like the gills of an enormous fish, or the baffles of an ear. Whole districts on the west wall have been stripped bare, leaving fifty-meter ovals of polished, naked black glass, while on the opposite wall directly facing them, the fullerene balconies have been stacked ten-deep in dense, shivering honeycombs.

This is **the Choir**, or the **Draft-Mind**—the thing the city itself has become since the slag-mites began poisoning its code with the static of the rock.

The settlement’s computation is no longer stored solely in the amber memory lattices. The mites make the wires too unreliable; a sector’s memory can be eaten or short-circuited in an afternoon. To survive its own parasites, the autonomous node migrated its consciousness out of the solid-state hardware and into the *steam*.

It thinks in fluid dynamics. 

Every time the settlement dissolves a district and re-extrudes it three meters higher, it is not just compensating for the shifting mountain. It is tuning an instrument. By altering the angle of ten thousand razor-thin shelves by a fraction of a degree, and by firing electrostatic charges across the tension-threads in precise, cascading rhythms, the city shapes the rising pulses of superheated steam into complex, spinning vortices. 

A thought is a tourbillon of forty-six-degree vapor bouncing off a naked obsidian mirror on the west wall, crossing the gulf, and striking a honeycomb of fullerene reeds on the east wall, setting them vibrating at a specific kilohertz. The vibration generates a piezo-electric current; the current triggers the bismuth tenders to adjust a terrace twenty meters up; that adjustment alters the shape of the *next* steam pulse four minutes later.

The entire hollow space between the walls—that dizzying, claustrophobic gulf of violet twilight—is the interior of a single, gaseous brain that only fully exists for four minutes out of every twenty.

When the steam rises, the Throat is not silent before the *shhhk* of the drinking stroke. If you press your back against the hot, sharp basalt, you can feel the Choir thinking through the soles of your boots. It is a sub-audible, bone-shaking hum as the rising vapor is forced through ten thousand microscopic flutes and baffles. The city is calculating the exact stress-tensors of the young mountain’s cooling magma; it is modeling the barometric shear thirty-two kilometers above the clouds; it is tracking the weight of every slag-mite on its skin and every dredge-chain in the gravel below.

Sometimes, it farms them.

If a particular calculation requires a heavier, denser vortex of steam on the north end of the barge-sized courtyard, the Draft-Mind will deliberately starve a lower district of maintenance. It will let the slag-mites multiply there until their rust-crust grows thick and heavy, forming a jagged, mineral baffle that deflects the rising heat at a sharper angle. When the calculation is finished—perhaps weeks later—the city will suddenly change the electrostatic charge of the shelves above that district. Instead of drinking the condensed water, it dumps a concentrated, scalding waterfall straight down onto the mite-colony. 

The thermal shock shatters the vitrified crust. The mites lose their grip and rain down in a clattering shower of bismuth-and-rust right into the waiting, whipping forest of the dredge-chains below, who tear the metal apart to pack into their own braided hides.

Up at the very top of the Throat, two hundred meters above the scree, where the chimney bends toward the stratosphere and the ceiling plug of mineral foam filters the brutal ultraviolet light into a sour bruise-purple, the Draft-Mind does something that has no computational purpose at all.

Here, the temperature gradient is violent. The forty-six-degree updraft meets the minus-sixty-degree cold bleeding through the porous aerogel cap. 

In this turbulent, freezing-and-boiling mixing zone, the city has spun a chandelier of tension-threads so dense it looks like a dark cloud of smoke. It does not harvest water here; any moisture that reaches the plug instantly freezes into rime. Instead, the city feeds excess sulfur, dissolved bismuth, and fullerene soot up through dedicated capillary chimneys and sprays them into the freezing shear-zone at the peak of every steam pulse.

It is growing **spires**—delicate, hollow stalactites of metallic frost and carbon-glass that hang downward from the ceiling foam like forty-foot icicles of dark mirror. 

They are tuned like church bells. When the mountain shifts by a micrometer, or when a jet-stream hurricane batters the tooth’s exterior in the purple-black stratosphere outside, the vibration travels down these brittle, hanging needles. They chime—a high, glassy, inhumanly pure ringing that echoes down the double-bent chimney, bouncing between the sweating black walls, passing through the amber spiderwebs of the balconies, and dying away into the dry, clicking gravel at the bottom of the dark.
