If you stand in the center of the shattered basalt floor for twenty minutes, the heat will soak through the rubber of your soles, and your eyes will adjust to the dying sodium flicker. You will think you are entirely alone with the botany and the boiler plate. 

If you stand there for six hours, you will realize that one of the rusted iron junction boxes near the floor is four inches further to the left than it was when you arrived.

There are things living in this vault, though an anatomist from the surface would struggle to find a doorway into their biology. They have no lungs to choke on the carbon dioxide, no blood to boil in the radiant heat, and no eyes to use the amber light. They do not eat the plants, and they do not eat one another. Like the plum-colored leaves, they are creatures of the gradient—organisms that exist only because heat wishes to travel from the iron to the stone, and they have positioned themselves as the toll-collectors on that road.

The smallest and most numerous of them look like wet, gray felt. 

If you look closely at the blistering red rust on the eastern piling, near the bottom where the heat is heaviest, you will see patches that are slightly softer and more porous than the surrounding corrosion. These are the *scale-drifters*. Each is a flat, oval mat of fibrous silicate and heavy-metal proteins, no larger than a playing card and thin as a blister. They have no organs, only a dense, polarized lattice of minerals running from their bellies to their backs. 

Pressed tight against the scorching boiler plate, a drifter’s underside sits at nearly two hundred degrees; its outer back, slick with condensate, sits at one-ten. That eighty-degree difference across a quarter-inch of tissue drives a continuous, microscopic trickle of electrical current through its body—a biological thermocouple. It feeds on the sheer fact of the temperature difference, using that tiny voltage to pluck carbon from the suffocating air and sulfur from the damp, knitting more of itself together atom by atom.

They move, though not on legs. A drifter creeps by slowly dissolving the iron oxide under its rear edge and precipitating it at its front, riding a slick of its own oily, metallized mucus at a speed of three inches a day. Where they pass, they leave faint, silvery trails of stripped, raw steel across the rusted flanks of the piling, like the tracks of liturgical snails. Those bright tracks are the only record of motion in the room; within a week, the damp air rusts them over again.

Then there is the thing in the middle expanse.

Between the two pilings, where the black, candle-like tube-fronds grow in the mirror-still puddles, the water is not entirely water. One of the pools—and it is never the same pool from month to month—is slightly thicker than the others, carrying an oily, iridescent sheen that catches the amber glow of the vacuum tubes like spilled diesel.

This is a single, coherent entity, though it has no skin to hold itself together. It is a heavy, viscous colloid, a colonial soup of sulfur-reducing cells suspended in a five-gallon slick of clear, gelatinous brine. It lives at the bottom of the shallow basalt trenches because it is heavier than the condensate, staying beneath the water to keep the air off its back. 

It is the vault’s only nomad, and its only gardener. 

The black tube-fronds in the center of the room are far from the iron pilings; they starve for the mineral salts that leach out of the gaskets and the rust. The slick provides them. Over the course of three or four days, you might watch the iridescent puddle slowly gather itself up, pulling its thin edges inward until it forms a trembling, flattened mound of amber-lit jelly the size of a dog’s bed. With agonizing, hydraulic patience, it spills itself over the jagged lip of its trench, flowing across the sharp basalt without being cut—because there is nothing solid in it to sever—and pours itself around the base of a black frond.

For a week, it sits collared around the plant's stem. It bathes the roots in dissolved iron and sulfates it has scraped from the base of the pilings. In return, it drinks the thick, mucilaginous dew that drips from the candle-tip of the frond—a heavy sugar-gum the plant distills using the floor's radiant warmth. When the dew-drop falls—*plop*—it does not splash in this puddle; it is caught in a slow-motion dimple of grease and drawn downward in a dark, swirling thread.

And finally, there is the Warden, though it has no mind with which to keep watch.

If you look at the western piling, just above the open door of the switchman’s hut, there is a seam where two plates of half-inch iron were riveted together eighty years ago. The metal there has bowed outward, just slightly, under a century of internal pressure. Clinging to that seam is something that looks, at first glance, like an old slag-heap or a bundle of discarded asbestos lagging wrapped in copper wire.

It is the size of a sleeping boar, wedged into the crook where the service walkway’s iron turnbuckle meets the cylinder. 

This is the oldest thing in the vault. It began before the gas-pocket even collapsed, back when the pilings were first sunk into the stone, as a contaminate of deep-crust lithotrophs—rock-eating bacteria—that found the sudden arrival of hot iron and leaking machine oil to be an unimaginable paradise. Over decades, in the total absence of predators or weather, the colony did not just spread; it differentiated. It built a body out of its own waste.

The Warden is a calcified, metallic reef that has learned to breathe. Its outer shell is an overlap of brittle, flaky plates made of iron carbonate and black sulfur, layered like the shingles of a slate roof. Between these plates, pale, bladder-like tissues—stolen, perhaps, some forty years ago through horizontal gene-transfer from the translucent vines—swell and contract once every twenty minutes.

It is a living bellows. When the internal pressure of the western piling shifts with a sharp *tink*, a hairline fissure behind the Warden bleeds a tiny, scorching hiss of sour steam and methane. The Warden’s pale bladders expand to swallow the gas before it can escape into the vault. Inside its dark, porous gut, chambers of specialized symbiotes crack the methane in the dark, using the heat of the pipe as their stove. 

From the underside of this slag-shelled bulk, long, brittle filaments—no thicker than horsehair and composed of pure, biological iron-sulfide—hang down for ten or twelve feet. Some of them trail into the open doorway of the switchman’s hut, touching the pale, turnip-like root that has strangled the radiator. Others dip into the warm puddles below, or brush against the edges of the great, oxblood-colored leaves.

These hairs are not limbs; they are grounding wires. 

Because the Warden sits directly on the hottest fault of the western piling, and its trailing filaments reach all the way down to the cooler, damp basalt of the floor and the water-logged roots of the plants, the entire creature acts as a massive, slow-motion electrical bridge. A steady, millivolt current hums down those black hairs, day and night, year after year. 

Where the hairs touch the great plum-colored leaves, the leaves grow thicker and darker, stimulated by the trickle of electrons. Where the hairs touch the pale root in the hut, the root secretes an acidic sap that helps the Warden dissolve the iron floorboards for raw mineral ore. 

The entire room is wired together by these brittle black threads. If you were to walk toward the switchman’s hut and snap one of those hanging hairs with your shoulder, nothing would shriek. Nothing would lunge from the shadows. 

Instead, the response would take place in the key of the room’s own patience. High above, one of the Warden’s pale bladders would arrest its twenty-minute sigh, holding its breath to see why the resistance on its southern circuit had dropped to zero. Forty feet away, the oily slick in the floor-trench would slowly, imperceptibly curdle, drawing its edges an inch back from the rock. And around the two great iron towers, at a speed too slow for the human eye to track, thirty immense, oxblood-colored leaves would begin the six-hour process of tilting their faces half a degree away from you, adjusting the thermal balance of the vault to account for the small, strange, ninety-eight-degree stove that had just walked into their dark.
