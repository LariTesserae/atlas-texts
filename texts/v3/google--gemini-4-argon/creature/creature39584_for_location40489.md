If you pry a single creature from the wall—an act that requires sliding a flat bar beneath its belly, because its grip is hydraulic, using the syrup-thick moisture of the room to seal its underside to the glass—you do not get a whole animal. You get a *draft* of an animal. 

Away from the press of its neighbors, a lone specimen begins to lose its structural conviction. Its five appendages, which look so horribly like boiled human thumbs with too many knuckles, start to twitch out of rhythm. The black-veined heart visible through its gray, tallow-like cuticle beats erratically, knocking against its ribs like a moth in a lantern. Within a minute of isolation, it forgets how to hold its obsidian lancet; the sinew wrapping slackens, the glass shard drops, and the creature’s horn-tipped pincers curl inward until it resembles a wet, gray fig. It is not dying of cold or asphyxiation. It is starving for consensus.

They have no eyes, no ears, and no ganglia larger than a mustard seed. The intelligence that knaps the glass, knots the tendon, and coordinates the spear-teams does not live inside their skulls. It lives in the friction between their skins. 

When stacked three deep on the basalt shelves, their translucent bodies press together until the boundaries between them blur into a single, rippling mat of gray gristle. Through this slick contact, the black veins align across membranes. One creature’s pulse strikes the flank of the creature above it, which passes the hydraulic thump to the creature on its left, turning the entire writhing heap into a biological abacus. A thought is a wave of muscle contractions that travels four feet down the wall, strikes a corner, and rebounds as a decision to harvest. A memory is a specific knot of five individuals sitting in a corner, Passing a rhythmic twitch around their circle for six days straight to keep the blueprint of a better handle alive until a molting cycle provides the tendon to build it.

When they hunt the knotted water, they are not eating calories. There is no carbon in a thermodynamic glitch, no sugar in three minutes of trapped yesterday. 

Watch one as it shovels a dollop of the spilled, amber-thick heat into its mouth with a basalt paddle. Its mouth is a simple vertical slit underneath the main body mass, lined with bailer-plates like a crab’s. As the gelatinous warmth goes in, the creature does not digest it; it *inflates* with it. The gray flesh briefly turns the color of a bruised peach. Inside the translucent body, the ingested anomaly acts as a tiny, temporary engine of broken physics. For twenty minutes after feeding, that specific creature’s limbs move slightly faster than time allows; its pincers arrive at the obsidian wall a fraction of a second before the muscles in its shoulder have finished contracting to send them. 

They eat the anomalies to pay the energetic debt of their own impossible biology. Without a bellyful of unspooled heat and warped geometry, their hydraulic blood is too thick to pump, and the obsidian walls are too hard for their horn-tips to flake. They are creatures made of meat that require a constant diet of minor miracles just to remain animate.

Their lifecycle is dictated entirely by the discard pile in the center of the floor.

As they work—scraping, prying, lancing, and passing tools through the muffled, soup-thick air—their translucent skins accumulate micro-scratches from the airborne glass dust and the rough basalt. Over weeks, these scratches fill with the mineral-heavy runoff, turning the creatures stiffer, darker, and heavier. An old creature looks like a piece of leaded window glass, its black heart barely visible behind a crust of silicate and copper salts. It can no longer conduct the twitch-thoughts of the pile efficiently; it becomes an insulator, a dead spot in the room’s brain.

When an individual becomes too encrusted to pass a signal, the surrounding pile simply stops holding it up. The hydraulic seals release, and the heavy, calcified creature slides down the wet steps, tumbling with a series of soft, tallow-muffled *thumps* onto the central cone of dulled blades.

It does not die there. It becomes furniture, and then it becomes a nursery.

Pinned under the weight of discarded hammers and wax-softened glass, right over the hairline fissure where the normal water drains away, the old creature’s crust fuses with the pile. Its five thumb-limbs lock into the crevices between the dead tools. And then, using the very last of the stolen heat in its belly, its internal organs dissolve into a clear, viscous jelly that leaks out through its mouth-slit, coating the blunted obsidian shards beneath it.

This jelly is the only thing in the pocket that has an intention of its own. 

As it washes over the discarded, softened blades in the dark heart of the pile, the jelly treats the stone tools as bones. It wraps around a snapped obsidian lancet, pulls a flake of basalt beside it for a rib, and draws a discarded length of old tendon through the middle for a spine. Over forty hours, the jelly skins over, turning from clear to milky gray. A new black heart—initially no larger than a fleck of pepper—begins to beat in the center of the amalgam, kickstarted by the geothermal draft rising from the drainage fissure below.

The newly formed creature crawls out of the trash cone, dragging its constituent parts into a fresh five-limbed shape. As it leaves the pile and joins the writhing carpet on the walls, the old obsidian blade that served as its embryonic marrow is pushed out through its back skin, emerging clean, freshly sharpened by the birth-intent, and ready to be plucked by the nearest neighbor’s pincer.

And beneath the entire operation—beneath the trash cone, wedged five inches deep into the hairline drainage fissure where the water goes to remember how to be water—lives the only solitary thing in the pocket.

The gray creatures never go near the fissure’s lip if they can still crawl; they only drop their dead and their dull glass over it. If you were to clear away the cone of soft blades and peer into the crack with the room’s flat, sourceless daylight behind you, you would see what looks like a blockage of pale fat.

It is a single specimen that fell into the crack when the volcanic blister first cooled, before the tools were invented. Wedged into the drain, bathed in a continuous, scalding torrent of collapsing water-anomalies and dissolved mineral runoff, it never calcified. Instead, it grew to fit the plumbing. It is the thickness of a man’s thigh now, stretching four meters down into the dark plumbing of the mountain, a pale, blind sausage of pulsing gray meat with hundreds of vestigial thumb-limbs bracing it against the basalt walls.

It does not use tools. It is the reason the water knots.

As the mountain’s groundwater forces its way past this bloated, wedged ancestor, the creature’s immense, slow-beating black heart squeezes the liquid against the young glass of the fissure. It is digesting the mountain’s heat in bulk, and its waste product is the broken geometry that sweats up through the floor. The flat, unremarkable daylight that fills the parlor-sized room above is simply the light of the outside world from three days ago, swallowed by the deep roots of this drain-thing where it touches a glacier miles away, dragged through its veins, and exhaled as a warm, shadowless damp into the nursery above.

The thousands of tool-makers on the walls are not an ecosystem. They are the white blood cells and the fingers of the thing in the drain, scraping the walls to widen its shell, lancing the blisters of its breath to keep the pressure down, and dropping their dead back into its throat so it can remember what hands feel like.
