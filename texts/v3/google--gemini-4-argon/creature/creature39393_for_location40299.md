To see one of the builders moving through the olive-amber gloom is to mistake them, at first, for a thick root-bundle drifting on a thermal updraft. They do not swim with the frantic, kicking propulsion of surface creatures. In a world where every stone edge is a fresh surgical scalpel and every structural beam is edged in raw volcanic glass, sudden movement is fatal. A uncontrolled drift of six inches can sever a tendon against an unweathered basalt column. 

Instead, they move by a slow, deliberate brachiating—hand over hand, foot over foot—using limbs that have become long, corded, and prehensile at all four extremities. 

They are hominids, though generations in the warm, buoyant broth have softened their verticality. Without gravity to compress their spines, they grow tall and willow-limbed, their joints loose and fluid. Their feet are no longer arched for pounding flat earth; the big toe has migrated inward, long and opposable, capable of pinching a slimy guide-rope or curling around a raw basalt strut with the same dexterity as their fingers. 

Their skin is their strangest armor. Normally, prolonged immersion in warm water macerates human flesh, turning it soft and vulnerable. But the water here is thick with dissolved silicates and alkaline ash, and the inhabitants coat themselves from birth in the rendered oil of the gourd-fruits mixed with the dense, polymer-like mucus of the giant glass-shelled snails. Over decades, this treatment—combined with the mineral bath—cures their outer epidermis into something resembling oiled chamois or thick, supple parchment. It is pale grey-ochre, entirely hairless, and marked at the knuckles, knees, and shoulders by wide, calloused pads of silica-hardened horn. When they brush against a stray flake of obsidian, the blade skitters off these calluses with a dry *scritch* rather than drawing blood.

Even so, they carry their history in their hides. Because the water is so silica-rich, every juvenile scrape and quarry-nick absorbs the suspended ash as it heals, leaving raised, glossy black keloids. An elder’s skin is a complex topography of dark, glassy lines—a literal map of every sharp angle they misjudged in their youth, worn with quiet pride.

Look at their faces when they emerge through the circular water-trap at the floor of a mortar dome. As their heads break the surface into the trapped pocket of warm, damp air, a second, translucent eyelid—a milky membrane that shields their corneas from the abrasive volcanic grit—retracts into the inner corner of the eye. Their eyes are huge, dark, and wide-set, adapted to drink in the bruisy, scattered twilight of the basin. Before speaking, they tilt their heads to the side and pinch their nostrils shut, blowing sharply to clear the warm broth from their wide, muscular nasal cavities and the fleshy valves of their ears.

They do not stay in the air long enough to dry out. If they did, their skin would tighten, crack like drying mud, and bleed. The air-domes are not living rooms in the terrestrial sense; they are lungs and workshops for things that require dryness. Inside these grey, shell-mortared inverted bowls, the air smells heavily of sweet, slightly fermented plant-gas, crushed snail-lime, and wet body heat. There is no fire—smoke would poison the pocket in minutes, and nothing here is dry enough to burn—so there is no hearth. Instead, the domes are used for sleeping (propped in woven hammocks with their lower halves still trailing in the warm water of the entryway so their skin stays moist), for nursing infants, and for curing the plant-gums and mastics that only set when exposed to oxygen.

Their chests are massive and barrel-shaped, housing lungs that have expanded to take in huge volumes of the thin, high-altitude air harvested from the flora, alongside spleens the size of loaves that contract to flood their blood with oxygenated red cells the moment they slip back down through the floor-hole. 

At the waist, every citizen wears their gravity: a broad, plaited belt of siphon-kelp fitted with toggles and net-pouches. Here they balance their personal buoyancy. A child starts with a belt of light, frothy pumice to keep them from sinking into the scalding depths below; a pregnant woman adds buoyant oil-gourds to offset the weight of the child; a deep-quarry mason hangs heavy, dense prisms of black basalt from their hips so they can sink effortlessly to the lower cliff-faces and stand upright on the narrow fault-ledges without floating away from their work. Adjusting one’s belt—swapping a finger-sized shard of dense stone for a hollow reed-plug—is an unconscious, continuous habit, like a surface-dweller shifting their weight from foot to foot.

Out in the amber avenues, they do not speak with their mouths. Spoken words require expelling precious air, and bubbles rising through the city are messy, disruptive things that get trapped in the upper fronds and rot them. 

Instead, they speak through the architecture. 

Every adult wears a "singing-stone" strapped to the tough inner wrist or the sternum—a flat, resonant plaque of dense basalt. In the knuckles of their opposite hand, they carry a small striker of horn or dense wood. By tapping, scraping, and pressing the striker against their stone plaque, they send crisp, high-frequency clicks, buzzes, and chimes rippling through the water. Because water conducts sound four times faster than air, a mason hanging on the cliff-face two hundred yards away hears the *tink-scritch-tink* of his partner’s wrist-stone as clearly as if it were whispered against his skull. 

For private conversation, two people will drift together in the lee of a giant fern, hook their toes into the same anchor-vine, and press their foreheads or collarbones together. They hum deep in their closed throats, letting the vibrations pass directly from bone to bone—a resonant, purring intimacy that no one three feet away can overhear. The city is full of these silent, forehead-to-forehead clusters, swaying gently in the thermal updrafts like paired seed-pods.

Their craft is a slow, meditative choreography with the mountain and the jungle. 

Watch a quarry-team at work on the caldera wall, fifty fathoms down where the twilight turns the color of dark tea and the water throbs with the heat of the magma chamber below. Four workers hang in harnesses of braided vine against the pristine, hexagonal columns of basalt. They do not swing hammers; the water would rob the blow of its force, and a wild swing would shatter the brittle stone unpredictably. Instead, they inspect the hair-thin cooling cracks in the virgin rock with their sensitive fingertips. Using heavy, two-handed push-awls tipped with fire-hardened wood, they press dry, compressed wedges of dead siphon-kelp deep into the fissures. 

Then, they wait. They hang there in the warm dark, humming to one another bone-to-bone, sharing sips of air from a tethered pig-sized bladder of bladder-weed gas, while the dry wood drinks the sea. Over an hour, the wedges swell with immense, hydraulic patience. The mountain gives a sharp, metallic *ping* that rings through the teeth of everyone within half a mile, and a twenty-foot lath of six-sided basalt peels away from the cliff face. Before it can plummet into the scalding abyss, the team has already lashed it to three giant oil-gourds. The two-ton stone beam hangs weightless in the broth, and two workers simply tow it home like a sleepy dog, walking hand-over-hand along the highway vines.

Closer to the domes, the *arbor-weavers* tend the living structure of the city. They are the surgeons and geometers of the flora. Armed with crescent-shaped obsidian blades lashed between their fingers like claws, they move through the dim green-and-bronze canopy, pruning a frond here to let a warm current rise toward a nursery-dome, nicking the bark of a thigh-thick suspension vine there so it will bleed sticky sap and fuse with a neighboring stalk. They do not build a bridge; they irritate two trees on opposite towers until the trees reach out, tangle, and strangle each other into a load-bearing arch. This takes five years. The inhabitants do not mind. Nobody here is in a hurry.

Their diet is as soft and mineral-heavy as their surroundings. Because there is no grain, no mammal meat, and no cooking fire, their food is cured by chemistry and geothermal heat. They farm the giant, glass-shelled gastropods on suspended stone tables, feeding them scrap-kelp and harvesting their foot-meat in careful, non-lethal slices that the snails slowly regenerate. Mostly, they harvest the snail’s eggs—translucent, gelatinous spheres the size of plums—and lower them in wicker cages fifty fathoms down into the black water near the thermal vents. The scalding, sulfurous updrafts poach the eggs in a few hours into firm, custody curds that taste of brine, iron, and egg-yolk. They eat the pale, oily flesh of the gourd-fruits, fermented in ash-lined pots until it turns into a pungent, buttery paste that can be sucked directly from a leather tube while submerged.

Everything in their society is measured by buoyancy, tension, and the slow accumulation of silica. 

When a person grows very old, their skin—after eighty years of absorbing dissolved silicates and snail-mucus—begins to stiffen. Their joints calcify; the glossy black keloids of their scars widen and merge until their limbs take on the sheen and hardness of gray chert. They lose their neutral buoyancy, growing heavier year by year, requiring larger and larger gas-gourds at their waists just to ascend to the air-domes to sleep.

Eventually, an elder decides they are tired of floating. 

The ceremony is quiet. Their family gathers on the lowest terrace of the neighborhood, where the anchor-cables disappear into the hot, black depths. The elder unhooks their belt of buoyant gourds and hands their carved obsidian tools and their wrist-stone to their grandchildren. They take one last, deep breath from a shared gas-bladder, press their forehead to each of their kin in a final, long, bone-hummed goodbye, and let go of the guide-rope.

They do not plummet. Weighted only by the mineral density of their own ancient bones and petrified skin, they sink with that same dreamlike, syrupy reluctance as a dropped tool. The family watches from the wicker railing as the pale ochre figure drifts downward through the olive-amber gloom, growing smaller and dimmer, passing through the lower plantations of siphon-kelp, until the dark takes them down to the unweathered basalt floor—where the scalding, silica-saturated water will, over a few quiet centuries, turn them entirely to stone, fusing them into the young bone of the mountain itself.
