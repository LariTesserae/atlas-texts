At first, you only see their knuckles. 

They look like river stones—smooth, gray-black, and wet—hooked over the upper rim of a cast-iron harvester. If you watch long enough through the golden, drifting steam, a head rises slowly from the crimson moss on the cylinder's leeward side. 

The eyes are the first shock. They are huge, convex, and entirely black, covered by a thick, amber-tinted nictitating membrane that slides horizontally across the cornea to filter the brutal, UV-heavy glare crashing down from the violet sky. The creature is hominid, perhaps the size of a ten-year-old child, though its proportions are radically rearranged by the thin, wet air. Its chest is an enormous, keeled barrel, housing lungs that take up two-thirds of its torso, tapering down to a narrow, almost insectile waist and long, frog-like legs folded tightly against the warm iron.

There are perhaps four hundred of them in the basin. They have no name for themselves, because they have no spoken language; the thin air makes shouting a waste of precious oxygen, and the sixty-hertz hum of the valley floor drowns out whispers. Instead, they live inside the hum.

When the creature lifts its head fully, you see its throat. Beneath the hairless, slate-colored skin—slick with a natural, waxy sebum that repels the ninety-eight-degree damp—hangs a loose, ribbed dewlap. When it spots an otter-cat sliding into the runoff stream ten yards away, the creature does not shriek. It presses its sternum harder against the vibrating iron of the harvester, opens its mouth slightly, and inflates the throat-sac. By tightening the muscles of its neck, it catches the sixty-hertz industrial drone passing through its own skeleton and modulates it, turning its body into a biological resonator. A silent, low-frequency pulse ripples out through the wet moss and down the copper-nickel cables. 

Fifty yards away, at the next harvester, three more of the creatures feel the altered rhythm in the pads of their feet. They do not look up; they simply draw their long legs a few inches higher out of the mud. 

They are the descendants of nobody knows what—perhaps a maintenance crew stranded five generations ago when the lowland civilization that dropped the harvesters collapsed, or perhaps something engineered specifically to tend the grid and then forgotten when the automation proved flawless. Whatever they were, the caldera has edited them ruthlessly. 

Their feet and hands are extraordinarily wide, the digits elongated and webbed to the middle knuckle, tipped not with nails, but with thick, calloused pads of dead, rubbery skin. They never touch the black silt if they can avoid it. The silt is unpredictable; it burbles with scalding pockets of sulfur and hides the armored catfish whose dorsal spines carry a necrotic venom. Instead, the creatures are entirely arboreal, though their "trees" are the infrastructure. They move through the marsh along the braided copper-nickel highways, walking with a low, swinging, four-limbed gait that keeps their center of gravity inches from the black rubber sheathing. 

The beetles that march along the cables do not scatter when one of the creatures approaches. The creature simply parts the metallic column with a slow sweep of its forearm, picking up two or three of the insects as it goes, crushing their copper-sheened carapaces between heavy, flat molars before dropping the shells into the mud.

As you watch one, you realize it isn't merely resting on the harvester. It is working, though it has no concept of labor. 

Around the base of the cast-iron cylinder, one of the thigh-thick hydraulic vines has begun to strangle the primary heat-sink—a series of thick iron fins near the mud-line that must remain exposed to the cooler runoff water to maintain the internal temperature differential. Left alone, the vine would insulate the fins, the solid-state engine inside would equilibrate, and the sixty-hertz hum in this sector would flatline. 

To the creature, a silent cylinder is an obscenity. A dead harvester is cold, and worse, it is *quiet*—a terrifying sensory blind spot in the vibrating web that connects the tribe. 

The creature reaches down to its hip, where a rough harness woven from dried, silica-stiffened reed fibers holds a single tool. It is a wedge of dark basalt from the high rim, knapped to a serrated edge and lashed to the jawbone of a marsh-cat. With slow, economical movements—never panting, its huge chest rising and falling only four times a minute—it saws through the pulsing vine. White, latex-like sap bleeds into the steaming water. The creature strips the severed vine away from the iron fins, scrapes a mat of crimson moss from the intake vents with its calloused thumb, and pauses.

Inside the drum, the thermoelectric couples register the sudden drop in external temperature. The current surges. The iron casing shivers, its sixty-hertz purr deepening, growing richer and warmer against the creature’s pressed belly. 

The creature closes its eyes, the amber membranes sliding shut. A slow, rhythmic shudder runs down its spine—pleasure, or relief, or worship. Around its knuckles, the translucent palm-sized frogs creep back onto the newly cleared metal, climbing right over the creature's hands to reach the hottest spots. The creature lets them settle, before gently plucking one that has laid a string of gelatinous eggs across its wrist. It sucks the nutrient-rich eggs from its own skin, then sets the unharmed frog back onto the humming iron.

High above, a shadow crosses the golden haze. A cold downdraft from the stratosphere spills over the jagged basalt teeth of the caldera’s rim, slicing through the warm marsh like a blade of glass. Where the freezing wind hits the ninety-eight-degree mire, the air instantly clots into a blinding, white-out fog.

A scalding wave of vent-water chooses that exact moment to spill over the upper travertine terrace. 

All around the harvester, the broad-leaved ferns snap shut with a sound like a thousand wet pages turning at once, showing their pale, silica-armored bellies to the boil. The creature doesn't flee. It simply climbs two feet higher up the cylinder, folds its long legs into its chest, and wraps its long, webbed arms around the crown of the iron drum. Two smaller ones—juveniles, their skin still a pale, translucent gray like the frogs, their lungs not yet barrel-swollen—scurry up the thick transmission cable from the gloom and press themselves into the hollow of the older one's back. 

Together, they form a single, slick mound on top of the machine, indistinguishable from a boulder or a burl of iron. The boiling water washes over the base of the cylinder beneath them; the freezing wind shears through the canopy above them; the blinding UV light bleaches the fog around them. They do not shiver. They press their chests against the warm, cast-iron hide of the engine, feeling the megawatts pour down the mountain through their bones, waiting in the dark for the leaves to open again.
