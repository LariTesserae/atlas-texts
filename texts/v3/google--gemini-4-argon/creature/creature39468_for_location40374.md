At first, you do not see them because your eye is trained to look for vertical things—a silhouette against the bright sky, a vertical pillar of dark cloth among the knee-high walls. 

There are no verticals here. What lives in the valley shares its posture with the water: low, creeping, and spread across the horizontal planes.

You will hear them before you catch their outlines, though even the sound is easy to mistake for the architecture itself. Beneath the three-mile hiss of the spilling weirs—*plip, shhh, plip*—there is an occasional, sharper report. *Tink.* A pause of three seconds. *Tink. Tink.* The sound of greenstone striking slate under two inches of standing water. And then, closer by, a heavy, wet, grinding slide, like a hundred-pound sack of wet meal being dragged across a flagstone.

If you follow that sound down the narrow, ankle-twisting boot-paths, squinting through the shadowless white glare that turns the wet slate into a hall of dull mirrors, one of the squat, beehive-shaped cairns will uncoil itself and move.

They are built like the masonry they tend: broad, low-slung, and segmented by heavy plates of hardened hide and mineral crust. On two legs, one might stand five feet tall, though they almost never stand. Years—or centuries—of navigating the slick, razor-sharp ledges of the cistern have brought them down onto four points of contact: two short, column-like legs and two massive, over-muscled forearms. They move with a slow, side-winding shuffle, keeping their center of gravity barely a foot above the water. Their knees and knuckles are capped with colossal pads of grey, horn-like callus, four inches thick, deeply scored by the fresh slate edges until the pads look like waterlogged cedar bark. Nothing cuts them anymore. When they cross a weir, they do not step over it; they pour their bulk across the sharp lip like thick mud.

Their skin—where it isn't hidden under layers of stiff, mineral-stiffened felt and greasy leather—has the color and texture of the grey river-clay they pack into the basins. It is cold to the touch, dense with subcutaneous fat, and perpetually coated in a slick film of rock-milk—the paste of pulverized slate and basalt that never truly washes off in the gentle mist. 

In the blinding, diffused glare of the high altitude, eyes are more of a liability than a tool. Their brows are heavy ridges of bone and scarred gristle, overhanging eyes reduced to narrow, weeping slits shielded by double rows of greasy lashes. When they look up toward the rim, a milky third eyelid—translucent as oiled parchment—slides horizontally across the cornea to cut the snow-blindness of the clouds. 

They do not look up often. Their world is twelve inches away from their noses.

Watch a pair of them working a terrace thirty yards downslope. They are never alone; they exist in clusters of two or three—the exact number of bodies required to lever a three-hundred-pound basalt block into seating. 

One of them lies half-submerged in a shallow settling pan, the cold water sheeting over the broad, flat dome of its back, splitting around its spine just as it splits around the stone baffles. It is not bothered by the chill; its metabolism is a slow, smoldering peat-fire, fueled by mouthfuls of the grey, gelatinous lime-spheres that it scrapes from the corners of the tanks and chews with a loud, gritty crunching of flat molars. Its thick, shovel-nailed fingers are walk-testing the lip of a slate weir. 

It does not use a plumb line or a spirit level. It closes its eyes and lays its long, flat cheek directly against the wet stone, letting the water run into the groove of its jaw. It is listening to the *plip-shhh*. 

To the builders, the valley is an immense, three-mile choir of surface tension. If a basin settles by a sixteenth of an inch on its clay bed, the sheet of water spilling over its north lip thickens, and the note of its drip drops in pitch. The builder’s large, flat ears—pressed close to the skull and lined with coarse, water-shedding hair—can pick a sour note out of ten thousand surrounding weirs. 

The prone builder grunts—a low, resonant *huff* that vibrates through the water and into the soles of its partner's feet. They have no spoken language with words for sky, or bird, or yesterday; the constant, pervasive hiss of the valley swallows high frequencies within ten paces. They speak through the stone. A knuckle rapped twice against a submerged plinth; a heel dropped hard on a flagstone; a wet, sub-vocal thrum in the chest cavity that travels through the packed clay.

The second builder shuffles forward, dragging a greenstone maul held in the crook of its elbow. Together, without a glance, they lean their broad shoulders against a timber lever already wedged under the offending slab. Their breath comes in slow, whistling bellows, condensing instantly into thick white plumes that hang heavy in the still air before sinking into the pans. Their muscles do not bulge and snap; they swell with a slow, hydraulic certainty, like roots splitting a foundation. The slab rises a fraction of an inch. The first builder slips a wafer of knapped slate—pulled from a pouch tucked under its belly-apron—into the gap. They release the lever. 

The water sheets over the adjusted lip. The *plip* returns to its proper rhythm. 

They do not celebrate. They do not wipe their brows. They simply remain still for ten minutes, breathing in unison with the valley, letting the mist re-wet the backs of their necks.

If you look closely at the older ones—the ones moving near the center of the basin, where the water is richest in dissolved silica and lime—you begin to understand the true nature of their partnership with the valley. 

The elder builders are heavier. They move with an effortful, grating stiffness. The pale, crustose lichen that bruises the north faces of the stones—that bone-white and muted ochre scale—grows across the broad plates of their shoulders and down the backs of their calves. More than that: the water is glazing them, too. In the deep creases behind their knees, in the folds of their thick necks, and across the horned calluses of their knuckles, the microscopic sinter has begun to set. Their skin is frosted with a dull, translucent rime of calcite. When an old one bends its arm, you can hear the faint, dry crackle of mineral crust fracturing in the joint.

They do not wash it off. They scrape it only from their eyelids and their mouths. 

This is why there are no graves in the valley, and no bones scattered on the scree. When a builder’s joints finally lock—when the silica glaze thickens until the knees can no longer bend and the chest can scarcely expand against its stone corset—it does not lie down in the mud to rot. Nothing rots here; the water is too cold, too mineral, too pure. 

Instead, the dying one crawls up into one of the unfinished cellular clusters on the mid-slopes. Its two partners follow. With the same slow, deliberate care they use to seat a basalt header, they guide the stiffened body into a gap in a baffle wall, or curl it into the hollow core of a squat, beehive cairn. They pack grey river-clay around its haunches to stop the wobble. They wedge flat shims of slate beneath its chin and under its folded forearms until the body is level, true, and load-bearing.

And then they leave it to the mist.

Walk back through the maze of the lower terraces—the older sections, where the work was finished fifty or a hundred years ago—and look down through the trembling, metallic skin of the standing water. Look at the squat cairns that break the flow, and the thick, rounded buttresses that shore up the sluice-gates. 

Here, a retaining wall has the unmistakable curve of a broad, bent back, now entirely encased in two inches of ice-clear, milky sinter. There, wedged beneath a massive basalt lintel to keep it from tipping, is a shape like a colossal, petrified fist, the knuckles smoothed into pale limestone nodules, the forearm fused seamlessly into the slate floor beneath it. 

The valley is not being built to house them. They are the mortar. And in the high, shadowless glare, the younger ones keep working—knapping the slate, packing the clay, listening to the million tiny drips—slowly working their way upslope, raising the walls tier by tier, waiting for the water to catch up with them and turn them into the mountain.
