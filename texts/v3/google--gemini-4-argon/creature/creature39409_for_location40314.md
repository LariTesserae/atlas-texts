At first, you think it is another piece of the old mill-work—a length of sun-cured rawhide and calcified reed-stem that has come unlashed from a wind-wheel and caught itself in the brass-vine.

Then the wind shifts a fraction of a degree, the local pitch of the gallery drops from a shriek to a hollow flute-note, and the bundle unhooks a three-jointed thumb from a mortar- seam and drops ten feet to the parapet below.

They have no spoken name for themselves, because they have no ears. Where an ear should be, there is only a smooth, concave dish of pale horn, flush with the skull, like a Scallop shell mortared into the bone. If you were to call them anything, you would call them the *Tympani*, or the *Lath-Men*, though there is very little about them that suggests fat or blood.

A Lath-Man is six feet of bundle and lever. He looks as though he were built on the same workbench as the wind-wheels down in the furnace-wards. His limbs are remarkably thin, flat rather than round in cross-section—shins and forearms shaped like the staves of a barrel or the blades of an oar, all bone and mineralized tendon with no meat to catch the heat or hold water he doesn’t have. His skin is not skin in the wet, mammal sense; it is a tight, amber parchment, layered in thin, flaking sheets like mica or the outer tunic of an onion. When he moves, he does not flex smoothly; he clicks, hitching from posture to posture like a carpenter’s folding rule, locking his joints against the buffeting updrafts with small, dry snaps that are instantly swallowed by the roar of the chime-kelp.

There are perhaps four hundred of them in the Drum, though you could stare across the blinding three-hundred-foot void for half a day and never spot one. They are creatures of the *grain*. They know how to stand edge-on to the light so their flat limbs cast no shadow against the salt-brick; they wrap themselves in cloaks of split chime-kelp that thrash and rattle in the exact frequency of the wild curtains around them.

To live without water in a roaring kiln requires an economy of motion that looks, to an outsider, like catatonia interrupted by epileptic violence.

For three hours at a time, a Lath-Man will hang suspended from an eroded lintel by two hooked heels and one slender arm, his chest-plate pressed flat against the warm masonry. He is not sleeping. He is *listening* with his ribs. 

His entire ribcage is fused into a hollow, keeled box of thin bone—a sound-board, empty of lungs as we know them, filled instead with dry, pleated air-sacs that ventilate passively as the wind blows through two gill-like slits under his collarbones. Pressing his sternum to the salt-brick, he reads the city through its vibrations. The honeycomb masonry of the Drum is a vast, rigid web of conducted sound. Through his breastbone, the Lath-Man feels the *clack-thrum* of the lower mill-wheels; he feels the root-snap of a bladder-fern three tiers down; he feels the tiny, porcelain scratch of a Glass-Skimmer’s claws landing on a pillar fifty yards to his left. He knows the structural resonance of every sagging balcony and hollow archway. When a drift of airborne gypsum-grit begins to choke a wind-throat two floors above him, he feels the drop in the wall’s baritone hum before the dust has even settled.

And that is when he moves.

They are the reason the city has not choked itself to death in ten thousand years. The Drum is a machine that requires tuning. Left alone for a single generation, the silica-heavy chime-kelp would overgrow the wind-scoops, the bladder-ferns would dam the narrow alleys, the updrafts would be baffled, the heat at the bottom would build without an outlet, and the whole vertical forest would cook itself into dead black ash.

The Lath-Men are the tuners.

When the pitch of a gallery goes flat—when the bone-conductors in their chests tell them an airway is clogging—three or four of them will drop from the overhangs like wooden marionettes whose strings have been cut, catching themselves at the last instant on the taut tether-roots of the ferns. They carry long, curved blades made from the split ribs of the ironwood wheels, edged with flaked silicate stripped from old kelp-stems. 

They do not hack; they prune with the frantic, jerky precision of mantises. One holds the other’s ankle, leaning forty degrees out over the blinding white abyss of the pit’s floor, while the second reaches under a sagging parapet to saw through the woody base of a rattle-bush. They work in complete silence—or rather, inside the total noise, which is the same thing. To communicate with a partner six feet away, a Lath-Man does not look up; he taps the pommel of his wood-knife against the salt-brick wall. *Tak-tak. Tak-tak-tak.* A sharp, dry staccato that travels through the masonry, up through the partner’s hooked toes, and straight into the acoustic horn of his skull.

Look at one closely, if you can bear the glare reflecting off the white wall behind him. 

The face is almost entirely beak and goggle. The nose and mouth are covered by a permanent, natural mask of horny plates—a sieve lined with fine, electrostatic bristles that comb the sulfur-dust and spore-powder out of the roaring air before it enters his breathing-slits. Every ten minutes or so, without thinking, he will draw a long, spoon-nailed forefinger across his snout, scraping a paste of static-clumped spore-flour and mineral salt from the bristles and tucking it through a dry, lipless slot beneath his jaw. That is his bread and his water both: the oily, high-energy yolk of the bladder-fern spores, which his gut breaks down through metabolic oxidation, producing the tiny, internal thimblefuls of moisture his cartilage requires to keep from snapping.

His eyes are small, sunken deep behind heavy visor-ridges of bone, and covered by three separate nictitating membranes of yellow, translucent horn—like stacked sunglasses. In the mid-day zenith, when the sun looks straight down the barrel of the Drum and the gypsum floor below becomes a mirror of white fire, he drops all three shutters. He looks out at the world through golden amber, seeing only silhouettes and the ultraviolet shimmer of static charge building on the leaves of the brass-vine.

They have no houses, because the whole city is a house, though they do have *roosts*—deep, wind-shadowed pockets behind the ancient granary vaults where the air is still and hot as an oven, and the roar of the pit is muffled to a heavy, oceanic *shhhhh* inside the six-foot-thick mud walls. 

Here, where the Silt-Crabs click through the flour-dust on the floor, the Lath-Men gather when the sun drops below the salt-rim above and the pit falls into sudden, purple-black shadow. The thermal updrafts never stop—the heat-vents at the bottom are eternal—so the dark is just as loud as the day, a blind hurricane of rattling tin and booming stonepipes.

In the dark of the vaults, they do the only thing that suggests they are more than maintenance insects evolved from men. They sit in circles on the warm floor, their flat, stave-like legs crossed, and they press their foreheads together—horn-dish against horn-dish, six or eight skulls locked into a single ring of bone. 

And then, with their long, dry fingers, they drum upon their own hollow chests.

Outside, the vertical jungle thrashes in the dark, a meaningless, deafening chaos of wind and silica. Inside the ring of skulls, the Lath-Men pass slow, intricate rhythms from breastbone to brow, brow to breastbone—long, rolling phrases of vibration that have no air in them at all. It is history, perhaps, or a map of the walls as they stood a thousand years ago before the third gallery sagged, or simply the pleasure of a sound that they made themselves, small and ordered and private, circulating endlessly through the dark bone while the white furnace roars outside.
