To an eye accustomed to the sun-bleached world above, the people of the Hollow look at first like carvings made from the very wood and resin they inhabit—figures whittled from aged boxwood, rubbed with beeswax, and left to cure in the warm dark. 

They are small, rarely rising past the chest of a flatlander, with the compact, dense-boned build of creatures bred in a tight space. Evolution and forty thousand years of vertical living have reworked their proportions. Their calves and forearms are thick and ropy, corded with the slow-twitch muscle required for hanging and hauling, while their shoulders are extraordinarily broad and loose-jointed. Their feet have forgotten shoes; the big toe is splayed wide and opposably strong, capable of pinching a grease-vine or a notched climbing-pole with the casual security of a hand. Where their heels and palms meet the polished ironwood of the walkways, the skin has thickened into yellow, horn-like pads that click softly against the planks as they move.

They do not sweat. In an economy where a cup of free water is worth a year’s labor, a body that leaks moisture into the air would die in a week. Instead, their skin is non-porous and heavy, with the supple, matte texture of fine chamois leather. To cool themselves and keep their hides from cracking in the dry heat, they exude a very slow, lipid-rich sebum which they augment with the pressed oils of the tallow-fern. Consequently, a villager is never quite dry to the touch; they are cool and slick as soapstone, and they smell strongly of their cosmetics—a sharp, medicinal perfume of camphor, nutmeg, and old amber.

Because they have no water to wash with, their grooming is mechanical. Throughout the day, whenever two people pause on a catwalk to speak, their hands are busy on one another’s shoulders, working small, crescent-shaped strigils of polished beetle-horn across the skin to scrape away the emulsion of oil, dead cells, and chitin-dust. It is the basic gesture of their society: a mother scrapes the back of her nursing infant; a suitor scrapes the forearms of the girl he is courting; the Village Weigher scrapes the chest of a dying elder before the final rites. The grey, waxy ribbons curled up by the blades are never flicked away at random; they are rolled between the fingers into neat pills and dropped into the leather pouches everyone wears at the hip, to be weighed and fed to the rail-birds at dusk.

Their faces are broad and flat, dominated by eyes that have adapted to the bruised, amber twilight of the pit. The irises are huge, occupying nearly the whole of the visible eye, and are a pale, reflective gold like those of an owl or a gecko, with pupils that can dilate until the eye looks entirely black. To protect these large, precious surfaces from the constant drizzle of sawdust, frass, and wing-scales, they have double rows of stiff, greasy eyelashes and a pale, translucent third eyelid that sweeps horizontally across the cornea with a tiny, wet *snip* whenever they look up into the falling litter of the canopy. 

They wear almost no cloth, for cloth traps dust and harbors the biting mites that drop from the gliding-squirrels. Their hair is kept shaved to the skull with obsidian flakes—the hair itself immediately collected for cordage—leaving only a single, greased braid at the crown, through which they thrust their bone awls, strigils, and tally-sticks. For modesty and protection against the rough bark, they wear brief aprons of spider-silk beaten into felt and stiffened with resin, and articulated greaves of tortoiseshell-woodlouse plates tied over the shins and forearms.

A person in the Hollow is never still, yet they never hurry. Fast movement generates heat and wastes calories; it shakes the suspended walkways and startles the flocks of grease-vines. Instead, they move with the three-limbed, deliberate flow of chameleons or slow lorises, their weight always distributed across three points of contact, their bare toes testing the tension of a rope before the knee unlocks.

Their minds are shaped like their village: stacked, circular, and entirely preoccupied with weight and balance. 

In a closed system eighty paces wide, population and mass are matters of religious terror. A baby cannot be born until an elder’s weight has been returned to the basal dust; a new house cannot be mortised into the wall until an old one has been unlashed, chewed down by the wood-grubs, and its resin redistributed. The closest thing they have to priests are the *Counter-Weights*—men and women with blindfolds of dark leather over their golden eyes, who sit at the central intersections where the great main-vines meet the rock. By keeping their fingertips pressed to the taut cordage of the village, they can feel the tension of the entire settlement. They know by the vibration of a single sinew-rope if three too many people have gathered on a fourth-tier balcony, or if the root-cellars are growing light on tubers while the upper drying-racks grow dangerously heavy with nuts.

When the Counter-Weights strike their hollow wooden blocks with a bone mallet—*tock, tock-tock*—the villagers shift automatically, five people climbing down a notched pole while six climb up on the opposite side of the hall, keeping the great, hanging basket of their world trim and level.

Their language, spoken behind nearly closed lips to keep the tongue wet, is a marvel of density. A single pharyngeal click followed by a nasal hum can indicate not just an object, but its current tier in the village, its moisture content, and whose turn it is to scrape it. They have no words for *weather*, *horizon*, *stranger*, or *tomorrow*. Time is not a line to them, because nothing goes anywhere; time is a vertical column, like the compost on the floor. The past is simply *down*—down in the dark among the blind badgers and the root-cellars—and the future is *up*, where the pale roots drink the light at the lip of the pit.

When a villager grows too old to climb, they descend step by step through the tiers over the course of years, moving from the airy, violet-lit nut-racks of the canopy down to the middle workshops, and finally to the lowest platforms just above the floor, where the air is hot as breath and thick with the ammonia-and-sage stink of the badgers. 

When they die, there is no weeping, for tears are an unthinkable extravagance. The family gathers on the lowest catwalk, scrapes the body one last time with great tenderness, and anoints the skin with the heaviest, sweetest nutmeg-paste from the heart of the wax-trees. Then they lower the body into the knee-deep, seething litter of the floor. Within an hour, the badgers and the great horn-beetles have begun their work. Within three days, the clean, white bones are hauled back up in a basket, carved into pulleys, needles, and strigils, and lashed back into the walls of the houses—so that a man’s grandson may climb to his bed by stepping on his grandfather’s thighbone, and the village remains exactly the weight it has always been.
