To find the maker of the looms, you have to stop looking at the water and look into the fractures of the basalt. 

There is a seam on the back wall, five feet long and no wider than a boot-heel, where the black rock has split along a crooked fault. Stuffed into this slot, wedged so tightly against the stone that it looks like a vein of dark, bruised ore, is a body.

At first glance, you would take it for a drowned mountaineer—something that fell into a glacier five hundred years ago and was chewed through the mountain’s plumbing until it jammed here. It is roughly shaped like a human, though flattened and widened, as if it had been passed through heavy iron rollers. Its skin has the color and texture of wet slate or the belly of a scaleless cat-shark: a tough, poreless, Blue-black hide that does not wrinkle or bloat in the freezing water.

Then a shelf of fresh aragonite three feet above it snaps off with a noise like a cannon shot. The current shifts its angle of attack by four degrees. 

Instantly, a hand comes out of the fissure.

It is not a swimmer’s hand, webbed and paddle-like. It is an iron-worker’s hand, or the claw of a crab that has decided to be a man. There are four fingers and two opposing thumbs, one on either side of the palm, and they have no fingernails. Instead, the final joints are bare, yellowish-white bone—the skin simply stops at the second knuckle, cuffed like a sleeve, leaving the raw phalanges exposed. And because nothing in this water stays bare for long, those protruding finger-bones are heavily armored in wild, jagged deposits of quartz and calcite. The creature has literally grown crystal thimbles onto its own skeleton.

It jams these mineral-crusted tips into a razor-edged notch in the basalt, locks its wrist with an audible *clack* of tendons, and hauls itself out into the blinding light of the room.

It does not swim. Nothing with a backbone could swim in this. It climbs the water the way a spider climbs a chimney in a hurricane. 

Fully emerged from the slot, the Weaver—for lack of a better word—is five feet tall and weighs perhaps three hundred pounds, almost none of it fat and very little of it ordinary meat. A creature made of red muscle and filled with blood and air would be pulped against the selenite window in three seconds, its lungs imploded by the pressure drops and its ears burst by the cavitation shriek. 

The Weaver has no lungs. Its ribcage is not a barrel, but a flat, overlapping lattice of broad, flexible plates, like the horn staves of a composite bow. It has no belly to speak of; its waist is a thick, braided cable of white tendon sheathed in black skin. Beneath the base of its skull, flanking its spine, are two wide vertical slits covered by stiff grates of bone. As it hangs onto the wall, it faces into the screaming updraft, letting the forty-mile-an-hour current ram straight into its open, toothless mouth and blast out through the vents in its back. It breathes by ram-jet. The water is so hyper-oxygenated by the mountain’s violence that the creature doesn't need to pump its gills; it just lets the mountain blow through it.

Its head is wide and low, shaped like the head of a splitting wedge or a shovel-nosed sturgeon, designed to spoil the lift of the current and press its face *into* the rock rather than peeling it away. Its eyes are tiny, deep-set beads covered by thick, immovable lenses of clear horn—goggles grown from its own skin to keep the silica needles from blinding it. 

Right now, the Weaver is molting, and it is using the room to do it.

All along its broad back and the outsides of its forearms, the black skin is frosted with the same deadly white crust that is consuming the wicker looms. If the Weaver stayed still for three hours, it would become a statue, pinned to the basalt by a thousand tiny needles of stone. So as it creeps horizontally across the wall toward the nearest sinew-line, it performs a strange, shrugging dance. It flexes its flat, bow-stave ribs in a sudden, violent ripple. A sheet of white aragonite three-eighths of an inch thick cracks off its shoulder blades in a single piece, spinning away into the vortex and shattering against the ceiling. The skin underneath is raw, silver-grey, and bleeding a tiny, viscous ribbon of pale yellow fluid that isn't blood, which the water instantly shears away.

It reaches the first copper piton. 

You can see now that the Weaver did not bring the mammoth bone or the willow twigs *into* this sealed room from some sunny valley outside. There is no door big enough for it to leave. The Weaver has been in here since it was the size of your fist, slipped through a deep subterranean sluice-gate before the tectonic shift pinched the exit shut twenty years ago. It is a prisoner—or a monk in a cell of its own choosing—and the mountain feeds it its raw materials through the floor.

The roaring vent at the bottom of the chamber is the throat of a ten-mile siphon that drains an alpine plateau three peaks over. When an avalanche sweeps a herd of ibex or an ancient, thawing riverbank of mammoth ivory into a sinkhole five miles away, the mountain grinds it up in the dark and spits the splinters up into this lit box. 

The Weaver waits for the trash of the upper world to shoot through its floor. Most of it blasts straight through and jams in the ceiling cracks, or gets battered to dust. The Weaver’s entire life is a game of high-speed salvage and slow-motion masonry.

It hooks one of its four-toed, double-heeled feet behind a mammoth-bone wedge and reaches out to the vibrating sinew line. The line is singing at a high, frantic C-sharp, blurring in the clear water. The Weaver’s mineral-crusted fingers close on the gut string, and you can see how it hears: it presses its flat lower jaw directly against the taut cord. 

Its skull is solid bone, without sinuses, acting as a sounding board. Through its jawbone, it listens to the pitch of the string.

The pitch is too low. The crescent-shaped blade of woven willow at the center of the line has accumulated enough chalcedony over the last six hours to change its weight, dragging the frequency of the cord down. If it stays in that eddy ten minutes longer, the stone will grow too thick, the edge will blunt, and the turbulence will snap the frame off the line.

With a movement that is terrifyingly fast—a snapping, hydraulic uncoiling of its white-tendon limbs that beats the water at its own game—the Weaver reaches into a pouch made of an old, turned-inside-out trout stomach tied to its hip. It pulls out a wedge of split green slate. With one blow of its bare, rock-crusted fist, it drives the slate into a hairline crack three inches to the left of the loom.

Instantly, the water hitting the slate kicks sideways. A sheet of silver cavitation bubbles—a blade of pure, screaming vacuum—slices across the face of the half-grown crystal knife. 

The Weaver watches with its pinhole eyes, its jaw still pressed to the string, listening to the shriek of the collapsing bubbles as they lathe away the excess mineral crust. It is not carving the stone with a chisel; it is steering the water’s violence with shims of rock, using cavitation as a grinding wheel to sharpen a blade of pure quartz.

Why does a creature trapped in a forty-foot stone box need a crescent-shaped knife of translucent chalcedony? Why does it need a vessel of stone-crusted reeds, or a lead-weighted mallet banging forever against the ceiling?

Look up at the mallet.

The leather tether is old, frayed, and stiff with lime, and the wood of the hammerhead is petrifying, growing heavier every day. As the current surges, the mallet rises and slams against the exact spot where the clear selenite crystal meets the black basalt of the back wall. *Thud. Thud. Thud.* You can barely hear it over the roar of the water, a dull, heartbeat thump beneath the shriek of the bubbles.

Directly beneath the mallet’s impact point, a hairline fracture in the ceiling is wedged open with three copper pitons. 

And wedged into the fracture *beside* the pitons are four older blades of chalcedony, grown on the looms months ago, all of them snapped off at the hilt from the unimaginable pressure of the rock.

The Weaver is not making art, and it is not making weapons for some war outside. It is a miner. And it is not trying to get out into the air—an air-breathing world would suffocate it in minutes, and its heavy, dense body would crush its own organs without the buoyant, violent lift of the updraft to hold it together.

No. If you press your face against the outside of the selenite wall at noon, shielding your eyes from the glare, and look past the Weaver, past the trembling looms, down toward the bottom of the back wall near the roaring intake vent, you will see the other seam.

It is narrower than the one the Weaver sleeps in—barely four inches wide—and it goes straight down into the hot, black roots of the mountain, where the silica comes from. jammed inside that lower crack, facing *up* into the light, are three smaller shapes. 

Their skin is not yet slate-black; it is the translucent, milky pink of cave-newts. Their ribs are still soft cartilage. They are wedged together like peas in a pod, safe in the dead water of the deep crevice, their little shovel-heads pointed up at the dazzling, roaring cathedral above them. 

The pocket is filling up. The mountain is healing its own fracture, precipitating tons of aragonite and quartz every year, slowly choking the room with white stone. Within five years, the drainage cracks at the top will seal shut, the current will stall, the oxygen will vanish, and the chamber will become a solid geode with four corpses locked in its crystal heart.

The Weaver finishes tuning the eddy around the crescent blade. It takes one of the blind, rivet-suckered leeches off the rock beside its knee—peeling it up with a sharp, stony thumb—and pops it into its mouth, swallowing it whole to be ground up by the pebbles in its gizzard. 

Then, gripping the tiny, razor-sharp ledges of the basalt with its bone-tipped fingers, it begins the long, brutal climb toward the ceiling, fighting every inch of the way against the water that wants to hurl it down, carrying a fresh copper wedge in its teeth to widen the sky.
