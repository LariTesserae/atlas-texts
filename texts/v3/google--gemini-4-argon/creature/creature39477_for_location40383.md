To see the basin’s true tenant, you have to stand still until the *shuck-shuck* of the scale-shells and the three-four drip of the flanges stop registering as separate noises and flatten out into silence. You have to look at the donkey-engine, and then look five feet to the left of it, at a stout, six-foot column of weeping travertine that has grown up around a vertical exhaust pipe.

After ten minutes, the column breathes. A broad, vertical seam in the porous stone parts with the sound of peeling wet canvas, revealing a slick, lead-colored interior, and then seals shut again.

They have no name in the county histories because the pumping station was abandoned ninety years ago, and it takes at least sixty years of drinking the heavy, lithium-and-iron-salted seep water for a thing to become what these are. There are three of them in the basin. 

From a distance of ten paces, they are indistinguishable from the tufa pillars, and this is not a matter of camouflage; it is a matter of custody. Whatever they were before—an assembly of stranded deep-trench annelids pulled up through the artesian fractures, or the fused, calcified remnants of the livestock that once fell down the slump-clay embankments, or something that arrived in the bilge-ballast of the original barge that brought the boiler—the water has long since rewritten their anatomy. 

They are roughly the height and bulk of a hogshead barrel tilted on end, though their shape is accommodating and imprecise. When wedged into a narrow cleft between two standpipes, a tenant can draw itself up into a tall, fluted cylinder five feet high and scarcely eight inches wide; when settled into a broader pool where three sluices meet, it slumps into a terraced, broad-skirted mound like a dropped sack of wet lime.

Their hides are a thick, rubbery mantle of pale, lard-colored tissue, utterly hairless and cold to the touch. Because they rarely move more than three feet in an afternoon, the basin’s relentless mineral drip builds up on their shoulders and crowns. They wear heavy, terraced shawls of yellow travertine and rust-scale that grow directly into their outer epidermis. When one of them shifts its weight, this stony crust cracks along old fault lines with a dry, grinding *crunch*, shedding small flakes of carbonate into the tea-colored water before the bacterial slime quickly seals the fissure again. 

They have no eyes—there is nothing in the permanent four-o'clock fog worth focusing a lens upon—and no true head. Instead, the upper third of the bulk is a series of heavy, overlapping fleshy cowls, arranged like the gills of a mushroom turned upside down, or the leather bellows of a smithy. 

These cowls are their ears and their noses, tuned entirely to the hydraulic and electrical geometry of the hollow. When the copper cables in the pools go *tic-tic-tic*, a faint, sympathetic shiver runs through the tenant’s mantle. They have an exquisite, lateral-line sensitivity to millivolts. They know the exact battery-taste of the mud. They know when the tide turns a mile away by the change in the barometric sigh of the intake pipes, and they orient their bodies toward the brass pressure-relief valve on the leaning manifold as if it were a hearth fire.

Every three or four minutes, when that brass valve lifts with its greasy *clack* and drools its quart of warm, rust-red water, the nearest of the three tenants leans its upper cowls against the cast-iron housing. It does not fight the others for the spot; they rotate this privilege on a schedule measured in days, passing one another in the narrow stone alleys with a slow, soapy slide of flank against flank that takes twenty minutes to complete. The one at the manifold presses a wide, puckered ventral slit against the iron and drinks the warm iron-broth before it can cool, its internal cavities gurgling in exact, hollow sympathy with the pipe’s bronchial wheeze.

They are not hunters; hunting requires a metabolic haste that would ruin them in this thin soup. They are farmers and cisterns.

Inside the deep, quiet pleats of their mantle-cowls, the tenants cultivate thick pastures of the iodine-smelling bacterial mat, enriched by their own slow ammonia secretions. The blind, thumb-sized amphipods and the oil-crabs are drawn to the warmth and the chemical richness of these folds. A tenant will stand motionless for six hours at a sluice-gate, its lower skirt spread out into the ankle-deep water, its cowls relaxed and gaping like old boots left in the mud. The amphipods scull in to graze; an oil-crab steps delicately onto the calcified shelf of its hip, picking at the sulfur-slime with needle-legs.

Then, with the slow, hydraulic certainty of a canal lock closing, the outer lip of the cowl rolls inward. There is no snap, no thrashing—just a thick, muscular peristalsis. The water is squeezed out through a fringe of stiff, baleen-like bristles that look remarkably like rusted iron filings—and actually *are* cored with precipitated magnetite—while the crabs and the shrimp and a quarter-pound of bacterial lard are drawn inward. Inside, the tenant’s stomach is a gizzard full of swallowed foundry-nuts, flaking rivets, and chunks of hard shale, which grinds the shells and carapaces down with a muffled, rhythmic *clink-scritch* that sounds like someone turning a wrench in a bucket of gravel.

Their relationship with the machinery is goes beyond mere shelter. They are the reason the system has not entirely choked itself to death in its own stone. 

The travertine grows fast here; left alone, the mineral curtains would seal the brass relief valve in a month and bury the live copper cables in a year, turning the basin into a dead, stagnant bog. The tenants prevent this. They have an appetite for the copper-salts and the specific, sour ochre that forms on active metal. At night—when the grey four-o'clock light dims to the color of wet slate—they unseal themselves from their resting alcoves. Moving on broad, muscular pedal-discs that leave wide, wiped-clean tracks through the slime, they haul their stone-crusted bulk up the drunken angles of the cast-iron standpipes.

They are surprisingly strong, with the slow, leveraged strength of tree roots. One will wrap its heavy mantle around a seized flange or the crusted housing of the donkey-engine’s flywheel. From its underside, it extrudes a broad, rasping ribbon of chitin—a radula the width of a mason's trowel, studded with teeth made of pure, biologically forged goethite, the hardest iron oxide in the pit.

For hours in the dark, there is a new sound in the basin: a deep, resonant, metallic *scree-scree-scree* that sets the whole network of pipes vibrating down into the rotten shale. The tenant scrapes the new stone off the brass valve so it can continue to clack and wheeze. It licks the green verdigris and the calcareous rime off the split gutta-percha cables, keeping the copper raw so the stray current will keep ticking into the brine, keeping the hydrogen fizzing, keeping the worm-bundles fat and the bacteria blooming. 

When a cable finally rots through and the *tic-tic-tic* stops in a particular pool, the largest of the three—an ancient, broad-backed pillar whose upper cowls are permanently stained the bright, robin’s-egg blue of copper sulfate—will sluggishly bulldoze the silt with its prow until it finds the two dead ends. It will gather both frayed copper braids into the deep, wet fold beneath its throat, holding them together in the conductive salt-slime of its own body for weeks on end, acting as a living splice, letting the phantom current trickle through its own grease-pale flesh just to keep the pool alive until the mineral drip cements the wires back together.

Nothing in the basin knows them as predators or masters. To the scale-mollusks that creep across their stony backs, they are simply pipes that occasionally walk five feet to the east. To the braided worms that sometimes anchor themselves to their shoulders, they are just warm chimneys that smell of good broth. They have no thoughts that would translate into words, only a deep, cold, three-part patience—a dim, heavy satisfaction in the *clack* of the brass, the *tic* of the wire, and the slow, salty taste of the iron turning into mud.
