To see the true fauna of the Great Shingle, you have to stop looking for movement and start looking for errors in the geometry. 

In a landscape without shadows, protruding from the ground is a vulnerability; it exposes a body to the relentless photonic glare and the slow, unspooling hunger of the static membranes. Thus, most of what lives here has surrendered the third dimension entirely, or only borrows it for a few seconds at a time.

Look closely at the exposed, laminated faces of the whale-backed ridges, where the millions of paper-thin strata are visible like the pages of a waterlogged book. Sometimes, a section of that grain—perhaps two meters long and no thicker than a fingernail—will detach itself from the cliffside and slide sideways. 

These are the **Foliates**. They are creatures of pure cross-section. 

A Foliate has no permanent volume. Its body is a ribbon of flexible, semi-conductive polymers and gold-leaf circuitry, evolved from the ancient maintenance routines that once defragmented the megastructure’s memory banks. It lives *between* the layers of the ground. By generating a localized shear-charge, a Foliate parts the fused ceramic plates of the earth by a fraction of a millimeter, slipping through the solid rock the way a bookmark slips between pages. 

As it glides through the tightly packed matrices under the surface, it does not eat matter; it eats *structure*. Its underside is lined with microscopic reading-heads that scrape across the crushed crystalline memory-blocks. When it encounters a pocket of surviving data—a trapped, fossilized loop of ancient mathematics, an intact logic-gate, or a fragment of a dead simulation—it induces a tiny short-circuit. The data is erased, the ordered crystal collapses into amorphous glass, and the Foliate absorbs the minute burst of entropic heat released by the destruction. Behind a passing Foliate, the ground settles with a faint, dry *shhhk*, now slightly denser, slightly blanker, and entirely dead.

Sometimes, two Foliates will intersect along the same stratum. Because they cannot pass through one another in their two-dimensional plane, one is forced to the surface. It erupts from the ground like a sudden, glittering fountain of cassette tape, twisting blindly in the shadowless white glare, its gold-leaf skin blistering under the unbroken light before it can find a seam and slice itself back into the dark of the floor.

Those brief, frantic breaches are what the **Gossamers** wait for.

You cannot see a Gossamer until it is directly between you and a patch of dark thermal-venting ground, because a Gossamer is made almost entirely of spun glass and empty space. They are the apex predators of the middle air, evolved from the fiber-optic bundles that once carried light-speed thought across the continent. 

A Gossamer looks like a vast, tangled chandelier of raw, unclad optical fibers—a tumbleweed of pure silica the size of a house, yet weighing less than a handful of coins. Because the sky is an omnidirectional white ceiling, light enters the tips of the Gossamer’s billions of glass hairs from every angle, bouncing internally through its body and exiting at the opposite ends. The result is a creature of total optical camouflage; it doesn't cast a shadow, and it shines with the exact same surgical brightness as the sky behind it. 

It moves by riding the thermal updrafts of the kilometer-wide louver-patches. When a section of the plain flips to slate-gray and dumps its heat into the air, the Gossamer drifts over it, expanding its fragile lattice to catch the rising column of warm fluorocarbon gas. It hangs there, thirty feet up, dangling miles of microscopic, hair-thin glass snares that trail across the white silt below. 

When a Scourer glides beneath it, or a Foliate breaches the crust, the trailing fibers brush against their charged hulls. Instantly, the Gossamer dumps a massive, concentrated laser-pulse down the length of that single fiber—light it has been harvesting from the sky and amplifying within a central, spherical lens at the core of its tangled body. The target is spot-welded to the floor in a silent flash of violet vapor. The Gossamer then settles slowly downward, wrapping the crippled machine in a cocoon of brittle threads, dissolving the precious rare-earth metals out of its chassis with droplets of condensed acid-vapor, and leaving behind a hollowed-out shell of matte-white ceramic.

Then there are the things that build.

Where the Scourers have passed, leaving trails of dead, bleached voltage-flora, you will find the **Cairns**. From a distance, a Cairn looks like a tall, narrow column of stacked dinner plates, or a grotesque stalagmite of pale gray slag, swaying slightly as it walks on six stilt-like, multi-jointed legs. 

They are composite organisms—the closest thing the Shingle has to a scavenger-hermit. At the core of a Cairn is a wet-dry chemical matrix, a fist-sized knot of self-replicating fluorocarbon gels that would boil away in minutes if exposed to the dry air and the fierce overhead light. To survive, the gel-core extrudes calcified tendons into the surrounding debris, picking up flat shards of ceramic, dead Scourer plates, and shattered crystalline strata, and mortaring them together with sintered metallic dust. 

Because there are no shadows to hide in, the Cairn must *carry* its own shade. It builds a tower directly over itself. As the intense light slowly degrades the top layers of its ceramic shell, turning them brittle and chalky, the Cairn reaches down with long, spindly manipulators, pries a fresh slab of laminated rock from the ground, and wedges it into the base of its own body, jacking its entire spire upward. The top of the spire eventually crumbles away into white dust, while the bottom is constantly renewed. 

A Cairn is a walking core-sample of the territory it has crossed. By reading the bands of gold, gray silicon, and purple photovoltaic dust in its swaying six-foot shell, you could map its entire life's journey. 

They move in slow, clicking herds of five or six, following the great translucent weather-membranes. When a membrane passes and the sorted dust snows back down in neat atomic bands—first the heavy gold, then the silver, then the light silicon—the Cairns are there, frantically scraping the pure, sorted metals into the seams of their shells before the ground's shifting magnetic fields can mix the silt back together.

And finally, there is the **Mire**. 

It is not a creature you see, because it is a creature you walk upon. 

In the lowest depressions of the basin, where the pulverized dust of the ancient canyons has pooled to depths of several hundred feet, the silt has become something else. It is a vast, lenticular lens of powdery grey and tarnished gold, stretching for ten or twenty miles between the whale-backed ridges. From the edge, it looks firmer and smoother than the surrounding plain, like a frozen lake of mercury dusted with talc.

It is a single, super-colonial organism made of trillions of motes of "smart dust"—ancient, microscopic diagnostic relays that have lost their network, fused their protocols, and gone feral. 

Normally, the Mire sleeps as a solid expanse of packed silt. Its trillions of particles lock themselves together using short-range electrostatic charges, forming a crust strong enough to support the weight of a wandering Cairn. It waits for decades, drinking the overhead light, humming with a sub-audible, collective computation that has no purpose other than to maintain its own cohesion.

When a Cairn—or a rare, heavy Scourer whose magnetic cushion has begun to fail—wanders out onto the center of the flat, the Mire wakes. 

It does not ripple or splash. It simply cancels the electrostatic charge between its particles in a twenty-foot radius around the prey. Instantly, the solid ground becomes a frictionless, fluidized bed of dry powder. The prey drops straight down, silently, as if falling through air, swallowed to the bottom of the basin in less than a second. 

Once the victim is submerged in the pitch-black, suffocating pressure of the deep silt, the Mire begins to circulate. Driven by its own internal magnetic currents, the dust begins to spin around the trapped object at high speed. It becomes a localized, underground sandblaster. In complete silence, thirty feet beneath the bright, placid surface of the plain, the prey is abraded down to its constituent molecules in a matter of hours. The ceramic is ground into fresh powder to expand the Mire’s mass; the copper, gold, and charge-carrying polymers are sorted and digested into the colony. 

Above, under the relentless white sky, there is no sign that anything has happened at all—only a brief, faint *hiss* like salt poured onto paper, and a slight, shimmering bloom of tarnished gold spreading slowly across the pale face of the flat.
