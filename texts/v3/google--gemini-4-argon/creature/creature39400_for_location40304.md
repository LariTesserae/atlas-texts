To see what else lives here, you have to stop looking for flesh and start looking for distortions in the light. 

In a medium where visibility reaches thirty miles and the background is a complex geometry of black stone and iron trestles, the only true camouflage is a matching index of refraction. Half a mile off the starboard flank of Derrick Nine, suspended in the dead space between two mooring cables, the water seems to warp slightly, as if a lens of denser brine were refusing to mix with the surrounding sea. It is only when the thing rotates five degrees toward the sun that the edge of its mantle catches the zenith light, throwing a sharp, prismatic flare of magenta and cyan across the basalt below.

This is a glass-mantle—an apex invertebrate the size of a mainsail, though folded in upon itself like a collapsed parachute. It is not gelatinous; soft jelly would be shredded by the acoustic violence of the tectonic slips or sucked into the intake manifolds twenty miles away. Its body is composed of cross-linked, rubbery silicates, stiff as boot-sole leather and clear as optical quartz. The creature has no brain in the centralized sense, because a dense mass of gray matter would show up as an opaque smudge against the sky. Instead, its nervous system is spun out into a wide, hexagonal lattice of silvered nerve-fibers that looks, from beneath, exactly like the sun-rippled surface of the brine three hundred feet above, and from above, like the fractured cooling-cracks in the basalt floor.

It does not swim. Swimming creates low-frequency pressure waves, and in this hyper-dense acoustic chamber, a single kick of a fluke is a dinner bell rung across three counties. Instead, the glass-mantle sails on the sound itself. By stiffening or relaxing the silicate ribs along its outer margin, it alters its own acoustic impedance, catching the relentless, booming *thrum* of the bronze flywheel turbines and riding the standing pressure waves the way a vulture rides a thermal. It tacks across the basin on the noise of Derrick Nine, sliding sideways until it intercepts the overlapping rhythmic hammer of Derrick Twelve, hovering motionless at the node where the two mechanical roars cancel each other out.

Down on the floor, where the long, knife-sharp shadows of the olivine needles never move save for the slow wheeling of the sun, something else is creeping along the insulated discharge pipes.

They look, at first, like mineral accretions—lumpy, gunmetal-gray sleeves wrapped around the iron trestles. The line-crews who occasionally drop small, heavily weighted bathyspheres from the surface ice-cutters call them *rivet-hares*, though they bear no resemblance to any mammal. They are low, heavy-bodied mollusks, cousin to chitons, whose shells are made not of calcium carbonate—which dissolves too readily in this acidic, mineral-hungry brine—but of iron sulfide and scavenged brass. 

A rivet-hare spends its life clamped to the vibrating steel of the infrastructure with a foot of dense, gray muscle coated in a biological adhesive that cures only in sub-zero brine. It moves an inch an hour, its radula—a ribbon of teeth tipped with magnetite—scraping away the gray bacterial frost that creeps up the pilings from the fault vents. Because the machines never stop, the rivet-hare’s entire physiology is outsourced to the industrial rhythm. It has no heart. It does not need one. The sixty-cycle-per-second vibration of the cast-iron pilings shakes its hemolymph through a series of one-way valves in its visceral mass. If a turbine ever seizes—a rare event that brings the repair barges out across the roof of the sea—the rivet-hares on that station simply suffocate in their own stagnant blood within an hour, still locked so tightly to the dead steel that they must be chipped off with cold chisels.

But the derricks are not merely reefs; they are nurseries, and they have bred specialists that could not have existed five hundred years ago before the first pilings were driven into the plateau.

Behind the great intake manifolds, where the bronze blades cavitate and throw off clouds of microscopic vacuum bubbles that collapse with the force of tiny hammer-blows, lives the *trestle-weaver*. 

You will never see the animal itself unless you crack open the flange of a low-pressure exhaust housing. It is a colonial worm, no thicker than sewing thread, dull red and blind, packed by the millions into the grease-grooves and bolt-sleeves of the machinery. What you see—what changes the very geometry of the derricks—are its nets. 

From the shivering eaves of the iron complexes, stretching out fifty or a hundred yards to anchor on the tips of the needle-spires, hang vast, geometric webs of biological wire. The worms extrude a protein doped with dissolved heavy metals drawn from the vent-brine, spinning a filament that has the tensile strength of piano wire and the thickness of a hair. In the crystal water, these webs are invisible until a ribbon-chordate, blinded by the acoustic shadow of the derrick, drifts into one. 

The filament does not entangle; it slices. The steady, relentless draw of the intake current pulls the gelatinous ribbon-fish through the taut grid like cheese through a wire cutter. The severed strips of mirrored cartilage drift downward, settling into the warm forty-degree plume of the exhaust ports, where the worms extend long, pale feeding-siphons from between the iron rivets to dissolve the catch.

And sometimes, the webs catch something larger.

The bone-plated spindle-predators—the *tuning-forks*, as the acoustic technicians on the shore call them, because of the long, hollow rostrum that projects four feet in front of their jaws—know about the wires. They have to. A five-hundred-pound fish moving at forty knots in water five degrees below freezing cannot afford to collide with a cable of bio-steel. 

The tuning-fork does not use its eyes to find the webs; its eyes are small, sunken behind thick lenses of flush bone to protect them from the brine, useful only for gauging the broad silhouettes of the basalt needles against the sun. Instead, its hollow snout is filled with a dense oil that matches the acoustic properties of the water, lined with sensory hairs that read the "shadow" every object casts in the din of the turbines. To a tuning-fork, the world is a blindingly bright three-dimensional map of roaring sound. The basalt spires are tall, sharp-edged silences; the haul-cables are singing lines of thirty-hertz hum; the ribbon-chordates are fluttering, silvered echoes.

To hunt in the wire-gardens around a derrick, a pair of tuning-forks will wait forty minutes—the exact interval of the plateau’s tectonic clock.

They hang fifty feet off the bottom, motionless in the lee of a green olivine dike, their pectoral fins locked out like hydroplanes, their gill-slits barely cracked to let the freezing brine trickle over pale, purple filaments that use copper instead of iron to bind oxygen. Their overlapping armor of yellowish, enamelled bone ticks faintly as it expands and contracts in the stray eddies of the exhaust vents.

Then, sixty miles to the north, the fault slips.

The shockwave arrives not as a sound, but as an instantaneous wall of overpressure that turns the water momentarily opaque as dissolved salts flash-precipitate and redissolve in a millisecond. The steel haul-cables whip sideways like struck guitar strings, throwing off a secondary shriek that deafens every sensory organ in the basin. The silicate webs of the trestle-weavers go momentarily slack as their basalt anchor-points shudder; the schools of ribbon-chordates convulse, their mirrored organs flashing in simultaneous, chaotic panic, turning the middle depths into a blinding blizzard of white light.

In that three-second window of acoustic whiteout, when the turbines' rhythm is swallowed by the planetary groan and the worms cannot feel the tension on their lines, the tuning-forks move. Their tails—crescent moons of stiff bone—blur once, twice, cavitation bubbles trailing from their sharp rear edges like silver smoke, and they are through the web-corridors, jaws unhinging with the dry *clack* of billiard balls to shear the largest ribbon-fish in half before the echoes have even reached the far wall of the basin.

Yet for all this violence, the strangest inhabitants of the brine are the ones at the very bottom of the deepest trenches, three miles out from the nearest machine, where the basalt cracks open into the root of the plateau.

Here, the brine is so heavy with leached barium and lithium that it forms a separate ocean within the ocean—a pooled, syrupy layer ten feet deep that never mixes with the water above it. The sunlight reaches this floor just as clearly as it reaches the shallows, illuminating the halocline like a sheet of polished chrome laid down in a canyon of black glass.

Nothing swims down into the heavy layer; the osmotic pressure alone would draw the water from a fish’s cells in minutes, turning it into a salted mummy. Yet across the black volcanic floor beneath that chrome blanket, there are tracks.

They are neat, parallel grooves, four feet wide, pressed into the razor-sharp basalt gravel—not by weight, for nothing has much weight in liquid that dense, but by sheer, crushing traction. If you watch a single patch of the deep floor through a telescope from the rim of the plateau (for on a calm day, through the double lens of the surface and the deep halocline, you can count the pebbles at four hundred feet from a cliffside two miles away), you will eventually see one of the *dredgers* pass through the frame.

It takes an hour to cross twenty yards of stone. It is an arthropod, or something that arrived at the same conclusion as an arthropod from a very different starting point—a flat, articulated shield of black, basalt-colored chitin twelve feet across, low as a manhole cover, with no head and no tail, capable of moving in any direction on forty stubby, piston-like legs tucked entirely beneath its rim. 

It does not eat the bacteria, and it does not eat the falling scraps of silvered cartilage that hit the chrome interface above it and bounce off like leaves on ice. It eats the rock. Beneath its carapace, a ring of diamond-hard mandibles grinds away at the fresh olivine and volcanic glass, cracking the crystal lattices to harvest the trapped hydrogen gas and trace sulfurs locked inside the newly cooled stone. As it moves, it leaves behind a neat ribbon of pulverized, snow-white silica sand—the only soft thing in the entire ocean—which the dense brine immediately cements into a hard, pale pavement.

Over the centuries, these creatures have paved the bottom of the trenches in a crazy-quilt of white highways that gleam up through the three hundred feet of blue water like chalk lines drawn on a blackboard.

And when a haul-cable on one of the derricks finally fatigues and parts—an event that happens once a decade, the triple-braided steel whipping down through the column to lay across the trenches like a dead serpent—the dredgers do not go around it. Over the course of months, they climb up onto the pickled iron, their mandibles finding the rich, refined carbon-steel far more nutritious than the raw basalt. They sit on the cable in a long, black row, vibrating in time with the distant turbines, slowly chewing the six-inch hawser into a neat line of sparkling gray dust, while three hundred feet overhead, the sun pours down through the freezing, empty blue, magnifying every tooth-mark on the steel for anyone who cares to look down from the sky.
