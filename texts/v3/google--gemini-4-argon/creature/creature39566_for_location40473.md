To find what lives here, you have to stop looking for flesh and start looking for an interruption in the geometry. 

In a room where everything is constantly being lacquered in fresh stone, the only way to survive is to either move faster than the rock can set, or to let the rock do the moving for you. The residents of Junction 14-Sub-Zero have chosen a third way: they have become the mason, the mortar, and the chisel.

If you watch the half-buried wrench on the grating during the forty-minute quiet phase, you will notice that its pearl-grey nodule of false flint is not growing evenly. The calcium builds up on its left flank, yet the right flank remains stubbornly bare, weeping a thin, clear oil that smells faintly of turpentine and old brass. 

Then, a piece of the rimstone terrace three inches away detaches itself with a dry *snap*.

At first, it looks merely like a sliding crust of blue-grey scale, the size of a pocket watch, skittering down the slope of a stalagmite. What gives it away is that it moves *uphill* against the frozen tallow bank, hauling itself along on six rigid, needle-sharp stilts of crystallized copper sulfate. 

The maintenance crews two levels up have no name for them, because no crewman has knocked the ice off the inspection hatch since the winter of ’94. If a naturalist were lowered into the cistern on a bosun’s chair, he would try to classify them as crustaceans or arachnids, and he would be wrong. They have no shell in the biological sense. Beneath that roughly hexagonal cap of layered boiler-scale and lime, there is only a tight, pulsing knot of pale, rubbery gray musculature—a naked, walnut-sized gizzard heavy with concentrated ammonia and dissolved lead, operating at twenty-six degrees without freezing for the same reason antifreeze does not freeze. 

Everything else about the creature is borrowed architecture. 

Its legs are hollow tubes of brittle mineral crust that it extrudes from six weeping pores along its underside. It does not grow these legs so much as it *leaks* them, letting the freezing room-air harden a trickle of lime-rich mucus into a rigid stilt, which it then levers with short, hydraulic spasms of its core. When a leg snaps—which happens constantly, with a sound like a dropped teacup—the creature simply abandons the shard on the terrace, seals the pore for three minutes to build pressure, and forces out a new, wet needle of stone that freezes solid before it hits the floor.

There are perhaps forty of these small, nameless *scab-walkers* in the cistern, though you will never see more than three or four moving at once. During the long freeze, most of them are fused directly into the landscape. They park themselves along the razor-sharp lips of the rimstone basins, lower their carapaces flush with the wet, syrupy stone, and let the room cement them in place. For half an hour, they are indistinguishable from bumps of grey slag. 

Beneath its dome of false flint, however, the walker is busy. Its underside is a wet, rasping plate of microscopic iron teeth—precipitated directly out of the viaduct’s rust-water—with which it endlessly scrapes at the black, gelatinous oil-lichens beneath it. It does not eat the lichen for calories alone; it eats it for the grease from the leather gaskets above, which it renders inside its freezing gut into the thin, turpentine-scented oil it uses to keep its joints from calcifying.

They are not the only things here. They are merely the quarrymen.

Look higher, into the three-inch collar of perpetual mist where the horsehair-wrapped steam pipes meet the freezing draft. The silicate-turf—those spun-glass fronds that *tick* and contract when hit by a drop of brine—is not entirely flora. Or rather, it is a flora that has learned to string wire.

Stretched between the warm canvas of the steam-return and the bruised-violet ballast tube near the ceiling is a lattice of threads so fine they only show when the orange carbon-lamp catches them from beneath. They are not spiderwebs. They are drawn filaments of pure, flexible lead-soap and silicate, extruded by things that live inside the wet horsehair insulation like worms in an old coat. 

These are the *drafters*. They never leave the warmth of the canvas jacket. If you peeled back the wire-wrapped cloth, you would find only a slick, colorless jelly veined with bright blue copper-salts, smelling fiercely of ozone and tallow, throbbing in time with the fifty-cycle hum of the telegraph tube above. The jelly has no eyes, no mouth, and no boundaries; it is a single, spread-out nervous system that has soaked into the rotting horsehair over twenty years, kept warm by the exhaust steam of the municipal engines.

Its body stays in the cloth, but its reach fills the upper cistern. Every seven minutes, when the great flywheels three levels up engage the night-shift dynamos and the ironwork shudders, the drafter uses that vibration. As the pipes rattle, tiny beads of heavy, lead-saturated tallow are shaken loose from the creature’s pores along the underside of the canvas. They drop two, three, four inches into the freezing air, pulling a microscopic tail of viscous glass behind them before the twenty-six-degree chill snaps them solid. 

These hanging plumb-lines are snares, though they do not catch flies. There are no flies. They catch the weather.

When the brass governor on the eastern wall clicks, and the counterweight drops with its heavy, oiled sigh, the whole ecology of Junction 14-Sub-Zero shifts into its twelve-minute frenzy.

At the first cough of the relief pipe, the scab-walkers break their stone seals. All across the terraced floor, forty-odd nodules of pearl-grey flint suddenly *crack* upwards like miniature trapdoors as the walkers pump hot, ammonia-rich fluid into their stilts, shattering the new crust that tried to entomb them over the last forty minutes. 

As the five gallons of forty-degree greywater surge across the basins, the walkers do not flee the flood; they wade into the scalding forty-degree torrent on their brittle stilts, leaning into the current like crabs in a tide-pool. The hot water dissolves their old, overly thick mineral legs, whittling them down to needle-thinness, while the creatures frantically shovel the fresh, floating scum of boiler-scale and pneumatic grease into their vents before the ammonia wall can freeze it.

Above them, the localized snow-squall hits the ceiling. 

As the bank of steam shears against the sub-zero western bulkhead and turns into sideways-drifting flakes of greasy frost, the yellow sulfur-molds burst into their golden clouds of peppery spores. The convection currents carry this rich, sulfurous smoke straight through the harp-strings of the drafter’s glass snares. 

The threads do not stick; they ring. 

Vibrating in the fifty-cycle hum of the violet ballast tube, the silicate threads pick up an electrostatic charge from the dry, freezing draft. The golden spores and the rising droplets of mineral-heavy steam don't just drift into the threads—they leap to them, drawn by static, coating the glass lines in a thick, crackling rime of sulfur, lime, and grease within thirty seconds. 

Then the drafter reels them in. 

It cannot pull the brittle glass back into the canvas, so it does something simpler: it secretes a single drop of warm, caustic potash from the horsehair at the top of the thread. The drop runs down the snare, melting the glass-and-sulfur icicle into a heavy, yellow syrup that runs *up* the thread by capillary action, drawn straight into the warm horsehair mat to be digested over the next forty minutes of winter.

Sometimes, a drop of that caustic melt misses the uptake pore and falls.

If it hits the floor, it hisses and burns a pinhole in the rimstone. If it hits a scab-walker wading through the blow-down slush below, the potash eats straight through the creature’s borrowed dome of boiler-scale in three seconds.

When this happens, the walker does not thrash. It simply stops. Its six copper-blue stilts lock into the cooling slush. Before the twelve-minute cycle has even ended—before the drain-trap at the lowest corner has sucked the excess water down with its hollow, metallic gulp—the other walkers have already converged on the breached shell. They do not fight over the rubbery gray gizzard inside; they neatly scissor it into four or five pale ribbons with their iron-toothed undersides, tuck the pieces beneath their own domes to replenish their antifreeze, and leave the empty, half-dissolved cap of stone sitting in the terrace.

By the time the steam vanishes into the bronze mesh of the intake vents, the empty shell is already glazing over. 

The miniature blizzard stops. The violet light hums. The orange carbon filament burns in its porcelain cage, throwing the long, red shadows of the standpipes across the wet, gleaming steps of the basin. 

Down on the grating, thirty-nine walkers settle back into the niches of the rimstone, fold their freshly extruded stilts beneath them, and let the weeping brine cement them back into the floor. Two inches from the half-buried wrench, a new, smooth nodule of pearl-grey false flint begins to form over a hollow dome of scale, indistinguishable from the rest of the mountain range, waiting for the seven-minute shudder of the dynamos above.
