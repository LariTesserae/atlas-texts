At first, it is easy to believe the city is entirely automated—a monument built by absent algorithms for a population that hasn't arrived yet. You can watch the pearlescent needles for hours from the basalt flats and see nothing behind the seamless, unpaned curves of the upper levels but the reflection of the moving clouds. Even the silver pods that zip beneath the transit ribbons are opaque, their hulls uninterrupted by windows or doors.

If you want to see the citizens, you have to watch the bases of the towers when a squall clears, or look very closely at the herds of pale ungulates moving across the wet black stone.

A figure will detach itself from the seamless base of a needle—not through a door, because there are no hinges or lintels, but through a temporary dilation in the ceramic skin that seals behind them like heavy cream closing over a dropped stone. 

They are tall, though their height is exaggerated by the proportions of their bodies and the way they dress for the plateau. They are human, or were several generations ago before the high-altitude adaptations were quietly edited into their marrow. Their chests are noticeably deep, barrel-vaulted to house lungs that take in the thin, six-thousand-foot air without haste, and their skin has a faint, slate-violet undertone caused by a dense, hyper-efficient hemoglobin that laughs at the cold. 

When they walk out onto the basalt, their boots do not clink. They wear footwear grown from the same programmed matter as the city’s foundations—thick, dark soles that read the sharp geometry of the rock beneath them and soften instantly at the point of impact, flowing over the razor-sharp hex-edges like stiff putty before springing back. To watch a citizen cross the water-flats is to watch someone walking on deep moss; they move with a long, rolling, buoyant stride that leaves the shallow mirrors unbroken.

Their clothing is a study in silent utility. They wear long, multi-layered cloaks of a heavy, matte-charcoal fabric that mimics the black lichen on the ridges. These garments have no zippers, buttons, or flapping hems to snap in the wind; the edges adhere to one another through electrostatic charge, wrapping the wearer in a series of overlapping, draft-proof folds. As they walk through the drifting silver mist, the fabric drinks the moisture just as the lichen does, channeling the cold water down microscopic grooves in the weave until it drips off a single pointed tail at the back of the ankle, keeping their boots dry.

They are a profoundly quiet people, raised in an acoustic environment where a shout feels like an obscenity. Having no walls to bounce sound off of, and living among millions of wild animals whose panic could easily turn destructive, the citizens have abandoned outdoor speech almost entirely. 

When two of them meet on the flats—perhaps a municipal tuner checking the mineral uptake of a growing foundation, and a herd-watcher returning from the western ridges—they do not call out. As they approach within fifty paces, the high collars of their cloaks stiffen slightly. They communicate through a sub-vocal apparatus resting against their collarbones, speaking in low, throat-clicks and hums that are translated into tight-beam ultrasonic pulses, or simply passed as text across the thin,amber-tinted lenses they wear over their eyes. To an observer standing ten feet away, the two citizens simply pause in the mist, incline their heads toward one another for a long minute while the calf-sized wading birds preen unbothered around their knees, and then walk on.

Their work is slow, observational, and deeply entangled with the biology and geology of the plateau. They call themselves *Litho-curators* or *Tenders*, though not in words. 

A Tender’s job is to manage the marriage between the growing city and the wild basin. The towers, extruding themselves from the volcanic crust, are hungry for specific silicates and iron oxides; left entirely to their own programmatic greed, the buildings would leach the rock brittle and drink the warm runoff dry. So the citizens walk the miles between the needles, carrying long, slender staffs of pearl-ceramic. Where a tower’s root-zone is drawing too heavily from the basalt, a Tender will press the tip of their staff into a flooded hexagon, releasing a slow, chemical counter-persuasion that tells the rock to stay rock, redirecting the city’s growth three yards to the left so it doesn't disturb a breeding pool of the translucent amphibians.

Others live almost entirely among the animals. Because the pale grazers and the wolf-martens have no fear of the architecture, they have no fear of the architects, provided the architects behave like topography. 

You can see a biologist—a *Pelt-reader*—standing dead still in the middle of a three-thousand-strong column of the heavy-shouldered ungulates. The citizen’s cloak is dialed to match the exact pale, creamy-grey of the herd’s winter coats, and its thermal insulation is so total that the animals cannot smell human heat on the wind. As the vast, papery shuffle of padded hooves flows around them, the citizen reaches out with a gloved hand that exudes a mild, numbing electrostatic warmth. They run their fingers along the flank of a passing bull, the glove’s sensors reading parasite loads, fat reserves, and mineral deficiencies in the animal's blood, while simultaneously brushing away the sharp basalt splinters that occasionally work their way into the creature's fetlocks. The bull does not even break stride; it simply leans an ounce of its massive weight into the stroke, lets out a long, steaming huff of breath, and keeps walking.

The towers have no stairs or elevators in the traditional sense. Because a needle might be only forty feet wide but two thousand feet tall, the interior is organized like the stem of a marsh reed—a bundle of hollow, pearlescent tubes through which citizens rise and fall on columns of magnetically buoyed air, stepping off into private alcoves that are grown to fit the exact curves of their bodies. 

Here, high above the mist, the silence finally breaks, though never into noise. The interiors are warm and smell faintly of wet stone, ozone, and the bruised-apple scent of the red scrub they cultivate in vertical hydroponic troughs for tea. In these high rooms, the walls are turned completely transparent from the inside out. A family will sit together on terraced, heated floors that steps down toward the invisible curve of the outer wall, eating cured grazer-meat and pale, blind cave-fish raised in the dark, subterranean reservoirs beneath the power grids. 

They talk indoors, their voices remarkably low and resonant from those deep, high-altitude chests—a slow, vowel-heavy dialect that sounds like water moving through deep rock. They watch the white-winged raptors land three inches from their faces on the outside of the wall, seeing the pink skin of the birds' feet as the building’s exterior raises its microscopic ridges to catch their talons. 

They are a people who have traded the frantic, noisy friction of history for the pace of geologic time. When a young couple decides to form a household, they do not buy an apartment; they go down to the flats, walk half a mile out from the nearest pylon until the water-mirrors hold no reflections of other buildings, and drive a seeded ceramic stake into a crack in the black basalt. 

Then they go back to their parents' needles, or camp among the warm data-vaults with the sleeping predators, and they wait. It takes twelve years for a needle to grow high enough to pierce the cloud layer. During those twelve years, they visit their rising home every few days, watching it push up four inches at a time through the silver mist, pruning its pearl-white flutes with their staffs, and watching the wading birds learn to use its shadow. By the time the tower is tall enough to live in, they know every square inch of the stone it was made from, and every animal that sleeps against its roots.
