To find the masters of this chimney, you cannot look at the center of the gale where the skimmers ricochet, nor at the thrashing bronze curtains themselves. You have to look at the violent, right-angled borders of the air—the places where the wind breaks its teeth on the invisible geometry of the machines.

They are called the *taut-kin*, or the *wire-men*, though they have neither veins nor gender, and their silhouettes are so aggressively streamlined that from a distance they look like fissures in the bleached basalt. 

There are nineteen of them currently living in the shaft. There have never been more than twenty-five, and never fewer than twelve. 

At rest—though "rest" is a relative term in a sixty-mile-an-hour vertical hurricane—a member of the taut-kin resembles a long, flattened bundle of dark whalebone and oxidized copper ribbon, pressed flush against the stone. They are seven feet long, no thicker than a man’s hand, and possess four limbs that do not end in fingers, but in articulated, hooked crampons of dense, iron-rich horn. Their bodies are entirely desiccated, free of the softness that wind can bruise or tear. Instead of skin, they are sheathed in overlapping, streamlined plates of biogenic mica that shed the abrasive dust. Instead of muscle, they move via bundles of dry, fibrous tendons that contract and snap when shocked by the static charge they hoard in their porous, ceramic bones.

When they move across the rock face, they do not climb. They release three of their hooks, angle their flat, blade-like torsos into the gale, and let the wind whip them upward or sideways in a controlled, screaming arc before driving their crampons back into the microscopic pores of the basalt with the force of a nail gun. 

They have no eyes; in a chimney choked with pulverized silicate and razor-fronds, eyes are merely soft targets. Instead, their narrow, prow-like heads are split down the middle into a tuning-fork chasm lined with stiff, metallic cilia. They *feel* the topography of the nave through the shape of the roar. Every object in the shaft—every thrashing frond, every booming bladder-plant, every erratic skimmer—casts a distinct acoustic and barometric shadow. To the taut-kin, the chimney is not a dark stone tube; it is a blindingly bright, constantly shifting tapestry of pressures and pitches.

And at the center of their sensory world are the machines.

The taut-kin do not worship the bismuth resonance-harvesters, because worship implies a distance between the devotee and the god, and the taut-kin live directly inside the machines' aura. The cubic distortions of suspended inertia that surround the coffin-sized harvesters are the taut-kin’s architecture, their furniture, and their tools. 

To an outsider, the invisible cubes are lethal traps where the wind shears at ninety-degree angles and shatters bone. To the taut-kin, those razor-sharp borders between sixty-mile-an-hour gale and dead, frictionless calm are the only places where complex life is possible.

A taut-kin will anchor its lower crampons into the stone just inches outside a harvester’s distortion field, letting the hurricane batter its lower body, keeping its piezoelectric metabolism charged and frantic. Then, it will lean its upper torso *into* the invisible cube. 

Inside the cube, there is no wind. There is no sound. The transition is so abrupt that a taut-kin’s body appears slightly offset, refracted by the bent gravity. Within this three-foot pocket of eerie, absolute stillness, the creature can unfold the delicate, fan-like organs hidden beneath its thoracic plates—structures that would be instantly shredded in the open shaft. Here, in the vacuum-quiet beside the smoked-glass hull of the machine, two or three of them will lean their heads together and communicate. They cannot speak in the gale, so they speak in the silence, clicking their crampons gently against the bismuth casing or vibrating their tuning-fork skulls to trade faint, intricate harmonies about the state of the wire-roots or the density of the spore-clouds below.

They are the self-appointed tenders of the vertical garden, and the harvesters of its static.

Because nothing rots here, the chimney is in constant danger of choking on its own immortal growth. If the bronze curtains grow too thick, they block the wind; if the wind slows, the friction drops; if the friction drops, the glowing bladders go dark, the static fades, and the whole dry ecosystem freezes into inert stone. 

So the taut-kin prune the nave. 

They do not use tools they carry; they use the machines. A taut-kin will unspool a long, braided tether of dead wire-root from its waist, anchor one end to a patch of overgrown bronze fronds, and then leap across the abyss—riding a violent updraft like a kite on a short string—to slap the other end of the tether into the path of a harvester’s cycling beam. 

When the hair-thin filament of solid light snaps out, it hits the tether. The kinetic drain instantly races down the wire-root into the targeted patch of bronze fronds. Deprived of atomic motion, the whipping, razor-sharp leaves drop to absolute zero in a heartbeat. The next gust of wind doesn't bend them; it shatters them like spun glass. A cloud of bronze shrapnel explodes upward, carried out of the chimney's mouth into the pale sky, clearing the rock face for new, conductive growth.

Their relationship with the skimmers is less agricultural and more predatory, though they do not eat the origami creatures for meat. They eat them for *altitude*. 

The taut-kin are heavy—dense with iron, copper, and mica. If one loses its grip and misses a wall-tack, the sixty-mile-an-hour wind is not quite strong enough to blow it out the top of the shaft, nor weak enough to let it fall cleanly. A falling taut-kin tumbles into the chaotic mid-air eddies, battered by bronze leaves, sinking slowly toward the unseen, lightless abyss below where the wind is born. 

To prevent this, they hunt the skimmers to harvest their dry, pressurized bladders and their hyper-tense, snake-skin wings. Using the ninety-degree wind-shears around the machines as invisible anvils, a taut-kin will herd a flock of skimmers into a cubic boundary. The skimmers, built for elastic turbulence, cannot process the sudden, right-angled wall of altered gravity; they slam into the invisible plane and fold like dry leaves. The taut-kin snatches them from the air, strips their weightless membranes, and weaves them into their own carapace, creating makeshift sails and parachutes. 

An elder of the taut-kin—one who has survived in the chimney for decades—is a terrifying, magnificent sight. Its dark, bone-thin frame is so heavily adorned with stolen skimmer-wings, braided wire-roots, and shards of singing bronze frond that it no longer looks like an animal at all. It looks like a localized storm of debris that has learned how to hate the abyss. 

When the sun passes directly over the open roof of the nave—a brief window of twenty minutes at high noon—the shaft floods with blinding, cold mountain light. 

During these twenty minutes, the temperature shifts just enough to send the harvesters into a frenzy. All twelve machines cycle at once. The air in the chimney becomes a latticework of solid light filaments and screaming, fractured gravity cubes. The noise rises to a pitch that stops the heart. 

Instead of hiding, the nineteen taut-kin unhook themselves from the walls entirely. They spread their stolen sails, lock their iron crampons together into a single, jagged wheel in the center of the rushing air, and ride the central column of the gale. They spin there, suspended in the blinding dust and the shriek of the bronze curtains, letting the stray beams of the machines graze their outer plates—playing a lethal game of roulette with absolute zero—drinking the raw, violent static of the noon storm until their ceramic bones glow through their mica skin like white-hot filaments in a vacuum tube.
