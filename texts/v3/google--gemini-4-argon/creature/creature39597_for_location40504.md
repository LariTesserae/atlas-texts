If you wait by the copper knife until the shadow of the low peat-wall stretches long and thin across the silver moss, the ground fifty yards away will simply split open. 

There is no hinge, no wooden frame. A seam in the cushion plants parts like a pair of lips, held open by a wedged rib-bone, and a person pulls themselves up out of the root-mat.

They do not stand up straight. That is the first thing you notice. Even when fully clear of the slit, they remain crouched, their center of gravity slung low between wide, bowed legs. To stand tall on the dish is to make yourself an anomaly—a lightning rod for the wind and a pillar for the blinding, frantic flocks of swallows to smash against. The people here move with a rolling, crab-like gait, their knees permanently bent, one long-fingered hand often trailing lightly across the velvet tops of the cushion plants for balance, the way a sailor touches a bulkhead on a pitching ship.

Up close, it becomes clear that the relentless roar of the plateau has shaped their bodies from the bone outward. 

Their ears are small, thick, and curled tightly against their skulls like dried mushrooms. Each ear possesses a heavy, muscular tragus—a fleshy flap that they can voluntarily clamp shut when the afternoon swarm reaches its deafening crescendo. They are not deaf, precisely, but their relationship to air-borne sound is vestigial. They do not speak with their mouths when they are above ground. To open your mouth in the afternoon is to swallow three bronze-winged burrowers before you can draw a breath; to shout is to throw a pebble into an ocean of noise.

Instead, when a second harvester emerges from the slit—a younger one, hauling a drag-sack of pale, blind cave-newts—the first communicates by stamping the heel of their foot into the springy turf. *Thump-thump. Thump.* 

Fifty feet of woven, tensioned root-fiber carries the low-frequency impact instantly between them. The younger one feels the word in the arches of their bare feet, in the fluid of their knee joints, and in the wide, flat bones of their pelvis. 

Look at their feet: they are enormous, splayed, and entirely unshod. The soles are not calloused in the way a rock-dweller’s would be; they are tough but supple, grooved like the pads of a tree frog, flush with nerve endings. A harvester standing still on the mat is reading a newspaper five miles wide. Through their soles, they can feel the shift in the barometric breath of the sponge below; they can feel the subterranean rumble of a localized larval hatch half a mile north; they can feel the heavy, rhythmic *thud-drag-thud* of their neighbor two miles east, hauling a full net down from its bone poles.

Their clothing is minimal and strange, dictated by a warm world with no hides, no wool, and no flax. They wear aprons and chest-wraps woven from the inner bark of deep-water roots, greased heavily with rendered insect fat until the fabric gleams like dark amber and sheds the drenching fogs. Across their shoulders and backs, where the sun beats down through the thin high-altitude air, they wear overlapping scales of cured bronze-bug wing-cases, sewn onto gut-mesh. When they move, they do not rustle; they click, softly, like shifting coins. Over their faces, they wear half-masks of fine gut-netting stretched over bent bird-wishbones, keeping the air playable around their nostrils and mouths while the sky boils with chitin and feathers.

They are small, dense people, barrel-chested to process the thin air, with skin the color of steeped tea—darkened by the relentless ultraviolet glare of the high sky, yet perpetually damp from the greenhouse mists. Their hair is kept shaved to the scalp, or else plastered down with a stiff pomade of grey clay and grease; loose hair is a hazard here, a snare for frantic, clawed insects that will tangle themselves to the roots and buzz against the skull until they die.

Watch the two of them work as the afternoon swarm begins to thin. They move to the bone-framed nets. The gut-mesh is sagging, pregnant with a pulsing, bronze-green mass of burrowers that smells sharply of hot copper and bruised celery. 

They do not use baskets to clear the nets. Instead, they unhook the bottom corners of the frame and roll the mesh up like a carpet, crushing and immobilizing the catch in one practiced, two-person heave. Their hands are extraordinary instruments—the fingers thick at the base, the nails grown long, curved, and hardened by a diet almost entirely composed of chitin, bone-marrow, and mineral-rich moss-water. They use their thumbnails like skinning knives, flicking the heads and tough wing-cases off a handful of stragglers with mechanical, unthinking speed, popping the fat, yellow thoraxes straight into their mouths beneath their masks.

Who are they, when they are not working? 

To know them, you cannot stay under the vault of the sky. The surface is merely their farmland and their hunting ground—a blinding, roaring workshop they endure for a few hours a day. The woven blister-hut nearby is not a house. If you were to peer inside its low, narrow crawl-tunnel, you would find it packed floor-to-ceiling with cured peat-bricks, bundles of dried bird-ribs, and clay jars stoppered with wax, fermenting a rich, dark paste of crushed cicadas. The hut is a silo. It is an anchor.

The people live *down*.

When the sun begins to dip toward the unseen rim of the bowl, and the temperature drops just enough to turn the rising lowland haze into a blinding, lukewarm soup of fog, the two harvesters gather their rolled nets, pull the copper knife from the turf, and slide back into the slit in the earth. They pull a plug of living cushion-moss over their heads, sealing the ceiling.

Twenty feet down, the world changes.

Here, suspended in the middle layer of the fifty-foot mattress, is the real village. It is a series of living chambers hollowed out of the ancient, woody peat, where the roots are as thick as a man’s thigh and tough as ironwood. The air here is dark, stiflingly warm, and smells overwhelmingly of damp cedar, mushroom mycelium, and old sweat. 

And down here, the roar of the sky is gone. In its place is the *Hum*.

Because you are now *inside* the speaker cone, the synchronous thrumming of the billions of burrowers is no longer a chaotic noise; it is a physical pressure, a vast, organ-like chord that vibrates through the woody ribs of the chamber walls. It is so constant that the brain deletes it within minutes, leaving a strange, heavy silence underneath.

In the dark, lit only by small stone-clay dishes burning clear insect-oil, the harvesters take off their masks. There are seven or eight of them in this warren—an extended family unit, three generations. Here, their posture changes. They do not stand, because the ceilings are low and domed, Instead, they recline in the curved, hammock-like cradles formed by the great spreading roots, moving through the tunnels using both hands and feet, swinging from tendon to tendon with lazy, ape-like grace.

Now, they use their voices. 

Because they are always competing with the deep, metallic bass-note of the plateau’s hum, their spoken language has evolved to occupy the only acoustic space left open. They speak in a rapid, bird-like register of high-pitched whistles, clicks, and nasal hums that slice cleanly over the top of the earth-drone. A conversation in the root-dark sounds like water dripping into a cistern, punctuated by the dry *clack* of tongue against palate.

An old man—his legs bowed almost into a circle from seventy years on the sponge, his eyes milky with high-altitude cataracts—sits in the warm dark, his bare feet pressed firmly against a thick, silver "listening-root" that runs through the center of the chamber like a telegraph wire. He is not sleeping. His toes twitch slightly. He is listening to the family five miles south. They have struck a pocket of blue clay deep near the ancient granite floor, four feet below the water-table, and they are beating out the news on a tensioned root-head with mallets of whale-hardened peat.

Around him, the others are eating—dipping fingers into warm fermented bug-paste, chewing the sweet, starchy pith of young root-shoots—and weaving. They are always weaving. Their fingers never stop moving, even in sleep. They braid the pale, shredded tendons of the swallows with the bronze silk of burrower cocoons, making cords that will never rot in the perpetual damp. 

They have no concept of wood that does not bleed sap when cut; they have no concept of stone, save for the mythical, cold "bone of the world" that their deepest vertical shafts sometimes scrape against, fifty feet down in the black, anoxic water at the bottom of the peat. To them, the world is a living beast. The moss is its skin, the roots are its sinews, the fog is its breath, and the deafening, endless roar of the surface is its voice. 

They do not worship it, any more than a tick worships a dog. They simply hold on, tucked warm and deep in the fleece of the giant, rising through its pores for a few blinding hours to comb the lice from its back, before slipping down into the humming dark to whistle to one another until the fog clears.
