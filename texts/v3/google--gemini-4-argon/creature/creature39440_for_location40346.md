They have the calves of timber-jacks and the flat, spread feet of bog-trotters, though they rarely go barefoot. To go barefoot is to invite the rot, or the small, blind leeches that ride up on the foam of the central cataract. Instead, they wear clogs carved from crippled cedar burls, strapped over stockings of greasy, unwashed goat-wool that smell fiercely of the billy even after three winters of wading through snow-melt. 

You can tell a native of the sinkhole before they open their mouth by the way they stand when they think they are standing still. They have a slight, perpetual knee-bend, a sailor’s roll in the hips, because the floor beneath them is never dead wood. It is always sighing, shifting an eighth of an inch to the left as a water-trough fills on the roof two tiers up, or shuddering as a neighbor’s wall-tree takes the brunt of a downdraft. If you put one of them on flat, dry granite, they get sick to their stomachs within the hour; they complain that the stone is "pushing back" too hard, that it has no give, that it jars the teeth in their heads.

Their hands are wide, short-fingered, and stained a permanent, bruised purple-black around the nails and knuckles from the tannin in the bog-water and the sap of the choke-cedar. It is a dye that does not wash out; a baby gets its first smudges before it can walk, from chewing on peeled willow-switches to ease its teeth, and an old woman goes into the sump with hands the color of an old iron skillet. Over the black stain, their skin is coated in a thin, waterproof glaze of mutton tallow, crushed fern-oil, and soot. They grease themselves the way water-birds preen, rubbing a lump of tallow over their cheekbones, the bridges of their noses, and the backs of their wrists every morning before pushing through the bladder-skin flaps of their doors. In the blinding, silver-white glare that follows an afternoon squall, the people gleam like peeled seals among the dark green leaves.

Because of the noise—the cataract’s bass roar, the *thump-clack* of the fulling-hammers, the hysterical, non-stop static of the ibises and choughs—no one in the basin speaks with a normal chest-voice. To use a chest-voice is to murmur into a gale; it gets swallowed three feet from your teeth. Instead, they have developed a way of throwing their voices through their noses and the roofs of their mouths, a high, flat, penetrating twang that cuts across the water-din like a cooper’s mallet hitting a hoop. To an outsider, every conversation sounds like an argument between two people being pinched. They drop all soft consonants. Everything is vowels and hard clicks, accompanied by a wide, sweeping shorthand of the arms—one hand slapped to the crown of the head means *watch the goat-drop*; two fingers hooked downward means *the lower sluice is backing up*; a fist knocked twice against a wall-bough means *this wood is taking too much sap, fetch the gouge*.

They do not think of their houses as buildings. They think of them as slow, stubborn beasts that have to be broken to the harness every spring and kept from bolting during the summer rains. 

Every household has its "girdler"—usually the oldest person whose knees have stiffened too much for the ladder-ways, or a sharp-eyed twelve-year-old who hasn't yet filled out enough to haul the fulling-baskets. The girdler’s whole life is spent listening to the house. They sit in the damp, smoky gloom of the interior, where the peat-fire smolders on a bed of wet slate layered three feet thick over the living root-floor, and they lay an ear against the main load-bearing ribs of the wall. They can hear the sap rising. They know by the pitch of the creak when a northern bough is drinking greedily from a new runnel and getting ideas about becoming a trunk again. 

"She’s hunching her left shoulder," the grandmother will pipe from the chimney-corner, not looking up from the fish-trap she’s mending with split osier. "She’s trying to throw the porch into the gutter. Go out and bleed her, Little Shag, and hang the three-stone rock from the corner-peg."

And Little Shag—every third child is called Shag, or Moss, or Crag, or Pout, until they do something stupid or clever enough at age fourteen to earn a real name—will take the bone-handled gouge, scramble out the smoke-hole onto the slick, heaving thatch of the roof, shove a brooding timber-pigeon out of the way with one clog, and make three neat, crescent-shaped cuts in the bark of the offending cedar-rib. The tree bleeds a clear, turpentine-smelling resin; the upward pull slacks off by evening; the front door closes square again. If they miss a week—if a fever goes through a family and nobody is out with the adze—the house will literally walk away from its neighbors, tearing its grafted walkways loose with a sound like ripping canvas, tilting the hearth-slates until the soup-pot slides into the fire, and opening great, ragged gaps in the sphagnum packing where the cliff-swallows immediately move in by the hundreds.

This closeness with the birds has given the people a strange, sideways set of manners. They have no concept of dirt as something to be kept *out*, only as something to be kept *moving*. Bird-lime on a shoulder is no more shameful than rain on a hat; you scrape it off with a horn spoon when you sit down to eat and flick it into the hearth-ash, where it helps the wet peat catch. The ibises—heavy, hook-billed creatures the size of geese, with eyes like yellow glass marbles—are treated like sour-tempered landlords. It is terrible luck to kill an ibis, not because they are sacred, but because an ibis eats its weight in the grey wood-boring beetles that would otherwise turn the town’s suspension bridges into sawdust in three seasons. So when an old cock-ibis decides to build its twig-nest directly over a family’s threshold, they do not knock it down. They simply lash a broad wooden shovel-blade six inches beneath its tail to catch the droppings, tilt the shovel toward the street-sluice, and duck their heads when they go in and out.

They eat from the walls and they eat from the cellar, which is to say the black water beneath the floorboards. Every kitchen has its "well-trap"—a square hole cut through the three-foot-thick weave of living root and moss, opening into the dark, gurgling underworld where the mountain runoff filters through the twelve-foot mattress of the sinkhole floor. When a wife wants supper, she doesn't go to a market; she kneels by the well-trap, pulls up a wicker kipe baited with mashed pigeon-guts and rancid goat-cheese, and dumps out six or seven blind, white mud-fish and a handful of thumb-thick black salamanders. The mud-fish are fat and sluggish, tasting strongly of wet leaves and iron, and are boiled whole with bitter bog-myrtle and pickled butterbur stems. 

Nobody is ever quite dry, and nobody is ever quite warm, except at night. 

Night is when the town pulls itself together. As the sun drops behind the western crags and the blue shadow fills the notch like cold water poured into a jug, the mountain wind dies for an hour, and the birds finally, mercifully, shut their beaks. They tuck their heads under their wings—ten thousand of them, puffed into slate-colored and mottled-brown balls embedded in the green walls—and the sudden absence of their screaming makes the roar of the central waterfall sound strangely hollow, almost gentle, like a kettle on the hob.

Then the people climb in. They do not sleep on beds; they sleep in "kists"—great, woven willow-baskets hung from the living rafters by rawhide cables, swinging slightly with the breathing of the trees. They pack the kists three-deep with dry, carded goat-hair and cured sphagnum that has been toasted over the smoke-holes until it smells like sweet turf. A whole family—mother, father, three children, an uncle with a game leg, and usually a favored kid-goat whose mother died in a rock-fall—wedges into the kist together, pulling a heavy felted blanket of oiled wool over the top and buttoning it down against the draft.

Inside the kist, it is pitch-dark, stiflingly hot, and smells overpoweringly of tallow, toasted moss, wet wool, and human skin. Beneath them, the house groans as the night-frost tightens the bark. The water-hammers down by the sluice keep up their steady, idiot rhythm—*thump-clack, thump-clack, thump-clack*—shaking the roots five fathoms down. Above them, three inches through the osier-weave and the moss-packing, they can feel the small, rapid, bird-hot heartbeats of a dozen nesting swallows pressed against the wood for warmth. They sleep like stones, tangled together like a ball of wintering adders, while the house grows an eighth of an inch around them in the dark.
