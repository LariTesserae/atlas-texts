If you stand beside the low wall of split bones and wait for your eyes to adjust to the soup of the dark, you will realize the nest is not empty. 

The mound of shed vulture feathers, dried valley-moss, and brittle arachnid husks rises and falls. It breathes, though at a pace so slow it mimics the geologic sighing of the vent beneath it. Once every full minute, the gray, crusted mat heaves upward two inches, holds for a long count while the warm bugs slide down its slopes, and then settles back with a dry, wheezing hiss that disappears entirely into the surf-roar of the cave.

When she wakes—usually when the smudge of silvery light up in the chimney turns the bruised purple of high-altitude dusk—she does not bolt upright. Nothing here moves fast enough to startle, and she has lived in this throat of rock long enough to take her tempo from the harvestmen. 

First, a hand emerges from the mulch. It is wide and flat, like a wooden spade, with four thick fingers and a thumb set low on the wrist. The skin of the palm is pale as lard and wrinkled like a washerwoman’s from decades of relentless, blood-warm sweat, though the knuckles and the back of the hand are armored in dark, horny calluses the color of old hooves. She does not brush the spiders off her arm as she reaches for the flint chopper; she moves her hand through them the way a swimmer parts duckweed. A few tallow-colored legs hook into the coarse, sparse hair of her forearm and are carried along for the ride. She doesn’t notice.

When she sits up, shedding feathers and blind beetles from her shoulders, she looks at first like an evolutionary cousin to the boulders around her. She is short—barely four feet if she ever stood straight, which she does not—and built entirely around a barrel-shaped ribcage designed to bellows-pump the thin, starved air of the fourteen-thousand-foot ridges. Her spine has a permanent, load-bearing curve, allowing her head to jut forward below the line of her shoulders. 

She is old, though she belongs to a line of things that do not show age by thinning out, only by thickening. Her skin, where it isn't caked in a protective rind of gray guano-paste and mutton-grease, has the dull, pickled look of bog-leather. Across her broad, bridgeless nose and high, shelf-like brows, a pale yellow lichen—some sulfur-loving crust that normally grows only on the lip of the steam vents—has taken root in the creases of her skin, mapping her wrinkles in dry, powdery lines. 

Her eyes are small, sunken deep beneath the bone of her brow to keep them from freezing when she goes out onto the scree. They are completely clouded over with cataracts the color of skimmed milk. In the blistered white glare of the mountain peak, those milky films act like snow-goggles, cutting the murderous ultraviolet dazzle down to a manageable gray blur; down here in the dark, she doesn't need eyes at all.

She sits for a long time on the warm basalt bench, her short, bowed legs tucked under her, listening to the ceiling. Her ears are large, flat against her skull, and hairless, twitching slightly as she reads the room. To a man from the plains, the sound of the cave is a single, maddening roar of clicking legs and rustling leather. To her, it is a ledger. She can hear the slight change in pitch where the swifts near the entry-flue are packing tighter against an early evening frost; she can hear the wet, rhythmic *slup-slup* of a dying bat thirty feet up the wall, already being disassembled by the beetles before its heart has fully stopped; she can hear the deep, subterranean *thrum* in the rock beneath her buttocks that tells her the magma seam is steady, that the heat will not fail tonight.

She does not light the horn lamp. The lamp is not for seeing. She only lights it during the deep winter blizzards when the chimney chokes with snow and the oxygen in the chamber begins to thin; she watches the little yellow tongue of flame through her milky eyes, and when it shrinks to a blue bead and gutters out, she knows the heavy, suffocating gas from the vent is rising, and she must climb up into the freezing throat of the entry-slot to breathe until the wind clears the flue.

Instead, she reaches over the bone wall—her fingers knowing every split femur and goat horn by the shape of its grease-polished splinters—and feels among the guano drifts until she finds two of the pale, rubbery tubers. She pulls them up. They come free with a soft *pop*, trailing white root-hairs and clusters of frantic, blind cave-crickets. She does not brush the crickets off; she crushes them into the sticky skin of the tuber with her thumb so they can’t jump away, and then shoves the whole mass into her mouth. Her teeth are worn down to flat, brown pegs from a lifetime of chewing grit and frozen marrow, and her jaw moves in a slow, rotary grind, like a millstone.

While she chews, she reaches up to the wall behind her head and plucks a bat from the bottom edge of the furry tile-work. The bat does not screech; it is torpid with heat and crowded too tight to panic. Its neighbors simply slide half an inch down the slick rock to fill the gap. She holds the creature against her chest, strokes its gray fur once with a heavy, spatulate thumb, and then gently spreads its leathery wing across her knee, feeling along the thin finger-bones for the tiny, pea-sized ticks that gorge themselves in the creases of the elbows. She pops three of them off with a thick fingernail, eats them like berries, and then presses the bat back against the wall. It hooks its thumbs into the fur of two others and goes back to sleep. She does not kill the things in the cave. The cave is her winter coat; you do not tear the lining out of your own coat.

Her meat comes from outside. 

When the tubers are gone, she reaches down into the bottom of her nest and pulls out what she dragged down the chimney at dawn: the hind leg of a mountain goat, already stiff and freezer-burned from three days on the high shale where the avalanches drop their dead. Down here, resting directly over the thermal crack, the meat has thawed to the temperature of a fresh kill and begun to sweat a rich, sour tallow. 

She takes the black flint chopper in her right hand. She doesn't swing it from the shoulder—there isn't room for grand gestures in the dark, and wasted motion costs heat. She rests the goat's shinbone across a heavy anvil-stone between her feet, places the sharp edge of the flint against a hairline crack she made yesterday, and brings a heavy cobblestone down on the back of the flint with a single, deadened *clack*. 

Around her, for a radius of four feet, the carpet of plum-sized arachnids freezes. A hundred thousand tiny knees lock. The *chitter-chitter* above her head stutters for half a heartbeat. Then, as the bone splits open and the warm, rank smell of yellow marrow blooms into the wet air, the carpet begins to flow toward her feet.

She does not kick them away. As she scoops the greasy marrow out with a splinter of vulture bone, she lets the tallow-colored harvestmen climb over her ankles and up her shins, their hair-thin legs tickling the leathery skin of her calves as they fight for the microscopic crumbs of fat and bone-dust that fall from her chin. When she is finished, she doesn't wash her hands; she simply lowers them palm-up into the living mat on the floor and waits. Within seconds, her fingers are buried under a boiling, clicking mound of beetles and arachnids. She closes her milky eyes, leans her lichen-crusted forehead back against the warm, sweating basalt, and lets twenty thousand tiny mouths lick her skin clean down to the creases of her knuckles.

Soon, when the moon is up and the wind outside has died from a shriek to a whine, she will smear another handful of cold mutton-tallow over her face and shoulders to seal in the cave's heat, wedge the newly split goat bone into a gap in her little wall, and haul herself up the twenty paces of the crooked chimney. She will emerge onto the silver, frost-brittle roof of the world like a spider coming out of a drainpipe—silent, hunched, reeking of ammonia and ancient sulfur—to walk the high ridges and see what the cold has killed for her while she slept.
