To find what else lives in the Sump—what stands outside the closed loop of grease, insect, bird, and furnace—you have to look at the places where the loop snags. 

A system this old, turning on the same oiled bearings for centuries, develops habits that look like intentions. Where the four-inch hole in the shale overflows into its drainage groove, the water does not always run clear. Sometimes a stray primary feather from a kettle-rook or the curved, brassy thorax of a large moth lodges crosswise in the stone channel. If it stays there for an hour, the water backs up by a millimeter. In the Sump, a millimeter is an epoch. 

Within minutes of a blockage, something comes to clear it. 

At first, it looks like a pair of oil-soaked canvas trousers and a heavy felt coat that have fallen off a gantry and become waterlogged, arrastrando slowly across the slick flagstones. As it nears the puddle, the shape resolves—though it never becomes entirely distinct from the materials of the basin. 

They are called the *Lagging-Men* by nobody, because nobody is there to name them, though if an old draftsman from the upper works were lowered down in a basket, he might recognize the silhouette of a nineteenth-century night-watchman or a turn-key, rendered entirely in the local biology. 

There are three of them in the basin. There have always been three, or perhaps the basin can only support the weight of three such slow, heavy condensations of matter. 

A Lagging-Man stands roughly five feet tall, though he is permanently stooped, his back curved like the housing of a flywheel. He has no skin in the mammalian sense. His exterior is a dense, felted mat of shed rook-down, asbestos fibers leached from the crumbling boiler-jackets, and the silk of clinker-moth cocoons, all bound together by the dark, rubbery paraffin-grease of the exhaust vents. Over decades, this composite hide has cured into something resembling vulcanized leather or heavy, tarred wool. It is completely waterproof. Rain-sheened puddles part around his wide, flat, pad-like feet without soaking in.

He moves at the speed of the cooling towers—which is to say, he makes the porcelain scavengers look frantic. While the white, rattling crabs dart out from the grates like pistons, the Lagging-Man crosses the fifty-yard plaza at the pace of an hour hand. His gait is a heavy, flat-footed slide, never lifting a sole entirely off the stone, so that he leaves twin tracks of wiped-clean slate behind him that immediately fill back in with rainbow- filmed water.

If you were to approach one in the flat, shadowless white light, he would not turn to look at you. He has no neck to turn, and nothing that functions like an eye. Where a face would be, under a shelf of stiffened felt that mimics the brim of a sou’wester hat, there is an recessed grate of bone and tarnished copper wire—a structure very similar to the tympanum of a grass-hopper, or the diaphragm of an early telephone receiver. 

He does not see the Sump; he *hears* its impedance. 

The three-part chord of the basin—the bass of the turbines, the midrange *shirr* of the moths, the treble hiss of the rooks—is the water he swims in. When a drain clogs, the pitch of the trickling water changes by a fraction of a hertz. When the crust of valve-lice on Tower Four grows six-and-a-half inches thick instead of six, the thermal insulation of the brick shifts, the boiler inside ticks slightly faster to compensate, and the brass standpipe alters its note. 

The Lagging-Man feels this dissonance in his chest-cavity, where his own slow organs—a series of muscular, bellows-like sacs that pump warm tallow-blood—are tuned in sympathetic vibration with the floor. 

He arrives at the blocked groove in the shale. He does not bend down; instead, his whole torso hinges forward from the hips with a sound like a heavy leather harness straining. From the folds of his grease-stiffened coat, an arm emerges. The forearm is plated in old, overlapping scales of oxidized iron that he has wedged into his own felt hide over the years, growing around the metal the way an oak tree swallows a fence rail. The hand has no fingers, only two opposed, spatulate thumbs of dense, yellowed horn, worn beveled at the tips like a pair of blunt chisels.

He pinches the jammed rook-feather, lifts it out of the groove, and drops it six inches to the left, onto the open flagstone. Instantly, a porcelain scavenger shoots from a nearby grate, seizes the feather, and drags it down into the dark. The water in the groove resumes its exact forty-drop-per-minute run-off. The pitch of the trickle returns to normal. The Lagging-Man remains hinged forward for three or four minutes, his auditory diaphragm vibrating softly until the local chord settles back into lockstep, and then he levers himself upright.

They do not eat the grease, and they do not eat the insects. Their metabolism is essentially geothermal and parasitic on the architecture. 

When a Lagging-Man is not clearing a runnel or scraping an overgrown knuckle of salt-efflorescence off a rail-track with his horn thumbs, he goes to the base of one of the seven towers. Each tower has two or three shallow alcoves near the foundation where inspection hatches used to be. The Lagging-Man backs into one of these alcoves, fitting his curved spine into the brickwork so snugly that he looks like a sandbagged buttress. 

As soon as he goes still, the clinker-moths and valve-lice—which treat him no differently than they treat a timber beam or an iron pipe—crawl over him from the wall. Within an hour, he is completely covered in a shivering, three-inch blanket of brass and silver carapaces. His internal temperature rises from the ambient forty-eight degrees to nearly ninety. While he stands there in the hot, humming dark under the insects, small, pale, root-like filaments—the true biological core of the creature—extrude from the soles of his feet and the palms of his horn hands, sliding into the microscopic cracks around the boiler seams. Through these filaments, he draws up hot, sulfurous condensate and dissolved mineral tallow directly from the weeping iron, drinking the machine’s sweat before it can hit the air.

When he has drunk his fill—which takes two or three days—he simply takes a single, heavy step forward out of the alcove. The living sheet of insects tears like metallic Velcro, thousands of moths tumbling down his chest and flying back to the tower wall with a brief, angry *clack-shirr* that quickly subsides into the general roar.

What makes the Lagging-Men strange, however, is not their maintenance of the drains, nor their slow, vegetative feeding. It is what they do with the things the furnaces cannot burn.

Every few years, something falls from the world above the eighty-foot concrete rim. A gull blows inland and dies in the tin-colored sky; a rusted wrench is kicked off the high perimeter wall by a surveyor who doesn't even bother to look down; a glass medicine bottle rolls through a weed-grown fence half a mile away and drops into the basin. 

The porcelain scavengers race out to meet the fallen object. If it is the gull, they strip it and take it down the grates in thirty seconds. If it is the wrench or the glass bottle, their mandibles skitter uselessly against the hardened steel or the silicate. After a few minutes of frantic, clicking investigation, the scavengers abandon the object, retreating to the grates and leaving the foreign thing sitting out on the wet slate. 

The Sump cannot digest it. It sits there, an intolerable rest in the musical score, deflecting the flat white light in the wrong way, interrupting the slide of the oily water.

Eventually, one of the three Lagging-Men will shuffle across the plaza, pick up the bottle or the wrench in his horn thumbs, and carry it to the northernmost corner of the basin, behind the Seventh Tower, where two iron rail tracks end abruptly against a wall of weeping black shale. 

Here, tucked into the six-foot gap between the tower's timber framing and the rock face, is a cairn. 

It is four feet high and ten feet long, built with the same blind, rhythmic precision as a swallow’s nest or a caddisfly’s case. In it, mortared together with stiff black bacterial grease and gray mineral salt, is every indigestible thing that has entered the Sump of Six-Gears since the turbines were first sealed below the floor. 

There are three hundred years of broken glass—green gin-squares, thick cobalt apothecary jars, soda-water torpedo bottles—all arranged with their necks pointing inward so they do not catch the drip and whistle. There are the brass button-hooks and pocket-watches of men who leaned too far over the rim in another century; there are seventeen iron rivets that popped from the retaining walls in the winter of some forgotten year; there are the white, porcelain-hard ear-bones of whales that were dropped as charms, and a dozen heavy lead plumb-bobs whose strings rotted away in the damp. 

Sometimes, two of the Lagging-Men will stand before this cairn at the same time, side by side in the narrow alley of roaring, moth-covered brick. 

They do not speak—they have no lungs for air, only the tallow-pumps in their chests—and they do not touch. They simply stand there in the forty-eight-degree drizzle, their heavy felt shoulders nearly brushing, while the kettle-rooks hiss like steam-valves above their heads. Every hour or so, one of them will reach out with a slow, armored arm, pry a green bottle out of the grease-mortar, turn it forty-five degrees so the flat white light from the sky hits a different facet of the glass, and press it back into the wall. 

Then he lowers his arm, and for the next six hours, the two of them stand motionless in the deafening chord, listening to see if the new angle of the glass has made the basin hum any sweeter.
