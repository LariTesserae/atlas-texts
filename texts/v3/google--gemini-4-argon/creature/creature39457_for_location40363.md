To find them during the bright, crushing heat of the day, you have to look at the masonry not as a floor, but as a blanket.

The courses of brick are not solid all the way down. Three or four layers beneath the sun-baked crust, the architecture changes into a labyrinth of dry-laid vaults, cooling flues, and low, corbeled galleries that run for hundreds of miles beneath the terraces. The air down here smells of damp basement, alkaline chalk, and old reed-matting. It is cool, fed by the slow seepage of the ankle-deep pools above, and it is through these dark, four-foot-high arteries that the builders move when the sky is too hot to bear.

When the brassy light of late afternoon finally fails and the bricks begin to radiate their stored heat back into the darkening sky, the wooden grates set into the dry canals are lifted from below. 

They do not emerge in crowds. The work is too vast and too thin for crowds. Instead, a crew of six or eight will climb out onto a single mile-wide terrace, while the next crew is four miles to the west, invisible behind the drop of the horizon. 

At first glance, in the purple dusk, a crew looks like part of the earthworks themselves—low, heavy-set figures the exact color of wet umber, moving with a slow, rocking gait that never hurries and never halts. They stand no higher than a tall man’s chest, but they are twice as broad at the hip and shoulder. Their proportions are built for downward pressure and lateral drag. Their legs are short, thick columns ending in wide, splayed feet with flat, horny soles that distribute their considerable weight so evenly they barely leave an impression in soft slip. 

They are mammals, or something very close to it, though the heat and the clay have pared away almost everything soft or decorative about them. Their skin is hairless, thick as harness leather, and deeply creased in geometric folds around their joints—grooves that hold pockets of gray silt and moisture, keeping them cool as the water evaporates. Their heads are set low between massive collarbones, with virtually no neck to speak of. Their eyes are small, dark, and set wide apart under heavy brows of bone and cartilage, shaded against the glare, equipped with a milky second eyelid that slides shut whenever the warm winds kick up alkaline dust.

They have no spoken language. In a world that echoes as flatly as this one, where the only acoustics are the slap of water and the hiss of drying mud, voices would be an intrusion—or perhaps simply unnecessary when there is only one task, and everyone already knows how it is done.

Instead, they communicate through the substrate. As a crew spreads out toward a curing pan, the lead builder—an older individual whose skin has taken on the pale, chalky patina of mortar—will lift one heavy heel and bring it down flat against the dry brick. *Thump.* The vibration travels cleanly through the tightly fitted courses, felt instantly by the padded soles of the others fifty yards away. Two quick taps with the edge of a heartwood paddle against a wooden weir sends a sharp *clack-clack* half a mile down a tile-lined sluice, telling a distant crew that the plank is being lifted, that water is coming.

To watch them work under the stars is to watch a process as steady and unhurried as sedimentation. 

Two of them step into the harness of a marsh-grass sledge—not with leather straps, which would rot or chafe, but by hooking their thick, curved forearms over a polished wooden yoke that rests across the meat of their backs. They lean forward until their flat noses are a foot from the pavement, and they walk. The sledge, piled high with cured bricks from the drying pans, slides over a slick of wet clay smeared ahead of it by a third worker carrying a basket of water. 

Their hands are extraordinary instruments. They have five fingers, all of nearly equal length, thick and square-tipped, with flat nails like horn chisels. When a builder kneels at the leading edge of an unfinished terrace, they do not use a trowel. They scoop the gray silt mortar from a reed basket with one bare hand, slap it onto the step, and draw the heel of their palm across it, leaving a bed of mud of an exact, uniform thickness. With the other hand, they lift a thirty-pound mud brick, drop it into place, and give it a single, settling blow with the meat of their fist. 

*Slap. Drop. Thud.* 

The rhythm goes on for hours, spreading out into the dark from a thousand scattered points across the continent. There is no supervisor. There is no blueprint. The logic of the basin is encoded in the angles of their own wrists and the length of their forearms. A brick is the length of a builder’s ulna from elbow to wrist; a mortar joint is the width of two fingers; the drop of a terrace step is the height of a knee. They are building the world to the exact measure of their own bodies.

As they work, they eat and drink without breaking stride. When a line of builders passes a silt trap where the blue-green sedge grows thick, they reach down and snap off the stiff, waxy blades, feeding them into the sides of their wide mouths. You can hear their heavy, flat-crowned molars grinding the fibrous stalks into paste—a dry, rhythmic crunching that matches the pace of their steps. When they are thirsty, they drop to all fours at the edge of a warm pool and drink like cattle, their broad ribs expanding as they take in gallons of the clay-clouded water, filtering the grit through stiff bristles inside their lips.

The strangest thing about them is not their silence, or their endurance, but their relationship to the mud itself. They do not wash it off; they cultivate it. 

Around midnight, when the bricks have finally cooled, a crew will pause in the middle of a wet curing pan. They lie down on their backs in the thick, chocolate-colored slurry, rolling slowly from side to side until they are coated half an inch deep. This is how they sleep, and how they shed parasites, and how they heal the small cracks and abrasions in their leathery hides. For two or three hours in the dead of night, the curing pans are dotted with low, breathing mounds that are indistinguishable from the surrounding earth. As they sleep, the mud dries on their skin, tightening, drawing the heat out of their dense muscles.

When the eastern horizon begins to pale—turning from black to the color of dirty pewter—the mounds crack open. 

The builders sit up. They flake the dried clay from their chests and bellies in large, curved shards, tossing them back into the wet mix to be dissolved and used again. They do not look at the sunrise. They do not stretch or groan. 

Instead, a smaller member of the crew—a juvenile, though it looks merely like a half-scale version of the adults, already thick-limbed and solemn—walks down the boardwalk with a heavy wooden scorer, dragging it through the stiffening mud to mark out the day’s baking. The others gather the heartwood paddles and the empty baskets. They stack the wooden molds in neat, interlocking pyramids at the edge of the terrace, aligned precisely with the mortar seams.

By the time the sun clears the horizon, turning the endless steps back into bands of pale cinnamon and sharp black shadow, the grates have been pulled shut from beneath. The last of the gray-skinned heels disappears into the cool dark of the under-courses. 

Up above, the first wisps of steam begin to rise from the retention troughs. A mile away, water reaches the lip of a newly laid weir—leveled to within a fraction of an inch by a thumb and a broad palm three hours before dawn—and spills over in a silent, glistening sheet, wetting the new work for the first time.
