You do not see him until the fog rolls in, because while the sun is out, he is pretending to be a stone. 

When the light is at its fiercest, he sits wedged into a vertical chimney forty feet up the eastern wall, where the black basalt absorbs the solar radiation and radiates it back like an open oven. He is wrapped in a heavy mantle of cliff-hare pelts, sewn with the fur on the inside and the grey, grease-cured hides facing out, dusted with pulverised basalt so that he disappears entirely against the young rock. 

He is small, compact, and shaped like a barrel. Generations of living above the timberline have given his people the chests of bellows—broad, deep-ribbed, and perpetually expanded to wring the thin oxygen from the violet air. His legs are short and slightly bowed, built for wedging into chimneys and mantling onto razor-edged ledges rather than striding across flat ground. 

It is only when the twenty-minute thermal swells over the lip of the abyss, swallowing the blinding nave in dense, freezing white, that he moves. 

In the canyon, the fog is a curtain. The lammergeiers and the falcons cannot hunt in the blind vapor, and the hares and partridges freeze in place, waiting out the three-minute night. For the man—and the two others who drop silently from higher crevices on braided yak-hair ropes—the fog is their operating hour.

They do not speak. The thin air makes shouting a waste of precious warmth and moisture, and the silence of the canyon is something they wear like a second coat. Instead, as they hit the spongy turf, they signal one another before the mist entirely swallows them: a quick flashed palm, pale against the dark stone; the flick of a bone-handled tool catching the last of the glare. 

Up close, their faces are astonishing. The skin is the color of old copper kettle-bottoms, crosshatched with a thousand deep creases from a lifetime of squinting into the ultraviolet fury of the sun. Around their eyes, they have smeared a thick paste of rendered marrow-fat and soot to cut the glare from the quartz veins, giving them the look of hollow-eyed masks. Even in the wet fog, their eyes are kept reduced to mere slits—or covered entirely by thin, curved visors of scraped horn darkened over grease-lamps, pierced with two tiny pinholes that let in only a fraction of the light. 

Their hands and boots are the heaviest things about them. The young basalt here will slice raw skin to the bone before the nerves even register the cold, so their boots aresoled with three layers of cured mountain-ox neck, stiff as ironwood, and their hands are wrapped in clumsy-looking mittens of untanned hide reinforced with flat splints of rib-bone across the palms. They move through the waist-deep rosettes with a rolling, heavy-footed gait, wading through the emerald velvet plants without breaking the brittle, water-hoarding leaves.

They have three minutes. Every movement is rehearsed, economical, and stripped of hesitation.

The youngest of the three—a girl whose black hair is braided with strips of white partridge-skin—goes straight to the catch-harps at the lip of the drop. The sinew nets are thrumming in the updraft, shedding heavy drops into the chiseled stone basins. Before the mist can thin, she uses the heavy bone mallet and an obsidian wedge to pop the previously frozen slabs of ice from the rock troughs, sliding them into a wicker basket slung over her broad shoulders. 

The other two move through the blind corridors between the giant cushion plants. They do not chase the snow-partridges. Instead, they check a labyrinth of hair-nooses set at bird-height along the tightest runs between the rosettes. Where a plump white bird sits strangling or frozen in the snare, a heavy mittened hand drops, a thumb snaps the neck with a sound no louder than a dry twig breaking, and the bird goes into a leather sack. They reset the horsehair loops by feel alone, their calloused fingertips nudging the knots into place while the freezing vapor condenses into frost on their eyelashes.

Before the three minutes are up, one of them kneels by the giant groundsels, using an obsidian scraper to harvest the thick, gelatinous sap that oozes from the base of the velvet leaves—the plant’s natural antifreeze, which the people use to coat their skin against windburn and to seal the seams of their water-bladders. 

Then, the wind shifts. You can feel it before you see it—a sudden drop in pressure as the thermal collapses back down into the ten-thousand-foot void.

The three of them do not linger to watch the curtain rise. As the fog begins to tattered and glow with the terrifying, violet-white brilliance of the returning sun, they are already retreating to the dry-stone windbreak under the overhang. 

The light slams back into the canyon. Instantly, the millions of water droplets on the cushion plants flash like diamonds, steaming into the dry air. 

Behind the stacked basalt prisms of the windbreak, sitting in the knife-edge shadow where the temperature plunges, the three figures crouch together, sharing their body heat like the cliff-hares on the ledges above. The oldest takes a piece of pale quartz and strikes a new tally mark into the bruised black skin of the wall—not counting the days, nor the fogs, which are too numerous to matter, but counting the *slabs*. Each mark is a block of clear ice stacked in the deep, sunless fissure behind the overhang. 

They are seasonal ghosts, climbing up from the lower valleys through the cloud-sea via routes of copper pegs driven into the sheer abyssal face. They come here at the height of the dry season, when the lower glaciers have retreated into rotten, rock-choked slush and the streams have vanished into the scree. They come here to farm the breath of the abyss. When the tally marks reach the natural vein of white quartz three feet up the wall, they will have enough ice lashed to their pack-frames to water their kin and their small herds of cliff-sheep waiting two thousand feet below, just beneath the cloud line.

Until then, they sit in the freezing dark of the overhang, chewing on dried meat and raw, fat-rich partridge livers, their chests rising and falling in slow, cavernous breaths. Their dark, slit-eyes watch the snow-partridges emerge from the rosettes to flash their ultraviolet tails in the sun. Nobody speaks. They simply wait twenty minutes for the sky to go blind again.
