If you stand on the mid-level grating for three or four hours—long enough for the tallow-colored light to stop trickling and settle into the back of your eyes—you begin to realize that some of the lagging on the pipes does not belong to the original blueprint.

There is a bulge on the underside of the number-four brine return, just above the junction where the three-inch copper bleed-lines cross like bundled faggots. It looks, at first, like an old canvas patch swollen with grease and mineral wool, bound onto the iron with turns of tarred marline. In a place where every gasket weeps and every joint has grown a beard of calcareous rime, a lump on a pipe is nothing. You would pass your shoulder within four inches of it in the alley and only notice that the water there smelled slightly less of iron and slightly more of old camphor.

Wait another hour—the time it takes the main counterweight in the north shaft to drop three links—and the marline unwraps itself.

It is not string. It is a bundle of long, flat palps, the color and texture of salt-cured pigskin, that have been holding a broad, mantle-like body pressed tight against the warm sweat of the flange. The thing does not drop from the pipe; the brine is so heavy with dissolved solids that nothing truly falls except the bismuth snow. Instead, it peels itself away with the slow, tacky reluctance of wet dough leaving a board, and settles onto the perforated iron walkway without a sound.

They have no proper name because the men who bolted the retaining plates into the basalt left before they arrived, or before they became what they are now. If the pit-works had a night-watchman, he might call it a *gasket-man*, or a *tallow-crab*, though it has no shell and no true legs.

Seen on the flat of the catwalk, where the copper-sulfate glow from a corroded junction box catches it sideways, it is the size of a butcher’s apron rolled around a boulder. Its body is a series of heavy, overlapping muscular skirts—three or four of them stacked like the leather bellows of a forge—pale grey where they are tucked in, darkening to the brown of walnut stain at the ruffled edges. Because the hypersaline water would draw the moisture out of any ordinary meat like a salt-cask draws blood from herring, the creature’s flesh is dense, vulcanized, nearly solid with waxy esters and heavy oils. If you struck it with a spanner, the iron would not bounce, nor would it break the skin; it would leave a dull, white dent that would take twenty minutes to slowly push itself back out from the inside.

It moves along the alley not by walking, but by *extruding* itself through the geometry of the plant.

It reaches forward with two primary trunks—thick, squared-off limbs that have no bone in them, only a hydro-static mesh of gristle that can stiffen like a fire-hose under pressure or go slack as an empty sleeve. The tips of these trunks are split into four heavy, opposing thumbs, tipped with horn that has been stained black and metallic by the bismuth precipitate. With these, it does not merely grip the ironwork; it fits itself to it. A thumb slides into the square recess of a sunken packing-nut; another hooks the lip of a flange; the main skirt hauls forward six inches, flowing over a thirty-pound rivet head as if ingesting it, and then leaves the rivet behind, polished slightly cleaner of its clay-film than it was before.

This is the first thing you understand about them: they have the mechanic’s touch, though they have no minds to know what a machine is.

In the tight, vertical city of the pit-works, where there is no flat ground except the grating and no open water wider than a vestibule, everything that lives must understand shapes. The parchment-eels understand the clearances of the flywheels; these heavier things understand threads, seats, and housings. Their ancestors, perhaps, were deep-trench holothurians or some blind, benthic mollusk that tumbled over the basalt lip seventy fathoms down when the brine-pool was first warming up. In the centuries since, the machinery has been their only geography. They have been molded by the standard Whitworth thread and the twelve-bolt flange.

When the creature reaches the end of the alley, where the great hogshead piston of the primary accumulator is five minutes into its downward stroke, it stops.

The gap between the descending iron cylinder and the cast-iron collar is less than an inch, weeping a slow, grey ribbon of mineral tallow that the pressure forces past the leather packing. The creature raises its upper fold. Underneath, where a head ought to be, there is only a deep, puckered recess lined with stiff, bristle-like plates of baleen, colored a bright, poisonous blue-green from the copper salts in its diet. It presses this recess against the weeping seam of the cylinder.

It does not eat the tallow for the fat alone; it eats it for the sulfur bacteria that bloom in the dark grease where the iron gets warm, and for the fine, microscopic flour of rust and bismuth that gets trapped in the lubricant. As the piston slides down—so slowly the eye cannot catch the motion, only the mind notices that a scratch on the steel is lower than it was—the creature’s muscular lip rides the moving metal, planing off a curl of sixty-year-old tallow as neatly as a carpenter’s iron taking a shaving from a joist.

While it feeds, you can see the things that live upon *it*.

The creature’s back—the broad, rubbery saddle of its uppermost skirt—is the only real estate in the yard that moves between the cold sumps and the warm condensers, and so it has become a traveling tenement. Rooted into the waxy dead-layer of its skin are three or four of the maroon kelp-bladders, smaller than the ones on the bulkheads, their acid-etched cups sunk right into the creature’s hide. The creature does not seem to feel them, or perhaps the acid is mild enough that it merely itches, a slow, chemical warmth in the sub-zero brine. Tangled around the bases of these bladders is a mat of the solder-wire stolons, braided tight, catching the plumb-line streaks of bismuth snow that fall from the upper darkness.

As the snow hits the creature’s back, it does not pile up into an avalanche as it does on the transformer roofs. Instead, the skin shivers—a single, localized ripple that travels from nose to tail once every five or six minutes—and the heavy, iridescent crystals are shaken down into the creases between its bellows-folds.

There, the skin secretes a clear, acidic slime that dissolves the bismuth just enough to turn it into a heavy paste. Over decades, the creature has incorporated this metal into its own outer rind. An old one—and there is one down in the lower sump-room, wedged permanently between two dead boilers where the intake augers turn, that has not moved fifty feet since the reigns of the last three kings on the surface—is so heavily plated with re-precipitated bismuth that it looks like an ingot of raw ore that has slumped under its own weight. Its skin has the stepped, geometric terrace-marks of a hopper crystal, catching the orange bulb-light in dull violets and tarnish-yellows.

Yet the one on the mid-level grating is not the ancient in the sump. It is smaller, restless, and it is not alone in its work.

A foot above its back, a relay inside a glass jar arcs with that faint, dental *tick*. The water tightens. Down the alley, a three-way brass cock, controlled by a float-chamber that has been half-choked with lime for eighty years, begins to groan—not a sound in the water, but a shudder in the deck-plates that makes the bismuth snow dance an inch off the iron before settling again. The valve is sticking. If it sticks entirely, the differential on the secondary condenser will stall, the warm brine-weep on the number-four return will go cold, and the sulfur-grease will harden into stone.

The creature on the grating pulls its blue-bristled mouth away from the piston with a slow, greasy *schluck* that dies immediately in the heavy water.

It does not know that the condenser will go cold. It knows only that a sticking valve vibrates at twelve cycles a minute through the soles of its trunks, and that twelve cycles a minute is an irritation, an itch in the cartilage that can only be stopped in one way.

It flows up the bulkhead. Two hundred pounds of salted, tallow-fed gristle, heavy with metal-snow, hauling itself up a vertical iron plate by hooking its split thumbs into the bolt-holes where a ladder used to be. When it reaches the brass cock, it wraps both trunks around the four-spoked hand-wheel. Wait ten minutes, watching through the lime-scaled gloom, and you will see the wheel turn.

It turns by a fraction of a degree. The creature hangs its whole weight from the right-hand spoke, using the density of the bismuth plated into its own flanks as a counterbalance, becoming, for half an hour, nothing more than a bag of living pig-iron added to the leverage of the plant.

*Clack.*

The sound goes through your ribs. The brass seat breaks free of its lime-crust; the brine surges through the pipe with a heavy, syrupy thrum; the vibration in the deck-plates smooths out into the old, drugged silence.

Up near the ceiling, where the pewter sky of the meniscus ripples lazily against the iron chimneys, one of the parchment-eels slips through the eye of a shackle, its copper cilia flattening down now that the current has steadied. On the bulkhead, the grease-eater does not let go of the wheel. The brass is warm now from the fresh flow. It tucks its split thumbs into its skirts, folds its rubbery margins down over the spokes until the wheel is entirely hidden inside its belly, and goes to sleep—looking for all the world like a spare diaphragm-housing bolted to the wall, waiting for the rust to claim it, while the metallic snow falls straight down through the dark and buries it to the knuckles.
