To find what lives here, you have to stop looking for an animal that moves *through* the clutter and look instead at the clutter itself—specifically, at the places where the timber and the brickwork have been forced to bend around something that was already there, or something that refuses to be built over.

At first, you think it is just another section of the scaffolding. Your shin is pressed hard against what feels like a bundled stilt of green hickory, slick with hot condensation and bound with wet, shrinking rawhide. You shift your weight to keep a swelling gourd-vine from pinning your wrist to a weeping brick, and the hickory bundle *shivers*. It does not creak like wood; it contracts with the thick, hydraulic *slurp* of a muscle sliding inside a tight sheath of boiled leather.

There is a resident in the shaft. Or rather, there is a mason, a trellis, and a womb, and they are all the same entity, wedged into the vertical choke alongside you.

It has no single, border-defined body because a discreet body would have been crushed to paste hours ago by the inward march of the yew beams. Instead, it exists as a distributed, load-bearing anatomy that shares its organs with the room’s primitive machinery. 

Look inches from your face, through the bruise-purple gloom of the pressed taro leaves. What you took for a heavy leather water-bucket hanging from a vine-pulley is actually a great, distended lung-sac. Its exterior is rough, pebbled hide—like pigskin cured in woodsmoke and tallow—and it is stitched directly into the split-reed matting of the wall with thick, black horsehair thread. As it inhales the scalding, turpentine-thick steam, its seams pull tight, and through a translucent patch in its base, you can see it is not breathing air, but filtering the airborne silt and suspended bone-ash from the vapor. Every time it exhales—with a wet, rattling *chuff* that synchronizes with the wooden gutters—it coughs a measured dollop of grey, lime-rich phlegm into a waiting wooden trough.

Follow the cordage leading up from that lung. It isn't hemp. It is a yard-long, braided tether of living tendon, glistening with hot grease, vanishing into a gap between two freshly extruded bricks. 

Inside the three-inch interstices of the weave—the narrow, suffocating gaps where the mortar hasn’t quite met the timber—the rest of it is threaded like a root-bound ginger plant in a clay pot that is five sizes too small. It is a sprawling, multi-jointed thing of boiled-pink cartilage, calloused yellow skin, and knuckles the size of river cobbles. It has no head—a head is a luxury for creatures that have a horizon to look at. Instead, it has *hands*. Dozens of them, sprouting from asymmetrical, meaty shoulders that are mortised directly into the green yew beams.

These are the makers of the thumbprints.

One of its arms is currently wedged diagonal to your ribs, pinned between a sweating copper pipe and a bloated bladder-fern. The arm has no elbow; it has three wrists in succession, allowing it to snake around the square corners of the self-materializing masonry. The hand at the end of it is massive, flat, and square—a flesh-trowel. Its skin is parboiled white and deeply furrowed from eternal immersion in hot wet clay, and its fingernails are not keratin, but thick, curving plates of black iron-slag that have grown straight out of the nail beds, split at the tips from prizing apart hot slates. 

As a pale root pushes out of the wall near your knee and begins to square off into a raw, steaming brick, this hand lunges—not through the air, as there is no room to swing, but by *extruding* itself along the surface of the wet wood like a muscular slug. It seizes the newly formed brick while it is still soft as dough. With a frantic, blind rhythm, the five thick thumbs on that hand press deep into the mud, leaving those frantic, overlapping prints you saw earlier. Then, the middle finger—which ends in a hollow bone stylus—jabs a sharp, three-line tally mark into the clay’s face. 

It isn't signing its work. It is *measuring* the intrusion. 

Because if you watch closely, you realize the creature is not building the room. The room is an affliction, a cancerous, altitude-sick miracle, and the creature is only trying to survive the crush by organizing it. 

Where a yew beam shoots across the gap, threatening to skewer the creature’s central liver-mass—a dark, pulsing gourd of flesh lashed with sinew to a corner post—two more of its hands intercept the growing wood. Using teeth that grow not in a mouth, but in a jagged, circular orifice in the center of its palm, a hand chews a rapid, frantic mortise-hole into the green timber, spitting out turpentine-scented woodchips that immediately sprout into tiny ferns. Another hand, thin and long-fingered like a weaver’s shuttle, rams a wooden peg through the hole, diverting the beam’s growth by thirty degrees so that it braces the wall instead of piercing the flesh.

The creature is in a state of permanent, feverish remodeling. Its own body is constantly being incorporated into the architecture, and it must constantly amputate and re-graft itself to stay ahead of the calcification. 

Down by your ankles, one of its lower limbs has been lost to the floor. A heavy forearm, thick with coarse black hair matted with clay, has been pinned beneath a freshly grown iron-ore root. The creature hasn’t struggled to pull it free; there is no leverage for pulling. Instead, the flesh at the wrist has simply given up being meat and has baked itself into a hollow terracotta drainpipe, while the upper arm has detached at the joint with a wet, fibrous *tear*, already budding a cluster of new, embryonic fingers from the raw stump to reach for the next falling bucket of boiling silt.

And the terracotta-shellfish—those blind, fist-sized idols that bubble out of the wall—are not separate animals. You see that now, as one of them crawls across the creature’s main thoracic knot. The creature does not brush it away. A wide, lip-like fold in the creature’s leathery side opens up—a kiln-slit glowing with that same dull, bruised-orange heat that lives in the mortar. The little clay crab climbs inside the flesh-pocket, clicks its copper mandibles once, and dissolves with a soft *hiss* back into raw, mineral-rich mud and shredded leaf-mulch, fueling the furnace of the creature’s metabolism so it can sweat out more horsehair, more rawhide, more bone-pins.

It is aware of you, though not as a person, or an intruder, or a meal. In this absolute density, anything that takes up space is simply a structural problem or a structural asset. 

You feel a wet, heavy heat press against the back of your neck. Another of its hands—this one broad and calloused as a hod-carrier’s shoulder—has wormed its way through the wickerwork behind you. It doesn't strike. Its five iron-shod thumbs press firmly, methodically against your spine, testing your rigidity, checking your load-bearing capacity against the inward-buckling wall of wet brick in front of you. 

Finding your bones sufficiently solid, a smaller, needle-fingered hand slips out from beneath a bronze-veined taro leaf. It holds a length of fresh, wet sinew that it has just stripped from its own forearm. With the practiced, unhurried speed of a basket-weaver who has been working in the dark since the sky froze, it loops the sinew around your collarbone, passes it through a bronze ring in the weeping slate, and pulls the knot tight with a sound like a snapping bowstring—lashing you into the plumb-line, making sure you hold up your three square feet of the sky before the bamboo overhead finishes braiding itself shut.
