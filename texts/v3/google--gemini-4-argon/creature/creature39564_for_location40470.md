To see what actually rules the valley, you have to look down into the flooded slots between the fins, where the pewter light dies and the water tastes of pennies and rotten eggs. 

The pale, tallow-like grazers—the ones the size of a hand that slide over the upper edges of the shale—are not independent organisms. They are merely tongues. 

If you were to take a pry-bar and shatter the fins surrounding one of these translucent pads, you would find a rubbery, cartilaginous tether dropping straight down from its underside into the dark water table below. Follow that tether three feet down into the packed, grinding base of the rock, and you will find the body it belongs to: a vast, flattened lattice of gristle and white nerve-cord that has grown through the interstices of the stone like wet mortar. A single one of these subterranean mats might span forty square yards, threading itself through thousands of hairline fractures, existing under immense, shifting pressure. It puts up fifty or sixty grazing pads into the twilight air to lick the bacterial milk from the slate, drawing the nutrients down into the dark. 

When the stone plates shift and snap, the creature is often sheared in half. It does not bleed; its flesh has the consistency of stiff vulcanized rubber. The severed section simply seals its pores and continues to feed, or else dissolves back into the sulfur-water to be drunk by its own neighboring mouths. In fact, the creature relies on the violence of the rock. It has no heart; its pale, sluggish blood is pumped entirely by the constant, microscopic pinching and releasing of the shale fins around it. The valley’s roaring geological settling is the beast’s pulse.

Then there are the tall, six-legged things that look like driftwood.

If you watch one for an entire day—standing in the deafening hailstorm of noise until your teeth ache from the vibration—you will notice something wrong with the way it hunts. It will stand over a pale grazing-pad for six hours. Then, with a sudden, ratcheting snap of its slate-gray leg, it will spear the pad. Look closely through the fog: it does not bring the meat to a mouth. It has no mouth. It simply holds the impaled pad aloft for twenty minutes, lets it drop back onto the stone, and takes two agonizingly slow, grinding steps to the left.

They are not arthropods. If you were to tip one over—which would take the strength of three men, as they are extraordinarily heavy—you would find that the six blade-like legs are actual pieces of blue-gray shale, selected for their curve and length, and bound together at the joints with thick, hardened wads of dark gray resin. The "body" is a bundle of smaller, resonant stones tied around a hollowed-out core of porous bog-iron. 

They are automata, though there is no clockwork inside them. Their joints are packed with a dense, fibrous gray mold that swells with tremendous hydraulic force when exposed to the sulfur-heavy mist, and shrinks rapidly when pinched. The entire six-legged frame is balanced on a knife-edge of tension. When a particularly loud, subterranean *crack* of settling bedrock travels up through a leg, the vibration triggers the mold in the joint to contract, lifting the limb. The weight shifts; another joint is pinched; the leg comes down in a new crevice. They walk on the noise. They are driven across the valley floor by the acoustic pressure of the snapping stone, wandering in blind, mechanical patterns that take decades to complete. 

They were built by the things that made the huts.

The builders are still here, though you will never see one walking the flat stone paths in the pewter twilight. They do not come to the valley a few days a century; they have never left. They are simply down in the basement of it.

They are called the *Silt-Wives* by the people who live thirty miles away on the high grass plateaus, though they have no gender and no matrimony. Long ago, they may have been human, or something adjacent to it, before they traded their bones for the local geology. Now, they live horizontally, wedged into the flooded, labyrinthine fault-lines ten feet beneath the valley floor, where the water is warmest and the roar of the shifting slate above is consolidated into a single, bone-shearing bass note. 

To survive down there, they have become flat, pale, and segmented, like six-foot-long earwigs made of wet leather and calcified gristle. Their skulls have broadened and flattened into spade-like plates of thick bone, which they use to wedge themselves between the groaning strata, holding open their flooded burrows against the settling weight of the valley. Their eyes are gone, replaced by broad, drum-like membranes of tight skin stretched over the hollows of their cheeks.

They do not see the valley; they hear its tension. 

To a creature down in the flooded dark, the deafening clatter of the four-mile basin is not random noise. It is a three-dimensional map of pressure. Every snap of a fin, every groan of a shifting slab, tells them where the rock is binding, where the water is pooling, and where a catastrophic buckle is building up that might crush their subterranean warrens flat.

That is what the huts, the walkways, and the six-legged stilts are for. The valley is an instrument under intolerable tectonic strain, and the things living under it are constantly tuning it to keep it from collapsing on their heads.

Once every few months, when the acoustic roar above develops a dangerous, rhythmic stutter—a sign that a massive fault line is locking up—the heavy slate gongs inside one of the dry-stone huts will suddenly *clang*. 

A pale, multi-jointed arm, slick with iron-slime and wide as a shovel blade, has reached up from a narrow vertical shaft in the center of the hut’s floor, struck the balanced slate with a rounded river-stone, and withdrawn into the dark. The low, booming resonance of the gong travels down through the stacked cairn, into the bedrock, and shivers a locked seam loose half a mile away. 

Sometimes, a gong is not enough. Then, in the pitch-black of night, when the fog is thickest, two or three of the leathery, flattened things will squeeze themselves up through the floor of a hut. They cannot stand upright; they move like giant, heavy-bodied salamanders, dragging themselves along the narrow, flattened walkways they built centuries ago. Their spade-shaped heads swing blindly from side to side, their cheek-drums twitching at the million-fold clatter of the dark. 

They haul themselves out to one of the tall, six-legged stone walkers. With their wide, blunt fingers, they scrape the old, spent mold from its stone joints, pack in fresh grey pulp from their own mouths, and re-tie the resin bindings. They turn the walker three degrees to the north, setting its stone legs into a new sequence of crevices, and send it marching back out into the blades. 

For the next forty years, that walker will slowly, ponderously stomp across a specific quarter-mile of shale, its heavy, iron-cored body dropping a leg every twenty minutes—*crack, crack, crack*—steadily bleeding off the tectonic tension of that sector, bleeding the pressure into harmless noise, while the pale tongues lick the mist and the things beneath the water table go back to sleep in the roar.
