At first glance, through the fluttering, amber-lit curtains of shale-moths and the constant, leaping cascades of pale stokers, the Spindle-Cleft looks entirely unmanned—an autonomous clockwork digestive tract that has been colonised by high-altitude vermin. 

You have to let your eyes adjust to the molybdenum sheen before you spot the machinists. They are the exact color of the lubricant.

The people of the Cleft—called *Gimbals* by the sparse oxygen-caravans that haul bar-stock up the mountain’s lower stages, though they simply call themselves *the Gauge*—are not visitors to this verticality; they are its third native species. Over nine generations at thirty-two thousand feet, the thin, dry stratosphere has reshaped them. A citizen of the Cleft is short, rarely topping five feet, but possesses a chest like a riveted boiler—deep, barrel-shaped, and wide from sternum to spine to house lungs capable of wringing life from the starving air. Their blood is so thick with red corpuscles that it moves with a heavy, sluggish pulse; beneath skin stained permanently pewter-grey by airborne graphite, their lips, gums, and the rims of their eyes are the dark, bruised violet of the zenith sky above.

To move through the Cleft is to watch them practice a strange, suspended geometry. Because the floor is an illusion—a mere grid of stamped steel that may drop six feet or slide four feet sideways at the whim of the mountain’s settling faults—no one here walks with a locked knee. They move in a low, fluid crouch, weight riding on the balls of their feet, hips loose. Every person wears a *crux-rig*: a harness of braided rawhide and spring-steel over their grease-blackened woolens. Extending from their hips are two heavy-duty leather lanyards tipped with brass *dogs*—spring-loaded, scissor-jawed clamps designed with a geometry stolen from the stokers' own double-thumbed paws. As a machinist traverses a swaying catwalk, they do not hold the handrails; their hands are busy with calipers, grease-horns, or brass-tipped prods. Instead, with every third step, a flick of the wrist sends a lanyard snapping out to bite onto a passing guide-wire or magnesium strut, releasing the trailing clamp in a subconscious, rhythmic cadence—*snick-thup, snick-thup*—so that when a micro-tremor hits and the bridge telescopes away from under their boots, they simply swing out into the four-hundred-foot dark like pendulums, wait for the rebound, and catch the grating with their heels without ever looking down from their work.

Look closer at their faces, and the adaptations of tool and flesh blur further. In an atmosphere of zero humidity, where the updrafts from the lower smelters carry a continuous blizzard of pulverized flint, mica-scales, and carbon powder, an unprotected human eye would dry out and score like cheap glass within an hour. The Gimbals wear *seal-cups*—goggles carved from translucent hornblende and edged with soft, cured stoker-hide that is glued directly to the cheekbones with pine-pitch and only removed once a week for cleaning. Their mouths and noses are covered by *lung-sponges*: copper-mesh cages stuffed with living, fibrous wads of the pale violet mineral-fungi scraped from the air-compressors. When a Cleft-dweller inhales, the dry, freezing air is drawn through the warm, damp fungus, which strips out the flint dust and adds a faint, sulfurous moisture to the breath; when they exhale, the moisture in their breath feeds the fungus. It is a symbiosis that leaves the lower half of their faces stained a faint, bioluminescent yellow when they unstrap their cages in the sealed, pressurized warmth of the sleeping-baffles.

They do not speak as lowlanders do. You cannot shout in the Spindle-Cleft; the air is too thin to carry the vibration ten paces, and the deep, panting breaths required by shouting would cause a worker to black out and tumble into the siphon-shaft. Instead, they communicate in *Cleft-Cant*, a language of high-frequency sibilance and mechanical percussion that slips right into the gaps of the machinery's own breathy noise. 

Listen to two valve-tuners working on opposite sides of a roaring, silent nitrogen vent: you will hear a series of sharp, wet clicks of the tongue, piercing two-note whistles that cut through the *shhh-tok* of the logic-valves, and the rapid-fire *tink-tink-tack* of their brass thumb-thimbles striking the iron struts. They use the architecture as a sounding board. Because every gallery is linked by tensioned cables and leaf-springs, a message tapped in code on a turnbuckle at the thirty-thousand-foot mark can be felt by the fingertips of a smelter-hand resting his palm against a counterweight guide three hundred feet below. The whole community is connected by a tactile nervous system of vibrating steel; they feel the mood of the city through the soles of their rawhide-soled boots and the palms of their three-fingered mittens.

Their relationship with the fauna is entirely unsentimental and deeply intimate. A lowland visitor is inevitably horrified by the stokers—by the way the pale, hairless creatures swarm over the machinery like maggots on a corpse, their heat-pitted snouts twitching. To the Gimbals, the stokers are both livestock and fellow crew. 

Watch an *exhaust-raker*—usually an adolescent, small enough to worm into the narrow, four-foot gaps behind the transformer arrays. The youth does not carry a lamp; instead, two or three domesticated stokers ride on the shoulders of their wool coat, clinging with their six leathery paws. The stokers’ heat-pits can detect a failing bearing or an overheating resistance coil long before it glows orange or throws a spark. When a stoker presses its flat, pale snout against a conduit and begins to chitter—a sound like dry peas rattling in a bladder—the raker knows the internal insulation is failing. In return, as the raker scrapes the thick, choking cakes of yellow sulfur-crust out of the flues with a long bronze hoe, they toss the richest, warmest slabs to the stokers, who devour them with greedy, grinding clicks of their mineral-hardened teeth. 

Nothing is wasted in this sealed verticality, not even the dead. When a stoker grows too slow to leap during a tremor and is crushed in a sliding expansion joint, or when a machinist’s heart finally gives out under the strain of hauling thick, sludgy blood through the thin air, there is no ground to bury them in, and the exterior rock would only preserve them as freeze-dried mummies on the razor-sharp ledges. Everything goes down the chutes to the lower gasifiers. The Gimbals view this without horror; to them, the heat that keeps the rime six thousand feet below their boots is a single, continuous fire composed equally of coal-gas, stoker-dung, shed mica-wings, and the rendered fat of their own ancestors. To stay warm in the Cleft is to be held by the dead.

As the ribbon of zenith-indigo sky overhead deepens to the absolute, star-pierced black of stratospheric night, the Cleft does not sleep—the engines cannot stop, or the brass would contract and shatter in the cold—but the shifts change. The *Night-Gauge* emerges from the insulated, double-hulled iron pods bolted into the deeper fault-lines. 

These are the elders and the *Spring-Masters*, those whose eyes have gone entirely milky from decades of arc-flash and sulfur-glare. They do not need to see. Moving down the central drive-cables like spiders on braided wool threads, they hang suspended in the dark abyss, their ears pressed to the rawhide sheaths, their bare, graphite-slicked fingers resting lightly on the huge hydraulic shock-absorbers. They are listening to the infant mountain grow.

While the young machinists above oil the wooden pulleys with dry carbon and brush the drifts of dead shale-moths toward the rotary brooms, the blind elders hang in the warm, musky updraft of the chasm, waiting for the deep, sub-audible groan in their molars that signals the next great thrust of the Aethelgard uplift. When they feel it coming—ten seconds before the rock actually shifts—their brass thimbles strike the main cables in a frantic, ringing chord that shoots four hundred feet up the iron chimney. 

Instantly, every human and stoker in the Spindle-Cleft freezes. Lanyards snap tight; wrenches are holstered; ten thousand pale marsupials coil their six legs under them. For three seconds, the only sound is the hiss of nitrogen and the dry flutter of a million mica wings. Then the mountain convulses, the whole iron world drops twenty feet on its greased rails with a giant, collective *whuff*, the counterweights plunge, the orange coils flare against the dark diorite—and before the springs have even finished their second rebound, the clamps click loose, the brooms begin to spin, and the people of the Cleft go back to their gauges.
