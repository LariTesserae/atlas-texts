If you stand long enough in the bruised light of an eroded portico, letting the heat soak through the soles of your boots until the boundary between your skin and the two-inch water begins to blur, you will realize that the Sump’s habit of forming shapes does not stop at architecture. 

A city implies a public. Because the marsh does not know what a person is, it can only approximate one through the same broken physics of memory and sediment that raises its blind towers.

At first, you take them for civic statuary that has suffered badly in the heat. At the far end of a drowned avenue, where the violet gloom pools in the sockets of a melted arcade, a knob of tallow-stone stands five feet above the waterline. It has the rough proportions of a bundled figure waiting for a carriage that has never been invented. It is entirely still, weeping warm water down its flanks. 

Then, a half-mile away, a hovering sheet of water drops from the dark with a booming slap. A low, warm wave washes down the avenue. As the ripple passes through the base of the stone figure, the stone forgets its density. The plinth softens into porridge, and the figure shifts its weight. 

They are the *Tenants*, though they pay no rent and occupy no rooms. They are not born, and they do not eat; they are precipitated out of the municipal tension between the rising silt and the falling water. 

Up close, a Tenant has the heavy, seamless anatomy of a rain-washed gargoyle or a badly melted wax candle. They have no faces—only the gentle, thumb-pressed indentations under a brow-ridge where eyes would be Suggested in a sketch. Their limbs do not end in fingers or toes, but taper into blunt, rounded nubs that merge continuously with whatever surface they are touching. When a Tenant walks, it does not lift its feet. Its lower extremities Liquefy into the submerged paving stones and Draw up again six inches further on, making a faint, rhythmic *schluck-schluck* that sounds exactly like a clogged drain clearing itself in an empty basement.

Their substance is a truce between the three materials of the Sump. Their cores are the dark, greasy silt-stone; their skins are a thick, rubbery pellicle of the clear mucilage that bleeds from the black survey-reeds. As they move through the floodplains, these long, invisible ribbons of slime catch on their bodies, winding around them like heavy, waterlogged drapery. Over decades, the mucilage traps the falling mineral sediment, hardening into the sagging, pleated likeness of wet overcoats, shawls, and aprons. 

Caught inside the creases of these mineral garments—around the collar, or tucked into the crook of a heavy, fused elbow—is the same dirty violet luminescence that clings to the lintels. In the dark, across a wide plaza, you do not see the Tenants themselves. You see only the dim, violet glow of their creases drifting slowly at waist-height above the black water, like a procession of bruised lanterns carried by invisible mourners.

They have no minds, only habits. Just as the mudflat feels an ancient, blind compulsion to arrange itself into boulevards and aqueducts, the Tenants are driven by the spatial grammar of the ruins. They are what happens when the posture of habitation outlives the inhabitants.

They do not build, and they do not repair, because in the Sump there is no distinction between ruin and construction. Instead, they *ratify* the architecture.

If the warm rain weeps down a buttress and extrudes a four-step staircase leading into the empty dark, within an hour three or four Tenants will have slouched across the commons to stand in a patient, silent queue at the bottom. One will mount the four steps, stand on the terminal nub for twenty minutes while the condensation drips from its heavy, hooded shoulders, and then step off into the air—falling four feet into the shallow water with a wet, boneless thud, half-dissolving on impact before slowly drawing its tallow-flesh back together to walk away.

They gather in the melted concentric rings of the amphitheaters, sitting shoulder-to-shoulder in the warm mud, facing an empty flooded pit where nothing is occurring. They stay there for days, entirely motionless, until their hips and flanks fuse together into a single, continuous bench of breathing stone. They remain a bench until a rising table of water lifts them twenty feet into the air; when it drops them back down, the impact shatters them back into seven or eight distinct individuals, who slowly pull themselves out of the silt and wander off in different directions, their errands completely reset.

Their relationship to the broken distance of the basin is what makes them seem almost alive. When the humidity thickens and two distant towers drift together across the mud, the Tenants occupying their thresholds are brought abruptly face-to-face. 

When this happens, they perform the closest thing the Sump has to social interaction: the *Congestion*.

Two or more Tenants, brought into proximity by the shrinking space, will lean their tallow-heavy heads together until their brow-ridges touch. The violet light in their collars flares slightly—from the color of an old bruise to the color of a fresh plum. For an hour, while the eaves of the towers mingle their runoff above them, the Tenants stand locked forehead-to-forehead. The warm water streaming down their backs merges into a single sheet; the silt of their bodies exchanges across the point of contact, equalizing their mass. If one was tall and attenuated from spending a week in a narrow alley, and the other squat and broad from sitting in a sunken plaza, they will part ways when the distance reasserts itself as two identical, medium-sized figures. They have exchanged the news of the districts; they have balanced the ledger of the mud.

Yet, there is a second kind of resident here, though it is even less an animal than the Tenants. It is a phenomenon of the rising water itself, known only by the sound of its passage and the brief, terrifying geometry it leaves behind.

When the wide sheets of two-inch water peel off the mudflats with that greasy *shhhk* and hover thirty feet in the air, they do not always drop back down as a flat table. Sometimes, the cooling air at the fifty-foot ceiling catches the hovering sheet and curls its edges inward before it falls. 

Instead of slapping flat against the avenues, the water rolls itself into a vast, coherent cylinder—a horizontal column of warm, tea-colored liquid, ten feet thick and a hundred yards long, which settles back onto the mud without breaking its surface tension.

This is a *Surveyor*. 

It has no skin, no stone, and no light. It is simply several hundred tons of sterile, copper-smelling marsh water that has temporarily forgotten how to lie flat. It moves through the city at a brisk walking pace, rolling over the low ruins like a log made of heavy glass. 

You know a Surveyor is coming because the silence changes quality. The three-second echoes of the drips are suddenly cut short, swallowed by the advancing wall of liquid. Then comes a deep, thrumming vibration through the basalt under your feet, like a heavy dray rolling over wooden cobbles.

As the Surveyor rolls down a boulevard, it picks up everything that isn't anchored to the deep bedrock. It swallows the black survey-reeds, snapping them off at the mud-line and suspending them inside its rotating belly like iron needles in gel. It rolls directly over the Tenants. 

A Tenant caught in the path of a Surveyor does not flee. It simply turns its faceless head toward the oncoming wall of water and braces its thick, root-like legs. The cylinder of water washes over it, lifting the Tenant off the ground. Inside the dark, spinning belly of the water-column, the high pressure and the violent churning strip the Tenant down. The hardened coats of mucilage are peeled away; the tallow-stone limbs are tumbled and eroded in seconds into smooth, featureless ovoids. 

The Surveyor rolls on for a mile or two, carrying a dozen tumbling, dissolving Tenants and hundreds of black reeds in its gut, reshaping the city as it goes. Where it passes over soft, porridge-stone foundations, its immense weight stamps the inverted relief of the last district’s cobblestones into the mud, literally printing the memory of the eastern avenues onto the western floodplains.

Eventually, the Surveyor strikes a structure that refuses to yield—a newly risen seawall or a cluster of towers that have condensed into a single mass. The surface tension fails. With a sound like a canvas sail splitting in a gale, the cylinder bursts. 

A localized flood roars through the dark, washing five feet deep through the empty ground-floor windows and colonnades. As the wave recedes, dropping back to its customary two-inch depth, it leaves the avenue freshly paved with stamped mud, littered with snapped black reeds, and scattered with smooth, grey boulders the size of crouched men.

For an hour, the district is entirely still. The drips resume their three-second echoes. The violet light seeps back into the mortarless joints. 

Then, one of the grey boulders in the middle of the street softens at its base. A thick, pale ribbon of reed-mucilage drifts against its flank and sticks. The stone extrudes a blunt, heavy arm, reaches out into the warm, dark water, and pulls itself six inches forward toward the nearest doorway, resuming its wait for the roof to be built.
