Between the things that surrender to the water and the things that resist it, there is a third category: the things that use the stone to cheat the water.

You do not see them at first because they are the exact color of the mortar joints—a dense, greased tallow-grey—and because they do not move with the rhythm of living muscles. They move with the rhythm of ratchets. 

When a twenty-knot rip-current tears down a smoothed colonnade, everything loose is blown away like chaff, and the hydrofoil-rays lock themselves down until the stone looks tiled in slate. But then, straight into the teeth of that invisible gale, something the size of a butchered hog comes winching itself along the floor. 

They are called *plumbs* by the few who have dragged their dead, ruptured carcasses from the ice-slush at the basin’s rim, though they have the general anatomy of a beast that remembers walking on dry land before its bones turned to gristle and ballast. A plumb is heavy—absurdly heavy. Its belly is a broad, calloused sled of grey hide, thickened to four inches of dead, insensitive horn that slides over the polished basalt like a greased iron shoe. It has no swim bladder; dropped in still water, it sinks like an anvil. Inside its torso, where a terrestrial mammal would keep light, spongy lungs, it has dense, calcified organs and a heavy, slow-beating heart that circulates blood thick with antifreeze proteins and dark purple myoglobin. 

It moves by six limbs that have given up the geometry of legs. The forelimbs and mid-limbs are long, shockingly muscular cables of tendon sheathed in loose, pleated skin, terminating not in claws—there are no cracks for claws to catch in this worn-out city—but in broad, flanged cups of stiff cartilage lined with thousands of microscopic, bristle-like ridges. 

Watch one cross an open plaza during a thermal downdraft. It does not walk. It fires a forelimb forward through the thin boundary layer of slower water hugging the stone. The cup strikes the smooth concavity of a worn paving slab and flattens; a thick cuff of muscle around the wrist contracts, pulling the center of the cup upward just a fraction of an inch. Against the thirty-four-degree stone, under twenty fathoms of head-pressure, this creates a dead-vacuum lock that three tons of shearing water cannot rip loose. Once anchored, the plumb’s shoulder—a massive, humped knuckle of white muscle behind its head—contracts. It winches its heavy, streamlined body forward five feet, slides its belly-keel over the stone, plants the next cup in the fluted hollow of an ancient gutter, releases the first with a tiny, mechanical *pop* of equalizing pressure, and hauls again.

They have no eyes. In the clear, pewter-colored twilight, eyes would only show them the endless, dizzying chaos of the silver-fish vortices and the barren grey wax of the architecture. Instead, the front of a plumb’s head is a blunt, rubbery prow pitted with thousands of gel-filled pores, like the snout of a shark or a grotesque, swollen thimble. 

With this prow, they read the city. Not by sight, and not by touch, but by the shape of the hum.

Every colonnade, every thinned-out tendon of a bridge, every hollowed-out granary in the valley has its own acoustic signature. As millions of tons of glacial meltwater squeeze through the apertures, the stone sings in deep, sub-audible standing waves. To a plumb, the sunken city is not dark or uniform; it is a blindingly loud, three-dimensional map of pressure gradients and vibrating bone. They feel the high, tight whine of a bridge-span that has been worn down to the thickness of a man’s thigh and know not to anchor there lest their own weight snap it. They feel the hollow, booming bass-note of a sunken courtyard fifty yards away and know that its curved walls are acting as a centrifuge, spinning a rich soup of blind eels and fat-globules into a dead-water pocket at its center.

They are the masters of the boundary layer and the lee. A plumb knows every shadow in the current across three square miles of masonry. It knows that if it hauls itself up the smooth, terraced mound of a worn ziggurat by follow-the-fluting—sliding its belly along the ancient drainage grooves that millennia of water have deepened into half-pipes—it can reach a lip of basalt where the updraft shears away from the stone. 

And it is not merely a solitary grazer. The plumbs have habits that look unsettlingly like industry.

Because they have no teeth—only four heavy, overlapping plates of black horn inside a recessed, downward-facing mouth—they feed by shear-force. A plumb will wedge its rear anchors into the deep mortar-groove between two monolithic blocks, locking its hindquarters down. As a great, silvery tornado of blind ribbon-eels is swept past the corner by a shifting gyre, the plumb does not lunge out into the current where it would be torn away. Instead, it reaches out with its two front limbs—which are longer, thinner, and folded back against its flanks when traveling—and holds them stiffly out into the boiling water at a precise, hydrofoil angle. 

It uses its own arms as baffles. By angling its thick, flat wrists into the twenty-knot stream, it trips the current, creating a sharp, local vortex—an artificial eddy that rips a dozen eels out of the main flow and slams them downward into the quiet pocket beneath the plumb’s belly, right against its snapping horn plates.

More than that: they farm the stone. 

In the deep, fluted culverts and the interiors of the hollowed-out towers, where the water is still too violent for the silver fish to rest, you will find stones that do not belong there. Since there is no natural rubble in the valley, the only loose stone comes from the occasional, echoing *clack* of a collapsing arch or a snapped lintel. 

When that sound rings through the clear water, every plumb within a mile stops winching. For ten seconds, across the grey expanses of the avenues, hundreds of heavy, pale-grey shapes freeze against the basalt, their blind prows lifted slightly into the current, tasting the acoustic echo to triangulate where the piece fell and which culvert it is rolling down.

They converge on the wreckage along the floor-grooves. A broken piece of a column, weighing perhaps three hundred pounds and worn as smooth as an egg, is a priceless commodity here. Two or three plumbs will surround the boulder. Anchoring their hind-cups to the pavement, they press their broad, rubbery snouts against the stone and heave together, using the slick grease of the silica bacteria as a lubricant. Inch by inch, waiting for the five-second lulls between the hydraulic hammer-blows of the gyres, they shove the boulder across the plazas. 

They do not use it to build shelters; they have no need of shelter from the water. They push the boulders into the mouths of the great drainage flumes and the narrow gaps between the columns, jamming them into the smooth throats of the architecture until the stone wedges tight. 

They are dam-builders, though they are not stopping the water—nothing could stop this water. They are *tuning* it. 

A single three-hundred-pound piece of basalt wedged into the throat of a worn-down colonnade changes the hydrodynamics of the entire avenue behind it. It breaks the laminar twenty-knot gale into a series of churning, predictable back-eddies. Behind that newly wedged stone, the current drops just enough that the pale-grey silica bacteria can bloom in thick, tallow-like carpets without being scoured away. The slate-colored hydrofoil-rays flock to the new lee by the hundreds to graze the bacteria; the barrel-sized anemones creep in to anchor in the mortar joints and whip their leather straps into the newly formed vortex; and the plumbs sit at the center of the wreck they have engineered, fat and heavy in the dead-water pocket, peeling the rays off the stone like scabs and biting them in half with their horn plates.

When they breed, or when the glacial melt at the surface freezes solid in mid-winter and the deep thermal vents slow down, they gather in the lowest points of the valley—the great, bowl-shaped amphitheaters where the polygonal floor-slabs are cupped three feet deep. 

There, forty fathoms down in the pewter dark, fifty or sixty of them will lock themselves together. Because the stone is too smooth to offer cradles, they become the architecture for their own young. The largest, oldest plumbs—creatures the size of draft horses, their hides scarred white where tumbling masonry has struck them, their backs encrusted with small, parasitic anemones—form a tight, outward-facing ring on the floor of the bowl. They lock their suction-limbs not only to the basalt, but over one another’s backs, overlapping their broad, calloused bellies like the scales of a pine cone until they have formed a single, living mound of dense flesh and gristle that the currents slide over without catching an edge.

Underneath that dome of heavy bodies, in the warm, still pocket of water heated slightly by their slow, massive metabolisms and smelling faintly of iron and musky grease, the newly birthed young creep over the smooth floor-stones. They are the size of river otters, translucent pink and soft-boned, practicing the pop and ratchet of their suction-cups against the undersides of their mothers' bellies, listening through the three-foot-thick wall of adult flesh to the great, booming flute-music of the sunken city outside, waiting until they are heavy enough to crawl out into the gale and not be blown away.
