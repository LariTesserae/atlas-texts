To find the people of the Sunken Grid, you must stop looking at the horizon and look instead at the joints in the floor. 

An outsider walking the three-hundred-foot avenues will think themselves entirely alone for hours, until a square of cast-iron grating twenty yards ahead seems to detach itself from the pavement and slide sideways across the wet basalt. What you took for a drain cover is a human back, clad in a rigid, articulated carapace of cured Sluice-Ray hide and riveted boiler-felt, stamped with the very same diamond-mesh pattern as the city’s ironwork. 

They are called the *Lappers*, or sometimes the *Plumb-Kin*, though among themselves they use names that refer only to the specific subterranean conduit or pressure-district they tend—*Third-Sluice-East*, *Brass-Sleeve*, *Nine-Valve*. For generations, their physiology and their habits have been ironed flat by the contradictions of the plateau: the crushing barometric weight of the steam, the thinness of the high-altitude oxygen, and the relentless vertical tyranny of a world that permits nothing to stand upright for long.

A Lapper rarely stands fully erect on the surface. To stand six feet tall in the open avenue is considered a vulgar, dizzying waste of breath and heat-tolerance; the air five feet above the pavers is slightly cooler, yes, but thinner and erratic with shifting sulfur drafts. Instead, they move in a low, fluid scuttling crouch, their centers of gravity slung inches above the scalding runoff. They wear heavy, broad-soled clogs carved from soapstone or dense, petrified mycelium, grooved on the undersides like tire treads to channel the slick water away so they do not hydroplane like the Rays. Another pair of smaller, saucer-shaped stone pads is strapped to their palms and knees. Moving across an avenue, a Lapper drops to all fours and *skates*—a rhythmic, frog-like pushing with the calves and a long, gliding slide on stone palms and knees, covering miles of wet grid with almost no vertical bobbing, looking from a distance like a heavy grey tortoise or a flat, sliding box of tools.

Physically, the Grid has bred them broad, short-limbed, and extraordinarily barrel-chested. Their lungs are immense, bellows-like organs adapted to draw oxygen from air that is forty percent hot water vapor. Their skin, when exposed inside their subterranean locks, has the pale, waxy, slightly yellow translucence of tallow, utterly devoid of hair—even eyebrows and eyelashes have vanished over the centuries, as hair only traps scalding condensation and blinds the eyes with mineral crust. Instead, their brow ridges are thick and overhanging, channelled with deep, natural creases that direct sweat and condensate away from the corners of their eyes toward their cheekbones. Their eyes are dark, small, and protected by a thickened, yellowish secondary membrane that leaves their gaze looking perpetually milky and half-asleep in the amber gloom.

Yet their life is not on the pavers; the pavers are merely the roof they must cross to clean the gutters. 

They live in the *Dry-Pockets*—the interstitial masonry voids between the surface tiles and the roaring geothermal Hell fifty feet below. The city’s original builders left millions of cubic feet of inspection galleries, counter-weight shafts, expansion joints, and dry-stone thermal baffles within the upper thirty feet of the basalt crust. Here, the heat is still tremendous—a constant, wet ninety-five degrees—but the air is stable, filtered through porous curtains of calcified sponge-stone. 

A family or a maintenance-clutch of eight to twelve Lappers will inhabit a horizontal gallery no taller than four feet high and six feet wide, stretching two hundred yards along the flank of a main steam-trunk. There are no chairs, no tables, no beds with legs. Everything is flush. The floors of their galleries are lined with thick, cured leathers made from the rubbery domes of the giant Blisters, peeled from the Catch-Basins before they can pop, tanned in alum and iron-salts until they resemble white vulcanized rubber. The Lappers sleep, eat, and mate lying prone on these warm, vibrating floors, their bodies pressed flat against the masonry so that the constant, barometric *thrum* of the bronze pistons below rocks them like a cradle. A Lapper taken off the plateau to quiet ground cannot sleep; the absence of the stone-pulse makes them feel as though their own heart has stopped beating.

Because speech carries poorly through solid rock and gets swallowed by the hiss of steam on the surface, the Lappers are an unsettlingly silent people with their mouths. Their vocal cords are atrophied by sulfur-scarring anyway; when they do speak aloud, it is a wet, raspy whisper no louder than the *shhh-tick* of the grates. 

Instead, they speak through the Grid. Every Lapper carries a *tuner*—a short, heavy rod of phosphor-bronze with a flat chiselled tip at one end and a bone knob at the other. By pressing the bone knob against their own mastoid bone (behind the ear) or against their front teeth, and resting the bronze tip against a grate, a standpipe, or a basalt paver, they can "hear" the machinery of the city for half a mile in every direction. They communicate with one another by rapping intricate, stuttering rhythmic codes against the iron frames of the hatches. A clan mother sitting in a Dry-Pocket twenty feet down can tap a complex pattern against a brass tension-rod with her bronze knuckle-rings, and five hundred yards away on the surface, her children—skating across the plaza on their stone pads—will feel the syntax of her message vibrate directly up through the soles of their clogs into their shinbones. 

Their relationship with the city’s machinery is neither that of master nor worshipper; they are more like symbiotes, or intestinal flora within a titanic bronze beast. They do not know who built the Sunken Grid, nor do they care to speculate. To them, the pistons, the paddle-turbines, and the thirty-foot Dredge-Gantries are geological facts, no different from the sky or the water. 

Their economy is entirely one of *clearance* and *salt*. The great enemy of the Grid is calcification—the slow, inexorable choking of the runnels, valves, and grate-meshes by precipitated lime, silica, and iron-scale. While the spindly Dredge-Gantries handle the deep, heavy sludge of the main shafts, the Lappers tend the capillaries. 

They go out in teams of three or four during the *Slack-Hours*—those periods when the subterranean valves shift the boiling rivers to adjacent sectors, dropping the temperature in the local shafts from lethal to merely blistering. Using long, flexible ribbons of spring-steel, they rod out the narrow precision runnels. They scrape the velvety red thermophilic mold from the mortar joints before it can rot the stone, harvesting it in perforated brass baskets. This red mold is their flour and their tallow; pressed into bricks, washed of its excess sulfur in the cooler condensate-traps, and baked against the skins of the underground steam-pipes, it becomes *rust-bread*—a dense, coppery, umami-heavy cake that tastes of iron, mushrooms, and salt. They hunt the Sluice-Rays not with spears, which would bounce off the basalt and ruin the edge, but with weighted rings of chain dropped flat around the creature’s perimeter, pinning its undulating margins to the stone until it can be slid onto a flat tin shovel and carried down a hatch.

Perhaps their strangest custom revolves around the Dredge-Gantries. Because the Gantries take ten minutes to complete a single stride and follow paths dictated by ancient, buried cam-shafts and pressure-logic, the Lappers treat them as moving architecture—slow, iron weather systems. 

A Lapper clan that is moving its territory, or traveling to the distant Catch-Basins to harvest Blister-skins, will not skate the whole twelve miles across the exposed pavers. Instead, they wait at an intersection for a Gantry. As the padded iron foot settles into the runoff, the Lappers swarm up the spindly, greased struts like pale crabs, hooking their broad leather belts to the trusses just below the machine’s heated waist-trough. There, suspended twenty feet in the amber fog—the highest a Lapper will ever go in their life—they wrap themselves in their ray-skin cloaks and sleep for days as the machine steps across the avenues, warmed by the radiating heat of the dissolving-trough, oiled by the rising steam, swaying gently in the dark sky while the bronze bucket rises and falls between them like a pendulum.

When a Lapper dies, there is no ground to dig, and wood for a pyre does not exist within a hundred miles. They do not leave their dead in the Dry-Pockets to rot in the wet heat. Instead, they perform the *Dissolution*. The body is stripped of its stone clogs, its bronze tuner, and its leather vents, and carried out to the nearest working Gantry. When the machine hauls up its bucket of calcified sludge and dumps it into the superheated acid-bath of its waist-trough, the clan climbs the leg and slides the dead Lapper in alongside the mineral crust. Within three strides of the machine—half an hour—bone, tallow, and muscle are rendered down into heavy salts and warm slurry, and flushed down the bronze standpipe back into the deep turbines, adding their small fraction of grease and mineral weight to the endless, silent momentum of the floor beneath their children’s feet.
