They are called the Gantry-Men by the three or four municipal engineers who have seen the silhouettes on the dead-end inspection cameras and chosen not to file a report, though they are not men, and they do not use the gantry. They belong to the floor, the lower six feet of the chamber’s air, where the sulfur-heavy heat and the ammonia rising from the central guano ridge create a broth too thick for human lungs to clear.

At first glance, if you were standing by the rusting boring shield in the monochromatic amber gloom, you would take one for an industrial casualty—a bundle of heavy, wet canvas tarpaulin or a discarded heap of leather welding aprons slumped against the base of a cast-iron column. They do not have the clean, sharp outlines of surface animals. Their anatomy is a slow, slumped compromise between the pale, grease-slicked skin of a blind cave salamander and the heavy, segmented architecture of the things that eat the dead on the bottom of the sea. 

There are four of them in the four-hundred-foot hall. The territory is divided not by walls, which they largely ignore, but by the cones of orange light cast by the seven surviving sodium lamps.

When one moves, it does not walk; it shifts its weight with the agonizing, hydraulic slowness of cooling slag. Its core is roughly the size of an oil drum, low-slung and flat-bellied, dragged across the warm basalt on six thick, jointless limbs that end not in feet, but in wide, grey pads of calloused muscle, like the foot of a giant snail. As it advances through the inch-deep puddles, it leaves a broad, clean trough in the iridescent film of oil and iron bacteria, a path that the millions of cave crickets immediately part to allow, their long antennae folding back in unison like grain before a scythe, then snapping back into place once the bulk has passed.

The creature’s skin is its most active organ. It is thick, loose, and folded over itself in heavy, pendulous pleats that drape down to the stone, colored the exact dead-violet of wet basalt under sodium light. Because the air is a hundred percent saturated and a hundred degrees, the creature cannot cool itself by sweating; instead, the skin is vascularized right to the surface, a dense network of dark, slow-pulsing vessels that drink the slightly cooler condensation dripping from the overhead iron. Along its spine—a long, knobby ridge that mimics the bolted seams of the cast-iron columns—grows a living thatch of the same white, thread-like fungus that glazes the guano mound. This is not an infection; it is a farm. The pale, blind millipedes and beetle larvae that blanket the floor climb freely up the creature’s weeping flanks to graze on the fungal coat, and every few hours, a long, flat, ribbon-like tongue—grey and sticky as flypaper—unfurls from a horizontal slit beneath the creature’s front hood, sweeps across its own back with a wet *slap*, and draws a thousand tiny, crunching bodies back into its gut.

It has no eyes. Eyes are for things that need to know what is happening fifty feet away, and in the vault, fifty feet away is identical to here. Instead, its forward end tapers into a broad, shovel-shaped hood of cartilage, pitted with rows of deep thermal pores and fringed with thirty or forty stiff, translucent whiskers, some three feet long, which trail through the warm puddles and brush against the steel rails. 

Through these whiskers, the creature reads the room as a series of vibrations. It feels the sixty-cycle hum of the transformer behind the east bulkhead as a constant, comforting north star. It feels the deep, rhythmic *thrum* of the six-foot municipal water main near the ceiling as the sky. And it feels the floor: the tiny, dry patter of a million cricket legs, the wet *plop* of a dying bat losing its grip eighty feet up in the dark and hitting the guano ridge.

When a bat falls—and with three hundred thousand of them overhead, one falls every few minutes—the nearest of the four creatures alters its trajectory. It does not hurry. The beetles and the crickets will do the hard work of breaking down the tough wing-membranes and the fur first. The creature simply redirects its slow, hydraulic creep toward the impact tremor, arriving twenty minutes later to settle its wide, hot belly over the spot, dissolving the bones, the meat, and the five hundred scavengers that gathered to eat it in a single, external pool of clear, acidic digestive mucus before sucking the whole slurry back up through its throat-slit.

They have one other behavior, the one that gave them their name among the men who watch the monitors up-shaft. 

In a room where nothing changes—where the temperature never drops, the sun never rises, and the white noise of the colony never ceases—time has no edges. To keep their heavy, sluggish metabolisms regulated, the four creatures use the infrastructure. Every ten or twelve hours, one of them will haul its bulk over the buried rails to the north wall, right beneath one of the seven burning bell-jars. It will rear its front half up against the slumping gray shotcrete, hooking its two forward pads over the square iron plates of the rock-bolts. 

There, directly beneath the wire cage of the sodium lamp, where the stunted, translucent ferns grow, the creature goes rigid. It presses its broad, cartilaginous hood flat against the warm glass of the light fixture, or against the sweating cast iron of a column, right where the sixty-cycle vibration from the distant substation is strongest. It stays like that for hours, standing upright like a headless man in a heavy, glistening coat, letting the mechanical hum of the city’s forgotten grid rattle through its dense, cartilage skeleton, shaking the parasites loose from its folds and churning the chitin-heavy soup in its gut. 

From the far end of the hall, through the yellow fog and the drifting snow of bat-dust, you can see two or three of them at any given time, spaced fifty feet apart along the colonnade—dark, upright pillars of wet hide and white mold, leaning quietly against the iron, waiting for the next small thing to lose its grip on the ceiling.
