The plateau exists at an altitude where the atmosphere is thin, dry, and warm, like the air trapped inside an unventilated attic. There is no sky to speak of—only an unbroken ceiling of velvet black that presses down on the expanse, starless and moonless, as if the world were enclosed inside an immense subterranean vault whose roof is simply too high to ever be found. 

Beneath this darkness lies the Salt-Iron Flat, a regional tableland of ancient, exhausted geology. Billions of years of dry wind—now long ceased—have ground the former mountain ranges down into low, whale-backed swells of smoothed basalt and packed ochre dust. There are no sharp edges left in the natural stone. Even the boulders have been rubbed down into oblate spheroids, half-buried in drifts of dry, flour-like silt that has settled into its final, motionless equilibrium. There is no water here; there has not been rain in epochs. The only moisture is the heavy, metallic oil that weeps from the deep tectonic seams, pooling in shallow, lightless mirrors that never evaporate and never ripple.

Across this wide, dark plain sits the Foundry-Ward, an urban sprawl of cast-iron and sintered slag that shares the same ancient stillness as the stone. It is an industrial city, but its layout is remarkably sparse. Great, windowless manufactories, skeletal cooling towers, and battery-blocks of riveted plate rise from the silt, yet each structure or cluster of structures is separated from its neighbors by half a mile of empty dust. Narrow-gauge rail lines and bundled pipelines run straight as plumb-lines across the dark flats to connect them, elevated on low stone trestles that have been worn smooth by time.

The city is not ruins; it is simply in a state of near-static operation. Because there is no natural light, the only illumination comes from the infrastructure itself. Every few hundred yards, a tall, crooked lamppost holds a sodium-vapor bulb burned down to its final, weakest filament, casting a dim, bruised-orange cone onto the grey dust below. Along the flanks of the distant factories, tiny indicator apertures glow in dull, unblinking rows of oxblood red and amber. The darkness between these points is absolute. To walk from the Smelting House to the Condenser District is to step out of one faint pool of orange light and spend twenty minutes wading through warm, ankle-deep silt in pitch blackness, guided only by the smooth, warm curve of the iron pipeline beside your hip and the distant pinprick of the next district’s pilot light on the horizon.

The warmth of the plateau does not come from a sun, nor does it come from the furnaces, most of which are banked to a low, maintenance smolder. The heat is radiogenic, rising evenly from the ancient crust below, holding the entire tableland at a constant, blood-warm seventy-eight degrees. There is no temperature gradient, and therefore there is no wind. The air hangs completely dead. The thin plumes of sulfur-yellow smoke that rise from the five-hundred-foot chimneys do not billow or disperse; they ascend in ruler-straight, pencil-thin columns until they vanish into the upper dark, unchanging from year to year.

Because the air is so still and thin, the city is profoundly quiet. An industrial metropolis usually roars, but the Foundry-Ward operates on a geologic timescale. The great flywheels inside the turbine halls are forty feet tall, cast from pig-iron, and they turn at a rate of one revolution every four hours. If you press your ear against the warm, bolted hull of a processing plant, you can hear the deep, subterranean groan of a gear tooth engaging, followed by twenty minutes of silence before the next tooth catches. Outside, in the open spaces between the sectors, the only sound is the occasional *tink* of cooling metal, or the soft, dry hiss of pressurized gas equalizing through a brass valve three miles away. 

Nothing grows here. There is no moss on the iron, no lichen on the ancient, rounded basalt, no dry scrub in the flats. The world is entirely mineral and metallurgical. Yet, it is not completely devoid of life. 

In the warm, dark spaces beneath the pipeline trestles, and clinging to the brickwork of the thermal-exchange stacks, live the *bellows-worms* and the *slag-ticks*. They are rare, solitary creatures with shells of calcified iron-sulfide that look indistinguishable from rusted bolt-heads or clinkers of industrial waste. They move no faster than the hour hand on a clock, grazing on the thin films of yellow sulfur that sublimate around the pipe joints, or drinking the heavy, non-aqueous grease that drips from the axle-casings of the stalled railcars. 

Occasionally, a larger presence crosses the open flats between the factory blocks. These are the *dredgers*—long, low-slung, multi-limbed things that may be animals or may be self-maintaining machines that have forgotten their original purpose over the millennia. Their carapaces are made of layered, flaking mica and old boiler-plate. They move across the silt plains in the pitch dark, silent except for the soft, rhythmic *shhh-shhh* of their flat bellies sliding through the dry dust. As they pass through the rare cones of orange streetlight, they do not pause or look up; their eyeless heads swing slowly from side to side, sifting the flour-silt for dropped rivets, copper wire, and heavy mineral nodules, which they swallow into their warm, grinding gizzards.

From the roof of the highest structure—the Central Manifold, a windowless ziggurat of black brick—the whole of the high plateau is reduced to a sparse constellation of tiny, dying embers spread across thirty miles of void. Nothing flickers, because there is no breeze to disturb the air between the eye and the light. A distant turbine hall, five miles to the east, lets out a single, low-frequency hum that vibrates through the soles of the boots rather than the ears, lasts for six seconds, and stops. Then the silence returns, warm and dry and heavy, holding the iron towers and the ancient dust in the same unbroken keep.
