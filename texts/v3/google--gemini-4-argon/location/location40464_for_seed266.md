The Spindle Range does not rise to a summit; it rises to a lip, and beyond that lip, the stone simply drops away for nine vertical miles until it disappears into a dense, amber-colored dry haze that functions as the world’s floor. There is no ocean down there, and there has never been rain up here. The erosion that carved these pillars—and they are carved, smoothed into curving, aerodynamic flutes and immense, hollow arches—is the work of four hundred million years of steady, warm wind carrying fine silica flour from the equatorial flats. 

The rock is a pale, porous travertine-analogue, soft enough to be scored by a thumbnail on its outer skin, yet dense as iron at its core. Under the high, butter-yellow sun, the cliffs glow with a matte, chalky warmth. Shadows here are not black; they are a deep, dusty violet, bounced full of ambient light from the opposing canyon walls thirty miles across the void.

Because there is no soil, there are no roots to split the stone, and because there is no ice, there is no frost-heave to shatter it. The mountains do not crumble in jagged slides; they wear down like bars of dry soap, grain by microscopic grain. The result is a geography of breathtaking, swooping curves—miles-long stone ribbons, vertical troughs polished to a satin finish, and massive overhangs that thin to translucent stone membranes at their edges.

Clinging to these vertical sweeps are the cities of the Sump-Drawers, though from a distance they look less like cities and more like massive, rusted radiator fins bolted directly into the pale stone. 

The architecture is entirely vertical and sparse. A single district might stretch four miles down a cliff face and only two hundred yards across. There are no streets, only the *tracks*—three-foot-wide rails of dark, vanadium-alloy steel that run vertically and diagonally across the rock face, connecting widely spaced terraces of pressed-slag brick and corrugated iron. Between the buildings, there is always a vast, dizzying amount of empty wall. The structures are tall, narrow towers that project outward from the rock like bracket fungi, held aloft by sweeping, riveted trusses that hum faintly in the constant updrafts.

The industry here is atmospheric and geological, and it operates at the pace of a clock’s hour hand. There is no steam—water is a precious, closed-loop industrial solvent locked inside hermetically sealed copper vats—so the machines run on gravity, thermal differentials, and the tension of immense, coiled flywheels. Every half-mile along the cliff, gigantic, slow-turning wind-screws made of stretched canvas and oiled bone-wood rotate in the warm updrafts coming off the deep abyss. They turn at three revolutions per minute, utterly silent save for the deep, rhythmic creak of their main bearings, which sounds less like machinery and more like the shifting of tectonic plates. 

These screws drive the *Sifters*—vast, terraced refineries that draw in the dust-heavy air from the lower depths, electrostatically separating the heavy metals, sulfur, and silicate salts from the wind. The waste dust is poured back over the edge in slow, continuous waterfalls of pale powder that never reach the bottom, dissolving instead into the amber haze a mile below, creating permanent, stationary rainbows of refracted dust-light.

Despite the lack of a single green leaf or blade of grass, the cliffs are teeming with life. The ecology is built entirely on airborne detritus, static electricity, and the thermal vents of the factories. 

Every warm iron casing, every high-tension cable, and every sun-baked ledge below the Sifters is colonized by the *Clinkers*—creatures that bridge the gap between insect, mollusk, and mineral. They grow shells out of the very slag and metallic dust the factories excrete. The most common are the Vent-Limpet and the Copper-Back, slow-moving, plate-armored things the size of dinner plates that graze on the electrostatic films of sulfur and lipid-rich dust that coat the undersides of the iron gantries. They move at the speed of a shadow crossing a courtyard, leaving behind clean, polished trails on the oxidized metal.

In the open air between the sparse towers, the sky belongs to the *Kites*. They are not birds; they have no feathers and cannot flap. They are vast, diamond-shaped membranes of dry, fibrous leather stretched over hollow, pneumatic bones, some with wingspans of forty feet. They never land on the horizontal—they would be helpless there. Instead, they ride the thermal columns rising from the sun-baked walls and the warm exhaust flues of the electro-smelters, hanging motionless in the air for hours at a time. Their undersides are patterned in grey and rust-red, mimicking the corrugated iron of the industrial terraces. To feed, they unfurl long, sticky, fibrous trailing-lines that drift hundreds of feet below them, catching the smaller, winged mineral-moths that swarm around the magnetic coils of the power stations.

The soundscape is profoundly hushed, swallowed by the sheer volume of the empty air. There are no engines coughing, no water rushing, no foliage rustling. The dominant sound is a low, velvet *shhhhh*—the friction of warm wind against millions of square miles of smooth stone. Over this base note, sounds travel with an eerie, dry clarity, but they are sparse: the sharp *ping* of a cooling iron strut contracting in a passing cloud-shadow; the dry, hollow clatter of a herd of Spire-Crabs migrating across a sheet-metal roof; the distant, two-toned chime of a counter-balanced funicular car reaching a station platform two miles up the wall.

When the light begins to fail, it does so from the bottom up. The deep abyss darkens to the color of bruised plum while the upper spires and the highest factory cranes remain blazing in hot, yellow sunlight. As the thermal differential shifts, the great canvas wind-screws slow to a halt, wait for ten minutes in the dead-still air of twilight, and then slowly, ponderously begin to turn in the opposite direction as the cool air from the high plateau begins its long, quiet slide down into the dark. Along the cliff face, thousands of low-wattage arc lamps flicker on—not bright white, but a deep, sodium orange—marking out the sparse, vertical constellations of the sleeping factories against fifty miles of naked stone, while the great leather Kites hook their wing-claws into the suspension cables, folding themselves up like dark laundry to wait for the morning updraft.
