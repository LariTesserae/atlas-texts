There is no light here, not even the memory of a horizon. The air is the temperature of a closed mouth, heavy and saturated, pressing against the skin with the weight of a damp wool blanket. Because there is no vision, the scale of the place is felt through acoustics and pressure: it is a vast, low-lying basin, miles across, where every sound has room to travel before it is swallowed by the wet stone. 

The ground is new. It has never been softened by wind or ground down by centuries of footsteps. It is a vast floor of freshly cracked, hexagonal basalt and jagged, glass-sharp shale, pushed up from some subterranean heave and left to cool in the dark. The edges of the stones are razor-keen, unweathered. Warm water—thick and faintly alkaline—sweats continuously from the pores of this young rock, filling the Geometric troughs between the slabs with ankle-deep pools and sluggish, braided streams that have not yet carved proper channels. Walking here requires a slow, shuffling gait to avoid slicing boots on the fresh obsidian lips of the stone, feeling one's way through a maze of sharp ledges and sudden, warm puddles.

Despite the openness of the basin, the air and the ground are crowded. You cannot swing an arm without brushing something. The space is packed with a dense, thriving ecology that has no use for eyes, mingled with the scattered, sweating hulks of heavy industry. 

Every few hundred yards, an engine rises from the flooded rock. These are not ruins; they are active, massive, and solitary. Cast-iron boilers the size of houses, grease-slicked turbines, and towering, unlit condenser coils sit bolted directly into the raw basalt. There are no walls around them, no roads connecting them, and no workers tending them. They operate on a glacial timescale. A flywheel thirty feet in diameter takes four minutes to complete a single, silent rotation, its iron skin weeping condensed moisture. A piston the thickness of an ancient oak slides forward an inch every hour, displacing a pool of warm water with a soft, hydraulic sigh. Heavy copper cables, thick as pythons, drape from these machines, sagging into the warm mud and running for miles across the sharp rocks to feed power into nothing at all, or into the stone itself. 

Around and upon these machines, and covering the jagged floor, the fauna gathers in suffocating abundance. The darkness is thick with life. The basalt is carpeted in dense, overlapping mats of blind, soft-bodied things—slugs the size of loaves of bread, ribbed amphibians with translucent skin, and slow, heavy crustaceans whose shells have not quite hardened. They pile three and four deep in the warm runoff near the engine exhausts, sliding over one another in a constant, languid churn. The air is equally crowded: heavy, wingless things drop from the tall iron struts of the machinery, suspended on long, wet threads of mucus, swaying gently in the thermal drafts. There is no predation, or if there is, it happens at the speed of rot—one creature slowly dissolving another over the course of days.

Vegetation is an afterthought here. Only occasionally do the fingers brush against something rooted: a solitary, leafless stalk, stiff as wire and coated in warm slime, growing directly out of a crack in an iron manifold, or a sparse patch of pale, fleshy tubers wedged into the sharp scree, feeding on the mineral-rich grease that drips from a gearbox.

The soundscape is a study in dampened resonance. With so many creatures and so much machinery, it should be deafening, yet the basin is mostly quiet. There is the endless, soft *plink* and *slosh* of warm water spilling over sharp rock edges. There is the wet, rhythmic friction of thousands of soft bodies sliding over wet iron and stone—a sound like heavy silk being dragged through mud. Beneath it all is the sub-audible, bone-deep hum of the scattered dynamos, vibrating through the soles of the feet rather than the ears, punctuated once every few hours by the colossal, muffled *thud* of a distant valve seating itself miles away across the dark flat.

And because the rules of this place are frayed, the boundary between the biology, the geology, and the machinery does not hold. 

As you move through the dark, the geography loses its permanence. The distance between two humming turbines might be fifty paces when walking north, and four thousand paces when turning immediately back south. The sharp basalt beneath the water occasionally loses its solidity, becoming warm and pliable like river clay for a few minutes before snapping back into razor-edged stone, trapping the sluggish crustaceans by their legs until the rock forgets to be rock again. 

Near the engines, the breakdown is more acute. The iron sweats not just water, but amniotic fluid. If you place a hand on a warm steel casing to guide yourself, you can feel a slow, irregular pulse beneath the rivets, as if the boiler is digesting the coal it burns. The blind creatures that pile against the foundations do not merely rest on the metal; over the slow weeks, their pale flesh grafts to the cast iron, their circulatory systems routing through the copper cooling tubes, until it is impossible to tell by touch where the wet flank of an amphibian ends and the greased housing of a sump pump begins. 

Gravity, too, has weak spots in the basin. In certain stretches of the marsh, the warm water does not pool in the crevices, but hangs in the air three feet off the ground in vast, trembling lenses, suspended and still. You must wade through chest-high globes of warm, floating water packed with suspended, sleeping eels, while your boots walk across bone-dry, newly fractured stone just beneath. Time pools just like the water. In the lee of a silent transformer, a dropped wrench might take twenty minutes to hit the stone floor, while ten yards away, a sparse cluster of wire-stalks grows, flowers, and rots to black slime in the span of a single breath. 

Nothing hurries. The engines turn at the speed of continental drift. The warm water seeps. The blind things pile together in the dark, trading heat with the iron, waiting in the vast, crowded quiet for a morning that isn't part of the world's design.
