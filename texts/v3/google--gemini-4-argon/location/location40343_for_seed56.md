There is no soil here, and there is no rock. The cavern—a sunken, cathedral-sized pocket three hundred feet beneath the permafrost—is made entirely of root, frond, and calcified cambium. 

To stand at the bottom of the Cyst is to stand on a mattress of braided, living cables as thick as a human torso, their surfaces slick with a bitter, hyper-saline dew that refuses to freeze despite the sub-zero air. The temperature sits well below the freezing point of fresh water, a heavy, motionless cold that burns the lungs. Yet everything is wet. The moisture is a dense, viscous brine rich in natural glycols and suspended metallic nanoparticles, weeping endlessly from the overarching canopy of tuberous vaults.

The space is no larger than an old-growth grove, enclosed entirely by its own relentless growth. Because the environment is so young—an embryonic biome only a few decades into its emergence—nothing has rotted, and nothing has been worn down. There is no humus, no detritus, no soft mulch of dead leaves. Every surface is taut, turgid, and fiercely, freshly alive. The walls are curtains of pleated, translucent kelp-like sheets that grow in slow, hexagonal tessellations, creaking loudly as their cells engorge and vitrify.

That is the first thing that overwhelms the senses: the sound. The Cyst is deafening, though nothing moves quickly. Because the vegetation is growing under extreme cold-pressure and packing itself into a confined subterranean void, the structural tension is immense. A continuous, orchestral groaning echoes through the damp air—the sound of wood-analogues bending, of crystalline sap-ducts snapping into new alignments, of rubbery bladders inflating against one another. Superimposed over this deep, resonant bass is a high, metallic *pinging*, like a thousand tuning forks being struck by fingernails. The plants are piezoelectric; as they slowly crush against each other in the dark, their cellular walls discharge tiny, sharp arcs of acoustic and electrical energy.

Light comes from these stresses, and from the sap. It is a dim, bruised illumination—deep indigos, sickly chartreuses, and the faint Cherenkov-blue of fluid moving through microscopic, vacuum-insulated vascular bundles. You cannot see the ceiling, only the undersides of overlapping, umbrella-like structures that drip thick, silver-tinted water. 

There is not a single nail, wall, pipe, or wire in the cavern, yet the air tastes of high-order mathematics. The flora here is not evolved; it is an autonomous, post-industrial terraforming event in its larval stage, operating on principles of quantum biology and femtoscale material assembly. The plants are growing a subterranean computational matrix—or perhaps a geological stabilizer—out of raw subterranean minerals and heavy water. If you press a gloved hand against one of the broad, freezing leaves, you can feel a faint, rhythmic peristalsis, and beneath the waxy cuticle, the veins organize themselves into transient, shifting circuit-geometries that process the cavern’s thermodynamic state.

In the dense, dripping tangle of the mid-air fronds, the fauna gathers in thick clusters. They are abundant, cold-blooded, and disconcertingly patient. 

Thousands of creatures resembling shell-less, translucent sea-hares cling to the weeping stalks, their bodies filled with a dark, ferrofluid blood that shifts and spikes in response to the plants' magnetic fields. They do not crawl so much as glacially flow, taking hours to traverse the length of a single leaf, grazing on the excess metallic slush that sweats from the flora's pores. 

Between the tighter weaves of the root-floor, colonies of spindle-legged arthropods—things that look like harvestmen spun from spun-glass and pale tendon—hang in dense, motionless spheres, four or five feet across. Every few minutes, a wave of synchronous twitching passes through a sphere, producing a dry, loud clatter like castanets, before they settle back into their slow-motion metabolism. They are trimming the microscopic cilia of the roots, acting as living logic-gates that prune the vegetation’s growth vectors. 

Larger presences occupy the upper gloom: great, mantle-wrapped organisms that look like inverted squid, anchored into the ceiling's vascular nodes by fleshy, fibrous taproots. It is impossible to tell where the plant ends and the animal begins. Their long, pale tentacles hang down into the dim space like willow branches, coated in a thick, conductive mucus. When a droplet of the heavy, silver brine rolls down a tentacle and drops to the floor, it falls with a strange, syrupy slowness, pulled sideways in mid-air by the localized electrostatic anomalies generated by the flora.

Nothing hurries. The air is too cold for speed, the chemistry too complex for haste. A frond takes a month to unfurl, creaking like a galleon's hull the entire time. A grazing slug takes a week to digest a mouthful of superconducting moss. Yet the density of the place is claustrophobic. Every cubic inch of the cavern is spoken for—packed with overlapping leaves, hanging root-curtains, clustered carapace, and dripping mucilage. 

The Cyst is building itself outward, millimeter by millimeter, eating the deep permafrost and replacing the dead geology of the world with a freezing, wet, hyper-intelligent thicket. It is a cathedral with no architect, growing in the dark, loud with the sound of its own birth.
