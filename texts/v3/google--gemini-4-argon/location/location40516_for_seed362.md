There is no earth here, and there has never been rain. As far as the eye can reach across the shallow, continent-wide basin, the ground is a thick, interlaced mat of engineered root-iron and desiccated gourd-plate, grown in a three-dimensional weave over the rusted skeletons of a million low-slung assembly plants. 

The light is extraordinary: a hard, shadowless, actinic glare that pours down from an overcast of suspended sodium-lamps and high-altitude reflective chaff. It bleaches the landscape into a stark palette of bone-white, oxidized silver, and the pale, dusty celadon of the flora. Despite this blinding brightness, the air is distinctly cool—the crisp, dead chill of a cleanroom or a deep cellar, carrying the faint, dry scent of hot copper, old paper, and crushed sage.

Everything in this vast low-country is plant, and everything is factory. Centuries ago, the distinction between the two failed, and the logic of the space broke down with it. The vegetation does not grow *on* the machinery; the vegetation *is* the machinery, bred to metabolize dry mineral dust and raw electrical current in an entirely waterless ecology. 

Great, ribbed manifolds of petrified phloem arch out of the lower tangle like the hulls of capsized ships. Beside them, fields of pale, cabbage-like rosettes—each the size of a furnace—slowly rotate on fibrous ball-bearings, their leaves coated in a fine, photovoltaic frost of zinc. Tendrils of braided copper-vine as thick as anchor chains loop from gantry to gantry, pulsing with a slow, peristaltic rhythm as they pump dry graphite powder and fluidized sulfur from one processing grove to the next. 

The density is overwhelming, yet nothing is lush. There is no sap, no rot, no dampness. When a leaf dies, it does not wilt; it vitrifies, turning into a brittle, translucent pane of silica that eventually snaps off and slides down into the dry, rasping mulch of the under-layers. This mulch is yards deep—a shifting bed of shed gears, porcelain husks, dry seed-bearings, and asbestos-like root hairs that have been worn smooth by centuries of dry friction. 

Because the landscape is so active, it should be deafening, yet it exists in a state of profound, muffled quiet. The air is thin and dry, swallowing noise before it can travel. A hundred yards away, a grove of piston-trees—tall, segmented stalks of chitinous wood that drop and lift heavy counterweights of cast slag—is working at full capacity, yet the only sound that reaches the ear is a faint, papery *shhh-tok, shhh-tok*, like a grandfather clock ticking in another room. The massive, continent-spanning conveyor-vines that haul woven baskets of ore across the middle distance move on greased lignum-vitae rollers that whisper like dry leaves dragged across concrete.

Here and there, the reality of the place frays under the weight of its own ancient automation. The botanical and industrial codes have copied themselves so many times across the miles that the geometry has gone slightly wrong. 

In the shallow valleys—mere dips in the endless, flat canopy of the truss-thickets—there are zones of recursive growth. Here, the plant-machines are stamping out products that have no three-dimensional sense: miles of dry, woody ribbon extruded with the half-formed faces of gauges and dials already barked over; seed-pods that split open to reveal not seeds, but smaller, identical seed-pods arranged in a cold, fractal infinite; lathe-willows that whittle their own branches into perfect, smooth spindles, drop them into the chaff, and immediately grow a new branch from the shavings before the spindle has even hit the floor. 

In some places, the blinding light casts shadows that do not match the objects above them—the shadow of a rectilinear crane-arm falling across a bed of pale moss where only a twisted, briar-like condenser coils in the air. Space itself feels slightly compressed under the dense canopy of bus-bars and dry foliage, so that walking ten paces forward through a gap in the briar-looms sometimes leaves one looking at the exact same weathered junction box, its serial numbers grown out in raised, scarred bark, three times in a row.

Weather here is a mechanical and electrostatic phenomenon. Every few hours, the ambient voltage of the basin builds until the pale hairs on the millions of surrounding gourd-valves stand straight up. The air grows sharper, colder. Then, a "dry-squall" sweeps across the plain: a silent, mile-wide front of ionized dust and silver chaff lifted from the lower mulch by sudden electromagnetic updrafts. The squall moves fast, a blinding white-out of glittering particulates that scours the oxidized buildup from the leaves and polishes the exposed ironwood joints to a dull mirror sheen. As the front passes, millions of tiny, cup-shaped flowers snap shut in unison with a sound like a handful of gravel thrown against glass, trapping the charged dust to digest its static load.

Fauna is almost nonexistent, reduced to a few specialized, solitary anomalies that drift through the tangle like ghosts. Occasionally, a long, ribbon-like creature made of articulated horn and grey felt can be seen undulating along a power-vine, scraping the zinc frost from the leaves with a single, brass-tipped mandible. Farther off, perched atop a weathered cooling-stump, a four-legged thing with the proportions of a crane and a hide like cracked vulcanite stands completely motionless for days, waiting for a short-circuit in the brush below so it can drink the resulting ozone. 

Above it all, the flat, glaring sky never dims. The great, low sea of pale, dry, working brush stretches out to every horizon—an endless, chilly greenhouse of dry ash and bright wire, quietly manufacturing itself into eternity.
