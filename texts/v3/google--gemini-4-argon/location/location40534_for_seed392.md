The ground is not dirt, and it is not bedrock. For several miles in every direction, the low basin is floored entirely in hand-molded, sun-baked clay bricks, laid in a staggered herringbone pattern that refuses to settle. There is no underlying earth here; if you pry up a brick, there is only another brick beneath it, and another beneath that, going down into a warm, damp dark that smells of potter’s slip and river silt. 

Because the construction is young—or rather, because time here does not wear things down—every brick retains the sharp, ninety-degree crispness of its wooden mold. You can still see the fresh, swirling thumbprints of the makers pressed into the terracotta, and the crisp ridges where excess clay was scraped away with a bone flat. Nothing crumbles. Nothing rounds off. Yet the whole basin is wet, sweating from within. Lukewarm water wells up continuously through the unmortared seams between the bricks, pooling in the thumbprints, slicking the flat expanses in a sheet of glass-clear water a quarter-inch deep, and trickling into wide, step-sided cisterns that drop into the floor at sparse, measured intervals.

Above this vast, handmade patio, the sky is a glaring, uniform white—not overcast, just an omnidirectional, shadowless brightness that bakes the wet terracotta to the temperature of skin. The heat is heavy, thick as wet wool, and completely motionless.

In a normal place, the lushness would choke the brickwork, splitting the seams and burying the floor in humus. Here, the vegetation obeys the strange, sparse geometry of the basin. Every twenty paces or so, a massive, braided rope of hemp and reed drops straight down from the blinding white sky, anchored into nothing overhead, its frayed lower end wedged tightly into a gap in the bricks. Up these vertical ropes climb colossal, wax-leaved vines—monstera and elephant-ear and thick, weeping figs—their roots drinking directly from the wet clay floor without need of soil. They form isolated, towering columns of fiercely green foliage, spaced out across the glaring red-orange expanse like pillars in a hypostyle hall that has lost its roof. Between these towering green columns, the brick flats are entirely bare, save for geometric carpets of emerald-green liverwort that grow strictly within the rectangular boundaries of individual bricks, never crossing a seam, turning the floor into a scattered patchwork of blazing terracotta and velvet green.

There is no wind to rustle the leaves. The great braided ropes do not sway. The heat and the glare hold everything in a high-contrast, feverish stillness. 

Yet the place is thick with animals. Because there is no decay and no true predation, they exist in a state of crowded, languid repetition. The warm, shallow water sheeting over the bricks is dense with thumb-sized, turquoise-shelled snails. As they glide, their shells should scrape against the grit of the clay, and when they bump into one another, they should click—but the air absorbs the sound before it can travel an inch. They move in total silence. 

On the low, square-edged brick plinths that rise here and there like empty altars, hundreds of heavy-bodied, emerald-and-copper lizards lie stacked three deep, their flanks pulsing rapidly in the heat. Pelicans and long-legged white ibises stand in the stepped cisterns or pace slowly between the vine-columns. When an ibis steps from the wet brick into a cistern, the water parts in sharp, glassy ripples, but the splash is missing; there is only a faint, dry *tick*, like a fingernail tapping paper. Occasionally, a flock of small, scarlet birds bursts from one column of foliage and flies across the open glare to another. Their wings beat frantically, but the sound is muted to a soft, distant rustle of linen, and halfway across the gap, the flock sometimes stutters—occupying the exact same patch of air twice for a fraction of a second—before completing the flight.

Where the basin rises slightly toward its perimeter, the brickwork steps upward in long, low terraces held back by dry-stone walls of knapped black flint. Every nodule of flint has been freshly struck; the edges are razor-sharp, gleaming with a wet, glassy luster in the white light, completely free of lichen or dust. Stick-built weirs of peeled willow wands are wedged into the terrace channels, guiding the seeping water down from level to level. None of the willow sticks have rotted; their peeled wood is still pale and slick with sap, as if stripped five minutes ago by a bronze knife.

The weirdness of the place settles heaviest in the open stretches between the vine-towers. Because the light is so bright and cast from everywhere at once, nothing has a shadow beneath it—not the ibises, not the flint walls, not the towering columns of leaves. Instead, the shadows have been built separately. A few yards away from an empty brick plinth, a flat, jagged patch of pitch-blackness lies woven directly into the reed matting that covers a section of the floor. It is the exact shape of a crouched wading bird, cool to the touch, though no bird stands near it. 

Nothing grows taller; nothing dies back. A drop of water swells on the glossy tip of a fig leaf, hangs fat and blindingly bright, falls onto the nose of a sleeping lizard, and splashes silently—and the lizard does not blink, and a new drop is already full-formed on the leaf tip, and the wet brick floor stretches out for miles under the white glare, pristine, steaming, and quiet.
