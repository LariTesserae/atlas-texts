Three miles beneath the crust, the rock stops being solid and becomes a thicket. 

There is no ceiling to see, because there is no open sky to look up through, nor is there a true floor. Instead, there is an endless, three-dimensional lattice of cooled mineral extrusions—black tourmaline, pale orthoclase, and massive, hexagonal columns of basalt that cooled so rapidly in some forgotten epoch that they cracked into sharp, ringing splines. This is the Spicule Forest, though nothing here has ever known a seed or a leaf. It is a continent-sized sponge of dry, jagged stone, where the struts and needles of rock are spaced anywhere from a few inches to twenty yards apart, intersecting at rigid, crystal-governed angles. 

The air is warm—roughly the temperature of human blood—and entirely motionless. Because there is no temperature gradient between the upper reaches and the lower depths, and no moisture to condense or evaporate, there is no wind. The atmosphere is heavy, dry, and smells faintly of ozone, flint, and old dust. It is a sealed equilibrium. Drop a flake of mica from a ledge, and it will fall straight down through the dark until it catches on a mineral needle ten yards or ten miles below, and there it will sit for ten thousand years.

It is almost entirely dark, yet the darkness is never quite absolute. Because the rock is young and under immense, static tectonic load, the crystals suffer from constant, microscopic internal stress. Every few minutes, somewhere in the middle distance, a spire of quartz or feldspar gives way to a fractional slip, producing a brief, silent flash of piezoelectric light—a pale, bluish-amber ghost that illuminates a hundred yards of jagged interlocking stone before vanishing. Closer at hand, the darkness is pricked by the dull, ember-orange points of bioluminescent lithotrophic bacteria that grow in dry, powdery crusts along the sharper edges of the stone, feeding directly on the mineral ions.

In this warm, dry, pitch-black lattice, life is thick, slow, and dry-shelled.

There is no water here, so the abundant fauna have built themselves around the conservation of grease, wax, and dry mineral salts. You do not hear them before you feel them, or see their silhouettes against a piezoelectric flash. Every surface—the undersides of obsidian blades, the vertical grooves of basalt columns, the tight intersections where five or six stone needles cross—is occupied. 

There are the *mantles*: creatures the size of dinner plates that resemble flattened, leathery chitons, their backs plated in overlapping scales of calcium and iron sulfide they have scraped from the rock and secreted through their skin. They move maybe four inches a day, grazing on the glowing bacterial crusts with rasping, metallic radulae. The sound of a thousand mantles feeding in the dark is the primary voice of this world: a dry, faint, intermittent *tick-tick-scritch*, like fingernail parings dropped onto a tin plate.

Between the spires hang the *weavers*, though they do not spin silk. They extrude thin, brittle filaments of spun glass and dried mucus, creating vast, rigid, three-dimensional harps between the stone needles. The weavers themselves are long, multi-jointed things, pale and translucent as paraffin wax, with limbs that span six feet but bodies no thicker than a thumb. They sit motionless at the hubs of their glassy webs for months at a time, burning almost no energy, waiting for a blind, warm-bodied glider—leathery, bat-like things that navigate by electrostatic fields rather than sound—to misjudge a gap in the dark and shatter a glass thread.

When a thread breaks, the sound is a single, pure, high *ping* that travels for half a mile through the still air before being swallowed by the density of the stone thicket.

Because the world does not erode—no rain to wash things away, no wind to sand down the edges, no rot that isn't immediately licked clean by dry, chitinous scavengers—nothing ever truly disappears. The landscape is an archive of every slight adjustment made within it.

And someone has been adjusting it.

Scattered throughout the middle layers of the lattice are the works of the builders. Who or what they are—whether a caste of the fauna or something upright and long-fingered that crawls through the narrower gaps—is hard to say in the dark, for they are as slow and quiet as everything else here. Their technology is entirely lithic and acoustic. Having no wood, no water for clay, and no fire (which would consume the stagnant oxygen and suffocate the local pockets of the lattice), they work with what the world provides: tension, cleavage, and bone.

Every few miles, the natural chaos of intersecting crystal needles has been cleared out, the spires neatly snapped off at their cleavage planes using heavy hammers of meteoric iron or dense basalt. The resulting clearings are spherical, maybe fifty feet across. Within these spheres, the builders have wedged the broken stone needles together without mortar, using sheer friction and the weight of the surrounding rock to create dry-stone cages, cantilevered ledges, and hollow cairns. 

They bind smaller structures together using the cured, waxy tendons of the giant weavers and the flat, iron-hard shells of dead mantles. You will find bridges made of three long selenite crystals lashed together with dried gut, spanning a gap between two massive basalt columns. The edges of the crystals are knapped like flint to give purchase to bare feet or claws.

More common than the shelters are the *lithophones*. In a world without light, geography is mapped by resonance. Every mile or so, suspended in a cradle of braided tendon or wedged precisely between two jaws of granite, hangs a flake of phonolite or obsidian, chipped to a specific thickness and tension. Beside it hangs a striker made of heavy bone. When struck, these stones do not ring loudly—loudness would be vulgar and dangerous in this dense ecology—instead, they produce a deep, thrumming hum that travels through the rock itself rather than the air. You feel the note in your teeth and through the soles of your feet: a low *B-flat* that means *safe passage downward*, or a sour, split-tone chord that means *dead-end chasm*.

Around these cairns and tuning-stones, the detritus of primitive craft lies in neat, undisturbed piles that could be five minutes or five millennia old. Flakes of black glass razor-sharp as the day they were struck; hollowed-out carapaces filled with rendering-fat rubbed onto the skin to prevent moisture loss; small, hand-carved toggles of gypsum whose purpose is entirely tactile—strange, asymmetrical knots of stone meant to be held in the palm and read with the thumbs in the dark.

Nothing moves quickly. A pale, six-limbed crawler edges down a basalt needle, its waxen joints creaking once in the silence. Sixty yards away, a stress-fracture in a tourmaline pillar flashes violet for a tenth of a second, revealing a dense, terrifyingly intricate jungle of black needles, glass webs, and sleeping, plated bodies stretching out in all directions—up, down, left, right—and in the center of it, a tiny, unmortared stone hut wedged into the crook of a giant crystal, its doorway dark and still. Then the flash dies, the afterimage fades, and there is only the warm, dry dark, and the faint *tick, tick* of iron teeth on stone.
