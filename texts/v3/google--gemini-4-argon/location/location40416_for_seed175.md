The place is a sunken ellipse of yellow soapstone, no wider across than a village commons, cupped near the summit of a worn, windless tableland. The stone is so old that its edges have forgotten sharpness; the rim of the basin rolls inward like the lip of a thick earthenware bowl, smoothed by ages of warm, mineral-heavy mist that rises from the plateau's flanks and settles here to condense. 

Because the air is both high and surprisingly warm—heated by some deep, drowsy geothermal temper in the mountain's core—the basin acts as a trap for moisture. It doesn’t rain here so much as the air continuously gives up its water. The yellow stone is perpetually slick, darkened to the color of bruised amber in its shallow troughs, where water gathers in flat, skin-thin sheets before sliding without a sound toward the center of the depression.

For all this moisture, the space is remarkably open. There is no tangle of undergrowth, no choking mat of roots. Instead, the lushness is concentrated into widely spaced, colossal individuals. Twelve or fifteen pillars of dark green, rubbery growth rise from the bare stone at generous intervals, arranged with the accidental geometry of pillars in a flooded crypt. Each is a colony of fused, succulent stalks as thick as a cistern, their surfaces grooved to channel the condensation downward. At the base of each pillar, a wide, pleated skirt of heavy leaves spreads out across the wet rock, drinking from the creeping film of water. Between these massive, solitary plants, the yellow stone lies completely bare for yards at a time, clean of soil, washed shiny by the slow seep. 

The light is clear, even, and shadowless, filtered through a high, bright fleece of cloud that never quite breaks and never quite thickens into storm. It turns the wet soapstone floor into a dull mirror, reflecting the pale sky and the dark green columns so that the boundary between the rock and the air feels negotiable.

Nothing moves with any urgency. There is no wind at this height, only a faint, warm draft that breathes upward from the basin's center once every few minutes, barely strong enough to stir the white, hair-like filaments that hang from the upper crowns of the plant-columns. There are no birds, no insects skating on the water-films, no amphibians tucked into the damp folds of the leaves. The ecology here is entirely botanical and mineral, locked in a silent, slow-motion exchange. Without animals to pollinate them, the great green columns reproduce by shedding heavy, bowling-ball-sized nodes of tightly packed leaves once every few years. A node drops with a dull, wet thud, rolls a few yards down the gentle grade of the soapstone until it finds a shallow pocket of water, and spends the next decade slowly uncurling and drinking the dissolved stone.

In the center of the basin, where the water collects in a knee-deep pool the size of a threshing floor, lies the only sign that hands have ever touched this place. There are no walls, no huts, no cairns—nothing built. Instead, someone has worked the living floor of the basin itself. Shallow, curving channels, no deeper than the width of two fingers, have been gouged into the soft yellow soapstone with harder river-cobbles or bone chisels. These carved veins redirect the natural seep away from some of the younger plant-nodes and gather it toward others, corralling the water into slow, deliberate spirals before it reaches the central pool. 

Beside the pool, half-submerged in the warm, clear water, three heavy wooden levers made of hardened, dead plant-stalks have been wedged beneath a massive, half-sunken boulder of red jasper—an erratic dropped here by some forgotten glacier eons ago. The wood of the levers is waterlogged and furred with a pale, aquatic moss, and the braided vine ropes that lash them together are stiff with mineral crust. Whoever wedged them there was trying to pry the red boulder out of its socket in the soapstone, perhaps to alter the drainage of the pool, or perhaps simply for the stone itself. Two hand-axes of chipped black flint lie in an inch of water nearby, their wooden hafts rotted completely away, leaving only a dark stain on the yellow rock that the creeping water has not yet managed to wash clean. 

The water in the carved channels moves without a ripple, too thin and slow to gurgle. The only sound in the entire bowl is the occasional, heavy *plop* of a fat drop of water falling from the pleated skirt of a plant-pillar into the central pool, sending a single ring outward to die against the red jasper stone. Then the surface goes flat again, holding the white sky in the warm, empty quiet.
