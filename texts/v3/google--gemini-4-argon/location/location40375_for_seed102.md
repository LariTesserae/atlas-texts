It is a pocket of dry, feverish warmth sealed eight hundred feet below a dead basalt plain—a single, jagged blister in the rock no larger than a village commons. There is no sky here, only a low, vaulted ceiling of recently fractured obsidian and gabbro, its ledges still razor-sharp and unweathered, holding the exact, violent geometry of the tectonic snap that opened this void a few centuries ago. Where the stone should be damp with cave-sweat, it is bone-dry. The air tastes of baked mica, static electricity, and old copper. It sits at a permanent, unshifting hundred and two degrees Fahrenheit. 

Because there is no water to soften the stone or rot the fallen matter, nothing here decays, and nothing erodes. Yet the chamber is lush. 

The flora does not drink; it feeds on heat and the slow, heavy drafts of sulfur-rich gas that bleed through hairline fractures in the floor. From the sharp talus slopes rise towering, translucent ferns made of spun silica and brittle chitin, their fronds wide and overlapping like layers of stained glass. Thick, velvet-skinned tubers—the size of hogsheads and dry as kiln-fired clay—anchor themselves to the sheer walls with roots of braided copper wire, drawing pure mineral up from the rock. Between them hang dense curtains of "ash-kelp," gray, ribbon-like vines that rustle with the sound of dry parchment even when the air is completely still. The vegetation is paradoxically both lush and sparse: individual stalks and trunks stand yards apart in the clean gravel, giving the chamber a wide, airy floor plan, yet their canopies flare out so broadly in the dimness that they weave a continuous, multi-tiered jungle overhead.

The light comes entirely from the plants' own slow, chemical friction. The undersides of the silica fronds glow with a bruised, sullen violet and a deep, ember-orange—just enough to cast six overlapping shadows behind every stone and turn the sharp obsidian walls into black mirrors. The illumination never brightens and never fades. There is no day, no season, no weather. The chamber exists in a locked thermal and chemical equilibrium.

And in this static, twilight furnace, the noise is deafening.

Because the world is made of dry crystal, hollow stone, and stretched chitin, the whole pocket acts as a sympathetic resonator. The fauna—abundant, dry-shelled creatures that never need to drink—are the musicians. Thousands of six-legged, porcelain-white beetles the size of human hands cling to the copper-rooted tubers, rasping their serrated hind legs against the hollow plant-skins to shed excess body heat. The sound is a relentless, metallic grinding, like a thousand scythes being sharpened on dry whetstones. High in the glass-fern canopy, blind, ribbon-bodied reptiles with scales of overlapping mica drop from branch to branch; every landing sends a sharp, musical *chink-shatter-ring* echoing through the chamber, a cascade of high-pitched marimba notes that takes minutes to die down. Beneath the gravel floor, colonies of burrowing, thumb-sized locusts vibrate their thoraxes against the bedrock to loosen the mineral dust they eat, producing a low, continuous bass hum that you feel in the fillings of your teeth and the hollow of your sternum. 

The clamor never rises to a crescendo and never drops to a hush. It is a solid wall of dry, rattling, ringing, stridulating constancy.

Scattered across the floor of this ringing hollow are the quiet works of whoever—or whatever—found this place before the upper fissures sealed. Because the density of the chamber's floor is low, you can see these structures from across the room, sitting in the clear spaces between the great tubers like museum pieces in a dark hall. 

There are five low domes built of dry-laid basalt shards, each no taller than a kneeling man. Because the builders used the young, freshly fractured rock of the cave floor, the walls of these huts are made of interlocking knives; you could not lean against them without drawing blood. Inside the domes, the floors are swept clear of gravel and lined with woven mats of tough, gray ash-kelp. There is no hearth—no one needed fire in this warmth—and no pots, for there was nothing to boil or store. 

Instead, the technology here is acoustic and mineral. Stretched between the silica-trunks are taut harps made of braided copper root and anchored with heavy counterweights of raw magnetite. When the mica-scaled lizards brush past them, the strings let out long, thrumming drones that harmonize with the locust-bass below. Around the stone domes, someone has arranged low cairns of tuned flint blades, balanced precariously edge-to-edge; they vibrate sympathetically with the beetle-grind, humming like tuning forks. On the flattest faces of the obsidian walls, primitive chisels of harder quartz have been used to peck out shallow, spiral grooves filled with crushed, glowing fern-spores—maps or calendars of a world that does not move, glowing with a faint, dusty purple light.

A stone mallet, bound to a handle of petrified reed with strips of dry lizard-hide, lies beside a half-finished flint cairn. It has lain there for ten days or ten thousand years. No dust settles on it, because there is no wind to blow the dust; no rust forms on the copper bindings, because there is no moisture in the air; no vines overgrow it, because the silica-ferns have already reached the exact limit of the thermal budget the floor provides. A white beetle crawls over the mallet's head, pauses to grind its legs—*shhhk-shhhk-shhhk*—and moves on, leaving the tool exactly as it was, in the loud, warm dark.
