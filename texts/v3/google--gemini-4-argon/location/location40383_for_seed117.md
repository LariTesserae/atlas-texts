It is a sunken basin, no wider than a town square, dropped into the coastal shelf like a collapsed cellar. The walls are not cliffs, which would suggest the sharp fracture of recent violence, but slump-shouldered embankments of dark, tallow-colored clay and rotten shale, worn down by centuries of seeping groundwater until they resemble the melted sides of an old candle. There is no sky here in the usual sense—only a low ceiling of maritime fog that catches on the rim of the depression and hangs there, heavy and grey as wet wool, keeping the light at a permanent, shadowless four o'clock in the afternoon.

Everything in the basin is crowded, slick, and softly weeping. There isn't a blade of grass or a tangle of kelp in the entire hollow; the chemistry of the seep-water is too heavy with dissolved iron and cold brine to permit anything green. Instead, the space is choked with a dense labyrinth of *tufa* and *travertine*—spongy, porous formations of precipitated stone that have grown up over the centuries around the basin’s hundreds of trickling vents. These mineral chimneys and blistered mounds stand shoulder to shoulder, leaving only narrow, ankle-deep sluices of tea-colored water between them. To walk through the basin is to turn sideways and edge between sweating flanks of stone that feel slightly soapy to the touch, coated in a pale, gelatinous bacterial biofilm that smells faintly of iodine and cold lard.

Threaded through this mineral congestion are the remains of an old pumping station, though the geology has long since taken custody of the iron. Cast-iron standpipes, thick as a man’s thigh and stamped with illegible foundry marks, rise out of the muck at drunken angles, their flanged joints bearded in orange ochre and grey mineral curtains. Half-buried in the silt at the center of the hollow sits a single donkey-engine, its flywheel seized by a crust of salt-scale, its boiler plates thinned to lace by the wet salt air. Yet the system is not entirely dead. Somewhere beneath the shale, an artesian head or a tidal piston still exerts pressure through the surviving lines. Every three or four minutes, a brass pressure-relief valve on a leaning manifold lifts with a soft, greasy *clack*, drooling a quart of warm, rust-red water down its housing before settling back into its seat with a long, bronchial wheeze. Copper cables, their gutta-percha insulation split and peeling like dead skin, loop from rusted stanchions into the pools, occasionally letting go a tiny, rhythmic *tic-tic-tic* of stray current into the brine—a phantom electrolysis that keeps the water around them fizzing with microscopic hydrogen bubbles.

There is no wind down here, only the slow, cool draft of air being pulled downward by the cold water, yet the basin is intensely busy. Because there are no plants to anchor an ordinary food web, the fauna feed on the mineral bacterial mats, on the chemical soup around the live wires, and on each other. 

Every submerged ledge and rusted bolt-head is shingled with flat, articulated mollusks the size of pocket watches, their shells dull and grey as boiler scale. They do not stay still; they creep over the ironwork in overlapping waves, grazing the ochre slime with an audible, dry rasping sound, like thousands of tiny fingernails scratching at slate. In the narrow water-channels between the tufa mounds, pale, blind amphipods the length of a thumb scull backward in frantic, jerky schools, stirring the brown sediment into clouds. 

The predators are stranger, adapted to the tight, vertical geometry of the pipes and stone chimneys. Perched along the upper rims of the mineral vents are things that look at first like clumps of black grease or bundles of wet leather straps. These are colonial worms—dozens of individuals braided together into a single muscular trunk the thickness of a wrist. When the stray electrical current ticks in the water below, the bundles unbraid their upper ends, fanning out hundreds of hair-thin, translucent feeding filaments into the damp air and the surface film of the pools, fishing for the panicked amphipods. When a filament touches prey, the whole braided column contracts with a wet *slap*, hauling the catch up into the central knot.

Larger still are the "oil-crabs" that haunt the hollow beneath the donkey-engine’s firebox. They have soft, leathery carapaces that allow them to squeeze through the four-inch gaps between the tufa pillars, and long, needle-tipped legs that make no splash when they step into the sluices. They are scavengers of rust and sulfur-slime, their backs coated in the same calcareous drip that covers the pipes, so that when one stops moving beside a valve housing, it simply becomes another lump of corroded plumbing.

The sound of the place is small, boxed-in, and wet, magnified by the close walls of clay and stone. There is no roar of surf or sigh of trees—only the tireless, busy percolation of a thousand tiny leaks. Water drips from the iron flanges in three-four time; it trickles through the porous stone with a sound like sand running through a glass; it gurgles in the choked intake pipes when the tide shifts a mile away. Over this constant hydraulic murmur comes the crisp, papery *shuck-shuck-shuck* of the grazing shells, the sudden wet snap of a worm-bundle recoiling, and, every few minutes, the heavy, sighing *clack-hiss* of the brass relief valve letting off the pressure of the dark earth below. 

The air stays at a steady, clammy forty-five degrees, smelling of wet iron, old oil, and the sharp, metallic tang of ozone where the drowned cables bleed their last millivolts into the mud. Nothing enters from the flat, empty moorland above; nothing leaves. The basin simply digests its own iron, grows its own stone, and turns its small, cold wheel of life in the grey dark.
