The water here is the temperature of a held breath, and it sits in the throat of a mountain ten thousand feet above the plains. 

There is no mountain left to see—only the masonry that replaced it, or filled its hollow, so long ago that the distinction between quarry and architecture has dissolved. The entire space is no larger than an abbey church, though it is oriented vertically: a drowned well-court of cyclopean basalt and soapstone blocks, stacked without mortar, rising toward a ceiling that is nearly closed. Far at the top, where the corbeled stone vaults pinch together like pursed lips, a single ragged aperture the size of a cartwheel lets in the thin, freezing air of the high altitude. 

Because the water within the cistern is warm—heated by some deep, dying geothermal suture in the peak—a perpetual, silent fog coils off the small patch of surface at the very top, vanishing instantly into the thin mountain night. Below that small meniscus, everything is submerged.

No light comes through the top except during the two hours of high noon, when a single, pale, bluish shaft of mountain sunlight spears down through the aperture, illuminating a column of drifting mineral silt before dying thirty feet down. Otherwise, the darkness is nearly absolute, broken only by the rare, stray ember-glow of a deep thermal vent hidden behind the lowest tiers of stone, or the faint, greasy luminescence left behind when one of the blind crabs scrapes its shell against the masonry.

The architecture is dense, crowded, and ancient beyond measure. It was built by hands using only stone mauls, bronze wedges, and patient abrasion, yet it fills the entire volume in a three-dimensional labyrinth of galleries, lintels, stepped buttresses, and overlapping bridges. There is no natural rock visible; every surface was once dressed and plumbed. Now, after ten millennia of slow, warm, mineral-heavy submergence, the stone has softened. Edges that were once ninety-degree angles have been worn down to the texture of sucked lozenges. Relief carvings—perhaps they were once livestock, or tall figures carrying jars—have been abraded by the slow thermal convection currents into vague, bulging undulations, like bones under a thick blanket. The blocks have settled into their final, irrevocable positions, locked together by their own immense weight and sealed with a rind of dark, calcareous silt that falls continuously, grain by grain, from the upper tiers.

There is no green here. No algae slicks the stone; no pale root probes through the chinks in the wall. The ecosystem has no need of the sun. Instead, the masonry is furred with a dense, velvet-like mat of chemosynthetic bacteria—gray, ochre, and dead-white—that feeds on the dissolved sulfurous salts weeping from the mortarless joints where the hot springs enter the cistern.

Because of this bacterial pasture, the dark water is thick with life. Every horizontal surface, every eroded windowsill and submerged staircase, is crowded with slow, heavy-shelled fauna. There are snails the size of fists with shells coiled like rams' horns, so heavily calcified they look like nodules of the wall itself, advancing an inch an hour to rasp the white bacterial felt from the basalt. Translucent, eyeless shrimp hang in dense, motionless clouds under the arches, their long antennae intersecting and tangling in the dark like fine hair. 

In the vertical shafts between the cross-bridges, blind eels as thick as a man’s forearm hang suspended in the warm water, their heads pointed down toward the rising thermal drafts. They do not swim so much as drift, adjusting their buoyancy with minute ripples of their dorsal fins, waiting for a crab to lose its purchase on the slick, rounded stone above. Even the crabs are sluggish, their movements viscous and deliberate, their pale carapaces crowded with small, tube-building worms that have cemented themselves to their backs. Life here does not dart or flee; it creeps, settles, and accumulates. To move a hand through the water is to brush against three or four soft or chitinous bodies that barely bother to recoil.

The silence of the cistern is profound, heavy with the pressure of the water and the thickness of the walls. There are no waves, no trickling brooks, no wind. From the outside world, nothing penetrates the yard-thick stone. Within the water, sound travels fast and dies instantly against the crowded, baffling surfaces of the drowned balconies. There is only the occasional, dry *tick* of a snail’s shell bumping against another as they pass on a narrow ledge; the soft, gritty whisper of a tiny avalanche of silt sliding off a rounded lintel; and, once every few hours, the deep, sub-audible *thrum* of the mountain’s plumbing far below—a bubble of warm gas shifting in the dark, rising through the tiers of stone, expanding as it climbs, until it breaks with a single, muffled *plop* at the small, steaming patch of air at the very top of the world.
