The altitude should mean thin, freezing air, a stark horizon of shattered slate and dry lichen. Instead, the air at twenty-two thousand feet is thick enough to choke on, hot as the inside of a kiln, and saturated to the point of weeping. Looking out from the central plazas of the city, there is no ground below—only a drop into a milk-white abyss of cloud that never rises and never parts. The city clings to a sprawling, vertical archipelago of obsidian and greywacke needles that jut miles into the upper atmosphere, bound together not by mortar or steel, but by the architecture itself.

It is an urban expanse with no history of tools. There is no smelted metal here, no glass, no wheel-ruts, no carved inscriptions, no scaffolding. Yet the city covers the entire mountain range, a dense, towering metropolis of housing blocks, stepped avenues, viaducts, and massive, hollow basilicas. The buildings are formed from the mountain’s own weeping stone and the calcified, interlocking roots of the *banyan-ash*—a lush, broad-leafed flora that thrives in the boiling altitude. It is impossible to tell if the city was grown by the forest to mimic a civilization, or if the geometry of the rock simply had no other way to crystalize except into colonnades, lintels, and ten-story tenement blocks. Every corner is rounded, softened by millennia of hot mist, existing in a state of settled, ancient equilibrium. The doorways are proportioned for bodies that have never existed; the stairs lead up through damp, vaulted interiors to balconies that overlook fifty-mile drops, worn smooth at the center of each step as if by ten million bare feet, though nothing has ever walked them.

The heat comes from the rock itself, or perhaps from the sheer, broken physics of the place. Water does not fall from the sky here; it condenses violently and continuously out of the hot air against every surface. Every wall, every monumental staircase, every broad, waxy leaf of the hanging gardens is slicked with a warm, running film of moisture. It collects in the grooved gutters of the stone avenues, pours through the unglazed windows of the high towers, and spills over the edges of the plazas in thousands of parallel waterfalls that vanish into the cloud-floor miles below. 

The vegetation is thick, heavy, and immaculate. Enormous ferns with fronds the size of sails crowd the courtyards; velvet-skinned mosses pack the joints between the masonry blocks, swelling like upholstery; black-stemmed vines as thick as anchor cables bridge the chasms between the stone spires, woven so tightly they form suspension avenues five lanes wide. Yet, despite this suffocating lushness, there is no decay. A leaf grows until it touches another leaf, and then it simply stays. There are no brown edges, no fallen mulch, no rotting timber. There are no insects to chew the foliage, no birds in the canopies, no amphibians in the steaming cisterns. The water in the tiered street-basins is crystal clear, warm as blood, and entirely sterile.

Because there is no wind and no animal life, the city should be silent. Instead, the sound is a physical wall that flattens thought. 

It is a continuous, deafening roar—a polyphonic, harmonic screaming that originates from the architecture itself. The extreme heat and the endless, cascading run-off of water moving through millions of hollow stone columns, subterranean cisterns, and porous root-viaducts turn the entire mountain-city into a single, colossal hydraulic organ. Every room is a resonance chamber; every doorway is a reed. The sound does not rise or fall. It does not echo, because an echo requires a beginning and an end. It is a solid, unvarying chord made of boiling acoustics, rushing water, and the high, tectonic vibration of the rock needles themselves. If a boulder were to fall, its crash would be swallowed instantly by the ambient thunder of the streets. 

And beneath the heat, the wet, and the noise, the rules of space have quietly come undone.

The light is the normal, flat, pale daylight of a slightly overcast afternoon—roughly four o’clock—and it never changes. There is no sun visible in the pale dome above, yet shadows fall sharply to the east. More unsettling is how the city occupies its own volume. The density of the masonry and the jungle is too high for the space the mountain peaks actually provide. 

If you look down a narrow, stepped alley choked with weeping fig-vines, you can see the open air of the western precipice at the end of it, perhaps a hundred yards away. Yet the buildings lining that same alley have dozens of windows opening into courtyards that are clearly miles deep, bathed in the same flat afternoon light. An aqueduct pours gallons of steaming water from a tower on a lower peak *upward* at a gentle, thirty-degree angle into a reservoir on a higher spire, the water flowing smoothly along an open stone trough in defiance of the cliff's verticality. 

In the grandest district, where five massive stone needles converge, the buildings are stacked in a way that violates depth. A mossy terrace in the foreground sits visually behind the spire it is built against; you can see the wet, glistening brickwork of the far tower *through* the solid, opaque stone of the nearer archway, not as if the stone were transparent, but as if both occupy the front of the eye at the exact same moment. Water drips from a gutter three hundred feet overhead and splashes onto a paving stone that is somehow positioned directly above the gutter it just fell from, creating a closed, six-foot loop of falling rain that has been splashing against itself forever.

Nothing moves here except the water, and even the water’s movement is a kind of stasis—an endless, fixed replacement of the exact same droplets along the exact same grooves. The leaves do not tremble in the deafening vibration of the air. The shadows do not creep a millimeter across the wet, warm flagstones. The city simply sits at the top of the sky, boiling softly in the afternoon light, roaring at the top of its lungs to an audience of nothing.
