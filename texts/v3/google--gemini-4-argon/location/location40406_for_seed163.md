The ground is not dirt, and it is not stone; it is fifty vertical feet of compressed, living *Astelia* and cushion-sedge, locked so tightly together that you can strike it with a pickaxe and the tool will bounce back with a dull, rubbery thud. 

From a distance, the valley looks like a vast, tilted bowl of hammered chartreuse and pale copper, open to a sky so thin and bright it hurts the eyes to look past the horizon. There are no trees, no shrubs, no distinct landmarks to break the view—only the massive, swollen geometry of the bog-mat itself, which rises in great, sluggish domes half a mile wide and drops off into sharp, unweathered fault-scarps.

Because the rock beneath this basin is newly uplifted—thrust toward the sun in raw, jagged steps of dark basalt that have not yet had time to crumble into soil or silt—the plants have had to build the topography themselves. Over centuries of cool, blindingly bright days, the cushion plants have grown over the knife-edges of the young stone, sealing the fractures, trapping the snowmelt, and holding the water perched high in the air. 

The result is a landscape that is entirely solid and entirely saturated. Every square inch of the surface is a microscopic forest of stiff, waxy rosettes, packed as densely as bristles on a wire brush, glistening with a permanent skin of cold water. The light here is ferocious. At this altitude, the sun doesn't warm the air—which remains crisp and smells faintly of wet iron and crushed celery—but it hits the billions of water droplets suspended in the plant-tips and turns the entire valley floor into a blinding sheet of white-gold glare. You cannot walk here without dark glass over your eyes. 

And you cannot hear yourself speak.

The sound is the first thing that claims you, long before your boots sink that first quarter-inch into the hissing sponge of the ground. It is an unrelenting, bowel-shaking roar that seems to come from the sky, the earth, and the inside of your own skull all at once. 

Miles away, at the upper lip of the basin where the young basalt steps down from the ice-fields, an immense sheet of meltwater pours into the valley. It does not flow in rivers—the rock is too new to have carved channels, and the vegetation is too thick to allow erosion. Instead, the water travels *through* and *under* the fifty-foot mat of living peat and root-fiber, pressurized by the weight of the mountain above. Where the underlying rock drops off in one of its jagged, tectonic steps, the plant-mat folds over the drop like a thick green carpet, and the subterranean water bursts out through the vertical wall of roots in thousands of high-pressure jets, falling fifty or sixty feet into the lower tier of the bog. 

These are not clear waterfalls; they are roaring curtains of white foam that erupt directly out of green, living walls, deafening and constant. The valley has three of these massive steps, each a couple of miles long, and the combined thunder of the water tearing through the fibrous mesh of the earth fills the open bowl of the valley like the sound of a foundry. 

Adding to the din are the engines.

They are few—perhaps half a dozen across the entire twelve-mile sweep of the basin—looking from afar like small, rusted beetles pinned to the vast, glaring curves of the moss-domes. They are heavy, autonomous pump-stations, anchored by thick steel cables driven deep through the vegetation into the basalt below. Each station is a boxy housing of corrugated iron, bleached pale by the high-altitude UV, trembling violently on hydraulic stilts. Their diesel-electric turbines run at a flat, screaming pitch, driving wide augers and suction lines that drink the mineral-heavy water from the base of the peat-mat and force it up through a single, three-foot-wide iron pipeline that snakes across the surface of the valley toward the northern pass. 

There are no roads between the stations, no fences, and no wires—only the single black pipe resting directly on the cushion-plants, which are already slowly, patiently swelling up around the iron, trying to swallow it. Close to a station, the mechanical scream of the turbine mixes with the deep, hydraulic hammer of the valves and the endless, cavernous roar of the weeping scarps, creating a wall of noise so physical it makes the fabric of a heavy coat vibrate against the skin.

Yet for all the violence of the sound, nothing moves quickly. 

The wind at this height is steady, a slow, heavy push that never gusts, because there is nothing to break it into eddies. The water bursting from the root-walls falls in columns that look frozen in the brilliant sun. On the flatter stretches between the domes, where the water rises above the skin of the plants, it collects in wide, shallow pans only an inch deep. These pools are so choked with pale, filamentous algae and stiff bladderworts that they cannot ripple; when the wind pushes against them, the water moves in slow, syrupy wrinkles that take minutes to cross from one side of a pool to the other.

Life, outside of the total monopoly of the mat, is almost nonexistent. There are no birds; the air is too thin, the glare too exposed, and the noise makes acoustic hunting impossible. If you kneel down—letting the cold water immediately soak through the knees of your trousers—and peer closely into the blinding, wet needles of the *Donatia* cushions, you might see a single, heavy-bodied weevil, black as coal and wingless, hauling itself from one waxy leaf to the next at a pace so slow it seems geared to the growth of the moss itself. It may take the insect an entire morning to cross the width of a boot-print. 

And the boot-print itself will not last the afternoon. Because the ground is entirely alive, pressurized from below by water and turgor, the crushed rosettes slowly, stiffly push back up as soon as your weight is gone. By evening, when the relentless white sun finally drops behind the raw, black teeth of the western ridge and the valley snaps instantly from glaring brightness into a cold, indigo shadow, the mark of your passage is already erased—smoothed back into the vast, roaring, empty curve of the mat.
