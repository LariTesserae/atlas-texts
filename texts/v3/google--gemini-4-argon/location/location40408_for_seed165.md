The basin sits at the roof of the world, wide enough that the far rim is reduced to a dark, notched line against the bruise-colored sky, yet small enough that you can see every feature it contains in a single sweeping glance. The air is thin and biting, just above the threshold of freezing, so that nothing quite turns to ice, and nothing ever warms. 

There is no sun, nor any moon, only a permanent, sourceless twilight that filters down from the high, bruised dome of the stratosphere. The light has a strange, flat behavior here: it casts no shadows, or rather, the shadows are detached from their objects, pooled like dark oil in the leeward hollows of the rock, completely indifferent to the geometry of the stones above them.

Despite the stillness of the air, the valley is deafening. 

A continuous, bone-shaking roar fills the basin, like a cataract of gravel pouring down an iron chute, or a gale tearing through a forest of bronze. Yet nothing moves. The sound is not produced by the wind—there is no wind—Wait, rather, the sound *is* the geology. The altitude is so extreme, and the reality of the place worn so thin, that the rock itself is auditioning its own mass. The dark grey greywacke and slate that form the vast, sweeping scree slopes and settled, rounded ridges are acoustically unstable. The stone hums, grinds, and shrieks at a sustained, unchanging pitch, a symphony of immense tectonic pressure venting not as motion, but as pure noise. To walk across the basin floor is to walk through overlapping zones of static and thunder; step three paces to the left, and the roar of a non-existent avalanche fills your teeth; step to the right, and it is the deafening, hollow clangor of a cathedral bell struck and held in the fractional millisecond after impact, forever.

Because the landscape has reached a final, settled equilibrium, no stones actually fall. The erosion here is ancient and finished. The cliffs have long since slumped into smooth, concave skirts of talus. The boulders are rounded, nested deeply into pockets of dark, cold grit, settled into the exact positions they will occupy for eternity. 

Water exists here, but it has forgotten how to flow. It hangs in the air as a heavy, stationary mist that coats the rock in a perpetual cold sweat, and it gathers in shallow, leaden tarns in the depressions of the valley floor. The surface of these pools is scarred with permanent, unmoving ripples—standing waves caught in the continuous acoustic vibration of the bedrock. If you dip a hand into the water, it is numbingly cold and oddly thick, clinging to the skin like mercury before beading and rolling off, leaving the flesh dry. In some places, three feet above the dry scree, sheets of rain hang suspended in the dim air—not frozen into ice, still entirely liquid and cold to the touch, merely halted in their descent because the local gravity has developed a blind spot. You have to duck under these hovering curtains of water or push through them, feeling the cold wet slap of the drops as your body displaces them, watching them lazily drift back into their suspended lattices behind you.

In the vast, empty stretches between the boulders, the flora is sparse and strange. There are low, sprawling mats of black lichen that grow not on the rock, but an inch *above* it, anchored to the stone only by hair-thin, rigid rhizomes that hum like violin strings in the ambient roar. Scattered miles apart are solitary, stunted shrubs made of something resembling flexible horn or baleen. They have no leaves, only branching, slick wet tines that comb the damp air. Near these shrubs, space is slightly misaligned; look through the branches of one, and the ridge two miles behind it appears upside down or magnified, as if the plant is secreting a lens of warped distance around itself to trap the meager, dim light.

Scattered across the twelve-mile expanse of the valley are the structures. They are rare—perhaps six or seven in the entire basin—built of the same dry-stacked grey slate as the mountains, with no mortar, no metal, and no roof timbers. They look like shepherd huts or low watchtowers, weathered to the exact texture of the surrounding cliffs, so old they have settled into the ground like native outcrops. 

Inside these huts, the geometry is broken. A circular stone shelter six feet across on the outside might require forty paces to cross on the inside, its dry-stone walls receding into the gloom, damp and dripping with condensation, while the open doorway behind you remains right at your shoulder, reachable with an outstretched hand no matter how deep into the room you walk. Another structure is merely a low wall, three feet high, running for twenty yards across a barren flat; yet if you look over it from the north, the valley floor is empty, and if you look over it from the south, you see the ruined foundations of a stone village that occupies no physical space when you walk around the wall to stand in it. Who built them is an irrelevant question here; they feel less like human habitations and more like architectural calluses formed where the world rubbed against itself too hard.

Life moves through this loud, dim emptiness, though you may look for days before seeing it. 

The creatures here have adapted to the breakdown of rules. High on the slate ridges, something resembling a mountain goat—pale, gaunt, with four curling horns and wool matted with cold moisture—stands utterly motionless for weeks at a time. When it moves, it does not walk; it simply ceases to be on one boulder and is instantly standing on another fifty yards away, skipping the intervening space entirely to avoid the effort of climbing. The displacement makes no sound above the omnipresent roar of the valley, only a brief, dark afterimage that lingers on the retina for several minutes after the beast has gone.

In the hovering sheets of water and the vibrating tarns, pale, blind amphibians the size of a man's forearm swim in slow, deliberate circles. Occasionally, one will swim right out of the edge of a pond and continue swimming through the empty, cold air three feet above the dry ground, sculling lazily through the gloom as if the water were still supporting it, gills flaring in the thin atmosphere, following some invisible phantom tributary that dried up ten thousand years ago, before eventually angling back down into another pool half a mile away.

Nothing decays here, and nothing grows. The cold is constant, the dimness absolute, the roar eternal. A bone dropped on the scree neither bleaches nor rots; it simply acquires a slick film of condensation and vibrates softly against the stones, humming its small part in the deafening, motionless choir of the heights.
