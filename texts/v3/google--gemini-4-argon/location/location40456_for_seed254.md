The brick vaulting of the municipal steam-exchange hasn’t been seen in forty years, though its curve still dictates the shape of the canopy. The room—if a subterranean cistern the size of a cathedral nave can be called a room—is packed to the groin-vaults with root, frond, and cast iron. 

It sits sixty feet below the street level of a city that has long since paved over the maintenance hatches, leaving only the narrow, iron-grated ventilation shafts that pierce the ceiling at twenty-foot intervals. Down those shafts comes a weak, tallow-colored daylight, immediately diffused into a uniform greenish-gray murk by the mist and the sheer density of the foliage. 

There is no bare ground here. The floor is a three-foot-deep mattress of sodden, black-brown peat formed from decades of rotted fern-fronds, liverworts, and the shed bark of massive, pale-stemmed *Ficus* that have adapted to the permanent twilight. Beneath that sponge is a grating of rusted wrought-iron catwalks, and beneath the catwalks, two feet of warm, dark water moves at an imperceptible crawl through brick-lined sluices. Every step on the catwalk—where the catwalk hasn’t been entirely swallowed by the bulging, muscular knees of the roots—sends a low *clink-thud* through the metal, muffled instantly by the wet moss that jackets every handrail and rivet.

The heat comes from the pipes. They are everywhere, running in bundled arteries along the invisible walls, crossing the narrow central aisle on heavy iron stanchions, some as thick as hogsheads, others thin as bundled reeds. They carry district steam from the river-edge turbines to the upper town, and their insulation—once wrap-cloth and plaster—rotted off generations ago, replaced by four-inch pelts of *Sphagnum* and *Leucobryum* moss that drink the condensation directly from the sweating iron. Where a flange joint has worn loose over the decades, a steady, pressurized hiss escapes, blowing a permanent, pale tunnel through the hanging curtains of creeping fig. These steam-leaks are the sun and wind of the vault. Around them, the leaves of the *Monstera* and elephant-ear philodendrons grow to the size of cellar doors, their waxy surfaces trembling continuously in the hot draft, slick with run-off that patters down onto the lower tiers like heavy, localized rain.

Because the space is small—perhaps two hundred paces from the northern bulkhead to the southern manifold—and the biomass is total, the architecture and the botany have fused into a single load-bearing mass. A massive gate-valve wheel, five feet across, hangs suspended ten feet in the air; the pipe it once governed has rusted through and dropped into the peat below, yet the wheel remains fixed in place, held by a braided column of aerial roots that have welded themselves to the ceiling bolts. Old porcelain insulators, still carrying humming, tar-cloth-wrapped electrical cables, stick out from the trunks of palm-ferns like pale fungi. At the eastern curve of the vault, a row of cast-iron pressure gauges has been completely engulfed by a curtain of maidenhair spleenwort; only the brass bezels and cracked glass faces peer out, their needles rusted tight against the pins, droplets of water magnified behind the lenses.

The sound is tremendous, yet entirely devoid of urgency. It is a thick, braided roar composed of a thousand small, relentless hydromechanical facts: the high, white-noise *shhhhh* of three dozen pinhole steam-leaks; the rhythmic, hollow *clonk-slosh* of an automatic float-trap cycling somewhere beneath the roots at the south end, driven by water pressure alone; the deep, sixty-cycle hum of the transformer boxes sweating oil and condensation into the fern-banks; and over it all, the vast, unhurried *shuck-shuck-plop* of millions of gallons of condensed vapour gathering on leaf-tips, falling six inches to strike a lower leaf, gathering again, and finally striking the flooded brick channels below.

Nothing scurries here. The air is too heavy with carbonic acid and hot wet iron, and the vents above are too narrow and barred with wire mesh for birds or rats to drop through without perishing on the grates. There isn’t a beetle or a spider in the fronds. Without herbivores, the leaves are pristine, geometrical, and impossibly crowded, overlapping like green slate tiles until they choke out their own lower branches, which drop silently into the warm water below to become black mud. 

Every few minutes, a thermal expansion loop high in the darkness reaches its threshold. A heavy iron collar slides half an inch along a greased sleeve with a deep, bass *groan* that vibrates through the roots and makes the water in the sluices shiver in concentric rings. A shower of lukewarm droplets shakes loose from the canopy, patters down through twenty feet of broad leaves, and then the room settles back into its steady, roaring, vegetative simmer.
