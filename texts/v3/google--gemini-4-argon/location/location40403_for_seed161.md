The entire settlement fits inside the throat of a single, ancient fumarole, three miles above the dry basin floor. 

Outside, the mountain is dead—a vast, high-altitude desert where the wind has spent fifty million years grinding the granite down into motionless dunes of grey flour. At this elevation, the sky is a dark, starved violet, and there is no rain. There is never rain. Look over the outer rim of the vent and there is only the blinding, freezing emptiness of the roof of the world.

Step inside the lip, however, and you enter the Chimney. 

It is a vertical cylinder of basalt, perhaps three hundred feet across and plunging down into the crust, though the bottom is choked off five hundred feet down by millennia of fallen rock and compost. What makes the Chimney live is the Breath: a continuous, slow exhalation of warm, faintly sulfurous, damp air rising from the dying magma chambers miles below. It is not a geyser or a steam vent; it is merely a warm draft, steady as a pulse, keeping the interior at a perpetual, balmy seventy degrees. 

The rock walls of this vertical tube are so old they have lost all sharpness. Millions of years of rising warm air and the gentle, abrasive dust of the upper atmosphere have polished the basalt into smooth, bulbous lobes, like the insides of a stone throat. 

There is no running water here—no puddles, no streams, no drips. The moisture exists entirely as a thick, buoyant haze that clings to the rock and hangs in the air, turning the harsh alpine sunlight from above into a dim, dusty gold that barely reaches the lower tiers. By the time the light filters halfway down the shaft, it is the color of bruised amber, and at the bottom, it is a perpetual, warm twilight lit only by smoldering balls of dried moss hung in stone cages.

Because the space is so small—no wider than a cathedral—everything is built on top of everything else. The architecture and the botany have fused into a single, dense plug of life filling the shaft. 

The flora is lush, but it is the lushness of drought-adapted epiphytes. Massive, shaggy curtains of pale-green beard-moss and silver tillandsia hang thirty feet long from the rock lobes, drinking the rising mist directly from the air. Huge, bloated bromeliads and grey-leaved agave wedged into the crevices catch the condensation on their waxy skins, funneling the precious drops down into their armored cores. Thick, woody lianas, rough as hemp rope, crisscross the open gulf of the shaft, anchoring one side of the cylinder to the other in a dense, three-dimensional rigging.

Woven directly into this rigging is the town. 

With no timber other than the twisted, slow-growing ironwood roots that grip the stone, the inhabitants build with what they have: bone, dry-stacked pumice, adobe made from volcanic ash and bird guano, and cured hides. The dwellings are small, bulbous pods and narrow ledges that spiral down the walls like the chambers of a wasp’s nest. Over centuries, the settlement has grown inward toward the center of the shaft. Bridges of braided vine and hammered copper wire span the gap, supporting suspended platforms, wicker granaries, and tiered net-gardens. To move through the town is to climb through a dim, warm thicket of dry leaves, mud-brick walls, woven baskets, and wooden scaffolding, where the roof of one house is the porch of the next, and giant, leathery fern-fronds brush against the clay chimneys of the kitchens below.

There is no metal harder than copper here; the technology is one of tension, counterweights, and patience. Great wheels of lashed bone and fibrous wood turn slowly in the rising thermal draft, driving simple pulley systems that haul baskets of ash and night-soil to the upper gardens, or lower clay jars to the bottom of the pit where the air is dampest. On the highest, brightest ledges near the rim, thousands of fine hair-nets are stretched between stone totems. They comb the rising mist, gathering beads of dew that trickle down waxed leather gutters into sealed cisterns of glazed clay. Water is never poured here; it is dabbed, wicked, and rationed by the thimbleful.

Despite the lack of open water, the density of animal life is staggering, packed into the warm cylinder because the world outside is a sterile vacuum. The sound of it is a constant, loud, reverberating din that echoes off the curved stone walls, unable to escape. 

Thousands of cliff-swallows, rock-doves, and heavy-bodied, flightless rails nest in the crannies between the mud houses and beneath the hanging gardens. Their cooing, clucking, and the dry *whir-clatter* of their wings fill the upper reaches. Lower down, the lianas and house-walls crawl with fat, mottled geckos the size of forearms, which bark at one another in the gloom—a sharp, hollow *chak-chak-chak* that bounces from wall to wall. Cicadas and giant, dry-shelled beetles drone from the depths of the moss curtains, a saw-toothed vibration you feel in your teeth. The inhabitants themselves add to the chorus: the rhythmic *clack-clack* of bone looms, the grinding of dry tubers on basalt metates, and the whistled language they use to cut through the ambient roar from one side of the shaft to the other.

Everything smells of warm stone, dusty feathers, dried sage, and the faint, sweet-rot scent of the deep compost bed at the very bottom of the shaft, where giant, blind millipedes as thick as a man's wrist slowly churn the fallen detritus of the town into rich, black soil. 

Nothing happens quickly in the Chimney. The draft rises at the same three miles an hour it has for ten thousand years. The giant lichens on the north wall expand by a millimeter a century. A dropped tool falls through six layers of netting, three gardens, and a dozen bird-roosts before it comes to rest. Outside, the high wind scours the dead peaks under a blackening sky; inside, in the crowded, noisy, twilight warmth, a lizard barks, a wooden pulley groans, and the moss drinks the invisible river in the air.
