The first thing that registers is not the drop, though the drop is absolute, nor the cold, which is the dry, static chill of a meat locker. It is the sound. 

The valley is a miles-wide basin of raw, unweathered stone, and the air within it is vibrating with a continuous, deafening roar. It is not the sound of wind, because the air is nearly motionless. It is the sound of the rock itself arriving. The geology here is unfinished, caught in the act of compiling. Across the vast, open gulf, towering needles of basalt and pale, chalky feldspar are extruding upward from nothing, growing by fractions of an inch per century. The noise is the friction of matter forcing its way into a space that has not quite made room for it—a sustained, tectonic bowing of a billion tons of stone, like an iron bridge being wrenched apart in extreme slow motion, amplified until it rattles the teeth in the skull.

There is no sky in the traditional sense, only an overhead ceiling of bruised, violet-gray gloom. Light does not fall from a sun; it leaks weakly from the seams where the cliffs are still knitting themselves together. It is the dim, sourceless illumination of dusk in a winter forest, just enough to show that the distance between one pinnacle and the next is miles of empty, freezing air. Because there is no water—no rain, no mist, no humidity at all—the air has a terrifying clarity despite the gloom. A spire five miles away is as sharp-edged as a shard of glass held before the eye. Nothing has ever been smoothed down. Every ledge is a fresh fracture; every talus slope is a spill of razor-flint that has never known the softening touch of moss or ice.

Yet the rock is heavily draped in vegetation. 

Hanging from the knife-edge ridges and spilling down the vertical faces are immense, lush tapestries of dry flora. These plants do not drink water; they metabolize the acoustic vibration and the raw spatial friction of the unfinished world. There are thick, velvety ferns the color of dried blood, their fronds as heavy as lead blankets, and vast, cascading curtains of silver-black vines that have the texture of spun fiberglass. When they grow, they do not uncurl; they crystallize, adding new leaves in sudden, silent increments. Some of the larger growths resemble weeping willows made of ash and charcoal-colored velvet, their canopies twenty feet thick, utterly motionless in the dead air. If you break a stem, no sap appears—only a puff of dry, cold spores that smells of ozone and crushed flint, which immediately falls upward or sideways, caught in the localized gravity faults that plague the canyon.

Reality has not settled here. The rules of perspective and solid matter are still loose, like poorly woven cloth. 

Look across the gulf at the central massif—a sheer wall of obsidian rising twelve thousand feet—and you will see that a colossal section of the cliff face is missing its middle. The base is there, and the peak is there, hovering in the violet gloom, separated by four hundred feet of empty air through which the far side of the valley is clearly visible. Yet the great mats of blood-fern grow continuously up the base, vanish into the gap, and emerge again at the floating summit without interruption. Elsewhere, shadows do not match the objects that cast them; a lone, jagged monolith casts three distinct shadows in different directions, and one of those shadows is solid—a three-dimensional wedge of absolute blackness that can be climbed like a staircase. Distance is unreliable. You can walk along a narrow, razor-backed ridge toward a cluster of silver-trees for three hours, watching them grow no larger, only to take a single step backward and find yourself standing beneath their branches.

Nothing moves among the leaves. There are no insects, no birds, no lizards on the stone. The silence of biology is complete, swallowed entirely by the ear-splitting harmonic groan of the cliffs. 

And yet, someone has been working here. 

Scattered at wide, lonely intervals along the precarious ledges and wedged into the crevices of the hovering rocks are small, primitive structures. They are built with a desperate, heavy simplicity. There are low, dry-stone cairns made of stacked slate, bound together with the tough, fibrous cables of the fiberglass-vines. There are small, beehive-shaped huts of unworked granite, their walls four feet thick, with no windows and entrances so low one would have to crawl inside on bruised knees. These shelters are clearly designed to baffle the sound. Inside them, the inner walls are plastered with a thick paste made of ground spores and rock dust, hardened into a gray, porous crust that drinks a fraction of the world’s roaring.

Near these huts are the tools and the works. Lying on a flat slab of emerging granite—a slab whose left edge ends in a pixelated blur of unformed grayness—are heavy mauls made of meteoric iron lashed to bone-dry branches with braided vine. There are chisels of chipped quartz and wedges of dense, petrified wood. 

Whoever used them was trying to correct the landscape. 

Where a seam of reality is leaking—a place where the air shimmers with a nauseating, double-exposed duplicate of the cliff face—someone has driven a row of quartz wedges deep into the nothingness, pinning the two halves of the space together like a carpenter mending a split board. Across a gap where a ledge simply ceases to exist for ten yards before resuming, there is a crude suspension bridge woven from thick bundles of dark fern-stems, anchored at both ends by massive piles of hand-stacked boulders. And hanging from the underside of the bridges, or suspended from tall, tripod-like wooden frames on the highest, most exposed crags, are great, clumsy chimes made of flat stone slabs. 

They do not ring in the wind, because there is no wind. Instead, they hang taut at strange, diagonal angles, pulled by the warped gravity of the valley, humming furiously in sympathy with the deafening ground-note of the mountains. Occasionally, once every few days or weeks, a stone slab on one of these frames will finally succumb to the vibration and shatter. The pieces do not fall into the abyss. They drift slowly outward into the dim, open air, rotating lazily, until they find a spot where the world is still thickening, and there they lock into place, becoming the seed for a new spire of rock that will take ten thousand years to finish arriving.
