The place is a flooded pit-works, no wider across than a parish church, dropped into the basalt throat of an oceanic trench. Above it, seventy fathoms of black seawater press down; within it, a denser, hypersaline brine has pooled, creating an under-lake with its own sluggish tide and a distinct, shimmering meniscus like hammered pewter. Only the very tips of the two tallest iron condenser towers break through this inner surface, reaching up into the lighter water above like drowned chimneys. Everything else sits submerged in the heavy brine.

The temperature is just below the freezing point of fresh water, kept liquid only by the sheer weight of the salt and the slow, hydraulic churning of the machines. Because the space is so confined—walled in on all four sides by plummeting basalt shelves that have been terraced and bolted with cast-iron retaining plates—the infrastructure has had to grow inward upon itself. It is an urban knot of plumbing and catwalks compressed into the volume of a single great room. Narrow gantry alleys, barely wide enough for a diving suit’s shoulders, thread between three-story manifolds, spherical pressure-vats, and bundles of gutta-percha cabling as thick as tree trunks. There is no sky and no horizon, only the immediate proximity of the next rust-velveted bulkhead, the next brass hand-wheel, the next overlapping tier of perforated floor-grating.

Despite the density of the engines, the basin is profoundly quiet. These are not combustion machines; they are driven by pressure differentials and the heavy, syrupy descent of the brine itself. Pistons thicker than hogsheads slide into their cylinders over the course of minutes, greased by mineral tallow that never washes away. Great counterweights of pig-iron rise and fall on chains whose links have been worn by centuries of salt-silt into smooth, boneless ovals. You do not hear them; you feel a subtle tightening in the water when a valve three alleys over seats itself, or a faint, sub-audible tick in the teeth when an electrical relay arcs inside its oil-filled glass jar.

The darkness is broken only by the work-lights, which have been running on the trench’s geothermal trickle for so long that their glass housings are half-blinded by lime scale. They cast a dim, tallowy orange that dies four feet from the bulb, and a sickly, copper-sulfate green where the wiring has bled into the water. In this gloom, the erosion of the place is tactile. Every sharp industrial angle has been softened. Rivet heads have slumped into tear-shapes; the embossed foundry marks on the turbine casings have been planed away into illegibility by the relentless, microscopic scouring of suspended clay. Iron and stone have traded skins—the basalt walls are stained deep ochre with migrated rust, while the iron framing is armored in grey, calcareous rime.

Life is scarce, adapted to the cold and the metal. Where warm brine weeps from the gaskets of the heat-exchangers, sparse patches of flora take hold: pale, leafless stolons that resemble braided solder wire, and solitary, fist-sized bladders of dark maroon kelp that stay anchored to the ironwork by secreting a mild acid, etching their own root-cups into the steel. Every few hours, one of the bladders detaches and rises with agonizing slowness toward the brine-meniscus, hits the boundary layer like a balloon hitting a ceiling, and drifts laterally until the intake flumes suck it back down to the bottom.

Through the narrow clearances between the pipes drift the only animals: flat, ribbon-like eels the color of wet parchment, entirely eyeless, with a fringe of copper-colored cilia along their spines that stiffens whenever one of the dynamo-coils cycles up. They are rare—perhaps four or five in the entire flooded yard—and they move with the same drugged, heavy deliberation as the pistons, threading through the spokes of turning flywheels with a precision that suggests they are less inhabitants of the machinery than another moving part of it. 

The strangest feature of the yard is the precipitate. The industrial process—whatever distillation or extraction it was built to perform—still functions in a closed, pointless loop. Out of the high exhaust vents near the ceiling-meniscus, a fine snow of heavy, bismuth-colored crystals is constantly shed. It falls through the dark water not like snow in air, but in straight, plumb-line streaks, collecting on the narrow roofs of the transformer huts and the shoulders of the boilers until the weight becomes too great, and a silent, sluggish avalanche slips off into the sump-alleys below, where the intake augers slowly chew it up to be boiled, condensed, and snowed down all over again.
