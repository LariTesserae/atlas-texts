The place is an amphitheater of chalky, ochre stone, no wider than a village square, cupped near the summit of a jagged spire that rises into a dry, amber sky. There is no rain here, and there has never been rain. The air tastes of baked mica, static electricity, and hot copper. Yet the basin is choked with a lush, violent abundance of dry-state flora, and it is deafening.

The source of both the noise and the life is the updraft. 

At the center of the stone bowl are five wide, scalloped vents—throats in the mountain that exhale a continuous, turbulent gale of warm gas from deep within the crust. The wind does not blow across the basin; it erupts from the floor and corkscrews upward, carrying a fine, shimmering haze of sulfur-ash and pulverized iron. The stone lips of these vents are worn smooth as river-bone, fluted and grooved by millennia of abrasive, dry gales. 

Despite the lushness, the basin feels strangely spacious, its architecture defined by the emptiness between things. The flora does not grow on the ground—the stone floor is too scoured and unstable—but instead anchors itself to the sheer canyon walls and spans the open air in a sparse, three-dimensional lattice. Great, braided cables of silica-vine stretch from rim to rim like the rigging of a ship, widely spaced so the updraft can pass through without tearing the whole web free. From these central cables hang immense, pleated sails of dry, leathery tissue—*parchment-kelp* and *bellows-ferns*—colored in muted shades of rust, bruised violet, and tarnished silver. 

Because there is no water, the plants drink the wind. Their leaves are brittle, hollow membranes coated in microscopic hooks that snag the rising dust and harvest the kinetic energy of the gale. Piezoelectric fibers run through their stalks; the harder they are whipped and thrashed by the turbulence, the faster their dry, chemical metabolisms run. They are constantly in frantic motion—snapping like bullwhips, unfurling, tearing, and visibly knitting themselves back together with fast-extruding threads of black resin. 

The sound is a relentless, percussive roar. It is the sound of a thousand heavy canvas sails luffing in a hurricane, layered over the deep, resonant organ-groan of the stone vents and the dry, metallic clatter of hollow seed-pods striking the rock walls. 

Nothing stays still. The light is a perpetual, bruised twilight, dimmed by the canopy of thrashing sails overhead and the perpetual ceiling of dust suspended at the rim of the bowl. Through this amber gloom shoots the fauna—an absurdly abundant, hyperactive ecology of creatures built like kites, darts, and living origami. 

There are no heavy bodies here. Everything is desiccated, hollow-boned, and paper-thin. Swarms of *shale-moths* with wings of overlapping mica scales ride the thermal columns, clicking against one another like castanets. Ribbon-like predators, five feet long and no thicker than a thumb leaf, undulate through the open air spaces between the vines, using the electrostatic charge of the dust to cling to the underside of the thrashing flora. Spindly, multi-jointed scavengers the size of dogs—built like bone-dry tumbleweeds with beaks of dark horn—vault from cable to cable, letting the gale catch their webbed limbs to fling them across fifty feet of empty space in a fraction of a second. They mate, hunt, and molt on the fly, shedding translucent husks that immediately join the swirling vortex of debris.

Scattered along the terraced ledges of the stone walls are the built things. Whoever—or whatever—made them used the only materials at hand: wind-polished stone, black plant-resin, and the shed chitin of the larger flyers. They are primitive, sparse, and entirely functional. 

Built into the leeward alcoves of the rock are small, domed wind-blinds, cobbled together from flat slabs of ochre shale cemented with resin. They look like giant mud-dauber nests, barely big enough for a crouched figure. Driven into the stone fissures near the vents are rows of heavy, crudely hammered bronze wedges and bone pitons, anchoring thick ropes of braided vine-husk that have frayed in the gale. 

Most striking are the chimes and the traps. Suspended in the wider gaps between the flora-cables are skeletal frames of lashed bone holding tensioned strips of cured hide and hammered, unrefined copper plates. As the turbulent gusts hit them, they vibrate at a furious blur, adding a rhythmic, thrumming hum and a harsh metallic clanging to the roar of the foliage. Some are clearly traps—sticky, resin-smeared nets strung across the predictable eddy-currents to catch the darting ribbon-fauna. Others seem to be purely acoustic or mechanical: primitive wind-vanes that spin wildly on greased stone axles, or hollowed-out boulders carved with spiraled flutes that whistle at a pitch that makes the teeth ache, perhaps meant to herd the chitinous swarms toward the ledges.

No builders are visible in the dim, roaring bowl, though a few of the resin-domes have fresh, sticky repairs smeared across their windward faces, and a stone-bladed scraping tool lies wedged into a crack near a pile of freshly shucked moth-scales. 

The entire basin operates at a fever pitch, a self-contained storm caught in a stone cup. Every few minutes, the deep pressure in the mountain shifts. The five vents stutter, the pitch of the stone-groan drops an octave, and for three seconds the wind dies. The sudden silence is shocking. The great leathery sails collapse inward; the swarms of flyers plummet twenty feet through the dim air; the bone-frames stop rattling. Then, with a percussive *thump* that shakes dust from the rim, the updraft blasts back to life, the sails snap taut like gunshots, the creatures catch the air and scatter upward, and the whole dry, frantic engine resumes its roar.
