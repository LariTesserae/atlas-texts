The horizon here is a smear where the pale, bright sky meets the reflection of the pale, bright sky. There are no mountains to stop the eye, nor even hills worthy of the name; whatever ridges once stood here were ground down to their roots eons ago. What remains is a drowned peneplain—an immense, table-flat expanse of ancient basement rock, worn as smooth as river soap and covered by a sheet of warm water rarely deeper than a human shin. 

Walking across it feels like walking across the polished floor of a flooded cathedral that has lost its walls. Underfoot, the granite and gneiss have been planed flat by time, stained in broad, swirling bands of ochre, rust, and plum by the slow leaching of iron and manganese. Where the stone rises an inch or two above the water table, it forms sprawling, whale-backed shields miles wide, slick with a microscopic film of dark cyanobacteria that glistens like wet sealskin in the sun. Where the stone dips, the water gathers in vast, lukewarm lagoons that have no current at all, only a lazy, rhythmic sloshing driven by the breath of the wind.

Between the sheets of bare rock, the earth has settled into its final, irreducible state: a heavy, silken kaolin clay, bleached white and lavender, mixed with quartz sand whose individual grains have been rolled and dissolved until they are completely spherical. When disturbed, the clay blooms upward in slow-motion clouds that hang suspended in the clear, amber-tinted water for days before settling back down.

Because the soil is so ancient and thoroughly leached of nutrients, plants have only a tenuous foothold. There is no canopy, no brush, no tangled marsh-grass to break the sightlines. You can see for ten miles in any direction. The vegetation that does survive is spaced out like sculptures in a gallery, adapted to poverty and warmth. At intervals of fifty or a hundred yards, solitary, swollen-trunked trees—something akin to dwarf cypresses or ancient pandanus—rise from low mounds of peat they have spent centuries accumulating around their own roots. Their bark is papery and pale, shedding in long strips that float on the water without rotting. Between them, the flats are dotted with small, fierce clusters of carnivorous pitcher-plants and red sundews, glowing like rubies against the grey-white stone, catching the insects that the barren rock cannot feed them. 

Despite the emptiness of the sightlines, the air is thick with sound. Because there are no trees or hills to baffle acoustics, noise travels unbroken across the water for miles, overlapping into a dense, continuous roar. 

The volume comes from the creatures that use this warm, shallow pan as a massive broth of small life. Millions of frogs—tiny, translucent things no bigger than thumbnails—hide in the wet crevices of the weathered rock and under the peeling bark of the stunted trees. Their call is not a croak, but a high, metallic clicking, like two pebbles struck together. Multiplied by the million across twenty square miles of reverberant stone and water, it creates a deafening, static-like shimmer of sound that hangs over the plain day and night, rising and falling in slow, hypnotic waves. 

Through this high-frequency wall of clicks comes the heavy, resonant booming of large wading birds and the wet, percussive *slap-slap-slap* of hundreds of flat-tailed amphibians thrashing through the ankle-deep shallows. Flocks of long-legged birds—stork-like, with plumage the color of wet ash and rust—stand spaced out across the mirroring water in the thousands. When one takes flight, the heavy, leathery beat of its wings sounds surprisingly close, even if the bird is half a mile away. 

The heat is constant, heavy, and wet, yet the light is clear. In the afternoon, the sun beats down on the dark rock shields and the shallow water, turning the whole landscape into a slow-simmering cauldron. The air directly above the water wavers with heat-shimmer, distorting the distant lines of wading birds until they look like tall, wavering reeds. By late afternoon, the air is so saturated with moisture that it can hold no more; as the sun drops, the atmosphere simply condenses in place. A warm, luminous mist forms right at eye level, turning the low sun into a huge, fuzzy apricot smear that reflects endlessly in the motionless water below.

Nothing happens quickly here. There are no flash floods, because the land is too flat to gather a current; when it rains, the sky simply opens up and the water level rises by an inch across the entire world at once, the droplets pockmarking the smooth lagoons with a sound like frying fat. Over weeks, the sheet of water creeps imperceptibly southward toward a distant, unseen sea, moving through microscopic gradients in the rock. A fallen branch takes fifty years to dissolve in the mineral-poor, tannin-slick water, becoming a smooth, blackened bone of wood around which tiny, armored catfish gather to scrape at algae. It is a landscape at the very end of its geological life—worn down to the bone, bathed in warm water and bright light, loud with the voices of small, patient things.
