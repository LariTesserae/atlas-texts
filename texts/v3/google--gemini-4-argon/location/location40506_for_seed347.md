The basin lies three hundred feet below the surrounding limestone plain, born from a catastrophic collapse of the caprock only a few centuries ago. Because the drop happened in geological infancy, the rim is not rounded or gentle; it is a jagged, vertical sheer of grey-white stone that rings the sunken valley like the walls of a quarry. From the high lip, the entire expanse is visible—roughly two miles across—open to the pale, overcast sky, yet entirely severed from the wind and weather of the flatlands above. 

Down inside, the space is packed to absolute capacity. There is no bare ground. What did not fall as talus was quickly drowned by the breached aquifer, and what was not drowned was built upon, and what was built upon has been swallowed by cold-weather flora. 

It is an urban sinkhole, a city of stacked, unmortared stone wedged tightly into the fracture zones, climbing the talus slopes and standing knee-deep in the black, cold water that pools across the basin floor. There is no metal here, no glass, no Mortar, no axle, and no chimney. The architecture is entirely dry-stone—millions of flat, sharp-edged slabs of fractured limestone levered from the collapse and laid in dense, interlocking courses. The buildings are four and five stories tall, crowding against one another until the canals between them are mere slits of dark water. Roofs are pitched with heavy slate tiles, overlapping like fish scales, each one upholstered in a six-inch pelt of emerald and rust-colored moss. 

The air is bitingly cold, hovering just above freezing, and saturated with moisture. Because the rim blocks the crosswinds, the dampness settles into a permanent, crystalline chill. Water is everywhere. It sheets down the sheer boundary cliffs in a thousand thin, silent ribbons, fed by the weeping water table of the plains above. It beads on the undersides of stone lintels. It runs down the center of the narrow stepped alleyways in carved stone rills. The basin floor is a labyrinth of cold marshes and flooded plazas where the water sits dark and clear over submerged flagstones.

Every square inch of stone that isn't actively scoured by footsteps is claimed by vegetation. This is a lushness born of cold saturation: thick, rubbery liverworts that coat the lower walls of the tenements; dense curtains of pale green lichen hanging from the eaves; forests of hart’s-tongue fern and brittle bladder-fern bursting from the dry-stone joints of the towers. In the narrow water-streets, tall reeds and cold-water sedges grow so thickly they brush against the walls on either side, leaving only a thin, dark channel in the middle for wading or poling narrow rafts of bound rushes. Twisted, dwarf alders and bog-myrtles grow directly out of the rooftops and upper terraces, their roots wedged deep into the dry-stone masonry, locking the city’s stones together even as they slowly pry them apart.

Despite the density of the construction and the abundance of life, the basin is astonishingly quiet. The three-foot-thick walls of moss and peat-stuffed stone absorb sound like a sponge. There are no wheels to clatter on cobbles, no engines, no striking of iron. Even the water makes very little noise; it doesn't roar in cataracts, it seeps, glides, and trickles through the muffling pillows of sphagnum moss. When a stone shifts or a droplet falls into a cistern, the sound is flat, deadened immediately by the wet greenery pressing in from all sides. 

Under the diffuse, grey daylight that pours cleanly into the open bowl, the ecosystem is in constant, quiet motion. The city appears uninhabited by humans now, or perhaps its builders simply built for an ecology that outlasted them, leaving behind a metropolis that functions as an artificial cliff-face and marshland. 

Life is crowded into every vertical layer. Down in the flooded foundations and cellar-pools, thousands of dark, spotted newts and blind cave-crayfish navigate the sunken rooms, slipping over windowsills beneath the surface. Above the waterline, the stone walkways and terraces are busy with small, thick-furred mammals—water voles, lemmings, and sleek, dark-pelted mink that hunt them through the dry-stone wall cavities. Because the masonry has no mortar, the walls themselves are hollow matrices, three-dimensional highways inside the stone where millions of creatures nest, hide, and travel unseen, only their breath visible as tiny puffs of steam emerging from the chinks between rocks.

On the upper stories, where the dwarf trees tangle with crumbling watchtowers and aqueducts, large birds dominate. Great blue herons and bitterns stand motionless on the moss-padded parapets, their grey plumage blending seamlessly with the raw limestone. Dippers and winter wrens flit through the fern-choked windows of multi-story dwellings, hunting the clouds of cold-hardy midges and stoneflies that hatch from the standing water below. High up on the raw, unweathered cliffs of the sinkhole's edge, mountain goats pick their way along inches-wide ledges, occasionally dislodging a fresh shard of flinty rock that tumbles down into the marsh-city with a brief, sharp *clack* before the moss claims it.

The cold keeps decay slow. Fallen timber from the rooftop alders does not rot quickly; it becomes waterlogged, sinking into the plaza pools or petrifying under blankets of fast-growing bryophytes. A constant, slow-motion exchange takes place across the valley: water seeps in from the plains, filters through miles of man-made stone courses and root-mats, collects in the central fens, and drains silently down into the deeper karst caves beneath the city floor. Fog forms in the late afternoon, rising from the canals to fill the narrow streets, wrapping the dense, towering stonework in white wool until only the highest, moss-heavy roofs and the jagged, newly-broken rim of the upper world remain visible in the pale light.
