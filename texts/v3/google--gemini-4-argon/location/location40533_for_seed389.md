At thirty thousand feet, the sky should be freezing and thin, a needle-sharp indigo that scrapes the lungs. Here, it is the color of unbleached linen, heavy with heat and a thick, soup-like pressure that defies the altitude. The city of Oru-Vane sits inside the notched lip of an upthrust basalt plateau—a geological tooth pushed so recently from the earth’s crust that its edges have not yet learned to crumble. The rock is fresh, knife-sharp, and glowing with a persistent, deep-core fever. 

Because there has been no wind or rain long enough to round them off, the natural stone and the industrial iron look remarkably alike. The city does not sit *on* the mountain; it is wedged into the raw, vertical fractures of the uplift, a dense, miles-wide congestion of cast-iron refineries, brick kilns, and terraced condenser-towers that climb the jagged steps of the caldera.

Everything is hot to the touch. The ambient temperature hovers near that of human blood, fed by both the sun—which hangs in the haze like a brass weight—and the relentless radiant heat of the rock itself. Yet there is no fire, no smoke, and almost no noise. The industry of Oru-Vane is vast and pervasive, an endless interlocking puzzle of pipes, vats, and counterweights stretching across the four-mile wide cleft, but it operates at the speed of cooling magma. 

It is an architecture of peristalsis and thermal expansion. Huge, bulbous boilers made of riveted copper sit directly over geothermal vents, their metal skins flexing outward by fractions of an inch over six-hour cycles, driving pistons so massive and geared so low that their movement is invisible to the naked eye. You can stand in a narrow alley—flanked by walls of unweathered obsidian and sweating iron plate, packed so tightly together that three people could not walk abreast—and feel the city working only through the soles of your boots. It is a sub-audible, rhythmic thrum, punctuated once every few minutes by the sharp *tink* of cooling metal or the heavy, hydraulic sigh of a valve equalizing pressure three blocks away.

Water does not fall here; it sweats. The high, hot air holds a stubborn, mineral-heavy dampness that condenses on the slightly cooler flanks of the shaded ironwork. Every overhang, every gantry, every braided bundle of copper conduit is beaded with warm, oily moisture. The city is rigged to catch it. Thousands of shallow, fluted gutters of galvanized tin trace the undersides of the bridges and pipeline-bundles, channeling the trickle into lead-lined cisterns. Where the water leaks—and it leaks everywhere, in slow, rhythmic drips—it leaves behind crusts of yellow sulfur, copper sulfate, and bone-white salt. This is the only erosion the city knows: not a wearing away, but a slow, mineral accrual. A wrench left on a catwalk will, within a season, be welded to the grating by a rind of crystalline carbonate.

There is no green. Not a blade of grass, not a cushion of moss. The nearest thing to flora is a dry, rust-colored lithotrophic crust that spreads across the shaded sides of the brick vents, feeding on the iron oxide and sulfur dioxide. It looks less like life and more like a particularly organized stain.

Instead of plants, the crevices of the city are occupied by the *vitrifex*—the glass-makers. They are rare, solitary creatures, roughly the size and shape of a flattened dog, though their radial symmetry suggests something closer to a terrestrial starfish or a multi-limbed crab. Their carapaces are composed of laminated silicate plates, dull grey and slag-like, which allow them to blend seamlessly into the heaps of industrial clinker. They move with the same agonizing slowness as the machinery, creeping along the scalding intake pipes at a pace of a few feet an hour. They graze on the mineral crusts and the copper-sulfate blooms, their mandibles grinding the crystals with a sound like two river stones being rubbed together in a wool sack. When they die, their bodies do not rot; the heat bakes their soft tissues dry within hours, leaving their silicate shells fixed to the pipes like strange, ornamental bosses.

The density of the place is suffocating, yet the views are dizzying. You will be walking through a warren of warm, damp iron—pipes as thick as ancient oaks curving overhead, blocking out the sky, the air smelling of wet pennies, ozone, and hot grease—and then the alley will simply end at a sheer, unguarded drop. 

Suddenly, the scale opens out. Below, the raw, unweathered fracture of the mountain plunges two miles down into a dry, shimmering salt-flat, the rock face so new that the striations of its upward violent birth look like wet clay scored by a giant’s thumb. Across the gulf, three miles away, the opposite wall of the breach rises just as sharply, and it too is encrusted with the dense, quiet tiers of the city: thousands of distillation towers, cooling fins, and suspension cables hanging motionless in the thick air. 

Between the two halves of the city, thick iron hawsers are strung across the abyss. Suspended from them are the slag-buckets—cars the size of small houses. They are moving, carrying refined tellurium and heavy salts from the eastern smelters to the western fractionators, but their progress is so slow that they appear painted against the linen sky. If you watch one, fixating on its alignment against a distant chimney, you will realize after ten minutes that it has crept three lengths to the left.

The rules of this place are dictated by thermal inertia and patience. Nothing is wasted because nothing is fast enough to scatter. A spilled bucket of water does not splash and run; it forms a shallow, steaming lens on the hot basalt, slowly shrinking while the nearby condenser-flues draw its vapor back into the municipal pipes. The light does not sparkle; it gleams dully off wet iron and fresh volcanic glass, casting sharp, motionless shadows that only shift as the sun crawls across the zenith. It is a metropolis built to harvest the birth-heat of a mountain, operating on the mountain's own clock—a vast, sweating engine of black stone and red brass, ticking once an hour in the quiet at the top of the world.
