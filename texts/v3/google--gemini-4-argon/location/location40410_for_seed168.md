At this altitude, the air is thin, warm, and bone-dry, tasting faintly of ozone and warm zinc. The sky above is a pale, cloudless, unblinking blue—the flat, even illumination of a mid-morning that never progresses toward noon. 

The valley is an immense, open basin of raw geology, five miles across, rimmed by jagged, freshly uplifted ridges. Because there has never been rain here, the stone has never known the softening touch of erosion. There is no soil, no silt, no rounded pebbles or dry riverbeds. The terrain is made of pristine, fractured basalt and pale Feldspar, broken into vast, crisp-edged geometric terraces that step down into the basin. Every fracture in the rock looks as though it cleaved five minutes ago; the edges are glass-sharp, the mineral faces glittering and unoxidized in the bright sun.

Yet, the valley is not barren. It is a place of vast, airy emptiness punctuated by pockets of startling, lush abundance—and a quiet, pervasive mechanical hum.

There are no buildings, no walls, no roads, and no shelters. The technology here is infrastructure without architecture. Tracing its way across the sharp-edged stone terraces is a sprawling, regional network of heavy-gauge copper busbars, cast-iron junction boxes, and insulated high-voltage cables. They are bolted directly into the raw rock with expansion pins. Every half-mile or so, sitting completely exposed to the dry air, is an industrial transformer or a heavy, flywheel-driven regulator, its casing painted a dull, utilitarian olive or slate gray. There are no fences around them, no warning signs, no sheds to protect them from weather that never comes. They sit in the vast emptiness of the rock like natural outcrops, humming at a steady, immutable sixty hertz. 

From these machines, thick braided grounding straps and feeder lines snake out across the stone, leading directly into the root-masses of the flora.

Without water, the vegetation has formed a symbiosis with the grid—or perhaps the grid was laid down specifically to feed it. The plants do not grow in continuous carpets; the density of the valley is too low, the space between things too immense. Instead, they rise in isolated, overflowing groves that cling to the electrical lines. Where they grow, they are breathtakingly lush. Broad, heavy-leaved fronds of deep emerald and dark, oily bronze spill over the sharp basalt ledges. Thick, succulent vines—filled not with water, but with a dense, non-aqueous dielectric gel—coil tightly around the copper busbars, absorbing the heat and the raw current. 

The foliage is warm to the touch, kept at a constant, feverish temperature by the electrical resistance running through its vascular systems. In the center of these groves, huge, rubbery-petalled flowers hang heavy and motionless, their surfaces coated in a fine, dry powder of metallic salts and silica. Because there is no wind and no moisture, nothing rots. When a leaf dies, it does not wither or brown; it simply vitrifies, turning into a brittle, translucent amber glass that remains attached to the stem.

Nothing moves. There is no fauna here—no insects buzzing among the heavy, chemical blossoms, no birds riding the thermals of the high ridges, no lizards darting across the warm stone. The absence of animal life gives the landscape a profound, museum-like stillness. The air does not stir. The shadows cast by the sharp rocks and the lush, clustered groves are crisp and black, and they do not seem to lengthen. 

If you stand on one of the upper basalt shelves, you can see the whole of the basin at once through the crystal-clear, desiccated air. Miles away, another lush grove spills down a stepped cliff face like a frozen green waterfall, tethered to the gray cylinder of a voltage regulator. Between here and there lies only the vast, pristine floor of broken rock, glinting in the steady light, crossed by the occasional dark line of an iron conduit. 

The only sound in the entire world is the faint, collective drone of the transformers—a soft, velvety vibration that you feel in your teeth more than you hear—and, every few minutes, the tiny, dry *tick* of a newly forming crystal cooling in the rock, or a vitrified leaf expanding slightly in the warm sun. It is a landscape locked in a permanent, sunlit equilibrium: half raw planetary crust, half open-air power plant, feeding a silent, thriving jungle that drinks only light and electricity.
