The vault is roughly the size of an overturned barge, wedged three hundred feet down in a freshly sheared fault of black anhydrite and brittle, unweathered hornfels. Because there is no water down here—not a bead of condensation, not a trace of dampness in the stone—the rock faces are as sharp as the day the slip-fault opened. Flakes of black stone still cling to the walls by static charge alone, their edges keen enough to split a thumbnail. 

The air is warm, close, and smells sharply of hot transformer oil, dry zinc, and ozone. It is a thick atmosphere, crowded with suspended mica dust that never quite settles because of the noise.

The noise is the architecture of the room. Halfway up the eastern wall, a fat, braided trunk of gutta-percha cabling emerges from a drilled borehole and feeds into a single squat housing of cast iron, bolted directly into the raw rock with sulfur cement. Inside that housing, an induction coil the size of a hogshead steps down a massive, invisible current coming from somewhere much deeper. The transformer does not merely hum; it sings a deafening, sixty-cycle baritone that turns the entire rock chamber into a resonating chest. The sound has no echo because the space is too small and too full; instead, it creates standing waves of air pressure. Moving two feet to the left makes the teeth ache; moving two feet to the right makes the eardrums flutter.

Light comes only from the leakages of this system. Three jury-rigged junction boxes hang from iron pitons driven into the ceiling cracks, their glass fronts fogged with static-clung dust. Inside them, mercury-arc rectifiers burn with a steady, dim, bruise-colored ultraviolet, while down near the floor, a bank of open-coil ballast resistors glows a dull, unchanging cherry red. The shadows thrown by this dual light are sharp-edged and double-cast—one rimmed in violet, the other in rust.

Nothing would live here if the chamber weren't so crowded with the things that eat the current.

Because the rock is fresh and the air is bone-dry, the ecology is entirely electrostatic and mineral. Every square inch of the jagged floor and the lower walls is packed with life, layered three and four deep, pressing inward toward the warmth of the ballast resistors and the vibration of the iron housing.

Closest to the rock are the sparse patches of wire-scrub—stiff, leafless thickets of a metallic epiphyte that roots directly into the iron pyrites in the hornfels. They look like tangles of tarnished copper wool, growing in crooked, right-angled spurts that follow the magnetic field lines of the room. They do not photosynthesize; they induce a tiny current from the air and use it to crack carbon from the dry atmosphere, shedding tiny, bone-dry flakes of graphite ash that collect in the sharp V-shaped troughs of the floor.

Over and through the wire-scrub, and piled six inches deep against the warm stone near the transformer, is the fauna. The chamber is choked with them. There are thousands of *shunt-ticks*—flat, coin-sized creatures with carapaces of layered mica and dielectric chitin. They stack themselves like poker chips into tall, swaying columns between the rock scars, their copper-bristled legs locked together to form living capacitors. When a column builds up enough static charge from the vibrating air, a tiny blue arc snaps down the stack, the ticks shudder in unison, and they digest the ozone.

Moving between the stacks are larger things: *commutator-worms*, thick as a man’s wrist and segmented like flexible metal conduit, dragging themselves over the sharp stone with a dry, rasping clatter that barely cuts through the bass roar of the transformer. They graze on the graphite ash and the dead, flaked shells of the ticks. Above them, clinging to the heavy gutta-percha cables and the rusted rims of the junction boxes, are dense clusters of *flash-moths*. They have four wings of translucent, brittle horn that they rub together in time with the sixty-cycle hum, generating static to comb the mica dust out of the air. They never fly—the space is too tight, the air too thick with their own bodies—so they simply crawl over one another in a slow, boiling carpet of amber and grey, their compound eyes reflecting the dim red of the resistors.

Here and there on the floor lie the scattered remains of the installation work: a wooden crate stenciled with faded lead paint that has been half-buried in shed mica shells; three porcelain insulators, unbroken, their glazed surfaces completely packed with the porcelain-white eggs of the commutator-worms; a heavy iron wrench leaning against a rock spur, now welded in place by the slow, crystalline creep of a zinc-Fixing lichen that grows at the rate of a millimeter a decade.

Despite the frantic, crowded density of the creatures and the roaring violence of the sound, the chamber is locked in a state of absolute, suspended permanence. There is no weather here. There is no day or night. The current coming down the cable never wavers by a single volt, and so the cherry-red glow of the resistors never brightens or dims. The heat is constant, bled off into the surrounding megatons of cold stone at the exact rate it is generated. 

Without water, nothing rots. When a flash-moth dies, it does not decay; it simply dries into a brittle husk of horn and trace copper, falls into the crevices, and is ground into clean powder by the rasping bellies of the worms, who re-incorporate the minerals into their own segments. The sharp edges of the broken rock do not round off; there is no wind or rain to soften them, and the creatures are too light to wear them down. 

If you were to stand in the narrow clearings between the tick-stacks, breathing the hot, metallic dust, your clothes lifting slightly from your skin with the static charge, you would see that even the motion of the animals is a kind of stillness. The moths crawl in closed loops around the cable insulation. The ticks fire their tiny blue sparks at precise, four-second intervals. The sixty-cycle roar presses against your chest like a warm, heavy hand, holding the dust, the bugs, the sharp black stone, and the dim violet light in the exact same configuration it held yesterday, and will hold until the distant, unseen dynamos finally wear through their bearings.
