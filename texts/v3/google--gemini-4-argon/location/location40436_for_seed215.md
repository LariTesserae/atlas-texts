The plateau is too young to have soil. Where the ground has been exposed for only a decade or two, the basalt is a sharp, unweathered charcoal-black, fractured into hexagonal stair-steps that still carry the glassy sheen of their cooling. You can walk for three miles across these flats and every footstep will ring with a hollow, ceramic clink, because the rock has not yet been ground down into sand or silt to muffle the boot. There are no rounded river stones here. Every edge is fresh enough to cut leather.

Yet the plateau is wet. At six thousand feet, the cold air holds a permanent, drifting silveriness—not quite fog, not quite rain, which collects on the sharp basalt facets and runs down them in thin, clear sheets. Because there are no carved riverbeds yet, the water doesn't know where to go. It pools in the geometric basins of the rock, spills over a sharp lip, drops three inches onto the next flat hexagon, and spreads out again, creating a thousand square miles of shallow, shivering mirrors that reflect a pale, high-altitude sky. 

Across this raw, wet expanse, the city is growing itself, though it does not group together the way older cities do. It is a sprawl of extreme distance. A single municipal district covers the horizon, composed of thin, towering needles and broad, cantilevered plates spaced half a mile apart. The buildings are not hauled here and assembled; they are extruded from the basalt itself through some quiet, molecular persuasion. At the base of the newer towers, you can see the transition where the wet, black volcanic stone seamlessly reorganizes its crystalline structure, rising out of the earth as a smooth, pearlescent ceramic that sheds water like wax. There are no cranes, no scaffolding, no dust. A foundation simply rises four inches a day, pushing up into the cold air in total silence.

Between the towers, the infrastructure of the city hangs without visible support. Transit corridors—ribbons of matte-gray material only two fingers thick—arch gracefully across the half-mile gaps, held rigid by internal tension fields that hum only if you press your ear directly against them. Every few minutes, a silver pod slides along the underside of a ribbon, moving at two hundred miles an hour with no sound other than a brief, soft displacement of the wet air, like a silk sheet being shaken out. 

Because the city takes up so little of the ground it claims, the space between the pylons belongs to the animals. And because the city’s silent infrastructure radiates a faint, ambient warmth from its subterranean power grids, the fauna of the high cold have gathered here in staggering numbers.

The water-flats are crowded with long-legged waders—birds the size of calves with slate-blue plumage and heavy, scissoring bills. They stand in the two-inch-deep geometric puddles by the tens of thousands, hunting the translucent, thumb-sized amphibians that breed in the warm runoff near the tower bases. Where the basalt steps rise into drier ridges, herds of heavy-shouldered, pale-furred ungulates move at a slow, steady walk. There are hundreds of them in a single column, their wide, padded hooves evolved for silence on bare stone. They graze on the only vegetation that has managed to take hold on the young rock: a sparse, wire-thin red scrub that grows directly out of the cracks, and thick, rubbery mats of black lichen that drink the condensing mist.

The animals treat the hyper-modern architecture as simple topography. A pack of long-bodied, silver-pelted predators—something between a wolf and a marten—uses the cantilevered roof of a subterranean data-vault as a windbreak, sleeping in a tangled heap on the warm, pearl-colored alloy while the mist beads on their guard hairs. High above, where the residential needles pierce the low clouds, migratory flocks of white-winged raptors roost along the sensor arrays. The buildings automatically adjust their surface tension to give the birds grip, tiny microscopic ridges rising under their talons and smoothing out again the moment they take flight.

Everything is in motion, yet the silence is immense. The cold air and the sheer scale of the plateau swallow small noises. There is no roar of traffic, no industrial rumble, no wind-rustle of forests because there are no trees. When a herd of three thousand pale grazers shifts direction, startled by the shadow of a hunting pack, you do not hear a stampede; you hear only a vast, papery shuffling, like dry leaves sliding across a slate roof, accompanied by the rhythmic puffing of their breath steaming in the chill. 

The weather moves across the urban basin in visible, majestic curtains. Ten miles to the west, between the distant needles of the water-processing district, a grey squall will drop from the sky, darkening the rock and turning the shallow basins into a silver froth of ripples. Twenty minutes later, the squall passes directly overhead—a brisk, icy downpour that drums briefly against the matte transit ribbons and the broad backs of the grazing herds—before sliding east, leaving the air washed clean and sharp. The pale sun comes back out, striking the wet sides of the half-grown towers, turning them into blinding white lines against the dark, dripping staircase of the earth.
