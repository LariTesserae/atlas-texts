The cistern-ward is no larger than an abbey nave, though it is difficult to judge the boundaries because the water and the machinery leave no empty space through which to look. Everything here is submerged in warm, mineral-heavy fresh water that tastes faintly of copper and peat. Only at the very top of the vaulted brick ceiling does a shallow, three-inch lens of hot, stagnant air remain, trapped between the masonry arches like mercury in a level. Below that slick of damp air, the world is entirely liquid, crowded to the point of suffocation with the apparatus of a drowned industrial borough and the thick, heat-loving flora that has colonized its every surface.

The water is the temperature of blood. That warmth comes from the engines—not because they are running at full stroke, for they have not run at speed in generations, but because they are geothermal and galvanic, drawing a slow, relentless trickle of heat and current from the deep limestone strata below the ward’s floor. Enormous cast-iron condenser vats, bundled brass heat-exchangers, and vertical turbine housings stand shoulder to shoulder like tenements in a walled city. Catwalks of perforated iron plate twist between them in three and four tiers, so closely stacked that a swimmer cannot rise vertically from the silt floor to the ceiling vaults without threading through a labyrinth of railings, valve wheels, and riveted flanged pipes.

Nothing is bare metal anymore, yet nothing is ruined. The erosion here has reached a heavy, velvet equilibrium. Every rivet head is rounded under a rind of dark green calcium-algal felt; every iron stanchion is sheathed in mineral scale that has bonded with the metal to prevent further rust. The boundary between the manufactured and the grown has blurred into a single dense fabric.

Because there is no sunlight fifty feet below the surface clay, the lushness of the ward relies on the machinery itself. Thick, cabbage-like rosettes of blind-kelp cluster around the warm brass manifolds, drinking the dissolved iron and sulfur that weep from the gaskets. From the undersides of the catwalks hang curtains of translucent, rust-colored bladder-ferns, their roots wedged into the bolt-holes of the ironwork, their fronds swaying in the imperceptible convective drift of the warm water. Where the insulated power cables run in bundled troughs along the walls, a distinct species of pale, waxy creeper has taken hold, feeding directly on the weak electromagnetic fields and the faint warmth of the gutta-percha sheathing. The plants press in upon the narrow alleys between the boilers until the passageways are mere tunnels through rubbery leaves and calcified pipe-work.

Light is scarce, sourceless, and amber-green. It does not fall from above; it leaks from within the tangle. Scattered throughout the packed infrastructure are old industrial gauge-clusters and heavy glass inspection ports bolted into the sides of the vats. Inside those pressure vessels, slow electrochemical reactions still turn over, casting a dim, tallowy glow through glass panes three inches thick and crusted with bryozoans. Elsewhere, where copper bus-bars have corroded into blue-green paste, colonies of galvanic moss emit a steady, sickly chartreuse luminescence. You never see more than ten feet ahead; you see only the silhouette of a five-foot gear wheel choked with vallisneria, and beyond it, the vague, glowing smudge of a submerged streetlamp or an indicator dial whose needle has not moved since the winter before last.

The ward is not dead—the machinery is still operating, though on a timescale that mocks the frantic gauges of the surface world. The pistons of the great vertical pumps still travel their cylinders, driven by pressure differentials in the deep rock, but a single stroke takes four hours to complete. You do not see them move. You only notice, if you linger by a crosshead guide, that a tiny crescent of bright, un-scaled steel is exposed at the base of the shaft, and that a curtain of water-moss has been pulled taut. Every twenty minutes or so, a brass relief valve somewhere in the packed gloom reaches its threshold and lets go a single, silvery globe of marsh gas or warm steam. The bubble rises lazily, flattening like a jellyfish as it squeezes between the leaves of the water-ferns, nudging against the iron grating overhead with a dull, muffled *thrum* before joining the thin skin of air at the ceiling.

That is almost the only sound. Water is a ruthless damper of noise, and the thick mats of vegetation swallow whatever vibrations remain. There is no roar of industry here—only an encompassing, heavy hush, punctuated at long intervals by the low *clink* of a thermal expansion joint settling by a fraction of a millimeter, or the deep, sub-audible hum that lives in the ribs and teeth rather than the ears, vibrating out of the dynamo casings.

Life moves at the pace of the pistons. In the warm, dim alleyways between the turbines, animal presence is rare and solitary. A few blind, translucent crayfish—the size of a man’s forearm and sluggish with the heat—spend days crawling the length of a single copper pipe, grazing on the galvanic moss with porcelain-white claws. Occasionally, an eel-like lungfish, mottled to match the rusted iron and the dark kelp, slips out from the firebox of a cold boiler, hangs suspended in the amber gloom of an inspection port, and then slides away into the dense brake of ferns without disturbing a single frond.

The entire chamber feels like an overcrowded conservatory that has been sealed inside a watchcase and dropped to the bottom of a warm well—every cubic inch spoken for by iron, brass, leaf, and water, all of it locked in a patient, noiseless metabolism that needs nothing from the world above.
