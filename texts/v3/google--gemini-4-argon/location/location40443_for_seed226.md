The settlement occupies a single, sky-facing box of newly split basalt, no wider than a town square and four hundred feet deep. High up in the thin, chill air, the mountain simply parted along a grid of geometric fault lines, dropping a central plug of stone into the dark and leaving behind four vertical walls of razor-sharp, unweathered rock. There is no talus at the bottom, no rounded edges, no river-worn gravel—only fresh, dark grey faces of hexagonal stone that look as though they were quarried yesterday, though the split is centuries slow in its widening. 

Above, a rectangle of pale, cold daylight pours straight down into the hollow. The middle of the space is entirely empty: a clear column of cool air where nothing grows and nothing is built. 

Everything clings to the perimeter. 

Because the rock is newly fractured, it bleeds groundwater from a thousand horizontal seams. The walls are slick with a continuous, cold sweat that never quite gathers into a waterfall, but drops instead in a relentless, heavy rain of large, slow beads. To live here, the builders have turned the four cliff faces into a vertical town of lashed timber, bone-pegged scaffolding, and dry-laid stone shelves, leaving the vast central shaft of the chimney completely vacant. You can stand on a narrow balcony of split pine on the north wall and look across two hundred feet of nothingness to the south wall, where every terrace, ladder, and lintel is visible in the crisp mountain light.

Yet the stonework and timber are barely visible beneath the green. Despite the bite in the air, the constant seepage of mineral-rich water and the shelter from the high-altitude winds have turned the vertical town into a hanging bog-forest. Every boardwalk, roof-thatch, and stone corbel is buried three feet deep in dense, cold-weather flora. There are cushions of bright emerald sphagnum that swallow a boot to the calf; thick curtains of pale, rubbery liverworts hanging from the undersides of the walkways; and forests of giant, translucent-stemmed ferns that lean out over the drop to catch the shaft of daylight. Where a builder wedged a rough slate gutter into a rock seam to divert water toward a cistern, the gutter has become a hanging garden of black-stemmed sedges and waxy, frost-hardy bells that drip continuously into the abyss.

The sound is immense, disproportionate to the smallness of the place. The four raw, flat walls of unweathered basalt act as an echo chamber with no soft soil to deaden the acoustics. Every heavy drop of water falling two hundred feet onto a broad fern leaf or a wooden plank snaps like a small whip; multiplied by ten thousand drops a minute, it becomes a continuous, roaring clatter, like a hailstorm trapped in a stone barrel. When someone on the eastern ledges strikes a stone chisel with a wooden mallet, or drops a water-bucket, the crack slams back and forth between the walls for seconds, overlapping with the roar of the seepage until the air itself feels percussive.

For all the noise and the crowding of the green along the walls, the place is extraordinarily still in its rhythms. The air in the shaft does not swirl; it hangs heavy and damp, smelling of wet slate, crushed fern-oil, and cold woodsmoke that rises in straight, ruler-thin lines from clay braziers before vanishing into the bright sky above. There are no flocks of birds cutting through the central void—only the occasional, solitary grey-feathered creeper working its way up a timber upright like a mouse, or a pale, flat-bodied salamander resting motionless for days in the cold trickle of a carved stone spout. 

The architecture is constantly, slowly adjusting to the mountain's embryonic shift. Because the fault is still parting—a fraction of an inch every few years—the rock faces occasionally let out a low, subterranean *thrum* that vibrates through the soles of the feet, followed by the sharp *tink* of a new hairline crack appearing in the basalt. The inhabitants build with this in mind. Spanning beams are never mortised rigid; they rest on greased stone rollers or hang in heavy slings of braided fibrous bark, creeping millimeter by millimeter out of their sockets over decades. Everything is lashed, wedged, and counterweighted with rough boulders hung in nets of vine, suspended out in the open air of the shaft like plumb bobs, dripping water and slowly turning green.
