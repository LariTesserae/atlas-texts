The place is a slumped, box-shaped collapse in a wider plain of grey clay—no wider than a dry-dock and perhaps forty feet deep, where an underground seam of heated brine gave way and dropped a section of the surface straight down. Because the drop happened recently, the walls haven’t softened into slopes; they are vertical, sheared faces of dark, greasy shale that still show the pale, jagged scratch-marks of the rocks that scraped past them on the way down. There is no horizon here, only the sharp, rectangular lip of the pit forty feet up, framing a flat, bright rectangle of sky that pours noon light straight down into the congestion at the bottom.

There is no room to walk on the floor of the sink. The collapse exposed three thick, cast-iron municipal or industrial pipes that had been buried forty feet deep, and the fall snapped two of them. They don’t gush water; they bleed a thick, warm, mineral-heavy condensate that smells of hot pennies and sheep fat. Around these iron stubs, the narrow floor is choked to the point of suffocation with a dense, interlocking lattice of fresh travertine shelving, rusted iron tension-rods from some collapsed surface gantry, and thousands of pale, cabbage-sized stone-bladders.

These bladders are the dominant presence. They are neither fully animal nor entirely geological—leathery, hexagonal sacs that grow directly out of the freshly sheared shale and the warm iron of the pipes, packed so tightly against one another that their sides flatten into honeycomb facets. They inflate slowly with the warm gas leaking from the lower fissures, swelling over twenty minutes until their skins are taut and translucent, showing a milky, veined interior, before a small sphincter at the top of each sac twitches open and lets out a wet, barely audible *phut* of warm steam. Then the sac collapses into a wrinkled fist, and the adjacent three swell into the vacated space. Because the pit is packed from wall to wall with them, the entire floor is in a state of slow, silent, rhythmic jostling, like a crate full of breathing lungs.

Threaded through the minimal gaps between the bladders are the remnants of the monitoring station that fell in with the dirt: a single, galvanized-steel instrument hutch crushed to the width of a suitcase, its dials still intact behind cracked glass; five or six heavy porcelain insulators sitting upright in the mud like large white teeth; and fifty yards of braided copper wire that the growing travertine has already half-swallowed, turning the wire into rigid, calcified ropes that bridge the pit three feet off the ground.

The heat stays trapped at the bottom, thick and damp as a laundry room, though there is no standing pool of water—only a constant, slick sweat that coats the fresh stone faces and collects in the dimples of the bladders. In this warmth, the fauna is thick and busy, operating in the two-inch gaps between the heaving sacs.

There are no birds—the pit is too narrow and steep for them to drop into—Wait, no: there are the *tin-ticks*, which are flat, saucer-sized crustaceans with shells the exact dull grey of galvanized steel. They don’t scurry; they hitch themselves along the calcified copper wires and the flanks of the iron pipes with a slow, ratcheting motion, grazing on the yellow sulfur-slime that weeps from the pipe joints. When two meet on a wire, they don't fight; one simply climbs directly over the back of the other, their serrated legs making a dry, metallic *click-click-click* that is the loudest sound in the hole.

Down in the dark, warm creases between the bladders live the *grease-eels*—blind, thumb-thick amphibians with skin like wet chamois leather. They never touch the dirt; they live entirely in the slick, warm lube between the rubbing skins of the gas-sacs, sliding from crease to crease as the bladders inflate and deflate, hunting the white, bristle-legged mites that congregate around the gas-sphincters. Every few minutes, a bladder deflates too rapidly and pinches an eel between two calcified rims; the eel doesn't thrash, just inflates its own throat-sac to wedge the stones apart, waits for the next cycle of inflation to loosen the vice, and slips deeper into the pack.

Vegetation has barely figured out how to use the place, as the rock is too raw and the bladders take up all the horizontal real estate. What grows here is strictly vertical and opportunistic: a dozen stiff, yard-high stalks of "joint-grass" that look like oxidized copper tubing, sprouting directly out of the greasy felt packing of the broken pipe flanges. They have no leaves, only rings of tiny, black, wet bristles at each node that trap the rising steam. Here and their, where a porcelain insulator has caught a pocket of rainwater and dead mites, a mat of bright orange iron-moss hangs down like a wet beard, dripping warm, rust-colored water onto the bladders below.

The rules of this room are dictated by pressure and lack of clearance:

First, nothing stays clean or separate for more than twelve hours. The travertine is growing so fast from the mineral mist that a dead tin-tick is glued to the floor by morning and encased in a smooth, bone-colored bump by the end of the week. The old industrial gear isn't decaying; it’s being laminated into the biology. A heavy iron valve-wheel near the center of the pit is still turned by the slow, collective heaving of the bladders beneath it—ratcheting one millimeter to the left over three hours as the sacs swell, then slipping back when they vent, keeping the internal gears of the buried pipe greased and moving in the dark.

Second, the light changes the speed of the pit. In the morning and late afternoon, when the sun is low, the pit is in deep, blue-grey shadow, and the bladders slow down, holding their gas until they are hard as river cobbles; the eels drop to the bottom creases, and the ticks clamp their shells flush against the iron pipes, looking like bolt-heads. For two hours at midday, however, the sun hits the bottom directly. The wet shale glitters like anthracite, the temperature jumps ten degrees in minutes, and the entire floor begins to work at double time—thousands of pale sacs heaving, rubbing with a sound like wet rubber boots, venting little ghosts of steam that rise ten feet and vanish into the dry air above, while the copper wires hum faintly from the thermal expansion of the buried pipes they are still bolted to.

If you were to drop a wrench from the rim forty feet up, it wouldn't hit dirt. It would hit the tough, springy hide of a bladder with a dull *thump*, slide immediately into a two-inch cleft between three wet sacs, get wedged against a porcelain insulator by the next inflation cycle, and within forty-eight hours, be cemented into the warm, breathing masonry of the floor, while three tin-ticks sat on its handle, scraping off the machine oil.
