The water here does not fall; it stands. Set into the high, fractured collar of a young mountain pass, the basin is no wider than a cathedral nave, bounded by sheer walls of freshly split obsidian and raw, unweathered diorite. The stone is sharp enough to slice leather, its black and salt-and-pepper faces completely bare of moss, lichen, or soil. Yet the entire room-sized cleft is submerged beneath forty feet of water so clear, cold, and heavily mineralized that it acts like a magnifying lens for the glaring alpine sun. 

There is no silt. There are no reeds, no algae, no drifting green threads. The light pours straight down from the thin, high-altitude sky, refracting through the dense, frigid brine to strike the jagged talus on the basin floor with blinding clarity. Everything is sharply lit, shadowless and crisp, suspended in a medium that feels slightly thicker than ordinary mountain runoff, heavy with dissolved sulfates and native copper.

Despite the total absence of plants, the water is choked with life—and that life is entirely industrial.

The basin is a natural foundry, occupied from wall to wall by colonial, metallic fauna that have evolved the mechanisms of the nineteenth-century factory floor. Anchored to the razor-sharp ledges of the volcanic glass are massive, tiered reefs of *bellows-worms* and *piston-clams*. Their shells are not calcium, but biological cast iron and precipitated brass, smelting themselves out of the mineral-heavy water through slow, endothermic reactions. Every vertical inch of the submerged rock is claimed. Plated, segmented creatures the size of engine blocks sit bolted to the stone by byssal threads of spun copper wire.

Because the water is near-freezing, their metabolisms are glacial, yet their sheer numbers create an overwhelming, deafening wall of sound. Water is an ruthlessly efficient conductor of noise. To be submerged here is to stand inside the cylinder of a working locomotive. 

A *valve-sponge* the size of a hogshead takes four full minutes to compress its vulcanized, rubbery chambers, squeezing out a dense plume of deoxygenated brine—*shhhhhh-CLANK*—as its heavy bronze check-valves slam shut at the end of the stroke. Beside it, a hundred thousand *ratchet-shrimp*, each no larger than a thumb and cased in overlapping plates of tarnished silver, click their gear-toothed claws in slow, rhythmic unison to grind up suspended pyrite dust. The sound is a deafening, reverberating roar of clanking, winching, hissing, and metallic grinding that echoes endlessly between the narrow obsidian walls, vibrating straight through the sternum.

Nothing darts. Nothing flees. The ecology operates on the patient, unstoppable torque of low-gear machinery. 

Drifting through the middle column of water, so densely packed their greased carapaces constantly scrape and chime against one another, are the *flywheel-medusae*. They are heavy, bell-shaped organisms with internal rotors of dense bone and magnetite. They do not pulse; they spin their internal weights with a low, thrumming hum that vibratingly churns the cold water, generating weak electrical currents. Pale blue arcs of static electricity occasionally jump between the crowded medusae and the copper-threaded reefs, illuminating the clear water with the sharp *crack* of a spot-welder before vanishing in the bright sunlight.

Near the bottom of the cleft, where the freshly fractured boulders form narrow, shadowed trenches, the waste products of this living industry accumulate. There is no mud—only gleaming drifts of biological slag. Iron filings, tiny hexagonal nuts of discarded shell, and ribbons of shaved lead form sparkling, metallic dunes. Through these drifts crawl the *auger-snails*, slow-moving gastropods whose spiral shells are hardened drill bits. Over the course of days, they bore neat, threaded tap-holes into the raw diorite boulders, extracting trace silicates and leaving behind perfectly machined borings that vent tiny, rhythmic bubbles of hydrogen sulfide.

Upward, only a few yards above the highest tier of the reef, the surface of the water is a flat, brilliant mirror against the pale sky. A dry, cold mountain wind sweeps across the top of the pass, kicking up small, rapid chop that breaks the sunlight into dancing geometric nets across the submerged engines below. On the dry rock just inches above the waterline, there is nothing at all—only the whistling wind and raw, young stone. Beneath that razor-thin meniscus lies the total saturation of the machine-reef: bright, freezing, deafeningly loud, and grinding forward at one revolution per hour.
