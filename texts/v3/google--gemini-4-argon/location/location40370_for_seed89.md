Fourteen thousand feet above the plains, where the air should be thin and brittle with frost, a chimney of dark basalt opens just wide enough to swallow the wind. 

You do not find this place by looking at the mountain; from the outside, the peak is a sterile, sun-blasted monument of gray scree and frozen shale. You find it by the smell—a rich, ammoniacal musk that leaks through the high fissures—and by the heat that rises from the rock like breath from a sleeping throat.

Step down through the choked entry-slot, and the cold alpine glare vanishes within twenty paces. The chimney bells outward into a single, vaulted chamber roughly the size of an overturned hull, sealed away from the sky by a crooked elbow of stone. There are no sharp edges here. For ten thousand years, warm, sulfurous vapor sighing up from a dying magma seam deep in the mountain’s root has met the frigid air seeping down from the ridge. The rock has been licked smooth by the marriage of heat and condensation. Every bulge of basalt, every slanting pillar and low, overhanging brow is rounded, polished like river-stone, and permanently slick with a skin of warm sweat.

There is no standing water, no pool or trickle, only a relentless dampness. The air sits at the temperature of blood, thick enough to coat the tongue. Moisture beads on the ceiling, swells into heavy, greasy droplets, and falls—*plip, plip, plock*—onto the floor.

Or rather, onto what covers the floor. You rarely see the stone itself. 

The chamber is packed, wall to wall and floor to vault, with life that has crawled in from the freezing heights to drink the warmth. The first layer is a carpet of wingless, harvestman-like arachnids, their bodies the size of plums and the color of tallow, their long, hair-thin legs overlapping in a slow, undulating mat three inches deep. When a drop of water falls from the roof, a dozen of them tilt their bodies upward to catch the splash on their carapaces, drinking from one another’s backs. 

Above them, pressed into every dimple and flute of the rock walls, hang the gray-furred swifts and high-altitude bats, wedged together like roof tiles. They do not fly inside the chamber; there is no room to spread a wing without striking three neighbors. Instead, they crawl over one another with hooked thumbs and stiff tails, shifting in slow, rhythmic waves as those on the outside of the huddle work their way inward toward the warmer stone, and those against the rock push outward to breathe. 

The sound is immense, yet nothing here is fast. There is no frantic scrambling, no shrieking panic. The noise is the product of sheer, suffocating multiplicity. Millions of dry tarsal claws clicking against wet stone; the papery rustle of folded leathery wings shifting by millimeters; the high, continuous, vibrating hum of insects chewing; the wet, percussive *chitter-chitter-chitter* of a hundred thousand small throats breathing and clicking in the dark. The curved walls catch these tiny noises and roll them around the dome until they merge into a single, deafening roar, like gravel turning in the surf.

Light is a rumor here. High up in the crooked throat of the entry-slot, a single smudge of pale, silvery-gray shows where the sky lives, its glare diffused through three bounces of wet, black rock. By the time that light reaches the main floor, it is exhausted—just enough to catch the wet glint of an eye, the oily sheen of a beetle’s back, or the pale, fleshy stalks of the cave’s only greenery. These are stunted, leafless cushions of *bryophytes* and liverworts that cling to the highest ledges where the bat dung isn’t too thick, drinking the faint scatter of ultraviolet light that filters down the flue. Beneath them, blooming out of the deep drifts of guano that choke the floor, are pale, rubbery tubers—plants that gave up on the sun epochs ago, feeding instead on the rot and the heat, extruding sticky, foul-smelling droplets that draw the blind cave-crickets in droves.

Nothing is built here, yet the chamber is not untouched. In the deepest, warmest recess, where the basalt forms a low bench over the main thermal vent, the guano has been cleared away in a rough semicircle. 

Arranged along the lip of the stone are three dozen mountain goat skulls and several heavy, mineralized thigh bones from the giant vultures of the peaks. They have not washed in; the entry is too narrow. They have been placed. Each bone has been split lengthwise with a heavy cobblestone to get at the marrow, and then wedged into the cracks of the rock to form a low, rudimentary retaining wall. Behind this wall of greasy bone, someone—or something—has raked together a deep nest of shed feathers, dried moss from the lower valleys, and the molted husks of the giant arachnids. 

Resting on a flat boulder beside the nest are two tools: a fist-sized nodule of black flint, crudely flaked on one side to make a chopper, and a hollowed-out horn core, plugged at the narrow end with pine resin and filled with rendered fat. A twist of braided moss sits in the fat, charred black at the tip, though it is unlit now. Whatever uses this hollow is out on the scree slopes, or perhaps buried deep under the mat of feathers and warm bugs, sleeping through the afternoon. 

It does not rule this room; it merely shares the heat. Even now, while the bone-lined hollow lies empty, the slow tide of blind beetles and long-legged spiders has crept over the flint chopper and the horn lamp, covering the worked edges in a living, breathing, clicking fur that rises and falls like a single chest in the dark.
