The chamber is roughly forty feet square and suspended six thousand feet above the planetary datum, bolted into an exterior notch near the crown of Vent-Stack Nine. There is no rock here, no soil, no natural substrate within a vertical mile. The floor is a continuous grid of stamped zinc-alloy grating, its diamond-patterned teeth still factory-sharp, unworn by footsteps. Touching the metal with bare skin results in immediate frost-adhesion; the ambient temperature sits twenty degrees below the freezing point of water, kept at an absolute thermal plateau by the cryogenic draw of the main condensers behind the bulkhead.

It is not dark. The space is open at the top to a thin, pale-cobalt sky, and the high-altitude sun pours down into the box with the crisp, shadow-carving clarity of a floodlamp. The light strikes the unpainted aluminum louver-fins that make up the four walls. Because the air is entirely desiccated—not a molecule of vapor survives the altitude and the industrial scrubbers—there is no frost, no rime, and no rust. The stamped serial numbers on the structural I-beams look as though they were cold-pressed ten minutes ago, their edges razor-keen and bright.

What defines the chamber, before sight or cold, is the sound. 

It is not a rhythm, because a rhythm implies change. It is a continuous, solid wall of acoustic pressure generated by the planetary atmosphere-reduction turbines directly behind the north bulkhead. The sound is a two-hundred-hertz roar braided with a metallic shriek at the very top of the register, so loud that it ceases to be something heard and becomes a structural property of the air. It vibrates the eyeballs in their sockets, blurs vision slightly at the edges, and packs the chest cavity with a heavy, thrumming weight. A shouted word here does not travel; it is erased at the lips. Yet nothing in the room moves in response to it. The bolts do not rattle; the heavy-gauge cables hung in neat catenaries along the eastern wall do not sway. The vibration is so high-frequency and uniform that the entire steel architecture seems locked in a rigid, screaming trance.

In this dry, deafening freezer, something grows.

Spaced ten or twelve feet apart across the zinc grating—leaving wide, clean expanses of empty metal between them—sit low, spiky bushes that look as though they were spun from dark copper wire and green glass. They have no roots; instead, their bases are fused directly to the zinc diamonds by pads of crusty, yellow sulfur-salt. These are piezo-flora. They require neither water nor warmth, feeding exclusively on the mechanical vibration of the decking and the fierce ultraviolet glare of the high sky. Their branches are brittle, geometric, and faceted, growing in ninety-degree increments that mimic the industrial lattice around them. If you were to snap a twig, it would break like a ceramic rod, revealing a dry, powdery marrow of zinc-oxide and carbon.

Because there is no wind inside the baffled alcove—only the motionless, compressed density of the noise—the plants never sway. They stand like welded sculptures in the clear sunlight, casting sharp, violet-tinted shadows across the silver floor. Between them, the only other features are three knee-high pressure-relief manifolds: stubby cylinders of cast iron painted a dull, matte ochre, each topped with a brass dial whose needle is pinned permanently against the red peg at the far right of the glass. 

Nothing crawls across the grating. No insects ride the updrafts this high up the stack; no birds can survive the acoustic shear zone that surrounds the tower for half a mile in every direction. There is no dust, because there is nothing to erode. The sun moves across the square opening overhead, dragging the sharp shadows of the louver-fins across the floor at a glacial pace, while the shriek of the turbines holds every atom of metal, air, and glass-leafed scrub in an unchanging, violent lock.
