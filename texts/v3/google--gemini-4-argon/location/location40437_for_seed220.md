The chamber is roughly the size of an old railway terminus, buried four hundred meters beneath the crust, though there is no rock anywhere in sight. Every boundary—the high arched ceiling, the sweeping terraced walls, the broad basin of the floor—is composed of a dense, dark-gray metamaterial that has been worn smooth over thousands of years. Originally cast with the sharp, microscopic precision of post-industrial architecture, the surfaces have been softened by millennia of thermal expansion and the slow creep of condensation. Balconies and housing alcoves still line the upper tiers like the hollowed-out facade of a theater, their geometric lintels drooped and rounded like wax held too close to a flame. Between these perimeter walls, there is nothing at all. The interior is a single, unbroken volume of hot, dark air.

You feel the chamber before you see it. The air is thick, hovering around thirty-five degrees Celsius, and it vibrates. The sound here does not echo so much as it lives in the space, a continuous, heavy thrumming that sits in the chest cavity. It comes from the deep-mantle pressure regulators embedded behind the eastern wall—machines that rely on principles of spatial folding rather than pistons or turbines. Every forty seconds, the system dumps excess kinetic energy into the structural hull. The result is a rising, brassy groan that builds until the air itself feels solid, followed by a sharp, concussive *thwack* like a wet sail snapping in a gale, and then a long, hissing exhale.

That exhale drives the room’s micro-climate. With each release, a wave of warm, damp air rolls across the dark basin, colliding with the slightly cooler ridges of the upper terraces. Moisture beads instantly on the worn polymer-stone. It gathers in the shallow, eroded runnels of old staircases and utility conduits, trickling downward in warm, oily ribbons that never quite form a stream, just a series of slick, shifting puddles at the lowest point of the floor. 

It is nearly pitch black. The only illumination comes from the walls themselves, and only when they are stressed. As the acoustic waves from the regulators roll through the architecture, the old, hyper-dense utility veins running beneath the surface briefly fluoresce. A pale, bruised-violet light ripples across the terraces in synchronization with the sound—a fleeting topographic map of glowing lines that reveals the curve of a worn balustrade, the hollow socket of a doorway, a slick patch of wet floor, before fading back into absolute black. 

In the brief flashes of violet, the vegetation becomes visible. It is sparse, clinging only to the intersections where the glowing conduits cross the dampness of the drainage grooves. These are wide, flat, leathery fronds—more like sheets of dark cartilage than leaves—that lie flush against the warm stone to absorb the brief bursts of photonic energy and the steady heat of the walls. From the edges of these dark mats grow tall, hair-thin stalks tipped with silvery bulbs. When the forty-second pressure wave hits, these stalks whip violently in the dark, casting microscopic spores into the warm updrafts.

A few creatures navigate this loud, empty dark. In the slick puddles at the bottom of the basin live flat, translucent hexapods the size of dinner plates. They move slowly over the wet metamaterial, grazing on the film of metallic silt and fallen spores, their bodies flattening completely against the floor whenever the pressure-crack rolls overhead. 

Higher up, clinging to the rounded eaves of the empty housing tiers, are the gliders. They are rare—perhaps four or five in the entire volume of the hall—resembling long, ribless kites of black skin with hooked limbs at their corners. They are entirely blind, navigating by the acoustic topography of the room. When the regulator's hum reaches its crescendo, just before the *thwack*, a glider will drop from its perch into the black void of the central space. It rides the resulting shockwave of hot air across the hall, banking silently through the brief violet web of light, its wide mouth-slit open to filter the airborne spores, before slapping wetly onto a terrace on the far side to wait out the lull.

Between the forty-second cycles, the heavy drone drops to a low, subterranean murmur. In those thirty seconds of relative quiet, you can hear the secondary sounds of the place: the rapid *tick-tick-tick* of cooling metal that isn't quite metal, the slow, warm drip of condensation falling fifty meters from an upper promenade to slap against the basin floor, and the dry, leathery rustle of a glider pulling itself up into the dark recess of an ancient doorway. Then the floor begins to hum again, the purple veins in the walls begin to ache with light, and the heat presses in.
