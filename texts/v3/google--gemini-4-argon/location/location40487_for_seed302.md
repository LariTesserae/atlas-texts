The valley is four miles across and has not changed its volume, its temperature, or its noise level in ten thousand years. 

It sits high in the thin, frigid air, a tilted basin of pale, blue-grey karst and milky quartz. Though the sky above is an arid, cobalt dome, the floor of the basin is a labyrinth of stone packed so tightly that a body cannot pass through it upright. This is the Fluted Reach: a forest not of wood or leaf, but of crowded, shoulder-to-shoulder mineral spires, weathered fins, and perforated limestone needles, each standing five to twenty feet high, separated from its neighbors by gaps barely the width of a hand.

There is no soil here, no moss, no green stain of lichen on the rock. The world is entirely mineral and water. 

Up at the basin’s western rim, where the rock meets the blinding sky, a subterranean river of cold, snow-melt water breaches the surface along a three-mile fault line. It does not crash down in a single waterfall; instead, it sheets over the lip and immediately fractures into the stone maze. Every spire is grooved with vertical runnels—rillenkarren—worn smooth and settled into their final, balanced shapes ages ago. Water coats every vertical surface in a slick, continuous glaze. It runs down the flutes, pools in shallow alabaster basins the size of dinner plates, spills over their lips, and drops through narrow stone throats into the dark, honeycombed conduits beneath the floor. 

Because the stone is wet and rich in mica and pale calcite, and because the high-altitude sun beats down through an atmosphere scrubbed of dust, the light is punishing. Every rounded pillar and razor-edged fin is smeared with a skin of water that catches the sun and splinters it. Looking across the top of the labyrinth is like staring into a welding arc or a field of broken mirrors. Even down within the tight clefts between the pillars, the light bounces endlessly from wet white wall to wet white wall, eliminating shadows, filling the fractures with a harsh, shadowless, silvery glare.

And then there is the sound.

The air moves down from the high peaks in a steady, unvarying draft—neither gusting nor dying, just a relentless, cool flow pressurized by the mountain geometry above. When this steady wind enters the millions of wet, perforated stone pillars, the entire valley becomes an organ. The rock is riddled with wind-worn eyelets, flutes, and hollow tubes. At the same time, billions of droplets are falling into billions of resonant stone cups, and subterranean siphons beneath the crust are sucking air and water down into the mountain's belly. 

The result is not the babble of a brook or the whistle of a breeze, but a total, physical wall of noise. It is a deafening, multi-tonal roar—a combination of a high-pitched harmonic shriek from the stone flutes and a deep, hydraulic thrumming that vibrates through the soles of the feet. The sound is completely static. It does not rise or fall. It is a solid chord of white noise and whistling stone that erases all other auditory information. If a boulder were to crack, you would not hear it; if you screamed, your throat would tear before the sound reached your own ears.

Nothing erodes here anymore, not at a rate that matters. The water carries just enough dissolved calcium carbonate to redeposit on the lip of the cups what it wears away from the grooves, holding the terrain in a state of loud, wet, shimmering stasis.

Life is present, though it looks like part of the geology. Without plants to anchor a food chain, the rare creatures here feed on the invisible: chemotrophic bacterial slicks that grow in the cold, mineral-heavy film of the water, and the sparse organic dust blown up from the lowlands leagues away and trapped by the wet stone. 

In the blinding glare between the spires, pale, blind amphibians the color of lard cling to the vertical runnels with wide, suctioned bellies. They have no ears—ears would be useless, even damaging, in the acoustic pressure of the Reach. Instead, they navigate by the temperature gradients of the water and the vibration of the stone. Occasionally, a dark, long-legged arachnid, its carapace coated in hydrophobic hairs that make it look like a bead of mercury, steps carefully across the razor-tops of the fins, hunting for springtails blown in from the lower world and stunned by the noise.

To cross the valley, one cannot walk. One must chimney laterally through the glare, wedging knees and elbows between wet, freezing stone walls that vibrate like the throat of a roaring animal, squinted eyes streaming from the light, moving inch by inch through a crowded wilderness of singing rock that never rests, never warms, and never goes dark until the sun drops flat behind the western ridge.
