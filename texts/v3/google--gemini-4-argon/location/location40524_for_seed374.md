The valley sits low in a shallow volcanic basin, where the earth is still cooling and the stone has not yet known a single century of weather. Everything here is sharp. The ground is a sheet of newly solidified basalt and obsidian, frozen in the middle of its flow—ropes of black rock, fractured columns, and glass-edged shelves that have never been rounded by wind or river. There is no soil, no sand, no silt. When a piece of rock breaks, it breaks with a fresh, conchoidal snap, leaving an edge that can slice leather. 

Over this raw, dark foundation, a city has been wedged and stacked, stretching three miles from one rim of the depression to the other. It is an immense, crowded labyrinth of dry-stone masonry. Millions of slabs of pried basalt have been levered up from the cooling crust and laid without mortar, fitted together by hand and stone maul. Because the rock breaks in flat, geometric planes, the buildings are angular, heavy, and starkly vertical. They cluster shoulder-to-shoulder—square towers, stepped terraces, low-roofed cisterns, and narrow, shadowed causeways barely wide enough for two people to pass. There are no arches, no domes, no metal girders, and no timber beams, for there is not a single tree or blade of grass within fifty miles. Roofs are made by corbeling flat stones inward until they meet, creating dark, cool interiors that smell of wet flint and tallow.

The light is a permanent, shadowless pewter. A thick ceiling of warm fog and thermal steam hangs just above the highest rooftops, trapped in the basin by the heavy air. The sun never breaks through as a disk; it merely turns the overcast sky from charcoal to a dull, pearlescent grey, like light filtering through horn. Because the stone is black and always wet, the city gleams with a low, greasy luster. 

Water is everywhere, yet there is no rain and no river. The moisture rises from beneath. Warm, mineral-heavy vapor hisses continuously from thousands of narrow vents and cooling cracks in the valley floor, condensing the moment it touches the slightly cooler stone of the buildings. Every wall weeps. Thick beads of warm water gather on the underside of lintels, run down the sharp facets of the masonry, and collect in shallow, geometric basins pecked into the basalt plazas. The streets are slick with a millimeter-thin film of warm runoff that never dries and never deepens.

Nothing green grows in the wet crevices. Instead, the moisture feeds a pale, translucent velvet of thermophilic slime and crustose lichens—organisms so simple they barely register as more than a mineral stain, slick as soap under a bare foot. 

What the city lacks in flora, it makes up for in flesh and chitin. The warmth, the wet stone, and the endless vertical surfaces have drawn an staggering density of animal life into the urban grid. Every ledge, parapet, and window-slit is packed with warm-bodied, cliff-nesting swifts and heavy, flightless water-fowl with webbed, leathery feet tough enough to walk on volcanic glass. Beneath them, the weeping walls are armored in overlapping layers of freshwater limpets, thumb-sized basalt snails, and pale, blind rock-crabs that sidle through the drainage channels by the millions, feeding on the bacterial slime and the droppings of the birds above. 

Because of them, the air is a solid wall of noise. The sound does not rise and fall with the hours; it is a continuous, deafening roar that vibrates in the teeth. Millions of birds shriek, clack their heavy bills, and beat their wings in the narrow stone canyons, their cries amplified and bounced endlessly between the hard, wet facets of the buildings. Beneath the avian din is the ceaseless, dry-stone rattle of millions of crab claws scraping against fresh basalt, and beneath that, the deep, pressurized *shhhhhh* of steam venting from the earth. To speak to someone an arm’s length away requires shouting into their ear.

The human presence is woven tightly into this loud, wet permanence. The people move through the dim alleys wrapped in oiled skins and woven bird-gut capes, carrying bone-headed hammers, flint knives, and baskets braced with whalebone or Split-horn. Their work is slow, repetitive, and entirely absorbed into the equilibrium of the place. They do not farm; they harvest. They scrape the grey lichen into stone troughs, pry snails from the lower courses of their own houses, and net the birds from low towers built specifically as rookeries. Smoke from fires fueled by dried bird-fat and dung rises lazily from roof-holes, immediately flattening out against the low ceiling of mist, adding a heavy, musky grease to the wet air.

Nothing here erodes, and nothing progresses. The water is too gentle to carve the rock; it only wets it. The temperature does not drop at night, held steady by the geothermal floor and the insulating blanket of cloud. The city does not expand or decay—when a dry-stone wall shifts, hands immediately lever the sharp blocks back into place. The noise never abates, the light never clears, and the warm water continues to bead on the black glass, trickling down through the crowded, deafening dark.
