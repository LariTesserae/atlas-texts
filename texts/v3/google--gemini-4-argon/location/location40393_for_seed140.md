The place is the size of an abbey’s nave, though it has no roof to catch the sky, nor any sky to catch. It sits high up inside the shoulder of an exhausted mountain range—so high that outside, the air is thin and cold, yet here inside the rock, the world has settled into a permanent, sealed fever. 

To understand the space, one has to understand the stone before the things that live on it. The rock is so old it has lost its grain. Billions of years of slow tectonic compression and chemical weeping have turned the walls and the dish-shaped floor into something resembling tallow or wet soapstone. There are no sharp edges anywhere. Boulders that fell from the ceiling epochs ago have slumped into low, rounded domes, their boundaries blurred into the floor as if they had partially melted. Everything is worn down to its final, most stable geometry: the curve, the trough, the shallow basin. 

Despite this geological exhaustion, the cavity is almost entirely hollow. The lushness here does not fill the room; it clings to the perimeter like a thick, wet pelt, leaving the central volume of air completely empty. Because there is no light—not a crack to the surface, not a single bioluminescent spore—the emptiness in the middle of the chamber feels vast and false, a pocket of warm nothingness suspended miles above sea level. 

The warmth comes from the artifacts. 

Scattered across the smoothed floor and half-sunk into the stone walls are five or six structures, none larger than a cistern or a way-shrine. They do not look built so much as extruded from a physics that has little to do with hammers or welds. One of them, near the center of the dish, is a torus of completely frictionless, non-reflective material that hovers four inches above a worn stone pedestal. It does not spin or hum, yet the air around it is constantly sheared of its moisture. Water condenses out of the warm atmosphere onto an invisible boundary field surrounding the ring, forming sheets of liquid that slide down nothingness and splash onto the rock below. 

These scattered machines are the climate engines of the hollow, though that was likely never their purpose; their heat and their moisture are merely the exhaust of some slow, unfathomable computation that has been running since the mountains were young. Another artifact—a cluster of slender, needle-like pylons leaning at an angle near the northern wall—bleeds a steady, thirty-degree warmth into the stone, keeping the chamber at the temperature of a closed greenhouse or a mammal’s throat.

Because there is no light to drive photosynthesis, the lush vegetation feeds on this ambient thermal bleed and the weak, complex fields the machines project. The flora is blind, heavy, and velvet-skinned. Thick, overlapping mats of black-and-ochre *bryophytes* (though their colors exist only in potential, never seen) carpet every square inch of the floor and walls, drinking the endless runoff from the condensing fields. From these mats rise the "trees" of the hollow: fleshy, branching structures with the texture of wet suede and the thickness of a man’s thigh. They grow with agonizing slowness along the invisible lines of force radiating from the artifacts, their blind leaves wide and cupped like satellite dishes, angled not toward a sun, but toward the torus and the pylons. They do not crowd the room. They hug the rock walls, forming a dense, spongy tapestry three feet thick that leaves the central vault entirely clear.

Water is everywhere, yet it never gathers into a stream. The stone is too flat, too settled into equilibrium for rapids or waterfalls. Instead, the moisture creeps. It weeps out of the porous, ancient ceiling in heavy, warm drops; it sheets off the force-fields of the artifacts; it saturates the velvet flora until the plants simply sweat it back out. The floor is a labyrinth of shallow, stagnant puddles connected by films of water a millimeter deep, slowly migrating across the soapstone at a pace too gradual to feel on bare skin. 

If you stand in the center of the hollow, in that great, warm, empty volume of air, the darkness is absolute. It is the kind of blackness that presses against the corneas until the brain begins to invent its own ghosts. 

And yet, the darkness is roaring.

Because the boundaries of the room are so close—just eighty paces from wall to wall—and because nothing can see, the abundant life of the chamber exists entirely in the medium of sound and touch. The velvet canopy along the walls is teeming. Millions of creatures, pale and eyeless and slick with the Warm Wet, crawl through the thermal-plants and across the weeping stones. There are heavy-bodied things the size of terriers, vaguely crustacean but soft-shelled, that graze on the heat-drinkers, their mandibles clicking in slow, rhythmic waves that travel from one end of the room to the other. There are long, ribbon-like amphibians that rest in the warm puddles beneath the hovering torus, inflating throat-sacs the size of melons to produce deep, percussive booms that echo off the tallow-smooth ceiling. 

The sound is constant, overlapping, and immense, yet nothing in the room moves quickly. The acoustic landscape is a study in patience. A chorus of high, metallic trills from thousands of thumb-sized insects living inside the hollow pylons will build over the course of twenty minutes, reaching a deafening crescendo that vibrates in the teeth, only to slowly phase-shift into a low, wet rattling as the creatures adjust to a micro-fluctuation in the artifact’s energy output. 

The air smells heavily of geosmin, warm wet fur, crushed celery, and the faint, sterile tang of ozone near the machines. 

Nothing here is violent. The ecology has had millions of years in this sealed, high-altitude womb to smooth itself out, just like the stone. Predators do not chase; they simply lie across the slow migration paths of the grazers with their mouths open, waiting days for a meal to amble into them, or they use low-frequency hums to lull the insects into dropping from the ceiling. Even the drops of water falling from the invisible fields of the artifacts seem to fall slowly, their sharp *plip-plop* swallowed immediately by the thick, living carpet below and the rich, deafening choir of the dark.
