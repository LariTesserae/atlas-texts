The city sits at the bottom of the Sink, a geological subsidence three miles wide where a limestone plateau collapsed into its own dry, hollowed-out belly ages ago. The rim is a distant, bruised ring of stone high overhead, holding back the surface desert. Between the rim and the floor hangs a permanent ceiling of fine, ochre dust, turning the sky into a flat, dim vault. It never rains here. The air is cool, kept at a cellar temperature by the sheer mass of the surrounding rock, and smells of dry clay, pulverized gypsum, and the sharp, formic tang of a million insects.

There are no sharp edges left in the Sink. Whatever jagged blocks fell when the roof caved in have long since been sanded down by the slow, circular thermal drafts that stir the basin. And what the wind didn't smooth, the builders did—or rather, time did to the builders' work. 

The city is vast, occupying the entire floor of the depression, yet it feels strangely empty. There are no streets, properly speaking, only wide, sweeping expanses of pale, talc-soft dust separating massive, low-slung mounds. These are the structures: ancient, mortarless stacks of ochre sandstone and mud-brick that have slumped and fused over millennia until they look like natural buttes. Their roofs have caved in or been intentionally filled, turning the buildings into terraced hills. Wide, shallow steps, worn down in the centers like old soap, wind up their flanks. There is no metal here, no glass, no wire, no wheel-ruts. Just stone that has settled into its final, most stable geometry, sitting heavy and patient in the gloom.

Despite the bone-dry air, the city is deeply, stubbornly green—though it is a dusty, muted green, the color of sage and tarnished copper. Every wide plaza and terraced roof is claimed by sprawling, slow-growing groves of xerophytic trees with trunks like braided leather and canopies of thick, felted leaves. Beneath them, spilling over the smoothed lips of the ancient walls, are massive cushions of pale, fibrous moss and sprawling fields of succulence—agaves the size of carts, and waist-high thickets of silver-haired scrub. 

This lushness survives without groundwater. It drinks the air. Every twelve hours, as the surface desert above cools or heats, a slow, heavy tide of air rolls down the walls of the Sink. It carries only a trace of moisture—a mere ghost of humidity—but the plants are built to strip it bare. Millions of microscopic hairs on the leaves comb the dust-laden breeze, trapping every stray molecule of vapor, while also catching the mineral-rich dust to feed their roots. The vegetation grows at an imperceptible crawl. A single woody vine, thick as a man's thigh, might take three hundred years to creep across the facade of a buried temple. 

Because the spaces between the building-mounds are so wide, the eye can travel for miles through the dim, amber twilight, watching the silhouettes of the terraces fade into the dry haze. Yet for all this visual stillness, the place is wrapped in a wall of sound so thick it presses against the eardrums.

It is the fauna. The city is an enormous, dry-land reef for creatures that never need to drink. 

The stone walls and the leathery trunks of the trees are carpeted, layer upon layer, with cicadas and locust-like orthopterans as large as sparrows, their carapaces the exact chalky yellow of the masonry. Millions of them stridulate in unison. The sound is not a pleasant summer chirp; amplified by the bowl of the Sink and bouncing off the hard-packed plazas, it is a deafening, metallic roar—a continuous, grinding drone that vibrates in the teeth and the sternum. 

Through this sonic fog move the birds. Vast flocks of sandgrouse, grey-winged doves, and pale, ground-dwelling swifts nest in the millions of eroded pockmarks and old window-slits of the sunken masonry. When a flock shifts from one plaza to another, the roar of the insects is briefly swallowed by the thunder of a hundred thousand wings beating the cool, dusty air, accompanied by a frantic, echoing clatter of dry, clicking calls.

On the ground, moving slowly across the open expanses between the groves, are the grazers: herds of long-necked, tortoise-like reptiles with shells worn completely smooth by blowing grit, and packs of small, big-eared fleet-mice that move in synchronized ripples through the silver scrub. None of them ever see standing water. They get their moisture entirely by chewing the fibrous, water-hoarding flesh of the succulents, and their kidneys are so efficient they leave behind only dry, chalky pellets of pure uric acid that quickly crumble and join the pale dust of the plazas.

Nothing hurries here. The predators—wide-headed lizards the color of dried mud, and silent, flightless owls that stalk through the rooftop groves—hunt by sitting still for days, waiting for a beetle or a mouse to wander within reach of a slow, deliberate snap. The insects sing, molt, and die in place, their dry shells drifting down to form crunchy drifts at the base of the walls, which are slowly eaten by detritivore beetles, whose own waste feeds the dust-moss. 

The light shifts only from the dark amber of day to the deep, starless charcoal of night, filtered always through the high ceiling of suspended silt. In the cool, dry dark, the pitch of the insects' roar drops an octave, becoming a deep, thrumming bass note that shakes the fine dust loose from the ancient, slumping eaves, grain by grain, century by century.
