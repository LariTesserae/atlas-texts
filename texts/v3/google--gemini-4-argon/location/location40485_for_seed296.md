At an altitude where the air feels thin and glassy in the lungs, the mountains have forgotten how to be sharp. 

This is the High Slump—a basin four miles across, cupped in the palms of primordial granite domes that have been ground down by eons of wind and frost heave into whale-backed ridges. There are no jagged peaks here, no raw talus slopes. Everything geological has settled into its final, exhausted posture. The rock is a pale, lichen-scabbed grey, smooth and convex, sloping down into a wide, dim bowl that holds the weather like a greased iron skillet.

It never truly rains here, because the clouds do not fall from above; they are born on the floor of the basin and dragged through it. A heavy, pewter-colored mist perpetually rolls over the western lip, crawls through the settlement of Spindle-Hollow, and spills out the eastern notch. The air is cold—a permanent, bone-aching forty degrees—and saturated with a fine, drifting grease-damp that beads on wool and oils the stone. 

Despite the altitude and the chill, the basin is choked with vegetation. It is not the bright, frantic green of a lowland jungle, but a dense, cold-adapted riot of biomass that feeds on the relentless fog. Giant cushion plants, wide as dining tables and hard as upholstered furniture, swell over the smoothed boulders. Above them rise tangled groves of dwarf iron-birch and weeping juniper, their trunks twisted like wrung rags, their branches draped in yard-long curtains of pale yellow usnea and rubbery black lungwort. Every surface is buffered by three inches of wet peat-moss and liverwort. The plants grow in a tight, claustrophobic weave, crowding every square foot of ground, their leaves waxy and dark—indigo, olive, bruised plum—drinking the grey light.

Woven directly into this cold thicket is the town, though it looks less like a place where people live and more like an industrial orchard of rust and stone. 

Spindle-Hollow is a municipal machine built to harvest the two things the basin produces in excess: the constant, heavy wind moving through the mountain passes, and the dense, conductive moisture of the cloud-banks. The architecture is vertical, narrow, and crammed together along the stepped terraces of the ancient rock. Four-story rowhouses of rough-quarried slate and dark brick lean against one another for warmth, their alleys so narrow that two people cannot pass without brushing their oilskins against the moss-furred walls. 

Above the slate roofs rises a secondary forest of iron. Thousands of tall, lattice-work masts and copper-finned condensers reach into the moving mist. Between them run cat’s-cradles of braided steel cable, gantry bridges, and insulated copper piping that sweats cold water onto the boardwalks below. There is no coal smoke here, no roar of internal combustion. The industry is pneumatic and kinetic. Huge, slow-turning Savonius rotors—shaped like split oil drums hammered from tarnished brass—revolve silently on greased bearings in the high wind. They drive long, reciprocating rocker-arms that travel down the hillsides like jointed insect legs, transferring mechanical power into the slate-walled mills below.

Because of the thick moss and the enveloping fog, the soundscape is strangely amputated. There is no echo. A dropped wrench hits the peat-matted cobbles with a dull *thump* that dies ten feet away. The overwhelming activity of the place—and it is relentlessly active, everything moving at once—happens in a kind of muffled, rhythmic hush. You hear the low, bronchial wheeze of leather bellows inside the compression sheds; the constant, soft *shuck-shuck-shuck* of the overhead rocker-arms; the oily click of ratchets; and the universal, inescapable sound of water dripping from copper fins into wooden sluices. 

The weirdness of the valley lies in how the ecology and the machinery have colonized one another, accelerated by the strange electrical charge of the high-altitude mist. 

The condensers do not just pull water from the air; they pull a faint, static charge from the friction of the cloud-banks grinding over the ancient granite. The town runs on this weak, cold current. Pale violet St. Elmo’s fire frequently crawls down the wet iron masts at twilight, pooling in the gutters before grounding out into the peat. 

The local flora has adapted to the current. A species of bloated, translucent bladder-fern grows exclusively along the uninsulated copper conduits, pulsing visibly as it draws trace warmth and ions from the lines. These ferns grow and die in a matter of days, swelling to the size of pumpkins, turning black, and liquefying into a rich, nitrous slime that the townsfolk scrape into buckets to fertilize their terraced root-gardens. When a wind-rotor stalls, it is rarely from mechanical failure; it is because the "strangle-moss"—a fast-creeping, fibrous bryophyte—has woven itself into the gearbox overnight, drawn by the heat of friction, packing the teeth of the iron cogs with wet green felt. Maintenance crews in heavy rubberized canvas spend their entire shifts walking the gantries with long brass hooks, pruning the living carpet back from the moving parts.

Animal life is sparse, wary, and oversized. There are no songbirds, no buzzing clouds of insects. Occasionally, through a gap in the drifting grey vapor, you might see a "mist-skate"—a creature somewhere between a ray and a giant moth, three feet across, with wings of grey, velvet-textured skin. They do not flap; they ride the dense, upward drafts of the thermal vents and the wind-shears off the rounded domes, feeding on the airborne spores that the bladder-ferns release in dark, drifting clouds. Lower down, among the dark tangles of dwarf-birch and rusted pipeline, move the tall, stork-legged scavengers the locals call *stilt-hounds*—hairless, pale-skinned quadrupeds with oversized ears that pivot independently, listening for the low-frequency hum of a failing bearing or the pop of a rotting fern-bladder.

Light in the basin is a precious, directional thing. The sky above is a uniform, luminous铅-grey that casts no shadows, making depth perception difficult; a gantry tower fifty yards away looks like a flat iron cutout pasted against cotton wool. To compensate, the streets and catwalks are strung with thousands of low-wattage carbon-arc bulbs and glass globes filled with bioluminescent bog-gas. They burn day and night with a dim, amber-orange glow that only penetrates a few yards into the damp before being swallowed.

Stand on one of the central stone viaducts at midday, and the whole basin feels like the interior of a gigantic, cold lung. The mist rushes past your face, smelling of wet wool, ozone, crushed pine needles, and machine oil. Around you, the dense thicket of black-green leaves shivers in the draft. Above, fifty brass rotors turn in unison, their wet surfaces catching the dull amber of the streetlamps. The iron rods pump back and forth through the moss-choked alleys; the condensers hiss softly as they bleed pressure; the ferns swell on the wires; the water trickles down the ancient, smoothed gullies of the mountain’s bones. Everything is working, everything is wet, and everything is happening in a deep, wool-wrapped quiet at the roof of an old world.
