The heat rises from the stone itself, not as a dry bake, but as a heavy, breath-like warmth that makes the air feel slightly too thick to inhale cleanly. This is a collapsed limestone sinkhole—an *aven* whose roof gave way centuries ago, though the aperture fifty feet overhead is now so choked with strangler figs and overlapping elephant-ear philodendrons that the sky is entirely erased. What reaches the floor is not daylight, but a bruised, submarine green that seems to stain the skin rather than illuminate it. 

The chamber is roughly the size of an abbey nave, oval and tall, with overhanging walls of dissolved gray karst that curve inward toward the roof. Everything has been rounded by millennia of patient chemistry. There are no sharp edges left. Fluted columns of flowstone—pale amber and tallow-colored—bulge from the walls like melted wax, their surfaces slick with a constant, feverish sweat. There is no running water here, no stream or subterranean pool, only this relentless condensation. The air is so saturated that moisture beads on every square inch of stone and leaf, gathering until its own weight pulls it down in slow, heavy drops. 

In this dim, thermal incubator, the flora has grown thick and strange, adapting to the scarcity of light by turning cannibalistic and parasitic. The floor is a deep, springy mattress of ancient humus, black as pitch and warm to the touch from the heat of its own rapid decomposition. Out of it rise dense thickets of broad-leaved ferns and pale, chlorophyll-starved arums whose leaves are the color of old ivory and veined in deep violet. Vines the thickness of a man’s wrist loop from the flowstone pillars, draped in curtains of Spanish moss and translucent, hair-like root systems that drink directly from the humid air. Because the space is enclosed, everything grows through everything else; shelf fungi bracket the trunks of living trees, and slick liverworts coat the backs of the older, slower-moving beetles.

And the chamber is teeming with animals, though you would not know it with your eyes closed. The silence here is profound, almost pressurized. The thick moss and deep rot swallow acoustics, and in so small a space—where a predator is never more than twenty paces away—making noise is a fatal mistake. 

Instead, the dynamism of the pit is entirely kinetic and visual. Every surface is in slow, crawling, or darting motion. The pale trunks of the cavern-figs are shingled with mottled moths the size of saucers, their wings slowly pulsing in unison like gills. Centipedes a foot long, colored a dull, wet carmine, pour over the contoured stone in liquid ribbons, hunting the soft-bodied crickets that navigate the gloom with antennae three times the length of their bodies. In the upper tangles of the root-curtains, small, lemur-like primates with enormous, amber-black eyes leap from vine to vine; their paws are padded with fleshy suction-cups that make only the faintest *tock-tock* against the wet bark. Beneath the mulch, blind, blunt-headed snakes untangle themselves through the warm rot, sensing the vibrations of burrowing grubs. Life here is a frantic, soundless pantomime of eating and being eaten. When a tree frog—translucent-bellied and the color of tarnished brass—takes a beetle, there is only the tiny, wet snap of its jaw, immediately smothered by the hanging moss.

Tucked into the deep undercuts of the limestone walls, and half-swallowed by the roots, are the remains of whatever brought people down here long ago. There are no tools, no metal, no glass—only dry-stacked stone Cairns and low, curving retaining walls built from the native karst, now so slumped and coated in velvet-green bryophytes that they look like natural ribs of the cave. Perched on ledges ten feet up are seven or eight hollow cylinder-houses made of woven river-cane and river-mud, no larger than kilns. Whatever roofed them has rotted away, and their interiors are now packed with the papery, tiered nests of social wasps—pale, blind insects that do not buzz when they fly, but drift through the gloom like falling ash. 

Into the soft flowstone near the floor, someone once carved a series of shallow basins to catch the condensation drips. They are arranged in a descending stair, so that when the upper bowl fills, it spills a thin, warm trickle down a carved groove into the next. The carvings around the rims—spirals, or perhaps coiled millipedes—have been worn nearly smooth by the weak carbonic acid of the water. Now, the bowls serve as nurseries for tiny, jet-black crabs with cream-colored claws, which sit motionless under the lukewarm film of water, waiting for a moth or a shed scale to drift down from the canopy above.

Every few minutes, the stifling equilibrium of the chamber shifts. The heat building at the floor of the sinkhole reaches a threshold and rises in a sudden, lazy thermal through the matted leaves at the top of the shaft. As it escapes, a draft of slightly cooler, drier air from the unseen world above slips down the walls. For ten seconds, the entire cathedral shivers. The hanging roots sway; a synchronized rain of heavy water-drops patters against the broad arum leaves like a brief, localized shower; a thousand moths adjust their grip on the bark at once, producing a dry, papery rustle like a turned page. Then the thermal passes, the air seals itself back into stillness, and the damp heat closes back in around the throat.
