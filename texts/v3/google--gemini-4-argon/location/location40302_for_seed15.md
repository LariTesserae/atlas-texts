The cistern is not large—perhaps thirty paces from one corner to the other—and it is entirely below the level of the ground outside, though there is no sense of cave or cellar about it. The roof is open to the sky, or rather, separated from the sky only by a dense latticework of split cedar poles laid across heavy, adze-squared beams. Through this grating, the midday sun drops in blinding, geometric shafts. 

Because the chamber is filled nearly to the lip with water, the light does not stop at the surface. It plunges twenty feet down to the floor, turning the entire volume of the room into a glowing block of pale, mint-tinted beryl. 

There is no natural rock visible anywhere. The walls, the floor, the stepped ledges, and the four thick pillars that hold up the roof beams are all constructed of large, cream-colored limestone blocks. The masonry is recent. The blocks have not yet been smoothed by the water’s motion or darkened by a century of silt. Their corners are still crisp and ninety-degree sharp, and across the flat faces of the stone, magnified by the clear water and the fierce overhead light, you can see the distinct, repetitive scallop-marks of copper chisels and stone mauls. White lime mortar, squeezed out between the courses like thick paste when the stones were laid, still clings in brittle, pale ridges that break off at a touch and drift to the bottom like chalky snow.

The water is warm—the temperature of a shallow tide pool in late afternoon, or water that has traveled for miles in an open, sun-baked stone channel before plunging here. That incoming flow is the source of the room's constant, heavy noise. High up in the eastern wall, just below the cedar ceiling-lattice, a wide stone chute projects into the chamber. From its mouth, a thick, unbroken cylinder of water pours out and smashes into the pool below. 

In this small, stone-lined box, the sound is immense. It is a continuous, hollow roar that vibrates in the teeth and chest, layered with the sharper, slapping echoes of waves rebounding off the vertical walls. The impact of the waterfall drives a relentless column of large, silvery bubbles ten feet down into the chamber’s depths before they arrest, break apart, and fizz violently back toward the ceiling. The surface of the water is never still; it heaves in steep, crisscrossing swells that slap loudly against the upper courses of masonry, keeping the top three feet of the dry stone dark and slick with splash.

Because of this constant agitation and the dazzling light, the interior of the water is a shifting cage of brightness. Caustic networks—brilliant, wavering lines of focused sunlight—race across the submerged steps and flicker up and down the four square pillars. The water itself is so clear that the bottom looks only arm's-length away, creating an illusion of emptiness, as though the pillars are standing in bright, heavy air rather than liquid.

Life has only just begun to find its way into this constructed pocket. There are no fish, no darting beetles, no snails rasping at the stone. The water is too new, or the intake chute too fast and steep for creatures to have colonized it yet. What exists is botanical, sparse, and pioneer in nature. 

In the mortar joints on the wall opposite the waterfall, where the current sweeps past in a slow, warm gyre, a few bright green ribbons of freshwater eelgrass have taken root. They are spaced widely apart, single tufts anchored in the lime crevices, bowing and straightening in unison as the surge passes them. Higher up, on the first two submerged steps where the light is almost painfully bright, patches of filamentous green algae have established themselves like scraps of wet silk pinned to the stone. They stream outward in the current, trembling with the vibration of the falling water, and their tips are covered in tiny, brilliant pearls of oxygen that detach every few seconds and shoot toward the surface. 

Up near the ceiling, where the water sprays against the underside of the cedar lattice, a few pale, broad-leafed ferns have sprouted in the damp rot between the bark and the stone cornice. Their fronds hang down toward the water, trembling constantly in the gusty, humid draft created by the waterfall. Occasionally, a dead cedar needle or a white blossom from the unseen world above falls through the grating, hits the turbulent surface, and is sucked down into the bright, roaring depths, spinning slowly through the beams of sun until it settles in a neat, drift-lined row at the base of the pristine walls.
