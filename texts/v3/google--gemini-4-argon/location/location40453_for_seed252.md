The chamber is a recent fracture in the deep granite, no larger than a shipping container, sealed hundreds of meters below the surface. Because the rock sheared only a few years ago, there is no silt on the floor and no softening of the walls. Every surface is fresh, angular, and sharp enough to catch the skin—a jagged puzzle of pale feldspar, dark mica, and quartz veins that look as though they were pulled apart yesterday. 

Despite the depth, the space is blindingly bright. There are no fixtures, no cables, and no emitters, yet the air itself is excited at the atomic level, sustaining a uniform, shadowless white luminescence—a byproduct of a deep-crustal stabilization field or a stray resonance from some massive, unmapped infrastructure miles away. The light does not come from a direction; it simply occupies the volume of the room. Every wet facet of the broken granite glints with needle-sharp intensity, turning the small pocket into a hall of mirrors.

The heat is immediate and heavy, hovering just above fifty degrees Celsius. It radiates from the raw stone, which sits close to a geothermal pocket, turning the chamber into a natural autoclave. Because the rock is constantly cooling the superheated vapor rising through the micro-fissures in the floor, the walls weep continuously. Water condenses out of the thick, luminous air in fat, heavy beads, gathering on the sharp overhangs and running down the sheer faces in thin, shivering sheets. It collects in the lowest V-shaped troughs of the uneven floor—clear, scalding pools a few inches deep that steam gently back into the glare.

Nothing green grows here. The rock is too new and the heat too fierce for spores or mosses. Instead, the dampness has invited a sparse, extremophile coat of rust-colored mineral bacteria and translucent, blister-like thermal fungi that cling only to the sharpest ridges where the water flows fastest. Most of the stone remains entirely bare, wide expanses of stark grey and sparkling white separated by an occasional, isolated smear of ochre biofilm.

The air is too thick to carry sound well, and there is no wind to make any. The baseline of the space is a dead, pressurized hush. Against this silence, the constant movement of the water becomes hyper-distinct: a sharp, resonant *plink* as a heavy drop falls from a ceiling tooth into a floor pool; the sudden, soft hiss of a water film sliding down a particularly hot slab of quartz; the faint, rhythmic ticking of the surrounding rock still settling under immense subterranean weight.

Every so often, a ripple breaks the surface of the shallow, scalding pools. The chamber supports a tiny, dispersed population of blind, translucent isopods, each no larger than a fingernail, their shells clear enough to show the dark line of their gut. They move slowly across the submerged, jagged granite, grazing on the edges of the thermal biofilms. On the drier, hotter upper walls, a single pale arachnid—long-legged and entirely adapted to the heat—waits motionless for hours on a dry shelf of feldspar, its shadow non-existent in the ambient glare, waiting for one of the crustaceans to crawl too far up a weeping channel. 

The water rises as vapor, hits the ceiling, beads on the sharp stone, and falls back into the pools. The light holds steady, brilliant and silent, illuminating every falling drop in the empty, baking room.
