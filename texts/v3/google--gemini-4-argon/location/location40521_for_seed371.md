At thirteen thousand feet, the mountain is still tearing itself apart, and someone has dropped an engine into the wound.

The space is no larger than a village square, though it stands on its end—a violent, three-sided notch blown out of the high ridge by frost-wedging and recent tectonic heave. The rock here has never had the time to round off or soften. It is young, dark hornfels and shattered slate, every edge sharp enough to slice leather, stacked in precarious, freshly split columns that groan when the wind shifts. There is no floor, only a steep, unstable throat of talus choked with vegetation so dense and rubbery it swallows the stone entirely. 

It is three hours before dawn, or perhaps the sky has simply closed over for the week. The darkness is absolute, broken only by the erratic, jumping arc-light of a shorting junction box and two portable halogen floodlights mounted on yellow tripods. The tripods are lashed directly to the trunks of dwarf *Polylepis* trees with rusted chain to keep them from blowing off the mountain. 

The light doesn't travel far. It hits a vertical wall of foliage and stops. Because the notch acts as a natural funnel for the thin, freezing cloud-drift rising from the valleys five thousand feet below, the air is a relentless horizontal soup of cold vapour. It is not rain—there is no freestanding water here, no streams, as the fractured rock drains instantly—but everything is slick with condensation and a greasy film of aerosolized diesel. Moisture beads on the undersides of giant, leathery *Gunnera* leaves and runs down the jagged stone faces in black, oily tracks. 

The botany here is aggressive, crowded into the wind-shadow of the cleft like survivors huddling in a doorway. Twisted, paper-barked trees grow nearly horizontally out of the vertical walls, their trunks buried knee-deep in dense cushions of *Azorella* and soaking wet sphagnum. Stiff, bayonet-leaved bromeliads lock into every raw fissure of the rock, growing over and through one another until the gorge is a solid, springy mat of wet green and brown fiber. Everything is cold to the touch, stiff with turgor, and violently in motion.

The turbulence is the defining condition of the pocket. The high-altitude gale hits the back wall of the notch and rolls downward into a trapped, screaming vortex. The vegetation doesn't sway; it thrashes. Giant leaves the size of truck hoods whip back and forth, slapping against the slate with the sound of wet canvas. Paper bark strips away in ribbons and whirls into the dark. And under the roar of the wind is the relentless, rhythmic hammering of a six-cylinder diesel generator and a hydraulic core-rig wedged onto a tiny shelf of raw rock in the center of the thicket.

There is no building here, no hut, no poured concrete, not even a cleared pad. The equipment was lowered into this vertical jungle by heavy-lift cable from the dark air above and chained directly to the mountain. The drill rig—painted an industrial orange now fouled with black grease and bruised moss—is actively chewing into the fault line. It shudders violently on its hydraulic legs, fighting the angle of the slope. Thick black rubber hoses, slick with condensation and hydraulic fluid, snake through the ferns like roots, pulsing and kicking with pressure surges. 

Nothing stays still. The halogen lights vibrate so hard on their tripods that the shadows they cast—millions of overlapping, jagged leaf-shadows and sharp rock-planes—blur into a strobing, chaotic gray. The generator’s exhaust pipe spits blue smoke and occasional orange sparks straight into a wall of hanging clubmoss, which immediately batters the smoke into ragged shreds. Where the hot exhaust hits the cold, wet foliage, a localized pocket of thick white steam erupts and is instantly torn away by the downdraft. 

Every few minutes, the vibration of the drill and the heaving of the wind-levered roots dislodge a piece of the young cliff wall above. You don't see it fall in the dark; you only hear the sharp, pistol-shot crack of fresh stone splitting, followed by a brief, heavy thudding as a slab of slate tumbles through the rubbery canopy, shearing off branches and crushing bromeliads before wedging into the talus beside the shuddering engine block. 

Nothing lives here that has eyes or lungs. The combination of the thin, freezing altitude, the ninety-decibel mechanical hammering, the diesel fumes, and the sheer physical violence of the thrashing leaves has scrubbed the cleft of any animal life. There are no birds in the tangled branches, no insects circling the blinding white halogens, no rodents under the rocks. There is only the blind, crowded heave of the cold plants, the fracturing stone, and the unsupervised machine pounding itself to pieces in the dark.
