At this altitude, the air should be freezing and thin, a sterile blue vacuum scraping over bare rock. Instead, it is hot, thick as grease, and dark as a bruised plum. The mountain range—if a continent-sized upheaval of raw, unweathered crust can be called a range—is still in the violent throes of its own birth. It does not rise in peaks; it juts in fresh, razor-edged plates of obsidian, bismuth, and porous basalt that have never known the softening touch of centuries. 

There is no wind here, yet everything is in a state of frantic, silent commotion. 

The rock itself is afflicted with an industrial madness. Though no hand has ever laid a brick or forged a bolt upon these heights, the geology behaves like a runaway assembly line. Along a thousand-mile fault line, titanic slabs of dark stone rhythmically piston up and down, sliding against each other with the slick, oiled precision of a stamping press. Where they meet, there is no deafening crash of stone—only a muffled, hydraulic *shhh-chunk*, as if the acoustics of the world have been packed in heavy wool. Sparks, thick and orange as molten iron, spray from the friction points in slow-motion arcs, illuminating the gloom for a hundred yards before winking out. Across the vast, tiered plateaus, natural fumaroles do not merely vent steam; they puff in synchronized, four-stroke intervals, chuffing pale clouds of sulfur and warm mineral oil into the dim sky, maintaining the muggy, greenhouse heat of the high altitudes.

Because the rock is so young and sharp, there is no soil. Yet the vegetation is lush, aggressive, and deeply implicated in the machinery of the terrain. Mosses of a metallic, verdigris hue grow in thick, rubbery mats directly over the moving stone, acting as gaskets and shock absorbers between the grinding tectonic plates. Great, fern-like flora with fronds of woven copper and dark, fibrous wood sprout from the vents, their roots plunging deep into the boiling subterranean reservoirs. When a shelf of basalt shears away—which happens every few minutes, the landscape constantly revising its own geometry—the torn roots of these plants bleed a warm, viscous sap that smells distinctly of turpentine and crushed mint. Within seconds of a collapse, new shoots erupt from the fresh, jagged scars, uncoiling with the twitching, accelerated violence of time-lapse photography, their leaves clicking softly like camera shutters as they unfurl to catch the dim, ambient glow.

Water does not fall as rain here, nor does it gather in rivers; the geometry of the land is too unstable to hold a riverbed for more than an hour. Instead, moisture exists as a heavy, perpetual sweat. The warm, chuffing exhaust of the earth condenses against the cooler, newly extruded facets of volcanic glass, forming thick beads of warm dew that roll down the razor-sharp inclines. It collects in temporary, geometric puddles in the stamped depressions of the rock—warm, rainbow-sheened pools of water and natural lubricants that evaporate into low, hugging fogs the moment a subterranean boiler shifts beneath them. 

Through this dense, steaming tangle of pistons and ferns moves an absurd abundance of life, all of it operating in the same eerie, dampened quiet. The fauna here has adapted to a world where space and matter are unreliable. Swarms of creatures the size of hounds, shaped like origami cranes folded from wet leather and brass foil, clamber over the shifting rock faces. They have no mouths; they absorb the warm, oily condensation through their feet. When a mountain face suddenly liquefies or folds inward—a localized breakdown of the rules of solidity—the creatures do not panic. They simply step sideways into the newly formed angles of space, their joints ratcheting with the soft, dry whirr of clockwork.

Higher up, where the dim light curdles into a permanent, tarnished twilight, the air is crowded with drifting, translucent forms that resemble a cross between jellyfish and hot-air balloons. Their internos are lit by the faint, flickering arcs of static electricity—living vacuum tubes that drift in the thermal updrafts of the great geologic engines. Thousands of them crowd the sky, bumping softly against one another, their tendrils trailing through the copper ferns. Where their tendrils touch the stone, the rock temporarily forgets its hardness, turning to a grey, bubbling paste that retains the memory of gears and cogs before hardening again into raw, jagged scree.

The scale of the place is suffocatingly vast, yet it feels crowded, over-populated with process. Look down any of the mile-deep trenches that yawn open and snap shut across the plateau, and you see layers upon layers of this silent, hyperactive ecology: millions of segmented, velvet-shelled beetles acting as ball bearings between sliding strata; vines that winch fallen monoliths back into the air with the slow, inexorable torque of cranes; whole cliffsides that are actually the calcified, breathing gills of some continental organism, rhythmically filtering the soot from the air. 

Reality here is not a fixed stage, but a turbulent, churning broth of biology and heavy industry, boiling over in the dark. A ridge three miles away suddenly stutters, repeating its own silhouette four times across the horizon like a skipped frame in a film reel, before collapsing into a cloud of warm, damp spores. And through it all, the silence holds—broken only by the wet hiss of steam, the soft *tick-tick-tick* of cooling metal-stone, and the muffled, rhythmic thrum of a planet furiously manufacturing itself in the dark.
