The basin sits low, a flat-bottomed depression five miles across where the crust has only recently cooled. The ground is not dirt. There has been no time for weather to grind stone into silt, nor for generations of rot to build a true soil. Instead, the floor is a continuous pavement of black, blistered basalt and glassy volcanic slag, sharp as broken bottles, warm to the touch. Every crack, cooling joint, and burst-bubble depression holds standing water—not fed by rivers, which have not yet carved channels through this fresh rock, but by the air itself, which is so saturated and hot that moisture condenses continuously on every surface, running in warm, mineral-slick sheets into the hollows.

Above, the sky is a permanent, low ceiling of bruised-ochre cloud. It does not rain, and the clouds do not break. The light that filters through is the color of tarnished brass—dim, shadowless, and heavy, turning the distance into a murky soup before it reaches the basin’s low, jagged rim. The heat is absolute and completely static. There is no noon and no night to cool the stone; the thermal output from the shallow mantle below matches the greenhouse trap of the sky above, locking the valley into a breathless, unchanging ninety-eight degrees. 

Despite the lack of soil, the basin is choked with green, though there is not a single tree. The flora is entirely rootless, gripping the raw glass and basalt with rubbery holdfasts and suction-cupped tendrils. Great, bloated liverworts the size of mattresses sprawl across the lava roping, stacked three and four deep. Fleshy, translucent stalks of giant hornworts rise six feet into the murk, lacking bark or wood, held upright strictly by internal water pressure. Everything is low-slung, wide-leaved, and overlapping, leaving enough open headspace to see a mile or two across the valley, yet packing the lowest ten feet of the world into a nearly impassable mat of wet, rubbery tissue. Where plants touch, they often fuse, forming living bridges over the razor-sharp crevasses in the stone. 

What makes the landscape truly dense, however, is that the vegetation is barely visible beneath the sheer volume of animal life resting upon it. 

Every broad leaf, every exposed knuckle of black rock, every warm puddle is coated in living things. There is no scurrying, no panicked flight, no predator-prey thrashing. The ecosystem has reached a bizarre, saturated equilibrium where energy is drawn directly from the ambient heat and the fast-growing, mineral-fed slime that coats the world. Pale, segmented amphibians with vestigial eyes lie stacked like cordwood in the warm trenches between basalt pillows, their gills pulsing in slow, synchronized waves. Millions of fist-sized gastropods with translucent, uncalcified shells cover the giant ferns like living cobblestones, grazing on the film of cyanobacteria that grows as fast as they can scrape it away. 

The air itself is thick with slow-moving, soft-bodied insects—creatures with four-inch wingspans that do not dart or hover, but drift through the heavy, wet atmosphere as if swimming through warm oil, constantly landing on one another, mating, and dropping eggs into the tepid puddles below. When you look across the basin, the ground seems to breathe; a slow, peristaltic heave that extends for miles, caused by millions of cold-blooded bodies shifting slightly against one another in the gloom.

Because nothing moves quickly, the sound of the place has no rhythm or punctuation. There are no sharp bird calls, no rustling of wind in leaves, no crashing of stalks. Instead, there is a deafening, uninterrupted wall of biological white noise. It is the sound of ten million wet throats vibrating at once—a low, guttural thrumming from the amphibians, layered beneath the continuous, wet *schluck-schluck* of countless radulae scraping over stone, and the high, papery drone of wings beating lazily in the soup-thick air. The noise does not rise or fall. It hangs in the basin at a constant, roaring pitch that vibrates in the teeth and chest, so total that after an hour, the ear stops registering it as sound and feels it only as an extension of the atmospheric pressure.

Nothing rots here in the normal way. When a creature dies, it does not fall to the ground—there is no room to fall. It simply softens in place, held up by the press of its neighbors, and is immediately absorbed by the rootless plants and the grazing snails, converted back into living tissue before it can smell of decay. The basalt beneath remains entirely unweathered, protected from the air by the very carpet of life it supports, its edges still glass-sharp under a three-foot mattress of breathing flesh and wet green pulp.
