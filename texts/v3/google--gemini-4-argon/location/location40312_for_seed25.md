The sound arrives long before the eye can make any sense of the dark. It is a continuous, abrasive roar—like a million ceramic plates being slid across rough stone, layered beneath a wet, rhythmic clicking that echoes from rim to rim of the basin. It is not the sound of weather, though a cool, heavy mist hangs permanently in the air, condensing on every surface until it runs in thin,mineral-bitter rivulets down into the gravel. The sound is the terrain itself moving.

Or rather, what covers the terrain. 

The valley is a wide, shallow bowl of ancient, heavily eroded limestone and slate, worn smooth by ages of slow trickle and chemical sweat. There is no sky to speak of, only a low, suffocating ceiling of dark vapor that lets through a bruised, twilight-gray luminescence—just enough to reveal the sheer volume of the valley’s curves, though never enough to see the far side clearly. There is not a single blade of grass, no moss, no lichen, no root. The mineral floor is entirely barren of flora. Yet, you cannot see the stone.

The stone is buried beneath a foot-deep, shifting carpet of chitin, shell, and wet bristle.

This is a world entirely given over to blind, cold-blooded congregation. Every square inch of the weathered karsts, the broad dish-shaped depressions, and the slotted gullies is packed with fauna. In the absence of light and leaf, an entire food web has been built upward from the dark rock and the damp air. At the bottom of the heap, pressed directly against the sweating limestone, are the lithovores—flat, gray, segmented creatures the size of human hands that rasp away at the mineral crusts and the pale, slimy mats of sulfur-eating bacteria that bloom wherever water pools. Their collective scraping is the bass note of the valley’s deafening chorus, a relentless grinding that slowly deepens the trenches they inhabit.

Over their backs crawl the filterers and the trappers. Because the valley is so dense with life, the very air is thick with organic detritus—shed skins, microscopic eggs, aerosolized secretions, and tiny, buoyant midges that drift like living dust in the cool fog. To catch this drift, millions of stalked, anemone-like worms and long-legged arachnids stand shoulder to shoulder on the ridges, their wet, feathery appendages fanned out into the breeze. When a current of cooler air rolls down the basin's slope, entire hillsides ripple in unison as ten million feeding fans turn to meet it, producing a sound like tearing silk that cuts right through the deeper grinding of the floor.

There is no dirt here; there is only *frass* and shell-hash. The low places between the eroded rock fins are filled with deep drifts of discarded exoskeletons, ground down into a pale, crunchy sand by the constant trampling of the living. When the intermittent rains come—less like falling drops and more like the fog suddenly deciding to collapse—this shell-sand turns into a gray, calcium-rich slurry that slides sluggishly down the gradients, carrying tangled knots of centipede-like scavengers with it. 

The valley is open—miles of rolling, fluted stone ridges and wide, flat sinks—yet it feels claustrophobic because there is no empty space. Everything touches everything else. Movement is a negotiation of leverage and armor. Predators the size of dogs, shaped like flattened, multi-jointed lobsters with long, whip-like antennae, do not stalk through the dark; they simply plow through the living carpet like icebreakers. As they move, the smaller creatures part in frantic, clattering waves, cascading over one another in a dry hail of legs and carapaces, only to flow back together immediately behind the hunter's tail. 

Here and there, rising out of the low, churning plains, are the spires—tall, smooth hoodoos of rock that have resisted erosion. They are the only things that break the horizontal monotony, and they are the epicenters of the noise. They are plastered from base to tip with large, bellows-lunged gastropods and heavy, cicada-like climbers that use the vertical stone as amplification chambers. They pump the cool, damp air through spiracles and rasping valves, screaming mating calls and territorial warnings into the murk. Each spire has its own pitch, a deafening, brassy drone that beats against the acoustic output of the neighboring towers, creating strange, warbling interference patterns in the hollows between them. 

Light exists only as a rare, biological grammar. In the deepest crevices, where the water collects in stagnant, oily puddles, pale blue and sickly green flashes erupt and die in fractions of a second. It is not a steady glow, which would invite the crushing jaws of the blind, heat-sensing hunters, but a frantic semaphore—a flash to lure a mate, a flash to startle a predator, immediately swallowed by the writhing dark. 

The ecology is a closed, frantic loop of consumption. Nothing goes to waste. When one of the great, armored predators dies, it does not even have time to cool to the ambient temperature of the slate before it is entirely obscured by a dome of smaller bodies. Within hours, the flesh is gone; within a day, the heavy shell is rasped thin by calcium-starved snails; within a week, the snails themselves are crushed by passing grazers, their stolen minerals returned to the pale gravel beneath. 

To stand in the center of this basin is to feel the vibration of it through the soles of the feet—a constant, low-frequency tremor of billions of mandibles, claws, and muscular feet working against the ancient, indifferent rock, slowly, noisily chewing the landscape down to sea level in the dark.
