The city of Oakhaven-in-the-Crag does not sit on the mountain; it is caught in the slow, grinding act of being extruded alongside the mountain from an elsewhere that has not yet finished rendering. 

At an altitude where the air tastes of cold iron and thin, bruised ozone, the basin stretches out for six or seven miles—a vast, tilted amphitheater of newly minted rock. The geology here has no memory. There is no scree, no talus, no river-rounded cobble. The cliffs that wall the valley are made of raw, unweathered hornblende and obsidian-black basalt that emerged from the thin air only decades ago, their edges still razor-sharp, their faces stepped in fresh, crystalline fractures that have never felt the smoothing thumb of a glacier. In places along the western rim, the mountain is still arriving: great, geometric slabs of granite hang three feet off the ground, translucent as smoked glass, taking forty years to fully densify and drop with a single, earth-splitting *crack* into the bedrock.

Across this wide, jagged bowl, the city is spaced out with a strange, agoraphobic deliberation. It is thoroughly urban—a landscape of brick-built manufactories, towering iron-framed condenser plants, terraced tenements, and elevated tramways—yet there are no contiguous neighborhoods. Every building or cluster of buildings stands isolated on its own sheer plinth of virgin stone, separated from its neighbors by hundreds of yards of raw chasm or flat, unweathered rock shelf. To get from the textile looms of the Lower Ward to the stamping mills of the North Shelf requires a twenty-minute ride in a suspended cable-car, swaying through empty, twilight-blue space while looking down at the sharp, dry geometry of the valley floor below. 

The light is permanently low, the color of tarnished pewter. Above the crags, the sky has the texture of heavy, wet wool, lit from behind by a sun that seems to be at the bottom of a deep well. It never rains, not truly, yet the entire valley is slick with a chill, pervasive damp. The industrial engines of the city—massive, slow-stroking atmospheric pistons that vent warm grease-steam into the freezing highland air—create their own weather. This exhaust mixes with the high-altitude mist, condensing instantly on the cold basalt and the cast-iron facades of the buildings. Everything drips. Water beads on the underside of the iron viaducts, pools in the razor-edged depressions of the newly formed rock, and runs in thin, mercury-colored ribbons down the masonry walls. 

Where the condensation collects, the vegetation is startlingly, aggressively lush. Because there is no soil—erosion has not had the millennia required to grind the rock into dirt—the flora has adapted to feed directly on the boundary between the industrial damp and the raw stone. Thick, heavy curtains of velvet-black moss, three feet deep, hang from the girder-work of the tramways. In the wide, empty plazas between the factory blocks, forests of giant, cabbage-like rosettes and towering, pale-green quillworts burst directly out of hairline fractures in the basalt, their roots secreting mild acids that etch the glassy stone. Enormous, rubbery ferns choke the intake grates of the boiler houses, thriving on the coal-warmth and the constant trickle of cylinder-bleed water. 

And the valley is alive with animals—an overwhelming, restless abundance of them that treats the industrial city and the unfinished geology as a single, continuous cliff-face. The air is thick with the heavy, leathery flap of thousands of roosting silhouettes. Great, slant-eyed caprids with coats like oiled shag-carpet and four curling horns pick their way along the I-beams of the elevated railways, licking the mineral salts that sweat from the cooling masonry. Beneath the slow-turning flywheels of the stamping mills, colonies of pale, long-necked martens weave through the machinery, entirely synchronized with the four-second drop of the counterweights. Flocks of heavy-bodied, flightless birds—the size of sheep, covered in filamentous grey plumage that beads with moisture—crowd the wide stone avenues, huddling against the warm brick walls of the smelting works, parting slowly and without fear when a steam-lorry grinds past on solid iron tires.

The sound of the place is immense, a continuous, cavernous resonance that fills the six-mile bowl. Because the rock faces are so fresh and hard, devoid of sand or soft earth to deaden the acoustics, every noise echoes five or six times across the chasms. There is the deep, rhythmic *chuff-thump* of the municipal air-pumps; the metallic shriek of cable-sheaves turning on high towers; the low, guttural coughing of ten thousand cliff-goats; and the constant, heavy chatter of the rock-fowls. Underneath it all is a sound that belongs neither to the beasts nor the engines: a low, glass-rubbing hum that emanates from the unfinished parts of the landscape.

For reality in the crags is frayed, worn thin by the combination of extreme altitude and the slow, mechanical strain of the city’s industry. The rules of space and materiality are not fully set. 

Along the eastern sector, an iron viaduct carrying a double-track railway arches gracefully across a dry gorge, only for the masonry pier on the far side to be missing; the tracks simply terminate in a smear of chromatic aberration, the iron rails stretching out like pulled taffy into nothingness, while the cargo trams still cross the gap by riding on the memory of the bridge's blueprint, their wheels clicking rhythmically against empty air. 

Near the center of the valley, a six-story brick tenement has slipped out of phase with the local clock. A veil of greasy condensation hangs over its facade, and through the tall, narrow windows, the yellow gas-lamps flicker at a rate of one cycle per week. A flock of copper-winged swifts that flew too close to its eaves three months ago is still there, suspended in a slow-motion arc above the slate roof, their wingbeats taking an entire afternoon to complete a single downstroke, their feathers beaded with water droplets that do not fall.

The factories themselves seem to harvest this breakdown. The great, soot-streaked stacks do not merely burn coal; they draw in the thin, unformed matter of the mountain’s edge—where the rock is still a grey, stuttering fog of potential—and compress it through massive, brass-bound turbines until it emerges as pig-iron, slate shingles, or heavy, lukewarm grease. Everything moves with a patient, inexorable weight. The pistons rise and fall like the chest of a hibernating bear; the cable-cars creep along their wires at a walking pace; the mountain takes a generation to solidify an inch of stone. 

Standing on an iron catwalk three hundred feet above the valley floor, the cold mist wets your face with the smell of wet wool, coal-tar, and crushed fern. Below, a herd of pale, horn-rimmed beasts sleeps in the warm shadow of a Dynamo Hall whose western wall has dissolved into a vertical sheet of black, unrippling water. Across the gulf, two miles away through the dim, pewter gloom, the orange eye of a blast furnace opens, casts a long, geometric shadow that points toward the light instead of away from it, and slowly, over the course of an hour, closes again.
