The plateau sits high enough in the atmosphere that the clouds do not pass over it so much as lean against its flanks, shedding their moisture into the dark stone before dissipating into the thin, bright air. Yet the basin itself is warm. A lingering, deep-earth heat radiates faintly through the bedrock—not enough to steam or boil, just enough to take the bite out of the altitude and keep the surface water at the temperature of skin.

Because the rock is geologically young, there is no soft soil here, no rolling hills or muddy riverbanks. The basin is a vast, open expanse of black basalt and dark grey greywacke, fractured in clean, rectilinear joints that look as though they were scored by a mason’s chisel. The ridges that ring the horizon are jagged and unweathered, their faces sharp enough to cut leather. Across the two-mile floor of the depression, the stone steps down in wide, natural shelves, over which a continuous, half-inch sheet of clear water glides without frothing.

It is an urban landscape, though built without mortar, metal, or wheel. The settlement stretches across the entirety of the flat basin, organized around the hydraulic geometry of the rock. Whoever—or whatever—built it used the stone’s natural cleavage to prize up massive, flat-sided slabs, stacking them into dry-stone plazas, knee-high retaining walls, and low, corbel-vaulted dwellings. Yet for a city of this footprint, it is remarkably sparse. A cluster of six or seven stepped-stone houses sits on a raised plinth, surrounded by three hundred yards of shallow, mirror-still water before the next cluster rises. Long, straight causeways of rough-hewn dark rock, barely two feet above the water line, connect the districts in a vast, open grid. You can stand on the western terrace and see all the way to the eastern Spillway, your gaze passing over dozens of low stone neighborhoods separated by wide, gleaming courts of water and vast beds of green.

The vegetation does not grow in dirt; it roots directly into the flooded fractures of the rock and the porous chinks of the masonry. Where the water pools to chest-depth in the wide squares between causeways, lush, emerald-green watercress and broad-leaved arums pack together in dense, floating islands. Along the stone walls, thick, velvety carpets of liverwort and bright green moss drink from the weeping seams of the rock. Ferns with fronds as tough as split bamboo sprout in neat, horizontal lines from the ledges of the buildings. There are no trees to block the sky, only these low, hyper-abundant mats of green that trace the stone architecture like mortar made of leaves. 

The light is even, high, and clear. The sun hangs in a pale blue sky that seems rinsed of dust, casting sharp, dark shadows behind the dry-stone towers and turning the wet basalt facets into blinding white mirrors. Because there is no wind at the bottom of the basin—only a slow, thermal drift that barely stirs the tips of the ferns—the water between the causeways holds a razor-sharp reflection of the sky and the jagged black peaks surrounding the plateau.

What defines the place, more than the stone or the water, is the stillness. There are no birds wheeling between the towers. No frogs drop from the moss into the canals; no silver flashes of fish disturb the submerged paving stones; no insects skitter across the surface tension. The lushness of the greenery is entirely untouched—not a single broad leaf is chewed by a caterpillar, not a single reed is bent by a nesting waterfowl. The plants grow to the exact limit of the nutrients dissolved in the mineral water and then hold there, suspended in a warm, sun-drenched equilibrium.

Even the water is quiet. The builders graded the stone sluices and stepped cisterns with such primitive precision that the runoff does not roar or babble. It slips from terrace to terrace in laminar sheets, clinging to the rock faces. If you stand in the center of a plaza, the only sound in the entire two-mile bowl of the city is a faint, glassy whisper—the sound of ten thousand small overflows sliding down dark, unweathered stone, and the occasional single *plip* of condensation falling from the ceiling of an empty vault into the dark pool below. 

Nothing rots quickly here, and nothing erodes. The sharp edges of the quarried basalt remain as crisp as the day they were split with wooden wedges and wet rope. The wooden lintels over the low doorways—made of some dense, fibrous stem imported from lower altitudes—are petrifying slowly in the mineral-rich damp, turning to stone without ever decaying. The sun warms the black rock, the rock warms the shallow water, the water feeds the moss, and the excess slips over the rim of the plateau into the clouds below, leaving the city behind in its bright, wet, unchanging quiet.
