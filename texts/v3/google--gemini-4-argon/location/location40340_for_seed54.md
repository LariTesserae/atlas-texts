The altitude makes itself known first in the weight of the air—thin, razor-cold, and so saturated with heavy brine-vapor that every breath tastes of iron and salt. This is the high basin of a young plateau, thrust up from the planetary crust so recently in geological time that its geometry has not yet been softened by wind or ice. The rock is dark, newly cooled basalt and green-black obsidian, fractured into vast, step-like terraces that drop away into a darkness too thick to measure. Every stone surface is razor-sharp, unweathered, and weeping.

Water is everywhere, yet almost none of it is ice. The city itself prevents the freeze. 

Stretching across six miles of this jagged amphitheater is a dense, tiered metropolis grown from self-assembling lithic polymers and black ceramic. The architecture does not sit upon the raw geology; it extrudes from it, using the same sharp, hexagonal tendencies as the cooling basalt. Towers of bundled, weeping pipes, cantilevered viaducts, and stepped ziggurats crowd the basin, packed tight against one another, their surfaces slick with a constant, viscous runoff. The buildings are still growing. At the edges of the terraces, where the rock is newest, a milky, mineral-rich fluid seeps from hair-thin nozzles in the infrastructure, hardening over weeks and months into new retaining walls, new conduits, new hollow buttresses. There are no seams, no rivets, no welds—only the slow, patient accumulation of dark matter, building itself millimeter by millimeter in the freezing dark.

The heat of this slow fabrication, combined with the heavy dissolved salts in the ground-seep, keeps the water liquid far below zero. It condenses out of the ink-black air in a heavy, perpetual drench, sheeting down the angular walls of the city, pooling in the stepped plazas, and cascading over the raw obsidian ledges into the lower tiers. There isn't a dry square inch in the basin. The streets are shallow, tiered canals of sluggish, near-freezing slush and black brine.

Light has no jurisdiction here. There is no sky—only an upper ceiling of freezing fog so dense it swallows altitude—and the city was not built for eyes. The only illumination comes from the occasional, hair-thin seam of pale violet Cerenkov glow deep inside the translucent cooling-towers, or the brief, static-blue spark of piezoelectric discharge when the shifting plateau groans and settles. You do not see the scale of the place; you hear it.

And the sound is immense, a deafening, wet, mechanical roar that fills the entire valley. 

Only half of that sound is the water and the deep, sub-audible hum of the city’s phase-shift thermal cores. The rest is the fauna.

Every vertical surface, every archway, every flooded gutter and warmth-bleeding conduit is packed, three and four layers deep, with life. Because there is no sun and no soil, there is no green thing anywhere in the high basin—no moss, no lichen, nothing with a root or a leaf. The ecology skips flora entirely, feeding directly on the chemical and thermal bleed of the city’s endless construction. 

The walls are carpeted in a living, shivering masonry of blind, pale-shelled crustaceans and calcified tube-dwellers that drink the mineral-rich fabrication runoff, building their own shells out of the city’s stray nanoscale mortar. Over them crawl sluggish, platter-sized gastropods with vents along their backs, grazing on the gray, gelatinous films of lithotrophic bacteria that bloom where the hot pipes meet the freezing rain. 

In the shallow canals and flooded plazas, the density of life becomes almost solid. Great, translucent, six-limbed amphibians—Blind-Waders, with skin like wet buckskin and gills that flare like dark fans—stand shoulder to shoulder in the freezing brine, millions of them across the tiers of the city, barely moving. They do not hunt; they simply filter the heavy, nutrient-dense slush that pours down from the upper districts, their throats pulsing in a slow, rhythmic unison. Above them, clinging to the undersides of the viaducts and stringing themselves between the closely packed towers, are massive, colonial siphonophores—drifting, liquid-filled curtains of flesh and nerve-cord that hang in the perpetual downpour to catch the mist, chiming softly as their heavy, glass-like air bladders clack together in the wind.

The sound they make is a continuous, deafening chorus: the millions of clattering chitinous plates, the wet, rhythmic *sluck-sluck* of grazing mouths, the deep, resonant croaking of the waders vibrating through the water and the stone, and the high, whistling hiss of gas-bladders venting into the cold air. It echoes off the raw, unweathered basalt cliffs and bounces between the tight, wet walls of the towers until the darkness itself feels solid with noise.

Despite the overwhelming crush of bodies and the roar of the acoustics, almost nothing moves quickly. The cold and the nature of the food source enforce a glacial patience. A wader takes four minutes to lift a heavy, webbed foot and set it down a few inches to the left. A wave of shell-creatures shifting along a warm pipe looks, in the dark, like the pipe itself is slowly swelling. Even the predators—long, segmented things with thermal-sensing pits along their jaws that slide through the crowded canals—do not strike; they simply press their mouths against their prey and wait hours for their cold-acting enzymes to do the work.

To walk through this place is to wade through a dark, freezing, deafening soup of stone, machine, and flesh. Beneath the soles of your boots, the sharp, unweathered gravel of a newborn mountain grates against the slick, self-healing polymer of a street that didn't exist ten years ago. Around your knees, the heavy, ice-cold water pushes past, thick with the nudging, indifferent bodies of creatures that have never known light. And all around, invisible in the pitch black, rises the wet, clicking, breathing geometry of a city that is slowly turning a raw crag of the world into a single, crowded organism.
