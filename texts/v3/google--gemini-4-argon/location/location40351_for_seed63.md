The Throat is a vertical chimney of basalt and sulfur-crusted rhyolite, situated near the crest of a dead volcanic ridge where the air is thin and tastes of old iron. It is not large—perhaps sixty paces from the eastern wall to the western overhang, and twice that from the lip of the drop down to the choked floor where the heat pools—but there is no empty space within it. 

The rock itself is ancient and settled, worn smooth not by water, which does not exist here even as vapor, but by the dry, greasy friction of millions of bodies over ten thousand generations. Every ledge, flute, and chimney-crack is rounded off, polished to a dull, graphite-like sheen. The stone is hot to the touch, fed by a dry magma conduit deep below the mountain's root, maintaining the chamber at the temperature of a baker's oven after the loaves are drawn.

In this dark, blistering pocket, the walls move.

What looks at first like a thick, cobbled rind of black and ochre mineral covering the rock is entirely alive. The chamber is packed to suffocation with a dry, thermophilic ecology that has bypassed water and plants altogether. The base of the chain is a lithovorous crust—not a moss, not a lichen, but a dense, leathery carpet of sessile, shell-backed tube-worms and sulfur-reducing polyps that drink the hot gases seeping directly through the pores of the stone. They grow over and upon one another in calcified tiers three feet thick, choking the chimney until the open air in the center is reduced to a narrow, twisting shaft no wider than a wagon.

Over this living masonry crawls the rest of the chamber’s weight. There are no gaps between them. Great, flattened arachnids with carapaces like flaking slate—each the size of a dinner platter—hang in overlapping shingles three deep, their twenty-jointed legs tucked tightly beneath them or hooked into the spiracles of their neighbors. Between and over the slate-backs flow slow, continuous rivers of blind, bristle-plated millipedes that feed on the shed skins and hot, dry dung of the larger creatures. Burrowed into the crevices between the tube-worms are colonies of pale, cartilaginous things shaped like skinned hands, which rhythmically fan the air to catch the microscopic snow of chitin-dust that constantly drifts downward through the dark.

Light barely reaches here. High above, the mouth of the chimney is pinched almost shut by leaning pinnacles of wind-scoured rock. At high noon, a single, razor-thin blade of white mountain sunlight slices down for twenty minutes, illuminating a vertical stripe of writhing, dusty brown and dull yellow before vanishing. Otherwise, the only light is the faint, bruised-purple radiative glow of the hottest vents at the bottom of the pit, and the occasional pinprick of dull orange bioluminescence when a predatory centipede is crushed or disturbed, leaking a dry, phosphorescent powder from its joints before its neighbors consume it.

Despite the staggering mass of life—tons of dry, armored flesh packed into a space the size of a village church—the Throat is eerie in its near-silence. There are no lungs here to shriek or hiss, no wings to buzz in the thin air. The heat and the crush forbid wasted motion. The sound of the place is a continuous, low-volume rustle, like a room full of dry autumn leaves turning over in their sleep, or wheat pouring slowly from a sack. *Shhh-tik. Shhh-tik.* It is the sound of ten million chitinous plates sliding over ten million other plates, of mandibles methodically scraping calcium from the stone, of slow, hydraulic legs shifting weight as the thermal currents rise and fall. 

And yet, beneath the crust of bodies, there are marks that do not belong to the animals.

Halfway down the chimney, where the heat is heaviest and the sulfur smell thick enough to choke a lunged creature in three breaths, a series of square niches have been chiseled into the primary basalt. They are arranged in a spiral that descends toward the floor. Most are completely packed with the segmented bodies of sleeping *skree*-mites, their outlines blurred by centuries of calcified worm-castings, but the geometry remains stubbornly visible under the living blanket. 

Spanning the narrowest part of the central shaft are three beams of petrified ironwood, dragged up from some long-vanished lowland forest ages ago and wedged into sockets in the rock. The wood has been baked black and hard as pig-iron by the dry heat, impervious to rot. Hanging from the center of the lowest beam, suspended by a braided rope of spun basalt-fiber, is a bell made of hammered copper. It has no clapper. Instead, its hollow interior is packed solid with a brooding cluster of velvet-skinned pseudoscorpions, their pincers locked together in a living ball that drips slowly like warm wax when the heat surges from below. 

Down on the floor—if it can be called a floor, being a five-foot-deep mattress of dry husks, shed shells, and hunting centipedes—stands a squat cairn of stacked stones that did not fall from the walls above. They are river-cobbles, carried up the mountain from miles away, smooth and foreign. The cairn is constantly being buried by the shifting drifts of biological detritus and constantly being uncovered again as the blind, shoveling beetles at the bottom of the food chain plow through the litter in huge, slow-moving spirals, grazing on the heat-cured remains of everything that falls from the crowded walls above.

The whole chamber breathes on a twenty-minute cycle. Deep in the mountain, a dry gas pocket builds pressure and vents through the floor. The temperature spikes twenty degrees. A wave of activity ripples upward from the bottom—a silent, frantic churning as the creatures nearest the vents surge outward and upward to escape scorching, forcing every tier above them to shift, climb, and compress. Predators use the chaos to lung-strike at newly exposed soft joints; eggs are released into the rising thermal updraft to lodge in the higher crevices; the dry whisper of chitin against chitin swells to the volume of a sudden rainstorm. Then the vent sighs shut, the heat settles back to its steady bake, and the millions of bodies slowly slide back down into their packed, interlocking equilibrium in the dark.
