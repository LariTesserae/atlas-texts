The water is the temperature of blood and heavy with chalk. It fills a shallow, bowl-shaped basin three miles across, sunk just below the shelf where the last of the surface light dies out. 

Nothing here was built with hands or iron, yet the basin is a city. It was secreted over ten thousand years by a vanished species of blind, colonial tubeworms that mortared their own calcium casings together with mineral paste drawn from the warm floor. What they left behind is an unbroken, multi-tiered metropolis of bone-white limestone and hardened silt: thousands of fused towers, aqueduct-like bridges, ribbed vaults, and honeycomb tenements that rise forty to sixty feet from the basin floor, separated by narrow, winding canals and sunken plazas. 

There are no sharp corners left. Centuries of warm, alkaline currents have licked the architecture smooth. Archways have been worn into oval throats; stair-stepped terraces have slumped into grooved ramps; the roofs of lower chambers have collapsed into circular courtyards choked with fine, velvety marl. Yet the layout remains stubbornly regular, an organized grid of thoroughfares and stacked chambers built to catch and funnel the thermal drifts that rise from cracks in the bedrock below.

The darkness is nearly absolute, broken only by the slow, metabolic embers of the things that live here now. A pale, greasy green outlines the lip of an archway where a mat of bacterial scum feeds on rising sulfur. A soft, bruised violet pulses in the deep recesses of a gallery as a colony of blind anemones digests a meal. You do not see the city all at once; you feel it in the sudden narrowing of the water pressure around you, in the thermal drafts that spill out of doorways like breath from an oven, and in the dim, shifting constellations of cold fire that trace the streets.

Plants cannot survive the dark, so what looks like vegetation is sparse and starved of color. Along the upper parapets, where the current is fastest, grow tall, solitary stands of black wire-kelp—not true kelp, but a tough, rootless fungus that feeds on dissolved nitrates. Its leafless stalks are as thick as thumbs and sway together with a slow, rubbery grace. In the courtyards below, pale, bulbous sponge-gourds cling to the lintels like slumped gargoyles, filtering the murky soup through gaping, toothless vents.

Because the builders are gone, the city has been thoroughly squatted by the basin’s fauna. The density of life inside the masonry is immense, layered floor by floor. Every calcified pore and tubular corridor is occupied. 

In the narrow alleys, shoals of finger-long, eyeless silverfish move in tight, braided ribbons, navigating the turns by the rebound of their own tiny pressure-wakes against the walls. When a ribbon splits around a worn pillar, it rejoins on the other side without a single scales-breadth of hesitation. Beneath them, the street-bed is a slow, shifting carpet of white porcelain-crabs and plated snails that graze the limestone bare, keeping the avenues clean of rot. 

Inside the hollow buildings, larger things hold territory. Broad, flat-bodied rays with skins like wet suede rest wedged into the ceiling vaults, dropping down like heavy blankets when the silverfish crowd too thick in the lobbies below. Three-foot eels, pale and translucent as boiled tendon, thread themselves through the plumbing of the ancient worm-tubes—entering through a bedroom window on the third tier and emerging from a cellar drain forty yards away. At the intersections of the wider avenues, giant, spindly-legged spider-crabs stand on stilts over the traffic, picking parasites off the backs of passing medium-sized grazers that queue up in the warm updrafts like cattle at a post.

Despite the crowding, the city is hushed. Water deadens impact, and the creatures here hunt and hide by touch and smell rather than sound. There are no roars, no splashes, no wind. Instead, there is an intimate, persistent under-noise: the dry, sandpapery rasp of ten thousand snail radulae scraping calcium; the soft, wet *thump* of a ray pinning its prey against a silt floor; the faint, rhythmic ticking of crab legs stepping over terraced roofs; and, underlying it all, the low, hollow *huff* of the thermal vents exhaling warm water up through the tall chimneys of the towers.

The whole basin breathes on a six-hour cycle. For four hours, the geothermal flues beneath the center of the town push hot, mineral-thick water upward. This is the active season: the chimneys smoke with white carbonate snow, the currents run briskly down the avenues toward the basin's rim, the wire-kelp stands straight, and the streets are choked with hunting eels and swirling bait-balls. Then the bedrock cools and inhales. For two hours, the cold, heavy water from the surrounding sea-shelf slides down over the rim of the bowl and floods the outer wards. The warm drafts stall. The white snow settles like dust over the roofs and windowsills. The crabs tuck themselves into alcoves, the eels coil inside the walls, and the silverfish sink into the cellars to wait for the stone to warm again. 

Nothing is ever built anymore, and nothing is ever truly demolished. The city simply wears down a fraction of an inch each century while the shells of the dead animals pile up in the basements, slowly cementing into new floor-stone under the weight of the warm, dark water.
