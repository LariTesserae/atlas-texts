The entire municipality of the Spindle-Cleft fits into a vertical fissure no wider than a courtyard, wedged thirty-two thousand feet up the sheer, unweathered flank of the Aethelgard uplift. From twenty paces away on the exterior ridge, there is no sign of it—only the brutal, freshly fractured planes of dark diorite and hornblende, sharp enough to slice leather, swept by a thin, dry stratosphere that smells of ozone and pulverized flint. 

Step through the wind-baffle—a slotted iron bulkhead bolted directly into a fault line—and the drop takes the stomach out from under you. The Cleft is four hundred feet deep and barely forty feet across, a jagged chimney of young rock that has never known rain. At this altitude, water does not fall; what little moisture exists in the world is locked in permanent, dirty-white rime six thousand feet below. Here, the air is bone-dry, thin, and bitingly cool, hovering perpetually just above freezing.

Into this narrow verticality, an entire industrial district has been packed with the density of a clock’s gearbox. 

There are no streets, only catwalks of stamped steel grating, counterweighted lifting cages, and tiered, cantilevered workshops built from riveted iron plates and magnesium-alloy struts. Everything is coated in a slick, dark patina of dry graphite lubricant—wet oil would gum and freeze in the draft, so the machinery of the Cleft runs on powdered carbon and molybdenum. The space is in constant, frantic motion, yet it produces almost no roar. The air is too thin to carry heavy bass notes, and the great drive-cables that drop down the center of the chasm are sheathed in braided wool and rawhide to keep their vibrations from shattering the brittle, newly-formed rock walls. Instead of the deafening clatter of a lowland factory, the Cleft is filled with a restless, breathy sibilance: the *shhh-tok* of pneumatic logic-valves, the whicker of leather drive-belts slipping over wooden pulleys, and the soft, dry hiss of venting nitrogen.

What gives the Cleft its true, chaotic turbulence, however, is not just the machinery, but the things living inside it.

Every warm exhaust manifold, every humming transformer box, and every tensioned cable is thick with the local fauna. Because the exterior mountain is a sterile void of razor-sharp stone, all biological life at this altitude has crowded into the heat-island of the industrial pocket. Most prominent are the *stokers*—pale, six-limbed marsupial-like creatures the size of terriers, with leathery, hairless skin and huge, heat-pits along their snouts. Thousands of them scramble constantly through the rigging. They have adapted entirely to the mechanized environment, nesting in the baffling of the condensers and feeding on the sulphur-reducing lithotrophic crusts—pale yellow and violet mineral-fungi—that grow inside the warm exhaust flues. 

Alongside the stokers are the *shale-moths*, heavy-bodied insects with wings like split mica that cluster by the tens of thousands around the dim, amber-glowing sodium vapor lamps. Their bodies are so numerous that as they shift and flutter in the turbulent updrafts from the lower smelters, they create moving, flickering curtains of shadow that make the entire iron canyon seem to breathe and warp.

The architecture itself is never still. The Aethelgard massif is geologically infant, still groaning and settling as it thrusts upward. To keep the rigid iron galleries from being crushed or torn apart by micro-tremors, the entire settlement is suspended on a web of massive leaf-springs, turnbuckles, and hydraulic shock-absorbers anchored into the raw diorite. Every few minutes, a tremor passes through the mountain—felt in the teeth rather than heard—and the whole city responds. Counterweights the size of freight cars drop twenty feet in a heartbeat; whole tiers of workshops slide laterally along greased rails with a muted *whuff* of displaced air; bridges telescope inward and spring back; and ten thousand stokers leap simultaneously from one swaying conduit to another in a silent, pale cascade of limbs and tails, only to immediately resume gnawing at the mineral-scale on the pipes.

Light comes in two forms, neither of them bright. From directly overhead, a razor-thin ribbon of sky—a bruised, zenith-indigo even at noon—lets down a cold, shadowless twilight that reaches only the topmost gantries. Deeper down, the darkness is broken by the orange glow of resistance coils, the violet arc-flash of automated spot-welders, and the biological luminescence of the yellow sulfur-crusts growing along the warm seams of the air-compressors. 

There is no soil on the ledges, no moss, not a single blade of grass or drop of mud. The floor of every platform is a crunchy carpet of shed mica-wings, graphite dust, and the dry, chalky pellets of the stokers, constantly swept into collection chutes by automated rotary brooms to be burned in the lower gasifiers. If you lean over a guardrail, the wind rushing up from the bottom of the siphon-shaft pulls at your clothes—a dry, warmish gale smelling of hot iron, ozone, and the musky, dry-fur scent of ten thousand warm bodies packed into the stone dark, all of it vibrating with the fast, quiet, relentless pulse of engines working at the edge of the sky.
