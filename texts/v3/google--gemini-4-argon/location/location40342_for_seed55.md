For three hundred miles in every direction, the seafloor is not sand or bedrock, but hand-fitted basalt. 

This is a submerged continent of terraced basins, canal trenches, and low, retaining walls, lying beneath two hundred feet of frigid, dark water. There is no natural topography left beneath it. Every rise is an ancient midden of quarried shale; every depression is a catchment basin dug out with horn picks and wooden shovels before the water rose, or perhaps built directly in the shallows as the ocean crept in over millennia. The builders are gone, and have been gone for so long that the corners of their millions of dry-stone walls have been ground into smooth, pillowy curves by the patient friction of the deep currents. 

The water is just above freezing, dense and heavy, carrying the faint, metallic taste of dissolved slate. Only a fraction of the sky’s light survives the trip down through the upper fathoms. What arrives at the stonework is a sourceless, gloomy indigo—not enough light to cast a shadow, only enough to separate the bulk of a wall from the water behind it, and to feed the dark-adapted flora that has claimed the masonry.

The stonework is entirely smothered in a lush, cold-water jungle. Because the underlying architecture is so uniform—endless tiers of chest-high walls, paved plazas, and miles of sunken causeways—the vegetation grows in strict, eerie geometries. Great forests of bull kelp and wide-bladed laminaria rise from the tops of the dikes like trees along an avenue, their pneumatocysts (gas bladders) straining upward toward the distant surface, holding the leathery brown fronds erect in the gloom. The stems are thick as a man's wrist, coated in a velvet of pale bryozoans. 

Lower down, filling the sunken courtyards and choking the ancient sluice-gates, are dense thickets of blood-red dulse, black Irish moss, and rubbery, translucent sea-lettuce. Where the mortarless joints of the stone floors have settled and pulled apart, pale holdfasts grip the crevices like gnarled knuckles. The vegetation is so thick that the stonework is often only visible by its shape: a forty-foot-wide avenue is now a long, ruler-straight trough of waving olive-drab fronds; a circular storage pit is a dense whirlpool of knotted, leathery ribbons. 

Nothing swims here. There are no darting silver schools, no crabs clicking over the cobbles, no eels in the masonry gaps. The water is too cold, or perhaps too poor in the right kind of lime, to support shell or bone. The only animal life is sessile and nearly indistinguishable from the stone and plant: patches of breadcrumb sponge the color of tallow encrusting the lintels of fallen doorways, and the occasional solitary sea anemone, pale and translucent as peeled grape, waiting in the shelter of a carved niche for falling detritus that rarely comes.

Without animals to stir the water or tear at the weeds, the environment is governed entirely by the slow, massive heave of the deep-water swell. 

There are no waves down here, only a vast, rhythmic displacement. Every twenty seconds, a million tons of cold water shifts five feet to the east; twenty seconds later, it shifts five feet back to the west. The entire submerged forest moves as a single organism. Millions of forty-foot kelp stalks lean in unison over the ancient roads, bowing their heads until their tips brush the cold basalt cobbles, holding that posture for a long beat, and then slowly, lazily righting themselves to lean the other way. 

This pendulum motion produces the only sound in the dark. It is a vast, low-frequency sigh—the wet, leathery rustle of billions of heavy fronds sliding against one another, accompanied by the deep, muted *clonk* of hollow kelp-bulbs bumping together in the gloom. Occasionally, a stone that has been pried loose by a wedging holdfast finally gives way, dropping two feet from a wall into a silt-choked gutter with a dull, muffled thud that travels miles through the dense liquid, before the silence closes back over it.

Time here is measured in the accumulation of marine snow and the wearing down of basalt. A fine, grey silt—made of dead plankton from the upper waters and the decayed mucilage of the kelp itself—drifts perpetually downward like ash. It settles into the ancient wheel-ruts, fills the carved drainage channels, and banks against the leeward sides of the walls, rounding off the last remaining angles of the architecture. When a giant kelp stalk dies, it does not rot quickly in the near-freezing dark; it lies across the submerged plazas like a fallen pillar of leather, taking decades to dissolve into the grey drift, slowly burying the handmade world beneath a blanket of its own quiet growth.
