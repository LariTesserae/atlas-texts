The air is the temperature of a recently vacated bed, thick enough to drink, and entirely dark. 

You know the scale of the basin not by sight, but by the delay of the drips. A heavy bead of condensation detaches from an overhead arch, falls, and strikes the shallow water below with a flat, leaden *plop*. Three seconds later, the echo returns from a retaining wall half a kilometer away, slightly warped in pitch, as though the intervening space had stretched while the sound was in transit.

This is the Sump of the Unbuilt—a sprawling, low-lying municipality that covers twenty square kilometers of sunken mudflat, though it has never known a builder, a tool, or a resident. The city is a geological and ontological habit. It is what the marsh does when the heat rises.

Beneath two inches of warm, tea-colored water lies a vast grid of boulevards, plazas, and foundations. The material is a slick, dark grey silt-stone that has the tactile memory of tallow. There are no sharp edges anywhere. Millions of years of thermal rain and rising groundwater have worn the structures into low, slumped approximations of habitation: colonnades softened into rows of dripping stalagmites, amphitheaters melted into concentric ripples of mud, aqueducts sagged in the middle like wet rope. The architecture is sparse, separated by wide, empty floodplains where the water sits entirely still, unbroken by wind or current. 

Light does not come from the sky—there is no sky, only a ceiling of black humidity that begins fifty feet up and continues forever. The only illumination is a sparse, bruisey luminescence that clings to the underside of the eroded lintels and seeps from the mortarless joints of the stone. It is the color of an afterimage when you press your palms too hard against your eyes: a dull, dirty violet that fails to illuminate the water two inches beneath it. It reveals only silhouettes and wet shine.

Nothing lives here in the zoological sense. There are no frogs in the tepid pools, no blind fish in the flooded basements, no insects whining in the wet heat. The water is sterile, smelling faintly of copper and steamed slate. Look closely at the edge of a sunken plaza, where the violet gloom catches the skin of the water, and you will see the sparse vegetation: solitary stalks of black, leafless reed, spaced ten or twenty meters apart like iron survey pins. They do not grow; they simply stand at fixed intervals, sweating a clear, viscous mucilage that runs down their stems and refuses to mix with the groundwater, drifting away in long, invisible ribbons.

The dynamic of the city is municipal, yet completely devoid of agency. The landscape is actively, restlessly revising its own layout, governed by a broken physics of fluid and memory.

Because the heat is so relentless, the water does not stay down. In certain districts—out across the wide, dark flats—gravity intermittently loses its grip on the moisture. You can hear it happening in the near-silence: a low, greasy *shhhk*, like silk being pulled through a ring. Across a three-block radius, the two-inch layer of marsh water lifts off the mud as a single, unbroken sheet. It rises slowly through the black air, carrying the impressions of the cobblestones and drain-grates it just left behind, holding their shapes in inverted relief on its underside. When this hovering table of water reaches a height of twenty or thirty feet, it cools by half a degree, loses its cohesion, and dumps itself back down—not as rain, but as a sudden, localized collapse of warm liquid that hits the ruined avenues with a single, booming slap, sending low waves washing through empty doorways a mile away.

The buildings respond to the water, or perhaps the water is what builds them. The erosion here works in both directions. Where the warm rain weeps steadily down the side of a worn buttress, the stone does not wash away; it accrues, thickening like candle wax, slowly extruding a new room, a blind window, a staircase that leads four steps up into empty, dripping air before terminating in a rounded nub. A half-mile to the east, an entire district of dark, porous towers is slowly sinking back into the silt, dissolving without a sound, their substance migrating through the subterranean mud to rise again as a newly formed, already ancient seawall on the western perimeter. 

To walk through this place is to wade through a warm bath that occasionally forgets its own depth. You might step onto a flat paving stone submerged under an inch of water, and find your foot sinking six feet through solid, weathered basalt that has temporarily surrendered its density, feeling like warm porridge before snapping back into stone the moment you pull your leg free. 

Distance is similarly waterlogged. Two isolated towers of weeping brick may stand half a mile apart across an empty, flooded commons. As the humidity thickens toward the center of the basin, the space between them condenses; their faint, violet-lit silhouettes drift together without traversing the mud, until their eaves touch and their runoff mingles in a braided stream. An hour later, as the groundwater drains into a subterranean sink, the space reasserts itself, and the towers are once again separated by a wide, dark expanse of tepid water and solitary black reeds.

There is no wind. The air moves only when displaced by a falling sheet of water or the slow, sighing collapse of a saturated wall. The quiet is immense, insulating, and wet—a vast, feverish dark where an empty city endlessly digests and reconstructs its own ruins.
