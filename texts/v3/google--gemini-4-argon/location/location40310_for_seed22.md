The sound arrives long before the ground makes sense. It is not the roar of wind or rushing water, because there is no wind to speak of and the water does not flow. It is a continuous, bone-shaking acoustic wall—millions of distinct, biological reports overlapping into a single deafening static. It sounds like an entire continent of dry wood being snapped in half, over and over, inside a cathedral made of brass.

When your eyes adjust to the gloom, the first thing you register is the emptiness. 

The valley is a shallow, forty-mile basin of fresh black basalt, dropped into the crust so recently that the rock hasn't had time to crumble into true soil. The cooling cracks are still knife-sharp, forming a neat, hexagonal grid across the lowlands. The air is warm, sitting at a perpetual, velvety seventy-five degrees, smelling faintly of ozone, wet slate, and crushed coriander. Above, there is no sky—only a flat, unbroken ceiling of sluggish violet cloud or atmospheric shielding that lets through a permanent, shadowless twilight. 

You can see for miles in every direction, unobstructed, and yet the valley is both a city and a jungle. The contradiction resolves itself only when you look at the ground, and then straight up. 

There is almost no three-dimensional mass here. The density of the world has been flattened or stretched into lines. 

The city consists of the Spires and the Traces. The Traces are ribbons of dull, silvery-black metamaterial inlaid flush with the basalt floor, organizing the entire basin into sweeping, geometric districts, avenues, and plazas. They don't carry traffic; they carry state-changes. Every few seconds, a localized gravity-ripple or a silent wave of gold luminescence washes down a Trace at four hundred miles an hour, leaving the air above it smelling of ionized copper. 

At the intersections of the Traces stand the Spires—residential and computational needles three thousand feet tall and no wider than a dinner plate. There are thousands of them, spaced hundreds of yards apart across the plain. They have no doors, no windows, and no visible seams. They are rods of pure, dark force-locked matter that hum at a sub-audible frequency, holding up nothing, casting no shadows in the diffuse purple gloom. You can walk right through a dense "downtown" sector and feel as though you are standing on an empty parking lot, save for the thin black lines drawn vertically against the distant mist.

The jungle has adapted to the architecture's refusal to take up space. 

Because the rock is young and dry, and the light is scarce, the flora does not grow in bushes or canopies. It grows as a blindingly lush, two-dimensional carpet and a network of high-tension wires. The basalt hexagons are entirely smothered in a six-inch-deep velvet of hyper-saturated emerald and cyan bryophytes—mosses so plush and metabolically fast you can watch their edges creep over a boot-tip in ten minutes. From this carpet shoot the *vesseltrees*: leafless, vascular vines no thicker than a human thumb, which use the city's Spires as trellises, or simply ride the ambient vertical magnetic fields straight up into the gloom. 

A single vesseltree might rise eight hundred feet without branching, a taut green wire strung between earth and cloud. Millions of them hang in the valley, spaced twenty or thirty feet apart, turning the vast open basin into a hall of vertical green strings. 

And that is where the water is. There are no rivers, no puddles, no rain. The moisture here is entirely captive, held in the air as a warm, clinging dampness until the city's Traces pulse. When a thermodynamic wave runs down an avenue, the air above it cools by three degrees for a tenth of a second. Instantly, beads of heavy condensation form on the millions of vertical vines and the slick skins of the Spires. The water doesn't fall; it glides down the vines in synchronized, silver rings, drunk greedily by the velvet moss at the bottom before it can ever touch the stone. 

This brings the fauna, and the fauna brings the noise.

Because the world is an open void strung with vertical lines, the animals do not walk, and they do not fly in flocks that would blot out the view. They are small, hyper-dense, and innumerable, clinging to the vines and packing the moss carpet like jewels in a watch. 

The deafening roar that fills the valley is the sound of *hydraulic predation* and *sonic photosynthesis*. 

Clinging to the tension-vines are millions of creatures no larger than walnuts, encased in glassy, geometric shells that mimic the city’s metamaterials. To feed on the descending rings of condensation and on each other, they move by explosive pneumatic leaps, snapping their shells shut to cavitate the humid air. Every leap produces a sharp, rifle-crack *pop*. With ten million of them hunting across the forty-mile basin at any given second, the pops blur into a physical pressure wave that vibrates in your teeth.

Worse—or grander—are the Choir-Galls. These are symbiotic growths where the lush green vines interface directly with the city's Spires. Swollen, basketball-sized bladders of translucent flesh, they absorb the waste-heat and computational runoff of the post-physical city and convert it into sugar by vibrating. They use the three-thousand-foot Spires as resonator rods, bowing the black needles like cello strings. Each Spire screams a continuous, multi-harmonic chord that travels through the basalt floor and up through the soles of your feet. 

Stand in the middle of a broad municipal plaza. The stone beneath you is a jagged, unweathered puzzle of black rock buried under neon-green velvet. Half a mile away, the next Spire rises like a pencil stroke against the dim violet horizon. You can see all the way to the jagged rim of the valley, ten miles off, through a haze of vertical green threads. There is nothing between you and the horizon to block your path. The space feels hollow, vast, and terrifyingly vacant. 

Yet the ground at your feet is a boiling carpet of iridescent, thumb-sized amphibians undulating in waves to chase the condensation rings. The air is thick with the warm damp of a bathroom after a shower. A silent flash of violet math races past your ankles along a buried Trace; a second later, ten thousand gallons of dew materialize on the vines overhead, sliding down in a glittering sheet; and in response, the entire basin detonates in a crescendo of biological artillery and screaming harmonic chords so loud it strips the thoughts right out of your head.
