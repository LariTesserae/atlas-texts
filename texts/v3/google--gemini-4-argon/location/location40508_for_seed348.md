The air in the Sump of Six-Gears tastes of cold brass, machine oil, and pulverized slate. 

It is not a large place—perhaps the size of a broad town square or an unroofed cathedral—sunk into a natural basin of dark, stratified shale. Its boundaries are steep, terraced retaining walls of poured concrete and riveted iron plates, rising eighty feet to a rim where the sky hangs like a sheet of galvanized tin. The light is the flat, shadowless white of a high, unbroken overcast. It does not brighten toward noon or dim toward evening; it simply remains at the exact luminosity of a draftman’s studio, laying bare every bolt, puddle, and seam in the basin below.

In this wide, sunken plaza, the buildings stand far apart. There are only seven of them: tall, narrow, windowless towers of corrugated iron, brick, and heavy timber, spaced across the shale floor like pieces left on a vast chessboard. Between them stretches open ground—flat flagstones worn smooth as soap, interlaced with shallow drainage channels and sunken iron rail tracks that terminate abruptly in the rock. There is not a blade of grass in the basin. There is no moss in the mortar, no lichen on the stone, no weed breaking through the slag. The only thing that resembles growth is a pale, mineral efflorescence—a salty, crusted bloom that creeps an inch up the bases of the brick towers—and slick, leathery aprons of black bacterial grease that pool beneath the exhaust vents.

Despite the emptiness between the structures, the air is deafening. 

Beneath the flagstones, deep in the under-plenum, gigantic flywheels and subterranean turbines spin in an endless, governor-locked equilibrium. They never speed up; they never wind down. Their work is the steady, rhythmic thrum of a thousand tons of oiled steel turning in the dark, venting upwards through brass standpipes as plumes of warm, wet breath. When this breath hits the cool air of the basin, it condenses instantly. Everything in the Sump is damp. Water beads on the iron rivets, runs in thin, oily rivulets down the brick facades, and collects in shallow, iridescent puddles in the saucer-like depressions of the worn flagstones. 

And covering the towers, the gantries, and the pipes, in numbers so immense they alter the architecture of the buildings, are the creatures.

To a glance, the towers appear to be lagged in thick, shimmering, copper-colored scales. Look closer, and the scales are breathing. Millions of *clinker-moths* and *valve-lice*—heavy-bodied arthropods with carapaces that look like stamped brass and tarnished silver—blanket the vertical surfaces. They cluster six inches deep around the warm seams of the boilers and the steam-jackets, arranged in overlapping, geometric shingles. Above them, occupying every iron truss, telegraph wire, and crane-jib, sit the *kettle-rooks*: squat, oil-slicked birds with heavy, hooked beaks like pipe-wrenches and throats that pouch out like leather bellows.

The noise of the Sump is a permanent, solid wall of sound—a three-part industrial chord that has not varied in pitch or volume for centuries. The deep bass is the subterranean rumble of the engines; the midrange is the continuous, metallic *clack-shirr-clack* of millions of brassy insect wings rubbing together to shed condensation; the treble is the piercing, rhythmic hissing and valve-clatter of the kettle-rooks, which mimic the pressure-release vents so precisely it is impossible to tell bird from boiler.

Nothing here hunts, and nothing flees. The ecosystem is as locked and time-invariant as the turbines below. 

The cycle is entirely closed. The underground vents cough up a steady, warm mist rich with sulfur, paraffin-tallows, and mineral salts. This mist settles onto the iron plates, feeding the black bacterial grease. The valve-lice and clinker-moths graze on the grease, scraping it from the metal with mandibles that sound like tiny files. The kettle-rooks walk methodically along the gantries, plucking up the older, slower insects with the regularity of stamping-presses. When the birds or the insects die, their heavy, metallic-shelled bodies drop onto the wide flagstones below. 

There, in the open spaces between the towers, the ground-fauna waits. Pale, multi-legged scavengers the size of terrier dogs—creatures that look like articulated porcelain insulators strung on tendon—scurry out from the drainage grates. They dismantle the fallen shells in seconds, dragging the chitin and bone down into the grates, where the organic matter falls into the subterranean furnaces to be rendered back into tallow and steam. As soon as a shell is cleared, the porcelain scavengers retreat to the grates. The wide, wet flagstones are left completely bare again, shining under the flat white sky.

The erosion here is profound, yet entirely stabilized. Under the millions of tiny, gripping feet, the brick corners of the towers have been rounded off like river stones. The iron railings along the catwalks have been polished to a bright, greasy silver by the bellies of the rooks, while the non-structural ironwebbing between them has rusted through and vanished long ago. Water drips from a specific bolt on the third gantry into a puddle below; it has dripped at the rate of forty drops a minute for so long that it has drilled a neat, smooth cylinder four inches deep into the solid shale, which overflows into a worn groove that leads to a grate. 

There are no seasons in the Sump. No wind reaches down over the eighty-foot concrete lip to disturb the vertical rise of the steam plumes. The temperature remains a permanent, clammy forty-eight degrees—except within three inches of the tower walls, where the combination of boiler-heat and packed insect bodies creates a tropical micro-skin.

If you stand in the center of the stone plaza, you are in an wide, empty room, fifty yards from the nearest structure. Your boots stand in a quarter-inch of cold, rainbow-sheened water. Around you is a vast openness of grey slate and wet iron rails. Yet the surrounding vertical surfaces are so dense with life that the walls themselves seem to writhe in place—a slow, seething tapestry of tarnished bronze and wet feathers, roaring with the combined voice of a foundry and a rookery, suspended forever in the cool, grease-scented light.
