The air at this altitude is thin, cool, and dry enough to preserve anything forever. It tastes of chalk and static. 

You are standing on the Spine of the Oldest Fault, though "fault" implies a violence the stone forgot a billion years ago. The elevation is extreme—far above where clouds would form if there were any moisture in the atmosphere to make them—yet the terrain is not jagged. Time has licked the mountain range smooth. The peaks are vast, undulating domes of pale, waxy granite and slate-gray talc, worn down to their structural cores by an eternity of dry, thin wind that no longer blows. 

Between the domes, the drops are vertical and bottomless, plunging miles down into a dry, lavender haze. Yet the ridges themselves are wide, gentle, and open, stretching out for leagues in a series of sweeping, interconnected plateaus. The light comes from a sun the color of unbleached linen, pinned permanently at a forty-five-degree angle in a slate-blue sky. The shadows never lengthen; the rocks never warm past the temperature of a cellar root.

At first glance, you think the ridges are covered in deep, drifted banks of gray-white gravel or bone-meal. Then your eyes adjust to the density of it, and you realize the ground is entirely composed of *them*.

The fauna here has reached its terminal maximum. Every horizontal surface, every shallow basin, every saddle between the smooth peaks is packed three-feet deep with living, overlapping organisms. They have no common name, only appearances: things like six-legged alabaster tortoises with shells as thin as paper; long, segmented cylinders of dry chitin that curl around one another like sleeping hounds; creatures resembling basketball-sized bundles of pale feathers and calcified knuckles. 

There are millions of them in a single valley. They are packed shoulder to shoulder, shell to carapace, creating a continuous, bumpy, living pavement that stretches across the pale stone for miles. 

Despite their overwhelming numbers, there is almost no movement, and even less sound. The metabolic rate of this place has slowed to a geological crawl. Every few minutes, a single ivory leg will extend with agonizing slowness, shifting a creature’s weight by half an inch, producing a dry, papery *scritch* that travels clearly through the thin air. A million hearts are beating here, perhaps once an hour. They do not eat one another. There is no predation, no panic, no mating season. They have achieved a crowded, motionless truce with existence.

Because there is no water, their biology is mineral and electrostatic. And because the world here is fraying at the seams, they do not quite obey the laws of solid matter. 

Look closely at where the crowd is thickest—in the wide, saucer-like depressions of the ancient stone. The creatures do not merely rest on top of one another; they intersect. A pale, segmented flank passes directly through the ribbed dome of its neighbor, the two bodies occupying the same space with a faint, smoky blur at the seam, like double-exposed film. Where three or four creatures overlap in the same cubic foot of space, the air above them shimmers with a dry, geometric distortion—little floating cubes of inverted light, or tiny, silent tears in the air that show the rock three feet below as if the creatures weren't there at all. 

They have been sitting here so long that reality has grown tired of calculating their boundaries.

The only vegetation in this high, parched crowd consists of the Glass-Briers. Every hundred yards or so, rising up through the carpet of motionless beasts, is a solitary, leafless bush made of brittle, black silica. Their branches grow in rigid, ninety-degree angles, like three-dimensional crosshatching. Sometimes, a branch grows straight through the torso of a white-shelled beast; neither the plant nor the animal seems inconvenienced by the intersection. The briers feed on the static electricity generated by the slow, occasional friction of the millions of shells, and perhaps on the ambient breakdown of space itself. 

Scattered across the ridges are the remains of those who tried to herd or harvest this endless, static flock. 

The structures are small and far between: waist-high windbreaks built from flat slabs of talc, mortarless and dry. Inside these rings, the fauna is just as thick as outside, having slowly flowed over and *through* the stone walls centuries ago. Rising from the pale sea of carapaces are tall, crooked tripods lashed together from the long, hollow femur-bones of larger, extinct things, bound with strips of stiff, untanned hide that have freeze-dried into iron hardness. 

Hanging from these tripods are primitive tools: heavy mallets chipped from flint, long pry-bars of cold-hammered native copper, and bone chisels. If you pick up a copper pry-bar, you will find it has no weight at all until you lift it above your head, at which point it becomes almost too heavy to hold. Gravity here has developed localized habits, pooling in some spots and draining entirely from others. Near the edge of the great chasms, you can see flat, chipped-stone hand-axes hanging motionless in the empty air, three feet out over the abyss, left behind by hands that let go of them a thousand years ago in a spot where "down" simply ceased to apply.

Occasionally, a sound breaks the vast, cool quiet. It is never a cry or a call. It is the sound of a primitive stone cairn, built on a high dome, suddenly settling—not by falling down, but by two of its stones quietly sliding *into* one another until they occupy the same volume, accompanied by a soft, low *thrum* like a plucked bass string muffled by wool. 

Then the silence returns, broken only by the rare, dry *tick* of a chitinous claw shifting against ancient granite, under a linen-white sun that refuses to go down.
