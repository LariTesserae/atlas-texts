The place is a sunken quarry-ward, no larger than four city blocks, dropped into a clean, rectangular fault in the coastal shelf. Because the rift is geologically young, the walls of the basin have not yet slumped into hills; they rise in sheer, unweathered steps of dark grey basalt, their corners still crisp and square as if cut by a mason’s saw only a season ago. Looking up from the basin floor, the sky is a neat, bright rectangle of pale morning light, clear and shadowless, pouring straight down into the stone box.

Despite this enclosure, the ward does not feel crowded. The urban infrastructure here was built with an unusual, deliberate sparseness. Six tall, narrow brick switching-towers and three long cast-ironboiler halls stand at wide intervals across the flat basin floor, separated by broad expanses of unworked stone and deep, lush beds of vegetation. There are no alleys or warrens; between any two buildings lies fifty paces of open air and waist-high greenery. Connecting the towers at thirty feet above the ground is a minimal grid of black iron gantries and thick copper power-lines that hum with a steady, low-voltage vibration.

The air is cool and perennially damp, though it rarely rains. There is no river at the bottom of the fault, no standing ponds or marshes. Instead, the moisture comes from the machines. The three boiler halls work on a slow, heavy cycle, venting plumes of warm exhaust that immediately condense against the cold basalt walls and the cool morning air. Everything is coated in a fine, cold sweat. Beads of water gather on the undersides of the iron gantries, on the glass globes of the arc-lamps, and on the flanks of the brass pressure-tanks, dripping down in a continuous, irregular patter onto the foliage below. 

Fed by this relentless mechanical dew, the plant life is thick and vigorous, though it grows in distinct, manicured-looking pillows rather than a tangled wilderness. Great, waxy-leaved gunneras and banks of emerald hart’s-tongue fern fill the sunken squares between the paved walkways. Moss the color of oxidized copper climbs the brick foundations of the switching-towers, stopping in a neat horizontal line six feet up where the bricks grow too warm from the internal dynamos. The contrast is sharp: black, angular volcanic rock, red industrial brick, and the bright, wet, rubbery green of cold-weather foliage. 

What dominates the ward, more than the sight of the ironwork or the ferns, is the sound. Because the basalt walls are flat and unweathered, the quarry acts as an acoustic amplifier. The industrial machinery moves slowly—huge, greased flywheels taking ten full seconds to complete a single revolution, giant brass pistons sliding inward with a patient, glacial deliberation—yet every intake of air and every release of pressure is deafening. A valve opens on the eastern boiler hall with a long, resonant *shhh-clack* that echoes four times off the quarry walls before dying. Beneath the slow, rhythmic sighing of the engines is the constant, loud roar of the ventilation shafts drawing cool air down into the earth, and over that, the ear-splitting clamor of the birds.

The ward is teeming with animal life that has adapted to the warmth of the pipes and the safety of the vertical walls. Thousands of starlings and heavy, slate-colored rock doves roost along the iron gantries and in the sharp horizontal crevices of the basalt. Their chattering and wing-beats merge with the hiss of the steam into a single, continuous wall of noise. Down among the wet ferns, the ground is alive with large, slow-moving creatures: colonies of speckled land-crabs that sidle across the warm iron floor-plates of the engine rooms, and fat, sleek-coated civets that sleep curled against the base of the humming transformers, entirely unbothered by the din. 

Nothing here hurries. The workers who tend the ward—only a dozen or so visible at any time across the wide, open plazas—walk with a measured, unhurried gait, wearing heavy wool coats dark with condensation and brass ear-defenders clamped over their heads. A rail-cart loaded with coal slag inches along an elevated track, pulled by a cable that winds onto a drum at the speed of a growing vine. It takes the cart twenty minutes to cross the four hundred yards from the central shaft to the northern lift. As it moves, the birds barely shift out of its way, stepping aside onto the warm copper cables, preening their damp feathers in the clear, bright light while the great iron lungs of the buildings breathe slowly in and out below them.
