The water has been up here longer than the gravity has known what to do with it.

At an altitude where stone should be sharp and air should be thin, there is instead the Shelves—a continent-wide tableland of drowned plateau, five miles above the true lowlands, held in place not by basin walls but by a tiredness in the local physics. Water does not cascade off the edges of this height; it thickens there, turning gelatinous and uncertain at the drop-offs, curving back inward like mercury on glass. You are inside a warm, perched ocean that has forgotten how to fall.

The stone beneath is so old that its mineralogy has simplified into something like grey tallow. There are no pebbles, no grains of sand, no sharp fractures. Millions of years of warm, slow-churning currents have licked the mountain summits down into immense, undulating whale-backs of smoothed basal rock. They rise and dip across thousands of leagues, never breaking a surface that doesn’t quite exist. Above you, the water does not end in a chop of waves and sky; it simply loses conviction, grading over two hundred vertical feet from warm brine into a breathable, hot mist so dense with suspended globules that fish occasionally swim right out of the ceiling and hang in the fog, perplexed, before sinking back into the wet.

Light is an internal property here, sourceless and bruised. It is the color of dark amber and wet slate, filtering sideways through miles of suspended silt and botanical sheddings. Nothing casts a shadow. Instead, objects cast faint halos of slightly clearer water around themselves, as if presence itself displaces the murk.

In this warm, dim soup, the flora has detached from the concept of soil. Because the rock is too slick and ancient to take root in, the forests are suspension-bound. Great, tangled mat-systems of bladder-fern and iron-kelp hang at mid-depth, some the size of provinces, drifting at the pace of continental drift. Their roots don't grip; they dangle down to brush the greasy stone, absorbing dissolved mineral paste through pale, feathery fingers. Above these root-curtains rise the canopies: bulbous, bloated trees made of rubbery cartilage rather than wood, filled with warm gas that pulls them taut toward the mist-ceiling. Their leaves are thick as tongues and arranged in spirals, constantly opening and closing in slow, peristaltic rhythms to gulp the nutrient-broth. Everything is close. You cannot swim ten yards without parting heavy, velvet curtains of hanging moss-slime or squeezing between the warm, pulsing trunks of gas-willows that grow horizontally, braiding into one another until the water is segmented into millions of soft, green-lit rooms.

It is furiously alive, yet almost entirely hushed. Water this thick and cluttered swallows vibration. There is no roar of surf, no trickle. The dominant sound is a wet, subterranean *thrum*—the collective hydraulic creak of a billion gas-bladders expanding and contracting as thermal plumes rise from the ancient rock below. Now and then, a mile-wide mat of vegetation tears apart under its own growth-weight, and the sound that reaches you is only a muffled, bass *flump*, like a heavy blanket dropped in an adjacent room. 

The weirdness of the Shelves is a matter of tired geometry. The place has been eroding for so long that the distinction between *object* and *medium* has worn down. Where the warm currents swirl for centuries in the lee of a stone mound, the water itself becomes semi-permanent, developing a skin, a grain, a history, until you can push your hand against a whorl of current and feel it give like warm wax before it remembers to be liquid. Conversely, the oldest stone outcroppings have absorbed so much water into their lattice that they have become osmotic; they swell during the warm seasons and slump during the cool, slowly oozing downhill through the tangle of roots like grey glaciers of warm clay.

Distance is unreliable. Because the light bends around the thermal layers and the dense vegetation, you can sometimes look through a gap in the bladder-ferns and see the back of your own head, fifty yards away and slightly below you, wavering in a greenish mirage. Swim toward it, and the corridor of kelp simply lengthens, the plants growing into the space faster than your stroke can close it, until the corridor pinches shut with a soft, slimy kiss and leaves you in a different chamber altogether.

Scattered through this green-and-ochre labyrinth are the Webs. They are the only built things, though "built" implies an architect with hands, and nothing here has had hands for an epoch. They are structures of braided calcium and dark, cured tendon, stretched between the oldest stone humps or anchored right into the stiffened eddies of the water itself. They look like the ribcages of cathedrals or the frameworks of enormous, basket-woven astrolabes, twenty or thirty feet across, covered in a velvet coat of brown algae. If you cut the algae away, the bone-white material underneath is warm—warmer than the water—and hums lightly against the teeth. They do nothing. They are not shelters; they have no floors, for nothing here walks. They are simply alignments. If you float at the center of one of these bone-cages, the local water clears instantly, the surrounding kelp pulls back as if scalded, and for a few minutes, gravity reasserts its traditional direction with terrifying sharpness, pulling your blood to your feet before the ancient, sideways logic of the plateau washes back in and dissolves the sensation.

Animal life is an afterthought, dwarfed by the aggressive botany. You might float for three days through the pulsing, cartilaginous colonnades and see nothing with eyes. Then, a shadow will detach itself from the underside of a rock-mound: a thing like a forty-foot mantle of sheer, translucent bowel, rippling without fins, digesting the silt directly through its skin as it glides. Or a cluster of small, shell-less snails the size of fists, which do not crawl on surfaces but climb the boundaries between warm and hot water layers, grazing on the refracted light that gathers at the thermal seams. They leave trails of silver mucus that hang in the water like tinsel for days, slowly tangling in the branches of the drowning trees.

And always, the weather moves through the submerged heights. Great fronts of super-heated, mineral-heavy water roll in from the center of the plateau—underwater storms that don't bring wind, but rather a sudden, suffocating increase in density. When a front hits, the dim amber light bruises to a deep, venous purple. The hanging forests are pressed downward toward the greasy stone floor by the sheer weight of the heavier water above them. The gas-trees groan—a low, cello-like vibration you feel in your sternum—as their bladders compress. Spores the size of melons are squeezed from the canopy in millions, glowing with a faint, sickly bioluminescence as they sink, only to fall upward instead, caught in the inverted gravity of the storm's wake, drifting into the ceiling-mist where they vanish into the warm, grey nothing that serves this world for a sky.
