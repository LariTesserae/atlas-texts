There is no ground here. If you drop a stone—Assuming you could find a stone, which you cannot, so say instead that you drop a dense, calcified seed-gall—it does not strike a floor. It falls through the warm, wet gloom for three minutes, four, ten, until the mist swallows it, or until its trajectory bends sideways and it comes to rest against the underside of a braided arch six miles away, held there by a sudden, localized misunderstanding of gravity. 

The world is an endless, three-dimensional lattice of living basketry, suspended in an abyss of warm cloud. 

Everything is vegetation, and everything is built. There is no wild growth. Every trunk, every tendril, every spanning root-cable has been split, pleated, half-severed, bent over a knee, and grafted back into itself. It is an architecture of pleaching and wattling on a continental scale. Great ribs of dark, fibrous wood, thick as river-barges, curve upward for leagues to meet in vaulted groins, their joints lashed together by parasitic vines that were tied into square knots centuries ago and have since swollen into bulbous, living knuckles. Between these primary ribs stretch miles of kosong—vast, hollow voids of thin, warm air—giving the whole world the density of a spiderweb spanning a canyon. You can look down, up, or sideways for twenty miles and see only the faint, charcoal-sketched lines of distant causeways, hurdles, and woven aqueducts receding into the mauve twilight.

Who wove it is no longer a sensible question. The tool-marks are everywhere—the long, scalloped scars of flint adzes, the bruised fibers where stone mauls hammered a tendon-vine flat to make it split and branch, the crude bone wedges still embedded in the forks of ancient boughs, now half-digested by dark green bark. There is no metal here, no glass, no wire, no engines. Just the patient, monstrous accumulation of manual craft, a billion simple joins made by hand, left to grow, sag, and fuse over geological time. 

And it is old. The weave has settled. Great spans of wickerwork that were once taut bridges have slumped into deep catenary bellies, their undersides bearded with grey, weeping moss. Where a living truss snapped five hundred years ago, the frayed ends did not rot away; they sprouted pale, blind rootlets that groped through the empty air for decades until they found a neighboring strut, latched on, and pulled themselves taut again, healing the ruin into a new, skewed geometry. 

The air is high-altitude thin, leaving the lungs slightly frantic, yet it holds the wet, enveloping warmth of a greenhouse at night. There is no sun, only a pervasive, directionless bruise-colored luminescence that seems to seep from the wet bark itself, or from the miles of cloud compressed between the upper and lower vaults. Because of the scale of the voids, the lattice makes its own weather. Warm, damp updrafts rise from the unfathomable depths, cooling just enough as they pass through the high, sparse woven vaults to condense directly onto the wood. It rarely rains in drops; instead, the world sweats. Every braided balustrade, every knotted wall-screen is slick with a permanent film of warm water. It runs down the grooves of the bark in silent rivulets, pooling in the hollows of giant, spoon-shaped leaves that have been stitched together with thorn-needles to form cisterns the size of amphitheaters. When a cistern grows too heavy, its supporting vines stretch, tipping the leaf-basin until tons of water spill over the lip—not falling with a roar, but shearing apart in the thin air into silent, drifting curtains of mist before they have fallen a thousand feet.

Sound does not carry here. The combination of the thin, high air and the millions of acres of damp, spongy bark and moss acts as an absolute acoustic deadener. A scream turns into a flat, muffled *tock* fifty paces away. The entire biosphere moves in a state of hushed, velvet suppression. 

Yet the lattice is teeming. In the dimness, the woven structures are constantly shifting with a slow, crawling skin of fauna. Because the spaces between the wooden struts are so vast, the animals are built for the void or the tightrope. Great, flat-bodied creatures like living cloaks of mottled lichen glide silently from one distant colonnade to another, taking twenty minutes to cross a single bay of empty air. Along the wet, braided cables move processions of long-limbed, gangly beasts covered in damp fur that grows its own camouflage of liverworts; they walk with slow, deliberate placements of four-toed, grasping feet, never making a slip, their dark eyes enormous in the gloom. Predation happens in absolute silence: a camouflaged section of woven guardrail suddenly uncoils, reveals itself to be a hundred-foot length of segmented cartilage and Chitin, envelopes a passing glider, and slowly constricts while the surrounding mist absorbs even the sound of cracking bone.

The deeper one goes into the lattice, the less the rules of space hold together. The architecture was woven over such a long span of time, and across such unmapped immensities, that the geometry has gone sour. 

Perspective fails first. You can walk along a damp, springy catwalk of braided cedar-analogue, aiming for a watchtower of woven thorns that looks to be a half-mile ahead. You walk for three hours. The tower does not get larger; instead, the catwalk beneath your feet widens until it is a boulevard, and the tower is suddenly beside you, small enough to rest your hand upon its roof, teeming with thumb-sized, six-legged apes that watch you with solemn, wet eyes. 

Direction is a matter of biological habit rather than physics. The sap in the great structural vines flows toward whatever the plant considers "up," and gravity follows the sap. Thus, a traveler walking along the top of a wet, mossy arch may watch a herd of grazing, slug-like browsers creeping along the underside of the very same branch, their heavy shells hanging straight "down" toward the sky, droplets of water falling from their snouts upward into the abyss. Where two mega-vines grown from different epochs are lashed together, their respective realities chafe. At these junctions, the air shimmers with a greasy, chromatic haze. Step across the knot, and the warm mist instantly coats your skin in a rime of boiling-hot frost; look back, and the path you just walked is no longer behind you, but transects your current position at a ninety-degree angle, receding into a dimness of a slightly different hue.

Sometimes, the weaving loops through time as casually as it loops through space. You will come across a section of hurdles that is currently being built—freshly split green wood oozing white, pungent latex, bone wedges driven into the clefts, the bark still warm from the friction of a stone saw—though there is no builder in sight, only the vast, silent emptiness and a few translucent amphibians licking the sap. Follow that fresh rail for a hundred yards, and without seam or transition, it is four thousand years old: petrified into iron-hard heartwood, choked with climax-epiphytes, half-collapsed and re-sprouted, holding up a terrace where generations of blind, silk-spinning birds have built a city of wet grey cocoons. 

There is no center to the lattice, and no edge. There is only the warm, damp smell of crushed leaves and old water, the springy give of living wicker beneath the feet, the silent drift of pale creatures across the gulfs, and the endless, patient work of the wood, knitting the void together in the dark.
