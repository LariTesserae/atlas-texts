The altitude is high enough that the sky has lost its color, thinning into an indigo so dark it borders on the astronomical, even when the sun is theoretically above the horizon. Below that void lies the Caul: a wide, bowl-shaped plateau rimmed by ancient, rounded teeth of dark basalt. The rock is old—not jagged, not freshly broken, but smoothed by eons of ice-scour and wind into immense, whale-backed ridges that enclose a basin ten miles across. 

Between the ridges, the space is mostly air. Cold, thin, razor-dry air.

Yet the stone itself is hidden beneath a yard-thick pelt of black and silver flora. It is an alpine tundra gone hypertrophic. There are no trees—nothing rises above knee-height—but the ground is a continuous, suffocatingly lush mattress of deep-pile mosses, rubbery-leaved rosettes, and coiled, metallic ferns. This vegetation does not grow toward the light, because there is almost none. Instead, it grows tightly packed to trap the geothermal heat bleeding slowly from the old volcanic stone beneath, and to drink the mist. 

Water does not fall here as rain; it rises and settles as condensation. Every few hours, the barometric pressure in the basin drops, and the air turns briefly into a cold, clammy fog that coats everything in a slick, dark sheen before freezing into rime, then sublimating back into the dark sky. The moss drinks the phase-change. If you press a boot into the turf, dark, icy water wells up around the sole, though a foot above the ground the air will chap skin in minutes.

Across this wide, empty bowl, the machines are grazing.

There are no roads, no pylons, no sheds or fences. There has never been a human structure here. The machines are an introduced, feral industry, dropped here generations ago or migrated from some lowlands long since forgotten, now entirely integrated into the biological pace of the plateau. They are large, six-legged dredge-engines of tarnished brass and weathered cast iron, shaped vaguely like harvesting combines crossed with crabs. There are perhaps a dozen of them visible across the ten-mile expanse, spaced miles apart, maintaining a vast, territorial emptiness between one another.

Because the air is so thin and the moss so thick, the machines make almost no sound. Their massive, articulated legs are wrapped in thick bushels of dead lichen that act as snowshoes and mufflers. When a five-ton iron dredge shifts its weight, there is only the wet *shhhk* of compressed turf and the faint, rhythmic ticking of cooling metal. Their engines do not roar; they run on closed-cycle thermal differences, producing only a deep, sub-audible vibration you feel in your teeth when one passes within a hundred yards, accompanied by a plume of warm, greasy steam that immediately condenses into the surrounding dark.

They move constantly, raking the metallic ferns with articulated iron combs, harvesting the mineral nodules that the plants draw up from the basalt. And because the machines are warm, and because they disturb the turf, they are the center of the plateau’s ecology.

Every dredge-engine is trailed by a silent, bustling wake of cold-adapted life. Pale, eyeless quadrupeds the size of foxes, covered in dense white quills, trot directly beneath the machines' bellies, snapping up the sluggish, antifreeze-blooded worms exposed by the iron rakes. Flocks of heavy, flightless birds with greasy black feathers roost directly on the machines' exhaust manifolds, preening in the dark, fighting silently with hooked beaks for the warmest spots on the trembling iron casings. When a machine vents a burst of condensation, the birds scatter in a quiet flurry of leathery wings, only to settle back onto the pistons a moment later.

Above them, riding the freezing thermals of the empty air, are the gliders—translucent, manta-like creatures five feet across that never land on the ground, instead draping themselves over the tall brass intake-chimneys of the machines to absorb heat before peeling off into the gloom to hunt the quill-foxes. 

The light comes entirely from the dynamic friction of this ecosystem. As the iron combs scrape through the dense, metallic ferns, they trigger a chemical panic in the plants—a slow, bruising bioluminescence. Behind each moving engine, a half-mile scar of deep, industrial violet light glows in the moss, slowly fading over the course of an hour back into blackness. From the rim of the plateau, looking down into the vast, hollow basin, you cannot see the terrain at all. You see only a gulf of dark air, a few faint stars above, and the slow, silent cross-hatching of purple tracks creeping across the void, each headed by the dim, orange pinhole of an engine’s pilot light and the pale, ghostly shapes of animals moving in its warmth. 

Nothing calls out. In the extreme cold and low pressure, vocalization is a waste of heat and breath. Predation happens in total silence: a glider drops from the dark, envelopes a quill-fox in a rubbery membrane, and the only sound is a brief rustle of stiff moss. A machine reaches the edge of a weathered basalt gully, pauses as its gyroscopes click softly in the dark, and pivots, venting a sigh of steam that freezes instantly into diamond dust, drifting down to feed the waiting black ferns below.
