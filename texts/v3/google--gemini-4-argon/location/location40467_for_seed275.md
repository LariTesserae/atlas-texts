There is no rock down here, or if there is, it lies miles beneath the root-mat and has not been seen for centuries. The cavern is the size of a broad river valley, enclosed not by stone, but by an overarching vault of living, fibrous timber—a subterranean forest that has grown together at the crown and hollowed itself out from within. 

The air is cool and bone-dry, smelling sharply of turpentine, static electricity, and fresh-sawn cedar. Because there is no water, nothing rots. Because there is no wind or rain, nothing erodes. Every surface in this vast, hollowed-out gourd of a world is pristine, sharp-edged, and dry to the touch.

Light pours down from the vaulted roof in steady, chalky-white shafts, bright as an overcast noon on the surface. It comes from the *lumin-galls*—enormous, crystalline blisters grown intentionally into the ceiling boughs, stimulated by thick copper cables that snake across the upper canopy. The electricity feeds the wood; the wood swells into glass-like nodules that glow with a cold, shadowless incandescence.

Across the miles of the valley floor, a city is growing, and it is being built by the floor itself.

The architecture is an collaboration between arboriculture and heavy industry. Where a district is planned, massive iron looms and tension-rigs are bolted directly into the pale, chitinous heartwood of the ground. Thick cables winch the fast-growing shoots upward, forcing them through cast-iron jigs and steel lattices. You can watch a wall rise if you stand still for an hour: pale, sapless tendrils extruding from the floor, braiding together with a faint, dry *creak-pop* as they are forced into the geometry of tenements, viaducts, and factory halls. The buildings are young, embryonic; whole neighborhoods stand half-extruded, their upper stories still translucent and pliable, framed by steel scaffolding and fed by hissing hoses that pump pressurized carbon and mineral gas into their bases. Nothing is worn down. Every lintel and sill has the waxy, unmarred sheen of a newly opened seedpod.

Despite the urban scale, the city feels wide open, almost empty. The buildings are spaced far apart across the undulating, bone-white xyloid plains, separated by broad avenues of smooth bark and sunken plazas where no dust ever gathers. The density is kept low so the substrate doesn’t choke on its own vigor. Between the rising industrial blocks and the long, low manifolds of the gas-works, there are great, quiet expanses where the floor simply breathes—slowly heaving up and down by inches over the course of an afternoon as pressurized air moves through the deep vascular tunnels below.

In a world without water, the machinery runs on tension, pneumatics, and dry static. Great flywheel towers spin in the middle distance, storing the kinetic energy of the shifting roots. They make no roar, only a low, velvet *whuff-whuff-whuff* of displaced cool air and the occasional sharp *snap* of a wooden gear advancing a tooth. Overhead, suspended from the ceiling vault by miles of braided vine-wire, cargo gondolas glide on greased iron rails. They move with the eerie, coasting silence of spiders on a web, ferrying pig-iron and mineral canisters from the deep-tap mines to the grafting yards.

The fauna here are creatures of static and timber, moving briskly through the bright, cool air. Flocks of *splinter-moths*—insects the size of dinner plates with wings like shaved balsa—drift between the tension-towers, feeding on the ozone that collects around the copper power lines. On the broad avenues, long-legged, six-limbed grazers that look like a cross between greyhounds and walking-sticks trot in nervous packs. Their hooves are padded with dry felt, making no sound as they run; they use chisel-like iron teeth to scrape the fast-growing sugar-scale off the sides of the factory manifolds, keeping the vents clear before the municipal sweepers chase them off. High up in the steel-and-vine trusses, pale, angular predators with eyes like polished amber cling to the girders, dropping silently onto the grazers when the shadow of a passing gondola briefly dims the light.

A dry weather moves through the valley. Every few hours, the deep-taps miles below release a surge of cold nitrogen and powdered calcium. It rises through wide, gridded vents in the avenues as a silent, glittering white fog, hugging the ground for ten minutes before the floor absorbs it. When the fog passes, the whole city shivers with a sudden burst of growth. A million tiny *ticks* and *snaps* echo softly across the open plazas as the buildings stretch another half-inch into their iron frames, the steel cables groan a fraction of a tone higher, and the dry, bright valley settles back into its busy, whispering quiet.
