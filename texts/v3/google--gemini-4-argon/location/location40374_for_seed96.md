The valley is three miles across from rim to rim, and there is no bedrock visible anywhere within it. Every square inch of the basin—the terraced walls, the broad, stepped floor, the causeways that rib the slopes—is composed of hand-dressed slate, mortarless basalt, and packed, grey river-clay. It sits high in the thin, chill air, just above the timberline, though there are no trees below it either. There is only the stone, laid down in millions of tight, overlapping courses, so freshly split that the edges of the slate will still catch and slice the leather of a boot. 

Above, the sky is a pale, blinding sheet of high-altitude glare. The sun does not break through the cloud layer so much as it turns the entire dome of the sky into a single, luminous diffusing glass. Shadows do not exist here; instead, there are only variations of wet shine. Because everything is wet. The high, cool air is supersaturated, a permanent mist that condenses instantly on the cold stone, turning the entire constructed valley into a vast, weeping cistern. 

Water does not roar or rush here. It creeps. The architecture is designed to hold and slow it. The valley is packed to capacity with low, dry-stone structures—thousands upon thousands of shallow basins, knee-high baffle walls, narrow sluices, stepped plinths, and squat, beehive-shaped cairns, all jammed together with barely enough room for a narrow boot-path between them. One structure’s retaining wall is the next structure’s foundation. They crowd the slopes in dense, cellular clusters, like a mineral honeycomb built by things with calloused hands and bone wedges. 

Because the construction is so recent, the water has not yet worn channels into the rock. It sheets over the crisp, ruler-straight lips of the slate weirs in uniform, trembling skins. It collects in the shallow, clay-lined pans until the surface tension breaks, spilling over the chiseled notches in slow, rhythmic pulses. *Plip. Shhh. Plip.* Across three miles of packed masonry, millions of these tiny transfers happen simultaneously, merging into a low, pervasive hiss—like sand shifting in an hourglass, or a room full of people breathing in their sleep.

There is no soil, and there are no leaves. The only concession to life is a pale, crustose film—something between a primitive lichen and a mineral scale—that bruises the vertical faces of the north-facing stones in patches of muted ochre and bone-white. Here and there, in the stagnant corners of the deeper settling tanks, grey gelatinous spheres the size of marbles cling to the submerged slate, absorbing the dissolved lime. Nothing swims among them. No water-strider dimples the bright, metallic reflection of the sky. No bird crosses the glare. 

The tools that made this place are still here, integrated into the fabric of the walls. Wedges of harder greenstone are left hammered into splits in the basalt blocks; wooden levers of some dense, pale timber—brought up from very far away—are wedged beneath half-shifted slabs, swollen and dark with water. There are no machines, no pulleys, no iron spikes. Everything is the size that two or three pairs of hands could lift, lever, or drag on stone sledges. Along the rims of the upper terraces, piles of flake-scree from the knapping of the slates sit neatly corralled in wicker-like enclosures made of thin, overlapping stone slabs. The quarry marks on the blocks—the fan-shaped strikes of stone mauls—are still dusty with pulverized rock paste that the constant drizzle hasn't yet washed out of the grain.

To walk through the valley is to navigate a maze of ankle-deep water and slick, sharp edges. The air smells intensely of wet flint, cold clay, and ozone. There is no wind strong enough to raise a wave in the millions of shallow pans; the breeze only drags a veil of condensation across the terraces, making the bright, shadowless light flare and dim on the wet surfaces as the mist thickens and clears.

The whole landscape is a single, slow hydraulic engine whose purpose is entirely passive. Water condenses on the cold, high-surface-area labyrinth of the upper cairns, trickles down through the packed stone lungs of the terraces, pools in the broad clay flats of the center, and slowly, almost imperceptibly, seeps down through the graded gravel beds at the valley’s lowest plug. It takes days for a drop of mist gathered on the rim to navigate the thousands of steps to the floor. As it moves, it deposits a microscopic layer of dissolved silica and calcium onto the fresh, sharp slate. Very slowly—over decades, over centuries—the water is mortaring the dry-stone valley together, glazing the raw, chipped edges in a dull, translucent sinter, sealing the work in ice-clear stone.
