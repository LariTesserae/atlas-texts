It is not a valley, though it sits low in the crust, and it is not a cave, because it was built. It is a sunken courtyard no wider than a village square, dropped forty feet into the seamless, bone-dry shale of the lowlands. The air inside it is a furnace—thick, desiccated, and hovering just below the temperature where dry wood begins to char on its own.

There has never been water here. The mortar binding the tiered, inward-leaning walls was mixed from dry-rendered ash and volcanic glass, cured by heat rather than hydration. The architecture is dense and urban in its ambition, yet entirely miniature and utterly empty of furniture, debris, or bodies. Hundreds of tiny, square-linteled doorways, no taller than a human knee, are stacked in precise, dizzying galleries up the four walls. Walkways of fitted slate, barely the width of two fingers, connect cantilevered balconies and stepped causeways. Every edge is rounded, every sharp lintel smoothed down to a velvety curve by centuries of relentless, dry friction. 

Because the pit is so deep and its rim is overhanging—nearly sealed off by curved hoods of hand-stacked basalt designed to catch and funnel the surface winds—almost no sky is visible. The light is a profound, coppery gloom. Stray shafts of red dust-light needle through small ventilation slits high in the masonry, illuminating an interior that is Mostly Void. Between the intricately carved walls, the central volume of the shaft is an unbroken, hollow drop. 

Yet the masonry is choked with life.

The flora here does not drink; it smelts. Thick, lush curtains of *ferro-lichen* and *ash-ivy* drape from the miniature balconies, hanging in heavy, metallic-gray and deep-ochre swags across the empty shaft. These plants are pyro-lithic: they draw sulfur, iron dust, and silica out of the roaring updrafts and fix them using the ambient geothermal heat rising from the floor of the pit. Their leaves are brittle, thin as beaten tin, and wide as platters, arranged in dense, overlapping rosettes that obscure whole neighborhoods of the carved stone. 

This place is never still, and it is never quiet.

Because the bottom of the courtyard sits directly over a dry thermal vent, a column of superheated air constantly rushes upward, while cooler, dust-heavy surface air is sucked down through the angled basalt hoods at the top. The architecture acts as a vast, primitive organ pipe. The wind screams softly through the thousands of tiny doorways and flutes through the chimney-slits, creating a continuous, multi-tonal roar that vibrates in the teeth. 

In this violent draft, the dry greenery goes mad with motion. The metallic leaves clatter against one another like thousands of tiny cymbals, a deafening, rhythmic *shhhk-shhhk-shhhk* that fills the dim air. Long, braided vines of glass-root—pale and translucent as pulled sugar—whip horizontally across the gulf, anchoring themselves into opposing doorways, snapping taut, and thrumming like bass strings in the gale. 

Nothing crawls here. There are no insects in the dry bark, no birds in the rafters, no lizards on the hot stone. The only movement is the wind, the frantic, mechanical thrashing of the heat-loving plants, and the slow, cyclical shedding of the ecosystem. Every few minutes, a section of heavy iron-moss grows too dense for its balcony, and with a sound like a dropped sack of nails, it tears loose. It plummets down into the dim, empty center of the shaft, where the updraft catches it, tumbles it in mid-air, and grinds it against the rough lower walls until it disintegrates into a cloud of glowing, rust-colored spores. The spores ride the thermal column back up to the galleries, snagging on the smoothed stone lintels to begin growing again, endlessly sanding down the miniature city that houses them.
