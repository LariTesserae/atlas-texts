There is no stone here, and if there is dirt, it lies miles beneath the floor. What serves as ground across this high plateau is the *bark-plate*: a pale, silvery-ochre crust of living wood that stretches from horizon to horizon, rising and falling in smooth, wind-planed swells. 

It is a single organism—or a fused mat of ancient organisms—that grew over a mountain range eons ago and simply became the topography. Over millennia, the dry, hot winds at this altitude have eroded the living wood the way they would sandstone. They have carved long, swooping coulees, smoothed out mesas of pale timber, and polished the great burls into gleaming, whale-backed ridges. When you walk on it, it does not give like soil; it has the dense, warm, slightly resilient hardness of bone. Because the vegetation *is* the continent, there are no trees, no shrubs, no grass. The surface is entirely bare, lending the landscape a stark, terrifying emptiness. The sky above is an immense, faded dome of hot white-blue, empty of clouds, dropping down to meet a pale wooden horizon that curves with the planet.

Yet the ground is alive, and it breathes.

Every few hundred yards, the smooth timber-crust puckers into a deep, fluted depression—a stomatal vent as wide as a house. Around these vents, the wood changes color, blushing from dry silver to a slick, bruised violet. At dawn and dusk, the vents exhale great, slow plumes of warm, damp vapor gathered from some unreachable subterranean aquifer. The vapor doesn't rise high; the plateau's thin, hot air quickly drinks it up, so it rolls sideways across the timber-dunes in knee-deep ribbons of scalding fog, leaving behind slick trails of condensation that gleam like snail tracks before evaporating in the noon heat. 

And then there is the sound. 

The landscape is deafening. It is not a chaotic noise, but a massive, layered wall of biological machinery. Beneath the soles of your feet, you feel the bass note: the deep, rhythmic *thrum-shuck* of the plateau’s vascular system, pumping slow rivers of sap through trunks the size of canyons miles below. Above that is the wind, which hits the eroded hollows and stomatal vents at just the right angle to turn the whole plateau into a colossal bass flute, droning a single, wavering chord that never stops.

And on top of the drone is the scream of the fauna.

Because the plant-mass has consolidated itself into a single, armored floor, the animal life has nothing to hide behind, and yet it is everywhere. The plateau is overrun with creatures adapted to feed on the wood-crust, and creatures adapted to feed on them. The air and the pale ground shimmer with them. 

The loudest are the *stridulators*—flattened, platter-sized arthropods with shells the exact bleached-silver of the ground. They clamp themselves to the timber swells by the millions, driving long, drill-like proboscises down into the phloem. As they pump the hot, sugary sap into their bodies, their overlapping wing-cases vibrate to shed excess heat. A single one sounds like a fingernail dragged across a comb; ten million of them, carpeting a three-mile valley, sound like a jet engine made of dry leaves and brass. When a shadow passes over them, they all flatten simultaneously with a sharp, collective *clack* that echoes across the miles, and the valley instantly changes color from shimmering silver to dull grey.

Moving through the stridulator fields are the heavy grazers: great, radial-bodied things that roll and heave across the swells like tumbleweeds made of muscle and chitin. They have no eyes, navigating instead by the acoustic vibrations of the wood. Beneath their bodies, rings of basalt-hard teeth scrape constantly at the living ground, gouging shallow, weeping troughs into the bark-plate. You can hear a herd of them from five miles off—a wet, splintering crunch like a ship grinding against a reef. 

Where the grazers gouge the wood, the plateau bleeds. Thick, amber sap wells up in the heat, instantly skinning over into a rubbery rind. Before it can fully harden, the sky darkens. Flocks of thermal-gliders—long-necked, leather-winged creatures that spend their lives riding the ferocious hot updrafts—drop down in screaming, chaotic tornadoes of wing-beats and snapping beaks. They fight over the sap-wounds and the slick, violet rims of the steaming vents, their high, whistling cries cutting straight through the drone of the stridulators. 

In the midst of this acoustic violence, the vastness of the space remains untouched. A flock of gliders might number in the tens of thousands, a swirling black pillar of noise and teeth, yet against the sheer, unbroken sweep of the wooden plains, they look like a pinch of soot dropped on a ballroom floor. Ten miles away, another dark tornado rises; twenty miles beyond that, a long dust-devil of shed chitin spins lazily across a ridge. Between them lies nothing but the blinding, pale curve of the world, baking in the sun.

Scattered at immense intervals across the high ridges are the marks of people. 

They are tiny, desperate, and ingenious things, built entirely in relation to the wind and the noise. Because you cannot dig into the bark-plate without the wound sealing itself over and crushing whatever is inside, the structures sit lightly on the surface, tethered by braided sinew to the natural burls and knots of the wood. 

A typical site is a lone wind-harp and a condenser trap, separated from the next nearest human trace by three days' walk. They are made from the shed plates of the great grazers and the hollow, calcified wing-bones of the sky-gliders, lashed together with cured sap-rubber. The condensers are fan-like ribs of bone stretched with scraped gut, anchored at the lips of the violet stomatal vents. As the hot, damp breath of the plateau rolls out at dusk, it hits the taut gut, beads into water, and trickles down grooves carved in the bone into swollen bladder-skins buried in the shade of the ridge. 

Beside the condensers stand the acoustic shelters—low, aerodynamic domes of overlapping chitin shingles, packed between the layers with dead, fibrous pith to deaden the roar of the world. You can tell how old a shelter is by how the floor of the world reacts to it; around the older domes, a slow collar of pale callus-wood has crept up the shingles, trying over the course of decades to swallow the foreign object. 

Near these huts, you sometimes find the tool-pits: shallow, natural hollows in the wood where nomadic toolmakers have sat to work. The ground around them is littered with flakes of amber—not stone, which does not exist here, but aged, petrified sap harvested from ancient scar-tissue in the deep coulees, knapped like flint into razor-sharp blades and scraper-heads. 

Standing by one of these abandoned shelters at high noon, the heat presses down like a physical weight. The sun is an ordinary, clear yellow star, casting sharp, dark shadows across the pale, undulating ground. The air smells intensely of hot turpentine, ozone, and the sharp, musky vinegar of a billion insects. A mile away, a stomatal vent sighs, sending a phantom wave of steam curling over a ridge. Millions of silver shells ripple in response, the pitch of their grinding song shifting up a half-step, holding steady, and filling the vast, empty bowl of the sky until there is no room left for thought.
