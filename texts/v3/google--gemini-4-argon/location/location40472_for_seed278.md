The plateau does not end so much as it submits to the curvature of the earth. It is an upland of dark, iron-rich basalt and slate, planed flat by glaciers that retreated ten million years ago, leaving behind a stone table three thousand miles wide. The rock is deeply fatigued. There are no jagged peaks left, no fresh fractures—only vast, shallow basins and low, whale-backed ridges that have been sanded down by the thin, freezing air until their surfaces are as smooth as river cobbles. Everything has settled into its final angle of repose. 

Above, the sky is a permanent, low vault of pewter. The sun is never a disc here; it is merely a slow-moving bruise of pale amber that migrates across the southern horizon, offering a twilight that lasts for fourteen hours before sinking into a starless, overcast black. Because of the altitude, the air is thin and achingly cold, hovering just below freezing for most of the year, rising just above it for a few weeks in the middle of the long season. 

There is no rain, and strangely, no snow. Instead, there is the Sweat. For several hours every afternoon, a dense, freezing fog rolls across the basins, condensing instantly upon the dark stone. The entire continent glistens in the dim light, slick with a millimeter of black moisture. It collects in the shallow depressions, forming mirrors of stagnant, mineral-heavy water an inch deep and five miles wide, which freeze into dull gray rime by nightfall and sublime directly back into the dry morning air without a sound.

Despite the vast emptiness between the ridges—where a traveler might look across forty miles of bare, damp stone and see only a few spaced boulders and the occasional patch of grey-green crustose lichen—the plateau is utterly dominated by life, and that life is entirely industrial.

Across the slate plains move the Dredgers. They are biological organisms, roughly the size and shape of locomotive engines, covered in plates of oxidized iron-chitin that they refine directly from the stone. There are millions of them, though the plateau is so immense that they appear scattered, spaced half a mile apart like ships in a convoy that covers the world. They have no eyes and no mouths, only a low-hanging ventral grate that scrapes against the wet basalt. 

In the absolute, crushing silence of the thin air, their work is barely audible. There is no roar of combustion, no clanging of metal. The Dredgers move at a pace of ten feet per hour, propelled by hundreds of muscular, hydraulic feet padded with dense, rubbery cartilage. As they creep, their ventral grates secrete a mild sulfuric acid that mixes with the afternoon condensation, softening the ancient rock. Internal peristaltic mills—great, slow-turning stomachs lined with basalt teeth—grind the slurry, extracting trace heavy metals and sulfur, which the creatures use to power their internal galvanic cells. 

Behind each Dredger lies a perfectly flat ribbon of pale, leached stone, three meters wide and stretching back for hundreds of miles, marking decades of travel. Where these ribbons intersect, the stone is worn down into smooth, geometric trenches. The Dredgers are the primary agent of erosion now, having taken over from the ancient ice, milling the continent down by a fraction of a millimeter every century.

Where the Dredgers pass, the smaller fauna congregate in overwhelming numbers, turning the sparse tracks into dense, localized ribbons of silent activity. 

Millions of fist-sized, six-legged crustaceans—the Sifters—march in lockstep behind the engines, their shells the dull gray of lead. They feed on the warm, refined slag extruded from the Dredgers’ rear vents. They do not chitter or fight; they move with the synchronized, mesmerizing monotony of an assembly line, sorting the slag by grain size, rolling the waste into neat, spherical pellets that carpet the trenches like ball bearings. 

Above them, tethered to the plated backs of the Dredgers by thick, black cables of braided silk, float the Condensers. They are pale, translucent bladders the size of weather balloons, drifting thirty feet in the freezing air. They have no brains, only a network of dark veins that rhythmically pulse once every two minutes. They harvest the afternoon fog and the static electricity generated by the high-altitude wind shearing across the plateau, trickling both water and low-voltage current down their tether-cables into the Dredgers below. When the wind gusts, a thousand Condensers across a fifty-mile basin will tilt at the exact same angle, a vast, silent field of pale lamps leaning into the dark.

There is no human hand here, nor has there ever been. There are no ruins, no roads, no rusted hulks of steel. The industry belongs entirely to the ecology. 

Every few miles, sitting isolated on the swell of a ridge, is a Foundry—a sessile, colonial organism that looks like a cluster of dark, basaltic termite mounds, thirty feet high and weeping yellow sulfur-foam. When a Dredger’s internal metal reserves are full—a process that takes forty or fifty years—it alters its course and creeps toward the nearest Foundry. It docks with the mound, sealing its chitinous skirts against the Foundry’s base. 

For months, in total silence, a chemical exchange takes place. The Foundry draws off the refined iron and copper from the Dredger, using the heat of the chemical reaction to incubate broods of larval Sifters and new Dredger-seeds deep within its insulated core. The only sign of this massive smelting operation is a thin, lazy plume of white steam rising straight up into the pewter sky, and a faint, rhythmic *thrum* that cannot be heard through the air, only felt through the soles of the feet if one is standing within a hundred yards on the solid rock.

Between these slow-moving convoys and spaced Foundries lies the great, quiet emptiness of the plateau. In the wide intervals where no Dredger has passed for a thousand years, the sparse flora holds on. Low, woody shrubs that grow flat against the rock—hugging the stone for warmth like spilled black ink—open tiny, crystalline pores when the afternoon fog rolls in. They grow perhaps an inch a century, their wood as dense and heavy as lead, entirely fireproof and rot-resistant. 

Nothing rots here. The cold and the thin air forbid it. When a Dredger finally dies—its internal galvanic batteries shorting out after five hundred years of grinding—it simply stops. Its Condenser deflates, draping over the hull like a shed skin. The Sifters methodically strip its soft tissues over the course of a year, without a single sound of tearing or chewing, leaving the pristine, iron-chitin hull sitting on the plain. 

Because there is no built environment, these hulls are the only architecture. They sit for ten thousand years in the dim light, slowly gathering a beard of pale lichen on their leeward sides, waiting until another live Dredger, following a trajectory laid down before the hulls were born, encounters the obstacle. The living engine does not go around. It simply mounts the dead hull, drops its acid-grate, and over the course of three weeks, grinds its ancestor back into the raw material of the plateau, continuing on its slow, straight line toward the horizon.
