There is no rock down here, though you are deep beneath the ground. What holds the earth back is a continuous, self-repairing rind of pale xylem and knotted root-plate, flexed outward like the inside of a lung. 

The space itself is small—perhaps the size of an overturned barge or a sunken courtyard—and almost entirely hollow. A great, tense emptiness occupies the center of the chamber. All the mass of the world has been pushed to the perimeter, woven into the ceiling, the floor, and the four curving walls. These walls are not covered in vegetation; they *are* the vegetation, a single, hyper-dense mat of flattened stems, interlocking bark-scales, and pale, chlorophyll-starved boughs that have fused together under immense subterranean pressure. Because this pocket opened only recently—torn apart by some sudden shifting of the deep root-tectonics—the inner surfaces are still raw. Where the wood split to form the room, the grain is jagged and splintered, weeping slow, warm beads of amber resin and clear, mineral-heavy sap.

Despite the depth, the chamber is flooded with the bright, flat, unmistakable light of a slightly overcast Tuesday afternoon. It does not filter down through a shaft. It simply exists inside the hollow, radiating evenly from the empty air in the center of the room, casting sharp shadows back *into* the crevices of the root-walls. If you look closely at the corners where the ceiling meets the walls, the geometry fails to resolve. Two roots will cross each other at an angle that belongs to a much larger horizon, and looking between them, your eyes have to adjust as if peering across three miles of sunlit hazy space, even though you can reach out and touch the back of the gap with your knuckles.

It is loud in here. The walls are under ferocious hydraulic tension. Thousands of gallons of water are being dragged upward through the surrounding vascular tissue toward some unimaginable canopy far above, and the sound of it is a relentless, pressurized roar—like standing inside the hull of a wooden ship while a cataract rushes past the planking. Beneath that white noise is the sharp, rhythmic *crack-pop* of new growth. Because the chamber is young, the boundary is still adjusting to the void it created. Thick, pale shoots the size of a man’s thigh erupt from the floor, arch across the empty space in a matter of minutes with a sound like creaking leather and snapping violin strings, and slam into the opposite wall, where they flatten, split, and graft themselves into the existing hull to reinforce the room. 

The air is warm—skin-temperature, thick with the smell of crushed celery, hot sawdust, and ozone. There is no standing water, no stream or pool, only a constant, heavy dampness that sweats out of the young wood. Every few minutes, a localized pressure-drop causes the humidity in the center of the room to flash-condense: a sudden, violent cylinder of rain falls for three seconds straight down from mid-air, drum-rolling loudly against the tight-woven floor-mat before being sucked instantly back into the thirsty bark.

Nothing has been built here; no human hand has ever found a way through the miles of solid root-mass that encase this bubble. Yet the room is full of primitive manufacture. 

The plant-mass, in its blind, panicked effort to shore up the anomaly of the empty space, has begun to imitate the mechanics of tool-use. Along the lower walls, fibrous vines have twisted themselves into three-ply ropes, looped around jutting burls in flawless clove hitches to haul sagging sections of the ceiling upward. Where a fissure in the floor threatens to widen, the surrounding briars have dropped heavy, iron-hard heartwood wedges into the gap, and a thick, club-like branch hangs above them on a hinge of green sinew, rising and falling every few seconds to hammer the wedges deeper with a deafening, hollow *thwack*. Elsewhere, sheets of fibrous phloem are being peeled from the walls by curling tendrils, laid across the floor in a criss-cross warp and weft, and beaten flat by rhythmic, paddle-shaped leaves until they cure into tough, felted mats. It is carpentry without a carpenter—an ecosystem panicked by a tear in its own fabric, hallucinating levers, pulleys, and joints out of raw botany.

Animal life is almost nonexistent, reduced to a few bewildered stragglers caught in the wood's strange physics. High up near the un-angled corners, a single, palm-sized beetle with a carapace like tarnished brass walks along a root. Every time it crosses a certain threshold of the bright, sourceless light, its body stutters—leaving a dry, hollow husk of itself behind while the living insect continues forward, only for the husk to dissolve into damp sawdust a moment later. Down near the floor, hiding behind the rhythmic thudding of the wooden mallet-branch, a pale, eyeless lizard licks the condensation from a bruised fern. It moves in abrupt, jerky increments, its metabolism synced not to its own heartbeat, but to the loud, snapping cadence of the room’s shifting architecture. 

You cannot stay on the floor for long; within an hour, the weaving tendrils will quietly lash your boots to the substrate, mistaking your weight for a loose piece of timber that needs to be mortised into the foundation.
