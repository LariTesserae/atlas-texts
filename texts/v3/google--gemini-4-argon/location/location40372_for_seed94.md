The plateau does not end at the horizon; it curves with the planet, an unbroken shelf of weathered graywacke and shale lifted two miles into a thin, chilly sky. There are no peaks here, no jagged teeth of fresh stone. Millions of years of frost-wedging and persistent runoff have ground the mountains down into vast, whale-backed swells that roll outward for a thousand miles in every direction. 

Despite the altitude, there is no bare rock. The world is upholstered. A dense, waist-high mat of vegetation covers every contour, so tightly interwoven that a dropped stone will not reach the soil. This is a cold-weather jungle in miniature: springy hummocks of silver-green cushion plants, dwarf conifers twisted like bonsai that grow horizontally rather than up, stiff-leaved rosettes the size of dinner plates, and fibrous, weeping mosses that drink directly from the passing clouds. The plants are packed together with zero interstitial space, their root systems locked into a single, continuous mattress of wet peat five feet thick. Walking across it is like walking on a cold, saturated sponge; every footstep presses a gallon of tea-colored water up around the boots, which instantly vanishes back into the weave as the weight is lifted.

The air is crisp, hovering just a few degrees above freezing, and smells sharply of wet slate, crushed pine needles, and ozone. The light is the clear, high-altitude daylight of an overcast noon—bright enough to make the eyes water, shadowless, turning the wet surfaces of a billion waxy leaves into points of pale glare. Weather does not visit this plateau; it is manufactured here. You can watch it happen across the fifty-mile troughs between the swells: great bellies of mist condensing out of the saturated turf, lifting slowly, joining the low ceiling of stratus, and drifting twenty miles east before collapsing back into the moss as a fine, drenching drizzle.

What makes the plateau unusual—what introduces the unmistakable signature of heavy industry into an entirely unbuilt wilderness—is the iron. 

The underlying shale is rich in pyrites and magnetite, and the miles of saturated, acidic peat act as a continental-scale wet-cell battery. Over eons, the flora and the geology have fused into a vast, slow-motion industrial process. There are no buildings, no pylons, no poured concrete or forged steel, yet the landscape is humming with mechanical and chemical work. 

In the shallow basins between the swells, where the water pools into dark, mineral-slick tarns, massive tuberous plants grow in concentric rings. Their stalks are woody and segmented, coated in a natural precipitate of rust and copper sulfate gathered from the groundwater. These plants act as biological smelters, drawing up heavy metals and extruding them through their bark in brittle, slag-like nodules to keep from poisoning their own tissues. The edges of every tarn are choked with heaps of this botanical clinker—millions of tons of porous, metallic ore, refined and discarded by the vegetation, clinking softly as the water laps against it.

And then there is the fauna, which has evolved to exploit the metal and the current.

The plateau is teeming with life, all of it operating in near-total silence. The thick, wet turf swallows sound like acoustic foam; even the wind makes no noise as it slides over the greased, tightly packed leaves. Yet everywhere you look, the brush is parting. Herds of stocky, six-legged grazers, the size of wild boars and covered in dense, water-shedding wool, move through the troughs in tight formations. Their hooves and blunt snouts are plated in biological iron, dark and dull as cast-iron skillets, which they use to shear through the tough, metallic stalks of the smelter-plants. They do not grunt or bleat. They communicate via low-frequency electrical pulses sent through the wet peat under their feet; if you stand still while a herd passes thirty yards away, you hear nothing but the soft *shhh-shhh* of wet wool brushing against wet fern, while the soles of your feet prickle with the rhythm of their silent conversation.

Closer to the ground, the density of life is staggering. Part the cushion-plants anywhere and the dark, wet interior is a highway of pale, segmented arthropods the size of human hands, their carapaces shimmering with an oil-slick iridescence. They move with the steady, rhythmic precision of assembly-line machinery, harvesting the rust-nodules, rolling them into spheres, and packing them into subterranean chambers to regulate the heat of their nests through rapid oxidation. Where the colonies are largest, the peat is warm to the touch, and thin, oily plumes of steam rise straight up into the cold air like exhaust vents from an underground factory.

Predators work the margins of these steam-vents. Long, low-slung amphibians with skin like vulcanized rubber lie half-submerged in the acidic runnels, their eyes pale and lidless. They have no vocal cords. When they strike at the wool-grazers, the only sound is the heavy, wet slap of muscle against moss and the brief, sharp hiss of discharged static electricity—the amphibians use the conductivity of the bog water to stun their prey, sending a localized ripple of dead, twitching insects radiating outward for twenty feet around the kill.

Stand on the crest of one of the great swells, and the scale of the place presses in on you. For a hundred miles in every direction, the grey-green slopes rise and fall under the bright, cool sky. Thousands of tiny white steam-plumes feather the distance, marking the oxidation furnaces of the insect cities. Dark ribbons of herds wind through the valleys like conveyor belts, steadily processing the metallic brush. The air tastes faintly of copper and cold rain. A million tons of ore are being leached, refined, and oxidized every hour; a billion volts of low-grade current are trickling through the roots; millions of animals are grazing, hunting, and dying in the tangle at your boots. And over all of it hangs the profound, muffled quiet of a wet coat—nothing audible but the occasional, soft *tink* of a metal-plated hoof striking a piece of slag, and the endless, tiny sound of water dripping from leaf to leaf.
