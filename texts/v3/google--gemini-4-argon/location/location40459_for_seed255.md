The valley is five miles wide and has no dirt in it. From rim to rim, the low basin is packed with dry-laid slate, unpeeled cedar logs, and heavy, gray-green mortar that has not yet cured. 

There is no sky to speak of, only a thick, permanent ceiling of cold coal-smoke and tallow-soot that hangs just above the highest timber frames, keeping the world in a greasy, perpetual twilight. To see anything beyond twenty paces, you have to rely on the cressets—thousands of basket-braziers hung from notched poles, burning chunks of peat soaked in mutton fat. They don't cast much light; mostly they make orange smudges in the dark and drop gobs of black grease onto the stonework below.

Nothing here is finished, and nothing has had time to wear down. The slate is fresh-split. The edges of the steps, the retaining walls, the plinths, and the dry-wells are so sharp they will slice the leather off a boot sole or open a palm if you stumble. There are no rounded corners, no lichen, no moss, not a single blade of grass pushing through the cracks. The cracks are too new to hold dust, let alone seed. When you walk through the narrows—the endless, shoulder-wide alleys between the terraced foundation-blocks—your coat brushes against wet lime plaster on the left and rough, resin-bleeding bark on the right.

The density of the place is suffocating, even where the valley floor opens out. There is no flat ground left; every square yard has been claimed for some rudimentary infrastructure whose overall purpose is impossible to read in the dark. Low, overlapping corbelled domes sit cheek-by-jowl with sunken cisterns, which are bridged by three-log trestles, which in turn support wooden sluices that carry no water, only wet gravel being shoved along by heavy wooden rakes. Timber scaffolding climbs over everything like a second, stiffer forest—thousands of poles lashed together with wet rawhide thongs that shrink and creak as the air cools. 

Because the mortar is still setting across millions of tons of masonry, the whole basin sweats. It is a chemical dampness. The lime-slaking pits—wide, shallow basins dug into the rubble—let off a low, alkaline steam that drifts through the scaffolding and condenses on the cold slate. The stone is slick with it. Water doesn’t flow in streams here; it collects in the depressed centers of newly laid flagstones, forms stagnant, milky puddles in the wheel-ruts, and drips relentlessly from the undersides of the timber trestles. 

The noise is immense, hollow, and continuous. Because there is no soil or foliage to absorb sound, every noise bounces off a hundred thousand flat, hard, wet surfaces. A wooden mallet striking a chisel half a mile away echoes through the stone corridors like a gunshot. And the valley is never still. There is a constant, rhythmic *clack-clack-clack* of treadwheels turning on ungreased oak axles. Heavy stone rollers, pulled by unseen teams on the upper tiers, grind over the raw slate rubble with a sound like breaking teeth. Every few minutes, a load of quarry-spoil is dumped down a timber chute somewhere in the dark—a long, roaring rattle that ends in a dull, booming crash, followed by the ring of iron-shod shovels scraping stone. Even the air is loud; the draft pulled down into the basin by the thousands of burning cressets whistles through the scaffolding joints and sets the loose rawhide lashings thrumming.

There are no builders in sight—at least, none with hands. The work is maintained and nudged forward by the valley's sparse fauna: heavy, low-slung beasts the size of oxen, pale and hairless as cave-grubs, with thick, calloused plates on their chests and foreheads. They move slowly through the gloom, alone or in pairs, leaning their bulk against the capstan bars or shoving mounds of wet mortar along the troughs with their flat snouts. They have no eyes, only deep, wet slits that flare to smell the quicklime. When two of them meet in a narrow passage, they do not fight; they press their horned brows together and shove in silence until the weaker one is forced backward down a stairwell or into a cistern, its heavy hooves scrabbling wildly on the sharp slate. 

Smaller things live in the scaffolding: pale, six-legged creatures the length of a forearm that scurry along the cedar poles, chewing the rawhide bindings into a sticky paste and spitting it into the masonry joints, where it hardens like bone glue. You hear them overhead in the dark—a dry, rasping *scritch-scritch* as their mandibles work the leather, followed by the wet *plop* of their spittle hitting the stone.

The rules of the basin are simple and physical. Nothing rots, because the lime-damp and the heavy coal-smoke pickle the timber before fungus can take hold. Nothing erodes, because the rain is too weak to wash away stone, only strong enough to slick the surfaces and feed the mortar-pits. And the floor is always rising. When a tier of slate walls and dome-frames reaches the height of the scaffolding, the treadwheels shift their creaking rhythm, the chutes are dragged higher by the pale beasts, and a new layer of rubble is dumped directly over the old one, turning yesterday's alleys into tomorrow's cellar-work, leaving the lower cressets buried in the dark to choke out in their own grease.
