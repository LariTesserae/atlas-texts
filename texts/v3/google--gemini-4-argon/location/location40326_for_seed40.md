The valley is not carved out of stone; it is stacked out of cast iron, extruded brass, and glazed porcelain, rising to a high, thin altitude where the air smells of ozone and cold grease. Everything underfoot and overhead is manufactured, a dense, regional basin of interlocking machinery that stretches for miles between jagged ridges of stacked boilerplates and copper heat-sinks. Because it was assembled recently—or because it constantly re-extrudes itself—nothing is worn down. The edges of the steel terraces are still burr-sharp from the milling; the miles of threaded rod that buttress the eastern cliffs show no rust, only a fresh, blueish factory temper. 

Above it all hangs a blinding, unblinking white sky. The light has no single source; it radiates from the thin atmosphere itself, bouncing off millions of slick, zinc-plated roofs, porcelain insulator-forests, and polished brass pistons. The glare is clinical and immense, carving razor-sharp shadows beneath the endless catwalks and viaducts that choke the basin. 

Despite the density of the engines—flywheels the size of apartment blocks, forests of intake manifolds, miles of tightly packed condenser coils—the valley is profoundly, unnaturally quiet. A twenty-ton stamping press drops onto an anvil of rolled steel three hundred yards away, and the impact produces only a soft, dry *tick*, like a fingernail tapping a wineglass. The great turbines spin at a glacial pace, taking four minutes to complete a single revolution, their massive copper windings exhaling a faint, rhythmic sigh that barely carries across the nearest gantry. Sound here does not travel; it is swallowed by the geometry of the architecture, or perhaps the physics of collision have been rewritten so that kinetic energy converts into light and condensation rather than noise.

Water exists only as sweat. The cold mountain air meets the lukewarm breath of the slow-churning engines, and the entire landscape weeps. Thick, oily beads of condensation gather on the undersides of I-beams, cling to the millions of hexagonal bolt-heads, and pool in the stamped depressions of the deck-plates. Every few hours, when the barometric pressure shifts, a brief, localized drizzle falls from the high scaffolding—not from clouds, which do not exist here, but from the supersaturated air catching on the high-tension wires. The dampness collects in narrow, rectangular sluice-channels that run between the turbine housings, forming sluggish, iridescent creeks that smell of distilled water and machine oil.

Life has colonized the machine, though it is difficult to tell where the factory ends and the biology begins. Sparse, tough vegetation grows in the damp seams where steel plates meet. There is no soil; the plants root directly into the metric threads of bolts and the grease-packed races of ball bearings. Stiff, grey-green ferns with leaves like feeler gauges uncurl from the exhaust vents, drinking the warm, damp drafts. Tough, woody vines—their bark cross-hatched like knurled steel—climb the upright stanchions, producing small, translucent bladders filled with a pale, conductive fluid.

The fauna, however, is everywhere, thriving in the silent, bright congestion. Flocks of creatures that are half-pigeon, half-caliper roost on the high voltage lines. When they take flight, their wings make no flapping sound, only the faint *snick-snick* of sliding vernier scales, and their silhouettes momentarily stutter in the bright air, leaving brief, bronze afterimages suspended in the sky for several seconds after the birds have moved on. 

Down in the dense labyrinth of the lower manifolds, thousands of pale, six-legged beasts the size of hounds move in slow, deliberate herds. Their hides are hairless and vulcanized, grey as gasket material, and their ribs are external, hinged plates of bone that expand and contract to vented rhythms. They graze on the mineral efflorescence—the white, powdery salts that bloom where dampness meets raw aluminum—and lick the cold condensation from the cast-iron cylinders. They do not bark or bleat; they communicate by extruding thin, copper-colored filaments from their snouts and touching them together, completing a tiny circuit that causes their eyes to dim and brighten in unison.

The deeper one looks into the packed tiers of the valley, the less the rules of perspective and permanence hold. Because the environment is so densely constructed, the spatial logic of the architecture frequently overloads. A stairwell made of perforated steel plate climbs up the side of a condenser tower, turns a corner, and arrives at a landing that is thirtyfeet *below* where it started, yet still commands a view from the higher elevation. Pipes of spun glass, carrying slow-moving bubbles of mercury and rainwater, pass directly through solid cast-iron pillars without joints or holes, the glass and the iron occupying the exact same space in a shimmering, unresolved blur.

In the middle distance, a massive gantry crane spans a gorge of stacked radiators. It moves east at a pace of three inches an hour. Yet, without ever stopping or reversing, it is always positioned directly over the center of the gorge. Shadows do not always match the objects that cast them; a cluster of porcelain valves throws the long, unmistakable shadow of a pine tree across the bright zinc plating of a neighboring roof, though no pine tree has ever existed in this world. 

Occasionally, the slow cycle of the valley reaches a silent crescendo. For ten minutes, every piston in a three-mile radius will lock at top-dead-center. The air grows colder. The condensation freezes into geometric frost-patterns that look like circuit diagrams. The abundant beasts stop grazing and turn their blank, porcelain-white faces toward the blinding sky. A low, sub-audible vibration passes through the soles of the feet—the feeling of a massive gear slipping a tooth somewhere deep in the planet’s core. Then, a single drop of water falls from a brass petcock, hits a steel plate with a tiny, crystal *ping*, and the millions of tons of metal resume their slow, frictionless, and impossible heave.
