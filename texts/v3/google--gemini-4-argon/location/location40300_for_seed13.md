The valley sits high enough that the air is thin and permanently crisp, possessing the sharp, metallic clarity of an autumn afternoon that never progresses toward evening. The light comes from a sun fixed forty degrees above the southern ridge—bright, pale yellow, casting crisp, cobalt-tinted shadows that do not lengthen or rotate. Because the light never shifts, the eastern faces of the cliffs are perpetually blinding, while the western crevices hold a frost-rimmed gloom that has never known direct warmth.

The rock here is young. It has not been ground down into gentle hills or silted basins; the mountain walls and the valley floor alike are composed of fractured, stair-stepped basalt and slate that look freshly split. The edges of the natural stone are sharp enough to slice leather. Yet this raw geology has been entirely claimed by an expansive, sparsely arranged city of dry-stack masonry. 

There is no mortar. Every wall, terrace, causeway, and tower is made of hand-fitted slate and dark fieldstone, wedged together with smaller shims of flint. The architecture is massive in footprint—an urban grid of plazas, sunken courtyards, and stepped pyramid-foundations stretching three miles from spur to spur—but the buildings stand far apart from one another. Between a four-story watchtower and a neighboring granary, there might be two hundred yards of flat, paved stone plaza. This gives the city an austere, breathable geometry, as if the builders had infinite space and chose to leave most of it open to the crisp air.

Though there are no rivers and it never rains, the city is wet. The moisture comes from the stone itself and the low, invisible vapor that drifts across the plateau. Every horizontal surface—every slate roof, every broad courtyard flagstone, every step of the monumental staircases—is coated in a beads-of-sweat condensation. This perpetual dampness feeds a lush, resilient flora that softens the knife-edges of the young rock without breaking them. 

Thick, velvety carpets of emerald and chartreuse moss drape over the tops of the dry-stone walls like heavy pelts. From the unmortared chinks in the buildings sprout stiff, waxy-leaved ferns, rosette succulents the size of dinner plates, and woody shrubs bearing clusters of pale, hard berries. In the wide spaces between the buildings, groves of dwarf, flat-topped conifers grow directly out of narrow fissures in the bedrock, their needles constantly dripping single, slow pearls of water into the moss below. The greenery does not overtake or ruin the city; it has reached a static equilibrium with the masonry. The roots do not heave the stones; the moss does not rot. 

The city is heavily populated, not by people, but by animals that move through the avenues with the calm predictability of clockwork. There are hundreds of long-necked, slate-gray ungulates—something between an ibex and a vicuña—with wide, spongy hooves that make no clatter on the stone. They stand on the stepped roofs of the low buildings or lie in the centers of the vast plazas, chewing cud. Alongside them are flocks of heavy-bodied, flightless birds the size of turkeys, covered in iridescent black plumage that sheds water droplets as they walk. In the permanent shadows of the western walls, translucent, slow-moving amphibians as long as a human forearm cling to the vertical damp slate, their throat sacs pulsing once every few minutes.

Despite this abundance of life, the valley is profoundly quiet. There is no wind to hiss through the conifer needles. The birds do not call; the grazers do not bleat. The primary sounds are small, dry, and localized: the soft *pad-pad* of spongy hooves on wet moss, the occasional *tink* of a dislodged pebble falling down a quarry face, the quiet *plip* of water dropping from a fern frond into a stone cistern. 

Here and there, evidence of the builders’ tools remains, integrated into the stillness. On the unfinished upper tier of a terrace, a bronze pry-bar lies beside a wooden mallet and a basket of woven reeds filled with stone shims. The wood of the mallet is damp and dark, free of rot. A copper plumb-bob hangs from a wooden tripod over a half-laid wall; the string is taut, and the weight points straight down, utterly motionless. 

Nothing here is decaying, and nothing is growing larger. The grazers crop the moss, and the moss is always the same thickness. The cisterns in the plazas are always full to the exact same chisel-mark on their rims, neither overflowing nor evaporating. A herd of fifty ibex-creatures will walk down a broad avenue, turn left at a monumental obelisk, file up a ramp onto a roof terrace, and settle down in the exact patches of sunlight warmed by the unmoving sun, while another herd rises from a neighboring roof to take their place in the street below. The air smells faintly of wet slate, crushed pine needles, and clean wool—a cool, bright, breathable museum where the exhibits are alive, the masonry is sharp, and the afternoon is permanent.
