The basin is thirty miles across and has been ground flat by epochs of heavy, weeping sleet. There are no mountains left on the rim, only low, slumped shoulders of lead-colored slate that were planed smooth long before anything had eyes to see them. The sky hangs low—a dense, unmoving ceiling of pewter vapor that sheds a sourceless, twilight grey across the mire. It is well below freezing, yet nothing is solid. The water is too heavy with dissolved metallic salts, grease, and mineral slush to lock into ice. Instead, it lies in vast, shimmering sheets of black brine, or seeps endlessly from the porous stone in thick, syrupy curtains.

There are no buildings here. No stone has ever been stacked upon another by a deliberate hand; there are no roads, no pilings, no ruins, no fences. Yet the entire basin is a roaring, saturated engine. 

The ground does not consist of soil, because there has never been a leaf or a root to rot into dirt. The floor of the basin is a packed, writhing mattress of living iron and slick cartilage. What lives here—and the basin is choked with life, jammed shoulder-to-shoulder in the freezing mud—has bypassed the vegetable entirely and arrived at the industrial by way of the biological. Or perhaps the laws of matter have simply frayed at the edges until anatomy and machinery lost the ability to distinguish themselves.

Every square inch of the bog is occupied. Great, blubbery bellows-beasts, the size of locomotive boilers and covered in wet, shingled hide, lie half-submerged in the slush, rhythmically inhaling the freezing mist and exhaling blasts of scalding, sulfurous steam through flanged brass blowholes. Their ribs are cast-iron arches; their bellies thrum with the low, percussive *chug-thump* of internal pistons. Beside them, packed so tightly their flanks scrape and spark against one another, are colonies of sessile, piston-necked siphon-worms. They drive hollow, fluted drills into the ancient bedrock, drawing up black, mineral-rich slurry and spitting it into the air in rhythmic, clattering arcs.

The noise is immense and relentless. It is the sound of a continent-sized foundry operating underwater. There is no wind, yet the air vibrates with the wet, sucking wheeze of ten million leather valves, the high-pitched whine of spinning bone-turbines, the constant *clack-clack-clack* of chitinous gears failing to mesh in the cold, and the deep, tectonic groaning of the packed herds shifting their weight in the grease-silt.

Nothing is built, because everything is grown, and everything grows in the shape of work. 

In the middle distance, where the black water pools into a shallow, slush-choked lagoon, a herd of tall, spindly striders wades through the mire. They have no heads—only cantilevered booms of wet, yellow bone slick with synovial oil, from which hang clusters of clicking, galvanized hooks. They dip their hooks into the mud, dredging up squirming masses of blind, segmented cable-eels. When a strider catches a tangle of eels, it does not eat them; it splices them directly into the raw, sparking nerve-bundles frayed along its own spine, shuddering as the fresh voltage hits its joints, before marching three paces forward to repeat the motion.

And then the sky hiccups, and the striders are already three paces back again, dredging the exact same spot.

This is the fundamental rot at the core of the basin: causality has been eroded as flat and featureless as the surrounding slate. The air is thick with a cold, greasy condensation that smells of ozone and wet copper, and where the mist pools thickest, the rules of sequence and space pool with it. 

Look closely at the packed flats and the geometry curdles. A massive, slug-like dredge-crawler—forty tons of weeping grey fat and grinding rotary teeth—pushes through a bank of smaller, clicking valve-crabs. It crushes them into a paste of black blood and tiny brass springs. Yet ten yards behind the crawler's wake, the mud shivers, the sound of the crushing plays backward like a sucked-in breath, and the crabs are whole again, clicking their mandibles in a 4/4 industrial time signature, waiting to be crushed. 

Water flows uphill along the slick, hairless backs of dormant boiler-whales, drawn not by gravity, but by a localized magnetic pull Toward-Which-Things-Fall. In the dim, leaden light, you can see bands of the atmosphere that have sheared loose from the rest of the world. Through these hanging lenses of air, the landscape is visible from angles that do not exist—you are looking down at the mud from fifty feet up while standing ankle-deep in it; you are seeing the interior gearing of a creature three miles away superimposed over the weeping stone at your feet.

At the very edges of the wet flats, where the ancient rock meets the churning mass of fauna, a pale, non-living rime accumulates. It is the closest thing the basin has to lichen: a crystalline crust of solidified static and frozen grease. As the loud, rhythmic vibrations of the creatures pass through it, the crust shatters and reforms in geometric, circuit-like fractals, glowing for a fraction of a second with a cold, dead-blue arc light before the weeping brine washes it dark again.

Nothing here knows why it is pumping, dredging, filtering, or steaming. There is no product. There is no factory floor, no overseer, no blueprint. The ancient basin simply settled, eons ago, into its lowest possible energy state, and in this broken corner of reality, that state happens to be a freezing, biological machine shop, roaring at full capacity in the dark, grinding the same wet mud through its teeth forever.
