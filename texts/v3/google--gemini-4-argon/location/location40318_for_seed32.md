It is no wider than a courtyard, and it drops into the ground like an inverted bell. 

There is no native bedrock visible here, no soil, no clay, no unworked stone. Every square inch of the basin is composed of pale, unglazed ceramic tiles—millions of them, each no thicker than a thumbnail, stacked on their edges in tight, concentric rings that spiral down to a choked nadir. Whatever tools or kilns produced this place are gone, or were never machines to begin with; the tiles have the rough, pinched look of things shaped by fingers or mandibles, pressed from chalky kaolin and baked in an ancient sun. 

Because the tiles are packed so tightly, there is no empty space. It is a solid geometry of masonry compressed upon itself. Where the walls step downward, they do so in clustered, bulbous terraces that crowd toward the center, leaving scarcely enough room for a body to turn around. Over centuries of dry settlement, the sheer weight of the packing has crushed the lower rings into stabilized rubble, and the constant, dry wind has sanded the sharp rims of the pottery down into soft, powdery curves. Everything has found its final resting angle. Not a single shard has slipped or shifted in five hundred years.

The light is blinding. Above the narrow lip of the basin, the sky is a cloudless, pale slate, pouring a vertical flood of white daylight straight down into the throat of the depression. Because the ceramic is bone-white and lightly dusted with its own abraded powder, the light doesn’t just illuminate the space; it bounces furiously between the crowded terraces. There are no true shadows, only pockets of pale grey glare nestled in the tightest crevices. To look down into the center of the pit is to squint into a dry, matte brilliance that bleaches all color from the eye.

Despite the brilliance of the light, the air is distinctly cool—the sharp, cellar-like chill of clay that never retains heat. And the air is never quiet. 

The wind across the low plain above does not dip into the basin so much as it draws air out of it, creating a permanent, roaring draft that pulls upward through millions of tiny, dry interstices between the stacked tiles. The entire structure acts as a single, dense panpipe of baked earth. The sound is not a melody, because the wind above does not gust or falter; it blows with a rigid, flat consistency. Thus, the basin produces a continuous, deafening chord—a loud, hollow, multi-tonal *hiss-hum* that vibrates through the soles of the feet and fills the skull. It is the sound of a kiln cooling for eternity, a dry acoustic pressure so dense it feels like another solid material packed into the room.

Nothing crawls here. There are no spiders in the crevices, no beetles, no lizards warming themselves on the white edges. The dryness is absolute—an arid purity that desiccates anything soft within hours—and the roaring vibration of the tiles drives away anything with ears or nerve endings. 

Yet the masonry is not entirely dead. Wedged into the hairline fractures between the biscuits of clay are sparse, rigid fans of silver-grey scrub and wire-lichen. They do not grow so much as they simply persist, drawing whatever dry mineral trace they need from the clay and the airborne dust. Their stems are woody, pale as bleached driftwood, and brittle as glass. They have grown flat against the contours of the pottery, conforming to the packed density of the room so closely they look like pale mortar squeezing out from between the layers. Because the air current never varies its path or its force, these tough little plants do not sway. They are held at a constant, trembling tension, locked in place by the upward roar of the draft.

Nothing happens here. The sun does not seem to track across the opening so much as the ambient glare simply brightens and dims once a day, though while it is bright, it feels endless. The dust does not swirl; it hangs in fixed, standing waves in the roaring vents where opposing drafts meet and cancel out. It is a tiny, suffocatingly crowded room of white clay, roaring at the sky, chill to the touch, bleached to the bone, and completely finished with time.
