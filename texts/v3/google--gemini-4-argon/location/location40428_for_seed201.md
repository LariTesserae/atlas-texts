The rock here is young enough that it still holds the memory of its splitting. Three walls of raw, blue-black basalt rise straight up for two hundred feet, enclosing a space no wider than a cathedral nave. The stone is fractured into hexagonal columns that look freshly cleaved, their edges keen enough to slice leather, untouched by the rounding patience of ancient ice. Yet the fourth side of this box canyon does not exist; it simply drops away into an abyssal sea of cloud. 

We are at an altitude where the air is thin, frigid, and dry as bone, except for what the updrafts bring.

Because the canyon faces the sun and is walled in black stone, the light is staggering. It is an aggressive, high-altitude brilliance. The sky above isn't blue so much as a violet-tinted white, and the sun strikes the quartz veins in the basalt with a glare that forces the eyes to a permanent squint. Shadows don't fade here; they are cut with a knife. Step out of the sun, and the temperature drops twenty degrees in a heartbeat.

Despite the freezing air, the floor and lower ledges of the amphitheater are smothered in a bizarre, hyper-specialized lushness. There are no trees—nothing with wood could survive the nightly freeze—but there is a waist-deep carpet of giant alpine rosettes, velvet-leafed groundsel, and massive, spherical cushion plants that look like boulders upholstered in emerald moss. They survive on the breath of the abyss. Every twenty minutes or so, a thermal swells up from the unseen valleys ten thousand feet below, pushing a dense tongue of freezing fog over the lip of the drop. The fog rolls through the canyon, instantly condensing on the millions of fine, translucent hairs covering the giant plants. For three minutes, the world is blind and white and damp. Then the wind shifts, the sun burns the vapor away in seconds, and the plants sit glittering with millions of water droplets that freeze into pearls of ice in the shade and steam away in the sun.

This constant, rapid cycling of sun and fog drives an ecosystem in frantic, silent motion. 

The air is too thin to carry sound well, and the thick, spongy turf absorbs whatever noise remains. Yet the space is teeming. Hundreds of snow-partridges, plump and white-feathered with black, scaly legs, move through the corridors between the giant rosettes like flocks of ghosts. They do not call to one another; they communicate by flashing the bright ultraviolet patches on their tail feathers, visible only in this blinding light. Higher up, on the razor-sharp ledges of the hexagonal columns, dozens of cliff-hares sit huddled in the sun-traps. They have oversized, black-veined ears that they angle toward the sun like solar panels to warm their blood before ducking back into the freezing shadows to graze on rock-lichen.

Everything happens fast, and everything happens quietly. A shadow drops from the violet sky—a lammergeier or a white mountain-falcon—and the canyon erupts into kinetic panic. Hundreds of hares and partridges scatter in a synchronized, silent wave of white and grey, diving into the crevices of the young rock or beneath the spiky armor of the rosette leaves. The strike is just a dull *thump* of feathers, a puff of kicked-up frost, and the predator is already banking out over the void, dropping into the clouds with its prize. Within seconds, the remaining animals emerge, chewing rapidly, their breath making tiny, frantic puffs of steam in the cold air.

Whoever comes here understands the rhythm of the fog and the sun. Their presence is spare, fitted into the violent geometry of the stone. 

Near the lip of the precipice, where the updrafts are strongest, someone has erected a series of catch-harps. They are simple, primitive frames lashed together from the bleached rib-bones of high-altitude grazers and anchored into the cracks of the basalt with wedges of hammered copper. Across the bone frames, they have strung nets of braided sinew and horsehair. When the fog rolls in, the nets catch the moisture, which trickles down into shallow, hand-chiseled basins in the rock floor, freezing immediately into clear, harvestable slabs of ice.

Further back, tucked into a shallow overhang where two walls meet, is a single dry-stone windbreak, waist-high, built from fallen basalt prisms stacked like firewood. The builders have left their tools behind, neatly arranged on a flat stone: a few scrapers knapped from black obsidian, a heavy bone mallet, and a coil of thick yak-hair rope weighted down with stones. Chiseled into the dark rock behind the shelter are tally marks—hundreds of them, fresh and pale where the stone’s dark skin has been bruised away, counting the days, or the fogs, or the migrations of the silent birds. 

Aside from the low, breathy whistle of the wind through the sinew nets and the occasional sharp *crack* of the young rock expanding in the fierce sun, the canyon is as quiet as a tomb, even as a thousand warm-blooded hearts beat frantically within its walls.
