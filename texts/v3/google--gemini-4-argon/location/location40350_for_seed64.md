It is a pocket no larger than a cistern, hollowed out of the vertical granite near the summit of an air-starved, jagged spire. Outside this rock chimney, the world drops away for miles into thin, freezing emptiness. Inside, there is no room to stand upright. The space is packed to absolute capacity—a dense, suffocating knot of dry biology and old iron wedged into a narrow cleft of stone.

There is no water here, and there never has been. Even ice is absent; the altitude is too dry, the stone too desiccated. Yet the pocket is choked with growth. The vegetation is lush, heavy, and entirely metallic-mineral in nature. Thick, overlapping fronds of brittle silica-kelp and dense, rubbery rosettes of sulfur-moss press against every inch of the granite walls, feeding not on moisture, but on the relentless mechanical vibration and the thin, ionized drafts whistling through the rock. Their leaves are fat and gray-green, dusted with a dry, talcum-like pollen that never settles. Vines as thick as ship’s cables, armored in flaking slate-colored bark, coil over one another in a strangled, impenetrable mattress that fills the center of the hollow.

Wedged deep within this botanical crush are the machines. They are not large—an assortment of cast-iron housings, brass pressure-spheres, and heavy, grease-caked flywheels the size of millstones, bolted directly into the bedrock centuries ago by unknown hands for an unguessable purpose. The vegetation has not destroyed them; it has integrated them. Vines wrap tightly around the stationary axles and thread through the gaps in the iron grates, holding the machinery in a rigid, dry embrace. 

The air is cool and smells sharply of ozone, pulverized flint, and cold machine oil. 

Despite the lack of movement, the place is deafening. A relentless, high-altitude gale blows across the jagged lip of the chimney thirty feet above, turning the cavity itself into the pipe of a colossal stone organ. The wind does not stir the packed plants at the bottom, which are wedged too tightly to sway, but the acoustic pressure fills the pocket with a deep, thrumming roar. Beneath the wind-roar is the sound of the machines. They have no moving external parts—the flywheels are locked tight by the woody coils of the vines—yet their interiors are furiously alive. Inside the heavy iron casings, unseen dynamos and high-frequency oscillators spin endlessly, powered by the thermal gradients of the deep rock or the roaring wind-catchers above. They emit a piercing, industrial scream, a layered chord of grinding gears and electrical hum that vibrates straight through the soles of the feet and into the teeth.

It is this vibration that the plants eat. The leaves shiver at the exact frequency of the dynamos, converting the raw kinetic rattle of the iron housings into slow, dry cellular growth. Where a frond presses directly against a humming brass plate, its veins glow with a faint, sickly amber luminescence. 

This is the only light in the hollow, aside from a meager, slate-blue smudge of sky visible through a crooked fissure far overhead, choked with the silhouettes of iron intake-vanes and dry briars. In this dim, bruising twilight, the textures of the pocket blur together. It is difficult to tell where the pitted, wind-scoured granite ends, where the bolted iron flanges begin, and where the woody, gray-scaled hulls of the tubers take over. Everything has been worn down to a shared, matte smoothness by centuries of high-frequency shivering and the microscopic abrasion of airborne rock dust. The edges of the bolts are rounded; the oldest stalks of the plants are polished like pewter.

Nothing crawls here. There are no insects, no spiders, no mites in the dry duff of shed silica-leaves that packs the floor three feet deep. Without water, animal chemistry is impossible, and the air is too thin and loud for birds to ever drop down the flue. 

The pocket exists in a state of loud, crowded paralysis. The plants have grown until there is nowhere left to expand, pressing against the stone, against the iron, and against each other until they have achieved a solid, interlocking equilibrium. The machines have reached a terminal pitch that never wavers by a single hertz. A broken pressure gauge, its glass face cracked in three places, has been held together by the grip of a split tendril for a hundred years; the needle is jammed hard against the brass pin at the maximum reading, trembling slightly in the amber gloom, measuring a pressure that never drops and never explodes.
