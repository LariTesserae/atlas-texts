The space is no larger than a tall pantry or the interior of an ancient cistern, yet it is built like a city. 

There is no stone here, no mud or mineral dirt. Every wall, floor, and ceiling is a continuous, living mat of hyper-dense, pale-green bryophyte and calcified root-horn, cold and slick to the touch. The plant matter has grown so tightly upon itself that it has achieved the hardness of oak, though it still weeps a constant, frigid sweat. Because the growth is young—having surged into this subterranean pocket only a few seasons ago—its forms are not rounded like typical moss, but sharp and turgid. It grows in crisp, angular shelves, overlapping plates, and rigid, thorns-outward pleats that have not yet been softened by decay. 

Whoever or whatever shaped this room used bone chisels, flint saws, and heavy bindings of split sinew to carve and train the rapid growth into an intricate, multi-tiered settlement. The four walls are terraced from floor to ceiling into hundreds of miniature, sharp-edged avenues, aqueducts, and recessed alcoves. Tiny suspension bridges of braided, living grass span the empty central shaft of the room, pulled taut and anchored with pegs of shaved horn. Despite the complexity of the carving, the middle of the chamber feels wide and vacant; the air is thin, frigid, and still, giving the vertical city a sparse, cavernous breathing room.

It is pitch black, save for the faintest, ghostly smudge of grey-violet bruise-light bleeding through a dense plug of translucent tubers at the very top of the shaft. 

In this freezing dark, the architecture is completely overrun. 

The carved terraces and woven causeways are packed shoulder-to-shoulder with a slow, churning metropolis of blind, cold-blooded life. Pale, translucent isopods the size of plums trundle down the vertical avenues, their segmented plates clicking softly against the crisp root-stone. The carved alcoves—perhaps meant as shrines, granaries, or dwellings for some absent builder—are stuffed with clusters of sleeping bats whose frost-tipped fur rises and falls in a single, synchronized heave. Long,ribbon-like worms, striped like agate, loop themselves through the woven railings of the grass bridges, acting as living pulleys that raise and lower balls of chewed pith from one level to the next. 

Everything is wet. Cold condensation forms constantly on the jagged tips of the plant-leaves, swelling until the droplets break loose and plummet through the dark central shaft. The primitive builders anticipated this: they notched sharp, V-shaped gutters into the living walls to catch the run-off, channeling the freezing water through split-reed pipes and down into a geometric grid of sunken basins in the floor. 

The ecosystem operates in a state of frantic, chilly urgency, yet it makes almost no noise. Because of the cold, the metabolism of the swarm is heavy and deliberate. There are no buzzing wings or chittering calls. Instead, there is only a thick, velvet hush, punctuated by the heavy *plop* of falling water drops and the soft, wet slide of thousands of shells, scales, and damp pelts brushing against one another in the dark. 

Along the third tier, a phalanx of spider-like creatures with legs like peeled twigs moves in a disciplined line, clearing a fresh growth of pale shoots that threatens to choke an intersection. They use their heavy mandibles to snip the crisp stems, passing the trimmings backward down the line to be packed into the retaining walls. Right beside them, oblivious, a broad-backed toad with skin like wet slate sits wedged into a carved archway, its throat pulsing slowly as it waits for the blind beetles migrating up the central stairwell to walk directly into its mouth. 

The city is growing, and the city is being eaten, and the city is being repaired. If a bridge snaps under the weight of a climbing salamander, the frayed ends of the living grass immediately begin to curl and seek each other out in the damp air, while spiders quickly lash the gap with cold, sticky silk to keep the traffic moving. It is a vertical box of freezing, blind industry—a carved botanical engine running silently in the dark, completely full of cold blood and moving feet.
