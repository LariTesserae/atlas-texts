The basin extends for thirty miles in every direction, a flat pan of newly fractured slate and raw feldspar that has never known the softening touch of rain. The rock is fresh, broken into crisp, unweathered polyhedrons with edges sharp enough to draw blood. There is no dust here, no soil, and no mud. Every surface is bone-dry, radiating the steady, pleasant warmth of stones baking under a late-morning sun. 

Yet the basin is choked with a thick, towering jungle.

The flora does not grow from the rock so much as it extrudes from the geometry of the space itself. Without a drop of water in their veins, the plants rely on a non-aqueous, post-biological chemistry that blurs the line between botany and quantum engineering. Great, overarching fronds of coppery, woven carbon-lattice unfurl from the dry stone, their surfaces warm and supple like dry leather. Beneath them, dense thickets of broad-leaved shrubs crowd every cubic foot of air. Their leaves are translucent, layered like sheets of mica, and filled with a pressurized, incandescent gas instead of sap. Where the clear, bright daylight strikes them, the leaves do not cast simple shadows; they refract the light into localized spatial anomalies. Look through a canopy of these leaves, and the background fractures—a boulder twenty feet away is duplicated seven times in a radial pattern, or seen from three different angles simultaneously.

There are no buildings here, no pylons, no wires, no walls, and no ruins. Whatever technology brought this place into being has no physical footprint of its own; it has been entirely subsumed into the rewrite of the local physics. The environment itself is the machine, running on an operating system that has dispensed with thermodynamics and causality.

Because there is no wind, the jungle does not sway. The air is dead-still, warm, and smells faintly of ozone and hot quartz. The sun—or the bright, white source of illumination that hangs at a sixty-degree angle in the pale, cloudless sky—never moves. It is locked in permanent mid-morning. Because there is no wind and no decay, the jungle is trapped in a state of lush, overflowing equilibrium. Nothing withers. No dry leaves fall to create litter on the sharp-edged gravel below. The plants have grown to their maximum density, leaves pressing flat against neighboring leaves, vines of spun boron braided tightly around trunks of dry, fibrous ceramic, and then they have simply stopped, drinking the static light without producing waste or motion.

As you walk through the low-lying expanse, the breakdown of reality becomes a physical obstacle. The density of the synthetic vegetation alters the metric of the space between the trunks. A gap between two towering, fern-like structures might appear to be three feet wide, yet require twenty paces to cross, the dry gravel crunching rhythmically beneath your boots while the leaves on either side pass by in extreme slow motion. In other places, the flora grows in four dimensions; a thick, woody vine will curve upward through the warm air, vanish cleanly into nothingness where it intersects an invisible topological fold, and reappear ten feet to the left, continuing its static climb toward the light. 

It is an overwhelmingly quiet place. Without water, there is no drip or trickle. Without wind, there is no rustle of foliage. The silence is vast, open, and dry, pressing in from the distant, sharp-toothed ridges that ring the horizon. 

The only sounds are the rare, dry clicks of the local fauna and the quiet hum of the broken geometry. Occasionally, a creature moves through the dense undergrowth. They are scarce, solitary things that look like biological afterthoughts—slender, six-legged forms composed of articulated flint and golden, dry muscle tissue. They do not eat the plants, nor do they drink. They navigate the thickets by phase-shifting. When a creature approaches a dense wall of dry, rubbery stalks, it does not push them aside. Instead, its body drops in resolution, flickering into a coarse grid of dull light, passing silently through the solid matter of the plant, and snapping back into sharp, physical focus on the other side with a sound like a single snap of static electricity. 

Here and there, in the rare clearings where the razor-sharp bedrock is exposed to the bright sky, the air itself exhibits shear-faults. A horizontal line, six feet off the ground, will shimmer faintly like a heat mirage. Above the line, the tops of the motionless trees are displaced three feet to the left of their trunks below. If you reach a hand across the boundary, you feel no temperature change, no resistance—only a sudden, dizzying disconnect as your fingertips appear three feet away from your wrist, still warm, still dry, bathed in the unchanging, brilliant light of an afternoon that never arrives.
