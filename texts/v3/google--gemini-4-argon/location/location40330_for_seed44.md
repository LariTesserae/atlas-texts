The place is a sunken industrial courtyard, no wider than a cathedral’s nave and perhaps twice as long, boxed in on all four sides by four-story brick mill buildings that have long since surrendered their facades to the wet heat. It sits in a low, flat river basin where the water table is practically at the soles of your boots. 

You do not walk on dirt or paving stones here; you walk on a dense, springy mattress of root-bound industrial detritus. Decades of fallen leaves, sloughed bark, and rotting fern-fronds have mulched into the cinders and iron filings, creating a dark, spongy peat that squelches under weight. When you step, black water wells up around the edges of your shoes, warm as bathwater and filmed with a faint, iridescent sheen of machine oil. 

The vegetation is not merely growing in the courtyard; it has become the architecture. Giant tree ferns, their trunks thick and fibrous as coconut husks, press right up against the rusted cast-iron pillars of the old loading docks. Strangler figs and massive, scalloped philodendrons have climbed the brick walls, their woody aerial roots dropping straight down like plumb lines to drink from the flooded floor. They have woven themselves through the fire escapes, pried apart the mortar, and completely encased the overhead pipe-racks. From one side of the yard to the other, what looks like a series of green, living suspension bridges are actually heavy-duty steam conduits and electrical cable trays, entirely swallowed by booming masses of epiphytes, staghorn ferns, and thick, wet curtains of Spanish moss.

Beneath the foliage, the machinery is still running. It has been running for fifty years, unattended, doing the heavy, patient work of keeping the broader district’s water table from swallowing the lowlands entirely. 

At the center of the court, half-buried in a mound of creeping ficus and weeping moss, sits a pair of colossal, low-pressure centrifugal pumps and their accompanying steam condensers. They are cast from pig iron a century ago, painted a faded, chalky machinery-green that is now almost indistinguishable from the lichens crusting their flanks. They do not move fast, and they do not whine. Instead, they produce a heavy, rhythmic, percussive thrum—a *chug-thump, chug-thump* that you feel in your sternum before you hear it in your ears. 

The sound is loud, trapped and amplified by the surrounding walls and muffled only slightly by the wet leaves. Added to the deep bass of the engines is the constant, rushing hiss of water. Condensation pours off the cold intake pipes in steady ribbons. Steam vents from a brass pressure-relief valve high up on the north wall, letting go with a regular, rhythmic *shhhhh-clack* that sends a fresh plume of warm vapor rolling into the canopy of broad-leafed monstera. Water drips from every leaf-tip, runs down the grooved bark of the vines, and splashes into the shallow, warm pools between the foundation blocks.

Light filters down from the open rectangle of sky four stories up. It is a flat, humid, coastal light—the white glare of an overcast afternoon—that turns green and submarine as it passes through the three-tiered canopy of broad leaves and hanging roots. Down on the floor of the court, the illumination is even and shadowless, catching on the wet brass of an oiler cup, the slick green skin of a banana leaf, or the slow, greasy turn of an eight-foot flywheel emerging from a bank of horsetails.

Because the machinery is loud and the space is completely walled in, very little animal life ventures here. There are no birds in the canopy; the steady, bone-shaking vibration of the brickwork keeps them away. Occasionally, a pale, blind millipede the length of a finger will curl over a warm copper pipe, or a single large, rust-colored moth will spend three days motionless on the underside of a corrugated iron awning. The ecology here is almost purely botanical and mechanical, locked in a slow, warm clinch. 

Everything is in a state of settled, soggy equilibrium. The iron rusts, flaking off in thick, dark red scales that enrich the muck for the ferns; the roots of the figs wrap tighter around the vibrating housings of the turbines, actually anchoring the old bolts in place long after their threads have rotted out. A thick, warm smell hangs over the whole enclosure—a heavy broth of boiled river water, wet limestone, crushed greenery, and hot axle grease. Nothing happens quickly. A flywheel completes its revolution; a leaf tips its load of collected condensate onto a warm iron cylinder where it sizzles softly away; a root creeps another millimeter across a brick sill.
