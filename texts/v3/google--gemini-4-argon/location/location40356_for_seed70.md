High in the mountains, a vast, bowl-shaped valley sits completely submerged beneath two hundred feet of warm, gin-clear water. 

There is no river feeding it, and no waterfall spilling over its lip. The water is held inside a continuous, jagged ring of recently uplifted basalt—a geological bowl that rose from the crust so recently that its underwater cliffs are still razor-sharp, unrounded by time or current. The water itself is heated from below by a broad, quiet thermal plate, maintaining the entire miles-wide basin at the temperature of a tepid bath. Because the air at this high altitude is much cooler than the water, the surface of the lake—far overhead—is permanently obscured by a thick, blinding blanket of white steam. 

Down inside the basin, however, the world is illuminated by a bright, shadowless daylight. The sun at this elevation is fierce, and as it hits the ceiling of steam, the light scatters into an even, glowing luminescence that penetrates all the way to the valley floor. Looking up from the bottom, there is no sun and no sky, only a flat, radiant roof of pearl-white light.

Beneath this bright ceiling, a terrestrial alpine valley has adapted to a liquid sky. 

The terrain is a chaos of fresh rock. Tall, hexagonal columns of dark grey stone rise from the floor like bundles of freshly snapped pencils. Talus slopes of jagged, blue-black shale spill down from the cliffs, their edges still crisp enough to cut. There is almost no silt here, no mud to cloud the water. The rock has not yet had the millennia required to grind itself into sand. 

Instead of soil, the lushness of the valley grows directly out of the sharp stone, clinging to the fractures with pale, resinous holdfasts. And because the water is warm and rich with dissolved volcanic gases, the flora has grown to a staggering, crowded density. 

It is a forest that belongs simultaneously to the mountain and the reef. Towering, conifer-like structures rise eighty feet into the water column, their trunks flexible and boneless, swaying in imperceptible thermal updrafts. Instead of needles, they are draped in heavy, velvety fronds of deep burgundy, olive, and bruised violet. Between these giants, the space is packed with terraced gardens of translucent, cabbage-like rosettes, some six feet across, which cup the diffused light like satellite dishes. 

Because there is no wind and no current, the vegetation grows with a geometric fragility that would be instantly shattered in the open air or a moving sea. Spires of glassy, spiral-shaped kelp thread their way through the denser canopies, held strictly vertical by tiny bladders of warm gas located at their tips. Webs of golden, hair-thin filaments stretch from one basalt spire to the next, forming living curtains that filter the warm, mineral-heavy water. The density is overwhelming; to move twenty feet requires parting three different layers of slick, rubbery foliage, yet the water between the leaves is so clear that you can see for half a mile down the length of the valley through gaps in the canopy, watching the dark, sharp ridges fade into a pale, turquoise haze.

The rules of this place are dictated by slowness and weightlessness. Nothing falls quickly. When a heavy, broad leaf finally detaches from its stem, it does not drop; it drifts downward over the course of days, spinning at an agonizingly slow pace until it settles onto the sharp shale below. 

The plants do not rely on insects or wind for pollination. Instead, at irregular intervals, the tall burgundy spires release clouds of luminescent yellow spores. Without currents to scatter them, these clouds hang together in the water like suspended smudges of paint, stretching into long, lazy ribbons over the course of weeks as they are pulled by the weak, rising warmth of the valley floor. 

It is a profoundly quiet place. The ears are filled mostly with the soft, warm pressure of the depth itself—a blank, enveloping hush. There is no crashing of waves overhead, no rushing of underwater streams. The only sounds are internal and textural: the occasional, resonant *tink* of a cooling basalt rock fracturing further down on the talus slope, or the faint, rhythmic creak of a massive, water-logged trunk leaning against its neighbor. 

If you wait long enough, you can hear the breath of the valley. Millions of tiny, silver bubbles of oxygen and volcanic gas form on the undersides of the translucent leaves. They swell slowly over hours until their buoyancy overcomes the surface tension of the plant. When a large bubble breaks free, it rises with a soft, liquid *plop*, wobbling upward through the crowded branches, occasionally striking another leaf with a dull thud as it ascends toward the ceiling of steam.

Animal life is almost entirely absent, making the lushness feel strangely unmonitored, like an overgrown greenhouse abandoned by its caretakers. There are no schools of fish darting through the branches, no crustaceans scraping at the rock. The rare creatures that do exist here move at the same pace as the plants. 

Once in a great while, a pale, ribbon-like swimmer—something between an eel and a flatworm, ten feet long and nearly transparent—can be seen undulating across the open spaces between the basalt columns. It moves so slowly that at first glance it appears to be another drifting trail of spores. Elsewhere, clinging to the jagged edges of the dark rock, are solitary, shell-less mollusks the size of clenched fists, their bodies the exact color of the basalt, taking months to graze a single square inch of mineral film from the stone.

The valley exists in a state of suspended geological youth. The sharp rocks have not yet begun to crumble; the warm vents have not yet begun to cool; the dense, tangled jungle has no predators to prune it back. It simply sits in the high mountains, bathed in its own warmth and the diffused, milky glow of the highland sun, growing thicker and quieter with every passing century.
