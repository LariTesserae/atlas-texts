The place is no larger than a great abbey courtyard, bounded on four sides by sheer walls of hand-mortared yellow brick, yet it has no outside. If a mason’s chisel were driven through the outermost course of brick, the tip would immediately poke out through the opposite wall, fifty paces away, emerging from a mortar joint at the exact same height. There is no bedrock beneath the floor, only vaults beneath vaults of fresh, sharp-edged terracotta tile, and above, there is no sky—only a luminous, chalky ceiling of stretched parchment held up by pegged cedar trusses, glowing with the steady, shadowless warmth of an afternoon that has forgotten what a sun is. 

The air is parched and fever-warm, smelling of baked clay, beeswax, dried sage, and old linen. There is no water here—no dampness in the mortar, no condensation on the copper brackets, no sap in the stems. Everything is bone-dry, freshly built, and untouched by weather. The chisel-marks on the limestone plinths are still white and powdery; the wooden scaffolding beams have not yet grayed or split.

Yet the courtyard is choked with lush, dry greenery. 

The vegetation grows directly out of the joinery. Thick, succulent vines with leaves like hammered green felt uncurl from the dry gaps between the bricks, fed not by moisture, but by *measure* and *angle*. Where two walls meet at a ninety-degree corner, a dense bank of ferns—stiff and papery as dried lotus leaves, yet vibrant emerald—spills outward, drinking the sharpness of the intersection. Broad-leafed shrubs with bark made of tightly wound hemp twine root themselves in the geometric centers of floor tiles. If a tile is cracked or shifted off-center, the bush above it yellows and crisps into ash within an hour. The plants do not photosynthesize light; they metabolize the labor of the invisible hands that laid the brick. They are lush because the masonry is fresh and precise.

Because the space is small and the architecture is cramped—filled with low brick buttresses, waist-high partition walls, dry cisterns, and wooden A-frames that hold up nothing at all—the foliage turns the courtyard into a dense, chest-high thicket of parchment-leaf and dry briar. To move through it is to brush constantly against warm, stiff leaves that leave a streak of green chalk on the skin.

And the thicket is teeming with life, though almost none of it makes a sound.

They are creatures of workshop scrap and geometry, born from the breakdowns in the courtyard’s crowded rules. In the warm shade beneath the brick arches, hundreds of creatures the size of hares—built of braided straw, unrefined beeswax, and knapped flint—nest in the dry ferns. They have no mouths and need no water; they breathe by slowly expanding and contracting their ribcages like fireplace bellows, taking in the warm air and exhaling a faint dust of powdered lime. 

Higher up, clinging to the cedar trusses and the dry vines, are thousands of slow, heavy beetles with carapaces of glazed pottery shard, and long, multi-jointed lizards that look as though they were whittled from seasoned boxwood. When a wooden lizard crawls across a brick wall, its body does not bend over the mortar joints; instead, its middle section ceases to exist as it crosses the gap, its head emerging on the next brick while its tail is still vanishing from the last. 

This is the fundamental nature of the courtyard: space here is stitched together by hand, and the seams are loose. 

Scale and distance misbehave whenever the eye loses track of a straight line. A low brick well-curb in the center of the room is three paces wide if walked around to the left, and eleven paces wide if walked around to the right. Beneath a dense canopy of dry, broad-leafed fig-vines, a flight of three stone steps leads down to a sunken flagstones landing; if you descend those three steps, you find yourself standing on a wooden catwalk twenty feet above the courtyard floor, looking down at the very vines you just ducked beneath. 

The fauna navigate these fractures with slow, habitual grace. A flock of flightless, plump birds with feathers of split goose-quill and raw wool will step off the edge of a high scaffold, fall two inches, and land softly on the courtyard floor thirty feet below, having bypassed the middle air entirely. Two of the wax-and-flint hares can occupy the exact same patch of warm tile at the same time, their bodies intersecting like two shadows cast by different candles, twitching their long ears in slow, synchronized contentment without ever touching.

Despite the crowding of beasts and vines, the silence is profound. There is no wind to rustle the stiff leaves, no water to drip, no insects that buzz. The animals have no voices. The only sounds are dry, domestic, and small: the occasional *tick* of cooling terracotta, the soft *shhh-shhh* of a thousand wooden lizards dragging their bellies over unsealed brick, the faint *creak* of a hemp-tree thickening its trunk by another twist of fiber, and the muffled, rhythmic *puff* of the bellows-hares breathing in the undergrowth.

Everything happens at the pace of an hourglass running out of very fine silt. A pottery-beetle takes all afternoon to cross a single floor tile. The light in the parchment ceiling never dims, only shifts its hue imperceptibly from the yellow of fresh butter to the amber of old rosin over the course of weeks, before slowly creeping back. 

In the corners where the foliage is thickest, the masonry is slowly continuing to build itself, or perhaps unbuild itself—it is impossible to tell which. A wooden trowel rests beside a neat pile of dry mortar-dust; every few hours, a single grain of sand lifts from the pile and seats itself into a wall joint with a microscopic *click*, and from that newly sealed joint, a tiny, emerald-green shoot of dry ivy immediately pushes out its first papery leaf.
