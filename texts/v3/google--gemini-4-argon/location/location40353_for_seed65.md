The air up here is a physical wall, moving from west to east without pause, without gusts, without lulls. It is not weather; it is the permanent condition of the altitude. At this height, where the sky has thinned to a hard, mineral violet-blue, the wind passes through the perforated limestone of the massif and produces a sound so immense it erases the concept of hearing. It is a continuous, polyphonic roar—part sub-bass rumble that vibrates in the sternum, part high, whistling shriek—generated by miles of wind-scoured flutes, arches, and vertical chimneys acting as a single, planetary-scale organ. Because the wind never alters its velocity, the chord never changes. It has been playing the same deafening note for ten thousand years.

The landscape is an open basin of bleached, bone-white stone, several miles across, tilted slightly toward a blinding sun. The light is ruthless. With so little atmosphere to scatter it, the glare turns the pale rock into a mirror, and the shadows cast by the great, eroded pillars are pitch-black and razor-sharp, as if cut from tin. Yet for all the brilliance of the sun, there is no warmth in it. The rock remains cold to the touch, and the wind strips heat from anything exposed in seconds. 

There is almost nothing in the middle of the basin. The erosion here is ancient and finished; the loose scree and dust were blown off the edge of the world ages ago. What remains is a vast, sweeping floor of bedrock, polished to a dull satin sheen, interrupted only by widely spaced, streamlined monoliths that lean into the wind like the prows of ships. Between these formations, the emptiness is absolute. You can see for miles with complete clarity—every crack in a distant pillar five miles off looks close enough to touch—creating a vertigo of scale where distance loses its meaning.

Water never falls from the sky here, nor does it pool into streams. Instead, it arrives on the wind as microscopic mist, torn from cloud banks miles below and driven upward against the stone. On the windward faces of the monoliths, this vapor condenses under pressure, forming a perpetual, glistening sweat. The rock is dark and slick on the west side, bone-dry and chalky on the east. In shallow, scooped-out hollows in the stone floor, small lenses of icy water collect, their surfaces vibrating in tight, geometric standing waves driven by the acoustic resonance of the roaring rock. 

Around these damp aprons and vibrating puddles grows the only vegetation: a tough, wire-thin scrub that hugs the stone as tightly as solder. Its leaves are grey and coated in a silica rind that clatters faintly when brushed, though no one could ever hear it over the wind. Long, rust-colored lichens trail from the leeward edges of the pillars, stiff as dried sinew, drinking the mist that eddies behind the stone.

Despite the barrenness and the staggering noise, the basin is teeming with life, though none of it walks in the open. The rock itself is porous, riddled with thousands of horizontal fissures and wind-carved tubes, and every crevice is packed with living things. Millions of heavy-bodied, wingless insects—nearly the size of a human hand, with flat, streamlined carapaces the color of wet slate—crust the damp leeward walls, grazing on the fast-growing lichen and microscopic algae brought in by the mist. 

Feeding on them are the cliff-runners: dense flocks of plumed, lizard-like creatures with four legs and heavy, counterbalancing tails. They have no ears—in this acoustic pressure, eardrums would rupture—and instead communicate entirely through visual flashes, erecting crests of iridescent, ultraviolet-reflecting scales that catch the brilliant sunlight like signal mirrors. Across the wide, empty stretches of the basin floor, the stone seems to shimmer with tiny, silent explosions of violet and blinding white light as thousands of these animals signal from crevice to crevice. Above them, heavy, leathery gliders with rigid wingspans do not flap; they simply step off the ledges into the ninety-mile-an-hour gale and hang completely motionless in the air, suspended on the standing updrafts like kites pinned to an invisible ceiling, dropping like stones only when they spot prey in the shadow-lines below.

Here and there, wedged deeply into the stone slits where the wind cannot pry them loose, are the tools of the people who work this height. There are no huts, no cairns, no walls—anything built above the profile of the bedrock is eventually shaken apart by the acoustic vibration or sheared off by the gale. Instead, there are only long, curved gaffs made of laminated bone and horn, bound with raw sinew, left anchored in cracks for the next passerby. There are shallow troughs chiseled by hand into the sweating west faces of the pillars to guide the condensation into rows of hollowed-out stone bowls. And stretched across the mouths of the largest wind-tubes are heavy nets woven from human hair and twisted gut, permanently bellied out and taut as drumheads, harvesting both the fat, blown-in insects and the careless gliders that drift too close to the rock face. 

Nothing rots here; the cold and the wind desiccate any dead thing into light leather within hours. A bone gaff wedged into a fissure fifty years ago looks as fresh and stark under the unblinking sun as one placed there an hour ago. The gliders hang motionless in the violet sky; the water shivers in its stone basins; the light blazes; the mountain screams its single, unending chord.
