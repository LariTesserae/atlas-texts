To stand on the upper cornices of the Shelf is to look out over an altitude that has no end, a sheer, ragged tilt of the planet’s crust where the stone was broken yesterday in geological time and has not yet learned to soften. The air is thin, brilliantly clear, and piercingly cold. The sky above is the dark, hard blue of high altitudes, casting a sharp, unblinking daylight across five hundred miles of dry-stone masonry. 

There is no distinction here between the mountain and the city; there is only the Escarpment, and the Escarpment has been entirely disassembled and stacked back together by hand.

Because the rock is young—a brittle, dark-grey slate shot through with veins of white quartz—it splits cleanly along flat planes. Over thousands of slow generations, without mortar, pulleys, or iron, the builders have pried the mountain apart with fire-wedges and bone levers, rearranging the talus into an unbroken, continent-spanning warren of terraces, retaining walls, corbelled domes, and narrow, wind-baffled alleys. The city does not rise into towers; it hugs the verticality of the range, clinging to the forty-degree slopes in millions of overlapping, flat-roofed tiers, dense as barnacles on a hull. From a distance, the vast face of the range looks as though it has been cross-hatched with a fine steel nib. 

It is a dry cold, mostly. There are no rivers—the elevation is too high, the air too chill to hold the heavy rains of the lowlands—and there are no glaciers, because the atmosphere is too starved of moisture to dump heavy snows. Instead, the city drinks the clouds. 

Every afternoon, a slow, oceanic bank of fog rises from the unseen basins leagues below. It climbs the lower buttresses and infiltrates the miles of grey stone. When the cloud passes through the city, the dry slate turns the color of wet charcoal. Moisture beads on the rough surfaces of a billion stacked flagstones. The inhabitants have shaped the city to harvest this dampness: thousands of miles of delicately tilted stone gutters, slick with frost in the mornings and weeping in the afternoons, channel the condensation drop by drop into subterranean cisterns lined with stamped, icy clay. Beside these trickling seams grows the only green the altitude allows—thick, rubbery aprons of black lichen, knuckles of bright yellow frost-moss, and low, woody mats of grease-bush that burn with a slow, oily heat when dried.

Despite the austerity of the rock and the sparse vegetation, the city is thick with warm blood. You cannot walk ten paces down a stepped alleyway without giving way to the beasts that make the city possible. 

The stone terraces are crowded with slow, heavy-bodied ungulates—creatures something like musk-oxen, something like dromedaries, covered in triple-layered skirts of coarse, russet wool that drag along the damp flagstones. They stand shoulder to shoulder in the wind-breaks, thousands upon thousands of them, their broad split hooves clicking softly on the slate. Their collective body heat is the architecture’s mortar. The stone dwellings are built directly around and beneath the pens, so the rising warmth of the herds heats the sleeping chambers above. As the animals breathe, great plumes of white steam rise from the alleys, hanging in the cold air and adding their own dampness to the frost-slick walls. 

Above them, every ledge, lintel, and chimney-vent is crowded with heavy, grey-plumed rock-fowl, sitting chest-to-chest like rows of cobbles, muttering low in their throats. In the crevices between the unmortared stones, fat, pale-furred pikas and rock-hyraxes dart in and out in the thousands, harvesting dried moss and chewing on leather harnesses. High overhead, riding the thin, freezing thermals that slide up the endless urban cliff-face, huge vultures with ten-foot wingspans hang entirely motionless, waiting for an old ox to lie down in a courtyard five miles away.

For all this density of life and stone, the landscape is profoundly hushed. 

The thin air does not carry sound well, and the endless labyrinth of rough-hewn slate absorbs noise like a sponge. Moreover, the cold dictates a slow metabolism. Nothing hurries here; to run is to tear the lungs with freezing air and waste precious grease. The herds shift their weight with glacial deliberation. The people move through the steep, narrow corridors at a measured, swaying trudge, swaddled in five layers of greased felt and shaggy hides, their boots wrapped in braided hair that makes no sound on the stone. 

When you stop and listen, the vastness of the city reveals itself not through roar or clatter, but through a wide, quiet hiss. It is the sound of the afternoon cloud-bank scraping through ten million stone teeth; the faint, dry rustle of heavy wool brushing against slate; the occasional *clack* of a horn against a wall; the low, chest-deep murmur of a million nesting birds; and, beneath it all, the patient, rhythmic *tink... tink... tink* of bone-headed chisels splitting another ledge of raw mountain to widen a terrace by three inches before the next winter comes. 

As the sun drops toward the invisible horizon far to the west, the light turns the color of cold brass, striking the endless tiers of grey slate at a flat, blinding angle. Millions of tiny plumes of grey smoke—fueled by dried dung and oil-bush—begin to thread upward from the flat roofs, rising straight and slow into the freezing air until they merge with the golden belly of the evening fog, and the whole vast, breathing cliffside settles into the dark.
