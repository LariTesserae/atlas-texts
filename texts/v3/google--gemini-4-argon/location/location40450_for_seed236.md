The entire settlement sits inside the throat of a dead fumarole, three thousand meters up a young basalt ridge where the rock has not yet had time to soften. 

From the rim, looking down, the caldera is no wider than a town square—perhaps eighty paces across—bounded by sheer, hexagonal columns of dark grey stone that still hold the knife-edge fractures of their cooling. There is no sky visible except the sharp oval directly overhead, which pours down a clear, high-altitude afternoon light, bleached and steady. Yet within this narrow, bone-dry stone chimney, there is a city, a forest, and a crowd, all pressed so closely together that it is impossible to tell where the masonry ends and the rind of the vegetation begins.

Nothing here is wet. There is no rain at this altitude, no groundwater seeping through the fresh volcanic glass, no dew. The air tastes of warm chalk, pulverized pumice, and cured resin. The warmth comes not from the sun, which only strikes the upper tiers for three hours at midday, but from the stone itself—a lingering, low-grade geothermal breath that keeps the pit at the temperature of an animal’s flank.

Because there is no mud for mortar and no timber that isn't too precious to burn, the architecture has been grown and stacked rather than built. The walls are dry-laid slabs of fractured basalt, wedged tightly into the natural steps of the chimney, and then bound together by the deliberate training of *strangle-fig* and *tallow-vine*. Over generations, the builders have coaxed the thick, rubbery roots of these dry-land plants to pour over the stonework like slow amber, filling the crevices, hardening into a wood-like cement that holds the five-story tenements to the cliff face. The buildings have no roofs—there is no weather to keep out—only woven canopies of dried frond-parchment to break the noon glare. Rooms are small, hexagonal or octagonal, mimicking the basalt columns they are pried from, stacked like the cells of a dry paper-wasp nest, linked by hundreds of narrow ledges, root-bridges, and stone stiles.

The lushness of the place is startling because it has nothing to do with water. The flora here runs on sulfur vapor, dust, and a slow, waxy chemistry of oils. Great, bloated rosettes of *slate-agave* jut from every balcony and cornice, their leaves the color of oxidized pewter and coated in a millimeter of white mineral powder that rubs off on the clothes. From the undersides of the viaducts hang curtains of *cinder-moss*—not soft like bog moss, but crisp, dense, and rust-red, drinking the warm volcanic updrafts. Broad-leaved fan palms with trunks like stacked leather washers crowd the narrow courtyards, their leaves so thick with heavy, non-evaporating oils that they feel like warm bacon rind to the touch. There is no bright chlorophyll green; the palette of the foliage is sage, ochre, plum, dusty violet, and the deep, waxy black-green of laurel.

Despite the lack of a single drop of liquid, the place is teeming. 

Every warm basalt ledge, every root-banister, every warm courtyard flagstone is occupied. The primary architects—or perhaps just the dominant tenants—are a species of bipedal, roughly hominid creatures the size of ten-year-old children, covered in a short, velvety pelage the color of wood-ash. They have no metallurgy, no looms, no wheels, no fire. They do not need them. Their fingers end in flat, horn-like spades suited for prying young slate and braiding tough plant fibers. 

Alongside them, sharing the exact same vertical streets and unroofed rooms, are thousands of other dry-country organisms. Tortoises with high, fluted shells that look like stacked clay pots move at a glacial pace down the stepped alleys, browsing on the fallen, oil-rich leaves of the fan palms. Up the sheer masonry walls, hundreds of foot-long, flat-bodied lizards—colored like lichen in patches of mustard and grey—hang motionless for hours, their toes hooked into the microscopic pores of the volcanic rock. In the canopy of the tallow-vines, thick-bodied, flightless beetles the size of walnuts crept along the stems, extruding long, crystalline threads of sugar-wax after feeding on the sap, which the ash-furred hominids harvest with their thumbnails directly from the beetles’ backs.

The density is overwhelming, yet there is no commotion. 

This is a metabolism of extreme conservation. Fast movement wastes precious internal moisture; noise wastes breath. The air is too dry to carry sound well anyway, and the porous volcanic stone and thick, waxy leaves swallow whatever little vibration occurs. A visitor standing on one of the middle-tier bridges, surrounded by four hundred living creatures within a stone’s throw, hears almost nothing. 

There is no chatter. The hominids communicate by shifting the posture of their large, membranous ears—which flush dark maroon or pale cream depending on the blood flow—and by touching wrists as they pass on the narrow ledges. When a mason sets a new basalt flake into a terrace wall, he does not strike it with a hammer; he seats it into a bed of living vine-putty and leans his weight against it for twenty minutes until the plant’s latex oozes and sets. When a tortoise nudges past a sleeping cluster of juveniles in a warm alcove, there is only the dry, papery scrape of its shell against the pumice—a sound like a single page being turned in an empty library. 

Even the wind does not howl here; the sharp rim of the crater above shears the high-altitude gales clean off, leaving the air inside the pit heavy, still, and stratified.

Time moves at the speed of the plants tightening their grip on the rock. In the lower courts, where the geothermal heat is strongest, old walls have been completely swallowed by the gray-barked vines, turned into seamless, bulbous corridors of living wood with small, dark windows where the stone still peeks through. On the upper tiers, nearer the bright oval of the sky, new nurseries are being pried out of the pristine, sharp-edged basalt columns—fresh black rock exposed to the light for the first time since the mountain cooled, already dusted with the yellow spores of dry-lichen, waiting fifty years for the vines to climb up and lock them into the quiet city.
