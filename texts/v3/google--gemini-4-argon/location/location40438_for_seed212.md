There is no soil here, no granite bedrock, no clay. If you were to bore a hole three hundred feet straight down, your drill would only ever bring up bruised pith, snapping xylem, and hot, pressurized sap. The entire basin—some twelve miles from rim to rim—is a continuous, hyper-dense mat of vascular tissue that has forgotten how to be a forest and has instead decided to be a city.

From the low ridge of giant, interlocking root-knuckles that bounds the valley, the view is broad and violently active. The light of the pale, warm sun hits a metropolis of wood, leaf, and rind that is in a state of frantic, perpetual construction. There is no wind, yet the horizon boils. What looks from a distance like a district of terraced apartments—broad, flat shelves of scarred bark stacked ten stories high—will suddenly shudder, split down the middle with a sound like a cannon shot, and telescope upward into a fluted, translucent tower of pale green shoot-flesh inside of three minutes. 

The noise is the first thing that breaks a visitor’s mind. It is a deafening, inescapable roar made entirely of friction and hydraulic stress. Imagine ten thousand ship-timbers being bent to their breaking point simultaneously, layered over the wet, rubbery screech of giant leaves sliding against one another under immense pressure, and punctuated every few seconds by the concussive *thwack-boom* of turgor pressure releasing through blowholes in the ground. The valley is an engine of pure growth, and it is loud enough to vibrate the teeth in your skull.

Walking into the settlement—though nothing has ever walked here, and there are no feet to make use of the thoroughfares—means stepping onto an avenue paved in hexagonal plates of black, iron-hard seed-coat. The street is forty feet wide, cambered like a Roman road to shed moisture into deep, fibrous gutters, and flanked by colonnades of fibrous boles that have fused together to form load-bearing walls. The architecture is unmistakable, obsessive, and entirely devoid of inhabitants. There are porticos made of braided lianas that tighten and slacken like muscle fibers to adjust the shade. There are balconies of stiff, bracket-fungus-like shelf-leaves overlooking courtyards that no eye has ever admired. There are grand, sweeping staircases where each riser is a single, flattened root-buttress, leading up to second-story doorways that open into dark, humid rooms of woven thorns—rooms that have no furniture, no hearths, and no purpose, and which will be crushed and reabsorbed by the neighboring wall before noon.

Because the world is broken here, the vegetation does not merely grow into the shape of a town; it suffers from architectural delusions. The biology is trying to execute urban planning without a concept of what a city is for. 

In the central plaza—a sunken, mile-wide amphitheater of sweating, grey-green tuber-flesh—the plant-mass is caught in a stuttering loop of spatial revision. A bridge of cantilevered hardwood lunges across the plaza, forty feet in the air. Its underside is ribbed like a Gothic vault. Halfway across, the wood stops growing forward and instead replicates the exact same five-foot section of railing forty times in the same physical space, creating a shimmering, hyper-dense knot of timber that hums with a low, sickening vibration and sheds a fine dust of dry lignin. Nearby, a series of gabled roofs made of overlapping, slate-colored palm fronds are sprouting directly out of the pavement at a forty-five-degree angle, pushing up through a preexisting market-stall structure of hollow bamboo, tearing the bamboo apart with slow, agonizing shrieks of splintering fiber. 

There is no rain, and no river runs through the basin. The air is a thick, warm bath, eighty-five degrees and smelling fiercely of crushed tomato vines, hot cedar, and ozone. The only water is metabolic. As the buildings strangle and overwrite one another, they sweat. Great, viscous beads of condensation gather on the underside of the vascular arches; when a tower collapses—not by rotting, since nothing here has time to rot, but by having its sap-supply pirated by an aggressive new boulevard pushing through its foundations—it deflates with a wet, whistling roar, venting gallons of warm, mineral-heavy steam into the narrow alleys. The streets are perpetually slick with this thin, mucilaginous dampness. It collects in the geometric puddles of the plaza, vibrating in standing waves from the acoustic fury of the surrounding growth.

Nothing crawls in that dampness. No beetles bore into the fresh wood; no birds nest in the frantic, shifting eaves of the gable-fronds. If a single ant were dropped onto the hexagonal seed-pavement, it would be crushed within seconds by a shifting curbstone or sealed inside a wall as two adjacent facades decided to merge into a ziggurat. The only things that move independently are the "pedestrians"—tumbleweeds of tightly knotted, rootless nerve-vine that the city coughs up from dry vents in the street corners. These five-foot spheres of pale, twitching twigs roll down the avenues, propelled by the rapid snapping of their own internal tendrils, bouncing off the colonnades, following the rules of traffic down the right-hand side of the roads until they reach the edge of a district and are violently rooted into the ground by grasping ground-ivy to become the foundations for a new tenement block.

The light is bright and clear, pouring down from a featureless sky onto a million shades of chlorophyll—acid greens, deep waxy olives, the pale yellow-white of subterranean flesh suddenly heaved into the sun, the bruised purple of high-pressure sap veins pulsing just beneath translucent bark. Because the canopies are constantly folding and unfolding like massive, rigid origami, the shadows in the streets do not creep; they strobe. A courtyard will be flooded with blinding noon sun, then slammed into pitch darkness as a three-hundred-foot awning of leathery lotus-pad unfurls overhead with the sound of a cracking whip, only to be shredded into ribbons of blinding light ten minutes later as a spear-phalanx of giant reeds punches straight through the canopy toward the sky.

At the very center of the basin, the reality of the place thins out entirely. Here, the plant-city is trying to build a cathedral or a capitol, and the sheer density of the biological ambition has caused the geometry to curdle. Walls of solid mahogany-flesh stand perpendicular to the sky, but their surfaces are pixelated into millions of tiny, square buds that click open and shut in deafening, synchronized waves, mimicking the sound of a crowd roaring. Staircases spiral upward and connect cleanly to their own bottoms thirty feet below. If you look through the rose-windows—great, circular sphincters of woven briar that dilate and contract to let in the warm light—the interior space does not match the exterior volume. Inside the hollow trunk of a thirty-foot kiosk, you can see a vast, sunlit avenue stretching away for miles, lined with weeping, skeletal trees that are shedding leaves made of solid, green-tinted wood, each leaf hitting the ground with the clang of an iron plate. 

And then a tremor runs through the basin floor. A deep, subterranean root-artery shifts its course, redirecting ten thousand tons of hydraulic pressure toward the eastern rim. The rose-window snaps shut, biting through its own thorns with a wet crunch. The cathedral-trunk untwists itself like a wet rag, dropping four stories in ten seconds, and a fresh, pristine row of terraced, lime-green rowhouses erupts from its shoulders, their blind, bark-shuttered windows already rattling in the deafening, breathless heat.
