The valley does not have a single bottom; it has a series of wet, settled shelves that repeat themselves at impossible angles, as if the basin were a geological sentence that kept losing its place and starting over. From the rim—which is not high, merely a slumped lip of gray, porous shale—you can look out across four or five miles of open mire, though the distance feels unstable, foreshortened in patches like a view through old, thick glass.

There are no towers here, no rusted hulls, no concrete pilings or buried cables. Whatever intelligence intervened in this landscape did not build upon it; it edited the rules by which the matter of the valley holds together. The technology is dissolved entirely into the mud, the water, and the cytology of the marsh.

It is perpetually cold, the kind of damp, forty-degree chill that gets into the marrow and stays there, maintained by a low ceiling of bruised, slate-colored cloud that never breaks and never quite drops to the ground as fog. The light is the color of tarnished pewter. It doesn't seem to come from a sun behind the clouds, but rather filters sideways through the wet air, casting long, indistinct shadows that occasionally point in two directions at once.

Water is everywhere, and it is doing too many things at the same time. The valley floor is a dense carpet of peat hummocks, black silt, and broad, braided channels of runoff. In the channels, the water flows with a heavy, viscous lethargy, like chilled oil, yet it is completely clear. When rain falls—which happens every twenty minutes in sudden, localized curtains—some of the drops hit the mire and splash, while others stop six inches above the moss, suspended in dense, shivering lattices of clear beads that hang in the air for an hour before suddenly collapsing all at once with a sound like a dropped sack of wet laundry.

Out in the middle of the flats, where the water collects in broad, shallow meres, the surface does not ripple in concentric circles. It tessellates. The wind pushes across the water, and the surface breaks into millions of tiny, hexagonal steps that click against one another with a dry, chittering roar. 

The noise of the place is relentless. Because the valley is wide and unobstructed, the sound carries for miles, layering over itself until the air feels thick with it. There is the constant, heavy *shhh-clack* of the hexagonal water; the sudden, concussive thuds of suspended rain-columns losing their cohesion and hitting the mud; and beneath it all, a deep, resonant hum that vibrates through the soles of the feet. The hum comes from the geology itself. The low, rounded outcrops of stone that protrude from the bog—worn smooth by millennia of water—are not entirely solid. They are in a state of slow, continuous phase-correction. Every few minutes, a boulder the size of a haywagon will emit a loud, brassy *bong*, like a struck diving bell, and its surface will blur for a second, shedding a fine, wet slurry of powdered silicate as it adjusts its own density to match the atmospheric pressure.

Between these humming stones, the vegetation grows in cramped, suffocating abundance. There are no tall trees—nothing rises higher than a man’s shoulder—but the ground cover is three feet deep and fiercely tangled. The plants are mostly recognizable as marsh flora: broad-leafed skunks-cabbage, woody bog-myrtle, sedges, and thick, rubbery liverworts. However, their biology has been co-opted by the ambient computation of the soil. 

A patch of dark green rushes will grow in a dense, frantic spiral, the blades braiding themselves together so tightly they creak like ship’s rigging, only to untwist in a sudden, whipping motion that throws cold water in every direction. The mosses, which are the color of old bruises and oxidized copper, do not grow continuously over the peat; they grow in discrete, square patches that slide slowly over the slick mud like tectonic plates, colliding with neighboring patches and forcing up little ridges of vegetative matter that immediately die and turn to black slime. If you look closely at the broad, waxy leaves of the marsh-marigolds, the veins do not branch organically. They form microscopic, right-angled circuitries that pulse with a faint, bioluminescent grease—not bright enough to light the gloom, just enough to make the plant look wetly metallic.

The air smells intensely of ozone, crushed fern, and ancient, anaerobic mud. It is so saturated with moisture and airborne particulates—spores, microscopic water droplets, and shed mineral dust from the humming rocks—that breathing feels like drinking very cold, thin broth.

Animal life is scarce, pushed to the margins by the sheer density of the botanical and physical processes taking up all the available space. What does live here has adapted to the broken geometry of the valley. Once in a great while, you might see an eel-like thing, four feet long and the color of wet chalk, swimming not in the streams, but through the suspended lattices of rain that hang above the bog. It moves from one floating droplet-cluster to the next, snapping up the small, heavy beetles that get trapped in the high-tension water surfaces. When the rain-lattice collapses, the creature simply drops into the sedge and slithers back to a canal, unbothered by the fall.

Further out, where the valley floor dips toward a wide, grey river that flows uphill for three hundred yards before correcting itself in a roaring, foamy hydraulic jump, a solitary wading bird might stand in the shallows. It looks something like a heron, tall and gaunt, with feathers coated in waterproof gray powder. It stands utterly still for hours amidst the loud, clicking water. When it strikes at the mud, its neck does not bend; the space between its head and its shoulders simply omits itself for a fraction of a second, the beak appearing instantly buried in the silt, and then re-establishing the intervening neck-vertebrae as it pulls up a writhing, translucent worm.

Nothing here is new. The weirdness has no sharp edges left. The cliffs at the far end of the valley, where the land finally rises into a bank of impenetrable gray mist, have been folding in on themselves for so long that the spatial seams are choked with peat and dead reeds. Waterfalls pour off the lip of the shale, disappear thirty feet down into a horizontal tear in the air, and emerge fifty yards to the left, already at the bottom of the slope, having worn deep, smooth plunge-pools into the rock where no water should be falling from. The moss grows right up to the edge of the spatial tears, its roots dangling into nothingness, drinking the condensation that forms where the air of one part of the valley rubs against the air of another. It is a wild, ancient place, completely naturalized to laws of physics that were rewritten long ago and then left to go to seed.
