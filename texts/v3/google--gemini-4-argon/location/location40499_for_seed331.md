The basin is no larger than a walled orchard, though its boundaries are made of exhausted stone rather than brick. These are the roots of mountains that spent the last four hundred million years dissolving; what remains is a low, continuous rim of grey-white chert and milky quartz, worn down to the smooth, greasy texture of old soap. The depression holds heat like a cupped palm. The air is thick, wet, and bright—a relentless, shadowless glare diffused through a low ceiling of sunlit mist that never quite lifts and never quite turns to rain. 

Everything is slick. Water does not rush here; the land has lost the relief required for a true current. Instead, water seeps, sheets, and beads. It wells up from the warm, porous stone of the basin floor, sliding over flat expanses of pale silt in films an inch deep before disappearing into invisible fissures, only to sweat out of the low stone rim ten yards away. 

Despite the brightness and the greenhouse warmth, the basin feels spacious, almost bare in its architecture. The vegetation does not tangle or crowd. Instead, it grows in discrete, heavily spaced colonies, like sculptures placed in a flooded gallery. Every twenty or thirty feet, a single barrel-thick stem of *Vallis-glass* rises from the sheet-water, terminating in a wide, umbrella-like canopy of translucent green flesh that lets the pale light pass right through it, casting emerald puddles rather than shadows on the silt below. Between these sparse trunks lie wide, open flats of submerged white mud, bridged only by occasional ribbons of dark, rubbery moss that undulate in the slow thermal currents of the water.

Set into the worn stone and the bright mud are the needles. There are perhaps a dozen of them scattered across the basin, tilted at slight, tired angles. They are slender pilings of a material that looks like brushed osmium, though they have no temperature to the touch—neither warm from the glaring sky nor damp from the weeping air. Around each piling, the water and the air suffer a slight, continuous refractive error. When the mist drifts past a needle, the water droplets do not wet the metal; they organize briefly into geometric lattices—tiny, sparkling cubes of suspended moisture—before falling back into the mud as ordinary water. A foot above the ground, the air around the needles hums at a frequency felt entirely in the fillings of the teeth, an ancient infrastructure still executing a maintenance subroutine on a landscape that has long since forgotten what it was being maintained for.

And over the smooth stone, the open mud, the translucent trees, and the phase-shifted needles, there is the fauna. 

It is an overwhelming, silent tide. The sparsity of the plants and the emptiness of the basin floor exist only because the animal life is so thick it polishes the world clean. What looks, at first glance, like a pale, glistening bank of wet clay along the southern rim is, upon approach, a solid, three-foot-deep mattress of ghost-leeches and blind, shell-less gastropods, millions of them sliding over one another in a slow, muscular boil. 

There is almost no sound. The creatures here have no lungs, no chitinous legs to click, no vocal cords. They are hydraulic, gelatinous, and ciliary. 

In the two-inch-deep water flats, pale, ribbon-like flatworms the length of a human forearm move in synchronized, undulating shoals so dense they obscure the silt entirely, turning the basin floor into a shifting, opalescent carpet. They flow around the bases of the *Vallis-glass* trunks like heavy cream poured into a bowl. Above them, suspended in the warm, bright mist, are the drift-mantles—organisms like jellyfish made of spun sugar and wet silk. They pulse through the air in the thousands, propelling themselves by the silent rhythmic expulsion of warm marsh-gas, their trailing filaments brushing the water’s surface to snare the smaller worms.

When a drift-mantle brushes too close to one of the osmium needles, the ancient technology reacts. The air pleats. The creature is not burned or shocked; its cellular structure is simply edited into a cloud of sterile, silvery protein-snow that settles onto the water without a splash. Instantly, the opalescent carpet of worms beneath the needle thickens, boiling upward into a silent, writhing mound to consume the windfall. Within three minutes, the meal is gone, the mound flattens, and the worms resume their cursive, endless grazing.

High on the wide, translucent canopies of the spaced trees, heavy, slug-like predators the color of bruised peaches drop down on threads of clear mucus, plucking drift-mantles out of the thick air and drawing them upward to be dissolved. Every surface that is not actively repelling matter is coated in a living, breathing, digesting film. 

Yet the overwhelming impression is one of stillness and space. Because the terrain is so flat and worn, and the giant, translucent flora so widely spaced, you can see from one end of the small basin to the other without obstruction. The white sky glares down. The mist shines. A million soft bodies slide over a million others with the barely audible *shhh-shhh* of wet silk rubbing on wet stone, a quiet, frantic furnace of life burning silently at the bottom of an old, shallow bowl.
