The world has finished breaking down, and now it is just churning. 

For thousands of miles in every direction, there is no bedrock, no cliffs, no jagged stones—only the *Grist*. Every mountain that ever stood here has been ground down by eons of friction into trillions of tons of warm, heavy, oiled-looking spheres of dark mineral, ranging in size from peppercorns to cannonballs. They are packed together in a continental basin so wide it has its own gravitational tides, a low-lying, dish-shaped expanse where the boundary between ground and sky has been beaten into a warm, wet froth.

There is no empty space here. The air itself is packed to saturation. To stand in the Grist—or rather, to float upon its shifting, semi-buoyant crust—is to exist inside a warm, dimly lit emulsion. The atmosphere is a dense soup of lukewarm mist, suspended silt, and heavy water droplets that never quite finish falling. The light is a bruised, sourceless amber, like the glow inside a dirty aquarium or the last ten minutes before a summer storm breaks, stretching on forever. You cannot see the horizon; you can only see the murk thickening until it becomes opaque a few hundred yards out, though you can *feel* the immensity of the place in the way the pressure waves travel.

The sound is the first thing that dismantles the mind. It is a deafening, planetary roar—the sound of a billion tons of wet, rounded stone continuously rolling over itself. The landscape is in a state of permanent, hyperactive liquefaction. Driven by deep, rhythmic thermal vents and strange, localized gravity swells, the pebble-plains heave and boil like a heavy stew. Hills of slick black stone rise thirty feet into the air, streaming with warm, mineral-heavy water, only to collapse Seconds later with a bone-shaking *shhh-krrrrk* that vibrates straight through the soles of the feet and into the teeth. The noise is so absolute, so packed with frequencies, that it becomes a physical wall. You cannot hear your own voice; you can only feel your throat vibrating against the competing thunder of the churning flats.

And yet, the turbulence is not merely mechanical. The physics of this ancient basin have worn thin, rubbed raw by the endless friction just like the stones. Reality here suffers from shear-stress. 

As the great tides of wet gravel surge and collide, they create eddies where time and space lose their grip. You can watch a trough of foaming, rust-colored water rush between two rising mounds of shingle, and as the wave crests, it doesn't break—it *stutters*. The water hangs in the air, repeating the same half-second of splash five or six times like a skipping record, while the stones beneath it flow backward, uphill, dry and steaming, before the local rules snap back into place with a concussive *pop* that flattens the mist for a mile around. In some hollows, gravity tilts sideways for twenty minutes at a time; sheets of warm rain fall horizontally, slamming into the flanks of shifting gravel dunes, pinning them in place against all logic until the anomaly passes and the whole mass slumps back down into the mire. 

Because there is no room between things—because the mud, the stone, the water, and the heavy air are crammed together into a single, seething medium—these breakdowns are claustrophobic. You might walk forward through knee-deep, warm slurry and find that for ten paces, the air is suddenly solid with the ghost-echo of a granite mountain that stood in that exact spot four hundred million years ago. You can't see the mountain, only the dim, churning swamp, but you have to squeeze sideways through an invisible, crushing density that smells of dry ozone and ancient cold, your skin scraping against the memory of cliff-faces that have long since been ground into the pebbles rolling over your boots.

Life here is sparse, toughened to leather and gristle, and entirely adapted to the churn. 

Every few miles, riding the rolling swells of the stone-mires like buoys in a tempest, are the *Anchor-Groves*. They are not trees, not anymore, though they may have started that way. They are sprawling, low-slung thickets of black, rubbery vines as thick as a man’s thigh, knotted together into massive, self-healing spheres fifty feet across. As the ground heaves and liquefies beneath them, the whole grove rolls with the tide, its thousands of prehensile, root-like tendrils constantly whipping out to grab passing boulders, drinking the mineral-rich water coating their surfaces, and letting go before they are dragged under. Occasionally, one of these rolling thickets gets caught in a reality-stutter; half the plant will age a century in three seconds, blooming with violent, fleshy pale flowers that rot immediately into luminescent slime, while the other half remains dormant, resulting in lopsided, groaning masses of vegetation that leave smeared trails of glowing sap across the black gravel.

Fauna is rarer still. Most of what moves is microscopic, turning the warm standing pools into thick, iridescent broths that coat the rolling stones in a slick, mucilaginous film. When larger things appear, they are heavy, flat, and blind. There are the *Silt-Ploughs*—creatures the size of cattle, shaped like rusted iron teardrops, with no eyes and no limbs, only muscular, undulating bellies armored in overlapping scales of biogenic slate. They swim *through* the packed gravel as easily as water, riding the pressure waves, their wide, baleen-filled mouths straining the wet stone for blind worms and pockets of fermented bog-gas. You rarely see them; you only see the wake they leave—a sudden, violent parting of the stones, a geyser of warm grey mud, and a low, sub-audible thrumming that cuts briefly through the roar of the rolling earth.

Scattered across this vast, hyperactive purgatory are the remains of things that tried to build here—or perhaps things that were built when the world still had bones, now regurgitated by the endless turnover of the earth. 

There is no technology in these ruins, no metal or wire or glass. They are monolithic, primitive, and utterly defeated, yet too dense to be completely erased. As a gravel-wave subsides, it might briefly uncover a forty-foot archway carved from a single piece of milky, translucent jade, smoothed by millions of years of abrasion until its edges are like melted soap. For three minutes, it stands crookedly in the warm drizzle, water sluicing down its flanks, before the ground liquefies again and it sinks back into the grinding dark with a slow, dignified tilt. 

Elsewhere, there are the *Cairns of the Deaf*—stubborn, dome-shaped huts made of the very stones that compose the plain, glued together with a secreted, biological cement that smells faintly of cloves and old blood. Whoever made them is gone, or perhaps they are still inside, sealed up entirely against the noise. There are no doors and no windows. These squat, ugly domes do not sink. Instead, they bob like corks on the surface of the stone-sea, spinning slowly in the eddies, colliding with one another with dull, booming *thuds* that echo for miles through the wet air. Sometimes, when two of these domes smash together in a particularly violent surge, the space between them tears open for a fraction of a second, revealing not a hollow interior, but a slice of a completely different sky—cold, pitch-black, and prickling with hard, silent stars—before the warm mud splashes up and seals the wound.

There is no rest here. The warmth is the warmth of a fever or a compost heap; the wetness is the sweat of a world working itself to death. Everything is touching everything else, rubbing, grinding, dissolving, and reforming under a low, heavy sky that weeps warm water into a sea of rolling stone, roaring at the top of its lungs into an emptiness that is already completely full.
