The entire world here is contained within the flooded caldera of a dead, high-altitude peak, no wider across than a medieval market town. The water is snowmelt-fed, intensely cold, and glass-clear, so pure that the afternoon sun cuts all the way to the bottom, a hundred and fifty feet down, turning the liquid into a single block of pale, greenish-gold amber. 

There is no stone visible. Whatever volcanic rock once formed the walls of this basin was swallowed centuries ago by the *benthic cedar*—an ancient, fully aquatic tree that grows only in high, freezing lakes. Rather than branching outward into dense canopies, the wood grows by fusing with itself, forming sweeping, smooth-barked buttresses, vaulted arches, and hollow pillars as thick as watchtowers. Because the water has been so cold for so long, the wood does not rot; instead, the gentle, ceaseless convection currents of the lake have polished the living bark to the texture of worn river slate. 

The architecture of the town and the biology of the grove are the same thing. A vanished or amphibious people spent generations here with stone adzes, bone wedges, and twisted plant-fiber ropes, coaxing the slow-growing wood into an urban grid. It is a sunken hamlet of hollowed trunks, tiered plazas, and grand, aqueduct-like causeways that span the open water from one massive pillar to the next. Because the trees grow deliberately and without foliage—absorbing nutrients directly through their velvety, olive-green skin—the city has an extraordinary sense of spaciousness. Great, empty volumes of bright water hang between the wide wooden avenues. You can look from a carved balcony on the western rim and see clear across the empty central depth to the terraced apartments on the eastern wall, every peg-joint, lashed railing, and chiseled stairwell sharp and unobstructed in the crisp, cold light.

Nothing moves quickly, yet the place is deafening. 

Water is a ruthless conductor of sound, and the basin acts as an echo chamber for its abundant wildlife. Thousands of plump, barrel-bodied amphibians—creatures the size of hounds with mottled, leathery skin—cling to the sculpted colonnades and sit in the recessed niches of the wooden walls. They do not swim so much as crawl slowly along the carved thoroughfares, and as they sit, they sing. Their vocal sacs inflate like slow bellows, releasing resonant, metallic booming notes—like bronze bells struck with wooden mallets—that overlap and ricochet off the smoothed timber architecture. Beneath their chorus is the relentless, percussive *clack-clack-clack* of millions of fingernail-sized, translucent snails grazing on the microscopic algae that films the courtyards, their hard shells clicking against the iron-dense wood as they inch along.

Every few minutes, a deeper sound rolls through the hamlet: the long, low groan of the city itself shifting. As the afternoon sun warms the upper ten feet of the lake, a faint thermal current presses against the great suspension bridges of braided root-fiber and the hollow, silo-like towers. The ancient mortise-and-tenon joints, cut by hand centuries ago and now half-healed over by the living bark, creak in long, drawn-out sighs that vibrate right through the soles of the feet.

In the open plazas between the buildings, shoals of heavy, silver-scaled char hang nearly motionless, facing into the imperceptible drift of the water. They are so accustomed to the safety of the carved streets that they drift through the open doorways of the sunken houses and out the unglazed windows like lazy silver smoke. Above them, tethered to the high wooden parapets by thick cords of braided kelp, great bladder-fern lanterns—hollow, translucent seed pods the size of wine casks, filled with trapped oxygen—pull taut toward the surface. They do not sway; the water is too still for that. They simply strain upward, holding the catwalks and woven-reed roofs in permanent, patient tension, casting round, magnifying lenses of sunlight down onto the worn timber streets below.
