The city is built across the floor of a high, drowned caldera—though not drowned in any deep sense. The water here is a warm, persistent skin, an inch or two of sheet-flow that creeps over miles of worn, cream-colored travertine, pooled in shallow basins and sliding down low, scalloped terraces that have been smoothing themselves out for ten thousand years. The altitude is high enough that the sky above the rim is a pale, thin, washed-out blue, yet the basin itself is warm, heated from beneath by the slow, dying breath of the mountain and the vast, unhurried industry built into its stone.

Despite being entirely urbanized, the basin feels starkly empty. There is no crowding here. A foundry the size of a cathedral sits on its own broad shelf of weeping rock, and the next structure—a blocky, windowless housing ziggurat or an array of cooling towers—might be half a mile away, separated by wide, gleaming flats of wet stone and sunken causeways. Between them run the aqueducts and the pipe-bridges: heavy, riveted iron conduits five feet in diameter, supported on squat stone piers that have grown thick skirts of mineral deposit over centuries of constant dripping. Everything is spaced out, as if the builders had all the room in the world and a strict geometric mandate to leave the sightlines open. You can stand on a bronze-railed footbridge in the center of the municipal district and see four miles to the western rim, watching the sunlight glint off wet roofs, copper domes, and distant, isolated smokestacks that release thin, lazy threads of white vapor.

The sound hits you long before you understand where it comes from. It is not the frantic clatter of a busy port or the sharp screech of saws; it is a deafening, omnipresent, physical roar—a low-frequency hydraulic thunder combined with the deep, resonant hum of massive turbines buried deep in the limestone beneath the city's feet. Every structure acts as a sounding board. The stone vibrates against the soles of your boots. To walk across a plaza is to walk through a wall of noise that erases speech, a continuous, pressurized *shhhhh-hmmmm* that comes from everywhere at once: from the thousand-foot spillways carved into the caldera walls where warm spring water cascades into intake tunnels, from the colossal bronze relief-valves hissing at the intersections of the wide boulevards, and from the cavernous breath of the subterranean barometric engines. 

Nothing moves quickly inside this roar. That is the strangeness of the place: the sheer, ear-splitting violence of the acoustics paired with the near-total stillness of the view. Great iron balance-beams, forty feet long and slick with condensation, rock back and forth atop stone engine-houses, but they complete a single stroke only once every three or four minutes. Cranes on the distant terraced quarries pivot by fractions of a degree an hour, their counterweights dripping warm rust-water into the pools below. The city’s work is geological in its patience. It is pumping, filtering, precipitating, and pressing minerals out of the earth’s hot blood, and the machinery is geared down to match the tempo of stone formation.

Because the water is rich with dissolved salts and heavy metals, very little grows. Here and there, where a steam-pipe leaks a steady mist of purer condensate, a tough, rubbery mat of rust-colored moss clings to the undersides of the ironwork, or a solitary, stunted woody shrub with thick, waxy grey leaves thrusts out of a crack in a retaining wall. Otherwise, the ground is belonged entirely to the water and the stone. Even the iron is slowly turning to rock; old, decommissioned tracks and the lower flanges of the streetlamps are encased in pale, bubbly crusts of calcified mineral, fusing the industrial grid into the ancient riverbed until it is impossible to tell where the natural terrace ends and the poured-concrete foundation begins.

The air smells of wet chalk, warm copper, and ozone. In the normal, clear light of midday, the whole valley gleams with a dull, greasy luster—millions of square yards of wet masonry, glazed brick, and oiled iron baking gently in the high-altitude sun. 

Living things are as sparse as the greenery. You might walk for an hour along the raised causeways—stepping over the warm puddles that collect in the hollowed-out flagstones—without seeing a soul, though the great engines are clearly tended, their brass fittings freshly greased. The few inhabitants who do appear move at the same deliberate pace as the machinery, wrapped in heavy, oiled-canvas coats to ward off the perpetual spray, communicating with one another in a shorthand of broad, slow arm signals across the wide, roaring plazas. And occasionally, in the warm, shallow run-off channels that line the avenues, you will see the city’s only common wildlife: blind, translucent amphibians the length of a man's forearm, lying motionless on the warm grates of the storm drains, absorbing the heat and the deafening vibration of the world through their bellies, waiting for the water level to rise or fall by a fraction of an inch over the course of the afternoon.
