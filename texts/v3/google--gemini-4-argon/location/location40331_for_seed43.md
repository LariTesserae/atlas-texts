You do not walk through the Ossuary; you squeeze through it. 

It is a subterranean bowl, roughly the size of an amphitheater, dropped forty feet below the level of the upper flats. Yet there is no native rock here. No raw dirt, no bedrock, no limestone seam. If you dig your fingers into the floor, you find only a densely packed aggregate of crushed terracotta, charcoal, knucklebones, and river-silt mortar, laid down and tamped by hand centuries ago. Every square inch of this sunken basin is built. It is a labyrinth of dry-stone slate, sun-baked mudbrick, woven wattle, and heavy timber beams that have mineralized to the hardness of ironwood.

Originally, it may have been a communal kiln-yard, or a cistern complex, or a sunken granary for a vanished culture that worked only with stone adzes, bone awls, and wooden wedges. Now, it is an engine of warm, damp, echoing clutter. 

The space is choked with architecture that has slumped into a permanent, settled equilibrium. Corbelled arches lean against one another like tired shoulders. Low-ceilinged vaults have breached, spilling cascades of thumb-sized clay tally-tokens that now form shifting scree slopes. To cross the thirty yards from one rim of the basin to the other requires threading through narrow alleys no wider than a human hip, ducking under low lintels of split cedar, and stepping over thigh-high retaining walls that divide the space into hundreds of tiny, roofless rooms, alcoves, and shafts. 

Light enters from above, filtered through a chaotic canopy of woven-reed awnings, collapsed split-rail catwalks, and the narrow apertures of the upper masonry. It is a normal, terrestrial daylight, but sliced into a thousand stark geometries—bright, dusty shafts of gold intersecting with warm, ochre-tinted shadows. Where the sun hits the ancient adobe, the clay radiates a steady, baked warmth, holding the ambient temperature in a perpetual, sleepy eighty degrees. 

Despite the warmth, the air has a thick, cellared dampness to it. There is no stream here, no standing pool, yet the masonry sweats. Deep beneath the tamped-sherd floor, unseen thermal waters must pass, because a warm vapor rises perpetually through the terra-cotta flue pipes that honey-comb the walls. This vapor condenses on the cooler slate overhangs, gathering into heavy beads that drop with a rhythmic *plip-plock* onto the dry clay below, leaving dark, coin-sized bruises of moisture that evaporate before they can link into puddles.

The sound of the place is overwhelming. Because the stone and brick are packed so tightly—wall behind wall, niche within niche—the acoustics are bizarrely amplified, turning every small friction into a clatter. 

And there is plenty to make noise. The masonry is alive. 

Thousands of blind, pale-shelled land crabs, no bigger than walnuts, inhabit the mortarless gaps between the slate tiles. As they shift and forage, their carapaces scrape against the dry stone and terracotta, producing a continuous, deafening rustle, like someone endlessly shoveling gravel. Above them, in the warm drafts near the ceiling grates, colonies of muscular, bat-like swifts with leathery wings roost in the thousands. Their high, metallic chittering bounces off the curved mudbrick vaults, mingling with the heavy, booming *thrum-thump* of throat-sacs belonging to mottled, dog-sized geckos that cling to the warm chimney flues. The geckos hunt the crabs; when one strikes, its jaws snap shut like a wooden clapper, followed by the loud, brittle crunch of shell that echoes down a dozen tiny corridors.

The vegetation is sparse, tough, and entirely opportunistic, rooted solely in the dust that has gathered in the grooves of the brickwork. Thick, bloated vines with bark like gray elephant hide snake out of the dry cisterns, climbing toward the light-shafts. They produce no leaves along their lower lengths, only hard, woody knuckles that wedge themselves into the stonework, holding up walls that would otherwise have collapsed ages ago. Here and there, in the exact spots where the condensation drips from the slate lintels, mats of velvety, rust-red moss and small clusters of pale, translucent ferns shiver under the impact of the falling drops. 

Every few minutes, the micro-climate of the pit shifts. As the sun moves across the grates above, a shaft of light will strike a dark, damp chimney flue. The air inside heats rapidly, creating a sudden, localized updraft. You can hear it coming: a low, hollow hooting sound as the wind sucks through the perforated bricks, followed by a miniature gale that sweeps through five or six adjoining alcoves. The draft sets hundreds of suspended clay weights—left behind by the builders on rotting hemp cords—knocking together in a frantic, percussive chorus of *clack-clack-clack*. Loose flakes of mica and dried moss whirl into the air, the geckos flatten themselves against the bricks, and the swifts erupt into a brief, screaming vortex of wings before settling back onto their pegs.

Then the shadow returns, the clay cools, the draft dies, and the heavy, humid heat settles back in, dominated once more by the relentless, grinding rasp of ten thousand shells shifting inside the walls.
