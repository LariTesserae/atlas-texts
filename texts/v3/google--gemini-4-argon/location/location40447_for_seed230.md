The settlement occupies the entirety of a shallow, high-altitude limestone bowl, no wider from rim to rim than a cathedral’s length. Because the notched horizon cuts off the lower world entirely, the place feels less like a mountain top and more like an unroofed courtyard suspended in the cool air. 

Though small, the basin is entirely paved, walled, and terraced. It has the grammar of a dense city—a grid of thoroughfares, distinct districts, public plazas, and monumental retaining walls—executed in miniature and pulled apart so that immense, quiet spaces sit between the structures. Everything is built from the local stone: a pale, close-grained grey rock split and dressed with hand-adzes and stone mauls. There is no mortar. The blocks are fitted with patient, dry-stone precision, their edges softened and rounded by centuries of wind and slow chemical wear until the walls and the bedrock they rise from appear cast from a single piece.

The architecture is low, thick-walled, and sparse. A single two-story granary stands at the northern lip, its facade windowless and slightly battered inward for stability. Forty paces away across a broad pavement of worn flagstones sits a cluster of three rectangular dwellings; fifty paces further, a solitary gatehouse straddles an avenue that leads only to a sheer drop. Between these buildings lie the plazas—flat expanses of swept stone that take up four-fifths of the basin’s floor. The emptiness feels deliberate, as if the builders valued the intervals between walls more than the shelter within them.

Water does not fall here as rain, nor does it rise from springs. Instead, it arrives laterally as high, thin mountain cloud that drifts across the basin in the early hours before noon. To harvest this, the builders erected rows of tall, upright slate slabs across the widest plaza. These monoliths stand ten feet high, spaced wide apart like a stone orchard. As the cool vapor moves through the basin, moisture beads on the cold, grey surfaces of the slate, coalesces into heavy drops, and runs down into shallow, hand-pecked channels grooved into the plaza floor. The channels form a geometric web across the open spaces, guiding the slow trickle of condensation toward three central cisterns capped with heavy stone corbels. By midday, the clouds pass, leaving the channels dark and damp, holding small, still puddles in their worn depressions that slowly evaporate into the thin air.

The light is clear, even, and shadowless, filtered through a permanent, high-altitude haze that turns the sky the color of bleached linen. It neither glares nor dims, holding the grey stonework in a flat, crisp visibility where every chisel mark and lichen patch is distinct from a hundred yards away.

Life exists in the margins of the stonework, entirely at equilibrium with the mineral dominance of the bowl. In the damp joints beside the runnel channels, cushions of dark green moss and stiff, silvery-blue saxifrage grow in tight, neat bun-shapes, never spreading beyond the moisture’s reach. Out of the vertical cracks in the retaining walls grow twisted, ankle-high shrubs with woody stems as thick as a man's wrist and tiny, leathery leaves—plants that take a century to gain an inch of height. 

The air smells of cold wet slate and faint woodsmoke. If there are people here, they move mostly indoors or remain still for long periods; the only tools left in the plazas are heavy stone rollers, bone scrapers laid neatly on ledges, and coarse woven nets stretched between low stone bollards to catch additional dew. 

Occasionally, a grey crown-finch lands on the lip of a cistern, its claws clicking sharply against the rock—a dry, tiny sound that carries all the way from one rim of the basin to the other. High above, a pair of Alpine choughs ride the updrafts along the outer cliff face, appearing over the notched stone parapet, banking silently in the pale sky, and dropping out of sight again. Otherwise, the only sound is the low, steady friction of cool air passing between the distant buildings and the occasional, solitary *plink* of a water drop falling from the bottom edge of a collector slab into the stone groove below. Nothing is falling down; nothing is being built. The stone is settled, the channels are full enough, and the afternoon holds its breath.
