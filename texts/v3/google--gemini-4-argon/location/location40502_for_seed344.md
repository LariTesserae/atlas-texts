The ground has dropped away here, not by the slow carving of a river, but by the sudden collapse of an underground steam vault. The resulting pit is no wider than a city block, a sunken courtyard of shattered basalt surrounded by forty-foot walls of black stone. Because the collapse was recent, there is no soft scree at the bottom, no rounded boulders or soil. The floor of the basin is a jigsaw of immense, flat-topped polygonal blocks that broke along their natural cooling joints, their edges still razor-sharp and fresh. 

Between these tilted slabs, hot water rises. It does not bubble or geyser; it simply wells up from the dark, geometric fissures, filling the lower half of the pit with a network of steaming, mirror-flat pools. The water is clear but carries a faint, oily iridescence and smells faintly of wet iron and hot grease. The air is thick, registering near a hundred degrees Fahrenheit, saturated with moisture that condenses instantly on every cooler surface. The sheer rock walls are slick and black, weeping constant, silent sheets of runoff back into the basin.

Despite the stillness of the water and the heavy, stagnant air, the pit is deafening. 

In the north corner, half-submerged in a triangular pool, sits a single, massive cast-iron turbine housing. It appears to have been lowered here by cranes that have since been dismantled, left behind to tap the thermal pressure beneath the rock. The housing is the size of a shipping container, painted a dull industrial green that is already blistering in the wet heat. There are no catwalks leading to it, no fences, only two thick, braided steel cables that snake up the vertical rock face and disappear over the rim. Inside that iron shell, something is spinning at an immense, unvarying speed. It produces a high, penetrating whine layered over a deep, sub-audible thrumming that vibrates through the soles of the feet and sets tiny, concentric standing waves trembling across the surfaces of the pools.

Aside from the turbine, the human presence is sparse and purely functional. Thirty yards away, bolted directly into a tilted slab of basalt, stands a solitary gray electrical cabinet on stilts, its small indicator light blinking a steady, unhurried amber through the mist. A few rusted iron surveyor’s stakes are driven into the hairline cracks of the stone, their faded orange ribbons limp and heavy with condensation. Nothing else has been built. The space between the turbine, the cabinet, and the walls is an expanse of bare, wet, dark rock.

Life has barely begun to figure out how to use this place, and animals avoid it entirely—the metallic shriek of the turbine and the scalding water keep birds and insects beyond the rim. Only a few opportunistic plants have taken hold. In the narrower crevices where a handful of windblown dust has settled, pale, heat-tolerant ferns uncurl their fronds, trembling slightly in the acoustic vibration. Across the flatter, submerged stones, mats of thermophilic algae grow in slow, concentric rings of rust-orange and vivid sulfur-yellow, looking less like vegetation and more like mineral stains spreading across the basalt. 

Above, the sky is a pale, ordinary daytime blue, bright and clear. Sunlight pours straight down into the depression, illuminating the rising vapor and turning the air into a luminous, shifting haze. The light catches the sharp, unweathered angles of the wet rock, making them glint like obsidian. 

Nothing moves quickly here. The steam drifts upward at a lazy pace, dispersing before it reaches the top of the cliff. The water level rises by an inch over the course of six hours, submerging another millimeter of the orange algae, then takes six hours to recede, leaving a faint, chalky white line of mineral scale on the black stone. The amber light on the cabinet blinks. The iron housing screams its single, tireless note into the wet heat, a vast expenditure of energy happening in an empty room.
