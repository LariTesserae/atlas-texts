The Sump-Cathedral is an enclosed, brick-vaulted chamber buried three hundred feet beneath an abandoned municipal district, though no natural earth or bedrock is visible anywhere within it. The walls, floor, and lost ceiling are entirely composed of engineered materials—glazed kiln-brick, riveted cast-iron plates, and broad poured-concrete pilings—all of which have been fused into a continuous, seamless geology by centuries of heat and chemical deposition. The room is roughly the size of an old railway terminal or a drydock, yet its boundaries are difficult to sense because the space is choked from floor to roof with an immense, tangled lattice of industrial plumbing.

It is profoundly dark. There are no lamps, no skylights, and no shafts to the surface. The only illumination comes from the *telluric gauges*—thick glass cylinders encased in brass cages that are mounted at irregular intervals along the main manifolds. Inside these tubes, a sluggish, phosphorescent oil rises and falls over the course of weeks, casting a bruised, violet-amber glow that reaches perhaps four inches into the murk before dying against the soot-blackened iron. Everything else must be understood by touch, heat, and sound.

The heat is oppressive, stagnant, and greasy. It radiates from the *arterials*—pipes six feet in diameter that angle through the dark like fallen columns, wrapped in decaying blankets of slag-wool and tied with copper wire. What passes through them is never seen, only felt as a heavy, thermal thrum that keeps the air at the temperature of a fever. The air does not blow; it convects in slow, syrupy eddies, heavy with the smell of hot tallow, wet rust, and baked fuller’s earth. 

Because the chamber is cooler at its outer masonry shell than at its congested core, the air sweats. Moisture condenses on the high vaulting and the upper scaffolding, gathering into fat, mineral-saturated droplets that fall through the crowded dark. The sound of the chamber is largely the sound of this distillation: a sparse, rhythmic pattering of warm water striking different thicknesses of metal, trickling down flanged joints, and sizzling softly when a drop lands on an unlagged steam-trap. Now and then, a pressure-relief valve somewhere in the upper tiers will lift with a soft, greasy sigh, breathe out a cloud of sulfurous vapor for ten minutes, and click shut again.

Over the centuries, the relentless heat and the slow drip of alkaline water have eroded the sharp edges of the machinery into a secondary, settled landscape. Iron walkways have rusted through and slumped into the pipes below, only to be cemented in place by curtains of orange ferric flowstone. Great stalagmites of grey lime and copper sulfate rise from the grated floor, engulfing the bases of dormant turbine housings and fusing wrench-wheels to their spindles. The rust here is not flaky; it grows in dense, velvety botryoidal mounds, like black and ochre coral, smoothing the brutal geometry of rivets and hex-bolts into rounded, organic nodules. Beneath the lowest catwalks, in the shallow pans stamped into the iron floor, sit warm, shallow pools of oily water covered in a skin of iridescent scum that shifts like mother-of-pearl when a droplet strikes it.

Life has adapted to the thermal gradients and the total absence of the sun. The sparse vegetation is entirely lithotrophic and thermosynthetic. Along the weeping seams of the brass condensers grow *verdigris-ferns*—stiff, crystalline fronds of pale blue-green that feed on the copper salts leaching from the metal, their leaves brittle as mica. Wrapped tightly around the hottest exhaust flues are the *felt-vines*, flat, leafless ribbons of grey, fibrous tissue that absorb radiant heat directly, growing perhaps an inch a year and feeling precisely like damp flannel to the touch. 

The fauna are few, slow-moving, and blind. The most common is the *gasket-worm*, a flat, leathery annelid the color of red lead that wedges itself into the warm crevices between pipe flanges, extending a feathery, translucent gill-plume to catch the mineral-rich condensation. More rarely, one might hear the soft, metallic scrape of a *clinker-crab* hauling itself up a vertical conduit. Their carapaces are indistinguishable from blistered cast iron, encrusted with lime and actual hex-nuts they have cemented to their backs for armor. They move with agonizing deliberation, grazing on the sulfur-reducing bacterial mats that slick the undersides of the sump-pans.

The chamber is not dead, despite its stillness; it is engaged in a single, glacial cycle. The massive counterweights suspended on chains in the center of the room drop by a fraction of a millimeter each day, driving some vast, unseen governor beneath the floor. Every few years, a counterweight reaches the bottom of its pit, triggers a hydraulic trip-lever, and over the course of a month, a secondary engine wheezes to life, pumping water into a ballast tank to haul the weight back to the ceiling. When this happens, the pitch of the pipes changes by a quarter-tone, the violet oil in the gauges swaps places with the dark, and a warm rain of dislodged condensation falls for three days through the iron forest, before the chamber settles back into its long, simmering quiet.
