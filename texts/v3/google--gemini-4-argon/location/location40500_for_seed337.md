The sky here is not an expanse of air, but a distant, unweathered ceiling of pale grey limestone, pierced by three wide, ragged sinkholes that let down thick columns of ordinary daylight. Below this roof lies an open, subterranean basin two miles wide, dropped fifty feet beneath the surrounding plains. 

There is nothing worn or ancient about the rock. The basin is the result of a recent tectonic drop—a clean, jagged subsidence that happened fast enough to leave the geology raw. Every stone face is fresh-fractured. There is no soil, no river-rounded gravel, no sand smoothed by wind. The edges of the native rock are still razor-sharp, dusted only with the white, chalky powder of their own recent breaking. 

And yet, there is not an inch of empty floor left in the basin. 

From rim to rim, the depression is packed with a dense, interlocking honeycomb of unmortared stone rooms, sunken courtyards, retaining walls, and stepped terraces. It is an entire town built directly out of the rubble of the collapse, and it is still aggressively under construction. The settlement is a study in grey-on-grey: millions of flat, freshly split slabs of shale and tabular limestone stacked tightly by hand, wedged together with smaller shims of flint. Where a natural boulder proved too large to break, walls simply wrap over and around it, absorbing the raw geology into the architecture until it is impossible to tell where the fault-scarp ends and the masonry begins. Narrow alleys, barely shoulder-width, zig-zag between chest-high and two-story structures, the stone edges of the walls still sharp enough to catch clothing.

Despite the immense scale of the stonework, the basin is remarkably quiet. The air is cool—the steady, cellar-like chill of the shallow underground—and it absorbs sound rather than throwing it back. Because thebuilders use only horn wedges, wooden levers, and rawhide-wrapped stone mauls, there is no ringing of iron. Instead, the soundscape is a vast, low-volume patter: the dry *clack-clack* of stone shims being tapped into place, the dull thud of heavy slabs settling, and the constant, soft rasp of sandals shuffling across unpolished rock. Thousands of processes are happening at once, yet the acoustic effect is no louder than the shifting of shingle on a calm beach.

Above the roofs, a secondary architecture of wooden poles, hemp ropes, and stretched linen sails catches the eye. The basin has its own active weather cycle. When the cool air trapped in the packed stone streets meets the sun-warmed currents drifting down through the wide ceiling breaches, it condenses. Every few hours, a thick, pale mist rolls out from the deep shadows at the edges of the cavern and drifts across the town. 

The inhabitants have rigged thousands of angled linen sheets and broad, shallow stone troughs across the rooftops to harvest this moving damp. As the mist passes through the densely packed maze, the fabric darkens with moisture, and water begins to bead and run. It trickles down waxed twine into clay cisterns, or runs along hand-pecked grooves in the top-stones of the walls. For an hour after a fog-pass, the whole city gleams like wet slate, and the air fills with the quiet, rhythmic *plip-drop* of water falling into narrow-necked jars. Then the air clears, the shafts of daylight shift across the terraces, and the stone dries back to chalky bone-grey.

Life in this stone honeycomb is sparse and specialized, fitted into the tight margins of the masonry. There are no trees or grassy fields—only deliberate pockets of green where dampness collects. In the narrow drip-lines beneath the water-sheets, pale green moss and flat, ruffled lichens cling to the rough limestone. Here and there, in tiny, three-feet-wide courtyards where greywater is poured over crushed shale, tough, waxy-leaved tubers and creeping squash vines are coaxed into growing, their roots prying into the dry-stone crevices. 

Animals are rarely seen, though their presence is felt in the small movements at the edge of vision. There are no beasts of burden; the passages are too tight and the stone too sharp for hooves. Instead, small, slate-colored rock wrens flit silently from roof-pole to roof-pole, hunting the pale crickets and woodlice that thrive in the damp cracks between the unmortared stones. Occasionally, a long, rust-colored weasel will slip like liquid out of a wall drain, cross a sunlit terrace in two bounds, and vanish into the masonry of the next house over. High up, where the jagged lips of the sinkholes meet the surface world, a few cliff-swallows cut across the patches of blue sky, their sharp cries dropping into the cool basin and fading before they ever reach the crowded, shifting stone floor below.
