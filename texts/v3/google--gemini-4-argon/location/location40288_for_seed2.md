There is no geological stone here, no sand, and no sky, though looking upward gives the exact impression of a bright, overcast noon. The ceiling is a continuous, submerged grid of high-intensity carbon-arc floodlights sealed behind three-foot-thick panes of armored glass, suspended two hundred feet below the true surface of a cold, unseen sea. 

Below that dazzling canopy lies the Sump: a subterranean, concrete basin of continental proportions, flooded entirely with frigid, meticulously filtered water. 

Because the water is pumped through titanic intake screens miles away, it holds almost no silt. Visibility extends for miles in every direction, fading not into darkness, but into a pale, luminous cerulean haze. Looking down from the mid-water, the floor of the basin is visible three thousand feet below—a flat, seamless plain of poured aggregate concrete, divided into mile-square sectors by expansion joints as wide as canals. In the center of each square, stenciled in faded yellow paint with letters eighty feet tall, are sector designations: *B-04, B-05, B-06*, marching off into the blue infinity.

For all its total fabrication, the space is overwhelmingly empty. It is a box of water the size of an inland sea, designed to hold volume rather than machinery. The infrastructure that does exist is spaced leagues apart, rising from the concrete floor to the lighting grid like the pillars of a drowned cathedral. There are the Thermal Columns—cylindrical towers of riveted boiler-plate steel, fifty yards wide, carrying hot exhaust from deep-earth foundries up toward the surface. There are the Tension Cables—braided steel wires the thickness of ancient redwoods, descending at forty-five-degree angles to anchor points the size of city blocks. 

Everything looks newly minted. The coldness of the water—hovering just above freezing—and the constant, chemical scrubbing of the currents have retarded rust and rot. The rivets on the columns are sharp-edged; the bevels on the concrete expansion joints are crisp and unchipped; the galvanized steel of the maintenance catwalks gleams with a dull, zinc luster as if submerged only yesterday. The only concessions to age are thin, weeping streaks of bright orange iron oxide trailing down from a few poorly sealed bolt-heads, and a papery, translucent crust of lime scale building up around the warmer vents.

The place is deafening. Water is a ruthless conductor of sound, and the Sump acts as an echo chamber for distant, colossal engines. There is no ambient silence; instead, a continuous, rhythmic *thrum-clack, thrum-clack* vibrates directly through the ribs and teeth of anything swimming here—the sound of forty-foot intake valves opening and slamming shut fifty miles to the north. Overlaid on this is the perpetual, high-pressure hiss of the oxygenation manifolds, which release curtains of tiny bubbles along the sector lines, and the occasional, stomach-dropping *booooong* of a five-mile tension cable shifting in its socket, a sound like a plucked bass string the size of a skyscraper.

Artificial weather sweeps through this drowned void. When the northern sluices open to flush the basin, currents the width of the Amazon tear through the open water at thirty knots. Where this near-freezing inflow meets the warm water radiating off the Thermal Columns, massive hydro-thermals develop. The water shimmers and blurs like desert air as hot and cold layers shear against one another, creating invisible, violent eddies and vertical downdrafts that can drag a swimmer a thousand feet down in a minute. 

Life has colonized this immaculate industrial void, adapting to the vast distances and the brutal hydrodynamics.

Vegetation is scarce, given the lack of soil and the sheer force of the flushing currents. It exists only where it can weld itself to the architecture. Along the tension cables and the leeward sides of the concrete seams grows "wire-kelp"—tough, rubbery fronds of dark olive algae that anchor to bare steel with secretions of natural cement. They do not sway gently; they whip and snap like flags in a gale, streamlined and leathery, feeding on the nitrates dissolved in the water and the blazing, twenty-four-hour glare of the overhead arc lights. Around the base of the warm Thermal Columns, pale green turf-mosses form thin, patchy rings, scraping a living from the minerals flaking off the hot metal.

Because there is nowhere to hide in the bright, open water, the fauna relies on speed, schooling, or proximity to the few massive structures. Great, shimmering clouds of silver-sided smelt—tens of millions of them—hang in the mid-water, miles from the nearest pillar. They move as a single, nervous muscle, contracting and flashing white every time a valve-shockwave thuds through the water. They feed on the microscopic crustacea that breed near the lighting grid, where the glass panes are warm to the touch.

Patrolling the empty leagues between the pillars are long, spindle-shaped predators—cousins to the barracuda and the mackerel, evolved here into creatures of pure velocity, with mirror-scales that render them nearly invisible in the bright blue glare. They use the hydro-thermals like hawks use wind currents, riding the warm updrafts near the boiler-towers before diving into the silver bait-balls.

Down on the concrete plains, three thousand feet below the lights, the illumination dims to the color of a winter twilight. Here, spider-crabs with leg-spans of six feet march in slow, mechanical herds across the aggregate, picking at the lime-scale and the dead smelt that drift down from above. When the great sluice-currents begin to roar, the crabs do not scatter; they walk in single file to the nearest expansion joint, hook their front claws into the crisp concrete lip, and fold themselves flat, waiting out the artificial storm while the water howls over their shells and the great overhead lights burn on, indifferent and white, through the miles of cold blue water.
