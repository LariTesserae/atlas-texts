At fourteen thousand feet, the sky and the lake do not cleanly separate. They meet in a violent, silver-white emulsion of wind-whipped chop, horizontal sleet, and hanging cloud. This is the High Basin, an immense, bowl-shaped plateau perched on the roof of the continent, so ancient that its once-jagged peaks have been ground down by eons of ice and water into colossal, drowned whalebacks of dark grey gneiss. 

The basin is almost entirely submerged. It is a highland sea, cold and turbulent, where the water is only a few feet deep over the broad stone flats, but plunges into black, bottomless glacial trenches between them. Because of the extreme altitude, the air is thin and biting, carrying the constant, metallic tang of snowmelt and wet stone. The light here is the piercing, high-UV glare of the upper atmosphere, diffused through a fast-moving ceiling of cloud—a bright, shadowless, pewter luminescence that makes the water look like hammered lead.

There are no trees at this elevation, yet the basin is choked with life. Beneath the thrashing surface of the water lies a dense, submerged jungle of cold-water macro-algae, peat-mosses, and tough, vascular water-weeds. Where the ancient rock rises just inches above the waterline, it is immediately smothered in thick, spongy cushions of *llareta* and alpine bog-grass, forming vibrant green and rust-colored archipelagos that stretch for miles. Beneath the waves, forests of undulating olive-brown fronds grow twenty feet tall, rooted in the drowned valleys, their tips constantly sheared and whipped by the surface currents. The water is so packed with this trailing vegetation that waves do not break cleanly; they roll in heavy, sluggish surges, tangled with brown and emerald weed.

The sound is a relentless, percussive roar. The high-altitude gales never truly stop; they drop down from the surrounding smooth-worn ridges and scour the basin, driving sheets of rain and spindly hailstones across the water. The wind howls through the hollows of the rock, the waves slap rhythmically against the flooded peat-banks, and the sky is filled with the raucous, echoing cries of thousands of birds. 

Life here is hyperactive, compressed into a frenzy of feeding and movement by the cold and the thin air. Enormous flocks of slate-grey mountain gulls and heavy-bodied diving ducks wheel through the mist, plunging into the weed-choked trenches to pull up glistening, armored mud-fish and pink, cold-water crustaceans. On the half-submerged stone domes, herds of barrel-chested, semi-aquatic camelids—covered in thick, oily wool that sheds the freezing spray—wade chest-deep through the surging channels, tearing up mouthfuls of submerged kelp. They move constantly to stay warm, splashing and bleating against the wind, shadowed by sleek, otter-like predators that slip effortlessly through the underwater thickets.

Scattered miles apart across this vast, tossing expanse are the small, stubborn footprints of industry. 

Where the ancient rock forms stable shoulders above the chop, solitary structures of riveted iron and timber cling to the stone, anchored against the gales by thick steel cables drilled directly into the bedrock. These are automated pumping stations and hydroelectric bleed-valves, tapping the immense kinetic energy of the basin’s drainage. They are utilitarian, rusted orange by the constant wet, and slick with green algae. From their corrugated roofs, porcelain insulators hum with high-voltage current, carrying power along sagging, wind-strung cables that march across the water on tall, skeletal pylons before vanishing into the fog. 

Inside these small, unmanned shacks, heavy flywheels spin and brass-fitted pistons hammer away, their rhythmic, mechanical clanking swallowed fifty feet away by the roar of the wind and waves. Around their flooded foundations, old industrial detritus—a half-sunken barge of rotting pine, a coil of frayed wire rope, a cast-iron gear casing—has been completely colonized by the water-moss and freshwater barnacles. Small, silver-sided fish dart through the spokes of submerged iron wheels. 

Nothing here is still. The clouds tear themselves apart on the worn-down mountain rims; the water shifts from dark slate to blinding silver as sunbeams lance through the storm; the underwater forests sway in a violent, silent dance; and the great flocks of birds rise and fall like gusts of black smoke over a drowned and restless world.
