The space is no larger than a village square, a high-altitude basin perched on the shoulder of a mountain that hasn't finished arriving. There is no sky here in the traditional sense, only a low, warm ceiling of pearl-gray cloud that drags across the jagged rim of the hollow, keeping the light to a perpetual, shadowless twilight. The air is thick, balmy, and smells sharply of ozone and wet slate, like the inside of a kiln just after the steam has been vented.

Nothing here is worn down. There is no soil, no sand, no silt—not a single rounded pebble. The floor of the basin is a series of broad, flat steps made of dark, vitreous stone that is actively crystallizing out of the mist. As you watch, a seam in the rock will give a sharp, glassy *crack*, and a new shelf of basaltic crystal will push upward by an inch, its edges razor-sharp and hot to the touch. Because there has been no time for weather to soften the stone, every surface is geometric, pristine, and treacherous.

Water is everywhere, though there are no rivers. The warm air is supersaturated, and the newly formed stone is somehow cooler than the atmosphere—or perhaps it exerts some localized, engineered draw on the moisture. Water condenses continuously on the fresh rock faces, running in silver sheets down the hexagonal steps and pooling in shallow, perfectly square basins. From the mist above, spheres of water the size of grapefruits occasionally coalesce, hanging suspended in the low-gravity pockets created by the basin's machinery before dropping with a heavy, echoing *slap* onto the stone.

The machinery is sparse, easy to miss at first because it does not look like metal. Spaced forty or fifty paces apart are slender, waist-high pylons that seem to be grown from the same dark glass as the ground, except they are completely dry and hum with a low, throat-vibrating bass note. They are lithic looms—left here by whatever intelligence is compiling this mountain range. Occasionally, a hair-thin filament of violet light snaps between two pylons, and along that line, the mist instantly solidifies into a new ridge of raw, steaming rock. Near the center of the hollow stands a single, larger structure: an open-sided pavilion made of three intersecting arcs of a bone-white material that refuses to get wet. Inside the pavilion, nothing touches the ground; a cluster of polyhedral instruments floats at chest height, rotating slowly, clicking like a room full of metronomes as they measure the tension of the growing stone.

The noise is relentless. The acoustic walls of the mist and the stepped rock trap every sound. There is the constant, hissing rush of condensation sliding over fresh stone, the sharp *ping* and *shatter* of rapid geological expansion, and the deep, rhythmic thrum of the pylons. 

Layered over this mechanical and geological racket is the din of the basin’s wildlife. Despite the lack of soil, an opportunistic ecology has moved in before the floor is even finished. Vegetation is rare, limited to the vertical joints between the newest rock shelves, where pale, translucent ferns uncoil with visible speed, feeding on the heat of the stone's formation rather than the dim light. There are also patches of a black, metallic moss that grows in perfect concentric circles around the bases of the pylons, drinking the stray electromagnetic bleed.

The fauna, however, is crowded into this warm, wet pocket, drawn by the heat in the cold high altitudes. Because the ground is a maze of razor-sharp steps and shallow water, almost everything moves on stilts or by hopping. Hundreds of spindle-legged amphibians, their skin as reflective as mercury, skate across the wet glass terraces, their vocal sacs inflating to produce a loud, metallic *clank-clank-clank* that competes with the clicking instruments in the pavilion. 

Higher up, clinging to the weeping vertical faces of the basin's rim, are colonies of heavy-bodied, shell-less snails the size of loaves of bread. They graze on the rapid-growing ferns and the mineral film on the rocks. They move fast—visibly gliding—and behind them, their acidic slime trails leave the very first marks of erosion on the pristine stone, etching shallow, frosted grooves into the dark glass. 

Hunting them all are the basin’s apex predators: creatures that look like three-foot-long ribbons of wet red silk, which undulate through the thick air by manipulating the same localized gravity fields the pylons use. They drift silently between the sparse monuments, casting no shadows in the diffused gray light, waiting for the loud *crack* of a new rock shelf to startle a flock of mercury-frogs into leaping upward into the mist.

To stand here is to feel crowded by motion in an mostly empty room. The space between the pylons is wide and bare, just wet black terraces and gray vapor, yet the ground under your boots is incrementally rising, the water is pooling and draining as the geometry shifts, and the air is thick with the clatter of amphibians and the smell of a world being printed out of the fog.
