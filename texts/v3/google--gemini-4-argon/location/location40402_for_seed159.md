The floor of the Caldera-Trench is never the same twice because the rock is still arriving. 

You are three hundred feet below the surrounding continental shelf, standing in an open, mile-wide basin where the earth’s crust is thin and restless. Above, there is no ceiling, only a high, glaring sky trapped between the sheer, unweathered basalt walls that drop down from the upper world. The sunlight pours into this sunken trough and stays there, baking the rising mineral wealth into a persistent, humid warmth. 

Nothing here is worn down. There is no sand, no rounded river stone, no silt. Everything is sharp, crystalline, or freshly congealed. Across the wide valley floor, thousands of miniature geysers, mud-pots, and mineral vents burp and hiss, constantly laying down new coats of travertine, sulfur-yellow crust, and brittle black glass. Terraces of bone-white aragonite build themselves up an inch an hour, only to fracture and collapse under their own weight by afternoon, sending cascades of porcelain-like shards skittering into the steaming shallow pools below. The ground is an emerging architecture—sharp-edged steps, blistering domes, and raw fissures that smell of wet iron and hot chalk.

There isn’t a single blade of grass or smear of green algae in the entire expanse. The mineral violence is too fast for roots. Instead, where the warm condensation settles in the lee of newly risen rock-spires, thick, trembling mats of orange and violet chemo-synthetic slime drink the sulfur gas, and pale, bloated puffballs the size of fists erupt from the cooling cracks, maturing and popping into clouds of grey spores within twenty minutes before the next wave of mineral-heavy splash-water petrifies their remains.

Yet the basin is choked with animal life. It is a deafening, boiling metropolis of chitin, shell, and wing. 

Because the geology moves too fast for nests or burrows, the fauna has adapted to a state of permanent, frantic migration across the valley floor. Millions of fist-sized, six-legged crabs with translucent, heat-resistant carapaces clatter over the fresh travertine shelves, scraping the booming bacterial mats into their mouths with stone-hard mandibles. When a vent shifts and belches boiling brine, a ten-acre carpet of these crustaceans surges sideways all at once, their shells clicking together like a landslide of dry bones. 

Above them, the air is thick to the point of obscurity with heavy-bodied, leathery flyers—creatures halfway between locusts and pterosaurs, with four wings that beat with a dry, snapping *thwack*. They ride the violent thermal updrafts that roar out of the ground, feeding on the rising spore-clouds and on each other. Every newly formed basalt spire is instantly colonized by writhing, territorial heaps of long-necked, armored lizards that bask in the geothermal heat, hissing and snapping as the rock beneath them visibly inflates and cracks. When a dome bursts, releasing a cloud of warm steam and a spill of lukewarm mineral slush, the lizards leap into the air, gliding on ribbed membranes to the next rising pillar before the old one has even finished collapsing.

The sound is relentless: a high-volume symphony of hissing steam, the sharp *crack-ping* of cooling volcanic glass, the continuous maraca-rattle of millions of scuttling legs, and the shrill, whistling cries of the thermal-riders. 

Scattered through this hyperactive geological nursery are the works of hands—or claws—that understand tools. They are never permanent, and they are never occupied for long. Perched on the higher, slightly older shelves of slate and crusted salt are rough cairns of stacked slag, bound together with sinew and cured fungal-gum. Stretched between them are vast, tattered nets woven from braided gut and weighted with chipped obsidian sinkers, positioned across the thermal updrafts to snare the fat flyers by the hundreds. 

Here and there, someone has jammed hollowed-out femur bones and fire-hardened wooden tubes directly into the smaller steam vents, rigging primitive whistles and condensers. The whistles shriek a single, ragged note across the valley until the mineral deposits clog them solid, marking safe paths or claiming territory, while the condensers drip fresh, distilled water into waiting bowls of hammered native copper. You can find butcher-sites that are only hours old: a flat slab of still-warm basalt covered in cracked carapaces, a few abandoned flint scrapers left behind when the ground began to tremble, and three-pronged wooden tripods charring slowly as a new fissure opens beneath one leg, tilting the whole structure into a rising puddle of opalescent mud. 

To cross the valley is to walk through a warm, bright, deafening storm of matter. The soles of your boots crunch on porcelain-thin stone that didn't exist at dawn; warm condensation drips from your chin while the sun burns the back of your neck; heavy, armored bodies constantly brush against your shins in their rush toward the newest cooling vent; and fifty yards away, a stone chimney you were using as a landmark shudders, splits down the middle with a sound like a cannon shot, and exhales a fresh, glistening terraced garden of pink salt into the glaring light.
