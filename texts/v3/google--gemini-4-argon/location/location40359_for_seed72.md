The room is four hundred feet long, ninety feet wide, and eighty feet from the floor to the crown of the vault, though the crown is rarely visible through the vapor. It is an uncompleted junction chamber for a municipal deep-transit system, blasted out of the city’s underlying basalt two hundred feet below the street grid and then abandoned when the geothermal gradient proved too steep for standard ventilation. 

The rock is raw. Because the excavation was halted mid-process, the walls have not been smoothed or lined with precast segments; they retain the jagged, hexagonal fracturing of fresh basalt, still sharp-edged and unweathered. Every ten feet, the parallel, half-cylindrical scars of pneumatic drill-bores score the stone. A first pass of gray shotcrete was sprayed over the lower thirty feet of the north wall before the crews pulled out, and it hangs there like dried mud, frozen in the act of slumping, with the square iron plates of rock-bolts protruding through it at five-foot intervals. 

Between the walls, the floor is mostly bare, leveled stone, vast and empty. The urban infrastructure here was built for high volume, which leaves the space feeling starkly under-furnished now that it is disconnected from the grid. Two pairs of heavy steel rails run the length of the hall, terminating abruptly against a sheer, unblasted rock face at the eastern end. Every fifty feet, a pair of cast-iron columns—fluted, industrial, bolted into massive concrete plinths—rises to support an overhead steel gantry that carries nothing. At the western end, a single thirty-ton boring shield sits rusting in its cradle, its cutterhead wedged against the stone, fifty yards from the nearest column. Between these few massive fixtures, there is only flat, dark rock and shallow, lukewarm puddles that never quite join into a stream.

The heat comes out of the rock itself. It is a heavy, radiant warmth, hovering just above a hundred degrees Fahrenheit, fed by a magmatic intrusion a mile deeper down. Because the chamber intersects the lower water table, water is constantly forced through the micro-fissures in the basalt. It does not gush; it sweats. The walls are dark and slick with it. The air is so saturated that moisture condenses on the cooler iron of the columns and the underside of the gantry, gathering into heavy drops that fall with a slow, irregular *spit-spit* onto the stone below. The puddles on the floor are coated with a thin, iridescent skin of iron-oxidizing bacteria and machine oil, no more than an inch deep, warming like bathwater.

Light is scarce and orange. Along the northern wall, a single string of heavy-duty work lamps—low-pressure sodium bulbs inside thick glass bell-jars and wire cages—still draws power from a forgotten auxiliary substation up-shaft. Seven of the bulbs along the four-hundred-foot span are still burning. They cast a dim, monochromatic amber glow that reaches out thirty feet into the mist and dies, leaving the center of the floor and the high vault in deep shadow. Directly beneath these seven lamps, and nowhere else, grows the chamber’s only vegetation: thin, palm-sized mats of pale green moss and a few stunted, translucent ferns clinging to the damp shotcrete, surviving on the narrow spectrum of the sodium light.

The emptiness of the floor and the sparseness of the machinery only make the biological density on the upper surfaces more staggering. The chamber has become a thermal rookery. 

Every square inch of the upper basalt walls, the wide flanges of the overhead gantry, and the undersides of the iron ceiling arches is packed three-deep with small, free-tailed bats, drawn down through the vertical ventilation shafts by the reliable, tropical heat. There are hundreds of thousands of them. From twenty feet up all the way to the unseen roof, the rock is not rock, but a continuous, trembling pelt of dark brown fur and folded leathery wings. They do not fly much inside the chamber—the open air between the floor and the gantry remains largely clear—Wait, no: they shift. Like a slow-moving viscous fluid, the mass on the walls is constantly rearranging itself as individuals crawl over one another to regulate their temperature, creating slow, dark ripples across the stone.

Beneath them, on the lower walls and across the wide, flat expanses of the floor, is the second layer of life. A pale, heat-loving species of cave cricket and millions of three-inch millipedes blanket the stone, feeding on the steady, snow-like drift of guano and chitin that filters down from the ceiling. Where the amber light hits the floor, the stone appears to be breathing; the crickets sit shoulder to shoulder in vast, stationary carpets, their long antennae waving in a slow, unified rhythm in the wet air. The rusted flank of the boring machine is entirely invisible beneath a shiny, overlapping crust of dark beetles that move so slowly they look like blistered paint. 

The sound of the place is immense, though nothing in it moves quickly. Beneath everything is the deep, rhythmic, sixty-cycle hum of the transformer behind the east bulkhead, vibrating through the soles of the feet. Over that is the hollow, metallic roar of a distant water main—a six-foot iron pipe emerging from the rock near the ceiling and vanishing into the floor at the far end, carrying a river of municipal water under high pressure. And filling the entire middle register, amplified and doubled by the hard, wet acoustics of the basalt vault, is the sound of the colony: a continuous, high-volume static made of millions of tiny claws clicking against raw stone, the dry rustle of shifting wings, and a shrill, overlapping chorus of chittering that blurs into a single, unbroken sheet of white noise, like heavy rain on a tin roof.

Nothing here happens quickly. The air barely moves, rising in a lazy convective loop from the hot floor to the slightly cooler vault and sliding back down the weeping walls. Beneath the center of the gantry, a long, low ridge of guano has accumulated over the decades, slowly burying the steel rails; its surface is glazed with white fungal threads and crawls with pale larvae, rising by half an inch a year. At the base of the weeping walls, where the mineral-heavy groundwater meets the hot air, a paper-thin crust of white travertine is beginning to form over the raw drill-scars, sealing the unfinished room into its own geology, millimeter by millimeter, under the steady, orange watch of the work lights.
