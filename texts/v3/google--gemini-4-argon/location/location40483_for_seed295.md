Underfoot, the ground gives slightly, then holds with the dull, fibrous stubbornness of petrified leather. There is no rock here, no sand or clay, though the ridge rises six thousand feet above the surrounding plains. What forms the saddle of this pass—and the stepped terraces dropping away into the gloom on either side, and the thick, braided buttresses that hold up the slopes—is an ancient amalgam of chewed timber, red clay carried up from the lowlands three millennia ago, crushed beetle chitin, and dried saliva. It has the density of brick and the matte, light-absorbing quality of old soot. 

Everything here was put here. Nothing was quarried; nothing was forged. The entire valley—three miles across from horn to horn—is a biological edifice, an accumulation of huts, baffles, galleries, and retaining walls built by non-human mouths and claws over ten thousand generations, until the original granite peak was entirely entombed beneath five hundred feet of hardened mastic and dried mud.

It is late dusk, or what passes for it beneath a permanent, high-altitude cap of dark stratus clouds that drag their bellies across the upper spires. The light is a bruised, low-wattage indigo. You can see the silhouettes of the great chimney-mounds against the slightly paler sky, and you can see the sheen of condensation on the broad, waxy leaves five inches from your face, but twenty yards away, the terraces dissolve into a velvety, humming blackness.

Despite the altitude, the air is thick and warm, like the inside of a fermenting barn. The heat rises from the ground itself—a slow, steady geothermal exhalation produced by millions of tons of composting leaf-litter packed into the deep fungal vaults below the surface, compounded by the metabolic heat of the inhabitants. Because the outside mountain air is cool, the warmth escaping the vents and porous walls creates a perpetual sweat. There is no rain right now, and no running stream, yet every surface is slick. Beads of moisture gather on the undersides of the mud-arches, cling to the hairy stems of the creepers, and collect in the shallow, polished basins worn into the walkways.

The sound is total. It does not come from a single direction; it is atmospheric pressure converted into noise. 

There is a baseline roar—a dry, papyrus-like rustling created by tens of millions of tarsal claws scraping across dried mud and bark. Over this rises the stridulation: a saw-toothed, metallic ringing of katydids and heavy-bodied mountain crickets that vibrates in the teeth and behind the eyes. Deeper in the masonry, amplified by the hollow acoustic chambers of the mounds, millions of blind wood-borers and soldier termites hammer their heavy heads against the cured clay ceilings in slow, rolling waves of percussive warning that travel from one end of the valley to the other, taking four minutes to cross the dark. In the canopy of broad-leafed figs and climbing pandanus that choke the courtyards between the mounds, night-jars, tree-frogs, and small, wet-nosed marsupials scream short, sharp territorial claims just to pierce the insect wall. You cannot hear your own footsteps. If you shout, the sound is eaten two feet from your mouth by the sheer density of the ambient chorus.

Yet for all this frantic acoustic energy, the visual landscape moves at the pace of cooling wax. 

A single great archway—forty feet high, shaped like the parabolic ribcage of a whale, constructed entirely of mud-daubed sticks laid down by communal weaver-birds centuries ago—spans a sunken plaza. Its surface is deeply eroded, smoothed by centuries of acidic mist and the oily bellies of passing lizards into soft, undulating flutes, like melted candle tallow. Out of its mortar grow thick, pale-stemmed begonias and mats of liverwort. The plants do not sway; there is no wind down in the baffled corridors of the city-nest. A velvet worm, six inches long and the color of dried plum, takes twenty minutes to cross the damp lip of a cistern. Above it, a cluster of bats hangs like black fruit, shifting their thumbs by fractions of an inch, their wet membranes gleaming faintly in the near-dark.

To walk through the valley is to navigate a sponge of dry-mud architecture overgrown with a jungle that has adapted to rooting in dried spit and guano. The paths are not for bipeds; they are wide, rounded troughs, polished to a greasy shine by the passage of porcupines, giant civets, and flightless ground-parrots. When you place a hand on a retaining wall to steady yourself, the wall is warm—about ninety degrees—and vibrates faintly, like the throat of a purring cat. Within an inch of your fingers, a column of large, amber-colored ants is mortaring a hairline crack in the ancient wall, each worker placing a single pellet of wet clay mixed with chewed fern-pith, patting it down with antennae, and stepping aside for the next. 

They have been repairing this specific curve of the terrace since before the trees growing out of its roof were seeds. Five feet away, a massive coiled millipede lies dead on a bed of moss; it is already half-submerged under a slow, grey wave of carrion-beetles and white mycelium that will, by morning, turn its armored rings into another quarter-inch of soil for the figs, which will drop their leaves into the sorting-pits of the termites, who will chew them into paste to raise the arch another millimeter toward the clouds.
