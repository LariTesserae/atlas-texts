The plateau does not end at a horizon so much as it dissolves into the pale, bright ceiling of the sky. It is an upland of continental proportions, sitting high enough in the atmosphere that the air is thin and permanently chilled, holding a steady, crisp temperature that turns breath into brief ghosts. Despite the altitude, the light here is clear and generous—a high, shadowless illumination that pours down through thin, fast-moving cloud layers, turning every wet surface into a dull mirror.

And every surface is wet. The entire highland is a tilted table of ancient, blue-grey slate and dark basalt, fractured over geological ages into flat, manageable slabs. Across this expanse, for hundreds of miles in every direction, stands an uninterrupted, densely packed urban fabric built entirely without mortar, metal, or wheel. 

From a high vantage, the city looks like a vast, terraced reef of stacked stone. Millions of dry-stone structures—dwellings, retaining walls, windbreaks, cisterns, and watchtowers—crowd against one another, sharing walls, climbing over older foundations, and stepping downward along the slight, natural incline of the plateau. Because the builders used the native rock directly underfoot, the boundary between the geological and the architectural has long since blurred. The stone has been split and stacked for so many millennia that the buildings themselves have undergone erosion. Their corners are softened by wind and water; their roofs of overlapping slate shingles have sagged into gentle, stable curves. There is no wood here, no glass, no forged iron, and no smoke. It is an architecture of pure weight and friction, a staggering accumulation of hand-placed rock that stretches until the curvature of the earth hides it.

Water is the lifeblood and the constant motion of this place. The high plateau catches the bellies of passing clouds. Even when it is not actively raining, a dense, cool mist frequently rolls across the roofs, condensing instantly on the cold slate. Every wall weeps. To manage the endless runoff, the entire city is a single, integrated hydraulic system. Millions of narrow, stone-lined rills, gutters, and stepped channels thread through the dense warren of alleys and courtyards. 

This is where the landscape’s dynamism lives—yet it happens in near-total silence. 

The engineering is so established, so perfectly worn into balance over centuries, that the water does not roar or crash. Instead, it slides in laminar sheets down smooth basalt chutes, seeps through gravel-filled filtration terraces, and collects in thousands of rectangular, stone-rimmed basins that step down the plateau like giant staircases. The sound is never more than a faint, omnipresent sibilance—a soft *shhh* of water slipping over stone, accompanied by the occasional, hollow *plink* of a heavy drop falling from a roof overhang into a cistern. The dense labyrinth of thick dry-stone walls acts as a gigantic acoustic baffle, swallowing the wind and muting the movement of the water. You can stand in the center of a square surrounded by ten thousand buildings, watching the reflections of clouds race across wet flagstones and the ripples shivering across a hundred brimming basins, and hear almost nothing at all.

Life here is sparse, adapted to the chill, the stone, and the lack of deep soil. There are no trees to rustle in the wind. Vegetation is limited to hardy, low-growing things that anchor themselves in the damp crevices between the stacked stones. Thick cushions of emerald and rust-colored moss pad the tops of the walls, swelling with moisture. Pale, stiff-bladed sedges grow in the silt traps of the water channels, and leathery, grey-green lichens map the older flagstones in slow, concentric rings. Occasionally, a sunken courtyard—sheltered from the high-altitude wind and catching the cold sun—hosts a carefully tended patch of stunted, dark-leafed tubers, grown in gravel mulch brought up from the riverbeds miles below.

The builders—or the inheritors—of this endless stone metropolis are as quiet and rare as the rest of its fauna. You might walk for an hour through the narrow, damp-walled passages, stepping over brimming stone gutters, before seeing a living soul. When they appear, they are solitary figures wrapped in heavy, overlapping cloaks woven from the coarse hair of mountain grazers, dyed the same slate-grey as the walls. They wear soft-soled footwear of cured hide that makes no sound on the wet rock. A person might be seen crouching beside a sluice gate, lifting a flat stone to redirect a trickle of water from one terrace to another, or carefully resetting a frost-heaved shingle on a low roof. They use no levers or pulleys; they simply understand the balance of the stones. Once the adjustment is made, they move on, disappearing around a corner, leaving the courtyard empty again.

Other creatures are equally scarce and silent. Long-legged, slate-colored wading birds stand motionless in the shallow overflow basins, waiting hours for the tiny, blind crustaceans that wash out of the subterranean aquifers. High overhead, riding the brisk thermals where the cold air rises off the sun-warmed wet rock, a solitary scavenger with a ten-foot wingspan tilts its wings, adjusting to the wind without a single flap, its shadow racing silently over miles of glistening roofs, empty plazas, and overflowing gutters.

The weather shifts constantly. A bank of fog will materialise out of the thin air, swallowing a whole district in white gloom and coating every surface in fresh dew. Ten minutes later, the high wind pushes it apart, and the pale, bright sun returns, striking the wet city so that a million slate roofs and a hundred thousand brimming pools flash like polished pewter, stretching out in quiet, damp, stone-stacked infinity.
