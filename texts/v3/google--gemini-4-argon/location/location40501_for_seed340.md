The ground here is not rock or soil, and it has never known a drop of rain. It is a five-mile-wide subterranean basin—a freshly split rift in the planetary crust—completely choked with dry, violent, silicate growth. 

Instead of water, the ecology runs on heat, static friction, and rising geothermal gasses. The vegetation is a dense, tangled mattress of fibrous, mineral-drinking flora that grows with terrifying, visible speed. Great, ribbed stalks of obsidian-black *shatter-reed* and pale, porous *pumice-burl* heave themselves upward from the deep thermal vents, locking together into a false floor hundreds of feet thick. Because the rift is still pulling apart, the botany is in a state of frantic, embryonic construction. You can watch a braided cable of silica-vine as thick as a wagon wheel shoot across a chasm, whip-cracking through the dry air, its tendrils instantly calcifying into load-bearing bridges that have never been smoothed by wind or water. Everything is sharp, raw-edged, and newly extruded.

The light comes from the friction. As the massive, dry plants grind their glassy bark against one another in the rising thermal drafts, they generate continuous sheets of piezo-electric luminescence. It is not the dim glow of deep-sea bioluminescence, but a steady, diffuse, amber-and-violet daylight that illuminates the entire cavernous expanse, casting sharp, shifting shadows through the canopy of brittle, parchment-like leaves. 

The sound is an absolute, physical wall. You cannot hear your own voice; you can only feel it vibrating in your throat. The basin roars with the continuous tectonic groaning of the widening rift, the explosive *cannon-cracks* of over-stressed mineral trunks splitting under their own growth, and the deafening, metallic screech of a million dry branches sawing against each other in the hot updrafts. 

Layered over the botanical thunder is the shriek of the fauna. The canopy and the tangled floor are boiling with life—an overwhelming, hyperactive biomass of dry-shelled, kinetic creatures. Because there is no water to drink, the animals feed on the momentum and the mineral-pith of the plants. Clouds of cicada-like organisms with resonance-chambers made of hollowed flint cling to the vibrating stalks, drinking the kinetic energy and amplifying the noise into a blinding, rhythmic static. Great, multi-jointed grazers—looking like animated bundles of dried kindling and copper wire—crash through the brittle brush, snapping off newly grown silica shoots with stone-Toothed jaws. When they move in herds, the ground beneath them flexes and shatters, dropping tons of fresh, jagged mulch into the hotter depths below, where it is immediately consumed and re-spun by the lower roots. Flocks of flat, leathery gliders ride the furious thermal chimneys, their wings snapping like sails in a gale as they scoop up airborne spores and static-charged dust.

Nothing stays still. A hill of woven, bone-dry burls will inflate with subterranean sulfur gas, swell to the size of a cathedral over twenty minutes, and then rupture in a silent flash of static and a deafening avalanche of warm, dry ash, only for the surrounding vines to immediately surge into the crater, wrestling for the space.

Scattered across this churning, root-woven valley are the shelters of the basin's tool-users. They cannot build on the ground, because the ground is a slow-motion whirlpool of snapping wood and rising stone. Instead, their structures hang suspended in the mid-air gaps, lashed to five or six competing mega-stalks with thick ropes of braided chrysotile and sinew. The shelters are small, teardrop-shaped cages woven from flexible, fire-cured reeds and plastered with dry, acoustic clay meant to deaden the apocalyptic noise outside. 

Because the trees are constantly growing apart or twisting around one another, these suspended huts are never still. Around every shelter, dangling from the framing ropes, are primitive counterweights—heavy chunks of raw, unworked basalt notched with flint chisels—and tension-spools made of carved bone. When a stalk surges upward, the bone ratchets slip with a loud *clack-clack-clack*, paying out line so the house isn't torn in half. 

Here and there, stretched between the violent canopies, are long, ragged nets of woven plant-fiber anchored with stone hooks, set out to catch the migrating swarms of flint-shelled insects. Below the huts, on the heaving floor, lie the remnants of older structures that lost their tension: half-crushed cages of wicker and chipped stone tools being rapidly swallowed, lifted, and ground into powder by the relentless, dry jaws of the rising forest.
