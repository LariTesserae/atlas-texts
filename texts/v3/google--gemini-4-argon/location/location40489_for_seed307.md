High on the raw, unweathered flank of an obsidian horn—where the altitude normally strips the air to a dry, freezing whistle—there is a fracture no wider than a parlor. It is a collapsed volcanic blister, three paces across and four high, sealed off from the mountain wind by a hanging curtain of freshly cooled tachylite glass. 

Inside this pocket, the temperature is just shy of boiling. 

The rock walls are young, violently splintered, their edges still razor-sharp and untouched by frost or wind-scour. Yet they are weeping. Thick, mineral-heavy water beads on the dark glass and runs down the jagged basalt steps in syrupy, scalding sheets. The air is so saturated with steam that breathing feels like swallowing warm broth, though the vapor never quite turns to fog; the light inside—a flat, unremarkable daylight that has no business existing five meters deep in solid rock—keeps every wet angle and dripping facet clearly visible. There is no sky hole, no glowing lichen, no bioluminescence. The light simply sits in the room like a lukewarm bath, casting shadows that occasionally detach from their objects and slide down the walls with the runoff.

There is nothing green here. Even thermophilic slimes have barely gained a foothold, appearing only as faint, rust-colored smudges in the corners where the water eddies. Instead, the space is choked with animal life.

Every shelf, crack, and overhang is packed with creatures the size of human hands, resembling a cross between shell-less land crabs and multi-jointed thumbs. Their flesh is translucent and gray, pulsing with visible, black-veined hearts. There are thousands of them in this tiny room, stacked two and three deep, constantly shifting over one another in a slow, wrestling boil. Despite their overwhelming numbers and the frantic, continuous scrabbling of their limbs, they make almost no sound. The physics of the pocket deaden acoustics; when two creatures collide, or when a heavy mineral droplet hits a pool, the noise is muted to a soft, muffled *thrum*, as if heard through a thick wall of tallow.

These creatures are tool-users, and their industry is the reason the rock remains so jagged. Using pincers tipped with calcium-hardened horn, they systematically snap pristine flakes of volcanic glass from the walls. They pass these microscopic knapped blades from individual to individual in a silent, frenzied assembly line, binding the shards to lengths of their own molted, sinewy tendon using a glue secreted from their mouths. 

With these crude lancets, they do not hunt one another. They hunt the water.

In this pocket, the boundary between liquid, heat, and geometry has come undone. As the scalding water pools on the uneven floor, it does not merely drain away; it knots. The surface tension thickens until a puddle becomes a three-dimensional blister of water that refuses to flatten, rolling uphill against the grain of the basalt like a heavy mercury drop. Inside these rolling blisters, time and space refract strangely—looking through a droplet reveals not the black rock beneath it, but a tiny, distorted view of the same room from three minutes prior, or from an angle that shouldn't exist.

When a water-blister swells to the size of a fist, a dozen of the gray creatures converge on it. Moving with sudden, jerky speed, they drive their obsidian-tipped lances into the skin of the water. 

The blister does not pop; it bleeds heat and light. The creatures use flat, paddle-shaped stones to scrape the spilled, gelatinous warmth into their mouths, feeding on the thermodynamic anomaly itself before the punctured water remembers its nature, collapses back into ordinary wetness, and trickles down into the floorboards of the mountain. 

The air smells of wet flint, boiled cartilage, and copper. In the center of the floor, where the runoff gathers before vanishing into a hairline fissure, a discard pile of dulled obsidian blades and snapped stone hammers has grown into a sharp, glistening cone. The creatures crawl over the blades without cutting their soft bellies—not because their skin is tough, but because in the heavy, wet heat of the pocket, sharpness is a property that only applies when a tool is held with intent. Left alone on the pile, the glass edges go temporarily blunt, softening like warm wax in the quiet, crowded dark, waiting to be picked up again.
