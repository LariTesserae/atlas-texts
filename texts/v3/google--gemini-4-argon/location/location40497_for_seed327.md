The place is the interior of a breached stone chime, standing twenty-two thousand feet up in the pale blue air, where the surrounding world has dropped entirely out of sight. 

At this altitude, the sky does not feel like a ceiling; it is an abyss that surrounds the rock on all sides. The structure itself—a single, hollowed-out monolith of dark, iron-rich basalt—is no larger than a village church, its walls eroded over millennia into four curving ribs that meet at a broken apex. Between the ribs, there is nothing. No neighboring peaks, no foothills, no visible ground below—only a blinding, silver-white floor of cloud deck three thousand feet down, and the vast, empty vault of the upper atmosphere. The rock is a cage of stone suspended in a near-void.

Yet the stone is wet, and the hollow is deafening.

Warm, moisture-laden thermal updrafts rise continuously from the unseen valleys miles below, funneled directly through a porous chimney in the monolith's base. When this rising vapor hits the freezing, thin air of the extreme summit and the cold dark basalt, it condenses instantly. The rock does not merely sweat; it weeps in sheets. Water runs down the interior curves of the ribs in fast, silver braids, pooling in worn basins before spilling over the outer edges into the sky, where the wind immediately shears the waterfalls into mist that blows *upward*.

Because the four stone ribs are worn smooth and thin—established in their curves by ages of wind-scour and frost-wedging—they act as giant flutes. The high-altitude gale never stops, and as it passes through the apertures, the entire formation hums a low, vibrating chord you feel in your teeth and ribs before you hear it. Over this bass drone is the relentless, echoing clatter of the fauna.

The interior of the stone cage is a vertical bog, lush and violently green against the black stone and empty blue sky. Because the updrafts carry not just moisture, but dust, spores, and the stray bodies of lowland insects, the inner faces of the ribs are choked with life. There are no trees—the wind would tear them out—but there are cushions of moss three feet thick, swollen with water like green sponges. From the moss hang great, dripping curtains of dark red liverworts and rubbery, broad-leaved ferns that thrash constantly in the draft. A species of hollow-stemmed reed grows horizontally out from the crevices, each stem tipped with a bladder that traps the rising mist, swells to the size of a plum, and bursts with a sharp *pop*, raining nutrient-rich water onto the roots below.

Every square inch of this hanging garden is contested by birds and amphibians. 

Thousands of small, slate-gray swifts with hooked claws use the wet moss as a rookery. They do not fly so much as drop into the void and let the updrafts shoot them back into the cathedral’s interior like stones from a catapult. Their high, metallic shrieks bounce off the wet rock, multiplying until the air feels solid with sound. Beneath the moss, in the cold, dark water running over the stone, live pale, flat-bodied salamanders the length of a forearm. They have suction-cupped bellies and spend their lives inching up the vertical rills to snap at the swifts' ankles and raid the nests. 

Larger shadows cut through the normal, crisp sunlight that pours between the ribs: alpine raptors with double-jointed wings that fold completely flat against their bodies, allowing them to dive *through* the narrow stone gaps at sixty miles an hour, snatching swifts from the moss without ever touching the rock, then shooting out the other side into the empty sky. The biological pace here is frantic, driven by the constant rush of the wind and the narrow margin between the wet sanctuary of the stone and the sterile void five feet away.

Someone has been here, or comes here still, though never for long. 

Woven into the roots of the toughest ferns are small, hemispherical baskets made of split willow brought from far below, anchored to the basalt with pitons of hammered copper and bone wedges. Inside the baskets are heavy, grease-smeared river stones—heating stones, or weights, left behind. Carved into the black basalt just above the highest reach of the moss are notches, hundreds of them, arranged in long vertical tallies that have been smoothed at the edges by the running water. 

Stretched across the widest gap between two of the great stone ribs is a web of thick, braided hair and sinew ropes, bleached white by the intense sun. Hanging from the center of this web is a single, hollowed-out ram's horn, positioned precisely in the path of the strongest updraft. As the wet wind screams up from the cloud-floor and through the horn, it adds a clear, reedy, artificial note to the deep basalt drone of the rock and the frantic shrieking of the birds—a fifth voice in the tiny, crowded room at the top of the empty world.
