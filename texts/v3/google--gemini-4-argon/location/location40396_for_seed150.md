The settlement occupies a fresh, steep-sided notch in a high ridge, a geological fault that split open recently enough that the greywacke stone at its upper rim still breaks in sharp, unweathered knives. The entire cleft is no larger than a village green or a great cathedral’s nave, bounded on three sides by vertical rock and on the fourth by a drop into blank white cloud. Yet within this narrow rocky slot, there is no open ground and almost no visible stone. The space is packed to bursting with a dense, vertical town grown entirely from living, cold-adapted flora.

Nothing here is sawed, forged, or mortared. The architecture is an engineered entanglement of *nothofagus*—stunted, thick-trunked mountain beech—and a species of monstrous, woody cushion-plant that swells over the cold rock like hard green masonry. Generation after generation of deliberate grafting, pleaching, and root-binding has turned the grove into a single, honeycombed organism. Streets are narrow, sunken trenches—barely shoulder-width—threaded between swollen, moss-armored root-walls that rise three or four stories overhead. Walkways at the second and third levels are woven from living boughs forced to grow horizontally, their bark fused where they cross, forming knotted, springy suspension bridges and cantilevered balconies that drip with beard-lichen.

The air is perpetually near freezing, raw and wet. Because the cleft sits right at the condensation line of the mountain, a heavy, pewter-colored mist drags through the canopy at all hours. It never truly rains, yet everything is soaked. Water beads on the waxy, dark-green leaves of the roof-weaves, saturates the foot-deep carpets of crimson and rust-colored sphagnum moss that line the corridors, and runs in cold, clear threads down the grooved bark of the support trunks. Where the underlying rock pokes through in the deeper basements of the town, the stone is black with seepage, its jagged, newly-fractured angles cushioned by translucent liverworts.

Light is scarce and submarine. The high rock walls block the low sun for most of the day, and the dense thatch of living evergreens overhead filters the grey sky into a permanent, greenish-slate twilight. Down in the lowest alleys, where the root-buttresses crowd together so tightly a person must turn sideways to pass, it is almost night. The only brightness comes from the wet sheen on black bark and the pale, ghostly green of lungwort and fan-lichens that plaster every vertical surface, drinking the fog.

Despite the bitter chill, the town is restless with slow, biological industry. The plants here do not go dormant in the cold; they pump a thick, resinous, antifreeze sap that smells sharply of turpentine and crushed celery. You can almost feel the metabolic tension in the walls. The architecture is constantly trying to grow shut. Windows—ovals held open by wedged river-stones or bone spacers—are fringed with pale, vigorous shoots that must be snapped back weekly. The floors of the rooms, which are hollow chambers formed by training four or five trunks to dome together and fuse at the crown, are warm to the touch compared to the outside air, heated by the slow, fungal composting of peat packed beneath the root-mats. 

Sound is muffled, swallowed instantly by the tonnages of wet moss and dense foliage. There is no wind inside the structure, only a high, distant hiss when a gust shears across the rocky cleft far above. Down in the packed tiers, the acoustic world is small and intimate: the endless, irregular *tick-tock* of condensation falling from leaf to leaf; the soft, spongy *shuck* of waterlogged peat under weight; and, every so often, a deep, resonant *creak*—like a ship’s timber straining—as a load-bearing trunk shifts against the sharp rock wall or adjusts its tension in the frost. 

Animal life is sparse, dwarfed by the crushing monopoly of the green mass. A few glossy, black beetles the size of plums crawl with glacial slowness over the resin-weeps. High in the roof-thatch, rare, heavy-bodied birds with grey, hair-like plumage sit motionless for hours, digesting fermented berries, their dark eyes watching the mist. Every few minutes, a localized shudder runs through a street-wall as the town’s drainage system works: hollow, bamboo-like reeds, grafted directly into the boggy pockets where water collects, fill to capacity and tip over under their own weight, spilling a gallon of icy water into a stone-lined gutter before springing back upright with a wet *thwack* to begin filling again.
