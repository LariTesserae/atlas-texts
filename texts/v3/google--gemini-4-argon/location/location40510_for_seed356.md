There is no true ground here, or if there is, it lies miles beneath a floor of living, lignified root-plate that spans the continent. The world is low, flat, and so vast that the curvature of the earth folds the black horizon away before any boundary can be reached. It is a planetary basin filled entirely with a single, continuous architecture of vegetation, operating in the pitch dark like a silent, self-sustaining factory.

Because there is no sun, there are no leaves. Nothing here reaches toward a sky; nothing spreads a broad surface to catch light that does not exist. Instead, the flora has organized itself into the shapes of heavy industry: pipes, manifolds, condensers, and cabling, all grown from dense, dark-barked wood, vulcanized sap, and rigid, chitinous cellulose. 

 underfoot—if one were to stand on the primary deck of this vegetative mat—the surface is made of thick, interlocking root-ribs, slick with a constant, warm condensation. Between these ribs lie shallow, rectangular basins of black water, geometrical and sharp-edged, as if cast in concrete. The water does not flow; it sits at a permanent level, lukewarm and slightly viscous with dissolved minerals, trembling only with a microscopic, high-frequency vibration that runs through the entire global root-plate.

Rising from this submerged floor are the trunks, though they look less like trees and more like the cooling towers and distillation columns of an endless petrochemical plant. They grow at precise, densely packed intervals—vertical cylinders of black-ironwood, some smooth and sweating beadlets of moisture, others pleated with sharp, unweathered ridges that act as heat exchangers. At twenty, fifty, and a hundred feet up into the darkness, these trunks throw out horizontal runners at crisp, ninety-degree angles. The runners fuse into the neighboring trunks, forming a three-dimensional lattice of conduits that stretches for thousands of miles in every direction.

The darkness is nearly absolute, broken only by the occasional, utilitarian indicator of the forest’s internal chemistry. Every few hundred yards, a translucent bladder grown into the side of a trunk glows with a dull, steady, amber bioluminescence—the exact color of a low-wattage sodium vapor lamp seen through heavy fog. In the shallow water basins below, thin veins of pale, greenish-white vascular tissue run along the submerged roots like buried fiber-optic cables, casting just enough light to show the sharp, newly formed edges of the bark. Nothing here is rotted. Nothing is weathered or worn down. When a conduit-branch reaches its capacity, it does not decay; it calcifies into a sharp-angled truss of dark stone-wood, permanent and structural, while fresh, slick tubingextrudes beside it.

This is a purely botanical machine. Deep beneath the basin, the taproots drill miles down into the young, fractured crust of the earth, drawing up geothermal heat, sulfur, iron, and heavy brines. The forest processes these raw materials in the dark. The trunks are pressurized. If you press a hand against the wet, warm bark of a three-foot-wide column, you can feel the slow, hydraulic surge of fluid being driven upward by massive osmotic engines in the root-bed. Higher up in the canopy of pipes, great bulbous galls act as chemical reactors, cracking heavy molecules, stripping electrons from iron and sulfur, and synthesizing sugars without a single photon of light. 

Despite the immense scale of this production, the landscape is profoundly quiet. There are no animals—no insects clicking in the bark, no birds, no amphibians in the warm pools. There is no wind to rustle the canopy, because the dense grid of trunks and horizontal runners baffles the air into submission, holding it in a warm, stagnant suspension. 

The only sounds are the faint, muffled by-products of the flora’s silent industry. There is a continuous, almost imperceptible hum—the collective vibration of trillions of gallons of sap moving through high-pressure xylem vessels across a continent. Occasionally, a bladder-valve high overhead will equalize its pressure with a soft, wet *shhhk*, releasing a warm mist of excess water vapor and methane that drifts slowly down through the lattice. Every few minutes, a single heavy drop of condensed water detaches from a horizontal runner and falls into a basin below with a small, solitary *plip* that dies instantly against the sound-absorbing mass of the surrounding wood.

The air smells of warm rain on hot iron, of bruised fern-flesh, and the faint, sweet-sharp tang of turpentine and ozone. It is a closed, eternal loop. The vapor rises from the warm water, condenses on the cooler intake-vines overhead, is drawn back into the bark, cycled down to the magma-heated roots, and pumped back up again. There are no seasons here, no day or night, no succession of species, and no decay. The great dark engine simply holds its pressure, spanning the curve of the world, wet and warm and quietly humming in the dark.
