You enter sideways, through a fissure that scrapes the buttons of your coat, and find yourself in a space no wider than a threshing floor and no taller than a three-story house. Yet it is a city. 

The cavern is a blind pocket in deep, grey-blue soapstone, long ago hollowed out by a whirlpool that has since dropped to a lesser, steadier habit. The stone here is soft enough to be scored with a thumbnail, and whoever lived here—or whatever small, meticulous hands worked this room—carved it until almost no natural wall remained. It is a dense, vertical slum of miniature masonry and rock-cut architecture, packed into the belly of the stone. Thousands of niches, stoops, lintels, and tiered cisterns crowd inward from the walls, stepping down toward a central, flooded basin. The scale is slightly wrong for a human—built for something the size of a terrier or a child, or perhaps built by human hands not as dwellings, but as an obsessive, crowded ossuary or a mechanical shrine. Everything is within arm's reach, or seems to be; bridged by tiny viaducts of split slate, jammed together shoulder-to-shoulder, roof-to-step, until the rock face disappears behind a facade of wet, dark stone-craft.

It is loud here. Deafeningly, endlessly loud, though nothing moves.

The sound comes from the water and the architecture working together. There is no river, only a heavy, relentless seepage from the ceiling—thousands of steady trickles bleeding through a porous stratum overhead. Whoever built the pocket-city engineered it to catch every drop. The water falls into tuned stone basins, spills down stepped runnels, gurgles through clay pipes no thicker than a thumb, and drops from gargoyle-like spouts into deep, resonant pots carved into the floor. The whole chamber is an acoustic engine. Because the stone is hard-packed and the space is small, the splashing, drumming, and hollow *glug-glug* of ten thousand tiny drains overlaps into a single, roaring static. It sounds like a crowd shouting in a language made entirely of consonants, or a downpour on a tin roof that has lasted for ten thousand years. To speak to someone standing two feet away, you would have to cup your hands and bellow into their ear.

The air is cold—the precise, unchanging chill of a cellar dug below the frost line. It smells of wet flint, old lime mortar, and copper. Breath hangs briefly in the air before being beaten down by the damp. Every surface is slick with a glaze of mineral-heavy water. Over centuries of this steady, unvarying weep, the water and the carving have reached an agreement. Sharp tool-marks from bone chisels and bronze adzes have been softened by erosion, rounded until the little stairs and gutters look as if they were molded from tallow or dark wax. At the same time, calcium and grey silt have deposited in the corners, welding the stacked-slate roofs together into a single, seamless crust. Nothing crumbles anymore. The city has been pickled in its own lime-drip; it is a ruin that has forgotten how to decay.

Light has no source you can point to, yet the darkness is incomplete. A pale, tallowy phosphorescence clings to the wettest margins of the stone—not a plant, not even a proper fungus, but a mineral-eating slime as thin as a brushstroke that glows the color of watered milk. It outlines the lips of the aqueducts, traces the mortared joints of the miniature towers, and pools faintly in the standing water at the bottom of the well. In this dim, sourceless gleam, the wet slate shines like pig-iron. Shadows don't fall in one direction; they gather in the hollows—inside the thousands of dark, fist-sized doorways that honeycomb the walls.

Nothing green has ever lived here. There is no rot because there is no timber, no thatch, no soil. The materials of this world are entirely cold: split shale, river-smoothed pebbles wedged into arches without mortar, fired grey clay, and green-black bronze pins used to hold the sluice-gates open. The sluice-gates are frozen in their tracks by mineral crust, set to angles decided upon ages ago, dividing the water in fixed proportions that never vary by a drop. 

Yet the place is not entirely unpeopled by life. If you stand still on the narrow, slick ledge that serves as the pocket's only balcony, letting the roar of the water numb your ears, you will eventually see the residents. 

They are rare, and they move at the speed of clock-hands. An blind amphipod, translucent and the size of a man's palm, hitches itself out of a flooded window-arch, its long antennae trembling in the spray of a four-inch waterfall. Down in the central basin, where the water churns white and loud before draining into a grated sinkhole, a pale, flat-headed salamander clings to a submerged obelisk, waiting for the water to bring it a stray crustacean or a flake of bacterial mat. And higher up, wedged into the dry-ish armpits of the miniature buttresses, sit creatures that look like wet stones until they uncurl—leathery, shell-less snails that graze on the glowing slime, leaving dark, temporary tracks of blackness across the luminous walls before the water washes the glow back over their trail.

There are no seasons in this pocket. A blizzard could be scouring the mountains three hundred feet above, or a forest could be burning to ash, and the drip-rate in the street of the seven-inch arches would not speed up by a single beat. The temperature does not rise a degree; the light does not wax or wane. The water enters through the roof, runs through the empty, roaring metropolis of stone gutters, turns the little stone water-wheels that no longer grind anything—their axles worn smooth and loose in their sockets, spinning loosely and uselessly with a rhythmic *clack-clack-clack* under the constant stream—and exits through the floor. It is a machine that has outlived its purpose, reduced to pure maintenance of its own noise, sealed in the dark rock, busy forever.
