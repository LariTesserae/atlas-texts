The floor of the rift is four miles across and drops away from the upper plains in a single, violent fracture. Because the collapse happened in geological recentness, the walls have not yet slumped into soft talus slopes. They stand as sheer escarpments of dark grey basalt and pale limestone, scored with vertical chisel-marks where blocks the size of barns sheared off and dropped. The bottom is a chaos of angular slabs, tilted tables of stone, and wide, flat beds of crushed shale, all of it sitting well below the surrounding water table. 

Yet there is no river here, and no lake. The water comes out of the walls as a slow, cold sweat. It beads on the dark faces of the basalt, runs down the vertical cracks in thin, slick ribbons, and gathers in the flat-bottomed troughs between the fallen slabs. The air is perpetually cool—around fifty degrees—and holds a stationary, gray mist that hangs forty feet above the floor, never rising to clear the rim, never sinking to wet the ground. It acts as a lid. Sunlight from the surface hits the top of this mist layer and diffuses into a flat, pewter-colored twilight that casts no shadows on the rocks below. There is no morning glare and no evening reddening; there is only the even, leaden brightness of an overcast noon, stretching from wall to wall.

The stone is too fresh and the light too weak for soil to have formed. Vegetation is restricted to the fracture lines where the rock stays constantly damp. Here, a tough, blue-green liverwort forms leathery mats, and pale, knuckle-jointed ferns grow out of the gravel in solitary clumps, forty or fifty paces apart. Around the shallow, black pools of seepage water, a few stunted willows—none taller than a man’s waist—spread horizontally, their grey roots gripping the sharp edges of the limestone like fingers.

For all its barrenness, the basin is deafeningly, overwhelmingly alive. 

The stone is covered in birds. Millions of small, slate-backed cliff-swifts and pale-bellied crag-martins have claimed the miles of fractured escarpment and the fields of tilted boulders below. Because the rift is sheltered from the high prairie winds and holds a constant, cool temperature, the insect life bred in the damp gravel and seepage pools does not follow a seasonal cycle. Midges, stoneflies, and crane-flies hatch continuously, rising from the wet shale in dense, dark columns that look like smoke in the dim light. 

And so the birds never migrate, and their numbers never thin. Every horizontal ledge, every jagged step on the cliff face, every upturned edge of every fallen slab on the valley floor is packed shoulder-to-shoulder with roosting bodies, their feathers fluffed against the chill. Their droppings have painted the sharp edges of the young dark rock a stark, chalky white, streaking down the vertical planes like spilled lime. 

The sound is a continuous, abrasive roar. It is not the melodic singing of a forest, but a harsh, dry chattering—millions of sharp, clicking calls and high-pitched whistles overlapping until they fuse into a noise like a massive shingle beach being dragged back by a wave. Beneath the vocal din is the lower, rhythmic *thrum* of wings. The air between the floor and the mist-ceiling is thick with traffic, a constant cross-hatching of grey shapes banking and snapping at the columns of midges. When a section of the flock shifts from one boulder field to another, the air pressure changes noticeably, and the dry *whir* of feathers briefly drowns out the calling.

Down among the boulders, the ground is busy with the second tier of the basin’s ecology: the scavengers. The gravel between the slabs is a shifting carpet of dark-shelled carrion beetles, blind white crickets, and small, flat-skulled rock-lizards that move sluggishly in the cool air. They feed on the constant rain of feather-sheaths, dropped insects, cracked eggshells, and the bodies of swifts that strike the sharp stones in the dim light. Small, weasel-like mustelids with coats the exact color of wet shale slip silently through the crevices, so well-fed that they ignore everything five feet away from their noses, hunting without urgency.

Nothing is built here. There are no cairns, no walls, no cleared paths. Yet there are signs of a quiet, primitive industry tucked into the deep overhangs at the base of the eastern wall, where the rock stays completely dry. 

On a broad shelf of flat limestone, someone—or a small group of people—has been working. There are no huts or hearths; the wood of the stunted willows is too green and scarce to burn, and the cold is steady enough to be endured with heavy hides. Instead, there is an arrangement of tools left on the flat stone, already dusted with the grey down that floats everywhere in the air. 

There are heavy hammer-stones of foreign river-cobble brought down from the upper plains, and beside them, heaps of freshly knapped black chert. The flakes are razor-sharp, struck directly from the raw nodules exposed by the rift's collapse. Next to the stone-working pile lie three dozen snares made from the braided sinew of the rock-weasels and stiffened with stripped willow bark, weighted at the ends with grooved pebbles tied on with leather thongs. 

In a shallow depression nearby, several hundred swift-skins have been stretched and pegged out directly into the packed gravel using sharp splinters of adult bird-bone. Beside them sits a heavy limestone mortar, its hollow stained dark with rendered fat, and a bundle of five-foot throwing sticks carved from surface-wood, their ends fire-hardened elsewhere and notched to receive stone points. 

Whoever uses this shelf is not here now, or has stepped away into the gloom between the giant slabs. Their presence makes no dent in the valley. Twenty feet from the flint-knapping circle, the cliff-martins sit lined up along a knife-edge of basalt, indifferent, their black eyes fixed on the rising columns of midges. 

The mist hangs at forty feet. The cold water seeps down the black rock at the exact rate it evaporates into the damp air. The roar of the birds fills the four-mile trench from rim to rim, neither rising to a crescendo nor dying away for sleep, holding the exact same pitch in the shadowless grey light.
