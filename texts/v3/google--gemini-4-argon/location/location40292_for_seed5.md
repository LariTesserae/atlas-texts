It is a basin no wider than a courtyard, dropped twenty feet below the level of the grey clay plain. From thirty paces away, you would walk right past it, seeing only the flat, wind-scoured barrens that stretch to the low hills. You have to stand right at the lip to see down into the slot. 

What fills the pit is a city, or the calcified memory of one, packed so tightly that there is barely room to pass a flat hand between the walls. There are hundreds of structures down here, though none of them are tall enough for a grown man to stand inside. They are built of smooth, river-rolled slate and white clay mortar, stacked without plumbs or squares, their corners worn by centuries of frost and rubbing until the masonry looks less like laid stone and more like clustered barnacles or the chambers of a wasp’s nest. Narrow, stepped alleys, four inches wide, wind between multi-story towers that stop at waist-height. Tiny lintels of split bone frame doorways no larger than a boot heel. Everything is ancient, settled into its own weight; the stones have shifted as far as they will ever shift, wedged into permanent, load-bearing lock.

Above this miniature metropolis, spanning the entire mouth of the depression from rim to rim, is a massive, tangled canopy of frost-heather and silver-needled greasewood. The branches have grown across the gap and fused together over hundreds of years, their bark Pale as bleached linen. Above them, the sky is a permanent, cloudless sheet of white-blue ice-glare. The sun is low, cold, and blindingly bright, and as its light hits the pale canopy, the branches act like a diffuser. They scatter a fierce, shadowless, milky brilliance down into the crowded stone pit. Every crack in the slate, every bead of cold sweat on the mortar, is lit with surgical clarity. There are no dark corners.

The air inside the basin is dead still and bitter cold, hovering just above the point where water turns to bone. Yet it never quite freezes solid. A slow, rhythmic breath of slightly warmer air rises from some deep fissure beneath the packed foundations, meeting the frigid air trapped under the heather canopy. The result is a constant, clammy condensation. There is no rain, no running stream, only a pervasive, slick dampness that coats every surface. Clear beads of water hang from the undersides of the miniature stone arches, swelling over hours until they drop onto the slick cobbles below.

Because of the damp and the bright, trapped light, the greenery down here is riotous, though adapted to the chill and the cramped geometry. Lush, rubbery mats of blue-green liverwort and ice-fern choke the narrow streets, spilling out of the tiny windows and cascading down the terraced roofs like frozen waterfalls. Pale, cabbage-like rosettes with translucent leaves—filled with a natural antifreeze sap as thick as oil—wedge themselves into the mortar joints, prying at the ancient slate without ever managing to topple it. There is no bare earth and very little bare stone; where the masonry stops, the cold-loving flora begins, jammed root-to-wall in a dense, suffocating tapestry of jade, pewter, and frost-white. 

Nothing makes a sound. The thick mats of moss and the dense overhead canopy swallow whatever wind crosses the plain above. Even the water droplets fall onto cushioned beds of lichen without a *plink*. 

Yet the city is teeming. In the blinding, quiet glare, thousands of small lives move through the streets and over the roofs. The most numerous are the builders—or the inheritors, for no new stone has been laid here in generations. They are blind, hand-sized quadrupeds, something between a mole and a tortoise, with leathery, hairless grey skin and heavy, shovel-like foreclaws of horn. They move at a glacial pace through the four-inch alleys, climbing the worn steps with stiff, deliberate mechanics. Two of them meeting in a narrow lane will stop, press their cold snouts together for ten minutes without a twitch, and then slowly clamber over one another’s backs. 

Sharing the vertical faces of the towers are colonies of fat, translucent slugs striped with bright ochre, grazing slowly on the film of algae that grows in the condensation. Spiders with legs like spun glass sit motionless in webs strung across the bone-lintel doorways, their silk heavy with beads of fog. Under the eaves of the slate roofs, clustered like bats, hang rows of thumb-sized, featherless creatures with leathery wings folded tight against the chill, hibernating with their eyes wide open in the bright light.

Nothing hurries. The metabolism of the entire pit is cranked down to a crawl by the cold. A beetle takes an hour to cross a courtyard the size of a dinner plate. The liverworts grow a fraction of a millimeter a year. The miniature stone towers do not crumble, because the roots of the ice-ferns hold the weathered slate in a vice grip, and the stones shelter the ferns from the scouring wind of the plain. 

You can kneel at the rim, your knees soaking up the damp from the border-moss, and look down through a gap in the silver branches into the glaring, silent tangle of roofs, leaves, and slow-moving backs. Nothing looks up at you. The light stays bright and flat; the cold seeps through your coat; a single drop of water lets go of a bone lintel and disappears into the green below.
