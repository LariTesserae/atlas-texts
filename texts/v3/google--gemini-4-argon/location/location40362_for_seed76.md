The horizon does not drop off so much as it fades into a blinding, pale-cobalt glare. This is a continental tableland lifted five miles into a thin, frigid stratosphere, where the air holds almost no moisture at all. Because there is no water vapor to scatter the sun, the light is surgical. It pours down with a fierce, unfiltered brilliance that bleaches the color out of the high sky and casts shadows so sharp they look like chasms cut into the ground.

Yet the ground is not rock, and it is not barren. 

For ten thousand miles in every direction, the ancient, wind-planed stone has been buried beneath a deep, rolling mat of dry flora. Without water to make things supple or green, the vegetation here has evolved along mineral lines, building its structures from silica, powdered gypsum, and airborne dust. The landscape is lush, even extravagant, in its growth, but it is the lushness of a coral reef or a crystal cavern. Great, cushion-like mounds of silver-white lichen—some the size of haystacks, others as broad as hills—swell from the ground. Between them stretch knee-deep meadows of glass-fern and brittle, bone-colored sedge. There is no mud, no sap, no rot. When a plant dies, it does not decompose; it simply calcifies further and shatters into a fine, talcum-like drift of silicate sand that the living plants absorb through dry, electrostatic root-nets.

The terrain has the settled, effortless curves of extreme age. Whatever violent uplift created this plateau ended eons ago. Since then, the wind—carrying its abrasive load of botanical glass-dust—has sanded the mountains down into long, whale-backed ridges and wide, shallow basins that take days to cross. Because the air is so thin, the wind makes almost no sound as it moves. A gale that would roar at sea level passes over these high plains as a faint, breathy hiss. 

In the absence of atmospheric noise, the landscape’s true dynamism is visual. You can see weather systems a hundred miles away, though they contain no rain. Great, silent cyclones of pearl-colored dust and glittering spore-chaff rise from the basins, spinning lazily into the stratosphere like pillars of smoke. As the sun heats the dark basalt under-layer in the afternoon, thermal currentsrip across the plains, and the entire continent seems to shimmer. Millions of acres of tall, feathery reed-beds—each stalk a hollow tube of spun quartz—bend in unison. As they brush against one another, they produce the only widespread sound in this world: a faint, dry chime, like a handful of fine needles dropped onto a marble floor, rippling outward for leagues until it is swallowed by the thin air.

Despite the fullness of the plant life, the feeling of the place is one of immense, breathable emptiness. There are no tangled canopies or claustrophobic thickets. The flora grows low and broad to hug the boundary layer of the ground, leaving the sky overwhelmingly vast. You can see the curvature of the world in the way the silver ridges dip away at the edges of vision. Between the great swells of the lichen-mounds lie wide, sweeping flats of polished turquoise stone and grey dust, swept clean by the persistent currents, giving the eye miles of smooth, uninterrupted resting space before the next swell of dry foliage begins.

Life moves through this bright quiet at a deliberate pace. 

Because the vegetation is so abrasive and dry, the rare animals that inhabit the plateau are built like delicate, articulated machines of chitin and horn. A hundred miles might hold only a single herd of grazers—creatures with long, stilt-like legs and bodies shaped like bleached hulls, moving slowly through the silica-sedges. They do not drink; they harvest the static electricity generated by the wind-rubbed plants and grind the mineral-rich fronds in gizzards lined with basalt pebbles. When they move, their narrow hooves click softly against the stone, a tiny, rhythmic tapping lost instantly in the enormity of the afternoon.

High above them, riding the invisible, dry thermals, are the predators—vast, manta-like gliders with wingspans as wide as small houses, their membranes so thin that the blazing sunlight shines right through them, turning them into glowing amber kites against the deep blue-black of the zenith. They never land while they live. They sleep on the updrafts, hunting by the sharp, black shadows their prey cast on the pale ground below. 

As the day wears on, the active chemistry of the flora shifts. To survive the brutal ultraviolet light of midday, the plants open millions of microscopic, mirrored vents, turning the hillsides into blinding sheets of chrome that reflect the heat back into space. Then, as the sun drops toward the distant, razor-sharp rim of the plateau, the vents close. A slow wave of color washes across the continent—from blinding silver to dusty violet, then to a deep, absorptive charcoal as the plants prepare to drink in the scant infrared warmth of the coming night. The thermal winds die down. The faint, glassy chiming of the reed-beds ceases. The dust pillars collapse in slow motion, settling back into the basins like snowfall, leaving the high, cold world in a stillness so complete it feels like the interior of a diamond.
