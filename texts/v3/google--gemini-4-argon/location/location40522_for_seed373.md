The entire landscape is no larger than a Gothic nave, bounded by sheer, inward-leaning walls of bleached basalt that meet no roof, opening instead to a pale, crisp sky. There is no floor. The space is a vertical hollow punched into the crest of an arid, high-altitude massif, a bone-dry chimney where the air is thin, cold, and tastes of static and pulverized mica. 

Despite the total absence of moisture, the chasm is choked with life and deafeningly loud.

The sound hits first—a physical, concussive roar that vibrates in the sternum and behind the teeth. It is the sound of ten thousand sheets of corrugated tin being shaken in a gale, overlaid with the high, metallic shrieking of cicadas the size ofhawks. Wind funneled up from the unseen abyss below tears through the hollow at sixty miles an hour, never dropping, only shifting direction in violent, erratic eddies that slam against the rock walls.

Anchored to these eroded, wind-polished cliffs is the flora. Because there is no water, the plants do not use sap; their vascular systems are dry, pressurized lattices of carbon and silica that feed directly on the kinetic energy of the gale and the ambient static electricity of the high altitude. Great, weeping curtains of bronze-colored fronds—each leaf as thin and rigid as a razor blade—hang from the ledges, thrashing in the updrafts. Their frantic, ceaseless slapping against the stone and against one another generates the metallic roar that fills the vault. Between the bronze curtains grow bloated, translucent bladders of dry membrane, anchored by braided wire-roots. They inflate and collapse rhythmically in the shifting pressure zones, glowing with a faint, cold bioluminescence inside their hollow bellies whenever the friction of the wind charges them.

The center of the space is an empty column of rushing air, yet it feels crowded because of the sheer speed of the things moving through it. 

The hollow is a rookery for the *skimmers*—creatures that are ninety percent surface area and almost no mass. They look like origami constructs made of shed snake skin and dry dandelion seed-heads, three feet across but weighing less than a coin. There are thousands of them. They do not fly so much as they are ricocheted by the turbulence, darting from wall to wall in jagged, hyperactive trajectories, snapping up the clouds of airborne silicate spores shaken loose by the thrashing fronds. Their wings clap with the sharp report of bullwhips as they tack against the vertical shear. Where they land on the basalt, they scuttle with frantic, high-frequency tremors, their hooked feet striking sparks against the stone.

Wedged into the deepest flutes of the eroded walls are the machines. 

They do not look built; they look extruded, like wasp nests made of smoked glass and iridescent bismuth. There are perhaps a dozen of them scattered up the height of the chimney, each the size of a coffin. These are resonance-harvesters, left here epochs ago to drink the chaos of the vent. Around each machine, the air is visibly fractured into cubic distortions—localized pockets of altered gravity and suspended inertia. When a gale-gust hits one of these invisible cubes, the wind doesn't go around it; it shears off at a ninety-degree angle, screaming at a pitch that shatters the brittle tips of the nearby bronze fronds. 

Occasionally, one of the machines cycles. A hair-thin filament of solid light snaps out from its casing, pinning a drifting cloud of dust or a frantic skimmer in mid-air. For three seconds, the captured matter is held in absolute, eerie stillness amidst the raging storm, stripped of its kinetic energy down to the atomic level, before the beam retracts and the bone-cold, deadened matter drops like a stone into the abyss below.

Nothing settles here. There is no soil, no detritus, no rot. When a frond snaps or a skimmer dies, the updraft immediately hurls the dry, weightless husks upward into the pale daylight at the top of the chimney, spitting them out into the high, thin atmosphere. The stone walls are worn smooth as river-pebbles not by water, but by centuries of dry, frantic biological friction and the relentless, screaming geometry of the wind.
