The space is no wider than a courtyard, though it was never meant to be one. It is the hollow interior of a structural pier—a foundation box left unsealed beneath the southern approach of a low stone viaduct. Above, at the level of the road, the masonry was never finished; rough courses of pale limestone sit dry-stacked without their capstones, roofed over now by a dense, self-seeded vault of broad-leaved figs and wild creepers that have woven themselves across the gap. The city outside—if anyone is still building it—never reached this sector. There is no iron here, no mortar mixing, no scaffolding, no rubble of fallen walls. Only the clean, sharp-edged blocks of a project halted in the middle of a warm afternoon, before the wind or the rain had time to dull a single chisel mark.

Because the stone is so new, it holds no history of wear. The granite plinths at the base meet the clay floor at crisp, ninety-degree angles. The quarry-split faces of the limestone are still dusty with their own birth-powder, rough as raw sugar, their fossil shells split cleanly in half and glittering slightly in the green-filtered gloom. Nothing has crumbled. Instead, the landscape is growing *upward* and *inward* over the crisp geometry.

The air inside is thick, warm, and very still, like the air inside an unglazed water jar. 

There is no running water, no pool at the bottom, no gutter from the road above. Moisture arrives entirely by breath and seepage. Where the warm subterranean air meets the cooler mass of the newly laid foundation stones, the rock sweats. Pale beads of condensation gather along the sharp arrises of the blocks, swelling until their weight overcomes their grip, then tracing slow, dark verticals down the stone before soaking instantly into the dry, gray silt of the floor. 

Despite the lack of standing water, the vegetation is extravagant, though it grows with a strange, airy discipline. Rather than a choked thicket, there are distinct, dramatic architectures of green separated by wide, clean expanses of bare stone and dry earth. A single, massive *Monstera* clings to the northern corner, its perforated leaves the size of shields, held out on long, tense stems to catch the dim, olive-tinted shafts of light dropping through the canopy thirty feet above. Four feet away, the wall is entirely bare, showing the neat, parallel grooves of the mason’s point-chisel. Then, from a dry vertical joint, a spray of hart’s-tongue ferns arches out into the void, their undersides striped with plump brown rows of spores. Thick, pale roots—some as fat as a thumb, smooth and silver-skinned with green tips—drop straight down through the empty air from the fig branches at the roof, plumb-lines descending through the twilight to anchor in the floor, leaving plenty of room to walk between them, if anything that walked on two legs ever came here.

The silence is profound, buffered by four-foot-thick stone walls and the heavy blanket of leaves overhead. If there is wind in the low hills outside, it does not reach down here. What sound exists is small and dry: the faint *tick* of a beetle’s tarsus on raw limestone; the soft, papery rustle of a shedding scale; the occasional heavy *pat* of a single drop of sweat falling from a high stone lintel onto the taut drumskin of an elephant-ear leaf.

Yet the chamber is crowded with life, moving at a brisk, silent tempo in the warm gloom. 

Along the wide, clean bands of stone between the plants, pale house-geckos with translucent skin—through which the dark ovals of their organs faintly show—stalk flies and bark-lice. They move in short, whip-like darts, freezing against the chisel-marks. Lower down, where the dampness gathers at the base of the piers, brigades of giant pill-millipedes, shiny as polished horn, graze on the thin film of pioneer algae just beginning to tint the fresh stone green. 

In the open spaces between the hanging roots, large, long-legged orb-weavers have slung horizontal nets. The silk is nearly invisible in the low light until a shaft of afternoon sun, shifting across the gap above, catches the dew-beads along a spiral thread and turns the web into a floating saucer of dull pearl. Beneath the webs, the dry clay floor is dimpled with the neat, conical pits of antlions, spaced out like a miniature quarry field in the dry zones where the wall-sweat never reaches. 

Every few minutes, the ecology shifts its weight. A spider drops on a dragline to wrap a moth; a skink with a tail the color of wet copper slips out from an unmortared joint, takes a cricket before it can chirrup, and vanishes back into the square darkness between two blocks; a fig root elongates by a fraction of a millimeter, its damp tip feeling its way down the immaculate face of a stone that has never known a winter.
