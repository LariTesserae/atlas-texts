The basin sits so high that the air is thin and sharp in the lungs, a cool, metallic draft that smells of wet slate and ozone. Yet despite the altitude—where the surrounding ridges drop away into an abyss of dazzling white cloud—the valley floor is sodden. It is a three-mile expanse of pale grey limestone and blue-black chert, crowded with thousands of weathered, pillar-like hoodoos and smoothly fluted fins of rock. There is no soil here, no moss, no lichen, no grass. Only stone and water, packed tightly together in a blinding, high-altitude glare.

The light is the first thing that breaks the rules. The sky above is the dark, bruising violet-blue of the upper stratosphere, empty of clouds, yet the basin is flooded with a ruthless, shadowless white brilliance. The wet stone doesn't just reflect the sun; the water itself seems to concentrate and refract light from angles that don't correspond to the sky. Glare bounces off the weeping flanks of the pillars, illuminating the undersides of overhangs and the bottoms of deep, water-filled potholes with the same harsh clarity as the ridge crests. 

Every surface is slick. Water does not rain here, nor does it melt from snow—it simply sweats from the rock. It beads on the smooth, ancient faces of the limestone fins, coalesces into rills, and runs down into the labyrinth of shallow channels and broad, flat pools at their bases. 

Except it does not always run down.

In the center of the basin, the water obeys a localized, fractured geometry. A sheet of clear water slides down the fluted curve of a twenty-foot pillar, reaches the lip, and instead of falling, turns at a sharp ninety-degree angle to flow horizontally across three feet of empty air before slapping against the neighboring rock face. Elsewhere, runoff collects in shallow basins only to peel upward in slow, shivering ribbons, ascending twenty feet into the bright air to pool on the flat underside of a stone arch, hanging there like a wobbling mercury mirror. The air is dense with these floating conduits and suspended lenses of water. Walking through the forest of pillars means ducking under cold, fast-moving streams as thick as a man's thigh that bridge the gaps between boulders, held in invisible troughs of warped space.

Because of this, the valley is loud. It is a continuous, echoing roar of hydraulic percussion. Water slaps against stone from below, collides with itself in mid-air, and pours down stepped spillways that lead in closed loops—cascading down four tiers of worn grey rock only to splash back onto the top tier again, perpetually eroding the same smooth grooves. The sound is a vast, hollow clatter, like millions of glass beads being poured across a marble floor, underscored by a low, vibrating hum where the air itself is stretched tight between the stones.

Scattered throughout this dense maze of stone and braided, airborne water are the structures. Whoever built them used the only materials at hand: split slate, river-rounded cobbles, and thick, gummy clay dredged from the deep sinkholes. There are no roofs—perhaps because the rain comes from every direction and none at all—only low, dry-stone walls, spiral cairns, and intricate, chest-high aqueducts. 

The builders understood the broken physics of the basin and worked with them. They built curved stone troughs that end abruptly in mid-air, precisely positioned to catch a stream falling sideways out of nowhere. They wedged broad, flat slabs of slate into the gaps between hoodoos to act as splash-plates, diverting upward-falling spray into spiral cisterns. Many of the structures are half-dissolved by the centuries; the water has worn the hand-knapped edges of the slate smooth, carving deep, sinuous runnels through the middle of stacked walls, integrating the masonry back into the karst. 

In the wider, flooded flats between the rock clusters, hundreds of tall, needle-like cairns stand in the ankle-deep water. Tied to the tops of these cairns with rotting, mineralized cordage are rough-hewn stone rings and perforated disks of bone-white chert. As the errant currents of water arc through the air, they pass directly through the holes in the suspended stones, keeping the cords pulled taut at impossible angles—some pointing straight at the violet sky, some straining horizontally toward the valley walls, vibrating like plucked bowstrings in the spray.

Nothing grows in the water, and nothing swims in it. The only hint of something bordering on life is a pale, mineral crust—a false lichen made of precipitated calcium and silica—that blooms in rapid, geometric frost-patterns wherever two airborne streams collide and atomize into mist. These chalky, dendritic fans grow to the size of dinner plates in a matter of minutes, drinking the kinetic energy of the crash, before growing too heavy, snapping off, and dissolving back into the clear, frigid pools below.

From the rim of the basin, looking across the mile-wide expanse, the landscape looks like a vast, flooded quarry caught in a slow-motion explosion. Thousands of pale stone spires stand shoulder to shoulder, laced together by a glittering, three-dimensional web of water that flashes like polished chrome in the merciless light. The air shimmers not with heat, but with spatial distortion—pillars seem to step sideways when viewed through a hanging lens of water, and the distant ridge appears twice, stacked one above the other in the pale violet sky, while the relentless, cold roar of the water fills the basin to the brim.
