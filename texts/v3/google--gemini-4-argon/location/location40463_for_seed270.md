The space is no larger than a village church, a sunken oval basin dropped into the bottom of an ancient, wind-planed plateau. Above the rim, the world is flat, pale, and empty, scoured down to its lowest, most stubborn basement rock over eons. Down inside the pocket, however, the walls are smooth, concave, and greasy to the touch, worn into continuous, undulating scoops like the inside of an old mortar. There is no soil here, no sand, not even a single blade of grass or patch of lichen. There is only dark, heavy soapstone, polished to a dull pewter sheen by millions of tiny, chitinous feet.

The air in the basin is cool, bone-dry, and smells sharply of hot copper, static electricity, and crushed ants. 

At first glance, the floor of the depression appears to be a pool of boiling black gravel. Look closer, and the gravel resolves into a knee-deep, seething carpet of hard-shelled fauna. This is an entirely dry ecology, running on friction, minerals, and stored kinetic tension. The dominant creatures are the *shale-backs*—segmented, platter-sized arthropods with carapaces that look like flakes of dark slate. They do not walk so much as slide and clatter over one another in a dense, continuous slow-motion avalanche, three and four layers deep. Every movement produces a dry, ceramic click. Multiplied by tens of thousands within the enclosed bowl, the sound is a deafening, incessant roar, like a rock-crusher running in an echo chamber or a shingle beach being dragged back and forth by a heavy tide. 

Light pours directly into the basin from a pale, cloudless sky, striking the moving backs of the creatures and breaking into a restless, shimmering glare. Because the air is entirely devoid of moisture, the light has a hard, surgical clarity. It catches the faint, powdery haze of talc and pulverized shell that hangs four feet above the floor, suspended by the constant agitation below. 

Rising out of this living, clicking tide are the remnants of a small mining or monitoring operation, built decades or centuries ago to harvest the basin’s strange, dry biomass, or perhaps the heavy metals the creatures concentrate in their shells. Three stout, cylindrical condenser towers made of riveted iron stand near the center, their lower halves polished to a bright, silvery steel by the endless rubbing of passing carapaces. A narrow iron catwalk connects them, sagging where its central support strut has been worn nearly through at the base. At the northern end of the pocket, a squat generator shed of corrugated metal sits half-submerged in a drift of shed exoskeletons.

The machinery is not entirely dead. A thick, braided cable runs from the shed up to a series of heavy brass anodes bolted directly into the soapstone walls. Every few minutes, a flywheel inside the shed catches with a heavy, rhythmic *chug-chug-thump*, driven by some subterranean thermal draft or a blind mechanical clock. When the engine turns over, the brass anodes hum, a sharp smell of ozone cuts through the dry dust, and a visible shiver runs through the entire packed basin of creatures. 

The ecology here is hyper-dense and fiercely active, operating without a drop of water. The base of the food chain is a pale, mineral-leaching crust—barely biological, more like a self-replicating salt stain—that blooms rapidly across the soapstone wherever the electrical current from the anodes tingles through the rock. The shale-backs scrape this crust away with mandibles of black iron-sulfide, their bodies running cool and efficient, excreting tiny, dry pellets of pure, refined silica. 

Preying upon the shale-backs are the *spindles*: creatures that look like bundles of bronze wire and dried tendon, standing two feet tall on eight impossibly thin, stilt-like legs. They step gingerly across the shifting roof of the shale-back swarm, their bodies vibrating at a high, nervous frequency. When a spindle strikes, it drops like a needle pile-driver, punching straight through a slate carapace with a sound like a hammer hitting an anvil. Instantly, neighboring spindles converge, and a localized whirlpool of thrashing legs and flying shell-shards erupts before being swallowed back into the general, grinding flow of the herd.

Higher up, clinging to the undersides of the iron catwalks and nested inside the empty sockets of the industrial floodlights, are the *bellows-worms*. They are leathery, concertina-shaped scavengers the size of loaves of bread. They do not come down to the floor; instead, they rhythmically expand and contract, drawing in the thick, talc-laden air with a loud, wheezing *shhh-clack*, filtering out the organic dust and airborne spores, and exhaling jets of clean, cool air that send swirling eddies through the dust cloud below.

Everything in the pocket is in constant, frantic, dry motion. The ancient stone walls hold the din like a cup. A heavy glass insulator falls from a rusted bracket on Tower Two; before it even hits the bottom, it is caught on the backs of the swarm, tossed sideways by the current of bodies, and ground between two heavy millipede-like crushers until it is reduced to frosted powder, vanishing into the loud, cool, industrious dark of the pile.
