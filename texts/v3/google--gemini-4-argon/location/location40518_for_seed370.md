The entirety of the world is the interior of the Vent—a vertical, dry-stone chimney no wider than a threshing floor and no taller than a church nave, wedged high in the throat of a dead mountain where the air is thin, warm, and relentlessly mobile. There is no native rock visible here. Every square inch of the boundary, and nearly all the volume within it, consists of placed, shaped, and fitted matter, packed so tightly that moving from one side of the chamber to the other requires threading sideways through gaps no wider than a ribcage.

It is an architecture of dry, unmortared clay and hammered bronze, built by hands that worked with river-cobbles, bone chisels, and simple updraft kilns, then left to sit in a roaring thermal draft for ten thousand years. 

The space is choked with pillars, lintels, buttresses, and overlapping scales of low-fired terracotta that have lost all their edges. There isn't a sharp corner left in the chamber. The builders packed the chimney with thousands of hollow ceramic flutes, stacked slab-towers, and suspended bronze kettle-drums, jamming them together until the room was nearly solid with clay and metal. Over the millennia, the constant, dry gale rushing up from the deep earth has scoured these crowded forms into smooth, bulbous, bone-like curves. Two adjacent pillars of stacked brick have been polished by the rising dust until they resemble fused molars; the stepped ledges of the walls have melted into drooping, waxen folds of baked silt. You cannot tell where one block ends and the next begins. Friction and time have welded the dry masonry into a single, honeycombed mass of smoothed ochre and dark, tarnished alloy.

The sound is the first and only law of the place. It is not a noise that happens within the room; the room *is* the noise. 

Because the high-altitude updraft is forced through this dense, narrow plug of perforated clay and bronze, the entire structure functions as a single, deafening organ pipe. The air does not gust or falter; it rises at a fixed, furious pressure from the geothermal fissures far below, maintaining a steady temperature like the breath of a feverish kiln. Passing through thousands of worn flutes, eroded whistle-slots, and taut, thick sheets of hammered copper wedged between the stones, the wind generates a continuous, staggering chord. It is a dry, abrasive roar—half brassy shriek, half low, stone-shaking drone—that presses against the eardrums and vibrates in the teeth. Drop a stone here, or scream at the top of your lungs, and the sound is erased before it leaves your lips, swallowed whole by the unvarying harmonic wall.

Yet nothing moves. The bronze plates are wedged too tightly in their stone grooves to rattle; the clay drums are too thick to visibly tremble. The deafening volume comes from a structure that appears completely, stubbornly inert.

Light enters only from the very top, where the masonry pinches together into a crowded grille of eroded stone rafters. What filters down is a bruised, high-altitude twilight—the dim, purplish-grey of a thin atmosphere—which immediately catches on the suspended dust. The air is dry to the point of desiccation, carrying an ancient, talcum-fine flour of pulverized brick that never settles because the updraft never ceases, and never thickens because the stone has already been worn down to its hardest, most stable core. This perpetual haze turns the dim light into a dull, uniform amber glow that clings to the rounded shoulders of the clay columns and dies completely in the tight crevices underfoot.

There is no soil, no dampness, no moss. In the deepest lee of the bronze baffles, where the air spins in tight, warm eddies, there is only a dry, scaly patina—a sulfur-yellow crust that might be a mineral efflorescence or a dead, vitrified lichen that stopped growing epochs ago, preserved like salt in an oven. 

To exist in this small, crammed vault is to be wedged inside the throat of a brass-and-earthenware horn that has been blowing the same dry, warm, ear-splitting note since the builders sealed the last lintel and climbed away. Everything that could break has broken and been ground to dust long ago; everything that remains has found its final, smoothed shape against the wind, locked in a dim, roaring equilibrium that has no reason to ever stop.
