The geology here is less than a century old. It is a vast, low-lying basin of black basalt and jagged tuff, dropped like a fresh scar between two diverging tectonic plates. Because there has been almost no rain to smooth it out, the stone remains razor-sharp—a rubble of blistered magma ropes, collapsed lava tubes, and knife-edged fissures that stretch for twenty miles before hitting the sheer, freshly faulted cliffs of the valley wall. 

There is no sky to speak of, only an oppressive, low-hanging ceiling of volcanic smog and nocturnal cloud cover that traps the geothermal heat. The darkness is nearly absolute, broken only by the occasional, sickly green bioluminescence of root-molds and the faint, dull-orange glow of cooling vents deep in the crevices. 

Yet, despite the lack of sun and the scarcity of standing water, the basin is choked with vegetation. This is not a forest that grows toward light; it is a jungle that feeds on heat and sulfur. Enormous, pale, tuberous trees—the thickness of water towers but soft as cork—thrust themselves straight out of the dry, sharp rock, anchoring into the warm fissures below. From their bloated trunks hang curtains of rubbery, blood-dark vines and gray, net-like mosses. There is no mud here, no soft forest floor, just a dry, suffocating tangle of roots and fleshy leaves braided tightly over treacherous, ankle-breaking stone. The only moisture comes from the vents: a continuous, warm, mineral-heavy exhalation that condenses on the waxy undersides of the leaves and drips down in a slow, greasy sweat.

You hear the valley miles before you drop into it. The sound is a physical wall, a deafening, high-pressure roar that vibrates in the teeth and behind the eyes. It is the sound of billions of bodies in frantic, ceaseless motion.

The fauna has outpaced the flora, turning the dark basin into a churning, violent engine of chitin, skin, and wing. Every square inch of the pale bark and sharp basalt is contested. Blind, flightless beetles the size of dinner plates bulldoze through the dry leaf-litter, their carapaces scraping terribly against the unweathered glass of the volcanic rock. Above them, the air is a thick soup of leathery, echolocating flyers—creatures somewhere between bats and mantises—that navigate the pitch black via a relentless, overlapping barrage of ultrasonic clicks, shrieks, and buzzes. They collide with the foliage, drop, scramble up the vines, and launch themselves again. 

Nothing here sits still. The ecosystem is hyper-metabolic, driven to madness by the warmth and the crush of sheer numbers. Whole trees suddenly shudder and collapse, hollowed out from the inside in a matter of hours by swarms of pale, thumb-sized wood-grubs, their fall triggering a localized hurricane of panicked, screeching life that redistributes itself to the neighboring canopy in seconds. Packs of slick, long-limbed quadrupeds—hairless and glistening with the condensed vent-sweat—hunt through the low fissures, moving over the razor-sharp basalt with calloused, split-hoofed feet, communicating in barking, percussive chorus that echoes endlessly off the raw stone walls.

In this churning dark, there are no buildings, no walls, and no fire—because nothing dries out enough to burn cleanly, and any clearing is swallowed by creepers and crawling things within a day. Yet there is technology here, woven directly into the frenzy.

If you press through the suffocating curtain of vines, shielding your face from the whirring, biting pellets of airborne insects, you find the snares. They are everywhere, spanning the gaps between the basalt crags and the bloated tree trunks. Something with hands is using the environment against itself. 

Great, taut cables of braided gut and fibrous vine are strung across the blind flight-paths of the echolocators, weighted with chunks of raw obsidian. When a heavy flyer hits the line, a wooden toggle snaps, dropping a deadfall of jagged rock that crushes the creature against the ground. In the narrow, steaming trenches where the blind, armored crawlers funnel through the rock, someone has wedged rows of fire-hardened bone spikes and knapped basalt blades, angled to impale anything pushed forward by the crush of the swarm behind it. 

Most striking are the acoustics. Whoever—or whatever—builds these traps understands the deafening noise of the basin. Hollowed-out gourds and stretched bladders are tied into the upper branches where the thermal updrafts are strongest. As the hot, damp wind rushes past them, they emit a low, thrumming howl that mimics the mating or distress calls of the valley’s mega-insects, drawing roaring, chittering tornado-funnels of life directly into waiting nets of thorny bramble. 

You never see the trappers. In the dark, amidst the turbulent, roaring press of bodies, they are just another shape moving fast over the sharp stone. You only find the evidence of their frantic harvesting: piles of cracked chitin, neatly butchered joints hanging briefly in the hot sulfur vents to cure before the scavengers can find them, and the constant, rhythmic *clack-clack-clack* of stone striking stone somewhere in the pitch black, knapping a fresh edge amidst the deafening roar of the jungle.
