The water here does not have a surface, because the room has no ceiling that isn't already underwater. 

It is a pocket no larger than a root cellar, wedged into the fresh, jagged fault-crack of a drowned basalt shelf. When the rock split—recently, by the geology of the place; the stone faces are still razor-sharp, unrounded by silt, their feldspar crystals gleaming like broken glass—the water that rushed in to fill the void came from two different densities, and neither of them behaves quite like water should.

The result is a claustrophobic, cold cistern packed to the absolute limit with dark, rubbery life. There is no open water to swim through. To move from one jagged wall to the other—a distance of perhaps nine feet—you have to part a solid, wet curtain of vegetation. 

The flora is an entanglement of abyssal shade-plants, thick and cartilaginous, thriving in the near-total absence of light. There are bladder-fronds the color of bruised plums, heavy with gas that isn't air, pulling them taut toward the upper corners of the crevice. Beneath them grow dense, suffocating mats of black-green liverworts and blind, white root-lattices that feed directly off the mineral leach of the freshly split basalt. The plants grow into and through one another with frantic, slow-motion aggression. A pale,segmented stalk will pierce straight through the leathery leaf of a neighbor, not killing it, just fusing with it until the whole pocket is one continuous, cold, slippery organ of interlocking foliage.

The only light—and it is barely light, more like a rumor of visibility—comes from the "veins" in the broken stone. Where the rock fractured, it exposed seams of a wet, chalky mineral that reacts poorly to the reality of the water. These seams shed a faint, sourceless violet grease that drifts upward in slow threads, getting caught on the underside of the crowded leaves. You don't see objects here; you see the wet, shiny edges of things where the purple grease clings to them, outlining the serrations of a fern or the sharp, unweathered corner of a stone.

Because the space is so small and the water so cold, the weirdness of the pocket has nowhere to dissipate. It pools in the corners. 

Gravity in this cleft is undecided. The water is so saturated with dissolved stone and the thick mucilage of the crowded plants that it has developed a grain, like wood. In the lower left corner of the chamber, the water flows downward, heavy as mercury, pooling around a small, drowned shrine made of stacked, flat slates. Three feet to the right, the water is turned sideways; the broad, dark leaves of the water-cabbages strain toward the eastern wall as if that wall were the sky, their roots dangling horizontally into the thicket. 

Scattered through the dense press of stems are the remnants of whoever knew this crack was here before it fully flooded—or perhaps they built *after* it flooded, working by touch in the freezing dark. There are no machines, no metals, just things made by hands using what was available. Tied into the knots of the black roots are little cages woven from split whalebone and stiffened kelp, each no bigger than a fist. Inside some of the cages are smooth, gray river stones from somewhere far above, placed there for reasons that don't translate into the logic of the upper world. Near the center of the thicket, wedged between two raw, sharp slabs of basalt, is a low wall of dry-stone masonry, four courses high, leading nowhere. The plants have already bound it together, their pale holdfasts creeping into the chinks between the stones, cementing the ruin into the living mass. 

The weirdness is sharpest around these built things. Where a woven bone cage touches a bladder-frond, the water between them sometimes forgets to be liquid. It turns into a faceted, gelatinous solid for a few hours, trapping a single bubble of gas in a square shape before slowly melting back into the cold broth. If you follow a particular root with your fingers in the dark, tracing it from the stone wall into the center of the room, you will sometimes find both ends of the root anchored in the same spot, and a middle that loops through a space that feels much wider and colder than the nine-foot room allows, as if the plant is reaching through a hole in the back of the water to drink from a different winter entirely.

It is almost silent. The dense packing of the leaves deadens all vibration from the wider ocean outside the crack. There is no surf, no current-roar. The only sounds are small, intimate, and slightly wrong. Every few minutes, as the slow, patient pressure of the growing plants shifts a piece of the fresh basalt, the rock gives a sharp, crystalline *tink*—a sound too dry and high-pitched for an underwater place, like a glass snapping in an empty bedroom. Between those clicks, there is only the wet, rubbery *creak* of thick stems rubbing against each other in their glacial race for space, and a low, rhythmic *thrum* that seems to come from the little slate shrine, though nothing inside it is moving.

Animal life is almost entirely squeezed out by the botany and the broken physics, save for one or two residents. Clinging to the underside of the horizontal leaves is a single, saucer-sized creature that looks like a cross between a chiton and a wet grey ear. It has no eyes. It moves perhaps an inch a day, grazing not on the plants, but on the violet, glowing grease that leaks from the stone fractures. As it eats the light, its shell briefly turns transparent, showing a spiral of dark, slow organs that are arranged in the exact same geometry as the little bone cages tied into the roots. 

Occasionally, a pale, thread-thin worm—six feet long and no thicker than a sewing needle—draws itself through the packed foliage. It doesn't swim; it threads itself straight through the solid matter of the leaves and the soft slate of the shrine alike, passing through stone and cellulose without leaving a hole behind it, a slow white stitch holding the cramped, dark room together. 

Nothing hurries. The cold preserves the sharpness of the rocks; the darkness keeps the plants from blooming into anything showy; the sheer density of the thicket holds the little stone walls in place long after they should have toppled. It is a tiny, freezing knot of wet matter, slowly digesting its own geometry in the dark.
