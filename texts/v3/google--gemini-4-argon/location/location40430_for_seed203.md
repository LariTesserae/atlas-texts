There is no rock here. If there is a true crust to the world, it lies miles further down, beneath an unfathomable depth of root-plate, or miles above, sealed off by the blind, pale ceiling. 

What exists instead is the Hollow: a subterranean void of continental scale, carved not out of stone, but out of living, hyper-pressurized wood. 

The scale is dizzying, almost sickening. You can stand on the jutting lip of a freshly split timber-shelf and look out into fifty miles of empty, freezing air. The space is so immense that the atmosphere curdles into its own weather—great, slow-turning gyres of mist that never quite coalesce into rain, drifting between massive structural columns of pale, fibrous growth that rise from the abyss and vanish into the roof three miles overhead. Despite the depth, it is blindingly bright. The light comes from the vascular tissue of the wood itself. Every surface, every soaring buttress and arched rib, is threaded with arteries of cold, actinic white sap. Where the bark is thin or freshly torn, the glare is as fierce as snow-blindness, casting sharp, multiple shadows across the vast, empty basins.

It is a young landscape, and it is violently unstable. The vegetation that forms the world is in a state of frantic, turbulent growth and catastrophic self-correction. Because the air is near-freezing, the wood grows brittle under its own massive tension. The environment is constantly reshaping itself. You can watch a bridge of braided, alabaster roots—each thick as a river barge—thrust across a two-mile chasm in the span of an afternoon, driven by hydraulic surges of glowing sap. By nightfall, the temperature drops another five degrees, the sap freezes and expands, and the entire span shatters. 

The sound of this is strange. Because the void is so large and the air so thin and cold, the catastrophic snapping of a ten-thousand-ton root five miles away registers only as a dull, low *thump* that you feel in your teeth, followed minutes later by the dry, hissing whisper of splintered wood cascading down into the deeper trenches. Most of the time, the world is governed by a tense, watchful quiet: the faint *tick-tick-tick* of contracting bark, the high creak of timber under unthinkable load, and the soft drip of condensation thawing where the glowing sap-veins run closest to the surface.

The terrain underfoot is sharp and raw. Since there is no soil and no true erosion—no rivers to smooth the edges, no wind-borne sand to grind them down—the ground is a jagged anarchy of fresh splinters, heaving burl-domes, and deep, clean-edged fissures that smell sharply of turpentine, cucumber, and ozone. Pale, frost-rimmed mosses and sprawling mats of translucent lichen rapidly colonize the fresh breaks, drinking the spilled light and moisture before the wood scars over. In the hollows where snapped timber has piled up, pools of icy, mineral-heavy water collect—the runoff of millions of sweating, breathing pores.

Life here is sparse, swallowed by the sheer volume of the space. Occasionally, a flock of pale, membrane-winged creatures—something between a manta and a moth, their bodies no thicker than a leaf—will detach from a glowing cliff-face and ride a thermal updraft caused by a rupturing sap-vein, vanishing silently into the glare. In the deep, jagged valleys of the splinter-fields, long-legged, bristle-furred browsers move with extreme caution. They have hooves shaped like chisels to pry into the bark for the warm, sugary cambium beneath, and their ears are enormous, constantly swiveling to catch the first high-pitched *ping* of wood about to shear.

Scattered across this shifting, luminous immensity are the traces of those who try to live in it. They cannot build on the floor for long; the floor will heave, split, or fold over itself within a month. Instead, their presence is marked by suspended architecture of the most primitive, desperate kind. 

High up in the crooks of the mega-roots, or dangling beneath the overhang of a weeping callus-ridge, hang small, basket-like dwellings woven from supple, freshly harvested whip-vines and lashed together with braided sinew. They look like wasp nests or weaver-bird pods, utterly dwarfed by the titanic, glowing pillars they cling to. The inhabitants use no metal—there is no ore to mine in a world made entirely of plant matter. Their tools are carved from "iron-burl," the dense, blackened wood formed where the great roots twist and crush against one another under tectonic pressure. 

You see their marks everywhere, faint and temporary: ladder-notches hacked into a towering wall of white bark that has since grown thirty feet higher, stretching the notches into long, weeping scars; primitive wooden wedges driven into a fissure to tap the glowing sap for warmth and drink; cairns of stacked, jagged heartwood left on high ridges as directional markers, half of them already toppled by the relentless trembling of the substrate. Sometimes, walking through a newly formed valley of shattered, frost-rimed timber, you will find a rope bridge dangling from a cliff face, its far end snapped and hanging limp in the bright, cold air—the cliff it was tied to having pulled away and dropped into the lower dark days ago. 

The air tastes of winter and crushed leaves. Far out in the blinding white expanse, a column of wood the size of a mountain slowly twists, groans, and splits down its center, releasing a plume of glowing mist that rises, freezes into diamond dust, and drifts lazily across the void.
