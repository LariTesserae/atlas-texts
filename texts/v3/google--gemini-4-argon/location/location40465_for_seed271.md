The space is a natural amphitheater of pale, yellow-grey limestone, roughly the size of an old quarry or a sunken courtyard. The walls do not rise in sharp cliffs, but in bulging, pillowed tiers that curve inward toward the top, leaving only a narrow, ragged slot of sky sixty feet overhead. Through this opening, no direct sunlight ever reaches the floor. Instead, a diffuse, pewter-colored twilight filters down, giving the stone a soft, chalky luminescence that does not seem to shift with the hours.

Because the rock is warm and the air is trapped, the basin holds a constant, mild heat, like the inside of a greenhouse long after the sun has gone down. There is no wind. The air hangs motionless, heavy with the mineral scent of wet lime and old dust.

The stone itself has been worked by water for so long that every sharp angle has been dissolved away. Fluted channels and smooth, spoon-shaped hollows run down the walls, though no water flows through them now. Instead, moisture exists here as a slow, perpetual sweat. The stone is cool enough at a certain depth to draw condensation from the warm air, or perhaps groundwater is pressing slowly through the porous rock from some distant source. Whatever the cause, the walls are dark in patches with a thin film of dampness. Every few minutes, a single droplet gathers on the underside of an overhang, swells until its weight overcomes its grip, and falls.

The floor of the basin is mostly flat, covered in a compacted layer of pale silt and smooth, kidney-shaped pebbles that have been worn down to their final, stable forms. In the center, three shallow basins have been dissolved into the bedrock. They are filled to the lip with water so still and clear that the pools are only visible by the slight darkening of the stone beneath them and the occasional shiver of their surfaces when a drop strikes from above. 

Here and there, widely separated across the pale floor, sit low, dry-stacked walls of flat limestone slabs. They are no more than knee-high now, their mortar—if they ever had any—long since washed into the silt. Time and dampness have fused the stacked stones together with a thin skin of calcium carbonate, rounding their corners until they look almost like natural ridges of the floor. Near the back wall, three steps have been carved directly into a sloping shelf of bedrock, leading up to a flat ledge that holds nothing at all. In a dry alcove nearby, the bottom half of a thick-walled, unglazed clay jar sits wedged between two stones, half-filled with calcified silt. 

Nothing moves among these remains. There are no insects skating on the pools, no lizards on the warm rocks, no birds nesting in the high crevices. The water is too mineral-heavy, or the enclave too isolated, to draw anything in from the outside. Without animals to disturb the silt or wind to blow in debris, the floor remains immaculate, unmarked by tracks or burrows.

Life is limited to a few slow, silent forms that require almost nothing. In the dampest flutes of the walls, thin smears of dark green algae stain the rock like brushstrokes. Out of the hairline cracks near the base of the cliffs grow occasional tufts of maidenhair spleenwort, their black stems wiry and sparse, their small green leaves hanging motionless in the dead air. A few patches of pale, crusty lichen map the drier faces of the stacked stone walls. Because there are no seasons in this sheltered hollow, the ferns do not brown or die back, and they do not spread; they have occupied the exact crevices where moisture and dim light meet in sufficient measure, and they go no further.

The sound of the place is entirely the sound of its own geometry. Because the rock walls curve inward, the acoustics are sensitive and hollow. A single drop of water falling from the ceiling into one of the floor basins makes a sharp, clear *plip* that rings softly against the back of the alcove and dies away instantly. Five seconds later, from the opposite side of the room, another drop strikes wet stone with a duller *tick*. Between these small impacts, the silence is absolute, thick and warm, holding the stone walls and the empty steps in a state of permanent, unhurried rest.
