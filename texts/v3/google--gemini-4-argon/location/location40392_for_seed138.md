The boundary of the place is absolute, defined on all four sides by the sheer, unweathered faces of the quarry walls and the blackened brick backs of the tallow-works and the municipal turbine house. It is a sunken basin, no larger than a cathedral nave or a village square, excavated perhaps fifteen years ago from dark, vitreous basalt. Because the rock was blasted rather than worn away by water or wind, every ledge and fracture remains knife-sharp. The stone has not had time to soften or crumble; its planes are clean, geometric, and slick with a permanent film of warm condensation.

Above, the sky is reduced to a gray, rectangular lid of low-hanging industrial fog and coal-smoke, held down by its own heavy humidity. It does not rain here, nor does the sun ever break through to cast a sharp shadow, yet everything is perpetually wet. The air is an exhausting, tropical broth—ninety-eight degrees at noon and ninety-eight degrees at midnight—fed by the six cast-iron exhaust flues that jut from the base of the turbine house wall. These pipes, each the diameter of a hogshead, do not belch violently; they breathe out a continuous, pressurized sigh of spent steam and scalding condensate that collects in the basin’s low floor.

The floor itself is a wide, flat expanse of black mud, volcanic gravel, and ankle-deep pools of warm, oil-rainbowed water. The layout of the infrastructure within the pit is oddly spacious. There is no clutter. Three massive, brick-built condensing towers stand at wide intervals, linked by elevated catwalks of riveted iron grating that span the emptiness between them. The towers are slick with a microscopic sheen of wet soot, their mortar joints still crisp and unchipped. Beneath them, great cast-iron sluice gates and manifold valves sit in the warm mire like solemn, stationary monuments, spaced twenty or thirty yards apart with nothing between them but shallow, steaming puddles and angular heaps of blasted traprock. 

The light is the color of tarnished pewter, supplemented day and night by a dozen carbon-arc lamps suspended from high gantry poles. Each lamp is encased in a wire cage and a frosted glass globe sticky with moisture; they cast wide, overlapping cones of dim, buzzing, violet-white illumination that never wavers. Because the natural light is so weak and the arc lamps never turn off, the passage of hours is invisible. The basin exists in a state of suspended, greasy twilight.

The sound is equally constant. It is not the clatter of a working factory with shifts and whistle-blows, nor the clamor of a street, but the deep, unyielding roar of a system running at steady load. The turbines behind the north wall produce a bass vibration that lives in the soles of the feet and sets a permanent, geometric ripple across the surface of every puddle. Over this foundation lies the high, white-noise hiss of the steam vents and the hollow, metallic drumming of condensed water dripping from the high catwalks onto the iron housings below. *Clack-drip. Clack-drip.* It is loud enough that two people standing on the lower grating would have to cup their hands and shout into each other's ears to be heard, yet because the pitch and volume never alter, the ear eventually accepts it as a form of heavy silence.

Nothing grows in the basalt rubble except where the hot water pools around the masonry piers. There, thin, pale-green mats of thermophilic algae wave slowly in the current of the runoff, and a few stunted, wire-stemmed ferns cling to the weep-holes in the brick high above, their fronds stiff and coated in coal dust.

Yet the basin is teeming with movement. The sheer heat and the absence of predators have turned the wet industrial floor into a sanctuary for tough, scavenging life. The warm shallows are thick with dark, armored catfish and pale, blind eels that thrash lazily around the outflow grates, feeding on the organic grease and tallow-scum that washes down from the rendering plant's drains on the eastern wall. Thousands of large, mottled toads—their skin bumpy and black as the slag—sit in rows along the warm iron flanges of the pipes, barely blinking in the dim light, their throats pulsing in unison with the turbine hum.

Above them, the air between the warm brick walls is a slow whirlpool of midges, steam-flies, and heavy-bodied beetles, which in turn feeds a dense, permanent colony of chimney swifts and dark-feathered starlings. The birds nest in every rectangular bolt-hole and girder-joint of the gantry cranes. They do not migrate, and because the arc-lamps draw insects through the night, they do not keep normal hours; at any given moment, dozens of them are darting through the drifting bands of vapor, their wings slick with grease, snatching midges mid-air before returning to the warm iron ledges. Large, rust-colored wharf rats move along the catwalks with the unhurried confidence of domestic cats, drinking from the warm condensation trays and licking the tallow-drips from the brickwork.

Nothing rots here, because the scavengers are too thorough; nothing rusts quickly, because the air is too thick with oil-vapor; nothing erodes, because the water moves only in a slow, flat seep toward the central floor-drain. A wrench dropped onto a pile of sharp basalt five years ago lies there still, coated in a thin skin of warm grease, while a toad sits beside it, waiting for a beetle to drop from the hissing dark above.
