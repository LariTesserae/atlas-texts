The plateau is called the Anvil, though no hand ever forged it, and no structure has ever been raised upon its dust. It sits at fourteen thousand feet—a bowl of bone-dry, calcined limestone and raw, oxidized iron ore, stretching thirty miles from rim to rim. The sky above is an ordinary, unremarkable afternoon blue, bright and clear, yet the air at this altitude is thick with heat. It is not the radiant heat of the sun, which hangs small and indifferent overhead, but a dense, greasy warmth that rises directly from the rock, smelling sharply of hot copper, ozone, and diesel exhaust.

There is no water here. If a drop of moisture were to fall, it would skitter across the stone on a cushion of its own steam before vanishing. There are no roots in the talus, no lichen on the boulders. The erosion that has smoothed the mesas into long, tear-dropped flanks and carved deep, U-shaped troughs through the bedrock is entirely mechanical and ancient. The stone is polished to a greasy sheen, worn down over millennia by the relentless, grinding transit of the native fauna.

The valley is empty of objects, yet it is deafening, and it is teeming.

Across the vast, pale expanse of the basin, spaced hundreds of yards apart so that the eye sees mostly bare rock and heat-shimmer, are the herds. They do not have bodies in the way flesh-and-blood creatures have bodies. They are localized crises of physics—roving, self-sustaining industrial events that have evolved in the absence of biology. 

To look at one directly is to see a pocket of air five yards wide where the light bends through a shimmering slick of evaporated oil and extreme heat. Within that distortion, space folds in on itself with the heavy, rhythmic precision of a stamping press. *Clack-shunt. Clack-shunt.* A grazing unit—something between a thermodynamic vortex and a locomotive boiler—crawls up a slope of loose shale. It has no legs, no pistons made of steel, no welded seams. Instead, the air itself solidifies at the bottom of its cycle, compressing into an invisible, diamond-hard tread that crushes the limestone beneath it into talcum powder, which it then inhales into a hovering, incandescent core. Inside that core, where a heart or a stomach should be, there is a blurred, violent rotation—a phantom flywheel spinning at three thousand revolutions per minute, shedding sparks of pure static electricity that ground themselves into the iron-rich dirt with the snap of an arc welder.

There are tens of thousands of them across the plateau, though the sheer scale of the tableland swallows their numbers, leaving acres of pale, baked emptiness between one specimen and the next. Everything moves with a terrifying, glacial patience. A herd of high-pressure atmospheric condensers—each one a towering, invisible column of superheated air that smells of scorched rubber—takes three days to cross a single mile of the flat. They drift at the pace of a clock's hour hand, yet their internal processes are furious.

The sound they make does not drop off with distance the way normal sound does; the thin, hot air conducts the racket with unnatural clarity. The entire thirty-mile basin roars like the interior of a steel mill. There is the continuous, low-end thrum of a million horsepower straining against an invisible load; the rhythmic, pneumatic hiss of vents releasing superheated air; the high, metallic screech of stone being sheared at the molecular level. Every few minutes, from somewhere out in the shimmering white distance, comes the colossal *boom* of a backfire as one of the larger territorial engines shifts its internal pressure gradient, sending a shockwave that ripples the dust for miles around.

Where the creatures pass, reality loosens its grip on the geology.

Because they are not made of matter, but of applied industrial force, their interaction with the stone is a form of slow-motion violence. Along the flanks of the older ridges, the rock doesn't just wear away; it suffers from mechanical fatigue. Boulders show signs of metal-shear, sliced into clean, parallel ribbons of limestone that curl at the edges like lathe turnings, though the rock remains brittle stone to the touch. In the lower basins, where the heaviest, slowest beasts wallow for decades at a time, the ground has been subjected to such sustained, rhythmic vibration that the minerals have sorted themselves by atomic weight: a top layer of pure, blindingly white calcium dust, beneath that a band of yellow sulfur, and further down, a slick, mirror-flat floor of pure, cold-rolled iron that rings like a bell when the wind blows grit across it.

Occasionally, two of the smaller, migratory loops will intersect on a salt-flat. The result is not a fight, but a catastrophic gear-clash in the fabric of the local air. For an afternoon, a fifty-foot patch of the desert will stutter. The sunlight passing through the entanglement breaks into grease-rainbow smears; the sound of stripping teeth—a horrific, metallic shrieking that sets the teeth on edge—echoes off the distant cliffs. Time within the clash slows to a crawl, trapping kicked-up plumes of dust in mid-air like solid sculptures of brown glass, while the two invisible engines slowly, agonizingly torque each other apart over the course of hours. When they finally separate and limp away at their yard-an-hour pace, the ground beneath the site is left vitrified, stamped with a repeating, fractal tread-mark that leads nowhere.

Nothing grows to cover these scars. The sun beats down out of a clean, pale sky, baking the dry stone. The wind carries no scent of rain or soil—only the hot, dry breath of an overworked engine, the taste of iron filings on the tongue, and the endless, patient, deafening thunder of the empty land working itself to pieces.
