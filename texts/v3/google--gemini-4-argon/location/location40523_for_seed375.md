The whole place is the size of a dry dock or a sunken courtyard, bounded on four sides by sheer, glassy faults of black clinopyroxene that rose from the crust recently enough to keep their razor edges. There is no sky to speak of, only an overhanging shelf of pale, porous pumice a hundred feet up that traps the cool air and filters the outside glare into a persistent, shadowless slate-gray twilight.

Within this quarry-sized basin stands a city built without tools, mortar, or hands. 

It is an urban grid of bone-dry calcium and potassium-salt extrusions, organized into avenues, stepped ziggurats, and cantilevered arches. Because the density of the material is so low, the architecture is mostly empty space—a rigid, skeletal wire-frame of a town. Walls are not solid; they are wide-meshed lattices of brittle white mineral, needle-sharp and unweathered, enclosing vast, hollow volumes of cold air. Three-story towers have the mass of dried thistles. You can look straight through six consecutive buildings and see the black glass cliff at the far end of the basin, viewed through a geometric haze of intersecting white lines.

The silence is absolute and physical. The surrounding pumice and the millions of tiny structural voids in the salt-lattice drink up every vibration. There is no wind, no drip of moisture, no echo. Yet the city is frantically, invisibly busy. 

The dynamic driver of this place is a silent, dry weather cycle of electrostatic charge and thermal drafts. Every few minutes, a pocket of denser, cooler air spills down the black glass cliffs and rolls through the gridded avenues. It carries no dust—the rock is too fresh and sharp to have ground down into powder—Instead, it carries free-floating mineral ions and black, hexagonal flakes of biotite mica. As this invisible front passes through the city, the sparse vegetation reacts in unison.

The plants here are lithophytes that require no water, feeding instead on the static charge between the rock layers. They look like long, charcoal-colored feathers or bundles of dry vanilla beans wedged into the ninety-degree joints of the white latticework. When the cold draft hits a neighborhood, hundreds of these dark fronds snap open simultaneously—a frantic, coordinated *whuff* that makes no sound at all—fanning out to catch the drifting mica flakes and static electricity, then folding back into tight, defensive rods five seconds later. You can watch the shape of the air current move down an avenue by the sudden, silent blooming and collapsing of the dark fronds along the white scaffolds.

And the city itself is still growing, actively maintained by its rare fauna.

They are the Architects—six-legged, spider-like organisms the size of wolfhounds, though they weigh less than a handful of balsa wood. Their bodies are a translucent, parchment-colored chitin, completely dry, operating on a hydraulic system pressurized with nitrogen gas rather than blood. There are perhaps only a dozen of them in the entire basin. They move with terrifying speed along the razor-sharp rooflines and through the hollow interiors of the buildings, their needle-tips never slipping on the fresh mineral. Because of the sound-deadening stone and their own feather-weight, a creature the size of a dog can sprint three feet past your shoulder at thirty miles per hour in dead silence.

When an Architect pauses at an intersection, it lowers its abdomen and extrudes a thread of quick-hardening mineral foam, drawing it upward to form a new pillar, a flying buttress, or a bridge to an adjacent tower. The foam flashes into rigid, sharp-edged crystal on contact with the cool air. They are constantly revising the city. Elsewhere in the basin, another organism—something flat, gray, and slow, like a dry tongue of slate—slides along the base of the older structures, dissolving the calcium struts back into gas with a pale, fuming acid. 

The entire settlement is migrating slowly across the floor of the pit. At the north end, white geometric frames are being rapidly sketched into the empty air by the racing, silent spiders; at the south end, the oldest towers are quietly sublimating into thin, cold vapor, leaving the black volcanic floor clean and sharp behind them.
