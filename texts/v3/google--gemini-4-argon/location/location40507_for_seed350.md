The boundary between the water and the air has failed, leaving a warm, churning bath that fills the entire three-mile basin. It is not quite an ocean and not quite a storm, but a dense, pressurized suspension where sheets of tepid water hang in mid-air like trembling gelatin, separated by pockets of hyper-saturated steam. 

Below, the floor of the basin is brand new—and constantly renewing. Great, jagged plates of dark basalt heave upward from the crust, their edges razor-sharp and completely untouched by erosion. They do not wear down; they simply fracture, thrusting into the murk in fresh, violent steps before the weight of the water and the instability of the fault line drag them back under. Because the rock is young and lacks any silt or sand, the water should be clear, yet it is choked to near-opacity by vegetation and the relentless shedding of industrial flora.

The jungle here does not wait for soil. The moment a fresh blade of rock shears into existence, a mat of fleshy, translucent creepers and towering, broad-leafed ferns locks onto the pristine stone. The plants grow at a terrifying, visible speed, fueled by the ambient heat and the fracturing of local time. A frond will uncoil, reach twenty feet in length, blacken, and dissolve into a slick of chlorophyll within three minutes, only to have four more burst through the same space. The density is claustrophobic; through the dim, olive-tinted gloom, every cubic yard of liquid and steam is packed with thrashing stalks, floating root-balls, and rubbery canopies that overlap like roof tiles. 

There are no fish here. No insects, no amphibians, no birds. The turbulence is too absolute, the acoustic pressure too lethal for soft tissue and nervous systems. 

Instead, the basin is populated by scattered, roaring anomalies of heavy industry that seem to have bled through from another reality—or are being actively dreamed by the basin itself. Half-submerged in a thicket of thrashing kelp-analogues stands a solitary, sixty-foot stamping press made of cast iron and brass. It is connected to no power grid, served by no roads, yet its massive pistons hammer downward into the basalt at three hundred strokes a minute. A quarter-mile away, suspended entirely in a pocket of dense vapor and held aloft by the sheer knot of rubbery vines grown through its scaffolding, a bank of immense copper turbines spins wildly, venting thick yellow grease and blinding arcs of blue electricity into the surrounding wet foliage. 

The sound is a physical wall. It is a deafening, unbroken roar composed of millions of gallons of warm water slamming against sharp, newly birthed stone, layered over the metallic shrieking of unlubricated gears, the concussive *whump* of underwater steam-boilers, and the constant, high-voltage crackle of drowned dynamos. The noise is so heavy it vibrates the teeth in the jaw and turns the surface of every leaf into a trembling standing wave.

Nothing in the basin stays where it is. The reality breakdown manifests as a stuttering, violent spatial drift. The water itself flows in contradictory directions—a warm, syrupy current rushing east passes directly through a boiling vertical updraft without mixing, their borders defined like planes of glass. The scattered machinery shares this instability. A brick smokestack rises out of a trench of jagged obsidian, belching thick coal smoke directly into the water; every few seconds, the smokestack flickers, shifts forty yards to the left, and leaves behind a hollow cylinder of empty space that the water takes several agonizing seconds to remember to fill. When the water finally collapses into the vacuum, the resulting cavitation shockwave shreds a quarter-acre of floating ferns into green mist.

Light has no single source here. There is no sky—only an endlessly receding ceiling of colliding water-masses and dark, oily vapor. What illumination exists is a bruised, dim twilight, generated partly by the bio-luminescence of stressed, hyper-accelerating moss, and partly by the harsh, stuttering glare of industrial arc-lamps that hang from rusted gantries jutting out of the rock walls. The light does not travel straight; it bends around pockets of denser gravity, pooling in the hollows of the young, sharp rocks like spilled mercury before being swept away by the next violent surge of the basin's restless, roaring tide.
