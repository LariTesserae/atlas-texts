The chamber sits forty feet below the scrub-plain, hollowed out of a deep deposit of milk-white selenite and pale yellow limestone. Its ceiling is not stone, but a continuous, naturally fused dome of translucent quartz and gypsum, worn paper-thin by the wind above. Because of this mineral lens, the subterranean vault is flooded with a relentless, shadowless glare. It is brighter down here than on the surface; the stone walls catch the diffused noon light and bounce it back and forth across the empty floor until the air itself feels bleached and mildly feverish.

The chamber is roughly the size of a drydock or a village green, shaped like a broad, flattened gourd. There are no stalactites, no columns, no rubble piles cluttering the center. Millennia of slow, seasonal seepage have dissolved the interior obstacles, leaving a vast, clean concavity. The floor is a single sweeping basin of worn, cream-colored travertine, warm to the touch, smooth as soap, and entirely bare across its central expanse. 

Moisture arrives only through the walls. During the cooler hours, the warm air inside meets the slightly cooler stone near the floor, and the rock sweats. A thin, slick film of mineral-heavy water beads on the flank of the vault and slides down into shallow, meandering runnels no wider than a human thumb. There are no pools, only these damp grooves that cross the floor before evaporating in the dry, bright center of the room. 

Along these wet seams grows the chamber’s only flora: the glass-wort and the wire-fern. The glass-wort forms low, widely spaced cushions of bloated, translucent triangles that look like chipped ice. A yard or two away, a single black stem of wire-fern might rise three feet into the glaring air, completely leafless, terminating in a tight, metallic-green spiral that uncoils by a fraction of an inch each month. They do not crowd each other. Each plant sits in its own wide territory of pale stone, casting no shadow in the omnidirectional glow.

Despite the emptiness of the central floor, the perimeter and the high, concave walls are thick with slow, quiet life. Hundreds of broad-shelled, platter-sized gastropods—their shells calcified to the exact chalky white of the limestone—cling to the curvature of the ceiling and walls. They move at an imperceptible creep, grazing on microscopic films of endolithic algae that live just beneath the translucent skin of the stone. Every few minutes, a dry, ticking click echoes softly across the vault as one shell bumps against another. 

Lower down, where the damp runnels glisten, colonies of long-legged, spindly crabs with pale, unpigmented carapaces stand motionless over the water grooves. They do not scurry. They stand on tiptoe, filtering the warm air with feathery mouthparts, waiting for the tiny, blind midges that breed in the dampness of the glass-wort cushions. When a crab moves, it shifts a single leg, pauses for ten seconds, and shifts another, its joints making a faint, papery rustle that dies instantly in the wide volume of the room.

There are no buildings here, no walls or hearth-rings, because nothing stays here long enough to shelter. Yet the hand of a cautious, tool-using creature is visible at the margins. Along the lowest lip of the western wall, where the stone is softest and the condensation heaviest, someone—or something—has worked the rock with heavy river-cobbles and bone wedges. They have pecked shallow, curving troughs into the stone to redirect the sweat-runnels, gathering the moisture of twenty square feet of wall into a single dripping spout. 

Beneath this spout sits a hollowed-out nodule of flint, left behind to catch the slow drip. Beside it lie three knapped scrapers of dark grey chert, their edges dulled with white gypsum dust, and a digging stick of fire-hardened scrub-oak, polished slick by palms. These tools were clearly used to lever the giant white snails from the walls and to scrape the mineral-rich rind from the stone. In the corner, a neat, small pile of empty shells has been stacked—not thrown, but fitted together like bowls to take up the least possible space, preserving the great, dazzling emptiness of the floor. 

The air smells faintly of wet chalk, warm dust, and the cucumber-like scent of crushed glass-wort. Nothing hurries. A drop of water swells at the tip of the carved stone lip, hangs fat and brilliant with refracted light for three full minutes, and falls into the flint bowl with a single, resonant *plock* that briefly fills the bright hollow, before the dry silence closes back over it.
