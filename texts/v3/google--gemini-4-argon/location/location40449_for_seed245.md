There is no bedrock here, and there has never been dirt. As far out as the eye can follow—until the sightline is swallowed by the low, bruised-ochre ceiling of steam—there is only the Masonry. 

It is a planetary expanse of low-lying, stepped basins, sunken courtyards, and sprawling causeways, all constructed from slick, unbaked clay, hand-struck brick, and bundles of pale, pithy reeds that have no botanical origin in this world. Every square inch of the ground bears the mark of having been made. In the broad, shallow troughs between the levees, the clay is embossed with the sweeping, overlapping arcs of gigantic wooden trowels and the deep, frantic impressions of knuckles and thumbs—millions of them, pressed into the wet mud so recently that the ridges of the skin are still razor-sharp, completely unblunted by wind or rain.

The air is thick, fever-hot, and wet, like the inside of a fermenting kiln. Water does not fall from the sky; it sweats directly out of the architecture. The freshly laid bricks weep a warm, mineral-heavy brine that collects in ankle-deep sheets across the sunken plazas. Because the landscape is constantly heaving and resettling, this water is never still. It sloshes in long, rhythmic bores from one side of a twenty-mile basin to the other, foaming yellow at the edges, carrying rafts of pale mineral scum and stray wood-shavings.

Nothing grows, and nothing crawls, unless one counts the pale, gelatinous skins that form over the warmer puddles like the mother of vinegar, only to be torn apart when the floor shifts. Yet the place is violently, tirelessly busy.

The landscape is still in the act of being built, though there are no builders. 

Every few minutes, a mile-long section of low terrace will shudder. With a sound like a heavy wet linen being snapped in a closed room, a new tier of mud-brick and wattle simply *extrudes* from the existing floor, rising four or five feet in a matter of seconds. As it rises, the clay is visibly worked by unseen pressures—Pinched, smoothed, scored with cross-hatched drainage channels by invisible bone awls, and daubed with hot bitumen. There are no machines, no engines, no iron or wire. The entire continent is a storm of primitive craft executing itself in a frenzy of spontaneous revision.

In the middle distance, sparsely scattered across the vast, flat leagues, stand the larger works: solitary, truncated ziggurats of wet adobe; massive, crude wooden tripods the size of watchtowers bound together with wet rawhide; and endless, knee-high retaining walls that snake across the flats to hold back nothing at all. 

None of them last. The rules of space and permanence here are thin and feverish. A hundred-yard stretch of causeway will suddenly lose its structural logic—the bricks softening not back into mud, but into a grey, tallow-like grease that slides sideways up an incline, defying the flat gravity of the basin, before hardening again into a ring of crude, featureless kiln-chimneys. Elsewhere, the horizon stutters. A solitary timber scaffold will appear three times in a row across a mile of wet clay, each iteration slightly larger and more crooked than the last, as if the world’s memory is skipping like a scratched wax cylinder. 

Despite this ceaseless, boiling rearrangement of matter, the world is terribly quiet. The turbulence does not roar; it *squelches*. The ambient sound is an intimate, suffocatingly close chorus of wet friction: the *slap-slap* of clay being folded over itself, the dull *thrum* of massive hemp ropes tightening around timbers until the wood groans, the hiss of steam escaping a newly opened vent in a mortar joint, and the constant, low trickling of hot water finding new levels across freshly troweled floors. When a massive structure collapses—when a forty-foot cistern of pleated mud suddenly unweaves itself—it makes no crash, only a long, heavy sigh, like a sack of wet grain settling into a corner.

The light has no source. It is a dim, sour-brass luminescence that seems to belong to the steam itself, brightest right at eye level and fading into a charcoal murk both overhead and down in the deeper sumps. Visibility is strange: the air is so dense with moisture that things fifty yards away look like flat, charcoal smudges on wet paper, yet four miles out, a single, newly erected arch of rough-hewn limestone will stand out in crisp, impossible detail, lit by a private, amber glare that doesn't touch the water around its base.

To walk here is to walk on a floor that is still warm from the hand. The bricks give slightly under weight, sighing out little bubbles of marsh-gas and hot water. Behind a traveler, footprints do not fill with silt or wash away; within minutes, the mud simply rises up, pinches itself shut, and smooths the surface over with three brisk, invisible strokes of a wooden float, leaving the vast, steaming expanse pristine, raw, and newly made.
