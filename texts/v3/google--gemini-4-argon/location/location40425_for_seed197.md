The city is the size of a single cathedral nave, and it is entirely hollow. 

There is no dirt here, no bedrock, no stone. The floor, the walls, and the soaring, buttressed ceiling are woven from a single, ancient organism: a calcified, fibrous banyan-vine that grew into the shape of a high-density tenement block centuries ago and has been worn smooth by time. The space is no more than two hundred paces from end to end, bordered by thick, braided walls of pale wood that curve inward at the top to form a perforated vault. Outside this vault, there is nothing—not sky, not ground, just an unfocused, bright beige glare that filters through the canopy lattices like afternoon sun through a blind. 

Despite the total dominance of the plant, the interior is startlingly empty. The architecture consists of thin, taut tendrils and hollowed-out bark facades that mimic the layout of a cramped metropolis—avenues, cul-de-sacs, five-story walk-ups, bridges, and balconies—yet it possesses almost no mass. The walls of the "buildings" are paper-thin membranes of translucent leaf-skin, stretched between load-bearing roots the thickness of a human thumb. You can see the silhouettes of six "streets" away through four layers of apartments. Most of the volume of the room is simply warm, damp air, smelling faintly of crushed celery and wet linen.

The sound, however, is a physical wall. It is a continuous, bone-shaking roar that presses against the eardrums and vibrates in the sternum. 

The wood of the city is dry on the surface, worn down to a satin finish by centuries of friction, yet it weeps from within. Every few minutes, a capillary root overhead swells and sweats a heavy globule of lukewarm water. When the drop falls, it hits the taut, drum-skin membranes of the lower roofs with a report like a pistol shot. Because the entire settlement is a single connected acoustic chamber of hollow gourds, stretched fibers, and dry seed-pod resonators, the sound does not die. It amplifies. 

And the fauna feed the noise. The city is teeming with creatures, though they take up almost no space. Millions of flat, translucent insects—no thicker than a coat of varnish—skitter in synchronized, geometric lanes along the root-highways, their serrated legs rasping against the dry wood. The sound is like ten thousand heavy iron chains being dragged across a boardwalk. In the empty air between the tenement-vines, flocks of skin-winged, eyeless glides dart in rigid, right-angled trajectories, snapping up the flat-bugs. Whenever a glide turns at a ninety-degree angle, its wings produce a sonic crack that echoes through the gourd-chambers as a low, booming *thrum*.

Reality in the nave has worn down to a set of urban habits that the biology performs without knowing why. 

The vines do not grow toward the light; they grow in zoning districts. On the eastern side of the nave, the roots branch exclusively in ninety-degree grids, forming tiny, empty cubicles no larger than breadboxes, thousands of them stacked in sheer, fragile cliffs. Here, the air is slightly drier, and the resident creatures—spindle-legged crab-things made of folded, green chitin—spend their entire lives walking in single file up one side of a root, pausing at an intersection until a specific nodule on the bark turns from pale yellow to bruised purple, and then proceeding down the other side. It is rush hour, stripped of destination or purpose, played out at breakneck speed.

In the center of the floor lies the Plaza, a wide depression where the wood is polished to a mirror shine by the belly-scales of larger, legless lizards that slide across it in intersecting arcs. They never collide, because the space inside the Plaza does not behave consistently. A lizard sliding across the ten-foot span may take twenty minutes to reach the other side, its body stretching like taffy as it crosses a seam where distance is folded thick, while another darts across the same trajectory in a fraction of a second, emerging wet with condensation from a pocket of air that exists three days in the past.

Water collects in the folds of the spatial seams. You can see puddles hanging six inches off the wooden floor, shaped like the hollows of worn cobblestones that aren't there. The puddles vibrate violently with the deafening *thrum-crack-rasp* of the room, their surfaces standing up in sharp, cymatic spikes. Frogs with mouths that open vertically like cellar doors sit inside these floating puddles, adding a wet, metallic *clank* to the din—a sound like a wrench striking a radiator pipe, repeated four times a second.

Everything is weathered into a state of furious, permanent equilibrium. When a section of the tenement-vine rots from the damp, it doesn't fall; the bark dissolves into a swarm of pale, fluttering moths that immediately fly to a gap three stories up and press themselves flat against the wood, hardening back into fibrous wall-membrane within minutes. Predators do not kill prey so much as prune them; a vine-viper strikes a glide out of the air, swallows it, and immediately extrudes a fresh, wet tendril from its tail that grafts itself onto the nearest bridge, reinforcing the city's infrastructure.

There is no silence, no stillness, and no room for anything else, even though the chamber is ninety-nine percent empty air. The light stays at a flat, warm, four-o'clock glare. The rain falls upward from the lower puddles when the barometric pressure drops inside a single cubic foot of a third-floor apartment, drenching the flat-bugs, who immediately change direction and rasp a slightly lower, wetter chord against the floorboards, shifting the key of the entire screaming cathedral down by a half-step.
