The basin is no larger than a cathedral nave, a sunken oval of dark, plumbago-colored slate hemmed in by weeping walls of layered shale. Though the sky above is a flat, unremarkablezinc—offering the plain, even illumination of a coastal Tuesday—the floor of the depression is in a state of frantic, soundless industry.

It is a mud-pot of cold, heavy grease and water, crowded with a thousand stone bowls that have ground against one another for centuries. 

The ground is not solid; it is a dense, jostling mosaic of naturally hollowed limestone basins, ranging from the size of a teacup to the size of a baptismal font, packed rim-to-rim in a thick, buoyant slurry of grey clay and peat-water. Beneath the slate walls, deep subterranean pressures—perhaps tidal, perhaps the venting of cold, non-volcanic gasses—constantly heave and rearrange the mud. The bowls are never still. They rise, tilt, spill their contents into their neighbors, and subside, turning slowly against one another like gears in a clockwork that has lost its arbors. Centuries of this tight, relentless chafing have polished their exteriors to the texture of river-soap and worn their rims paper-thin. 

Despite the chaotic churning of the floor, the place is startlingly quiet. The clay slurry acts as a heavy lubricant, and the water is thick with suspended mica and dark silt, deadening every collision. Stones weighing several tons slide past one another with no more noise than a wet finger drawn across a slate. A three-foot basin of grey limestone tips, dumps forty gallons of pewter-colored water into a lower trough, and rights itself; the only sound is a soft, velvet *glug* that dies within six feet. The turbulence is entirely visual—a hyperactive, boiling geometry happening behind thick glass.

Long ago, someone attempted to harness this cold boiling. Scattered across the shifting floor and bolted into the weeping shale walls are the partial remains of an industrial pumping array. There is no whole building, only iron interventions: thick, flanged cast-iron pipes stained orange with ochre, emerging from the shale only to end in sheared-off couplings; a pair of squat, brick condenser housings slowly sinking at opposing angles in the northeast corner; and a web of slack, grease-blackened steel cables rigged between the walls and heavy iron collars bolted around the largest of the moving stone bowls. 

The machinery is not abandoned, or at least, it has not stopped working. As the stone basins heave and rotate in the mud, they pull at the cables. High up on the wet shale walls, rusted flywheels and rocker arms—slick with condensation and velveted with a thin, coppery moss—jerk into motion, spin furiously for ten seconds, and then reverse direction as the stones below settle into a new configuration. Gauge needles behind cracked, moisture-beaded glass swing wildly from zero to the red line and back again without a whisper. Leather drive-belts, sodden and heavy, slap softly against iron pulleys. What the apparatus was meant to measure or extract is unclear; the pipes merely suck up the grey water from one shifting pocket and jet it smoothly into another, participating in the basin's turmoil without altering it.

The air is cool and smells sharply of wet iron, turnip-cellar damp, and the faint, sweet ozonic tang of old grease. Because the air is so still and damp, condensation forms on every surface, feeding the constant, silent slip of water down the rock faces.

Life here is sparse, adapted to the shear-stress of the moving floor. No rooted tree could survive the grinding of the basins, so vegetation is limited to tough, rootless cushions of black moss that ride the rims of the stones like fenders on a tugboat, absorbing the rare impacts. In the narrow, vertical crevices of the shale walls, just above the reach of the churning mud, grow pale, waxy-leaved saxifrages and stiff, rush-like reeds that tremble whenever a cable-pulley groans in its socket nearby. 

Every so often, a long-legged, slate-colored heron drops into the basin from the flat sky above. It lands on the rim of a heaving stone bowl, riding the tilt and rotation with effortless, uncanny adjustments of its knees, its yellow eyes fixed on the turbulent gaps between the stones. Down in that lubricated dark, blind, alabaster-white eels—the thickness of a man's thumb—navigate the constantly opening and closing channels between the grinding rocks, feeding on the bacterial mats that thrive on the friction-warmed clay. The bird waits while its perch rises two feet into the air, tips thirty degrees, and slides sideways past a rusted steam-chest; then it strikes, swallows, and goes back to waiting in the busy, Dripping silence.
