There is no light here. Not the absence of sun, which implies a sky that could hold one, nor the dimness of a cavern that remembers the surface, but an absolute, foundational blackness so complete that the concept of an eye has never been invented along this entire continental shelf. 

Yet the space is immense. You know the scale of the High Sump not by looking out across it, but by the time it takes for the thunder of a collapsing peat-mesa to travel from the distant rim to where you stand. You hear an impact—a dull, wet, tectonic thud—and then you count four hundred heartbeats through the warm, dripping dark before the shockwave passes through the soles of your boots, followed ten seconds later by the airborne roar, softened and smeared by fifty miles of dense, warm mist.

This is a plateau at the roof of the world, though "roof" means nothing when there is no light above. The air is thin enough to make the lungs work, yet thick with suspended water, warm as the inside of a mouth. It smells of wet iron, crushed mint, ozone, and the deep, musky grease of ten thousand generations of blind things living on top of one another.

The terrain is old. Everything sharp was dissolved ages ago by the warm, perpetual drizzle that condenses out of the upper atmosphere. The bedrock is a porous, weeping limestone that has been slumped and sculpted into vast, terraced bowls, shallow basins miles wide, and long, winding hogbacks of slick clay. Water does not rage here; it seeps, sheets, and pools. Every surface wears a skin of warm moisture. Step off the rounded spine of a ridge, and you sink to the knee in sponge-mire—a dense, springy mattress of pale, rootless tubers and fibrous creepers that drink the mineral-heavy runoff.

Because there is no light to reach for, the vegetation does not grow upward in straight lines. It grows in heavy, tangled mattresses and low, muscular domes that hug the warm stone, feeding on the chemical soup of the groundwater and the ambient heat of the high crust. There are forests of what feel like giant, weeping ferns, their fronds thick as leather aprons and slick with mucilage, constantly uncurling and slapping softly against one another in the warm updrafts. Between them hang curtains of velvet-skinned bladder-vines, swollen with warm water and fermenting gas. When they grow too heavy, they rupture with a wet, hollow *pop* that scatters spores into the mist, adding a sour, yeasty note to the air.

Through this vast, wet dark, the machines work.

They are not clustered in cities. They are scattered at intervals of five or ten miles across the endless bog-terraces—solitary, towering rigs of cast iron and weeping brass, left to run on centuries-old instructions. You find them by their rhythm. Long before you touch the trembling slick of oil on the surface of a marsh, you feel the sub-audible thrum in your ribcage: *thump-hiss, thump-hiss, clack-clack-clack*. 

Up close, a rig is a deafening mountain of hot metal and slick condensation. Great flywheels, thirty feet across, spin at a blurred, terrifying speed in the pitch black, flinging warm water droplets from their rims like shrapnel. Massive pistons plunge into the limestone crust, pumping up the heavy, sulfur-rich brine from the deep faults, cycling it through cooling towers of slatted wood that waterfall continuously into the surrounding mire. There are no lamps on the catwalks. The builders did not need them, or the builders are gone and the bulbs burst three hundred years ago. The only illumination—if a creature with eyes were brought here to go mad—would be the occasional, violent blue-white crackle of a high-voltage arc jumping across wet porcelain insulators high in the mist, freezing a hundred square yards of dripping fern-domes and churning steel in a microsecond of stark, shadowless violet before the black slams shut again.

The ecosystem has swallowed the industry whole and made it part of the weather. The heat radiating from the boiler-houses creates permanent thermal plumes, drawing the warm, wet air upward until it hits the colder strata high above the plateau, condensing into localized, torrential downpours that fall straight back onto the roofs of the engine-sheds in a roaring, self-sustaining loop of warm rain.

Around these warm, vibrating iron legs, the fauna is thickest. The dark is crowded with motion. You cannot walk ten paces without brushing against something living, or hearing the wet scatter of dozens of heavy bodies giving way before you. 

Nothing here is silent, because silence is invisibility, and invisibility is for places with light. Everything announces itself, maps the world, hunts, and mates through sound and vibration. 

High in the latticework of the cooling towers, colonies of leathery, six-limbed crawlers—the size of terriers, with ears like satellite dishes—cling to the vibrating struts, clicking their heavy mandibles in synchronized, metallic choruses that mimic the *clack-clack* of the check-valves. They use the machine's own roar as sonar, reading the way the low-frequency engine-hum bounces off the soft bodies of the large, slug-like grazers moving through the fern-mats below. 

In the knee-deep warm water of the terraced pools, long, segmented amphibians with skins like wet velvet lie in tangled heaps, their throats inflating and deflating to produce a continuous, overlapping bass drone—a chord so thick and resonant it makes the water surface dance in standing waves. Predators move through the mire by matching their footsteps to the exact rhythm of the nearest steam-hammer, masking the splash of their approach beneath the industrial beat until they are close enough to smell the heat of their prey’s blood.

To stand in the High Sump is to be submerged in an ocean of warm, wet noise. There is no horizon, only depth of sound. In the immediate foreground: the constant, heavy patter of warm drops falling from broad leaves onto broad leaves; the squelch of living mud; the hot, rhythmic sigh of a pneumatic valve fifty yards to your left; the dry, papery rustle of a thousand blind beetles shifting simultaneously across the bark of a creep-tree. Beyond that, a mile out: the whistling roar of a high-pressure steam vent blowing off into the bog, surrounded by the high, flute-like trilling of the long-necked waders that nest in its scalding perimeter. And beneath and beyond it all, forty miles out across the dark plateau: the endless, rolling drum-roll of the great condenser-engines on the rim, mixing with the natural thunder of the high, sightless sky, everything wet, everything warm, everything moving in the dark.
