It is a room-sized blister in the lithosphere, forty feet below a dry scrub plain that has no idea it is there. 

The cavity is not old enough to have been worn down by water or time; it is still in the process of arriving. The walls are composed of a damp, unhardened white mineral—something between tallow, chalk, and wet porcelain—that extrudes itself out of nothingness in slow, silent convulsions. There are no stalactites, because nothing has dripped long enough to form them. Instead, the rock pushes outward in fresh, bulbous lobes and razor-sharp geometric pleats that have never known friction. If you press a thumb into the wall, it leaves a print that slowly fills back in from behind, warm for a second before dropping to the ambient, cellar-like chill of the room.

Despite being sealed beneath yards of solid overburden, the pocket is blindingly bright. The light has no source; it is a property of the air itself, a relentless, shadowless noon trapped in a subterranean lung. Because the light comes from everywhere at once—even from the gaps between grains of dirt—nothing in the chamber casts a shadow. Depth perception fails here. A wall ten feet away looks as though it is pressed directly against the cornea, until something crawls across it and gives the eye a reference for scale.

Water does not flow here; it condenses out of the bright air in thick, heavy beads that refuse to run together. It clings to the undersides of the emerging rock-folds like spheres of mercury, trembling when the walls shift. The air smells intensely of wet lime, ozone, and crushed celery.

Into this raw, un-eroded space, someone—or something with hands—has tried to establish an order, though the architecture is as embryonic and confused as the geology. Scattered across the floor are four or five low cairns made of unbaked clay bricks, and a single, freestanding doorway built of pale driftwood pegged together with bone. The doorway stands three feet high and leads from one side of a damp chalk blister to the other. The geometry of the space disagrees with the structures: if you look through the driftwood doorframe from the left, the space beyond is empty; if you look through it from the right, there is a small, three-sided clay kennel sitting directly in the frame. Neither the cairns nor the doorframe show any sign of tool marks, rot, or age. They look as though they were grown or dreamed into the room five minutes ago.

The floor between these sparse monuments is mostly bare, white, and damp, save for a few scattered tufts of wire-stiff, translucent grass. Each blade grows in a tight corkscrew, drinking the bright, sourceless light and the heavy condensation. When a blade reaches six inches in height, its tip simply ceases to exist—not eaten, not withered, just terminating at an invisible plane in the air where the rules of the room decide that grass no longer happens.

What fills the pocket—what makes it seethe despite the vast, empty gaps between the cairns and the grass—is the fauna. The chamber is choked with life, all of it operating in a state of frantic, dead-silent industry.

Because the acoustics of the room are broken, sound does not travel more than an inch from its source. A pebble falling from an extruding wall hits the floor with a dull *thock* that can only be heard if your ear is pressed against the stone beside it. Consequently, the frantic ecosystem plays out like a film with the audio track stripped away.

Thousands of creatures the size of thumbs—things that are halfway between salt-water shrimp and skinless birds, with too many joints in their pale legs and pulsing, translucent gizzards—scuttle in complex, braided migration paths across the white walls. They feed on the "new" stone, scraping away the mineral tallow at the exact moment it crosses the threshold from non-existence into matter. When a wall bulges outward in a sudden birth-spasm, hundreds of these pale grazers rush the site in absolute silence, their mandibles working furiously, harvesting the rock before it can fully solidify into three-dimensional space.

Hunting them are larger, flatter organisms that look like wet, gray handkerchiefs of muscle and eyes. They do not crawl; they translate from one damp patch of stone to another by sliding *through* the unformed geometry of the room. A hunter will slip under a bead of water on the ceiling and emerge instantly from a bead of water on the driftwood doorframe ten feet below, wrapping itself around three or four of the bird-shrimp and digesting them externally in a rapid, boiling froth of clear enzymes. 

The cycle is hyper-accelerated. The grazers mate, lay soft, square eggs in the crevices of the clay cairns, hatch, and reach maturity in the span of twenty minutes. The handkerchief-predators divide by tearing themselves in half on the sharp, un-eroded edges of the rock pleats whenever they grow too heavy. When anything dies and drops to the floor, it does not rot; the emerging floor simply rises up around the carcass, incorporating the bone and chitin directly into the white, tallow-like stone, which immediately begins to bulge and extrude a new pleat, drawing a fresh swarm of grazers.

Occasionally, a weather system occurs inside the twenty-foot room. The relentless brightness dims by a fraction—just enough to make the eyes ache—and the air pressure drops until the water beads on the walls flatten out. Then, for thirty seconds, it rains upward from the floor, the heavy drops lifting off the white mineral and plastering themselves against the ceiling, carrying tiny, kicking shrimp-creatures with them. The corkscrew grass untwists itself with a faint, dry hiss—the only sound that seems capable of piercing the room's acoustic dampening—before snapping back into coils as the brightness returns to full, glaring strength.

Through it all, the little driftwood doorway stands in the center of the floor, damp and shadowless, framing a small clay house from one angle and blank white rock from the other, while the room quietly, furiously digests and rebuilds itself around it.
