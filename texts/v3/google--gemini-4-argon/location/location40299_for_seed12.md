High in the teeth of a newly thrust mountain range, a volcanic caldera holds a warm, mineral-dense sea. Because the rock here is so young—barely cooled from the mantle, never touched by wind or rain before the basin filled—there is no silt, no sand, and no rounded pebbles. Everything beneath the surface is razor-sharp, glassy, and angular. Hexagonal basalt columns, fresh obsidian flows, and jagged fault-steps drop into the warm gloom in crisp, unweathered geometry. 

Yet the water is not clear; it is a dim, olive-amber broth, thick with dissolved silicates and suspended volcanic ash that refuses to settle. Sunlight hits the surface two miles above sea level and immediately scatters into a sourceless, bruisy twilight. 

Suspended within this warm, dim volume is a city that does not rest on the bottom, nor float on the top. It is a three-dimensional settlement of baskets, terraces, and woven conduits, tethered midway down the caldera walls where the thermal vents keep the water at blood-temperature. 

The architecture is entirely handmade from two materials: raw, freshly flaked obsidian and the woody, buoyant stalks of the *siphon-kelp* that chokes the basin. Because the water has not yet worn the stone, the builders can pry long, pristine laths of basalt directly from the cliff faces using simple wooden wedges that swell when wet. They lash these stone beams together with kelp-cordage to create hovering platforms, polyhedral cages, and stepped aqueducts that guide currents of warmer, mineral-rich water through the district.

There is no iron here, no kiln-fired brick, no wheels. Technology is an extension of weaving and stone-knapping. Dwellings are double-walled domes of plaited reeds plastered with a grey, self-setting mortar made from volcanic ash and crushed snail shells. Inside these structures, pockets of breathable air are trapped like inverted bowls, replenished by gas-bladders harvested from the surrounding flora. To move from the air-lock of one house to the market-lattice thirty feet below requires slipping into the bath-warm water and pulling oneself along thick, slimy guide-ropes made of braided vine, weighted at the waist with rough chunks of pumice and basalt to achieve neutral buoyancy.

The city and the forest are difficult to tell apart. The vegetation here is lush, adapted to the low light and high mineral content. Enormous, broad-leaved ferns with translucent fronds the size of sails grow directly out of the woven walls of the houses, their roots anchoring the mortar. Rope-like vines, thick as a man’s thigh, stretch across the open avenues between tower-clusters, bearing pale, gourd-like fruits filled with buoyant oil and sweet gas. The builders do not clear this growth; they braid it as it grows, training the living stems into arches, suspension bridges, and screens that divide the open water into neighborhoods and courtyards.

The scale of the place is vast enough that the far side of the caldera is invisible through the amber murk. Looking outward from a residential terrace, one sees only layers of silhouettes: the dark, jagged profile of a stone-and-wicker tower fifty yards away; behind it, the fainter cross-hatching of a kelp-plantation; and beyond that, nothing but the warm, olive-colored void. Upward, the surface of the lake is hidden behind a ceiling of floating bladder-weed that keeps the heat in and the mountain winds out, turning the sky into a mottled, bronze canopy. Downward, the tether-lines and anchor-columns vanish into black, unmapped depth where the water grows scalding.

Sound behaves strangely in this dense, submerged urbanism. It travels fast and comes from everywhere at once, yet the city is profoundly quiet. There are no footsteps, no clatter of wheels, no wind in the leaves. Instead, there is a constant, low-frequency pressure—the slow groan of the young mountain settling on its faults, and the soft, rhythmic *thrum* of thermal vents exhaling bubbles the size of carts far below. Nearer at hand, the noises are small and crisp: the sharp *clack* of an artisan flaking a fresh obsidian blade underwater; the creak of wet wicker straining against a buoyant load; the muffled, resonant thud of a wooden mallet driving a peg into a basalt fissure.

Animal life is scarce in this young, isolated altitude. Occasionally, a pale, flat-bodied salamander the length of a canoe glides on undulating frills through a street-canyon, ignoring the woven houses entirely. In the agricultural matrices, giant, slow-moving gastropods with shells of black volcanic glass graze on the sulfur-mats cultivated on stone slabs. They move so slowly they appear to be architectural ornaments, taking a full day to cross a single terrace.

Everything here happens at the pace of the snails and the growing vines. Currents are weak, driven only by the gentle gradient between the hot fissures below and the cooler canopy above. Debris does not fall; it drifts downward over hours, catching on the dense filigree of leaves and rope. When a builder drops a stone tool, it sinks with a dreamlike, syrupy reluctance into the dark. The city is still emerging, its inhabitants patiently splitting the raw, unweathered bone of the mountain peak and knotting the warm jungle around it, suspended in a quiet, amber amniotic sea at the roof of the world.
