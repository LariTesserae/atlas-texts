The valley is a shallow, bowl-shaped depression four miles across, though the margins are never visible through the slate-colored gloom. The floor is not earth, nor is it mud; it is a crowded, knee-high forest of upright, overlapping stone fins. They look like roof tiles made of dark blue-gray shale, jammed vertically into the ground by the millions, leaning against one another at slight, uniform angles. There is no soil between them, no moss, not a single blade of grass or thread of lichen. The rock is fresh and unworn, its edges razor-sharp and splintered, as though the entire valley floor were a single colossal stratification that buckled and shattered yesterday. 

Because the air is cold and thick with an inexhaustible, motionless fog, every stone surface is slick. Water does not fall as rain here; it simply materializes out of the heavy atmosphere, beading on the sharp edges of the shale and running down into the narrow, jagged slots between the stones. Beneath the packed fins, a few inches down, lies a shallow, invisible water table. 

Despite the stillness of the air and the slow, patient seepage of the moisture, the valley is deafening. 

The sound comes from the stone itself. As the cold, mineral-heavy water trickles between the tightly packed plates of shale, it pools in microscopic hollows and subterranean pockets, altering the tension of the freshly fractured rock. Every second, across the four-mile expanse, thousands of thin stone plates are shifting by fractions of a millimeter—settling, binding, and snapping against their neighbors. A single snap sounds like the crack of a dry knuckle or the ring of a struck anvil. Multiplied by millions, and echoing off the millions of wet, hard surfaces, the valley produces a continuous, roaring clatter. It sounds like a torrential hailstorm falling on a tin roof, or an endless avalanche of porcelain, though nothing appears to be moving at all. The noise is immense, metallic, and completely devoid of rhythm.

Light is scarce, filtering through the low, woolen ceiling of cloud as a sour, pewter-colored twilight. It catches only on the wet films covering the stone, turning the landscape into a dense mosaic of dull, greasy reflections. 

Walking here is nearly impossible without protection; the densely packed fins will slice boots to ribbons and snap ankles in their narrow, flooded crevices. Yet there are paths, of a sort. Scattered at wide intervals across the valley are low, dome-like cairns built by stacking the broken shale horizontally. These dry-stone huts have no mortar and no doors—only low, narrow crawlspaces facing away from the center of the valley. Inside, the stacked stones dampen the deafening clatter of the outside world to a heavy, vibrating hum. 

Around these huts, and stringing between them like low fences, are the "walkways": long corridors where the vertical fins have been painstakingly snapped off at the base and laid flat, creating narrow, precarious pavements of slick rock that wind through the dense fields of upright blades. No one lives in the huts now, or perhaps they are only used for a few days every century. Inside them lie occasional, inexplicable arrangements: spirals of pale, rounded river stones brought from somewhere far away, or heavy slabs of slate propped up on bone-white pebbles to act as crude, resonant gongs, though their purpose in a world already so loud is impossible to guess.

Life here is sparse, slow, and adapted to the mineral crowd. There are no plants to anchor a food chain; instead, the water itself carries a faint, milky suspension of chemosynthetic bacteria that smells faintly of sulfur and wet iron. 

Feeding on this film are the valley’s only visible inhabitants. Every hundred yards or so, clinging to the wet faces of the shale, are pale, translucent creatures the size of a human hand, resembling shell-less limpets or thick drops of tallow. They move at a glacial pace, sliding over the razor-sharp edges of the rock without being cut, grazing on the invisible bacterial slime. 

More rarely—perhaps only three or four in the entire valley—something larger moves through the gloom. They look at first like bundles of dead, waterlogged drift-wood, until they shift. These are tall, six-legged arthropods with carapaces that mimic the flat, jagged planes of the blue shale. They stand motionless for hours, their long, blade-like legs wedged deep into the flooded crevices between the stones, waiting to pluck the pale limpets from the rock. When they do move, lifting one stilt-like leg at a time with agonizing slowness, their joints make a loud, grinding click that vanishes instantly into the roaring chorus of the settling stone.

Nothing rots here, because nothing grows. The water seeps, the rock shifts by hair-breadths, the pale light holds steady for hours before fading imperceptibly into a pitch-black night, and the valley continues to roar at the low, gray sky.
