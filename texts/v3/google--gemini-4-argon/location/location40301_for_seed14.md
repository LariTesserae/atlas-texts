The water here is not empty space; it is a weight, and it is full.

Two hundred feet below the grey, chopping skin of a sub-polar strait lies a wide, sunken basin known only by the drag it puts on deep-sea lines. Once, tens of millions of years ago, there were mountains here, or at least the jagged shoulders of a rift. Now, the bedrock has been planed down to a sprawling, slightly concave dish of dark basalt and slate, polished to a greasy smoothness by an eternity of abrasive, shifting weight. There are no cliffs left, no overhangs, no sharp ledges. Everything that could break has broken, and the pieces have been ground against one another until they achieved their final, stubborn shapes.

Above this vast, flattened floor, the water is black, cold—hovering just a degree or two above freezing—and violently alive with silent motion. Two massive, opposing tidal currents meet over this basin, colliding and rolling over the depressed pan of rock. The result is a permanent, submerged storm.

Because the rock has been eroded to its lowest, most stable state, the billions of tons of stone that used to form ridges now exist as a three-foot-deep blanket of round, black cobbles, fist-sized stones, and dense, coarse gravel covering the basin floor for miles. Under the wrenching, chaotic pressure of the meeting currents, this entire floor is in constant, rolling transit. 

Yet, there is no roar. The immense viscosity of the frigid water, combined with the heavy curtain of suspended silt and mud, acts as an acoustic baffle. The violence is muffled, dampened into a low, rhythmic, subterranean *shhh-klok, shhh-klok*—the sound of millions of smooth stones shifting, sliding, and rolling over one another in a thick medium, like marbles stirred in a vat of heavy oil.

To hang just above the bottom is to be crowded on all sides. The water is packed to saturation. There is no clear liquid here; it is a dense, turbulent soup of organic detritus, dark mineral silt, torn ribbons of sunken kelp from shallower waters miles away, and the endless, rolling clouds of black sand kicked up by the current. The darkness is nearly absolute, save for the faintest, dirty-green smudge of noon filtering down from the distant surface—just enough light to turn the swarming particles in the water from black to the color of wet ash. Every cubic inch of the water column is occupied by something tumbling, sinking, or being swept sideways.

Rooted stubbornly into the rare seams of exposed bedrock—where the rolling cobbles get swept clean for a few hours at a time before being buried again—is the basin’s only vegetation. It is a sparse, tough, black-brown kelp, thick as a man’s wrist and rubbery as vulcanized tire tread. These stalks have no fronds to catch the current; they are whip-like, streamlined straps that thrash and writhe in the dark like frantic eels, snapping back and forth as the eddies reverse.

Life here is rare, built for crushing friction and freezing dark. Flat, pale-bellied bottom fish, heavy as iron skillets, press themselves down *into* the rolling gravel, letting the stones wash over their armoured backs. Thick-shelled whelks and blind, squat crabs with carapaces worn smooth and matte by the sand-blasting water cling to the base of the kelp whips or wedge themselves between the heaviest boulders. Now and then, a five-foot Greenland sleeper shark drifts through the murk—slow, milky-eyed, its rough skin bumping unbothered against the flying debris, its body undulating with the heavy, chaotic sway of the deep swell before the dark swallows it again.

There are no buildings here, no wrecks, no pylons. Nothing tall could survive the relentless lateral shear of the bottom-water. Yet, there are traces of a primitive, desperate industry. 

Weighted down in the heaviest troughs of the basin are traps. They are not made of wire or plastic, which the rolling stones would shred or flatten in a week, but of thick, braided willow and pitch-soaked spruce roots, lashed around frames of naturally curved driftwood and weighted with heavy, notched river-stones tied on with rawhide. They are crude, ancient-looking things dropped from skin-boats or dories far above in the freezing fog. Alongside them, occasionally tumbling through the gravel, are lost anchors: massive, oblong beach stones with grooves pecked around their middles by hand-mauls, trailing frayed tails of three-strand hemp rope. 

Most of these traps are broken within days, their ribs stove in by the migrating dunes of black cobble, their hemp lines sawed through by the relentless friction against the smoothed slate floor. They do not look out of place; made of wood, stone, and plant fiber, they simply join the dense, swirling pack of matter at the bottom of the sea, ground down by the same patient, turbulent dark that flattened the mountains before them.
