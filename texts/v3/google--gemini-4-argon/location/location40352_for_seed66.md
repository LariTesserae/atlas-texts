The plateau does not end so much as it dissolves into the pale, high-altitude haze of the horizon. For hundreds of miles in every direction, the world is a continuous, stepped expanse of engineered stone, heavy iron, and weeping slate. There are no mountains rising above it and no valleys cutting sharply through it; whatever jagged geology once existed here was planed down, terraced, and built over so long ago that the distinction between bedrock and foundation has entirely vanished. 

It is an industrial city of continental proportions, worn smooth by centuries of persistent, warm drizzle and its own tireless mechanics. 

The stone here—a dense, dark basalt mixed with poured slag-concrete—has been polished by time and water into rounded, pillowy forms. Lintels sag slightly under their own ancient weight; stairs are hollowed in the center like stone troughs; massive retaining walls bulge gently outward, held in check by thick iron tie-rods whose star-shaped anchor plates are rusted into permanence. Nothing is green. There is not a blade of grass, a vine, or a patch of moss in the entire sprawl. The only concessions to non-animal biology are the paper-thin, mineral-eating lichens that stain the leeward sides of smokestacks a dull ochre, and the slick, transparent biofilms that make the cobblestones treacherous underfoot.

Yet the city is practically solid with life. 

Every cornice, gantry, telegraph wire, and roofline is weighed down by animals. Because there are no plants to anchor the food web, the ecology is built entirely on the city’s own industrial metabolism—specifically, the warm, tallow-rich effluent and organic sludge pumped through the district-wide networks of open viaducts. Millions of broad-winged, heavy-bodied birds—creatures resembling a cross between pelicans and dark-feathered gulls—roost shoulder-to-shoulder along the warm brick parapets of the refineries. Their collective weight is an architectural factor; iron struts are reinforced to hold them, and roofs are pitched steeply just to keep the guano sliding into the street-level sluices.

Down in the avenues and the stepped basins between factory blocks, the air is thick with the smell of wet feathers, coal smoke, ozone, and warm chalk. The streets are rarely walked by anything on two legs; instead, they are rivers of slick, sluggishly moving amphibians and hard-shelled scavengers. Pale, knee-high crabs with carapaces stained the color of rust clatter over the wet paving stones in slow-moving carpets, feeding on the sulfur-crusts around steam vents and the constant rain of biological debris from the roosts above. In the warm puddles and overflow basins, blind, fat-bodied salamanders the length of a man's forearm lie stacked atop one another like sunken logs, barely stirring even when the heavy iron wheels of an automated slag-cart grind past on submerged rails.

The sound of the plateau is a vast, immersive roar that never rises to a shriek and never falls to silence. It is a dense composite of two things: heavy, slow-moving machinery and billions of throats. Beneath the stone underfoot, there is the steady, rhythmic *thrum-thud* of colossal subterranean pumps and steam-hammers, operating on a cadence so slow it feels geological—a stroke every ten seconds, pushing warm water and industrial slurry across hundreds of miles of mild gradients. Above that bass note is the continuous, rasping murmur of the fauna: the clicking of millions of mandibles, the wet rustle of shifting wings, the low, resonant croaking that echoes out of the drainage cisterns, and the steady hiss of rain hitting hot iron roofs.

The light is bright, diffuse, and shadowless. At this high elevation, the cloud layer is not above the city; the city sits directly inside its lower belly. The sun appears as a blindingly white, indistinct smear in a bright gray sky, turning the wet slate roofs and the miles of pooled water into sheets of dull silver. 

Because the landscape is so vast and the industrial heat so pervasive, the city generates its own slow-moving weather. Great, lazy columns of steam rise from the cooling towers, drift sideways for ten miles over the packed rooftops, cool in the high mountain air, and condense back down as a warm, greasy mist that coats the ironwork and the feathers of the birds alike. 

Everything here happens at an unhurried, inevitable pace. Great gantry cranes, spanning entire avenues on rusted trestles, inch forward at the speed of an hour hand, hauling ladles of molten silicate or vats of rendered tallow from one processing block to the next. As they move, the thousands of roosting birds resting on their upper girders do not even bother to take flight; they merely shift their weight, preening their damp feathers in the radiant heat of the machinery. When a piece of ancient masonry finally gives way from centuries of water-rot, it doesn't crash—it slumps into the street, where it is immediately climbed over by the crabs, slickened by the rain, and integrated into the terrain before the day is out. 

Looking out from the top of a viaduct, the view is staggering in its uniformity and density: an endless, undulating sea of wet black stone and oxidized iron, breathing steam, crawling with pale shell and dark feather, stretching out under a bright white sky until the sheer thickness of the damp air swallows the horizon.
