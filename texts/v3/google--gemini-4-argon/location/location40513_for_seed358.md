The space is a vast, low-slung subterranean basin—a regional sinkhole or a miles-wide cavern whose roof hangs so low and flat over the floor that the distance feels exclusively horizontal. It is a landscape of accretion rather than erosion. Nothing here is being worn away; instead, the entire valley is slowly, inexorably filling itself in, millimeter by millimeter, year by year. 

The air is warm—about eighty-five degrees—and so saturated with mineral vapor that breathing feels like taking in broth. Because of this constant, heavy precipitation, there is no empty space. The cavern is packed to bursting with newborn stone. Millions of overlapping travertine terraces, rimstone dams, and bloated stalagmites crowd against one another, their surfaces slick with a constant, warm, sheet-like flow of calcified water. The rock isn't gray and ancient; it is raw, embryonic, the color of mutton fat, wet bone, and oxidized copper, growing outward in bulbous, cauliflower-like concretions. To move through this basin is to squeeze sideways between glistening, weeping pillars that were ten inches thinner a decade ago, stepping from the lip of one overflowing mineral pool directly onto the rim of the next. 

The noise is monumental, physical, and inescapable. A thousand warm underground rivers breach the ceiling along the perimeter faults, dropping in thick, heavy curtains onto the crowded stone floor below. This continuous hydraulic roar is trapped underneath the low ceiling and bounced infinitely between the packed stone columns. It doesn't sound like a single waterfall; it sounds like the inside of a running turbine, a white-noise pressure that vibrates in the teeth and sternum. Yet despite the violence of the sound, the actual movement of the water across the basin floor is sluggish. Once it hits the terraces, the water spreads out into a billion shallow, steaming pans, spilling over the calcified lips in slow, syrupy sheets, depositing its dissolved load of limestone and sulfur before sinking through the porous floor.

Light is scarce, creating a perpetual, murky twilight. Five hundred feet overhead, a few jagged fissures in the cavern roof let down narrow, theatrical shafts of gray daylight, which immediately diffuse into the thick mist as faint, milky cones. Lower down, the only illumination comes from the scattered remnants of an old, half-swallowed industrial dredging operation. Every quarter-mile or so, an iron scaffold, a rusting pump-house, or a riveted boiler tank rises out of the crowded stone. These machines are still connected by heavy, insulated power cables draped from pillar to pillar like vines. Most of the machinery is dead, slowly being entombed in hard white stone—flywheels frozen in mid-turn by thick aprons of flowstone, iron hulls disappearing under yellow crusts—but the electrical grid still hums. Periodically, a surviving sodium-vapor floodlight or a string of caged incandescent bulbs glows with a sickly, bruised-orange light, illuminating a twenty-foot radius of steaming pools and dripping rock before the mist swallows the glow. 

Where those industrial lights still burn, sparse pockets of vegetation cling to the wet stone. Pale, leggy ferns, mats of liverwort, and sickly, translucent mosses cluster within inches of the warm glass bulbs, feeding on the artificial photons. Elsewhere, the flora is limited to thick, rubbery curtains of brownish-green bacterial mats that drape over the edges of the stone dams like wet leather, thriving in the mineral-rich dark.

Life, however, does not need the light here. The warm pools and wet rock faces are thick with fauna. Every shallow basin is crowded with pale, eyeless crayfish and armored isopods the size of a human hand, clacking over one another in the warm soup. The wet stone pillars are coated in millions of black-shelled grazers—snails and limpets—methodically scraping the fast-growing bacterial slime from the fresh travertine. Above them, where the air currents eddies around the old iron catwalks, the crevices are packed with bats and blind cave-swifts. Their bodies are pressed wing-to-wing in the thousands, entirely unbothered by the deafening roar of the water, adding their own high-pitched, clicking chitter to the upper registers of the cavern’s acoustic wall. Guano drops steadily into the mineral water, dissolving into the warm, chemical slurry that feeds the bacterial mats, which feed the snails, which feed the crayfish.

Nothing moves quickly here. The crayfish crawl with a slow, mechanical deliberation; the water sheets over the dams at a leisurely pace; the orange bulbs flicker on a dying current; and the stone itself creeps outward, sealing off passages, burying the iron pumps, and raising the floor toward the weeping roof at a pace too slow for the eye to catch, but fast enough to choke the valley within a century.
