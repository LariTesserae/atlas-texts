The ground is not dirt, and it is not stone. It is an archive that has worn down into a plain. 

To walk across the Great Shingle is to walk on the fused, flattened remnants of a trillion micro-architectures. Millions of years ago—or perhaps longer, the timescale here has lost its edges—this continent-sized basin was a stacked, hyper-dense megastructure. Now, it has settled into its final, lowest energy state. The towers have slumped into low, whale-backed ridges; the canyons of logic-gates and conduit-arrays have filled to the brim with their own pulverized dust. The result is an endless, rolling steppe of matte-white ceramic, tarnished gold, and pale gray silicon, all of it smoothed by dry, electrostatic winds until it resembles chalk or bone.

Above, the sky is a relentless, shadowless white. There is no sun, only a continuous, high-altitude photonic ceiling—an atmospheric shell engineered to diffuse light evenly across every square millimeter of the surface. It is bright enough to make the eyes water, a stark, surgical illumination that never dims, never shifts its angle, and leaves nowhere to hide. Because the light comes from everywhere at once, nothing casts a sharp shadow. The low ridges and shallow depressions are defined only by subtle gradients of ivory, pearl, and ash-gray.

The air is warm—like the exhaust at the back of an old machine—and bone-dry. There is no water here. Water would ruin the delicate, dry-state chemistry that still stubbornly persists in the substrate. Instead, the atmosphere is heavy with a fine, glittering suspension of particulate alloys and fluorocarbon vapors that taste faintly of hot copper and ozone. 

Despite the lack of wind-roar or running water, the plain is crowded. The density of the place is underfoot and all around, packed into the shallow verticality of the terrain. Every square meter of the ground is a laminated cross-section of millions of paper-thin strata. Where the low dunes of ceramic dust part, the exposed foundation shows a dizzying, microscopic grain: tightly packed matrices of fiber-optic threads, crushed honeycomb lattices, and crystalline memory-blocks, all fused by time and pressure into a synthetic metamorphic rock. 

And the plain is still active. The ancient programming has decayed into a kind of geological metabolism. 

Across the vast, flat expanse, slow waves of reconfiguration ripple through the ground. A kilometer-wide patch of the pale surface will suddenly darken to slate-gray as billions of microscopic louvers flip in unison, radiating stored thermal energy into the bright air, before flipping back to white. Elsewhere, the dust itself migrates. Driven by shifting electromagnetic fields beneath the crust, rivers of fine, metallic silt flow uphill, pooling on the crests of the low rises, stacking themselves into temporary, razor-thin fins and fractal terraces before collapsing back into smooth slopes when the field subsides.

All of this happens in near-total silence. The physics of this technology has long since bypassed the clumsy friction of moving parts. The shifting of the silt makes only a faint, dry hiss, like salt poured onto paper. The thermal venting is entirely quiet. The loudest sound is the occasional *tink* of a crystalline stratum fracturing under thermal expansion, a tiny, glass-like chime that travels for miles in the dense, still air before being swallowed by the vastness.

In the crevices where the electromagnetic currents are strongest, a sparse, strange flora has taken root. They are not plants, though they occupy the niche of scrub and lichen. Thin, brittle fans of dark purple and iridescent black grow upward from the seams in the ceramic crust, rarely reaching higher than a human knee. These are photovoltaic calcifications—self-assembling mineral structures that feed on the relentless overhead glare and the stray voltage leaking from the ground. They grow in slow, geometric bursts, adding a millimeter of filigree to their edges over the course of an afternoon, only to shatter into black powder when their internal charge exceeds their structural tolerance.

Life—or something indistinguishable from it—moves rarely across this bright, cluttered desert. 

Every few miles, one might spot a Scourer. They are low, flat things, shaped like skates or horseshoe crabs, their carapaces made of the same matte-white ceramic as the ground. They do not walk so much as glide on a cushion of diamagnetic repulsion, hovering a fraction of an inch above the packed silt. They are the size of dining tables, completely silent, drifting along the invisible contour lines of the ground’s magnetic field. As they pass over the brittle black fans of the voltage-flora, they drop a fine, hair-like curtain of copper filaments from their undersides, draining the charge from the growths and leaving them bleached and dead in their wake. 

Farther off, near the horizon where the white ground blurs into the white sky, larger processes are at work. Huge, translucent membranes—weather systems of pure static and localized gravity—drift across the basin. Where they pass, the loose dust rises in silent, slow-motion curtains, hanging suspended thirty feet in the air for hours, sorting itself by atomic weight into bands of gold, silver, and gray before gently snowing back down onto the ancient, tireless floor.
