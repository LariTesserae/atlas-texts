The space is a subterranean vault, no larger than a high-roofed parish church, formed where a massive pocket of subterranean gas collapsed and sheared the dark basalt cleanly along its crystalline planes. Because the collapse was geologically recent, the stone has not yet been softened by time. The walls are stepped in sharp, hexagonal columns and jagged overhangs, their edges still keen enough to catch leather or skin. There is no dust here; the rock is dark, glassy, and slick with a permanent film of warm condensation.

It is oppressively hot. The heat does not come from the air, which is stagnant and heavy, but radiates upward from the floor and outward from two massive, cylindrical iron casings sunk forty feet into the bedrock at opposite ends of the chamber. These are thermal exchange pilings—industrial artifacts from an operation that either finished its work or forgot this sector decades ago. They do not roar or chug; they have no moving exterior parts. They simply stand like blind towers of riveted boiler plate, transferring deep crustal heat into the upper rock. Their metal skins are blistered with red rust and hot to the touch, ticking once every few minutes as their internal pressures shift by a fraction of a bar.

The only light comes from the junction boxes mounted at the base of these cylinders, where rows of thick glass vacuum tubes and status dials glow a steady, low-wattage amber, and from a single service walkway suspended twenty feet up on iron turnbuckles. The walkway’s sodium work-lamps have long since burned out, except for one at the far northern corner that flickers at the very bottom of its filament's life, casting long, knife-edged shadows across the basalt floor.

In this near-darkness, without a single ray of sun, a dense, silent jungle has taken root. 

Because the chamber is sparse and mostly empty volume, the vegetation does not form a tangled mat; instead, it grows with architectural deliberation around the heat and the damp. The plants here do not use chlorophyll. They are thermotrophic—feeding on the steep infrared gradient between the scorching iron pilings and the cooler basalt walls. Their leaves are immense, smooth-edged paddles, some six feet across, the color of dried oxblood, slate, and bruised plum. They grow in slow-motion rosettes around the iron cylinders, angling their fleshy surfaces toward the metal like radar dishes to catch the radiant warmth. 

Where the steam bleeds from a hairline gasket fracture on the eastern piling, a curtain of pale, translucent vines hangs down from the catwalk. They have no leaves at all, only bladder-like nodes the size of fists that swell with condensed mineral water over the course of weeks, growing heavy until the stem stretches, touches the stone floor, and roots where it lands. Between the two great heat-stacks, the floor is largely bare—fifty paces of jagged, shattered rock split by shallow trenches where warm, rusty condensate pools in mirror-still puddles. Only the hardiest tube-fronds grow in this middle expanse, spaced five or six feet apart like black wax candles, their tips beaded with thick, mucilaginous dew.

Nothing crawls here. There are no beetles in the leaf litter—in fact, there is no leaf litter, as the plants rarely drop a frond, preferring to slowly reabsorb old growth back into their bulbous rhizomes over months. There are no bats in the high, dark apex of the ceiling, no blind fish in the warm puddles, not even the buzz of a midge near the glowing amber dials. The air is too heavy with sulfur and carbon dioxide for lungs or spiracles, and the chamber is entirely sealed off from the surface ecology.

Because there are no animals, there is no wind and no rustling. The silence is pressurized, thick enough to feel against the eardrums. The only sounds are the patient physics of the room: the *tink* of a cooling rivet; the low, sub-audible *hum* of the geothermal core vibrating through the soles of one’s boots; and, every quarter-hour or so, the heavy, viscous *plop* of a condensation drop falling forty feet from a basalt tooth on the ceiling to strike the rubbery surface of a dark leaf below.

Beside the western piling sits a small corrugated-iron switchman’s hut, its door hanging open on one hinge. A thick root, pale and veined like a turnip, has crept over the threshold, across the oily floorboards, and wrapped itself entirely around a cast-iron radiator, fusing with the metal so tightly that the iron has cracked like an eggshell. Inside the hut, a brass pressure gauge still works, its needle trembling imperceptibly against the red line. Outside, the great dark leaves lean in toward the doorway, completely motionless, soaking in the warmth of the dark.
