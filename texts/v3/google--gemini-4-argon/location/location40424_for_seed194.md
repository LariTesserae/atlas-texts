There is no sky here, and there is no stone. There is only the hollow inside the burl, and the city that was carved from its inward-growing face.

The space is roughly the size of an amphitheater, sealed entirely within the trunk of a subterranean organism so old that the dirt and bedrock that once held it have long since ground away into nothing, leaving the wood itself as the only geology. The interior is pitch black. It is not the damp, fertile dark of a cave, nor the velvet dark of a forest night, but the dry, cool, sealed darkness of a violin case or the inside of an ancient gourd. 

Because there is no light, the place is known entirely by its acoustics and its textures. It is loud. The wood is alive, though its metabolism is stretched across centuries, fed not by water—which vanished from this stratum eons ago—but by the slow, barometric inhalation of dry, mineral-heavy gases from the deep crust. As the surrounding root-mass shifts to follow these faint thermal gradients, the hollow acts as a resonating chamber. The entire world groans. It is a continuous, polyphonic timber-creak: the bass rumble of heartwood compressing under miles of dry overburden, the sharp, pistol-shot cracks of tension-releases in the ceiling, and the high, rosin-like singing of dry fibers sliding against one another. 

To cross the floor of this pocket-world is to walk on a drumhead.

The center of the space is completely empty. Nothing grows into the void, and nothing flies through it. There are no insects, no bats, no worms, no mold—it is too dry for rot, too sealed for intruders. The air is cool and crisp, smelling faintly of turpentine, old paper, and flint. It feels vast despite its smallness because the interior air is so utterly vacant; when a sound occurs on one side of the bowl, it travels unobstructed through the chill, thin air, bouncing off the opposing walls with a tight, frantic slap-back echo that makes the boundaries feel simultaneously immediate and dizzyingly hollow.

All the complexity of the world is pressed into the walls, floor, and ceiling, where the plant-mass has been worked into a dense, continuous settlement.

Whoever lived here—or whatever blind, patient culture still maintains the place during its long wakes—built nothing by adding material. There is no mortar, no brick, no imported stone. Everything was made by subtraction and training, using primitive wedges of harder, dead wood, flint scrapers, and tourniquets of braided fiber. The result is an urban husk: a three-dimensional relief of a town, facing inward around the central void. 

The architecture is ancient and deeply eroded, not by wind or rain, which have never touched this place, but by two things: the passage of countless dry hands over millennia, and the agonizingly slow refusal of the wood to stay carved. 

A doorway cut three thousand years ago with a stone adze has not crumbled; instead, its lintel has slowly bulged downward like cold tar, the grain flowing around the empty space, while its threshold has been worn into a deep, polished saddle by the shuffling of feet. The walls of the tiered houses are seamless with the floor and the ceiling. In this cool darkness, every corner has been rounded to the softness of river-stone or old ivory. There are no splinters. The wood has the density of ironwood and the finish of a cello neck. 

Streets are narrow, recessed grooves that spiral up the curvature of the hollow. Along them sit thousands of small, contiguous rooms, alcoves, and aqueducts that never carried liquid—they are vents, carved to channel the dry, sighing drafts of the wood's respiration through the settlement, turning the entire town into a massive, primitive wind-instrument. When a deep-earth pressure wave causes the mega-flora to inhale, a hundred carved flues along the lower tiers begin to hoot—a low, wooden chord that vibrates through the soles of the feet and hangs in the black air for hours before the pressure equalizes.

In the upper reaches, where the walls curve in to become the ceiling, the plant-architecture changes. Here, the builders did not chisel the deadened heartwood; they wove the living, hair-thin rootlets that hang down like dry Spanish moss. Over generations, these roots were braided while young, tied into knots around wooden pegs, and left to thicken and fuse through inosculation. Now they form a hanging infrastructure of catwalks, basket-vaults, and tension-bridges that span the upper dark. Like the carved wood below, these woven structures are ancient. The knots have swollen into smooth, fused knuckles of timber; the bridges have pulled taut as the walls slowly expanded over centuries, until the braided walkways hum like plucked bass strings at the slightest touch.

Because the environment is nearly waterless, the vegetation’s growth is glacial and strange. The sap is not a liquid, but a thick, volatile resin that moves like creeping glass. Where a builder centuries ago scored a wall with a flint knife to mark a boundary or a tally, a bead of this cold resin has emerged—taking fifty years to form a single tear—and hardened into a ridge of black amber. The residents used this, too: there are low relief-maps and tactile markers everywhere at hand-height, made by scarring the wood in patterns and waiting generations for the scar-tissue to rise into readable bumps.

The floor at the bottom of the bowl is the oldest part of the landscape. Here, the wood grain is so compressed and worn down that all the cellular structure has collapsed into a flat, undulating plain of lignum vitae. A fine, impalpable dust—the only loose matter in the entire world, the result of ages of friction and scraping—settles into the lowest depressions. It is dry as talc. 

Nothing moves quickly here, yet nothing is still. If you press your ear to the polished flank of a house-wall, beneath the loud, dry snapping of the outer shell, you can hear the interior of the plant-city: a sound like millions of pages being turned at once, very slowly, miles away, as the ancient wood continues its endless, blind adjustment to the dark.
