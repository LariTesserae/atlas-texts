The basin is six miles across, shallow as an iron skillet, and warm as the inside of a wrist. Its floor is a continuous sheet of pale grey anhydrite clay, baked firm in the open expanses between the vents, but slick and yielding where the condensation settles. There are no mountains on the horizon, only a low, continuous rim of worn basalt worn down to rounded nubs, like teeth ground flat by an eternity of chewing. 

Nothing here was built by a hand, yet the valley is an engine.

Scattered across the pale clay, separated by quarter-mile stretches of empty, grease-sheened mud, are the fumaroles—though they do not look like volcanic vents. They are natural castings of high-nickel meteoric iron and copper-sulfide ore, extruded from the crust millennia ago and worn by internal steam into vertical cylinders ten to fifteen feet high. Over epochs of chemical deposition, mineral crusts have sealed their tops into heavy, domed caps. Inside these natural boilers, groundwater meets a shallow, stable magma sill. The water flashes to steam, builds pressure beneath the mineral cap, lifts the heavy stone lid four inches with a sharp, metallic *chuff*, vents a white plume into the warm air, and drops the lid back into its beveled seat with a heavy, cast-iron *clank*.

*Chuff-clank.* 

Because the subsurface heat is uniform and the water table never fluctuates, each vent operates on a fixed cycle. A wide squat cylinder near the basin's center fires every eleven seconds. A cluster of three narrower pipes half a mile east fires in a staggered three-beat cadence—*clank, clank, clank-chuff*—like an unhurried three-cylinder marine engine idling in an empty harbor. Across the six-mile expanse, hundreds of these mineral pistons rise and fall in an unchanging, unsynchronized polyrhythm. The sound does not echo—the valley rim is too low and rounded to throw it back—so the noise simply fills the open air, a loud, greasy, mechanical chugging that never speeds up, never slows down, and never stops.

The sky above is a flat, bright, featureless silver-white, the color of aluminum sheet metal under a shop lamp. It casts a clear, shadowless, workday light across the basin. There appears to be no sun moving across it; the brightness is uniform from rim to rim, locked in a permanent mid-morning glare. 

Moisture does not fall as rain. Instead, the steam exhausted from the iron chimneys rises fifty feet, hits a permanent thermal inversion layer, and drifts laterally in thin, oily rags of fog before condensing back onto the clay. The ground is perpetually damp. In the shallow depressions between the vents—smooth-sided channels worn into the rock over millions of years—collects a warm, viscous fluid. It is rainwater heavy with dissolved sulfates, colloidal iron, and natural naphtha seeping up through hairline fractures in the basin floor. It looks and smells like cutting fluid on a lathe. Where it pools, rainbow slicks of mineral oil sit motionless on the surface, undisturbed by wind.

There is no green here. No moss, no grass, no lichen. The closest thing to flora is a black, rubbery sulfur-bacteria mat that coats the runoff aprons around the vents. It grows in concentric, ribbed rings that look precisely like vulcanized rubber floor mats, tough enough to walk on without leaving a print, slick with warm condensed water.

The basin is in absolute equilibrium. The vents do not build their cones any higher; the weak sulfuric acid in the condensing steam dissolves the mineral rims at the exact rate that the rising water deposits new ore from below. The drainage channels do not cut deeper into the clay; they are lined with a self-healing glaze of precipitated silica that repairs any scuff within hours. 

Life is rare, and it looks like hardware.

Clinging to the warm, trembling flanks of the iron stacks are creatures the size of dinner plates, shelled in dull grey hematite. They have no eyes or limbs, only a muscular, grease-sealed foot that acts as a suction cup. They feed on the vibration itself, their internal organs a series of piezoelectric crystalline plates that convert the constant *chuff-clank* tremor of the rock into metabolic energy. They sit bolted to the sides of the vents like inspection hatches, moving perhaps an inch a year.

More rarely, something moves across the open flats between the stacks. Once every few hours, a long, low shape will trudge through the shallow cutting-fluid puddles. It is an articulated animal the length of a workbench, encased in overlapping plates of tarnished brass-colored chitin that smell faintly of hot tallow. It moves on twelve stiff, piston-like legs, its gait completely unaffected by the slick mud. As it walks, it lowers a broad,shovel-shaped snout to skim the black rubbery bacterial mats, peeling up strips of the growth with a dry, scraping *shhk-shhk* sound that cuts briefly through the deeper thrum of the valley. Its joints weep a clear, viscous lubricant that leaves tiny, beaded trails across the grey clay—trails that are slowly washed away by the next cycle of condensing steam.

Stand in the middle of the flats, and the warmth soaks through the soles of your boots. The air tastes of wet iron, steam, and sweet heavy oil. A quarter-mile to the north, a tall, rust-streaked chimney blows a ring of white vapor into the bright silver sky, its heavy stone valve seating itself with a boom you feel in your sternum. Half a mile south, two squat vents answer in counterpoint. Nothing is being made here. Nothing is decaying. The valley simply runs, idling in the bright, warm damp, turning heat into noise and water into rust, forever.
