The chamber is an oblong sinkhole, no wider than a municipal reservoir and perhaps twice as deep, drop-forged into a stratum of porous, honey-colored limestone that has been stained the dark red of dried oxblood by centuries of aerosolized rust. There is no sky here, only a low, vaulted ceiling of sweating rock from which hang the remnants of an ancient pulley gantry, its iron I-beams flared and split like peeling bark from the slow, relentless work of oxidation. 

The heat is staggering, a thick, unmoving presence that feels less like air and more like a warm bath of grease and mineral spirits. It rises from the floor of the basin, where the housings of four massive, half-buried steam condensers sit like the skulls of butchered whales. These machines have been dead for a very long time, or else they have achieved a state of mechanical senility so profound it mimics geology. They do not turn, yet they remain fiercely, inexplicably hot, radiating a dry, kiln-like fever that keeps the chamber at a constant, suffocating simmer. 

There is no standing water—any puddle would boil off in minutes—yet everything is slick to the touch. The moisture exists entirely as a dense, low-hanging suspension, a perpetual sweat that beads on the underside of rusted rivets and coats the stone walls in a warm, minerally slime. 

It is this greasy condensation that feeds the flora. Because there is no sun, the vegetation has abandoned chlorophyll entirely, trading green for the bruisy purples, pale ochres, and tallow-whites of things that feed on heat and iron. The plants are dense, crowded together in the terraced hollows between the machinery, their forms thick and fleshy to hoard the meager damp. Tall, fluted stalks that resemble bundled organ pipes rise twenty feet into the gloom, their surfaces covered in a fine, coppery fuzz that traps the drifting steam. Around their bases, sprawling mats of "boil-cress"—a tough, rubberyvine with leaves like blistered leather—choke the old maintenance catwalks, their roots prying into the seams of the boilers to suck at the hot, oily sludge sealed within. 

Where the vines touch the hottest plates of the condenser casings, they do not char; instead, they vulcanize, turning into a hard, black, woody substance that smells faintly of burnt hair and cloves. Over decades, the plants have encased the lower halves of the engines in a living gasket of petrified root-matter, locking the iron in place.

The light comes from the plants themselves, and from the slow chemical rot of the old industrial insulation. A sluggish, sour-yellow bioluminescence leaks from the veins of the larger leaves, pulsing not in seconds, but in twenty-minute intervals—a slow, drowsy brightening that washes over the crowded leaves, holds for a long while, and then fades back to the color of a dying bruise. In the deepest shadows under the overhanging rock, spills of leaked transformer oil have been colonized by a crusty, subterranean lichen that glows a steady,furnace-door orange, casting long, upward-pointing shadows that make the hanging chains and vine-ropes look like the bars of a warped cage.

Despite the total absence of insects, birds, or even worms—nothing with blood could survive the heavy metal toxicity and the scalding, low-oxygen air—the chamber is deafeningly loud. The sound is a physical weight in the chest. Deep beneath the floor, some miles down in the crust, a geothermal vein periodically flashes ground-seep into high-pressure steam, which vents upward through the choked, rusted throats of the dead condensers. 

The machines act as gigantic, accidental brass instruments. Every few minutes, a buildup of subterranean pressure forces its way through a constricted valve or a split copper pipe, producing a long, flatulent, brassy groan that reverberates off the narrow stone walls. Elsewhere, the heat causes the ancient iron structures to expand against the stranglehold of the petrified roots, resulting in sudden, pistol-shot *cracks* and long, agonizing shrieks of stressed metal that take minutes to fully resolve. Even the plants contribute to the din: the tall, fluted stalks are hollow and pressurized; when they reach maturity, they split along a vertical seam with a wet, concussive *thwack* like a thick canvas sail snapping in a gale, releasing a cloud of heavy, rust-colored spores that hang in the stagnant air for days.

Nothing moves quickly here. A dropped wrench would be swallowed by the creeping, leathery fold of a vine, but the process would take three seasons. The steam vents shift their pitch by a quarter-tone over the course of a decade as mineral scale slowly chokes one pipe and forces the pressure into another. The spores drift down at a rate of inches per hour, settling onto the hot iron, germinating in the grease, and adding another millimeter of living insulation to the great, sweating furnace of the room. It is an ecosystem entirely closed upon itself, a tiny, booming hell of hot iron and rubbery flesh, stewing in its own ancient juices.
