There is no dirt here, and there is no stone. If you were to drive a chisel into the floor of this wide, high-altitude basin, you would not strike bedrock; you would strike wood—a pale, waterlogged, iron-hard cambium that immediately weeps a clear, warm sap to seal the wound. 

The entire valley, stretching seven or eight miles from rim to rim, is a single, continuous architectural mega-flora. It is a landscape that has been grown rather than formed, or built by an intelligence that used only meristematic tissue and turgor pressure in place of mason’s tools. Every terrace, every sweeping plaza, every towering, hollow viaduct that steps up the valley walls is living plant matter, locked in a state of pristine, unyielding maturity.

Despite the total saturation of life, the space is remarkably empty. The architecture does not crowd itself. A flat expanse of compressed, seamless bark—smooth as poured concrete and tinted a bruised, photosynthetic purple-green—will run for half a mile before meeting the base of a single, monolithic buttress. These buttresses rise hundreds of feet into the clear, warm air, curving over to form vast, rib-like arches that support nothing at all, framing huge quantities of bright, empty sky. There are no leaves, no frantic tangles of vine or brush, no rotting detritus. Because the structure is a single self-maintaining organism, it sheds nothing. There is no decay, and therefore no soil. Every surface has the crisp, embryonic perfection of a newly unfurled bud, hardened into permanent, load-bearing timber.

The valley is perpetually wet, though it never rains. The high elevation sits precisely at the dew point of the warm, rising air currents, and the architecture itself acts as a massive hydraulic pump. Water sweats continuously from billions of microscopic stomata in the pale walls, gathering into sheets that slide down the unmarred flanks of the monoliths. It collects in shallow, geometric basins—grown with ninety-degree corners and perfectly level rims—before spilling over into broad, stepped channels. The ground underfoot is always slick with a quarter-inch film of warm, crystal-clear water that smells faintly of crushed celery and ozone. 

This water is the source of the valley’s relentless, deafening voice. Because the built-grown chambers and towering, flute-like columns are hollow and rigid, they act as enormous acoustic resonators. The trickle of water inside a fifty-foot-wide vascular conduit is amplified into a deep, booming roar. Across the sparse, open miles of the basin, thousands of these internal waterfalls and pressurizing xylem-tubes harmonize into a continuous, loud, percussive hum—like a cathedral pipe organ holding a single, wet, vibrating chord for eternity. The sound comes from beneath the floor, from inside the distant arches, from the very air. 

Light falls across this wet geometry with a clear, high-altitude sharpness. It reflects off the sheeting water and the waxy, cuticular skin of the plazas, throwing bright, rippling caustics onto the undersides of the great arches. Because there is no wind-blown dust and no crumbling stone, the shadows are sharp-edged and cool, contrasting against the warm, glistening expanses of the sun-facing walls.

Animal life is an afterthought here, a rare punctuation in the vast, open spans. Occasionally, a creature the size of a heron, but possessing the translucent, segmented carapace of a shrimp, will pick its way across a flooded courtyard. Its long, needle-tipped legs find purchase in the tough epidermis of the floor without piercing it. Smaller things—flat, shell-less gastropods the color of wet jade—glide along the vertical walls, grazing on the microscopic film of sugars that the architecture exudes to feed them. In return, their movement polishes the cuticle to a high, mirror-like gloss. You might walk for two hours across the ringing, water-slicked terraces and see nothing else move save for the slow, rhythmic flexing of a drainage valve—a fleshy, sphincter-like rosette set into the floor, three yards wide, opening and closing once every few minutes with a loud, bass-heavy *thump* that echoes for miles across the empty, sun-drenched plaza.

Nothing here is wearing down. Where water spills over a lip of pale wood, it does not carve a gorge; the lip simply thickens its fibrous callous to match the friction of the flow. The valley is finished, yet entirely fresh—a vast, loud, flooded greenhouse with the roof torn off, holding its breath in the warm mountain sun.
