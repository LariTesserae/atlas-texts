The plateau sits at an altitude where the sky loses its pale wash and turns a hard, mineral cobalt, yet the air is anomalously warm. It is not the dry, baking heat of a low desert, nor the thin chill of an alpine pass, but a steady, radiant warmth that feels generated from within the ground itself. The terrain is a five-mile expanse of old, cream-colored travertine and smoothed limestone, worn down over millennia into shallow basins, fluted runs, and wide, sloping shelves. Every edge has been rounded off. There is no scree, no jagged talus; the rock looks as though it has been licked smooth by warm tongues of water over a hundred thousand years.

Water is present, though there are no rivers or lakes. Instead, it exists as a migrating sheen. At regular intervals, a warm, low-hanging fog rolls up from the drop-offs at the plateau’s edge, moves rapidly across the stone, and vanishes, leaving the rock slick and dark. The moisture collects in the thousands of shallow, spoon-shaped depressions worn into the stone. These pools are rarely deeper than a human hand is wide, and they evaporate quickly under the fierce, high-altitude glare, only to be replenished an hour later by another passing breath of mist or a sudden, localized condensation event. 

What makes the condensation localized—and what accounts for the plateau’s unnatural warmth—are the nodes. 

Scattered across the open expanse, separated by hundreds of yards of bare, sun-bleached stone, are structures no larger than cairns or survey markers. They do not look built so much as grown out of phase with the local geology. One might be a three-foot ribbon of seamless, matte-black material that loops out of the limestone and back into it without a joint or bolt. Another is a cluster of silver-gray polyhedra floating six inches above a stone basin, completely immobile, casting sharp, dark shadows that do not shift when the sun moves. These objects are remnants—or active endpoints—of an infrastructure that operates at the level of fundamental forces. They bleed waste heat into the limestone, warming the plateau, and they subtly warp the local atmosphere. Around each node, the air has a faint refractive index, like the shimmer above a hot road, and within a fifty-foot radius of them, sound simply fails to propagate. Walk past one, and the crunch of your boots on the dry crust of lichen drops to a padded, cottony nothing, as if the kinetic energy of the air molecules is being gently harvested to power some subterranean or non-local calculation.

Even away from the nodes, the plateau is remarkably quiet. The thinness of the high air dampens acoustics, and the wind—which moves constantly across the open shelf—makes almost no sound because there is so little for it to catch on.

The vegetation offers no resistance to the wind. It is sparse, tough, and adapted to the rhythm of the condensing mists. In the cracks between the limestone slabs grow low, sprawling cushions of wire-scrub, their leaves reduced to copper-colored needles that glisten when wet. Along the rims of the shallow basins, thick crusts of chartreuse and rust-red lichen spread in slow, concentric rings. The only plants of any height are the bladder-reeds, which grow singly or in pairs every fifty paces or so. They are translucent, pale-green stalks five feet tall, swollen with water and warm gas, swaying in slow, silent unison when a thermal passes through.

Despite the sparse cover and the vast spaces of bare, blinding stone between the plants, the plateau is teeming with animal life. Because the flora is minimal, the ecosystem is built almost entirely on two foundations: the rapid-growth microbial mats that bloom in the warm, temporary puddles, and the ambient energy fields of the nodes.

In the bright, shadowless glare, movement is everywhere, yet it happens in near-total silence. Herds of long-legged, spindly ungulates—creatures the size of foxes with oversized, vascular ears for shedding heat and wide, spongy hooves that make no click on the rock—drift across the shelves. They move in loose, shifting geometries, grazing on the slick bacterial films in the basins. Their coats are a pale, chalky white that matches the travertine so closely that when they stop moving, only their dark, horizontal eyes are visible in the brightness.

They are shadowed by predators that look like flattened, six-legged hounds covered in overlapping scales of keratin. These hunters do not stalk behind cover—there is none. Instead, they use the light and the nodes. They wait within the refractive shimmer of the black and silver artifacts, their bodies absorbing the waste warmth, until a herd of the pale grazers drifts close. The chases are sudden, explosive bursts of speed across the open stone, conducted in an eerie hush—just the soft *thrum-thrum* of padded feet and the whip-crack of displaced air, which dies out the moment the runners pass through a node's dampening field.

Above them, the bright air is carved by gliding scavengers—broad-winged, tail-less things that aren't quite birds, riding the thermal columns rising from the sun-baked rock and the warm anomalies. They never cry out. They communicate by flashing iridescent patches on their underwings, catching the intense high-altitude sunlight like heliographs. 

Closer to the ground, the damp pockets are frantic with smaller life. The moment a mist-bank passes and fills a depression with a quarter-inch of warm water, the stone comes alive. Translucent, ribbon-like amphibians uncurl from dry crevices in the rock, sliding into the water to feed and mate before the sun drinks the puddle dry again. Long-jawed, metallic-blue insects skitter across the meniscus, hunting the ribbons, while in turn being hunted by patient, stone-colored crab-things that sit motionless at the basin rims. 

Everything here moves with a brisk, purposeful urgency, driven by the high metabolism of the warmth and the fleeting nature of the moisture. A puddle forms, blooms with red algae, is swarmed by ribbons and insects, is drunk dry by five pale grazers, and evaporates back into the cobalt sky, all within forty minutes, leaving only damp tracks that vanish seconds later. And presiding over all of it, half-buried in the ancient, water-smoothed stone, the strange, silent machines continue their incomprehensible work, holding the warmth in the high air and drinking the noise of the world.
