The altitude is a physical weight, though the air is thin enough to make the lungs ache with every cold, wet breath. You are standing on the Shelf, a geological uplift of newly split basalt that stretches across the curvature of the hemisphere. Because it is geologically young, nothing here has been softened by time. The horizons are jagged, crystalline, and razor-sharp, receding into a pale, shadowless daylight for thousands of miles. There are no foothills, no gentle valleys—only vast, level steppes of fractured black stone interrupted by sudden, sheer drops of ten thousand feet, their edges freshly sheared as if the continent were broken over a knee yesterday.

Yet, for all its violent geometry, the stone is buried beneath a suffocatingly lush carpet of cold-weather flora. There are no trees, for the wind would snap them, and no grasses. Instead, the rock is upholstered in a yard-deep layer of pale green and silver-blue bryophytes, giant weeping lichens, and rubbery, cabbage-like rosettes the size of cartwheels. This vegetation drinks the atmosphere. The air is perpetually supersaturated, a bright, freezing mist that never quite coalesces into rain and never clears into blue sky. Water does not flow in rivers here; it sweats. It condenses on the millions of square miles of waxy leaves and sharp rock faces, trickling endlessly downward through the moss mat, pooling in shallow, mirror-still tarns of black water that reflect the blank white sky. 

The emptiness of the Shelf is terrifying. The air is so clear between the drifting banks of ground-fog that you can see for fifty miles in any direction, and for fifty miles there is only the wet, silver-green moss, the jagged black crags, and the sky. Not a single bird cuts the air. No insects hum in the damp foliage; no amphibians disturb the surface of the freezing pools. The flora reproduces entirely by spores and creeping rhizomes, an endless, silent, vegetative sprawl that has no need for pollinators and fears no herbivores.

They are not cities for living in; they are cities of pure infrastructure, dropped onto the young rock like immense iron stamps. From a distance, a Municipality looks like a dark, geometric reef rising out of the pale moss. As you approach, the scale becomes comprehensible only by degrees. There are miles of terraced concrete plinths, brutalist viaducts, and dense grids of windowless, corrugated-iron ziggurats. Great bundles of tarnished copper piping, each tube ten feet in diameter, run across the tundra on stilts, linking one Municipality to the next across the empty horizons. 

The city is deafening. Long before you reach its perimeter walls, the sound flattens every other sensory impression. It is a continuous, harmonic roar—the bass rumble of ten thousand subterranean turbines, the high-pitched, metallic shriek of pressurized steam venting through brass whistles, and the rhythmic, earth-shaking *thud-thud-thud* of drop-forges the size of cathedrals. Yet nothing in the city actually moves. There are no vehicles on the wide, wet avenues of stamped steel. There are no cranes swinging across the sky, no doors opening, no shifting lights. The industrial violence is entirely internal, locked inside the bolted iron casings of the buildings and the trembling pipelines. 

The strangest aspect of the landscape is its absolute temporal paralysis. The sky is locked in a permanent, even luminescence—the light of an overcast Tuesday at ten in the morning, unchanging, casting soft, omnidirectional shadows that never lengthen or rotate. The wind blows from the west at a steady, biting twelve miles per hour; it never gusts, and it never dies. At the edge of the Municipality, where a brass relief-valve vents a towering plume of white steam into the freezing air, the cloud of vapor rises sixty feet, curls over against the twelve-mile-per-hour wind, and dissolves at the exact same point in space, second after second, year after year. It is a sculpture made of roaring gas.

The water that condenses on the warm iron roofs of the factories runs down through cast-iron gutters and spills onto the basalt below. Where this warm, mineral-heavy runoff hits the cold stone, the pale moss grows into monstrous, swollen mounds, thirty feet high, pressing up against the riveted walls of the foundries. This is the only dialogue in the world: the hot, deafening, static machinery of the empty cities, and the cold, wet, silent plants swelling against their flanks. Nothing decays. No rust flakes from the wet iron; no leaves brown and rot in the bogs. The engines roar at a fixed pitch, the steam condenses on the sharp black rocks, the moss drinks the water, and the white sky hangs overhead like a painted vault, holding the vast, freezing highland in a state of loud and immaculate arrest.
