There is no dirt here. If you were to drop an iron bar and let it sink, it would push through five feet of warm, yielding green-brown sponge, a mat of interwoven root-hairs, bladder-moss, and flat, leathery rhizomes that drink from the air and from each other, before striking the older, calcified wood below. To walk is to depress a vast, springy mattress that immediately weeps pale, mineral-heavy water around the soles of your boots. The water wells up at skin-temperature, smells faintly of pennies and bruised fennel, and disappears back into the mat the moment your weight shifts.

The whole place is no larger than an old dockyard—perhaps three hundred paces from one side to the other—and it is boxed in by sheer, windowless walls of weeping brick. What lies beyond the brick is irrelevant; the walls are forty feet high, their mortar long since replaced by the gripping, pale tendons of *ferro-ivy*, and their tops are lost in a knotted ceiling of thick, horizontal trunks. Yet the light inside is not the gloom of a forest floor. It is a bright, shadowless, conservatory glare. High overhead, the canopy does not form leaves, but broad, translucent bladders stretched between the boughs like oiled parchment. They catch the midday sun and scatter it downward in a milky, golden wash that warms the neck and makes every bead of condensation on every waxy surface gleam like mercury.

The space is packed nearly to choking, yet arranged with a strange, muscular geometry. Great, smooth-barked tubers the size of boilers rise out of the floor-mat, crowding against one another so tightly that their flanks have flattened into six-sided columns where they touch. Between them, ropy lianas as thick as dock-hawsers drop from the bladder-canopy, wind around the tubers, and vanish into the underfoot sponge. Everything is wet to the touch—not from rain, which never falls through the sealed canopy, but from a continuous, heavy sweating. The plants exhale moisture and drink it back in an endless, closed-loop fever.

Scattered through this green pressure-cooker are the engines. 

There are perhaps a dozen of them within the enclosure, though you can never see more than one or two at a time through the press of the stalks. They are heavy, squat things of cast iron and dull brass: donkey-engines, flywheel housings, riveted pressure-vessels, and sets of massive, double-helical gears mounted on masonry plinths that have long since been swallowed by the root-mat. They have no operators, no fuel bunkers, and no clear purpose, yet they are running. 

They do not run on steam. Instead, the surrounding vegetation has grown *into* the machinery, not to wreck it, but to use it—or be used by it. A wrist-thick vine, pulsing with high-pressure sap, forces itself into the intake valve of a cylinder; forty minutes later, the piston reaches the top of its stroke with a wet, pneumatic *chuff*, and a counterweight of river-stone, lashed to a lever by braided roots, drops three feet into a cushion of moss. Across the clearing, a flywheel twelve feet in diameter turns at a speed so slow the eye can barely track the spokes, driven by the relentless, hydraulic swelling of dark green gourds wedged between its teeth and the backing plate. As each gourd is crushed by the iron it pushes, it bursts with a sharp, juicy report, lubricating the axle with slick, amber mucilage, while a fresh shoot is already swelling into the gap behind it.

Because of this, the grove is loud. It is not the frantic clatter of a working mill, but a continuous, echoing chorus of heavy, strained, and patient labor. There is the relentless, rhythmic *hiss-tick, hiss-tick* of brass escapements caught in the crotch of twin trunks; the deep, bass groan of iron shafts being torqued a fraction of a degree per minute by the thickening of the wood around them; the sudden, gunshot *crack* of a lignified tendon snapping under too much tension, followed by the slow, hydraulic sigh of a pressure-relief valve bleeding foamy green water into a fern-bank. Every sound hangs long and heavy in the humid, walled-in air.

Nothing with eyes or legs lives in this density; there is no room to dart or burrow, and the air near the floor is too thick with the sweet, metallic exhaust of the engines and the bruised-herb tang of the sap. Only at the very edge of sight, where the brass sight-glasses of the boilers are furred with verdigris, you might spot a solitary grease-slug—a flat, eyeless mollusk the color of old tallow—spending an entire afternoon gliding across a warm bearing housing to lick the oxidized oil from the seams.

It has been like this for a very long time. The iron is not rusting away; the sap that coats it dries into a tough, amber lacquer that seals the metal from the damp, while the constant, microscopic vibration of the gears keeps the moss from choking the teeth. Where a cast-iron strut has cracked under the centuries of slow pressure, the pale, stony wood of the lower roots has grown around the fracture LIKE a bone-callus, holding the alignment true. The machines and the biomass have reached a heavy, greased equilibrium. The sun heats the bladders; the bladders pump the sap; the sap turns the iron; the iron crushes the seed-pods and churns the warm, mineral soup below the mat; the soup feeds the roots. 

To stand in the middle of it is to feel like an intruder in a very small, very crowded engine room that has forgotten what ship it is driving, but continues to maintain pressure, stroke by thirty-minute stroke, in the warm, bright, green-smelling wet.
