At this altitude, the air has no weight, only a fierce, unbuffered heat. The sky above the caldera is the deep, bruised violet of the upper troposphere, yet the ground is a furnace. Normally, a basin four miles above sea level would be locked in ice, its crags scoured by freezing winds. Here, the mountain range sits atop a massive, shallow mantle plume, and the entire multi-mile expanse of the Sump is a radiator of blistered basalt, ochre sulfur crusts, and packed, steaming talus. 

Despite the open sweep of the valley—five miles from the eastern scarp to the western rim—there is nowhere to put a foot down without disturbing something. The terrain is congested, a crowded labyrinth of weathered rock pillars, slumped magma blisters, and dense, terraced ridges that have settled over millennia into a state of heavy, heat-baked equilibrium. The rock is smoothed by chemical weathering rather than rain, worn into bulbous, overlapping folds like hardened wax. Every inch of these stone ribs is occupied. Where the rock isn't colonized by gray-green crusts of thermophilic lichen or bursts of stiff, wire-stemmed brush, it is covered in life, or the rusted iron of old machinery, or the thick, chalky deposits of mineral sweat.

Water never falls from the violet sky; it rises from the floor. The valley breathes in a sluggish, twenty-minute rhythm. Deep subterranean shifts force superheated steam up through thousands of hairline fractures, fumaroles, and mud pots. Because the high-altitude air is thin and naturally cold just beyond the ground's radiant boundary, the steam condenses almost instantly upon emerging. It does not form clouds so much as a heavy, scalding dew that coats every surface in a slick, mineral-rich film. This moisture trickles down the bloated rock formations into shallow, transient basins—warm, alkaline puddles that last for three hours before evaporating or draining back into the crust, only to be refilled by the next exhalation.

Because the air is so thin, sound does not carry. It dies inches from its source. A landslide of pumice a quarter-mile away looks like a silent film; the rhythmic, violent venting of a steam fissure fifty yards off registers only as a faint, papery hiss and a tremor in the soles of the boots. The landscape is hyperactive, churning with heat and motion, yet wrapped in a cottony, high-altitude deafness. 

In this near-silence, the fauna moves in a dense, unbroken carpet. The heat and the mineral-rich dampness have bred an ecosystem of staggering crowding. Millions of pale, palm-sized crabs with thick, porcelain-like shells—adapted to the high UV light and the acidic condensation—clack over the basalt in slow-motion waves, scraping the fast-growing bacterial mats from the stone. Beneath and around them coil heat-sinking reptiles: broad, flat-bodied lizards the color of rusted iron and wet ash, lying three-deep on the warmest vents, soaking in the geothermal energy until their metabolisms reach a frantic pitch. Then they dart—silent blurs—snatching up the blind, translucent bristle-worms that boil out of the mud pots whenever the pressure surges. 

Even the air immediately above the rocks is thick with life, though it rarely rises higher than a man's waist, where the thermal blanket gives way to the thin, freezing drafts of the upper peaks. Heavy-bodied, flightless locusts with copper-colored carapaces hop in dense, synchronized curtains from boulder to boulder, pursued by long-legged, nocturnal spiders that have adapted to hunt in the blinding midday glare by hiding in the black shadows beneath the reptiles' bellies. Everything touches everything else. There is no bare dirt, only a shifting mosaic of shell, scale, wet stone, and the occasional patch of spine-grass—a plant that grows in tight, impenetrable domes like rusted barbed wire, trapping the condensing steam in its interior to nurse its own private reservoir of boiling mud.

Scattered across this biological and geological crowding are the vertical markers of an industrial enterprise that has mostly left the valley to run itself. Every half-mile or so, rising out of the packed talus and the heaps of basking lizards, stands a condenser tower or an automated borehole rig. They are heavy, riveted structures of cast iron and brass, stained bright orange and verdigris by the sulfur mist. There are no roads connecting them, no fences, and no buildings for workers—only the machines themselves, bolted directly into the bedrock decades ago and left to harvest the mountain's pressure.

Thick, insulated pipes snake across the valley floor, half-buried in the settled rubble and completely encrusted in salt and crabs. Inside the pipes, one can feel the heavy, rhythmic thud of turbine pumps driven by the geothermal vents, sending pressurized steam and precipitated lithium sludge miles down the mountain toward unseen lowlands. At the top of each rig, a heavy iron flywheel spins at a furious, blurring speed, venting excess pressure through brass whistles that produce no audible shriek in the thin air—only a shimmering distortion in the heat-haze and a vibration that makes the teeth ache. 

The animals have incorporated the machines into their crowding. The iron casings of the pump-jacks are invisible beneath layers of pale, calcified tube-worms that feed on the leaking lubricant and hot condensation. Lizards lay their leathery eggs inside the warm housings of the dynamo boxes, and when a piston drops with a silent, bone-shaking thud, a thousand copper locusts leap simultaneously into the dazzling light, hang for a second against the dark violet sky, and settle right back down onto the moving steel before the stroke is even finished.
