The amphitheater is no larger than a village commons, cupped high in the sheer, unweathered teeth of a newly split range. The rock walls that ring it are black basalt and raw greenstone, fractured so recently—or preserved so perfectly—that their edges are still razor-sharp. There is no scree at the base of the cliffs, no talus, no river-rounded cobbles. The stone has not yet learned to crumble. 

The air is thin, cold, and perpetually saturated. It does not rain, nor does the mist ever blow away; instead, a permanent, pewter-colored condensation hangs at chest height, turning the upper ledges into dim silhouettes. Every vertical surface weeps. Cold water sweats directly from the unpolished planes of the rock, pooling in the sharp, polygonal depressions of the basin floor to form a shallow, geometric marsh.

Despite the lack of a single human wall, road, or forged bolt, the basin is deafening with the sound of heavy industry. 

The vegetation is the source of the din. Because the space is sparse—a low-density scattering of distinct groves separated by wide expanses of dark, ankle-deep water—each plant-engine stands in clear relief against the pale fog. Great, towering ferns with fronds of green-black vulcanized tissue rise twenty feet into the chill air. Their central stalks are segmented like drive shafts, gleaming with a natural, cold-pressed oil that smells faintly of crushed pine and ozone. These stalks do not sway in the wind; there is no wind. Instead, they reciprocate. They pump up and down in jagged, four-stroke rhythms, sucking mineral-heavy water from the basalt fractures and venting puffs of cold white steam from fleshy valves along their ribs.

Between these solitary, towering ferns, the marsh floor is colonized by sprawling, lush mats of hepatics and cushion-mosses that grow in the exact configuration of cooling coils and braided copper cabling. They hum. It is a continuous, sixty-cycle electrical drone that vibrates through the soles of the feet and sets the standing water into permanent, intersecting grid-patterns of Faraday waves. The water does not ripple outward in circles; it locks into shivering, crystalline squares.

And then there is the fauna, which crowds the open water and the wet air in staggering numbers, operating as the mobile assembly line of the glade. 

Nothing here eats, and nothing grows old. The ecosystem is locked in a state of roaring, time-invariant equilibrium. Millions of amphibians—creatures the size of clenched fists with translucent, porcelain-smooth skin—sit arranged in long, equidistant rows along the razor-sharp basalt ledges. Inside their clear bellies, visible in the dim, sourceless twilight, small flywheel-organs spin at three thousand revolutions per minute. Every four seconds, in absolute unison, a row of ten thousand frogs clicks its jaws shut, producing a sound like a colossal iron loom throwing its shuttle. *Clack-shunt. Clack-shunt.* 

Above the water, the air is thick with heavy-bodied, metallic-sheened insects the size of sparrows. They do not dart or hover erratically; they travel in rigid, invisible pneumatic tubes of space, moving at right angles to one another. When they turn, they pivot instantly on a pinhead with a sharp *ratchet-tick*, their wings blurring with the stink of hot grease and wet wool. Beneath the geometric ripples of the marsh, schools of blind, silver-scaled eels swim in tight, interlocking figure-eights around the base of the piston-ferns, acting as living bearings, their slick skins secreting a pale mucilage that keeps the thrashing roots from grinding the stone to powder.

This is where the reality of the high mountain breaks down completely: the landscape is a closed-loop engine that produces only its own continuation. 

Time does not pass here in a linear stream; it cycles like a crankshaft. If you watch a drop of cold condensation detach from the jagged tip of a black rock overhang, it falls into the marsh with a loud *plink*. Half a second later, the exact same drop is hanging from the rock tip again, and the splash below has un-happened, yet the piston-fern beside it has not reversed—it continues its downward stroke. The glade runs on a thousand desynchronized micro-loops of time, stitched together so seamlessly that the whole small amphitheater appears to be a single, frantic, unchanging machine. 

A flock of tall, wading birds with legs like brushed steel calipers steps through the shallow water. Each bird takes three steps forward, plunges its bill into the vibrating moss to trip a biological relay—causing a bank of swollen, bladder-like orchids on the cliff face to vent a high-pressure shriek of air—and then the bird is instantly three steps back again, mid-stride, without ever having turned around or walked backward. There is no transition. The world simply skips the frame of their return, the way a scratched phonograph needle jumps the groove, yet the sound of the shriek never cuts out.

The light never shifts toward noon or night. It remains a flat, high-altitude overcast, the color of wetzinc, illuminating the lush, dark greens of the thumping flora and the slick, crowded bodies of the fauna. The air tastes of cold iron, bruised fern-sap, and wet slate. Stepping into the basin feels like stepping into a powerhouse carved by glaciers out of raw time—a wet, freezing, wildly overgrown room at the top of the world, roaring at full throttle to manufacture the exact second it is already in.
