There is no ground here, only the weave. 

You are inside a suspended womb of lashed timber, bone-ash mortar, and kiln-baked brick, wedged into the absolute zenith of an altitude so extreme the sky outside has thinned to a violet-black vacuum. Yet you cannot see the sky. You cannot see anything beyond an arm’s reach. The space is no larger than a cistern or an inverted chimney—a vertical pocket universe entirely constructed by hand, packed to the point of suffocation, and currently giving birth to itself in a fever of wet, boiling heat.

The architecture is primitive, frantic, and total. There is no bedrock. Every surface is made of hand-molded river clay still bearing the deep, frantic thumbprints of its makers, bound together by miles of knotted sinew, split-reed matting, and untempered copper pins. And none of it is weathered. Nothing here has aged a single day. The wooden scaffolding is green, its bark freshly stripped and weeping sticky, turpentine-scented sap; the clay bricks are still soft, steaming from the kiln, or perhaps generating their own heat as they materialize out of nothingness. 

Because here, at the roof of the world, reality has lost its ability to distinguish between the made, the grown, and the unformed. 

The walls are actively extruding themselves. In the gloom, you can watch a fat, pale root push out of a mortared joint, only for its tip to calcify, square off, and harden into a fired red brick, complete with a builder’s tally mark pressed into its damp face. Above your head, heavy yew beams are growing like vines, blindly feeling their way across the three-foot expanse of the shaft, knotting themselves with spontaneous bindings of wet rawhide that tighten with a sound like a snapping bowstring. There is zero erosion—no dust, no rot, no moss of age—only the relentless, violent emergence of fresh matter packing itself into a space that is already full.

The heat is staggering, a thick, equatorial bathhouse misery that should not exist at an elevation where air itself ought to freeze. Because the air outside is so thin and cold, and the interior is so ferociously hot, the condensation is torrential. It doesn't rain; the walls *sweat* in sheets. Every freshly formed stone, every taut rope, every slick, rubbery leaf is coated in a skin of scalding water that runs in continuous, braided rivulets down the vertical clutter. The air is a soup of steam and the smell of wet terracotta, boiled sap, and crushed chlorophyll. 

Crammed into every millimeter left unoccupied by the self-building masonry is the foliage. It is lush, dark, and aggressively turgid. Broad-leaved taro-like plants with veins of hammered bronze push through wicker lattices; swollen, bladder-like ferns Drink the hot runoff, expanding until they press flat against the wet clay walls, molding themselves to the thumbprints in the mud. The plants and the primitive machinery of the room are tangled in a single, frantic ecology. Wooden pulleys and counterweights made of bundled river-stones hang from vines that grow an inch an hour, hauling up buckets of boiling silt from nowhere to dump them onto ledges that have only just finished slotting themselves together.

The sound is deafening, amplified by the claustrophobic density of the chamber. It is a continuous, overlapping roar of creation: the *hiss-spit* of water hitting newly materialized, kiln-hot slate; the *creak-groan* of wet wooden wedges swelling in their joints; the loud, wet *pop* of bloated seed-pods bursting against copper-sheathed beams; the relentless *slap-drip-chug* of water running through hand-carved wooden gutters that wind around your knees and shoulders. Beneath it all is a low, rhythmic thrum—like a massive potter’s wheel spinning just behind the bricks, or the heartbeat of whatever altitude-sick dream is manufacturing this place.

Light is scarce, smothered by the sheer volume of leaves and scaffolding. What little exists does not come from the sky, nor from fire. It seeps out of the raw materials themselves—a dim, bruised-orange incandescence glowing deep within the seams of freshly poured mortar, cooling to a dull, muddy purple as the clay sets. Where the broad leaves press hardest against the weeping stone, a pale, bioluminescent bruise-light outlines their veins, just bright enough to show the sheen of sweat on everything.

Because the space is so tightly packed, you do not walk through it; you are wedged within it, bracing your elbows against hot, slick wickerwork while a fast-growing gourd-vine laces itself through your bootlaces, its tendrils already hardening into carved bone needles. 

Life—animal life—is almost entirely crowded out by the botany and the masonry, save for a rare, singular presence. Every so often, the wet clay of a wall will bubble, and a creature will un-mold itself from the structure: a heavy, blind thing the size of a fist, half-terracotta idol, half-shellfish. It pulls its legs free of the drying mud with a loud *suck*, clicks its copper-mineralized mandibles, and begins to frantically scrape at the lush foliage, rolling the shredded leaves into tiny, precise wads of mortar, which it then uses to plaster over its own eyes before hardening back into a decorative boss on the wall. 

Upward and downward, the shaft does not open out; it simply chokes itself off with its own proliferation. Three feet above you, a ceiling of woven split-bamboo is actively braiding itself shut, sealing off the violet nothingness of the extreme heights. Three feet below, the wooden floorboards are sprouting roots of raw iron ore, wedding themselves to a foundation that has no mountain beneath it—just a hot, loud, dripping knot of hand-made matter, hanging in the thin upper dark, building itself inward forever until there is no room left even for the steam.
