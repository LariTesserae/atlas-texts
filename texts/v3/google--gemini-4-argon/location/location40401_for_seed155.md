At twelve thousand feet, the rock has been gone for so long that the concept of mineral is mostly theoretical. What holds the plateau up—what constitutes the plateau—is an abyssal, compressed mat of ancient root-iron and calcified xylem, six hundred feet thick, breathing in a slow, damp rhythm that takes days to complete a single inhalation.

This is the Loom-Plateau of Oakhaven, though there are no oaks, and it has not been a haven since the geometries of the high air began to slip.

The sky is huge, pale, and clear—a brisk, high-altitude afternoon light that casts sharp, bluish shadows across a valley twenty miles wide. Because of the altitude, the air is cool and thin, smelling sharply of bruised pine needles, wet wool, and ozone. There are no trees, no bushes, no distinct leaves. Instead, the vegetation is total and horizontal: a continuous, rolling tundra of dense, muscular bryophytes, rubbery creeping ground-barks, and silver-green vascular mats that have swallowed the ancient topography and settled into long, whale-backed swells. Everything has been smoothed by eons of wind and the slow, digestive creep of the flora into its oldest stable state. 

Yet it is a city. 

The urbanism of the plateau is not built on top of the plant-mass; it is extruded from it, spaced out across the wide, open downs in a sparse, rigorous grid that feels both vast and half-abandoned. Every half-mile, a factory-burl rises from the silver moss. These are massive, block-like structures of scarred, living timber, grafted together with rusted iron girders and ribbed with heavy, sweating brass pipes. The walls are seamless, living wooden calluses that have grown entirely over the original brick and steel, adopting their right angles, their smokestacks, their sawtooth factory roofs, and their blind, shuttered clerestories. 

The city is sparse because the industrial burls require immense acreage of root-mat to draw up the turpentine and pale, heavy sap that fuels them. Between the structures lie wide, empty avenues of grey-green turf, scored by narrow-gauge rail tracks. The rails themselves are not steel, but specialized, silicon-rich surface roots, polished to a dull pewter shine by the rare passage of heavy, clockwork-driven haulers.

It is deafening here. 

The sound does not come from crowds—there are none—or from the wind, which only hisses low across the moss. The roar comes from inside the ground and inside the factory-burls. Beneath the velvet skin of the plateau, a million wooden pistons, fibrous valves, and lignified flywheels are turning, driven by osmotic pressure and subterranean steam. The ground vibrates under the boot soles with a continuous, bone-shaking *thrum-clack, thrum-clack*, like a continent-sized power loom running at full tilt in an empty room. Out in the open, the sound echoes weirdly between the distant, bark-sheathed towers, accompanied by the rhythmic, deafening shriek of relief vents blowing pale, lukewarm mist into the chill afternoon air. 

The dampness of the plateau is entirely produced by this metabolic industry. It rarely rains from the high, bright sky, yet the air is perpetually clammy with exhausted condensation. Great clouds of steam billow from the towering wooden chimneys, drift fifty yards across the open downs, and immediately condense onto the cold, silver moss, leaving the ground slick and weeping. Pockets of brown, tannic water collect in the seamless bowls where ancient quarries used to be, shivering in concentric rings to the beat of the underground engines.

And then there is the way the plateau breaks.

The ancient age of the place, combined with the incessant, rhythmic vibration of the bio-industrial engines, has fatigued the local physics. The air has a shear to it. Looking across a two-mile expanse of silver-green moor toward a distant refinery-stump, you will see the horizon fail to meet itself. A quarter-mile section of the valley floor—rail tracks, copper-sheathed telegraph poles, and forty feet of dense root-turf—simply hovers six feet above the ground, casting a crisp, dark shadow onto the moss beneath it. Where the roots are severed at the edge of the floating block, they do not dangle; they terminate in flat, cross-sectioned planes as smooth as glass, weeping slow drops of amber resin that fall four feet and vanish before hitting the earth below.

The industrial rhythm stutters across these faults. A rhythmic *clang-hiss* from a pumping station will arrive at the ear three times from three different directions, its echo bouncing off invisible, vertical panes of hardened air. In some of the sparser districts, the urban grid repeats itself by mistake: the exact same weathered, brass-bolted wooden warehouse, with the exact same crack in its upper masonry-bark and the same plume of white steam curling from its third vent, stands four times in a row along a half-mile avenue. If you throw a stone at the second one, the sound of the impact rings out from the fourth.

Very little moves across the open spaces between the machinery. The fauna is rare, reduced to creatures that can tolerate the relentless noise and the ontological shears. Occasionally, a line of pale, six-legged ungulates—their hides looking remarkably like stitched canvas, their antlers flat and broad like iron wrenches—will pick their way carefully across a rail-root. They move with a jerky, stop-motion gait, pausing for minutes at a time with one hoof raised, waiting for a localized spatial stutter to pass before committing their weight to the turf. High overhead, in the cold, bright blue, a single scavenger bird with wings like oiled parchment circles a chimney, rides a thermal straight through a grey seam in the sky, and comes out fifty yards to the left, flying backward for three beats before righting itself.

Walk to the edge of one of the broad, shallow depressions in the city center. The ground feels like a tight drumhead over a cellar full of drop-forges. All around, for miles, the low, silver-green swells rise toward the pale sky, dotted with the lonely, titanic silhouettes of the grown factories. The brass pipes sweat cold water; the wooden smokestacks cough white fog into the clear mountain light; the whole ancient, living crust of the world shudders and bangs with the work of making nothing at all, holding its broken pieces together by sheer, deafening momentum.
