The floor of the basin is poured basalt-concrete, cast in hexagons forty meters across, each plate slightly canted to direct the runoff into open, grates-free drainage canals. There is no bedrock beneath it, only the hollow, booming architecture of lower sub-levels, and there is no sky above it, only the Vault: an inverted bowl of ribbed, galvanized plating that curves up into a permanent bank of freezing, low-hanging cloud. 

It is a subterranean expanse the size of a small valley, built to house an automated, self-quarrying coolant exchange, or perhaps a subterranean lock system for ships that never arrived. Whatever its original intent, the machinery is not abandoned. It is simply running on an inscrutable, self-correcting schedule, vast and sparse, separated by long, echoing plains of wet grey composite.

The air sits at a heavy two degrees Celsius, thick with atomized grease and water vapor. Because the upper ribs of the Vault are chilled by whatever deep-crust refrigeration runs above them, and the lower floor is faintly warmed by sub-level turbines, the space generates its own relentless, miniature weather. Mist rises from the floor vents, hits the high iron ceiling, condenses on a million bolt-heads, and drops back down as a cold, erratic sleet. 

Everything is freshly wet. The concrete shows no deep wear; its cast-marks and aggregate edges are still sharp, recently poured or recently resurfaced by the slow, wide-tracked gantry-pavers that occasionally trundle across the horizon. Yet the water has already claimed the margins. The drainage trenches are choked with an industrial marsh-edge: thick, mineral-heavy slush and floating rafts of rust-scum that behave like peat. In the shallow depressions where the hexagonal plates have settled unevenly, cold black puddles gather, skinning over with thin, hexagonal crystals of ice whenever the nearby exhaust vents cycle down, then melting again when the distant boilers kick back on.

The sound here is immense, physical, and inescapable, yet comes from very far away. Two kilometers to the north, a line of intake louvers fifty meters high inhales and exhales with a rhythmic, metallic wheeze that pressurizes the entire basin, making the ears pop every forty seconds. Beneath that is the continuous, wet roar of high-pressure sluices flushing the perimeter walls, and the sudden, gunshot *crack* of newly poured concrete fracturing under thermal stress. 

Light is rationed by the geometry of the machinery. There is no central illumination. Instead, sodium-vapor floodlights are mounted on the undersides of the mobile cranes and the widely spaced pumping towers, casting long, amber cones through the fog. Between these islands of yellow glare lie vast stretches of twilight where the only light comes from the faint, cherenkov-blue glow of submerged heating elements in the canals, or the rhythmic orange flash of warning beacons rotating in the empty dark, illuminating nothing but falling sleet and wet, empty expanse.

Life has found a foothold in the thermal gradients, though it looks less like nature and more like an infection of the schematics. Along the warm, sweating flanks of the transfer pipes, sparse mats of wire-moss—a rigid, black, silicate-heavy bryophyte—grow in strict geometric bands, tracing the exact path of the internal welds. In the flooded trenches, where the water is rich with sulfur and machine-oil, Pale Sedge grows in solitary, shivering clumps. Its blades are translucent and brittle, rattling like glass rods when the air-pressure shifts. The plants do not grow toward the lights; they grow toward the vibrations of the sub-floor, their roots prying into the expansion joints of the young concrete, drinking the warm condensation that leaks from the gaskets.

Every few hours, the basin undergoes a "purge." A series of pneumatic sirens—more felt in the sternum than heard—winds up in the dark. The great sluice gates along the western retaining wall winch open, and a sheet of near-freezing, foam-topped water surges out across the concrete flats, ankle-deep and fast. It sweeps away the accumulated ice-slush, the sheddings of the wire-moss, and the chemical precipitates, dragging it all toward the central vortex drains with a deafening, hollow slurp. 

It is during the purge that the fauna appears. They are rare, solitary things, adapted to the cold and the metal. 

Out of the high scaffolding drop the Plumb-birds—heavy, flightless creatures the size of geese, covered in dense, grease-waterproofed bristles rather than feathers. They have long, scissor-like bills of dark keratin designed for scraping oil-larvae and frozen biofilm out of the expansion joints before the water recedes. They move with a frantic, clockwork efficiency, their splayed, magnetic feet clicking loudly against the rebar and iron grating. 

Lower down, in the churning foam of the drainage canals, something larger occasionally breaches: the Silt-Eels, six-foot tubes of pale, rubbery muscle that use the flooding cycles to migrate from one thermal vent to another. They have no eyes, only lateral lines of copper-colored scales that sense the electrical fields of the submerged power cables they follow like highways.

When the water drains, the sirens wind down, leaving the basin to the steady drip of the ceiling and the heavy breathing of the north louvers. A crawler-crane, five stories tall and entirely uncrewed, begins to move across the middle distance, its rusted tracks grinding fresh, white scars into the grey basalt-concrete. It carries a single, massive concrete slab suspended from its cables, swinging slightly like a pendulum, heading toward some structural revision in the dark—building, or rebuilding, a landscape that has never seen the sun, already slick with new frost.
