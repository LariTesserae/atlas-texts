The mountain range has forgotten how to be jagged. What remains at this high altitude is the Altiplano of the Humps—a vast, rolling expanse of granite and porphyry that has been planed down over five hundred million years into smooth, whale-backed swells. The rock is no longer grey; it has oxidized and weathered into the soft, chalky purples of a healing bruise and the pale ochre of dried bone. Though the sky is close and the air is thin, the horizon stretches away in gentle, unobstructed undulations for thirty miles in every direction, a high-altitude basin that has settled into its final, exhausted posture.

 despite the height, the air is remarkably warm. The purple stone absorbs the unfiltered, normal daylight and radiates it back as a steady, dry-oven heat, only broken by the slow, periodic drift of cloud-banks that do not float *above* the land, but drag their bellies directly across it. 

Because there is no sharp topography left to catch the wind, there is no wind. The clouds simply pool and slide through the shallow troughs between the stone swells. Where they pass, they leave a skin of warm condensation. The rock sweats. This moisture collects in millions of shallow, saucer-like depressions ground into the bedrock by ancient, vanished glaciers. The water never flows; there are no rivers or streams here, only these tepid, mirror-still puddles and the perpetual, clammy dampness of the stone after a fog-bank has crawled over it.

At a glance, the swells appear textured like heavy, grey-brown velvet, or perhaps decaying upholstery. It is only when the eye adjusts to the middle distance that the texture resolves into the true, overwhelming density of the place: it is not stone or moss, but flesh and shell. 

The landscape is carpeted, often three or four layers deep, in fauna.

With vegetation reduced to a few leathery, black-veined lichens and the occasional rubbery, leafless tuber wedged into a damp fissure, the animal life has turned inward, subsisting on a closed, sluggish loop of detritus, airborne spores, and the secretions of its neighbors. The dominant presences are the Mantle-Slugs—creatures the size of overturned dories, their hides dry and leathery like elephant skin, colored to match the ochre and violet rock. They sit bumper-to-bumper across the swells, thousands upon thousands of them, so close their breathing-pores press against one another. They move perhaps four feet a day, gliding on a minimal film of mucus that dries instantly into silvery, flaking trails. 

Atop the Mantle-Slugs, and packed tightly into the crevices between them, are the Porcelain Crabs—blind, pale crustaceans with long, spindly legs that move with the agonizing slowness of clock hands, delicately plucking mites and fungal blooms from the wrinkles of the slugs' hides. Suspended between the dorsal ridges of the slugs are the heavy, dew-soaked webs of colonial silk-worms, forming thick, translucent hammocks that sag under the weight of millions of pale, thumb-sized grubs. And drifting through the warm, thin air just inches above this living carpet are the Bladder-Gnats: fist-sized, hollow spheres of translucent membrane that do not fly so much as float on thermal currents from the warm stone, bobbing gently against one another like balloons in a crowded room.

The density is suffocating, yet the silence is nearly absolute. 

There are no vocal cords here. There is no Evolutionary need for alarm calls when nothing moves fast enough to chase or flee. The soundscape is entirely tactile, micro-acoustic, and low-volume: the faint, wet *shuck* of a slug’s foot detaching from damp granite; the dry, chalky *tick-tick* of ten thousand crab claws touching shell; the soft, unison sigh of pneumatic pores opening and closing as a cloud-bank rolls over a hillock, hydrating a million gills at once. It is the sound of a massive, crowded room where everyone is asleep.

Scattered across the crests of the stone swells are the Cairns. They are the only vertical interruptions in the horizontal world. Built of the same worn, purple-ochre fieldstone, they are low, beehive-shaped domes and knee-high walls that follow no discernible geometry. Whoever stacked them possessed no mortar, no metal tools, and no sense of right angles; the stones were simply fitted together by hand, using the natural, eroded curves of the rock. 

Whatever made the Cairns is long gone, or perhaps has degenerated into something unrecognizable among the creeping masses. The structures have been completely integrated into the slow ecology. A half-collapsed stone hut is not a ruin here; it is merely a slightly steeper hillock. The Mantle-Slugs flow over the low walls like heavy dough, filling the interiors of the roofless rooms until the stone is barely visible beneath a heaving, wrinkled mound of grey-brown skin. The blind crabs use the gaps between the unmortared stones as nurseries, cramming the dark spaces with calcified, pebble-like eggs that have built up over centuries into a solid biological cement, holding the ancient walls together long after gravity should have pulled them down.

In the center of the shallow valleys, where the condensation puddles are widest, stand occasional solitary pillars—not carved, just long, naturally eroded lozenges of stone tilted upright and wedged into the bedrock. They are entirely encased in a thick, trembling rind of anemone-like filter-feeders that only open when the warm fog rolls through. When the air is clear and the sun beats down, they retract, turning the pillars into glistening, knobby columns of maroon gelatin that slowly bake in the normal, indifferent afternoon light, waiting for the next cloud to drag its wet belly across the heights.
