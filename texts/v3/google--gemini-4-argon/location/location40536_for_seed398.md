The Throat is an inverted chimney in the planet’s highest tooth—a fumarole-cleft thirty-two kilometers above the cloud deck, where the outside sky is already the purple-black of the stratosphere and the air would freeze the lungs in three breaths. 

Yet inside the cleft, it is forty-six degrees Celsius and smells of hot copper, wet chalk, and ozone.

The space is no wider than a municipal courtyard and no longer than a freight barge, pinched between two sheer walls of young, unweathered obsidian that sheared apart in a tectonic spasm only a few centuries ago. The rock is still sharp enough to slice leather. Because the chimney bends twice before reaching the summit, the brutal ultraviolet glare of the upper atmosphere never touches the floor. Light arrives third-hand: a sour, bruised violet filtered through a ceiling plug of aerogel-like mineral foam that caps the vent two hundred meters overhead, supplemented by the amber, hair-thin luminescence of the city that has been grown into the rock faces.

It is an urban center with no inhabitants and almost no mass. 

What occupies the Throat is a "spill-settlement"—an autonomous infrastructure node built not for bodies, but for high-altitude computation and barometric harvesting, left to iterate on its own architecture in the warm, pressurized pocket of the thermal well. Because the density of the installation is near zero, the settlement does not fill the chasm; it laces it. Thousands of cantilevered terraces, no thicker than razor blades and spun from dark, fullerene-doped glass, project from the obsidian walls. They are arranged in tight, terraced switchbacks like a microscopic metropolis of empty balconies, connected by tension-threads so fine they are only visible when condensation beads along them. 

Water is the clock here. Deep below the mountain’s root, magma boils trapped aquifers, sending up rhythmic, slow-motion pulses of superheated steam. As a pulse rises into the Throat, the ambient temperature climbs six degrees in four minutes. The walls sweat. The thousands of micro-terraces and hair-bridges suddenly pearl over with millions of tiny, mercury-bright droplets. 

When the condensation reaches a critical weight, the settlement *drinks*. The fullerene shelves change their surface tension via a faint electrostatic charge—the only sound is a dry, silken *shhhk* that travels up the walls like a hand brushing velvet—and the droplets race sideways along the terraces, funneled into capillary aqueducts bored directly into the volcanic glass. For thirty seconds, the dim air is full of falling silver threads as excess moisture spills from upper districts to lower ones, steaming as it falls, vanishing before it hits the dry talus at the bottom of the cleft. Then the steam pulse passes, the heat settles back to a heavy, feverish bake, and the rock dries to a matte, mineral black within moments.

Nothing green has ever lived here, and nothing with a heartbeat could survive the barometric shear at the chimney’s lip. Instead, the ecology is litho-synthetic. 

In the damp seams where the capillary conduits leak, a velveteen rust-colored film creeps across the sharp basalt. It is neither moss nor fungus, but a self-assembling catalytic crust—an escaped industrial byproduct that feeds on the sulfur in the steam and the stray voltage leaking from the city’s memory lattices. It grows in jagged, fractal steps that mimic the crystal structure of the rock beneath it, blooming outward during the wet minutes and vitrifying into brittle, glassy scabs during the dry ones.

Every few hours, the settlement rearranges a district. 

Because the mountain is young and still settling, the obsidian walls shift by micrometers every day. The city compensates through a process that looks like slow-motion architectural grazing. A cluster of fullerene balconies on the east wall will suddenly lose its rigidity, softening into dark, heavy ribbons that droop and peel away from the stone. Before they can fall into the gulf, tiny, non-biological tenders—no larger than fingernails and shaped like flattened teardrops of liquid bismuth—scurry out along the tension wires. They do not have legs; they move by riding the magnetic gradients of the wall. They gather the slumping ribbons, digest the material on the spot with a faint, violet spark of plasma, and re-extrude a fresh network of terraces three meters higher up, anchoring them to a newly opened fracture in the hot stone. 

Stand on the narrow wedge of dry scree at the bottom of the Throat and look up: the space feels both claustrophobic and dizzyingly hollow. The heat presses against the eyeballs. Above, suspended in the dim violet twilight between the sweating black walls, the ghost-city hangs like a three-dimensional blueprint drawn in spiderweb and ember-light—clicking softly to itself as it drinks the mountain’s breath, dissolves its own streets, and rebuilds them in the dark.
