The cavity is roughly the size and shape of an upturned cistern, buried forty meters beneath a crust of frozen greywacke and salt-pan. There is no shaft leading down to it, no fault line wide enough for a boot; it is a sealed nodule of air and damp stone, hollowed out four centuries ago by a self-consuming survey probe that exhausted its mandate and dissolved into the floor.

The air inside is two degrees below zero, heavy and completely still. Because the atmosphere is dense with trace xenon and hyper-saline vapor, water does not freeze into hard rime here. Instead, it hangs as a slick, supercooled film over every surface, or collects in shallow, syrupy depressions in the rock floor. There are no streams, no standing pools deep enough to reflect a face—only a pervasive, weeping dampness that beads on the ceiling and drops, once every few minutes, with a heavy, oily *plop*.

It is dark. Not the absolute black of deep earth, because the walls are threaded with the probe’s surviving nervous system: hair-thin filaments of spun ytterbium and cold-light optics that have been colonized by the cave’s flora. The light is a bruised, sub-visual violet, hovering just at the edge of human perception. You do not see shapes so much as you sense their mass blocking the faint, ambient static of the walls.

And the space is choked with growth. In the absence of sunlight, the vegetation feeds on the low-grade quantum bleed of the buried hardware. Thick, tiered shelves of bracket-lichen—black as tar and leathery to the touch—project three feet out from the stone, overlapping like the scales of a pine cone. Between them hang dense curtains of *Cassiopeia-moss*, a Paleogene cultivar engineered for deep-crust carbon capture and long since gone feral. Its fronds are brittle, translucent, and packed so tightly together that moving an arm through the chamber requires parting them like heavy velvet drapes. They are freezing cold and slick with brine. Underfoot, the floor is lost beneath two feet of spongy, un-rotted detritus—centuries of shed tendrils and crystallizing peat that compress with a slow, wet wheeze when stepped upon. 

Nothing with eyes or legs lives here. There are no mites in the moss, no blind beetles in the peat, not even worms. The cold and the high-tech heavy-metal leeching from the substrate keep the chamber sterile of animal life. Yet the room is crowded, almost claustrophobic with presence. The plants grow over and through the scattered remnants of the ancient machine. 

There are three primary nodes left in the gloom. One is a knee-high polyhedron of matte-black ceramic that refuses to accumulate moisture; the moss grows around it in a tight, respectful halo, never quite touching its faces, leaving a millimeter of dry vacuum between leaf and composite. Another is a collapsed tripod of memory-glass, its legs snapped and smoothly rounded by centuries of acidic condensation, now serving as a trellis for a bulbous, cabbage-like rosette of frost-fern. The third is buried deep in the far wall: a fist-sized gravimetric gimbal that still works.

That gimbal is the engine of the room’s slow rhythm. Every eleven minutes, as the moon passes overhead or the continental plate shifts by a micron, the gimbal adjusts its internal mass to compensate. There is no mechanical whir—the engineering is solid-state, operating at the level of lattice-spin—but the adjustment sends a brief, silent ripple of localized density through the chamber. 

When the wave passes, the air pressure drops for half a second. Simultaneously, fifty thousand beads of supercooled brine detach from the tips of the moss fronds and fall together. 

For that one second, the dark room is filled with a sound like a handful of fine gravel thrown against wet leather. Then the droplets hit the peat, the moss settles back into its dense, freezing stillness, and a faint, metallic smell—like cold copper and crushed celery—rises into the black air. Over the next eleven minutes, capillary action draws the moisture back up through the roots, out to the tips of the translucent fronds, where the droplets slowly swell in the dark, waiting for the next silent pulse.
