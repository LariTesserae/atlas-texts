The air at five thousand meters does not hold much heat, nor does it hold much sound, though it is currently holding a great deal of water. It is an expanse of dark, newly risen rock—a broad, tilted basin five miles across, lifted toward the stratosphere so recently in geological time that the elements have not yet learned how to wear it down. There is no soil here, no sand, no rounded river cobbles. There is only the raw architecture of the uplift: massive, unweathered slabs of dark grey diorite and black basalt, sheared along razor-straight fault lines. Every edge is sharp enough to slice leather. When a freeze-thaw cycle splits a boulder, the resulting scree looks like freshly shattered cast iron.

Because the rock is entirely non-porous and has not yet been carved into proper drainage channels, the water has nowhere to go. It collects on the vast, flat steps of the tilted strata, forming thousands of shallow, black-bottomed sheets of water that reflect the heavy, pewter-colored sky. A dense, freezing mist hangs perpetually just above the stone, condensing on the sharp ridges and running down their pristine faces in continuous, weeping sheets. Everything is slick. The boundary between the wet sky and the flooded rock is vague, kept in motion by a brisk, steady wind that pushes ripples across the ankle-deep tarns and drives the fog in slow, swirling currents up the incline. 

There is nothing built here. No walls, no survey markers, no roads, no cairns. Yet the basin is currently hosting an industrial-scale survey, and the machines have arrived on their own feet.

Spread across the five-mile expanse, maintaining a strict, sparse grid with half a mile of empty stone and mist between each unit, are the crawlers. They are six-legged, autonomous platforms the size of shipping containers, painted a dull, oxidized yellow that barely registers in the dim, shadowless light. They do not require roads; their articulated, hydraulic limbs step precisely over the jagged fault-steps, their padded feet finding purchase on the weeping basalt. Because the air is too thin for efficient internal combustion, they run on heavy, sealed hydrogen-fuel cells. They make almost no noise. From a hundred yards away, a crawler is just a looming geometric silhouette in the fog, accompanied by the faint, rhythmic hiss of pneumatic valves and the wet crunch of shifting ballast. 

Every few minutes, a crawler stops, lowers a central abdomen toward a shallow sheet of water or a bare rock face, and fires a core-laser or a high-frequency sonic probe into the young stone. There is a brief, sharp hum—like a high-tension wire snapping in the cold—a flare of blue light that illuminates the swirling mist for thirty feet around, and the smell of ozone and steamed sulfur. Then the machine lifts its belly, vents a white plume of pure water vapor that merges instantly with the fog, and steps forward again. They are transient, leaving no tracks on the hard rock, harvesting data or rare-earth samples and moving on.

Around and beneath these walking engines, the basin is teeming with life that has already claimed the new altitude. 

The vegetation is sparse, restricted to what can anchor itself to unweathered stone in a freezing climate. Where the fault lines intersect and trap an inch of mineral grit, tight, dome-like cushions of moss campion and grey-green saxifrage bulge upward, hard as river stones to the touch. Along the submerged ledges, long, hair-like filaments of dark red algae wave slowly in the cold currents of the runoff. 

This scant flora is enough to anchor the base of a hyper-active food web, though the true engine of the ecosystem is the water and the insects. The shallow pools are black with the larvae of high-altitude midges and stoneflies—billions of them, writhing in the frigid, oxygen-rich sheets of water, feeding on wind-blown pollen and microscopic lichens. 

Because of the midges, the birds are everywhere. Hundreds of thousands of small, plump shorebirds—sandpipers, phalaropes, and mountain plovers—move across the wet flats in vast, silent waves. They are constantly in motion, their dark feathers beaded with mist, their bills working with frantic, sewing-machine speed in the shallow water. They part effortlessly around the descending hydraulic feet of the yellow crawlers, entirely unbothered by the machines. In fact, they follow them; when a sonic probe vibrates the stone, it stuns the aquatic larvae for twenty yards around, and a silent, fluttering vortex of birds descends on the milky water the moment the crawler steps away.

Higher up on the jagged steps, where the rock rises out of the standing water, herds of pale, heavy-shouldered ungulates—something between a mountain goat and a chamois, with thick, greasy wool that sheds the incessant damp—pick their way across the razor-sharp scree. Their hooves are adapted to the raw geology: soft, bulbous pads surrounded by a hard, sharp rim that grips the wet diorite like suction cups. When they move, there is only the occasional sharp *clack* of horn against stone, immediately swallowed by the vast, empty air. 

They are watched, and kept moving, by the predators. High in the dim sky, barely visible against the low ceiling of grey cloud, giant dark-winged raptors—lammergeiers and mountain eagles—ride the thermal updrafts created by the crawlers' warm exhaust vents. Every few minutes, a shadow drops out of the mist at eighty miles an hour, the wind roaring briefly through primary feathers before a sharp impact scatters a flock of sandpipers or knocks a young goat from a narrow ledge. On the ground, pale grey foxes and flat-headed mountain cats use the straight, geometric walls of the fault-scarps as ambush corridors, slipping through the fog like smoke, their coats permanently darkened at the belly from wading through the surface water.

It is a world of constant, quiet commerce. The clouds drop their mist onto the raw, black stone; the water sheets across the flat, un-eroded planes; the midges breed in the water; the birds eat the midges; the foxes hunt the birds; and the great, yellow machines step over them all, pausing to taste the rock with flashes of blue light, humming softly to themselves in the freezing dark of the afternoon.
