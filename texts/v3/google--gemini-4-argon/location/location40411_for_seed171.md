There is no dirt here, and there is no stone beneath the dirt. What looks from ten miles up like a bruised, slate-green mountain range is actually a continuous, self-weaving mat of vascular architecture, five thousand feet thick and still rising. 

To stand on the surface is to stand on the tightly packed crowns of *megaxylem*—fluted, hexagonal trunks the color of tarnished pewter and wet sage, pressed so tightly together that a knife blade cannot be driven between them. There are no gaps, no forest floor, no duff or rotting leaves. Nothing rots here because nothing has lived long enough to die. The entire continent-sized plateau is in its first furious century of extrusion, a terrestrial skin printing itself directly out of the heavy, cold air.

The air is the source of the deafening roar. It is not the sound of wind, though the wind is constant; it is the sound of *uptake*. 

Every square inch of the packed canopy is covered in rigid, cup-shaped bracts lined with microscopic silver cilia. These cilia vibrate at an ultrasonic, phase-locked frequency, ripping moisture and carbon straight out of the low-hanging cloud deck through acoustic cavitation. The collective noise of a trillion vibrating bracts across thousands of square miles produces a physical wall of sound—a relentless, metallic, hydraulic thrumming that vibrates in the teeth and blurs the vision. It sounds like a cataract the size of an ocean falling perpetually onto sheet iron, underlaid by the deep, tectonic *oomph* of subterranean turgor pressure shifting miles below.

Because of this acoustic harvesting, the atmosphere is permanently supersaturated and freezing cold. The sky is a low, oppressive vault of pewter mist that never quite clears and never quite turns to rain. Instead, the air simply condenses on everything. Cold, slightly viscous water sheets down the fluted sides of the trunks, fills the hexagonal seams, and pools in the rubbery, dish-like leaves that carpet the surface. When a new trunk forces its way up from the deep matrix—which happens every few minutes within any given acre—it does not break through soil. It splits the seam between three existing trunks with a sound like a cannon shot, sliding upward on a lubricating film of clear mucilage, its bark still translucent and embryonic, its internal veins visibly pulsing with faint, cold, Cherenkov-blue light as it draws power from the deep root-grid.

That root-grid is where the engineering lives, completely divorced from metal or glass. This flora did not evolve; it was compiled. If you peel back the wet, leathery epidermis of a juvenile frond, the venation does not branch randomly. It follows strict, recursive, space-filling algorithms—Hilbert curves and Voronoi tessellations executed in tough, lignified cellulose and doped with heavy silicates. The plants are drawing raw basalt from three miles down, dissolving it with chelated acids, and pumping it upward to crystallize inside their own cell walls. They are growing a mountain range out of wood that turns directly into stone without ever decaying. There is no erosion here; every edge is razor-sharp, freshly minted, unworn by water despite the deluge, because the material is hydrophobic and harder than granite the moment it finishes expanding.

Look out across a valley—though "valley" is only a temporary trough where the upward growth is forty feet behind the surrounding ridges—and you see the macro-scale mechanics at work. Because the density is total, the landscape moves like a single, crowded muscle. Wave-fronts of growth ripple across the plateau. A five-mile swath of dark, blue-green crowns will suddenly swell, rising ten feet in an afternoon as a subterranean conduit floods them with pressurized nutrient slurry. As they rise, they squeeze the neighboring tracts, forcing millones of tightly packed stems to groan and shear against one another, adding a deafening, bass-register *creak*—like the hull of a wooden ship the size of a country—to the ceaseless hiss of the canopy.

And then there are the vents. Every few leagues, the packed vegetation forms a seamless, parabolic chimney, three hundred feet wide, lined with slick, black, heat-absorbent leaves. From these throats, the biome exhales its waste: massive, roaring columns of freezing, oxygen-rich exhaust and excess water vapor that punch straight up into the dim cloud cover, creating localized, rotating storms of sleet and fog that immediately fall back onto the surface to be drunk again.

Nothing walks here. There isn't room for a beetle to crawl between the stems, nor a niche for a bird in an ecology that has no seeds, no fruit, and no insects. The only movement is the vertical, hydraulic creep of the world itself, the blinding sheets of cold water sluicing over fresh, unweathered biological stone, and the faint, rhythmic flash of pale blue data-pulses racing just beneath the wet bark—an entire planetary crust in the act of booting up, loud beyond comprehension, dim, freezing, and monumentally alive.
