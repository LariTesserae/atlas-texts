It is a pocket at the top of the world—a jagged, sky-highamphitheater of black basalt that split open too recently for the wind to have dulled its edges. Every stone is a blade. The floor of the cleft is no larger than an orchard, tilted at a dizzying angle, and bounded by sheer walls that lean inward as they rise, choking the sky down to a ragged, starless ribbon of bruised purple. At this altitude, the air is thin and cold, tasting of iron and static. Even at midday, the cleft holds a stubborn, subterranean twilight; by night, the darkness is absolute, thick enough to feel against the skin.

Yet the rock is never dry. Clouds do not float over this peak; they are impaled upon it. Mist drifts continuously through the lower notches of the spire, condensing on the freezing, glass-sharp surfaces of the stone. There is no rain and no stream, only a relentless, cold sweat. Water gathers in dark beads on the basalt facets, runs in slow, syrupy rivulets down the cleavage planes, and collects in shallow, knife-edged basins in the floor.

If you shine a light into the gloom—though there is no light here, save the faint, erratic bioluminescence of the grease-moss—you realize the walls are not stone at all. The stone is buried beneath a living coat of bodies.

Because the air outside the cleft is a lethal, freezing gale, everything on the mountain has crawled into this single split to survive, packing the space until it is heavy and suffocatingly dense. The walls are shingled with thousands of creatures that look like oversized, leather-winged horseshoe crabs, their carapaces slick with condensation, overlapping like slate tiles. Beneath them, wedged into the fresh, razor-sharp fractures of the rock, are pale, blind, multi-limbed climbers—things halfway between a spider and a starved macaque—huddled in tangled, shivering knots ten deep. 

They do not move quickly. The cold and the thinness of the air enforce a drugged, glacial metabolism. A limb shifts an inch over five minutes; a head turns with the agonizing slowness of a clock hand. Yet because there are tens of thousands of them packed into this stone room, the cumulative sound is deafening. 

It is a dry, hydraulic roar. The constant, slow scraping of chitin against fresh basalt; the wet, rhythmic clicking of thousands of gill-books laboring in the thin air; the low, resonant humming of the huddled mammalian-things, vibrating together to generate heat. The acoustics of the narrow chamber trap the noise and bounce it back and forth between the unweathered walls until the air itself feels crowded with sound—a relentless, grinding chorus of slow-motion survival.

Vegetation is an afterthought, existing only where the animals allow it. In the highest, wettest cracks, just out of reach of the clustered bodies, tough, wire-thin creepers hang down like black iron hair, drinking the mist. Pale, bloated tubers wedge themselves into the gravel at the bottom of the cleft, nourished by the thick carpet of shed carapaces, dung, and the mineral-rich slime that drips from the crowded ledges above. 

Here on the floor, among the jagged talus and the shallow, scummy puddles, the slowness of the place reveals its strangest habit. There are no buildings here, no fire, no metal, no cloth. The creatures are animals. Yet the larger, pale-limbed ones possess a dull, patient compulsion to arrange the world. 

Over weeks and months, moving with their terrible, cold-stiffened lethargy, they use heavy, unmodified wedges of fallen basalt to score the softer soapstone boulders at the center of the cleft. They do not build shelters. Instead, they knap the razor-sharp volcanic glass into crude, heavy hand-axes and wedges, only to stack them in meticulous, waist-high cairns in the dark. 

Around the shallow pools of condensation, where the water is black with organic silt, dozens of these creatures sit shoulder to shoulder, nearly motionless in the freezing gloom. In their heavy, calloused hands, they hold long splinters of slate. Slowly—so slowly the eye can barely register the intent—they saw the slate back and forth against the basalt edges, cutting notches into the stone. *Scrape. Pause. Scrape.* It takes an hour to deepen a groove by a millimeter. Millions of these notches cover the lower walls, layered over one another until the rock face is a dense, tactile braille of meaningless tally marks. 

When a stone tool breaks, or a creature dies and slowly slides from its perch on the wall, the fall is the only fast thing in the world—a sudden, clattering crash that echoes through the dark, followed by a slow, rippling adjustment as thousands of damp bodies shift an inch to claim the newly opened space, and the grinding, wheezing roar of the cleft settles back into its ancient, patient rhythm.
