There is no rock here. If you drop a wrench from one of the upper catwalks, it will fall for nine seconds, striking angled flange after angled flange, ringing out a descending scale of hollow, metallic notes before vanishing into the bright, hazy gulf below. Everything underfoot and overhead is riveted plate, cast-iron trusswork, and braided steel cable, rising out of a cloud-deck so far down it looks like a flat floor of blinding white wool. 

The structure—if a thing the size of a mountain range can be called a structure—is a series of colossal, terraced boiler-works and distillation towers arranged across a five-mile span of open, dizzying air. Whatever foundation holds it up is lost kilometers below in the crushing murk. Up here, the air is thin and razor-sharp, yet the landscape is baked to a greenhouse warmth by two competing forces: the relentless, unfiltered glare of the high-altitude sun, and the deep, throbbing radiant heat of the machinery itself.

Centuries of operation without maintenance have brought the ironwork into a settled, corroded equilibrium. The metal has not failed; it has simply softened. Rivet heads have swollen into porous rust-nodules the size of fists. Great flanged exhaust pipes have split along their seams like drying seed pods, and the vibration of the engines has shaken the loose flake-rust down into the junctions of the girders, where it has packed together over generations into a dense, ochre-colored soil. 

Out of this ferric dirt grows a thick, hanging jungle. Because there is no rain at this altitude, the ecology is entirely dependent on the plumbing. The machinery sweats. Miles of copper condenser coils, chilled by expansion valves, draw moisture from the thin air or leak pressurized steam from their gaskets. Every valve house is the headwaters of a warm, mineral-heavy trickle. This intermittent dampness feeds massive curtains of rust-drinking ferns, waxy-leafed vines with roots as strong as hawser cables, and bulbous, heat-loving epiphytes that wrap themselves around the hot water mains like green insulation. Where a steam pipe vents continuously, a localized cloud forest forms in miniature—fifty yards of dripping, emerald-green moss and broad-leafed canopy suspended over a three-thousand-foot drop, thriving in the permanent hiss of artificial fog. Where the pipes are dry and sealed, the metal remains bare, bleached pale orange and silver by the fierce ultraviolet light, too hot to touch at midday.

The scale of the place is legible only by looking across the great central chasm, where three main gantry-spines run parallel, connected by sagging suspension bridges and thick bundles of electrical conduit. From a distance, the opposing trusses look like cliff faces overgrown with hanging gardens, pierced here and there by the dark, round mouths of ventilation shafts and the slow, flashing brass of rotating governor assembly wheels.

Nothing moves quickly, except the birds and the insects. The machinery operates on a monumental, patient cadence. Deep inside the plated hulls of the lower tiers, pistons the width of cottages slide forward and back, taking two full minutes to complete a single stroke. You do not see them move so much as you feel the shift in the deck plates beneath your boots—a heavy, oiled *thrum* that travels up the shinbones, followed ten seconds later by a long, sighing exhalation of steam from a stack half a mile away. Great cog-wheels, thirty feet across and furred with green lichen on their non-bearing surfaces, turn so slowly that small lizards bask on their teeth, riding a single gear-tooth up into the sunlight over the course of an afternoon before hopping off onto a stationary bracket.

The noise is constant, vast, and layered, filling the open bowl of the trusses. There is no sharp clatter here; everything is too massive and too worn for high-pitched friction. Instead, there is a baseline harmonic rumble—a bronze-toned chord produced by thousands of turbines and dynamo brushes spinning in grease-packed housings. Over this sits the wet, rhythmic *chuff* of the slow-stroke engines, the constant sibilance of leaking manifolds, and the creak of expansion joints adjusting to the sun. 

And layered over the machine-noise is the din of the things that live in it. 

Because the iron stays warm through the freezing high-altitude nights, life is packed tightly against the metal. The air between the girders is thick with heavy-bodied, metallic-sheened beetles that drone like small motors as they fly from one warm transformer housing to the next. Flocks of soot-winged swifts nest in the thousands inside the acoustic baffles of the exhaust stacks, bursting out into the blinding canyon whenever a pressure-relief valve blows, wheeling through the white steam-plumes to catch parboiled gnats. In the dense tangles of cable-vine and ivy, long-limbed, rust-colored primates—or something very like them, with calloused pads on their hands and feet to grip hot iron—move along the vibrating guy-wires, their barking territorial calls echoing off the curved sides of silent storage tanks. In the warm, greasy puddles that collect in thestamped depressions of the deck plates, blind, translucent amphibians lay strings of jelly-like eggs that vibrate in time with the dynamos.

The light is extraordinary. At this height, the sky above is a dark, bruised cobalt, almost violet at the zenith. The sun strikes the brass fittings, the wet leaves, and the red-oxide plates with a theatrical, shadow-carving intensity. Where the foliage is thickest—around the leaking water-jackets on the middle tiers—looking up means looking through five or six layers of grated iron catwalks and overlapping palm-fronds, the sunlight slicing down in sharp, dusty shafts that illuminate drifting clouds of rust-spores and dandelion-like seeds.

Nothing is being manufactured here anymore, or if it is, the product is simply the heat, the water, and the current that keeps the system turning. When a massive cast-iron beam finally gives way to centuries of wet roots and oxidation, it does not crash. It settles. It groans over the course of three months, bending millimeter by millimeter until its load is taken up by the woody, petrified trunks of the strangler-vines that have woven themselves through the lattice, holding the dead iron in place in the bright, loud air while the engines below keep breathing.
