The ground is not earth, though it behaves like an ancient, waterlogged moor. It is a continuous crust of compressed, rust-fused slag and vitrified brick, laid down so many epochs ago that all sharp angles have long since dissolved. Rain and condensation have worn the foundry-stone into low, whale-backed swells and broad, shallow basins that stretch out for miles into the dark. 

Underfoot, the surface gives slightly—a dense, spongy mat of oxidized iron filings and pulverized clinker, perpetually saturated with cold, metallic water. There is no bedrock beneath this, only deeper, older strata of crushed conduits, collapsed boiler-shells, and petrified ash, compacted by their own weight into a seamless artificial geology. 

The air is a permanent, refrigerated mist. Above, there is no sky, or if there is, it is entirely occluded by a heavy ceiling of sulfurous cloud and coal-damp that hangs just a few hundred feet off the ground. Light does not fall from above; it leaks from below and across. A mile to the east, a line of sunken, automated blast-pits breathes a dull, rhythmic cherry-red into the underside of the fog. Closer by, tall, skeletal standards—spaced half a mile apart and leaning slightly like old pines—carry sputtering sodium-vapor lamps that cast small, jaundiced cones of yellow onto the wet black ground. Between these rare islands of light, the darkness is nearly absolute, broken only by the cold, wet gleam of standing water catching a distant flare.

Despite the emptiness of the flats, the noise is physical. It comes from everywhere and nowhere, pressing against the eardrums and vibrating in the sternum. Deep beneath the slag-crust, titanic, unseen turbines and reciprocal pumps are locked in an endless, heavy-industry labor. The ground thrums with a sub-audible bass note that sets the shallow puddles across the plain into constant, geometric standing waves. Every three minutes, the low roar is bisected by an immense, tearing hiss—miles away, a pressure-relief stack vents superheated steam into the cold mire, a sound like the atmosphere itself being ripped in half, followed by the slow, rhythmic *clank-shudder, clank-shudder* of a walking-beam engine the size of a cathedral, trudging in place somewhere beyond the mist.

Nothing with blood or breath lives here; the acoustic pressure alone would rupture a small lung, and the water tastes of copper sulfate and machine oil. Yet the landscape is not dead. It has developed a sparse, patient botany suited to the iron-bog. 

In the runnels where warm condensate trickles from weeping鑄-iron seams, thin, hair-like mats of sulfur-yellow algae sway in the current. Out on the open swells, spaced far apart like moorland heather, grow stunted, ankle-high thickets of wire-scrub—brittle, black-stemmed plants that draw oxidized metals up through their roots and exude them as tiny, crystalline beads along their thorns. Where a buried steam-pipe leaks steady heat through the porous floor, pale, rubbery lichens the size of dinner plates blister the ground, pulsing slightly in time with the subterranean vibrations.

The landscape is in a slow, perpetual state of automated rearrangement. The great machines below are not merely running; they are maintaining the basin according to a set of instructions whose original purpose has eroded as completely as the terrain. 

Occasionally, a section of the wet slag-flat will shudder and sink two feet as a deep-level sluice gate opens, draining a broad, black tarn in a matter of minutes with a deafening, guttural suck. Half a mile away, a corresponding swell of ground will begin to weep violently, cold water boiling up through the rusted pores of the floor until a new marsh is formed. Every few hours, the mist thickens and turns to a driving, greasy drizzle—not weather, exactly, but the condensed exhaust of the distant vents reaching saturation in the chill air and falling back to the iron floor to be pumped, filtered, heated, and vented all over again. 

In the vast spaces between the sodium lamps, the only things that move across the surface are the dredge-plows—low, turretless hulls of cast iron, slick with wet moss and rust, dragging heavy chains across the flats at a crawl. They move without headlights, guided by buried induction cables, their engines lost in the greater roar of the floor, scraping the ancient slag from one side of the valley to the other, smoothing the world down into its final, level state.
