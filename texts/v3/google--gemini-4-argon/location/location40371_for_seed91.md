The drop is four hundred feet down an unweathered fault, a clean geological rip where the plateau pulled apart recent enough that the granite walls haven’t yet rounded off. The stone is all fresh, sharp-edged cleavage and jagged overhangs, terminating in a sunken basin five miles long and half a mile wide. Because the trench is so deep and narrow, the low northern sun never touches the bottom. It is a permanent cellar, holding a dead, static pool of sub-freezing air.

And yet, you do not walk on rock. You walk on *Sphagnum* and *Polytrichum*, on stiff, frosted cushions of *Racomitrium* and liverworts, on a continuous, surging mattress of cold-weather bryophytes ten to fifteen feet thick. The vegetation here is not an canopy overhead; there are no trees, no bushes, no visual clutter to break up the vast, empty sightlines of the valley floor. Instead, the flora is the ground itself. It has swallowed the talus slopes, bridged the crevasses between raw boulders, and climbed the sheer, jagged walls in dense, velvety tapestries of olive, rust, and dark emerald. The sharp geometry of the young rock is still completely visible, only upholstered—every knife-edge ridge and boxy step of the granite wrapped in a tight, frozen skin of moss.

The air is clear and vast, giving an unobstructed view down the trench for miles, broken only by the occasional drift of freezing fog. Above, the sky is a pale, colorless strip of slate, letting down a dim, shadowless twilight that turns the mossy expanse into a muted sea of bronze and grey-green. 

The sound, however, is physical. It hits the chest before the ears can make sense of it. 

Spaced half a mile apart down the center of the trench are the intake towers: squat, brutalist cylinders of riveted Corten steel and corrugated iron, each the size of a grain silo. Inside them, thirty-foot axial fans—built to ventilate the deep-earth sulfite mines thousands of feet further down—spin at high, unvarying RPMs. The noise is a monumental, thrumming roar that has nowhere to go. It hammers against the sheer, unweathered granite walls and bounces back, setting up standing waves of low-frequency rumble and a high, tearing turbine whine that fills the entire five-mile basin. You cannot hear your own footsteps crunching in the frozen moss. If you shout, your throat aches, and no sound comes out. Yet the valley looks entirely still. Nothing moves. There is no wind outside the immediate suction cones of the towers; the deafening roar exists in a vacuum of visual calm.

Water never falls as rain here, and it never flows in streams. It arrives as heavy, drifting vapor—the exhaust of the deep earth mixed with the cold damp of the upper air—which the moss drinks directly through its leaves. Where the air touches the vibrating steel of the intake towers or the raw tips of protruding granite, it condenses into a slick, freezing sweat. This moisture creeps downward, freezing into thin, clear glazes of rime at the edges of the moss mats, or pooling in microscopic droplets on the tips of red spore-capsules. When you kneel, your knees do not get wet; the top three inches of the moss are crisp with frost, snapping faintly under your weight before the deeper,insulated layers yield like a firm sponge.

Between the great, roaring silos, the valley is sparse and open. A single, narrow-gauge rail line runs the length of the floor, its iron ties completely submerged in the creeping green mattress so that only the rusted steel rails themselves glint in the twilight, looking like two parallel pencil lines drawn across a rumpled green blanket. Every thousand yards, a galvanized steel pole rises from the moss, carrying three thick, ice-sheathed power cables that sag in long, patient catenaries from one transformer shack to the next. The shacks are small, windowless boxes of bolted steel plates, vibrating on their concrete pads, slowly being eaten from the bottom up by the slow, millimeter-by-millimeter creep of the *Sphagnum*. 

Life moves at the speed of the cold. Because the moss never rots in the freezing temperatures, it simply accumulates, compressing its own dead lower layers into dense, acidic peat while the top inches reach toward the dim light. It takes fifty years for a cushion of moss to swallow an abandoned iron wrench left beside the tracks, though the shape of the wrench remains perfectly preserved as a green mound. 

Animal life is almost entirely absent, driven out by the relentless pressure of the sound or the lack of vascular plants to browse. Only the deaf and the small remain. Down in the dark, damp spaces between the granite boulders—beneath ten feet of insulating moss where the turbine roar is muffled to a deep, womb-like hum—blind white springtails and ice-crawlers graze on fungal hyphae. Above, on the open floor, you might walk for three hours along the rusted rail line and see only a single crane fly, wingless and long-legged, crawling with agonizing slowness across the frost-tipped velvet of a boulder, indifferent to the hundred-decibel gale resonating between the cliffs.
