The basin sits eight thousand feet above the true sea, yet it holds a sea of its own. The air above the surface is thin and freezing, a colorless dome of cloud-fog, while below the surface, the water is thirty-four degrees and remarkably clear, illuminated by a sourceless, pewter-colored twilight that filters down from the high altitude. 

There is no natural bedrock here. There is no mud, no sand, no silt of broken mountain stone. As far as the eye can see through the dim, frigid water—across a sunken valley several miles wide—everything is masonry. 

It is an architecture with no memory of corners. Millennia of violent, surging currents have scoured the submerged city until every lintel, stair, column, and Causeway has been worn into the shape of river-stone. What remains is a vast, undulating topology of smoothed basalt and pale, dense mortar, resembling the bleached pelvic bones of leviathans or a landscape of frozen grey wax. A bridge does not span a chasm with sharp geometry; it loops across the void like a stretched tendon of dark stone, thinned to the point of snapping by ages of hydrodynamic friction. There are no machines, no rusted iron, no glass or wire. The builders worked only in fitted stone, and they built everything—the floor of the valley is paved in massive polygonal slabs now cupped and fluted like oyster shells; the hills are stepped ziggurats worn down into smooth, terraced mounds.

Despite the total absence of angles, the place is violently alive with motion. The water does not flow in a single direction; it boils in massive, cold thermal and barometric gyres. Enormous, invisible lenses of water—some slightly denser and colder from the glacial melt at the basin's rim, others upwelling from deep structural vents in the masonry—collide and shear against one another. 

To float in this valley is to be caught in a relentless, silent storm. One moment the water is dead still; the next, a violent down-draft drags everything fifty feet toward the smooth stone floor, followed immediately by a lateral rip-current that tears through the hollowed-out colonnades at twenty knots. 

Because there is no loose sediment—no sand to be kicked up, no silt to cloud the water—this constant, chaotic turbulence happens in near-total clarity and eerie quiet. Sound does not carry the way it does in the air above. There is no roar of waves, because the turbulence is entirely sub-surface. Instead, there is a deep, pressurized hum that one feels in the teeth and ribs: the low-frequency *thrum* of millions of tons of water pressing through smooth stone apertures, like wind blowing across the neck of a gigantic bottle. Occasionally, a stone arch, thinned for ten thousand years by the abrasive currents, finally gives way. It produces a single, sharp *clack* that echoes instantly across the miles of water, followed by the slow, tumbling roll of the broken pieces being swept down an ancient avenue until they jam into a curved drainage culvert.

Nothing grows on the stone. The masonry is too cold, too dark, and too furiously scoured by the currents to allow kelp, moss, or algae to take root. A thin, slick film of pale-grey silica bacteria coats the leeward side of some pillars, gleaming faintly like grease in the dim light, but otherwise, the stone is stripped bare.

Instead, the valley belongs to the swimmers and the clingers. 

Because the currents are so violent, the abundant fauna here has adapted to two modes of existence: absolute surrender to the turbulence, or absolute resistance to it. 

In the open water between the worn-down towers, the currents are made visible by thousands of translucent, ribbon-like eels and wide, muscular salps. They do not fight the gyres; they ride them. Great shimmering vortices of pale, blind fish spin like underwater tornadoes between the plazas, whipped into tight, silvery funnels by the shearing water, then abruptly scattered into long, streaming banners when the current shifts and shoots down a sunken boulevard. They feed on one another and on the microscopic fat-globules that drift up from the deep vents, their movements frantic, weightless, and entirely silent.

On the stone itself live the heavy things. Across the smoothed domes and hollowed viaducts creep thousands of flattened, leathery organisms—creatures halfway between chitons and rays, the color of wet slate. They are shaped like hydrofoils. When a turbulent blast of water hits an exposed wall, these creatures flatten their rims, creating a vacuum seal against the polished stone, so that the wall appears to suddenly break out in dark, overlapping scales. When the current slackens for five seconds, the scales lift, ripple, and scuttle rapidly across the masonry to grazing positions, scraping at the bacterial film with rasping, toothless mouths before the next hydraulic hammer-blow slams them flat again.

In the lee of the largest, most eroded structures—great bulbous monoliths that might once have been watchtowers or granaries—leathery anemones the size of barrels anchor themselves into the ancient mortar joints. They have no delicate tentacles; their feeding arms are thick, muscular straps that whip and snap in the boiling eddies like wet leather reins, snagging the smaller silver fish that are thrown off-course by the violent downdrafts.

Far overhead, the surface of the water is a churning ceiling of white foam and grey ice-slush, beaten by the mountain winds. Down here, twenty fathoms deep in the middle of the valley, the turbulence has no foam. It is just clear, freezing mass in constant, chaotic rearrangement, rushing through the smooth, hollowed ribs of a city that has forgotten every straight line it ever possessed.
