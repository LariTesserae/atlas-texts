The settlement—if a phenomenon of self-assembling matter can be called a settlement—occupies a shallow, steep-walled sinkhole no larger than a cathedral nave. There is no sky directly above it, or rather, the sky has been replaced by a flat, unmoving plane of pearl-gray daylight that hangs twenty meters above the highest structures like a dropped ceiling. The light is even, shadowless, and entirely neutral, the exact luminosity of a cloudy Tuesday in mid-autumn, though here it never flickers, dims, or shifts its angle.

Because the basin sits low in the surrounding flatland, the cold settles into it and stays. It is a dry, biting cold, just below freezing, except where the architecture exudes its own strange, localized warmth, creating micro-fronts of condensation. Every vertical surface is slick with a thin, greasy sweat of half-frozen moisture. Water does not rain here; it materializes on the undersides of lintels and cantilevered beams, gathering into heavy, viscous drops that take minutes to stretch and detach.

The space is choked with construction, though there are no builders. The density is claustrophobic. Narrow alleyways, barely wide enough for a pair of shoulders, wind between towering stacks of dark, matte-black modules and ribbed conduits of spun silicate. Everything is raw and embryonic, bearing the crisp, unweathered edges of geology that was calculated into existence five minutes ago. Nothing has been worn down by wind or footsteps. Instead, the landscape is in a state of slow, continuous extrusion. From the floor of the basin—which is not soil, but a tessellation of hexagonal basalt-like plates—new structures are constantly rising at a rate of millimeters per hour. A stairway will slowly telescope upward out of a plinth, its steps unfolding like origami made of slate, only to intersect an existing wall and seamlessly fuse with it, creating a blind, useless corner.

Here, at the bottom of the sinkhole, reality is poorly compiled. The rules of spatial exclusion are breaking down. 

Look closely at the walls of the densely packed towers: they are layered not just in three dimensions, but overlapping in the same physical space. A buttress of dull, frosted metal passes directly through a solid block of gray ceramic; at their intersection, the materials do not crush or displace one another, but flicker in a slow, silent argument for dominance, looking like two exposures on the same strip of film. There are doorways that open into the solid cores of adjacent pillars, and narrow bridges that cross an alley, turn ninety degrees in mid-air along an invisible axis, and terminate flat against a ceiling. Gravity within the basin is mostly vertical, but near the denser clusters of machinery, the hanging condensation drips sideways, drawn toward the heavy, humming seams where two pieces of architecture are trying to occupy the same coordinates.

Despite the overwhelming congestion of the built environment, the place is hushed. The silence is thick, insulated by the dense packing of the towers. Beneath the quiet, there is only the occasional, solitary *tick* of contracting metal, the slow *plop* of a heavy water droplet hitting a dry stone plate, and a faint, sub-audible thrumming—the sound of solid matter slowly pushing through solid matter. 

Life has found a minimal, uncanny foothold in the dampness and the glitches. The vegetation is sparse and opportunistic, consisting almost entirely of a pale, translucent lichen and small clusters of rigid, wire-thin reeds that grow out of the spatial seams where the buildings overlap. These plants do not photosynthesize the flat gray light; instead, they feed on the low-level thermodynamic friction generated by the colliding architectures. Where a pipe and a wall intersect in a slow-motion shimmer of broken physics, a sleeve of pale green moss will inevitably form, drinking the condensation that gathers at the fault line.

Fauna is even rarer. Occasionally, something the size of a hare, composed of what looks like folded, wet parchment and jointed bone, will pick its way down a stepped conduit. These creatures move with extreme deliberation, pausing for minutes at a time, their bodies subtly shifting shape to match the broken geometry around them. When they reach a solid wall, they do not go around it; they press themselves into the damp, cold surface and slowly phase through the flickering material boundaries, emerging on the other side several minutes later, glistening with condensation. 

The air smells of wet slate, ozone, and the sharp, sterile tang of deep cold. Nothing rots here; nothing erodes. The hamlet simply continues to extrude itself, packing more pipes, more blind windows, more staircases, and more impossible intersections into the narrow basin, slowly filling the volume of the sinkhole with an intricate, damp, and silent knot of solidified math.
