The chamber is roughly the size of a drydock or a sunken railway terminus, carved out of grey-green slate a hundred feet below the city’s cellar line. It is known simply as the Sump, or the Third Basin, though there is no longer a First or Second. Despite the urban weight pressing down from above, the space feels vast and vacant. The ceiling—a series of broad, shallow brick vaults supported by widely spaced cast-iron columns—sweeps up into a permanent, weeping mist. Everything here is slick, cold, and softened by centuries of slow water. 

The floor is not a single surface, but a shallow, dark lagoon interrupted by wide, sunken flagstones and low-lying brick causeways whose edges have rounded off like river stones. The water is two inches deep over the paths and six feet deep in the channels, utterly black and still, smelling faintly of wet iron, limestone, and old machine oil. 

Because the chamber is built for some vast, forgotten civic utility, the architecture is heavy and industrial, yet curiously sparse. There is no clutter. Seventy yards of dark, mirroring water separate one iron pillar from the next. In the center of the basin stands the Regulator: a three-story cylindrical housing of green-patinated brass and riveted iron plates, silent except for a single, massive flywheel that completes one revolution every four minutes. Its movement is so gradual the eye can barely track it, the greased spokes rising out of the black water, shedding drops with a soft, rhythmic *plink*, and submerging again on the other side. 

Light does not fall here; it seeps. High up in the walls, just beneath the vaulted ceiling, a single ring of heavy glass prisms—deck lights set into the gutters of the streets far above—allows a bruised, pewter-colored twilight to filter down through the mist. Closer to the water, widely spaced cast-iron stanchions hold glass globes filled with a pale, cold, amber gas-glow. The light doesn't carry far; it haloes in the damp air and reflects in long, greasy smudges across the flooded flagstones, leaving the spaces between the pillars in deep, velvety shadow.

The erosion has reached a state of quiet equilibrium. The brick walls have bowed inward slightly, their mortar leached out and replaced by curtains of slick, white mineral flowstone that look like frozen tallow. The ironwork is coated in a thick, velvety rind of rust that no longer flakes away, stabilized by a skin of clear, gelatinous slime. 

Plant life is scarce and strange, adapted to the weak, filtered light and the mineral diet of the masonry. Along the high mortar lines, pale, leafless ferns hang like bundles of green wire. Out in the water, tethered to submerged grates, float sparse colonies of "coal-lotus"—broad, waxy leaves the color of bruised plums that lie flat on the surface, drinking the faint warmth that radiates from the submerged steam pipes.

What the chamber lacks in flora, it makes up for in a dense, silent ecology of scavengers and filter-feeders that have made the machinery their reef. The Regulator and the iron pillars are encrusted with *slag-mussels*, their shells fluted and metallic, slowly opening and closing to strain the mineral-rich water. Across the wet brickwork move thousands of "gasket-snails"—fist-sized mollusks with flat, spiral shells the color of tarnished copper. They graze on the rust and the tallow-stone in slow, sweeping herds, their radulae making a faint, papery rasping sound that is the dominant noise in the room.

In the water itself, pale, flat-bodied eels as wide as boot-soles drift between the sunken walkways. They have no eyes, only rows of tiny, copper-colored sensory pits along their flanks that catch the amber light of the stanchions as they turn. And perched high on the warm necks of the gas-lamps, clustered like gargoyles, are the "vent-rooks"—heavy, flightless birds with oiled, bristle-like feathers and long, probing beaks used for prying mussels from the ironwork. They do not caw or flap; they sit for hours in the cold mist, shifting their weight once an hour with a dry click of claws on iron.

The rule of the Sump is conservation of motion. Nothing hurries because there is no season to race against, no predator fast enough to require flight. The air stays at a constant, clammy forty-eight degrees. The water level rises by three inches over twelve hours, and takes twelve hours to fall, governed by the glacial turn of the flywheel and the distant, muffled breath of the city’s hydraulics. When a drop of condensation gathers on the apex of a brick vault, it hangs for minutes, swelling until it finally lets go, falling fifty feet into the black lagoon. The resulting ring of ripples travels outward unimpeded, washing silently over the submerged boots of the statues, rocking the plum-colored leaves, and nudging the blind eels before dying out against the mineral-draped walls.
