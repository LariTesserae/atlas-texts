High up in the unmapped upper tiers of the Foundry—two miles above any true ground, where the barometer needles are permanently pinned to the low posts—there is a sealed inspection sump no larger than a cistern. It has no doors, only a heavy cast-iron manhole overhead whose bolts were welded shut before the metal had even finished cooling from the forge.

Everything here is brand new, and it has been brand new forever. The walls are thick plates of gray pig-iron that still bear the sharp, chalky ridges of the sand-molds and the crisp, unoxidized scorch marks of the cutting torches. There is no rust. There is no wear on the edges of the floor-gratings, no rounding of the hexagonal bolt-heads, no patina. It is a room caught in the exact second of its completion, held in a perpetual, roaring equilibrium.

The air is just above freezing. Because the chamber sits directly wedged between the outer atmospheric hull and the main high-pressure steam return-lines, it acts as a permanent condenser. Cold water does not drip here; it sheets. It materializes out of the dense, greasy vapor in thick, syrupy curtains that run down the raw iron walls, pooling an inch deep over the stamped steel floor before vanishing into narrow slots. The water tastes of machine oil, zinc, and unspent coal-gas. It is too cold to breed rot, and too fast-moving to freeze. 

The noise is total. It is not a sound that happens in the room; the room *is* the sound. Directly behind the north bulkhead, an unseen turbine of monumental scale spins at a constant, unchanging velocity. The vibration enters the soles of the feet and rattles the fluid in the eyes. It is a deafening, brassy, single-note shriek coupled with a low-frequency hydraulic thrum so dense it gives the air physical weight. Because the sound never varies in pitch or volume—not by a single hertz, not for a fraction of a second—it ceases to feel like an event and becomes an architectural feature, a solid wall of pressure that holds the water against the iron.

Only three cages of thick, green-tinted marine glass provide light. Inside them, carbon-filament bulbs burn at a starved, under-volted orange, casting a dim, bruised glow that barely penetrates six feet through the sleeting condensation. 

Yet, in this freezing, deafening, newly minted box, the walls are three inches thick with life. 

They are called the *shiver-ticks*, or the *gasket-lice*, though no one has ever been inside to name them. They are pale, blind, translucent crustaceans the size of walnuts, with shells as hard and gray as porcelain insulators. Millions of them pack the vertical plates, layered four and five deep, hooked into the microscopic pores of the fresh cast iron with serrated, copper-colored claws. They do not roam. They do not hunt. They exist to harvest the vibration. Each creature possesses a fan of stiff, fibrous bristles protruding from its back, tuned precisely to the frequency of the turbine behind the wall. The deafening shriek of the machinery drives their metabolism; their bristles blur in sympathetic resonance, converting pure mechanical violence into biological heat. 

They are packed so tightly that they form a secondary, living wainscoting over the metal. Cold water cascades continuously over their backs, carrying minute suspended droplets of paraffin and sulfur from the steam vents above, which the creatures filter through clicking, rhythmic mandibles. Beneath them, in the flooded troughs of the floor-grating, thousands of pale, eyeless eels—no thicker than heavy-gauge wire—knot themselves into dense, undulating mats. They never swim; they stay braided together against the current, feeding on the shed cuticles of the ticks above.

In the corners, where the cold water eddies and the grease collects in gray foam, a few sparse tufts of something like vegetation cling to the weld-seams. They are stiff, black, wire-like filaments—half-fungus, half-mineral—that grow no taller than a thumb. They have no leaves, only tiny, crystalline nodules at their tips that drink the stray electrical current leaking from the poorly grounded light fixtures. 

Nothing here grows older. When a crustacean dies, its shell does not decay in the sterile, freezing water; it simply drops into the grating, where the wire-eels strip the soft tissue in seconds, and the clean porcelain carapace is instantly crushed to powder by the hydraulic pressure of the water-slots, washed out into the greater piping of the world. Another tick immediately shifts half an inch to fill the gap. The newly cast iron underneath never sees the dim orange light, never touches the oxygen to rust, protected by a permanent, shivering blanket of bone and chitin.

The turbine screams on the other side of the plate. The cold water pours from the ceiling in the exact same braided ribbons it formed a hundred years ago. The orange filaments in the green glass cages tremble at the exact same frequency as the millions of pale shells below them. The room is finished, full to capacity, locked in the first violent second of its life.
