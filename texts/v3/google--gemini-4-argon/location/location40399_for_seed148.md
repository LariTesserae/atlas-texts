You are standing inside a hollow the size of a dry-docked cathedral, sunk low into the earth, and the walls are a town that is currently growing out of a turnip-like pith.

There is no stone here, and no dirt. The boundary of the space—floor, sheer walls, and the arched vaulting overhead—is composed of a continuous, rubbery, cambium-thick vegetable mass. It is the color of bruised celery and cold tallow, though you can rarely see the color because the light is almost nonexistent. What light there is comes from a few strung-up industrial arc-lamps whose filaments have been replaced by glowing, tortured knots of foxfire root, and from the faint, intermittent violet sparking of static electricity as the walls grow.

Because they are growing—rapidly, relentlessly, and in the strict geometric shapes of a nineteenth-century mill district. 

Along the curve of the dark wall, forty feet up, a row of tenement facades is extruding from the rind. Window frames, lintels, fire escapes, and corbels push outward from the living wall, composed entirely of unblemished, razor-sharp lignum. There is no wear on anything. The brickwork is actually tightly packed, square-stemmed tubers, their mortar-lines crisp as freshly cut tin; they have not existed long enough to gather dust or lose a single microscopic edge. A smokestack made of braided, hollow ironwood shoots toward the ceiling in a slow, observable shudder, adding an inch of height every few minutes, its rim weeping a thin, cold sap that smells of machine oil and crushed fern.

Despite the total dominance of this botanical architecture, the center of the chamber is profoundly, dizzyingly empty. The scale of the place is small—you could throw a heavy wrench from one side of the district to the other—yet the interior volume feels like a vacuum. The town does not fill the room; the town *is* the wallpaper of the room, a relief-map of factories, telegraph poles, and rail-sidings pressed flat against the boundaries, leaving the central air entirely vacant. 

Wait—not entirely vacant. If you squint into the gloom, you see that the air is full of animals, though they are spaced out on an invisible, three-dimensional lattice, hanging in the emptiness without touching one another. 

There are thousands of them, occupying the dark air like molecules in a thin gas. Pale, blind things with too many joints; heavy, oiled-leather moths the size of work boots; ribbon-like eels with the faces of terriers that swim through the cool, damp air by flexing their ribs. They do not flock or swarm. A strict spatial tyranny keeps them always three feet apart from their neighbors. When one darts forward to swallow another—which happens constantly, in a ruthless, silent flicker of jaws—every other creature in the chamber shifts three feet in a synchronized, frictionless slide to maintain the emptiness between them. It looks less like hunting and more like the shifting of beads on a vast, invisible abacus.

The air is cool, hovering just above the temperature of a root cellar, and damp in a way that doesn’t wet the skin until you move through it. Moisture does not fall as rain; it condenses in sudden, localized sheets when the pressure drops, hanging in the air as thin, vertical curtains of water—literally walls of dampness an inch thick—before being sucked sideways into the thirsty radiator-grilles of the living factories.

And the factories are running. 

Along the floor—which is a flat plain of hard, green bark embossed with the patterns of manhole covers and tram-tracks—vines the thickness of hawser cables are winching themselves through pulley-blocks made of hardened gourd. Pistons of peeled, pale wood slide in and out of gourd-cylinders, lubricated by clear mucilage. There are no workers, only the metabolic industry of the architecture itself. A loom made of thorns and tendon-like creepers weaves sheets of dark gray lichen at furious speed, feeding the cloth directly into the mouth of a furnace-bulb that digests it with a wet, rhythmic *slurp*, converting the biomass into low-voltage current that hums through copper-veined ivy along the baseboards.

The sound of all this should be deafening, a roar of industry and biology, yet the chamber is mostly quiet. Sound does not travel properly here; the reality of acoustics has decoupled from the actions producing them. A massive wooden flywheel spins at three hundred revolutions per minute five feet to your left, and all it produces is the sound of a dry leaf scraping across a slate floor. High overhead, a creature with the shell of a rusted boiler and the legs of a locust snaps a floating vole in half; the sound arrives four seconds late, down by your ankles, as a polite, metallic *tink*. The dominant noise is just the wet, rubbery creak of cell division—the sound of a million cold buds forcing themselves open into the shapes of rivets, girders, and stovepipes.

Every few minutes, the growth-cycle reaches a localized climax, and the geometry of the room breaks down entirely. 

Because the space is small and the growth is continuous, the architecture has nowhere to go. Instead of crowding the empty center, the space folds in on its own logic. You can watch a three-story textile mill on the northern wall swell, ripen like an overfull plum, and then *invert*—sliding backward into the two-dimensional surface of a leaf, its windows and spinning-jennies flattening into mere venation, while beside it, a microscopic spore on the floor violently inflates in ten seconds into a full-sized, dripping water-tower, complete with ladder and rivets, fresh and unweathered, already covered in a cold sweat of condensation.

A tram-car made of hollow, segmented cane glides along the floor-tracks. It has no wheels; its bottom simply grows forward at the front and rots away into nothingness at the back, propelling it through the dark at a steady clip. Inside its dimly lit ribs, fifty or sixty pale, badger-sized grubs sit upright on benches, facing forward, their bodies vibrating at the exact frequency of a running diesel engine, shedding a fine, damp mist of grease onto the glassless windows as they commute from one wall of the hollow to the other, vanishing straight into the bark at the end of the line without slowing down.
