The world here is no larger than a village church, and it is entirely filled with water that refuses to fall. 

High up in the spine of a jagged, newly-risen mountain range, a massive slab of translucent white selenite has split away from the black basalt behind it, creating a sealed, vertical fissure forty feet wide and fifty feet tall. This pocket is completely flooded—not by a quiet alpine pool, but by a trapped, pressurized artery of snowmelt that shoots up from a vent in the floor, slams against the pinched stone ceiling, and recirculates in a violent, endless gyre. 

Because the outer wall is pure, clear crystal, the noon sun strikes right through it. The interior of the chamber is blindingly bright, lit like an operating theater, every cubic inch of the roaring water illuminated in sharp, cold clarity. Yet for all the light, the chamber is mostly empty. There is no silt, no drifting cloud of detritus, no murk. The water is so distilled and the current so vicious that nothing can stay suspended for long. It is a clear, freezing vortex contained in a box of raw stone.

The rock itself looks as though it was split open ten minutes ago. The basalt of the back wall is a brutal mosaic of fresh fractures, every edge razor-sharp, completely unsmoothed by the water that batters it. In fact, the geology here is running in reverse: instead of eroding the chamber, the water is building it. The upwelling current is supersaturated with dissolved calcium and silica from deep within the mountain's young, hot roots. Wherever the turbulence hits a sharp corner of rock, the pressure drops just enough for minerals to flash-precipitate out of the liquid. You can watch it happen—white, skeletal crusts of aragonite and quartz visibly creeping across the black stone like frost on a windowpane, growing into jagged, geometric bleachers and fragile needles, only to be snapped off minutes later by a sudden shift in the current and swept out through the narrow drainage cracks near the ceiling. The walls are constantly budding, shattering, and regrowing.

The sound inside this flooded pocket is a physical assault. Water is incompressible, and trapped within the rigid stone box, the acoustic energy has nowhere to dissipate. There is the low, infrastructural thrum of the main current—a bass note you feel in the cavities of your skull and the marrow of your bones—overlaid with the deafening, high-frequency *shriek* of cavitation bubbles forming and instantly collapsing against the newly-formed crystal ledges. When a freshly grown shelf of stone snaps under the hydraulic pressure, the crack hits like a rifle shot in a tiled room.

And yet, somebody has been working in here.

Despite the bone-breaking current and the absence of air, the walls are dotted with small, deliberate interventions. Driven into the freshest fractures of the basalt are heavy wedges of dark, fibrous mammoth bone and pitons of hammered native copper, green at the edges. Stretched between these anchors are taut lines of braided sinew and gut, vibrating furiously in the current like harp strings played by a maniac. 

Attached to these lines are small, box-like wicker frames and stone-weighted looms. Whoever—or whatever—comes into this chamber uses the violent, mineral-heavy water as a forge. By tying a frame of split willow twigs into the precise path of a mineral-rich eddy, they let the water rapidly encrust the wood in solid, glassy stone. Half-finished artifacts hang trembling in the blast: a crescent-shaped blade being grown over a template of knotted cord; a hollow vessel of woven reeds already three-quarters turned to pure, translucent chalcedony. In one high corner, a wooden mallet, its head weighted with lead, floats upward at the end of a leather tether, banging rhythmically against the ceiling as the current surges. 

Living things are scarce in this hydraulic tumult, clinging only to the margins where the boundary layer of the water slows a fraction of an inch against the rock. In the lee of the copper pitons and behind the bone wedges grow sparse tufts of wire-moss. The plant has evolved to survive the blast by absorbing the dissolved silica, turning its own stems into flexible glass fiber; they look like bundles of green optic cables, whipping frantically back and forth, scattering tiny sparks of refracted sunlight. 

The only animal life is equally strange and sparse. Once in a great while, a pale, flat creature no bigger than a human thumb detaches from a crevice and shoots across the empty center of the chamber like a skipped stone. These are blind, cartilaginous leeches with suckers as strong as industrial rivets. They feed on the microscopic film of chemosynthetic bacteria that forms on the newly precipitated crystals. To move, they do not swim; they simply let go of the wall, allow the raging vortex to hurl them across the bright void, and slap onto a new rock face with terrifying precision before the water can smash them to pulp.

The rules of the pocket are simple and merciless: nothing soft remains soft for long, and nothing still remains whole. If a stray pine needle or a beetle is sucked in from the mountain streams below, it is either instantly shredded by the cavitation or pinned against the back wall and entombed in white stone within the hour. The light shifts slightly as clouds pass over the mountain peak outside, sending long, refracted shafts of violet and ice-blue sweeping through the roaring, empty water, illuminating the trembling looms and the self-building walls of the loudest room in the world.
