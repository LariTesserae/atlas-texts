There is no rock beneath the High Bough. If a planetary crust ever existed here, it was digested millions of years ago, its iron, silicon, and calcium drawn up into the vascular networks of the Xylem-Plain. What remains is a continent-sized plateau of petrified and semi-living cellulose, sitting high in the thin, frigid stratosphere, worn smooth by eons of dry wind. 

The ground is wood, though it bears little resemblance to the soft timber of wetter worlds. It is desiccation-hardened lignum, dense as anthracite and polished by ages of blowing lignin-ash into sweeping, low-cambered dunes and shallow basins. The color of the plain is a bruised, powdery violet—the shade of dry plum skin—fading into silver where the older root-plates have completely mineralized. There are no leaves. Leaves are for catching rain and trading water with the sky, and there has been no water here since the atmosphere thinned to its current crisp, cold equilibrium. Instead, the flora—which is the terrain itself—feeds directly on the hard radiation of the dim, copper-colored sun and the kinetic energy of the stratosphere.

Though the entire world is plant matter, the surface feels startlingly empty. The great ancient trunks that once formed the canopy have long since eroded into low, rounded domes and sweeping ridges, leaving miles of open, undulating heartwood under a twilight sky. The air is thin and cold, carrying the sharp, medicinal scent of dry camphor and ozone.

Across this wide, dim expanse, the city of the Vintners is not built *upon* the wood, but edited into it. 

At a glance from a high ridge, the settlement looks like a series of geometric fractures and luminous nodes scattered sparsely across twenty miles of the basin. The architecture relies on genetic and gravitational induction rather than masonry. Centuries ago, the builders introduced tailored retroviruses and localized gravity-shears into the ancient cambium below the plain. In response, the planetary plant grew upward in hyper-dense, hollow boles that automatically flattened their own internal grain into rooms, corridors, and cantilevers. These towers are widely spaced, separated by long avenues of bare, wind-scoured burl. Because the technology is deeply integrated into the cellular structure of the wood, the buildings heal when chipped by the wind, slowly sealing cracks with amber-like resin that hardens into a material tougher than ceramic.

Floating twenty feet above the wooden streets are the Tension-Looms—spherical anomalies of warped space that hum with a visible, violet distortion. They are the infrastructure of the municipality, drawing stagnant bio-electricity out of the deep roots and broadcasting it through the cold air. Where the invisible beams of the Looms intersect the living wood of the ground, the timber glows with a faint, subterranean Cherenkov blue, marking the transit lanes and municipal boundaries without a single physical sign or fence.

The sound of the place is immense, a relentless, dry orchestra that fills the thin air. Because the entire valley is a single, contiguous piece of tensioned wood, it acts as a massive sounding board. Miles down in the dark, anaerobic depths of the substrate, tectonic shifts in the ancient root-balls create low, resonant groans that travel upward through the grain, vibrating through the soles of the feet like the bowing of a planetary cello. Above ground, the wind drives constantly across the hollowed-out resonance chambers of the city's outer acoustic walls—structures grown specifically to harvest kinetic energy from the gales. The result is a continuous, polyphonic whistling and booming, like a gale blowing through a forest of giant glass bottles, punctuated by the sharp, gunshot cracks of dry surface-bark splitting in the cold.

This noise is amplified by the fauna, which have evolved to match the acoustic violence of their dry, wooden world. With no water to drink, the animals here are dry-shelled, metabolic engines that run on static electricity and the rich, hydrocarbon-heavy sap locked deep within the wood. 

Flocks of *Stridulators*—creatures the size of hounds, possessing six stilt-like legs made of hardened pith and bodies like folded wooden origami—skate across the polished ridges. They move with incredible, jerky speed, using electrostatic pads on their feet to cling to the sheer sides of the grown buildings. To communicate over the roar of the wind-harvesters, they rub their serrated thorax plates together, producing a deafening, metallic shriek that echoes across the sparse plazas. 

Beneath them, the flat expanses between the buildings are patrolled by the *Augers*. These are heavy, low-slung beasts armored in overlapping plates of dead bark. Instead of mouths, they possess rotating, diamond-hard beaks driven by biological induction motors in their necks. They move in slow herds across the valley floor, pausing to press their snouts against the ground and drill into the petrified wood with a high-pitched, dentist-drill whine, drinking the thick, volatile turpentines that serve as blood for the deep strata. Where the Augers drill, the city’s maintenance flora—tiny, engineered tumbleweeds of metallic moss—quickly roll in, drawn by the scent of the exposed sap, and plug the holes with fast-growing calluses that hiss as they cure in the cold air.

Overhead, the sky is a deep, dusty indigo, even at midday. The distant sun hangs like a cooling ember, casting long, sharp shadows across the violet wood. Periodically, a weather system moves through the valley—not of rain, which is impossible, but of Spore-Static. 

A great, billowing bank of black and gold dust rises from the eroded canyons to the west. As it rolls over the sparse towers of the town, the millions of microscopic, dry spores rub against one another in the frigid air, generating massive potential differentials. The Tension-Looms flare bright white as they drink in the sudden surge of atmospheric power. Sheet lightning, entirely silent in the thin air, flickers continuously through the dust cloud, turning the dim landscape into a stuttering, strobing relief of purple ridges and glowing blue streets. The Stridulators go into a frenzy, leaping twenty feet into the low-gravity gusts to catch the charged spores in their electrostatic bristles, their collective rattling rising to a crescendo that vibrates the very walls of the living towers, while deep underfoot, the ancient, drying world-tree shifts its weight and answers with a long, timbered sigh.
