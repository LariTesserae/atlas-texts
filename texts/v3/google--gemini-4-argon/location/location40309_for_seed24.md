The place is a sunken limestone basin, roughly the size of an amphitheater, dropped into the floor of a vast, low-elevation desert. Because the surrounding plains sit under a permanent, choking blanket of dark dust, only a faint, ember-red twilight ever filters down into the depression. Yet, fifty feet above the basin floor, the overhanging stone lips nearly meet, creating the sensation of a roofless cathedral or a vast, warm room. 

The air inside is motionless and holds a steady, bathwater warmth—around eighty-five degrees—retained by the dark, heat-drinking dolostone of the walls. There are no thermal currents here, no wind, and no rain. The erosion that shaped this place happened epochs ago. Ancient, vanished waters carved the tiered ledges, fluted the pillars, and smoothed the alcoves into soft, kidney-shaped recesses. Then the water dried completely, replaced by a century of wind that sanded the stone to a matte, velvety finish before the winds, too, died away. Now, the geology is in a state of complete, dusty equilibrium. The floor is covered in a ankle-deep layer of fine, dry silt the color of cocoa and ash, undisturbed by any breeze. 

Nothing drips. Nothing trickles. The closest thing to moisture is a sluggish, invisible exhalation of warm, mineral-heavy vapor that rises from deep fissures along the perimeter. 

This faint humidity is enough to sustain the basin’s only ecology. The vegetation is sparse, spaced out with deliberate, geometric regularity to avoid competing for the meager vapor. Growing directly from the bare stone ledges are low, sprawling bushes that resemble bundles of rusted iron wire. Their stems are woody and brittle, utterly leafless, covered in a microscopic gray fuzz that traps the rising subterranean dampness. Every ten or twelve feet across the silt floor sits a single, bulbous succulent, shaped like a resting boulder and colored a bruised, sunless purple. They grow perhaps an inch a century, their skins tough as cured hide, coated in a chalky wax that prevents evaporation. Between these scattered plants lies only the smooth, velvety dirt and the smoothed ribs of bedrock.

There are no animals here. No insects click in the brush; no bats hang from the overhangs; no lizards track the dust. Without wind or fauna to carry pollen, the plants reproduce solely by dropping heavy, dry seeds that roll a few feet down the slight incline of the floor and wait decades to germinate. 

Because nothing moves, the silence is immense and padded. The thick silt on the floor absorbs sound, and the rounded, weathered pockets of the limestone walls prevent echoes. The only native noise occurs once every few hours: the dry, papery *tick* of a wire-bush expanding slightly in the ambient warmth, or the whisper of a few grains of gypsum dust detaching from the ceiling to land soundlessly in the powder below.

Near the center of the basin floor, nestled between three low boulders, lies the only evidence that anything with hands ever entered this place. There is no shelter, no fire ring, and no stacked stone. There is only a flat, knee-high slab of indigenous chert, its upper surface worn into a shallow, polished saddle. Resting beside it in the dust are two fist-sized hammerstones of heavy black basalt, brought from somewhere outside the basin, and a scattering of sharp flint flakes. 

A few yards away, wedged into the dry cleft of a rock, is a digging stick made from the heavy, iron-hard root of a wire-bush, its tip charred for hardness and scraped to a bevel with a stone edge. Half-buried in the silt near the anvil stone are three wide, shallow bowls carved from soft soapstone, used for catching the chalky wax scraped from the boulder-plants. 

Whoever used these tools left a long time ago, though in this dry, windless dark, "a long time" could mean yesterday or three thousand years. The footprints they left in the heavy silt have slumped into faint, unreadable dimples, softened only by gravity. The basalt hammerstones sit at the exact angle they were dropped. The flint flakes are still razor-sharp, unrusted and unweathered. The chamber simply holds them in its warm, dark quiet, waiting for nothing, entirely finished with its own history.
