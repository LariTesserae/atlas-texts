There is no dirt under the Sump-Decks, and no bedrock beneath the pilings. Go down through the cast-iron floor grates, past the baffle-tiers and the condensation troughs, and there is only more iron—bolted, flanged, and riveted into a foundational gloom that smells of warm tallow and wet rust. 

Yet you are standing under an open sky. The light is broad, flat, and cream-colored, pouring down through a high overcast that never thins and never thickens. It illuminates a horizon that refuses to curve or break: an endless, low-relief plain of cast-steel manifolds, sunken sluice-beds, copper cooling-fins, and brick-lined condenser basins, all of it packed tight as watch-gears and stretching out for thousands of miles. There are no towers. Nothing rises higher than a two-story boiler house or a stout gantry crane, so the sky feels immense, heavy, and very close.

The air is the temperature of blood. It carries a constant, saturating sweat—not rain, which would require the violence of weather, but a perpetual, warm condensation. Every surface weeps. The grey iron plates of the walkways are slick with a film of mineral-heavy water. Fat drops gather on the undersides of brass grease-cups, swell until they reflect the pale sky in miniature, and fall with a soft *plink* into the ankle-deep standing water of the spill-basins below. Where the iron is newest—and nearly all of it looks cast only weeks ago, the mold-seams still crisp on the bolts, the serial numbers sharp-edged and unpitted—the metal gleams like dark wet slate. Only in the lowest seams does a bright, velvety orange oxidation take hold, looking less like decay and more like a mineral paste applied to seal the joints.

Nothing is turning. That is the first strangeness of the Sump-Decks. The great flywheels stand locked in place, their steel rims beaded with moisture. The walking-beams of the pumps are paused at mid-stroke. Yet the landscape is not dead; it is held in a state of pressurized, thermal equilibrium. Put your hand against a four-foot intake pipe and you will feel a profound, muffled thrum—not the clatter of pistons, but the steady, frictionless hold of a system balanced at exact pressure. A warm draft breathes continuously from the louver-vents, smelling of vulcanized rubber, distilled water, and warm brass. The engines do not need to move because their work is already done, or is being done by the sheer static tension of their arrangement.

And because the machinery is still and warm and wet, the living things have claimed every cubic inch of its geometry.

At first glance, the decks appear to be upholstered in wet suede and stacked ceramic plates. Step closer—though you cannot step anywhere without nudging something aside—and the suede resolves into millions of overlapping, palm-sized creatures as flat as flounders. They are the *gasket-slugs*, eyeless and leathery, coloured the exact dull plum of priming paint. They plaster themselves over the warm boiler-casings and along the flanges of the pipes, packed so tightly their mantles curl against one another like dried leaves. They do not visibly crawl; they shift at the speed of clock-hands, grazing on the thin, greasy biofilms that precipitate out of the vents, drinking the warm runoff that channels between their bodies.

In the sluices and the grid-lined basins where the water sits three inches deep, the crowding is even thicker. There are no plants—no moss, no algae, no reeds—only an unbroken, tiered pavement of *valve-crabs* and segmented, blind porcelain-worms. The crabs have square, box-like carapaces the color of tarnished pewter, engineered by evolution or design to wedge perfectly into the four-inch squares of the floor grates. Thousands of them sit flush with the iron, forming a living cobblestone. When a drop of warm grease falls from an overhead fitting, a dozen square shells tilt upward in unison, their delicate, feather-like mouthparts sweeping the water with a sound like a dry paintbrush drawn across silk. 

Look down any maintenance trench—they run straight as ruler-lines toward the pale horizon—and the air itself is dense with slow, bulbous flyers. They are the size of grapefruits, translucent and bladder-like, kept aloft by warm hydrogen gut-sacs and paddling through the humid air with four small, oiled-skin fins. They drift in sluggish, crowded currents between the pipe-runs, bumping softly against the pressure gauges and against each other with a faint, rubbery *thud*. 

Despite the staggering tonnage of flesh, shell, and iron, the world is remarkably hushed. There are no calls, no mating shrieks, no territorial displays, and no predators to trigger a panic. The ecology is as static and balanced as the machinery it inhabits. The flyers drift; the slugs leach heat from the boilers; the crabs filter the runoff; when one dies, it softens and dissolves into the warm sluice-water within hours, its minerals drawn back into the grates to feed the blind worms packed into the bolt-holes below. 

The only sounds are small, wet, and intimate. The steady, asynchronous *drip-pat-drip* of condensate falling from a million cast-iron lips. The faint, glassy click of ten thousand square crabs adjusting their footing in the grates by a millimeter. The soft, hydraulic sigh of a pressure-relief valve equalizing itself three miles away, a sound that travels flat and clear across the low roofs. 

You can walk for days along the catwalks—sliding your boots rather than lifting them, so as not to crush the dense carpets of plum-colored slugs that part sluggishly before your toes and seal back together behind your heels—and the view will not alter. The sky will not darken toward evening; the bright, shadowless overcast is permanent, lit by a sun that seems smeared across the entire vault of the atmosphere. The air will not cool. You will pass the same series of stout, salt-glazed ceramic insulators, the same clusters of six-bolt pipe flanges, the same sunken basins brimming with warm, slightly oily water and paved with pewter shells. Every mile is a repetition of the mile before it, fresh from the foundry, slick with sweat, and silent under the weight of its quiet, breathing cargo.
