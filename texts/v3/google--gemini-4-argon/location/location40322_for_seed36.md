The drop from the surface rim is abrupt, a sheer collapse of the upper crust that reveals a sunken basin several miles wide. Down here, there is no sky in the traditional sense, yet the light is blinding. The roof of the depression is not rock, but a continuous, tightly woven canopy of translucent, bladder-like leaves that filter the harsh exterior sun into a shadowless, incandescent glare. It is a greenhouse ceiling stretched over a geological birth-scar. 

Nothing here has ever rotted, and nothing has ever worn away. 

The bedrock at the bottom of the basin is raw, newly cooled basalt, frozen in the exact ripples and ropy folds of its emergence. The edges of the stone are still glass-sharp, entirely untouched by wind or water. Over and through this pristine volcanic floor, the vegetation has surged and then locked into a state of dense, hot equilibrium. There is no soil—no humus, no leaf litter, no decay. Instead, the ground is a three-dimensional mattress of interlocking, Pale-green root-buttresses and bulbous tubers. To walk here is to step from one firm, living knuckle of wood to the next, suspended ten feet above the pristine black stone visible in the bright gaps below.

The heat is immense, a heavy, radiant warmth that presses against the skin like a hot cloth. Because there is no wind down in the basin, the air sits completely still, thick with the dry, resinous scent of baked pine needles and sweet sap. Yet it is not arid. The heat draws moisture up from deep subterranean vents, which immediately condenses on the waxy, non-porous surfaces of the plants. Water never flows here; it collects. Every broad, paddle-shaped leaf holds a quivering, mercury-like bead of warm water in its center. The joints where branches meet form deep cups filled with clear, bath-temperature pools. Occasionally, a single heavy drop spills over a waxy lip, hitting a lower leaf with a sharp, percussive *tack* that echoes briefly in the quiet before the stillness reasserts itself.

The plants fill the valley in layered, architectural density, crowding every cubic foot of space without choking one-another out. Everything grows with geometric precision, as if the entire flora reached its maximum capacity at the exact same moment and simply paused. Thick, silica-armored stalks rise like fluted columns, their surfaces glittering in the bright, diffused light. Between them hang dense curtains of stiff, translucent vines that resemble pulled glass, refracting the glare into pale yellow and white halos. There are no brown edges on the leaves, no wilting petals, no blight. The biology is completely static, maintained by the constant geothermal warmth and the endless, unshifting light.

Scattered throughout this dense botanical matrix are the structures. They are small, no larger than a single room, and spaced hundreds of yards apart. Some are built directly onto the sharp basalt shelves below the root-mat, constructed from unmortared slabs of raw, jagged stone stacked with meticulous balance. Others are woven directly from the living, silica-stiffened branches of the canopy, forming hollow spheres and teardrops suspended mid-air. 

These huts and platforms are entirely empty, devoid of tools, ash, or refuse, yet they look as though they were finished an hour ago. Because there is no erosion and no biological decay, a stone placed upon another stone remains there forever; a branch bent and tied into an arch holds its tension permanently. Faint marks—lines scored into the waxy bark of the larger trunks, or chipped into the glassy basalt—serve as trails connecting the scattered shelters. The marks are fresh, the exposed inner wood pale and unoxidized, though no one walks the paths.

Animal life is an exception here, a rare punctuation in the plant-mass. You might sit in the blazing, humid quiet for three hours and see nothing move, not even the tremor of a leaf. Then, a single beetle, the size of a plum and the color of polished copper, will walk slowly across a translucent vine. Its footsteps make a dry, ticking sound against the stiff plant fibers. Farther off, in the lower layers where the roots grip the sharp stone, a pale, long-necked lizard might sit completely motionless for days, its throat pulsing once every few minutes, drinking the warm condensation that collects on its own scales. 

The soundscape is defined by this tension between density and stasis. There is no rustling, no chorus of insects, no running water. There is only the low, almost imperceptible hum of the earth’s heat beneath the basalt, the occasional resonant *pop* of a turgid seed-pod equalizing its internal pressure in the heat, and the sporadic, heavy drip of condensation falling from level to level through the bright, crowded quiet.
