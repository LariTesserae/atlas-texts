The settlement occupies a high, steep-sided sinkhole—a mountain notch no wider than a town square, where the rock itself has long since vanished beneath five centuries of root and bryophyte. There is no dirt here, and no exposed stone. What appears to be the ground is a twelve-foot-thick mattress of living peat, braided willow-roots, and dense, rubbery cushion-mosses that drink the constant mountain runoff. Step anywhere off the boardwalks and a boot sinks to the ankle with a cold, gaseous *schluck*, black water immediately welling up to fill the print.

The air is sharp, smelling of crushed fern, wet wool, and bird lime. It sits at that crisp, raw temperature where breath ghosts white in the shade, though the high sun—when it breaks through the racing mountain scud—strikes the wet leaves with a blinding, silver-white glare. 

Because the basin is small and the water never stops coming down from the snowfields above, the architecture has been forced upward into a tight, tiered knot. It is an urban warren built entirely through pleaching, grafting, and the rudimentary hewing of green wood. Generations of inhabitants using slate wedges, bone augers, and tallow-greased bindings have coaxed the native choke-cedars and bog-myrtles into growing as walls, trusses, and suspension cables. Where a bridge is needed across the roaring central sluice, five living trees have been bent horizontal from either bank, their bark stripped at the meeting points and bound together with rawhide until the cambium fused into a single, load-bearing span. The houses are bulbous, woven cages of living osier, their walls packed three feet thick with sphagnum moss that continues to grow, turning every dwelling into a shaggy, dripping hillock punctured by wooden smoke-holes and bladder-skin windows.

Nothing is still. The sinkhole is a wind-trap and a rookery, and the sheer volume of animal life makes the air feel crowded, almost solid. 

Tens of thousands of cliff-swallows, alpine choughs, and heavy, slate-colored mountain ibises nest directly in the living walls of the buildings, burrowing into the moss-insulation alongside the humans. Their wings beat a constant, dry rattle through the narrow alleys between the woven towers. Every horizontal branch and roof-ridge is slick and white-streaked, bustling with fat, mottled timber-pigeons that refuse to yield right-of-way to foot traffic. Below, in the dark, flooded under-story beneath the root-mat, the water boils with black-backed salamanders and blind, whiskered mud-fish that thrash in the wooden wicker traps suspended from kitchen floorboards. Large, shaggy mountain goats—semi-feral, with horns notched by bone knives to mark lineage—clatter across the steep, living roofs, grazing directly on the bedrooms of the townsfolk, sending showers of cold water droplets and dislodged moss raining down onto the walkways below.

The noise is relentless. It has the acoustic weight of an indoor bazaar held inside a waterfall. Beneath everything is the bass rumble of the central cataract plunging into the root-sieve at the bottom of the bowl, accompanied by the rhythmic *thump-clack* of scores of primitive wooden water-hammers—simple levered beams driven by overflowing wooden troughs, pounding flax, fulling wool, or crushing oil-seeds with no human hand near them. Layered over the water and the hammers is the deafening, raucous static of the rookeries: ten thousand territorial shrieks, bill-clatterings, and the whistling wing-beats of starling-like flocks that wheel through the basin in tight, panicked figure-eights whenever a mountain hawk drops over the rim.

To live here is to engage in perpetual, frantic maintenance against both growth and saturation. The town is an anthill of small, urgent repairs. Because the buildings are alive, they do not settle; they pull. A house’s northern corner will grow three inches higher than its southern corner during a wet month, warping the doorframes until the inhabitants come out with stone adzes and notched saws to cripple the dominant roots or lash heavy river-boulders to the eaves as counterweights. Sluice-channels carved into the bark of the giant trunk-columns clog hourly with shed feathers, moss-clots, and masses of frog-spawn; children armed with long, hooked poles scurry up and down the wet vine-ladders, clearing the gutters before the backed-up water can drown a hearth. 

When a squall drops down from the peaks—which happens four or five times an afternoon—the turbulence doubles. The wind catches the canopy of the upper town, making the entire interconnected structure of grafted walkways groan and pitch like a ship at anchor. Rain hits the broad-leafed butterburs with the sound of pelting gravel. Goats bleat and scramble for the lee of the chimneys; baskets of drying bog-iron and bundled reeds are hauled rapidly indoors on screeching wooden pulleys; the streams running down the center of the woven streets swell into brown, foaming chutes that sweep away loose bark, stray poultry, and kitchen refuse, carrying it all down into the dark, churning sump at the bottom of the green bowl, where the water vanishes into the mountain's throat. Ten minutes later, the cloud tears apart on the crags above, the cold sun slams back down into the wet saucer, steam rises from ten thousand sodden flanks and thatched roofs, and the birds begin screaming all over again.
