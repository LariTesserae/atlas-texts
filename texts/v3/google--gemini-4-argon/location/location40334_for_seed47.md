At this altitude, the air is too thin to carry the roar of the wind, though the wind moves at the speed of a falling stone. 

You stand on the Spine of the Craton, a plateau of basement granite and heavy basalt that was thrust twenty miles into the stratosphere eons ago. Because there has been no rain here since the tectonic plates locked, the mountains have not been washed down into the valleys. Instead, they have been dry-milled by the wind over four billion years. What remains is an architecture of extreme verticality and absolute smoothness. Spires of charcoal-colored rock rise five miles above the plateau floor, worn down to the thickness of needles. There are no talus slopes, no gravel, no jagged edges. Every surface is polished to a dull, waxy sheen. 

The missing mountain mass has not left the plateau. It hangs in the air and pools in the five-mile-deep basins as a hyper-fine, silicate flour. 

This is where the turbulence lives. Because the air has almost no moisture to hold heat and almost no density to offer friction, the thermal shifts between the sunlit spires and the shadowed chasms create catastrophic pressure gradients. Continents of slate-gray dust, fine as smoke, erupt from the canyons in silent, supersonic fountains. Entiredune-seas of talcum-like basalts migrate across the polished bedrock at a hundred miles an hour, cresting and breaking against the stone needles in frothy, bone-dry waves that reach thousands of feet high. Yet, standing a mile away on a windward shelf, you hear nothing at all. There is only the faint, rhythmic vibration through the soles of your boots—the deep, planetary hum of a million tons of powder scouring the ancient rock, and the dry static hiss of dust brushing against your suit.

The light is a crisp, clinical white, cast by a slightly pale sun through a sky the color of dark indigo. When the dust-storms surge upward, they catch this ordinary daylight and turn it into colossal, churning pillars of blinding chalk-white and bruised violet, casting sharp, racing shadows across the basin floors. The temperature sits permanently just above freezing—a dry, sterile chill that smells faintly of struck flint and ozone.

Scattered miles apart across these polished ridges are the Anchors. 

They do not look built so much as extruded from the physics of the plateau itself. Each is a slender, thirty-foot obelisk of a matte-black material that refuses to reflect the sun. They have no seams, no ports, and no foundations; they simply intersect the rock as if occupying the same space in a slightly different dimension. Around each Anchor, in a radius of about two hundred yards, the frantic, churning violence of the dust-storms simply ceases. A boundary of modified inertia hangs in the air. When a hundred-mile-an-hour wall of flying silt hits the perimeter of an Anchor, the kinetic energy is instantly bled away; the dust drops straight down like lead shot, forming a neat, circular ring-wall around a zone of dead calm. Why the Anchors were placed here, or what they are pinning to the bedrock of this dying world, is entirely unclear. Occasionally, the air three inches above an Anchor’s tip will pixelate—a brief geometric refraction of the sunlight—and a thread of violet plasma will discharge into the dirt to balance the immense electrostatic charge of the surrounding storm.

Life has found a way to use both the storm and the machines. 

In the leeward shadows of the stone needles, and clinging to the perimeter rings of the Anchors, grows the wire-gorse. It is a dry, woody net of grey-green filaments, almost entirely devoid of water, its vascular system filled instead with a thick, anti-freezing lipid. It does not root in soil; it grips the micro-fractures of the polished basalt with talons of iron-sulfide, extending stiff, feathery fans into the gale to comb microscopic trace minerals and airborne nitrates out of the racing dust. 

Higher up, riding the silent, violent updrafts, are the Scrim. You might look into the indigo sky for three days and see nothing but the churning white walls of the dust-typhoons, and then one will cross the sun. It is an organism the size of a mainsail and the thickness of parchment, translucent and ribbed with hollow silicon cartilage. It has no eyes, no mouth, and no heartbeat. Its body is a giant piezoelectric membrane; as it is thrashed and twisted by the dry, hyper-turbulent gales, the mechanical stress of its flexing body generates the electrical current it needs to fix carbon directly from the thin atmosphere. They never land. They mate and spore in the five-mile-high dust fountains, and when they die, they are ground into the same gray flour before they ever reach the bottom of the abyss.

To walk here is to move through a bizarre contradiction of senses. Your eyes tell you that the world is ending in a silent, planetary landslide—a white-hot, hyperactive chaos of collapsing dust-peaks, twisting grey tornadoes, and blinding sheets of mineral snow hurtling across a cosmic chasm. Your ears, and the cold, dead stillness of the air inside the Anchor-zones, tell you that nothing has happened here since the birth of the sun.
