The space is no larger than a village commons, yet it holds the density of an ancient capital. It is a sunken, cylindrical pocket-sinkhole dropped into the middle of a blinding salt flat—a vertical well of warm, roaring air only two hundred paces across and perhaps three hundred deep. 

There is not a drop of water in the Drum, nor in the salt wastes for a thousand miles around it. The floor of the pit is a dry, convex lens of compacted gypsum and chalk, scoured smooth as old ivory by the thermal updrafts that rise endlessly from the subterranean heat-vents beneath the crust. 

Yet the space is utterly choked with the architecture of a city.

Because the center of the pit is an empty column of rising heat and ferocious glare, the entirety of the urban infrastructure clings to the vertical walls. It is a ring-city of primitive, breathless intricacy, built inward from the cliff faces in thousands of tiered balconies, switchback stairs, and cantilevered plazas. There is no stone here hard enough to quarry in blocks, so the builders—whoever they were, or whenever they last showed their faces—constructed the city out of calcified reed-bundle, kiln-baked salt-brick, and a mortar made from dry ash and mineral resin. Over centuries of the pit's relentless internal wind, the sharp angles of this vertical metropolis have eroded into a single, continuous, bone-white topography. Balconies sag like melted wax; staircases have worn down into smooth grooves; doorways are oval wind-throats polished by airborne grit. The city and the cliff have settled into a single, hollowed-out honeycomb of pale, baked mud.

And over every inch of this eroded masonry grows the brass-vine and the chime-kelp.

This is a lushness entirely divorced from moisture. The vegetation here is dry, silicate-shelled, and thermophilic. It feeds on the sulfur-warmth rising from the pit’s floor, the blinding ultraviolet light bouncing off the chalk walls, and the static electricity generated by the constant friction of the wind. From the salt-brick parapets hang thirty-foot curtains of chime-kelp—broad, translucent ribbons of amber-colored cellulose as stiff as parchment. Below them, bursting from every cracked plaza and disused cistern, are dense thickets of rattle-pod and bloated, gas-filled bladder-ferns that strain upward on tethered roots like moored balloons. The foliage is a brilliant, sun-bleached palette of ochre, pale violet, tarnished silver, and blinding sulfur-yellow.

The center of the cylinder—ninety percent of the pocket’s actual volume—is completely empty. 

Sunlight pours straight down into this central void, striking the white gypsum floor and bouncing back up into the overhanging tiers of the city. The light has nowhere to escape; it ricochets between the polished white walls and the translucent amber leaves until the air itself seems incandescent, a dry, shadowless kiln-glow that makes the eyes ache. You can look straight across the empty middle of the world, through three hundred feet of nothingness, and see the tiny, weathered archway of a granary on the opposite wall with painful clarity.

It is the wind and the flora together that create the sound. 

The sound is total. It is a physical pressure that lives in the teeth and the sternum. As the hot, dry drafts rush upward from the vents at the bottom of the pit, they are funneled through the thousands of carved wind-scoops, narrow alleys, and perforated walls of the vertical city. The architecture itself is an organ pipe of ten thousand mouths, producing a low, continuous, harmonic earthen roar. Layered directly over this is the vegetation. Every leaf of the chime-kelp is hardened with silica; every bulbous pod of the bladder-fern is a dry, taut drumhead filled with rattling mineral seeds. In the updraft, the lush, hanging gardens thrash against the masonry and against themselves. The result is a deafening, metallic cacophony—like a million sheets of tin and dry bamboo being shaken inside a bright white cistern. You cannot hear your own voice when you scream. You can only feel the vibration of your throat competing poorly against the vast, dry maraca-roar of the forest.

Everything is in motion, though almost nothing is alive in the way an animal is alive. The dynamic of the place is mechanical and botanical. Across the void, a bladder-fern reaches the end of its growth cycle; its tether-root snaps with a sound lost in the roar, and the pale violet sphere shoots upward on the thermal column, bouncing off balconies and scattering spore-dust across the upper tiers before being spat out into the salt flats above. In the lower wards, where the heat is fiercest, ancient wooden wind-wheels—primitive machines of notched ironwood lashed with dry tendon—still spin furiously in their stone housings, turning grinding-stones that have had no grain fed to them in generations, their rhythmic, heavy *clack-thrum, clack-thrum* adding a steady percussion to the overarching shriek of the leaves.

Fauna is an afterthought here, reduced to rare, specialized ghosts that navigate the noise. Once in an hour, you might see a Glass-Skimmer—a flat, desiccated reptile no thicker than a playing card, its ribs spread wide to form a translucent glider. It drops from an upper-tier viaduct, riding the roaring thermals across the blinding central emptiness, its skin clicking like tapped porcelain as it snaps up airborne seed-spores before latching onto a salt-brick pillar on the far side. Or, deep in the shadowed recesses of a wind-smoothed room, a solitary Silt-Crab, pale as flour, scraping static-charged dust from the base of a rattle-bush with claws shaped like wooden spoons.

To stand on one of the mid-level plazas—five feet wide, jutting out over the white abyss, hemmed in by waist-high parapets of eroded mud-brick and buried knee-deep in thrashing, yellow parchment-ferns—is to be suspended in a blazing, roaring furnace of light and dry leaf-clatter. The air smells of hot chalk, ozone, and roasted resin. Above, the sky is a neat, small circle of hard blue; all around, the vertical city shimmers and vibrates, an ancient, empty colosseum grown over with loud, waterless jungle.
