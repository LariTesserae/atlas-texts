At eight thousand feet, the rock has been gone for a geological epoch. Whatever mountains once stood here were ground down to dust, dissolved by acid rains, and digested by root systems so old they have forgotten what stone feels like. What remains is a vast, high-altitude basin—forty miles across—whose floor is not dirt or granite, but a living, saturated mattress of pale-green and bruised-purple vegetation three hundred feet thick. 

There is nothing above head-height. The density of the air is low, the wind is a constant, freezing scythe, and the sky is a lid of heavy pewter that keeps the basin in a permanent, shadowless twilight. The horizon is an unbroken line of wet, heaving turf. Despite the total absence of trees, boulders, or artificial landmarks, the space feels aggressively empty—a vast, shivering room with a floor that refuses to stay still.

The plant life here has evolved past passive photosynthesis into a state of frantic, industrialized metabolism. Beneath the slick surface of intertwined sedges and rubbery, vascular mosses lies a labyrinth of *bellows-roots* and *piston-tubers*. To survive the bitter cold, the flora generates its own mechanical heat. Across the open miles, the ground rhythmically inflates and deflates, sucking in the thin mountain air through thousands of gaping, sphincter-like stomata and expelling it in blasts of hot, sulfurous steam. 

Walking here is like walking across the top of a submerged, biological factory. The sound is relentless: a wet, rhythmic chugging, the hiss of escaping vapor, and the subterranean *thump-thump-thump* of massive hydraulic taproots pumping boiling sap from the depths to the freezing surface. Black, oily resin—a natural antifreeze—weeps from the vegetation, pooling in the hollows and giving the wet landscape the sharp, chemical tang of a machine shop. 

Because the underlying geology is completely gone, the terrain has no fixed memory of its own shape. It is in a state of continuous, turbulent revision. Driven by the violent pumping of the deep roots, whole acres of the sodden mat swell into temporary hills, groan under their own weight, and collapse back into bubbling sumps of freezing water and black sludge. 

And where the energy of this botanical engine peaks, the local physics begin to fray.

The sheer volume of bio-industrial heat meeting the glacial mountain air—combined with some strange piezoelectric charge generated by millions of rubbing, fibrous roots—causes the atmosphere to stutter. You can watch a ridge of matted lichen rise twenty feet into the air, and as it crests, it doesn't just slump; it *skips*. For three seconds, the ridge is in two places at once, a double-exposure against the grey sky, before snapping back into the flat plain with a sound like a whip cracking. 

Rivers of meltwater don’t flow in beds; they are shoved across the landscape by the peristalsis of the ground. Occasionally, a stream will hit a pocket of spatial exhaustion and simply unhook from gravity, rising into the dim air as a writhing, thirty-foot ribbon of black water before the anomaly collapses and drops tons of freezing spray back onto the turf. Distance is unreliable. A steaming vent of tallow-grass might appear to be a hundred yards away, yet take two hours of slogging through ankle-deep, icy mire to reach, the space between here and there stretching like wet elastic.

Nothing has ever been built here. There are no ruins, no pylons, no survey markers—the churning ground would swallow concrete in an afternoon. Yet the fauna is staggering in its abundance, operating as the moving parts of the landscape’s great machine.

There are no mammals or birds; the ecology belongs entirely to the shelled, the segmented, and the amphibious, scaled up to industrial proportions and moving with frantic, assembly-line purpose. Great, flat-bodied crustaceans the size of manhole covers, their carapaces slick with grease, scuttle in long, rattling convoys across the moss. They act as living valves, clamping themselves over the breathing-vents of the ground to regulate the pressure, waiting until the subterranean steam builds to a roar before releasing all at once and riding the thermal updrafts fifty feet into the freezing mist.

Swarms of translucent, spindle-legged frogs—millions of them—migrate in frantic, pulsing carpets from one boiling sump to the next, their collective croaking sounding like the grinding of unlubricated gears. When a spatial skip occurs, splitting a herd in half, the creatures don't panic. The severed halves of the migration simply recalibrate, their internal compasses already adapted to a world where North is a temporary suggestion and a straight line is a rare luxury. 

In the dim, pewter light, the whole valley looks like the exposed, beating pericardium of the world—ancient, freezing, soaking wet, and working itself into a lather for no discernible purpose other than to keep from freezing solid. The wind shears the tops off the steam-plumes, the ground shudders with a fresh cycle of deep-root ignition, and a mile away, a section of the horizon briefly folds upward like the page of a book, drips black water into the clouds, and slams flat again.
