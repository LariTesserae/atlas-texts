The room is the size of a tithe barn, though its floor is three thousand meters above the valley floor, bolted directly into the wind-sheared crown of the peak. From the outside, it is an unweathered cube of galvanized iron and wired glass, so recently erected that the grease pencil marks from the foundry still show on the trusses. There is no rock visible inside. The floor is a continuous grate of fresh, dark-gray cast iron, its foundry-sand texture still sharp enough to catch the sole of a boot. Every bolt is hexagonal, unrusted, and greased.

Yet the air inside is thick as soup and smells of boiled cabbage, hot machine oil, and wet moss. 

Below the iron grating, huge copper condenser coils—fed by some deep geothermal tap or high-pressure boiler lower down the mountain—radiate a steady, feverish warmth. When the freezing alpine air leaks through the high louver vents, it hits this rising heat and turns instantly into a heavy, localized mist. The room makes its own weather. Six times an hour, the fog thickens until the far wall disappears, hangs suspended around the iron pillars for five minutes, and then condenses all at once on the cold glass roof, falling back down in a sudden, warm, two-minute rain.

Because of the heat and the constant wet, the machinery of the room is entirely overgrown, though nothing is old enough to have decayed. The architecture is a dense, vertical forest of cast-iron columns, catwalks, chain-hoists, and uninsulated brass pipes, all packed tightly into the narrow floor plan. Over every inch of this crisp, newly forged metal runs a riot of hot-house vegetation. There is no soil; the plants grow out of thick, felt-like mats of coconut fiber and slag-wool that have been wired directly around the warm pipes like lagging. 

Broad-leafed philodendrons with leaves the size of coal shovels press flat against the wired-glass walls, drinking the crisp, high-altitude afternoon sun. Ferns with metallic-blue fronds uncurl from the flanged joints of the steam mains. Thick, waxy vines—engineered or selected for their tolerance of copper-sulfate—hang in heavy curtains from the overhead crane tracks, their aerial roots dangling down into the shallow, oily bilge water that sloshes two feet below the floor grates.

Everything is in motion, though there is no wind and almost no noise. The machinery here does not clatter; it is hydraulic and pneumatic, driven by massive, slow-moving pistons coated in clear tallow. Every thirty seconds, a counterweight the size of a piano slides silently down a greased track in the corner, pulling a series of brass cables that rotate a bank of glass louvers toward the sun. The only sounds are the soft *shhh-ock* of leather gaskets breathing, the constant, irregular pattering of large water drops falling from leaf to iron to leaf, and the faint, rhythmic ticking of copper pipes expanding in the heat.

The ecology is small, purposeful, and slightly uncanny. The plants grow at a visibly accelerated pace, fed by carbon-dioxide vents that hiss softly near the floorboards. If you stand on the central catwalk for ten minutes, you can watch the pale, blind tip of a creeper feel its way across a fresh rivet head, testing the heat of the metal before gluing itself down with a clear, resinous sap. 

Now and then, a pale, translucent crab—no bigger than a thumbnail—sidles out from the wet matting around a valve wheel, uses one claw to scrape a film of condensed mineral salt off the hot brass, and retreats. Lower down, in the warm dark beneath the grates where the bilge water pools over the corrugated iron pan, long, slow-moving eels with external gills nose through the fallen leaves and grease-drips, keeping the drain-screens clear so the room never floods.

The light comes down in sharp, clean shafts through the wet glass above, bright as only mountain light can be, turning the mist into solid-looking geometry. Where the sun hits the wet iron, the metal shines like black lacquer; where it hits the overlapping canopies of the broadleaves, the entire room glows a fierce, translucent chartreuse. Nothing here is worn down. The edges of the leaves are as crisp and notched as stamped tin, and the ironwork beneath them has not yet grown its first fleck of orange rust. It is a tiny, steaming engine of a jungle, newly bolted together and running at full pressure in the cold sky.
