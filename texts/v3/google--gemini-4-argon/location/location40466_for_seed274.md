The air here does not move, and it has not moved for a very long time. It is the exact temperature of a closed mouth—warm, thick, and completely saturated with moisture. To breathe it is to feel the weight of the atmosphere settling into the lungs, heavy and metallic, smelling faintly of wet slate, chalk, and iron.

The entire world is the size of a village square, though it is impossible to see from one side to the other. The space is a low-lying, sunken basin, hemmed in by leaning walls of dark, weeping shale that curve inward overhead, blocking out all but a narrow, choked strip of sky far above. The light that filters down through this gap—and through the permanent, warm mist that fills the basin—is a dim, sourceless pewter. It casts no shadows. Instead, it catches on the slick, wet surfaces of the rocks, making every contour gleam with a dull, greasy luster.

There is no open ground. The floor of the basin is a packed, claustrophobic labyrinth of stone and standing water. Thousands of limestone and travertine formations crowd against one another—stunted pillars, swollen mineral blisters, and tiered, scalloped basins that look like giant, petrified shelf-fungi. The stone has been eaten away and rebuilt by water over ages until there is no sharp edge left anywhere. Every surface is rounded, bulbous, and slick. To move through this place requires turning sideways to squeeze between sweating pillars of rock, stepping from one submerged, slimy ledge to the next, with knees and shoulders constantly brushing against warm, wet stone.

Water is everywhere, yet there is no current. It sits in the countless terraced pools at knee- and waist-height, motionless and clear, though the dark rock beneath makes it look black. It clings to the walls in thick, viscous sheets. Because the temperature never fluctuates and the air never stirs, the water exists in a state of suspended equilibrium: endlessly condensing out of the heavy air onto the stone, sliding down a few inches, and pooling in the hollows. 

The only sound is the water, and even that is muted. There is no rushing, no trickling. There is only the slow, resonant *plock* of a heavy drop falling from an overhang into a deep pool, followed by a long silence before another falls somewhere else in the gloom. Each drop sounds loud and distinct, swallowed immediately by the dense, damp rock that crowds the space. 

Nothing grows here. The light is too weak, or the chemistry of the wet stone is too harsh—heavy with dissolved sulfates and lime. There is no moss, no fern, no lichen. In the absence of green, the rock itself provides a mute, mineral palette: the bruised purple-grey of the shale, the tallow-white and ochre of the travertine shelves, and, in the deepest, most stagnant pools, velvety mats of rust-red iron bacteria that tremble slightly when a water drop strikes near them.

Yet the basin is not entirely untouched. Woven into the tight, natural masonry of the rock are the remnants of human work, so old and so thoroughly coated in mineral deposit that they have nearly returned to the geology. Where two swollen pillars almost meet, someone long ago wedged flat pieces of slate to form a low weir, holding back a deeper pool of water. In the side of a bulbous limestone mound, a series of shallow, cup-shaped steps has been pecked out with a blunt stone tool, leading up to a narrow ledge. 

Tucked into an alcove just above the waterline sits a squat, beehive-shaped cairn made of stacked river cobbles, its mortar long since replaced by a thick glaze of flowstone that has fused the rocks into a single, lumpy mass. Nearby, resting on a submerged shelf, is a heavy trough hollowed out of granite. Inside it lies a single, broad blade of hammered copper, green-black with patina, its edges softened by the slow accumulation of calcite. Whatever people used this cramped, warm grotto—perhaps to gather the mineral-heavy water, perhaps to soak hides or leach clay in the tepid pools—they left behind only things that could not rot. Wood and bone would have dissolved or soured here ages ago; only the stone and the copper remain, held in the same unchanging suspension as the rest of the basin.

Life is nearly as scarce as light. On the underside of a leaning slate slab, just above a dark pool, a single blind cave-cricket hangs upside down, its antennae—twice the length of its body—drooping toward the water, motionless for hours at a time. Beneath the surface of the wider basins, pale, translucent flatworms, no larger than a fingernail, glide imperceptibly over the drowned stonework, grazing on the invisible film of bacteria. 

Nothing rots here, because nothing grows, and nothing dries, because the air is already full. The heat does not build toward a storm, and the dim light neither brightens into day nor fades entirely into night—it just hangs in the mist, grey and greasy, gleaming on the wet curves of the crowded stone and the ancient, calcified steps that lead nowhere.
