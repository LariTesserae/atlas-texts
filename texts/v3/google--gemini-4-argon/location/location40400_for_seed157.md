The cleft sits two thousand meters up the shoulder of an old, slumping mountain, though the height is only knowable by the thinness of the air and the deep, chill pressure of the permanent cloud that fills it. The space is no larger than a village church, a sunken oval room where a limestone dome collapsed ages ago and the rubble dissolved into smooth, rounded hummocks. The walls do not have corners or fractures; centuries of acidic mist and seeping groundwater have licked the stone into the shape of melted tallow, every ledge drooping like a heavy lip. 

There is no sky, only a ceiling of dense, dark fog caught in the tangled crown of a few stunted, ancient yews that lean inward from the rim. Below the yews, light scarcely exists. What reaches the floor is a faint, bruised-plum twilight that never brightens into day and never quite blackens into absolute night. It is the light of the inside of a slate cistern.

Because there is no wind down in the bowl, the wetness does not fall; it simply accumulates. The air is a cold, saturated soup. Every surface is sheathed in a skin of water held in place by surface tension. The stone walls are invisible beneath six-inch-thick carpets of liverwort, dark spleenwort ferns, and clubmosses so gorged on moisture they feel like submerged flesh. There is no bare dirt, no unclaimed inch of rock. Where a fern frond droops, three kinds of lichen and a velvet-black moss are already growing over its tip.

And yet, for all the greenery, the defining texture of the hollow is animal. 

The walls, the moss, and the undersides of the fat, waxy leaves are packed solid with slow, cold-blooded life. Millions of blind, translucent harvestmen hang in tiered, motionless drapes under the rock overhangs, their long legs interlaced like tangled hair, beading with fog-droplets. Beneath them, the moss is paved with flat-shelled, slate-gray snails and thumb-sized black slugs, packed flank to flank, grazing on a microscopic film of algae that grows precisely as fast as they can rasp it away. Pale, eyeless cave-salamanders, their skin the color of skimmed milk and textured like wet parchment, wedge themselves into every dimple of the eroded limestone, three and four to a pocket, their external gills bush-like and dark red in the gloom.

Nothing hurries. The chill—a steady, bone-deep forty degrees that never fluctuates by so much as a fraction—keeps the metabolism of the entire hollow dialed down to an almost imperceptible crawl. A salamander might take twenty minutes to turn its head; a beetle might spend three days crossing the breadth of a single cabbage-sized bog-plant. There are no bursts of predation, no chases, no rot that isn't immediately absorbed. When an amphipod dies in the sodden root-mats, the bristle-tails and blind isopods surrounding it simply shift their weight and consume it without breaking the stillness of the pile. The food web is a closed, motionless sphere.

The sound of the place is the sound of weight and dampness. There is no birdcall, no insect stridulation, no rushing stream. There is only the thick, muffled *plop* of a heavy drop of water letting go of a fern tip and striking the back of a frog, and underneath that, a faint, continuous, wet *schlupp-tick*—the microscopic sound of tens of thousands of radulae scraping stone, of damp carapaces shifting against damp moss, of tiny jaws working in the dark. It sounds like a room full of wet wool settling under its own weight.

Near the center of the floor, where the water collects in a shallow, black-bottomed seep no wider than a dining table, lies the only sign that anything from outside has ever visited. Half-buried in the silt and overgrown with bladderwort are three shallow bowls chipped from soapstone, and a single short tube made from the hollowed-out femur of a mountain goat, plugged at one end with pine pitch. 

They are not ruins, and they are not abandoned. A thin, greasy residue of crushed snail-paste and fern-oil still clings to the inside of the soapstone bowls, preserved by the cold water. Someone—some creature or person with calloused hands and a flint scraper—comes here at intervals so long they do not register against the hollow’s stillness, kneels in the dark among the cushions of salamanders, harvests the cold, mildly narcotic slime of the black slugs into the bone tube, and leaves without disturbing the equilibrium by so much as a dislodged pebble. Even the knee-prints in the moss have not sprung back; instead, they have filled with water and become tiny basins, packed to the brim with translucent shrimp that hang suspended in the dark, waiting for nothing at all.
