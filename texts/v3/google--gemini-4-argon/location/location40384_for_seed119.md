It is a single hall, roughly the size of an overturned barge, though its proportions are so devoid of interior structure—no columns, no beams, no furniture, no partitions—that the space between the walls feels immense and hollowed out. There is no raw earth here, nor any memory of it. Every surface is composed of hand-packed, fire-blackened clay tiles, each the size of a palm and bearing the distinct, swirling thumb-smudge of whatever ancient maker pressed it into place. The walls rise in a steep, corbelled curve to meet a narrow slit of a ceiling sixty feet above, and the floor dips toward the center like a shallow dish.

Because of the cool draft that constantly sighs through the high slit, the air inside is perpetually at its dew point. It does not rain, not precisely, but the vault sweats. Moisture gathers on the millions of clay thumbprints overhead, coalescing into heavy, cold drops that fall in a steady, erratic drumming against the concave floor. Where the water hits, it has, over centuries, worn the fired clay smooth, erasing the thumbprints and carving shallow, anastomosing rills that snake toward the center of the dish. The floor is never dry; it is a slick, dark mirror of wet terracotta and black silt, pooling to ankle-depth in the middle before draining through a crude, stone-plugged grate that gurgles continuously, like a throat clearing itself in the dark.

Light enters only through that high, narrow gap in the corbelling, filtered through what seems to be a permanent fog outside. It arrives at the floor as a weak, tallow-colored smear, barely strong enough to cast a shadow, leaving the curved corners of the hall in deep, wet gloom. 

Yet for all its architectural emptiness, the hall is furiously alive and deafeningly loud. The acoustics of the bare, damp vault amplify every small violence into a clattering roar. 

The walls are the territory of the *clack-weavers*—pale, spindly-legged crustaceans the size of dinner plates whose shells have evolved to mimic the exact curvature and soot-stained color of the clay tiles. Thousands of them cling to the corbelled slopes, forming a shifting, living mosaic over the masonry. When one moves, its calcified leg-tips strike the fired clay with a sharp, percussive *tack*. Because thousands are always moving—jostling for position near the wettest seepage lines, fleeing the larger, blind amphibians that haul themselves up the lower walls, or mating in frantic, tangled heaps—the hall is filled with an unrelenting, deafening hailstorm of clicking shells. It sounds like a thousand cobblers hammering tacks in a stone cellar.

The floor belongs to the *oil-backs*, heavy, legless creatures somewhere between a slug and an eel, which thrash through the shallow, cool puddles in the center of the dish. They feed on the sparse, pale fringes of blind-moss that manage to root in the lime mortar between the tiles—the only plant life here, looking less like greenery and more like frayed wet rope tucked into the cracks. The oil-backs are constantly slicking over one another, their muscular bodies slapping the wet tile with heavy, wet *thwacks* that provide a bass rhythm to the high, frantic clattering of the crabs on the walls.

The ecology is a closed, vertical loop driven by gravity and condensation. Above, near the dim slit of light, colonies of soft-bodied, winged things—more like featherless bats than insects—roost in the deepest grooves where the mortar has entirely washed away. They never leave the hall; they simply drop from the ceiling into the empty air, gliding in tight, desperate spirals through the center of the room to snatch smaller weavers off the walls, their leathery wings snapping like wet sheets in a gale. If they miss their purchase on the slick clay, or if a weaver’s pincer catches a wing-membrane, they plummet into the central puddle. The moment they hit the water, the oil-backs converge in a churning, frothing knot, tearing the fallen flyer apart in minutes, leaving only a few grey tendons to wash down the central grate.

The hall is old enough that the creatures and the craft have begun to merge. In the lower courses of the wall, where the erosion is worst and the old tiles have cracked and slumped out of the mortar, the dead shells of the largest clack-weavers have wedged into the gaps. Over generations, a mineral-heavy slime secreted by the oil-backs has calcified over these shells, cementing them into the masonry. The wall is repairing itself with the bones of its inhabitants.

To stand at the perimeter of this room is to feel the chill of the wet clay soaking instantly through boots, to smell the sharp, coppery tang of cold mud and crustacean musk, and to be swallowed by a wall of sound that has no echo because the echo *is* the room. The vast center of the air is entirely vacant, a grey cylinder of mist and falling droplets, framed by a perimeter of frantic, chitinous motion and ancient, thumb-marked mud that refuses to collapse.
