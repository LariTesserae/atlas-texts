The water here does not belong to an ocean. It is a vast, highland basin—a submerged plateau sitting two miles above the true sea level of the world, held within a ring of unseen, eroded peaks. Because of the altitude and the permanent shroud of thick, freezing cloud that rests directly on the surface fifty feet overhead, the water is perpetually just above the freezing point. It is fresh, snow-fed, and astonishingly clear, though there is almost no light to see it by. 

What filters down from the sky is a bruiselike, tarnished indigo—the twilight of a storm that never breaks. You do not look through this water so much as peer into a vast, cold room where the walls have been removed. The terrain below is a rolling expanse of dark grey slate and basalt, smoothed by millennia of heavy, shifting currents into long, whale-backed ridges and wide, shallow valleys. There is no silt. Any fine sediment was scoured away ages ago, leaving the stone bare, polished, and clean.

In this near-darkness, the flora has taken over the role of architecture and movement, though it grows with a deliberate, sparse discipline. There are no tangled jungles here. Instead, across the miles of slate, huge, solitary botanical forms rise at wide intervals, giving the valley the feel of an orchard planted by giants. 

The most dominant of these are the bladder-pines—towering, sixty-foot spires of black, rubbery wood that do not branch, but instead sprout hundreds of translucent, gas-filled spheres the size of medicine balls. These bladders strain upward with immense buoyant force, pulling the trunks taut like anchor cables. Beside them grow the wide, dish-like expanses of chalk-weed, twenty feet across, which slowly tilt and rotate on thick muscular stalks to catch the microscopic drift of freezing nutrients falling from the surface. There are fields of ribbon-fern, their fronds ten yards long and pale as tallow, which do not grow from the rock, but anchor themselves using heavy, calcified root-knots that roll slowly across the basin floor when the currents shift.

Because the light from above is too weak for normal photosynthesis, the vegetation relies on the cold, violent energy of the water itself—a biomechanical process that makes the valley intensely, startlingly loud. 

Every few minutes, a powerful thermal current—born from the clash of glacial meltwater pouring into the basin from unseen heights—sweeps across the plateau. You feel it first as a drop in pressure, a sudden tightening of the cold around the ribs. Then the sound arrives. As the current hits the miles of sparse, towering flora, the plants respond. The taut trunks of the bladder-pines vibrate like the lowest strings of a double bass, sending a deep, thrumming *hum* through the water that vibrates in the teeth and sternum. Simultaneously, the giant gas-bladders knock together with the hollow, resonant *clack-thump* of wooden barrels colliding in a flooded cellar. Across the valley, thousands of cup-corals and hollow tube-reeds catch the surge, whistling and groaning in a discordant, minor-key organ swell. 

There are no fish here. No crabs picking at the slate, no worms in the crevices, no darting shadows of predators. The cold and the bizarre chemistry of the high-altitude water have kept the valley entirely sterile of animal life. Yet the place is fiercely active. The plants themselves are in constant, restless motion. Without animals to pollinate them or disperse their seeds, they have developed mechanical solutions. 

Everywhere in the gloom, things are winching, snapping, and uncoiling. A stalk of bone-kelp will spend three hours slowly ratcheting itself backward against the current, building tension in its segmented, fibrous spine, until—*crack*—it releases, whipping forward through the dark water like a trebuchet arm to fling a cluster of heavy, stone-like spores a hundred yards down-valley. The sound is like a pistol shot underwater. Elsewhere, great, cabbage-like rosettes of black leaves rhythmically open and slam shut, pumping water through their cores with a wet, heavy *thwack* that echoes off the bare slate ridges.

Scattered among these spaced-out botanical engines are the remains of whatever hands once worked this valley. It is impossible to tell if they built these things before the basin flooded, or if the builders were somehow native to the freezing dark. 

The structures are primitive, constructed without mortar, metal, or glass, yet they are remarkably intact, preserved by the lack of silt and the absence of burrowing creatures. Every few hundred yards, rising from the smoothed grey rock, is a cairn or a low, circular windbreak made of stacked slate slabs. They look like shepherd huts, except the roofs are woven from the petrified, woody ribs of ancient bladder-pines, lashed together with thick, tarred plant-fiber cordage that has turned hard as iron in the cold.

Stretching between some of these huts, spanning the wide, empty spaces of the valley floor, are long lines of waist-high stone posts. Tied to the tops of these posts are heavy ropes made of braided kelp-hide, still stretched taut after untold centuries. They seem to be guide-lines, meant for someone walking along the bottom in the dark, pulling themselves hand-over-hand against the screaming currents. Here and there, wedged into the crevices of the rock or still lashed to the guide-ropes, are tools: oversized knives knapped from black flint, bound to handles of pale, water-cured drift-wood; heavy stone mauls grooved for lashings; and strange, wicker-like traps woven from flexible stems, anchored by stones, still waiting to catch the drifting, detached bladders of the pine-spires. 

The vegetation has not overgrown these ruins; rather, it has integrated them into its slow, mechanical choreography. A long, pale ribbon-fern will snag its stone-root against the wall of a slate hut, using the ancient masonry as a pivot point as it lashes in the current. Near the center of the valley, a massive, hollow cone of stacked stones—perhaps an old kiln or a signal tower—has been colonized by a single, immense specimen of lantern-moss. 

The moss does not glow continuously. It only illuminates when agitated by mechanical stress. When the great mid-day current comes roaring down from the invisible peaks, the water funnels through the narrow doorway of the stone cone. The moss inside flares to life in response to the friction—a sudden, cold, mint-green luminescence that pours out through the chinks in the dry-stone wall. For ten minutes, as the valley floor thrums with the bass-notes of the pines and the sharp *cracks* of the spore-flingers, that single ruined tower stands lit from within like a lantern on a dark moor, casting long, sharp shadows of swaying kelp across the empty grey stone, illuminating the taut, waiting guide-ropes that lead off into the freezing dark.
