The foundation of the valley was once a sprawling, high-altitude refinery—twelve square miles of fractionating towers, catwalks, and parabolic collector dishes perched in the thin, dry air of a plateau. That metal is gone, or rather, it has been entirely digested and replaced in the exact shape of its former self by the *ferro-brier*.

There is no soil here, and there has been no rain for ten thousand years. The floor of the basin is a packed, three-hundred-foot-deep mattress of carbonized husks, silica ash, and shed iron-scales, worn down by its own constant churning into a series of smooth, undulating dunes that mimic the slumped roofs of long-collapsed warehouses. 

Everything is hot to the touch, hovering just below the ignition point of paper. The heat does not come from the sky—which is a starless, soot-choked vault of permanent slate-black—but from the vegetation itself. The flora here is thermogenic and metallurgic. It runs on friction, geothermal drafts, and the slow, grinding oxidation of the ancient machinery it swallowed ages ago.

In the pitch dark, you can only see the landscape by its own fugitive sparks and the dull, cherry-red glow of its internal furnaces.

What looks at first like an industrial ruin—a dense, claustrophobic tangle of pipes, manifolds, and gantry cranes—is entirely alive, and it is moving with terrifying speed. Great cables of black, leafless vine, as thick as locomotive boilers and plated in overlapping scales of rusted iron, writhe over and under one another like a pit of blind snakes. They do not grow toward light; they grow toward heat and structural tension. A vine will shoot across a fifty-foot gap between two ancient smokestacks, latch onto the opposing rim with hooks of hardened horn, and immediately begin to twist itself into a braided suspension bridge. Within minutes, secondary creepers swarm across the span, welding themselves together with exuded drops of sizzling, pitch-like resin, building a latticework wall, a roof, a seamless dark tunnel—only for the tension to shift somewhere down in the valley floor, causing the entire structure to snap apart and re-weave itself into a spiraling tower a hundred yards away.

It is an architecture in a state of frantic, perpetual remodeling. The plants are executing the phantom blueprints of the dead factory, endlessly assembling and disassembling organic manifolds, timber-hard pistons, and bellows made of leathery, asbestos-skinned leaves.

Despite the violence of this constant construction, the valley is unnervingly quiet. There is no wind in the heavy, oven-hot air, and there is no water to hiss or drip. The plants have no sap; their vascular systems pump a dry, fluidized powder of graphite and sulfur that lubricates their frantic growth. Consequently, the chaotic reshaping of the landscape happens in a near-silent frenzy. The dominant sound is a vast, low-volume whisper: millions of dry, metallic scales sliding over one another like a continent of sandpaper. Now and then, the hush is broken by the deep, resonant *thrum* of a root-cable pulled to twenty tons of tension, or the dry, rifle-shot crack of an old, mineralized trunk snapping under the weight of new growth, immediately swallowed by the rustling tide of ash.

There are no animals here. Nothing crawls or flies through the blackened iron-thickets. The heat would dessicate flesh in an hour, and the sheer density of the shifting, writhing brier leaves no room for a creature to exist without being crushed or braided into the living scaffolding. The closest thing to fauna are the "tumble-gears"—spherical, hollow cysts of bone-white wood dropped by the canopy. They roll down the ancient, eroded slopes of the slag-heaps, propelled by the shifting floor, clicking softly in the dark as they wind up internal torsion springs of dried tendon, eventually wedging themselves into a crevice to unwind with a whirr, drilling new anchor-roots into the centuries of packed dust.

To stand on one of the high, worn-down ridges of compacted slag is to look out over a pitch-black sea of restless, muscled machinery. Below, in the dark basin, dimly lit by the dull orange glow of thermal vents and the occasional blue arc of static electricity discharging between iron-plated branches, the forest is tirelessly building a city for no one, tearing it down, and building it again before the dust can settle.
