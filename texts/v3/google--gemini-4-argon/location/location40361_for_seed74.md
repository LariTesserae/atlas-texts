The boundaries of the place are defined by a sheer, circular drop-off into an old limestone quarry, no wider across than a village square. Yet within this sunken basin, there is no stone visible, no soil, and no sky that isn't framed by a ribbed, living vault. 

The ground is a continuous, undulating mattress of *cork-sedge* and calcified root-mat, several yards thick, springy underfoot at the surface and dense as oak two feet down. From this living floor rise the great fluted columns of iron-wood banyans, spaced widely apart like the pillars of an empty nave. Between them, the air is remarkably clear, cool, and open. There is none of the tangled claustrophobia of a jungle; the dominant flora here is architectural and monopolistic, secreting an airborne enzyme that prevents smaller, chaotic weeds from taking hold in the open bays. 

What fills this sparse, pillared hall is an aging, thoroughly absorbed industrial district.

Decades ago—or perhaps centuries, the erosion is so uniform it is hard to tell—a complex of stamping mills, condensers, and cable-transits was built into the basin. The builders did not clear the trees; they grafted the town directly into the vascular system of the grove. Now, the boundaries between the botanical and the mechanized have worn away into a single, established equilibrium. 

High overhead, where the banyan boughs fuse into a translucent, yellow-green canopy that filters the daylight into a flat, shadowless studio glow, run the overhead gantry tracks. The iron I-beams are pitted and rounded like river stones, their bolts completely swallowed by tough, calloused collars of grey bark. Yet the tracks are still level, held in precise tension by the slow, muscular growth of the supporting limbs. Every few minutes, a suspended ore-bucket glides silently along a greased cable from one side of the basin to the other, pulled by an unseen winch buried deep within a hollow trunk.

There are no streets on the root-mat floor, only wide, shallow troughs worn smooth by the drag of heavy sledges and the natural channels of condensation. The air is crisp, hovering just above the point where breath turns to vapor, and carries a persistent, thin dampness—the smell of wet slate, crushed fern, and machine oil. There is no running river here, nor does rain beat through the thick canopy. Instead, moisture arrives as a rhythmic, localized sweat. The industrial condensers—tall, cylindrical towers of riveted copper that stand thirty feet apart in the open spaces—cool the air drawn down through canopy vents. Water beads on their green, patinated flanks, runs down into copper gutters, and is immediately drunk by the thirsty, lip-like root-flanges that have grown up to seal the base of every machine.

Despite the lack of people or animals, the basin is intensely busy. It is a landscape of silent, heavy work.

In the center of the floor, a massive cast-iron flywheel, twenty feet in diameter and worn smooth of all its maker’s marks, turns at a steady, hypnotic thirty revolutions per minute. Its axle is driven not by coal, but by the *turgor-engines*—banks of thick, muscular vine-pistons that swell and contract as they pump mineral-rich fluid up from the deep limestone aquifer. As the vines engorge with water, they heave against iron ratchets; as they vent the moisture in a brief, ghostly puff of cool mist, counterweights of smooth, eroded granite drop to reset the stroke. 

The soundscape is small, contained by the sound-absorbing sponge of the floor and walls. There is no roar of combustion. Instead, there is the rhythmic, wet *shuck-clack* of the hydraulic vines, the low hum of copper wires vibrating in the cool drafts, and the occasional heavy, wooden groan as a structural banyan shifts its weight to accommodate a load transfer forty feet up. 

The "buildings" are vertical looms and tiered housings of brick and oxidized brass, spaced fifty or sixty feet apart, leaving vast, sweeping courts of pale-green moss between them. The bricks have lost their sharp corners, softened by generations of velvet-lichen, and the mortar has been replaced entirely by a tough, white root-paste that holds the masonry together even where the foundations have subsided. Through tall, unglazed arched windows, one can see the interiors: rooms where automated brass armatures slowly braid fibers stripped from the ceiling vines into heavy industrial cabling, coiling it neatly onto wooden spools that rot back into the floor before they are ever collected, feeding the very roots that grow the fiber.

Fauna is an afterthought here. Every hour or so, a single, pale-shelled snail the size of a fist might be seen riding the slow-moving rocker arm of a pump, grazing on the grease-mold that forms around the bearings. High up in the rafters, a pair of slate-colored moths with wings like rusted tin flakes drift from one warm transformer box to another, entirely dwarfed by the scale of the timber and iron.

The dynamic of the place is a closed loop of growth and wear. On the eastern wall of the basin, a massive, geared sluice-gate is slowly being pried apart by the wedging action of a silver-barked fig; simultaneously, the fig’s own lower limbs are being methodically pruned and mulched by a pendulum-saw that derives its power from the very water the ruined sluice lets through. Nothing is winning. The rust feeds the iron-Fixing mosses; the mosses lubricate the slides; the slides trim the canopy to let in the cool, even light of an overcast afternoon; the light drives the sap that turns the wheels. It is a small, cool factory town that has forgotten what it was built to manufacture, continuing its shifts in the quiet, damp half-light as a purely biological habit.
