The basin is no larger than a walled orchard, notched into the shoulder of a newly uplifted mountain. Above its eastern lip, the sky is the hard, thin cobalt of extreme altitude, where the air ought to be freezing. Instead, it is a sweltering, breathless kettle. 

The walls are young basalt and obsidian, split along fresh, unweathered fault lines. Their edges are still wickedly sharp, flaking off in shards that could shave hair, untamed by wind or centuries of rain. Yet these jagged faces are weeping. A hot, mineral-heavy sweat seeps continuously from the microscopic pores of the stone, slicking the black glass with iridescent biofilms and gathering at the base of the cliffs in shallow, steaming aprons of water.

The heat—and the strange, quiet rules of the place—comes from the *pins*. 

There are five of them scattered across the basin floor, spaced thirty or forty paces apart with plenty of empty, flooded dark stone between them. They are cylinders of an unblemished, matte-white material that refuses to reflect the bright mountain sun, each the thickness of a wrist and rising six feet out of the rock. They have no seams, switches, or interfaces. They do not hum. Instead, they exert a localized, heavy-handed negotiation with physics. Within fifty yards of the pins, atmospheric pressure doubles, thermal energy refuses to radiate upward into the thin mountain air, and liquid water behaves with a lazy, syrup-like surface tension. 

When a bead of condensation rolls down a basalt shard near one of the pins, it does not splash into the ground-water; it merges with a slow, velvety *plop*, forming transient domes and ridges of water that take seconds to flatten out. The air is thick with vapor that hangs in clear, lensing blobs rather than diffusing into mist, drifting across the courtyard-sized space like invisible jellyfish that warp the view of the opposite wall as they pass.

Because the space is sparse and the rock so recently split, there is no true soil here—only wet gravel, obsidian grit, and warm puddles. Yet the vegetation that has claimed the cracks is monstrously lush, swollen with water and heat. There is no turf or continuous groundcover; instead, individual plants grow as isolated, architectural specimens spaced yards apart across the dark stone. 

In the middle of a shallow pan of water sits a single rosette of jade-green, rubbery leaves, each the size of a butcher’s apron and four inches thick, practically bursting with pressurized sap. Twenty feet away, rooted in a dry fissure just above the water line, grows a cluster of translucent, bladder-like stalks flushed with magenta and gold. These plants have adapted to the high-tech thermal anomaly: they do not bother with bark or fibrous stems, relying entirely on turgor pressure and the warped surface tension of the local water to hold themselves upright. At the tips of their broad leaves, excess water is forced out through wide pores, not in drops, but in long, coherent ribbons of warm liquid that drape down to the stone like strings of clear glass before finally snapping and coiling into the puddles.

The light is clear, direct, and relentless—ordinary high-altitude sunlight pouring into a very extraordinary pocket of air. Because the rock is black and wet, the basin is a place of harsh, dazzling specular highlights and deep, velvety shadows, with no soft mid-tones. Where the floating lenses of dense vapor drift between the sun and the stone, they cast brief, wandering caustics of bright light that slither up the jagged walls and over the bloated emerald plants.

It is almost entirely silent. The wind that scours the barren mountain peaks above hits the invisible boundary of the pins' influence and simply shears off, passing overhead without ruffling a single fleshy leaf below. There is no roar of mountain gales here. The acoustic landscape is intimate, small, and wet: the low, resonant *bloop* of high-viscosity water dripping into a pool; the sudden, crisp *tick* of young volcanic rock cracking further as it expands in the heat; the faint, rubbery creak of a massive leaf shifting its weight as it tracks the sun.

Life moves through the open spaces between the plants at a deliberate, sun-drunk pace. 

Striding across the surface of a three-inch-deep mirror of water is a solitary creature that looks like a cross between a crane-fly and a blown-glass orchid. Its six thread-thin legs are eighteen inches long, their tips resting on the tough skin of the water, indenting it into deep, wide dimples that throw dark rings of shadow onto the gravel below. It moves with slow, hydraulic grace, hunting the thumb-sized, shell-less gastropods that graze on the rainbow sheen of the weeping rocks. 

High up on the eastern rim, right where the tropical bubble meets the freezing mountain air, a constant, silent violence is taking place. Along that invisible line—which cuts straight through the middle of a jagged basalt spire—moisture rising from the basin instantly flash-freezes into a delicate, six-inch fringe of rime ice, which immediately melts again on its underside, raining warm drops back down into the courtyard. 

A small, metallic-blue reptile, no longer than a finger, clings to the warm rock just two inches below that frost-line. Its throat pulses slowly in the ninety-degree heat, its dark eye fixed upward, waiting for cold-stunned mountain insects to tumble through the invisible roof of its tiny, engineered Eden.
