The precinct is no wider than four city blocks, sunk into an oval basin of dark, weeping basalt. You do not walk on streets here; you walk on wide, flanged causeways of cast iron that cross six feet above a shallow, simmering marsh. 

To maintain the sparse layout required for the steam to vent, the buildings are spaced widely apart, standing like solitary brick chimneys or narrow, five-story clocktowers in an open flooded plaza. Each structure is tall, windowless on its lower tiers, and constructed from vitrified purple-black brick that never dries. Between them lies a great deal of open, misty space—broad expanses of black water skinning over with iridescent oil and pale green duckweed, interrupted only by the masonry piers that hold up the walkways and the heavy, insulated pipes that arc from roof to roof like iron vines.

The air is hot—hovering near body temperature—and so saturated with moisture that breath remains visible as a sluggish, heavy fog that refuses to rise. It is permanently dim. A dense canopy of structural condensers—vast, umbrella-like lattices of corroded copper mounted atop tall masts—spreads high above the basin, catching the rising thermal vents and turning them back into a constant, warm indoor rain. Whatever sky exists above the copper awnings is reduced to a bruised, pewter smear. Down on the walkways, the only true illumination comes from high-wattage carbon-arc lamps hung at wide intervals. They buzz like hornets and cast a flickering, violet-white glare that dies quickly in the mist, surrounded by halos of dancing water droplets.

The sound is immense, a physical pressure in the chest. Because the basin walls are close and made of slick, eroded stone, every noise echoes and overlaps. There is the rhythmic, bass-heavy *thud-shuck* of unseen subterranean pumps keeping the water level at its precise two-foot depth; the relentless, metallic pattering of condensation falling from the high copper grates onto the iron walkways; and, every four minutes, the deafening, valve-clearing shriek of a relief pipe vents a solid horizontal pillar of white steam across the open space between towers. 

In this heat and endless shower, the vegetation has gone mad, though it grows in strange, disciplined patterns dictated by the heat of the machinery. There are no trees in the mud below, only massive, standalone colonies of *boiler-fern* and *grease-lotus* spaced twenty yards apart like carefully planted gardens. The ferns grow directly out of the brickwork and around the joints of the hot pipes, their fronds six feet long, translucent as wax paper, and visibly trembling in the vibration of the engines. Where a steam pipe leaks a steady, scalding drip, bloated bladder-mosses swell to the size of hogsheads, glowing with a faint, sickly bioluminescence in the gloom, before bursting with a wet pop and slowly inflating again. Vines thick as hawsers, covered in velvety rust-colored hairs that drink directly from the fog, drape from the high catwalks down into the warm mire, swaying against the current even when there is no wind.

Despite the tropical luxuriance of the flora, the place is entirely devoid of animals. No frogs call from the reeds; no mosquitoes hover under the arc lamps; no rats scuttle along the flanges of the iron paths. The water is too heavy with dissolved sulfates and copper runoff for fish, and the air too thick with ozone and coal-gas for birds. Yet the landscape feels crowded with movement. It is a purely mechanical and botanical restlessness. The huge, dark leaves of the water-plants constantly pivot on their stalks to track the shifting plumes of hot water discharged from the submerged grates. Heavy counterweights slide up and down the outsides of the brick towers on greased cables, adjusting the tension of the overhead copper canopies. Sluice gates open with a ratcheting clank, sending miniature, foaming bores of hot water surging across the basin floor, flattening the reeds in one direction before the cross-pumps kick in and comb them back the other way.

Everything here is old, settled into a permanent, dripping equilibrium. The iron of the walkways is worn smooth in the center from boots that rarely come anymore, the bolt-heads softened by decades of mineral buildup that looks like grey candlewax. The brick towers lean slightly, having settled into the primeval mud long ago, held in rigid balance by the tension of the pipes connecting them. It is an engine that has become a swamp, or a swamp that has learned to keep its own boilers stoked, churning away in the loud, hot dark with no one left at the levers.
