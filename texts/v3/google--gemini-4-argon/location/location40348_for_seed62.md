The valley is five miles wide and sits high enough in the range that the air has a permanent, thin chill to it, like a cellar left open to the sky. The rock here is newly broken. There are no rounded river-stones or sloped shoulders of earth; the ridges and the sunken floor are made of long, unweathered slabs of dark grey chert and blue-black basalt that split along clean, razor-straight planes. Where a ledge has dropped, the fracture face looks as though it cleaved yesterday, dry and matte except where the cold air sweats against it.

And the air is always sweating. It never rains here, and there are no streams cutting through the stone, yet every surface is cold and damp to the touch. Moisture condenses directly out of the bright, thin atmosphere, beading on the sharp edges of the shale and the undersides of leaves, gathering in small, stagnant pockets in the joints of the rock.

Because the light is so fierce—an unrelenting, high-altitude white glare that bleached the sky of its blue—the vegetation does not grow pale or spindly. Instead, it grows thick, waxy, and tough, crowding every ledge and choke-point in the young rock. The plants have no need for deep soil. They are lithophytes and atmospheric feeders: waist-high rosettes of grey-green horn-flesh, braided mats of silver bladder-moss that squelch underfoot, and dense, shoulder-high thickets of silver-barked scrub whose leaves are swollen with cold water. They pack together so tightly that you cannot see the stone beneath them until a sheer vertical drop forces the greenery to part. Even then, pale, ropy roots hang down the black cliff faces like frozen waterfalls, drinking the film of condensation off the stone.

The strangeness of the valley is that the flora is so crowded, the light so blinding, and the air so completely devoid of sound. There is no wind at this hour, or most hours. The thick, rubbery leaves do not rustle; they are too heavy and stiff. When a heavy bead of water finally rolls off the tip of a leaf and drops into a lower rosette, the tiny *tup* of its landing is swallowed instantly by the surrounding sponge of moss. 

There are almost no animals to break the hush. Every few hundred yards, if you pry apart the damp plates of a ground-rosette, you might find a blind, translucent flat-beetle the size of a thumbnail, grazing on the velvet mold that grows in the dark crevices, moving so slowly it appears pinned to the leaf. Nothing flies. Nothing calls.

Scattered across the valley—perched on the jagged steps of the hills or half-swallowed by the rubbery scrub on the flats—stand the machines. 

They are not ruins; they are immaculate, heavy, and currently at work, though it takes several minutes of staring at one to realize it is moving. Every quarter-mile or so, an iron caisson or a squat, porcelain-ringed condenser rises out of the brush. They are built of dark, oiled steel, brass, and white ceramic, thirty feet high, bolted directly into the fresh basalt with massive hex-nuts the size of dinner plates. Between them run thick, lead-sheathed cables and seamless copper pipes, looping from ridge to ridge or disappearing into the dense mats of moss. 

The industry here operates at the speed of a clock’s hour hand, and in total silence. 

A lattice-boom crane near the center of the basin stands over a dry fissure. Its hook, carrying a four-ton block of freshly wedged chert, rises at a rate of three inches an hour, hoisted by a hydraulic ram whose polished steel cylinder gleams in the fierce sun. There is no chug of a diesel engine, no whine of an electric motor—only the imperceptible displacement of heavy oil through buried manifold pipes. Further up the slope, a battery of six tall, ribbed cylinders—atmospheric harvesters or gas-separators—rotates on greased bronze tracks. Each cylinder takes a full day to complete a single revolution. Their surfaces are slick with the same cold sweat that coats the leaves, and the glare off their white porcelain insulators is blinding enough to make the eyes water.

Where the copper pipes run through the thickets, the vegetation does not die back; it presses closely against the metal. The pipes are slightly colder than the air, and the plants use them to trap more moisture. Thick, rubbery vines with leaves like overlapping coins have braided themselves around the high-tension pylons, growing right up to the humming—no, not even humming, merely *live*—ceramic bells, where the static charge in the dry, bright air makes the tiny hairs on the plant-stems stand stiffly outward. 

There are no roads connecting these sites, no gravel pads or chain-link fences, and no footprints in the grey moss. Whatever placed the machines here either lowered them from above or built them in place long enough ago that the crowded, slow-growing thicket has closed every seam around their foundations. Here and there, a cast-iron junction box sits in a cleft of sharp rock, its brass sight-glass showing a clear, heavy fluid through which a single, walnut-sized bubble rises once every ten minutes. The bubble reaches the top, vanishes without a pop, and the glare of the sun continues to beat down on the cold, wet leaves and the dark, newly broken stone.
