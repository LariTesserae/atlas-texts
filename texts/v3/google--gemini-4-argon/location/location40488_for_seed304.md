The settlement occupies the entirety of a collapsed basalt blister—a roughly circular sinkhole no wider than a cathedral nave, where the roof of a lava tube gave way not many generations ago. Because the geological wound is still young, the perimeter walls are made of jagged, hexagonal columns with edges sharp enough to slice leather, completely untouched by the rounding patience of wind. 

There is not an inch of floor left visible. What the collapse created, the builders immediately filled, packing the pit from shear wall to shear wall with a dense, multi-tiered tangle of dry-stone rookeries, slate-shingle lean-tos, and steeply pitched scaffolds made of cured driftwood and whalebone. To move from one side of the depression to the other is not a matter of walking a street, but of threading through a labyrinth of shoulder-wide alleys, climbing notched-log ladders, and ducking beneath low-slung aqueducts made of hollowed-out cedar trunks. Everything touches everything else; the stone wall of a kiln acts as the structural support for a cistern, which in turn anchors the woven-wattle floor of a drying loft above it.

Water is the governing architecture here. The rim of the sinkhole sits just below the water table of the surrounding moorland, so the raw basalt walls weep continuously. Cold, mineral-heavy groundwater sheets down the black rock in a permanent, glistening skin. The inhabitants have turned the entire vertical town into a machine for catching, routing, and slowing this runoff. Thousands of thin, flat slate tiles have been hammered into the mortarless cracks of the dwellings at slight angles, functioning as microscopic gutters. They guide the constant seepage away from the living quarters and into a sprawling, suspended vascular system of clay pipes, split-reed chutes, and hide-lined troughs. 

Above, the sky is a flat, uniform pewter—bright enough to cast crisp shadows in the sharp geometry of the alleyways, yet stripped of warmth. The air inside the pit is permanently chill, hovering just above the point where breath turns to steam, and so saturated with dampness that clothing never truly dries. It smells of wet flint, peat smoke, and the faintly metallic tang of subterranean water.

Despite the relentless moisture, almost nothing grows. The rock is too new to have crumbled into soil, and the built environment is too tightly wedged to allow dirt to gather. Only in the unwalkable crevices where the bitter draft is strongest do a few stubborn clumps of rust-colored wire-moss and pale, translucent liverworts cling to the stone, feeding on the mineral sheen of the water. 

Yet the place is intensely, quietly busy, driven by a peculiar localized economy of stone and grease. The groundwater carries a high concentration of dissolved lime and a heavy, tallow-like mineral pitch that bubbles up from thermal vents far below the basalt. Left alone, the water quickly leaves behind a brittle, pale crust—a rapid calcification that threatens to choke the wooden chutes and cement the woven doors shut. 

Because of this, the settlement is in a state of perpetual, hushed maintenance. In the middle tiers, workers armed with flint scrapers and bone chisels methodically chip the milky buildup from the aqueduct joints before it can harden into limestone. Below them, in the dimmest, lowest sump where the water finally pools before draining into a subterranean fissure, others skim the congealed mineral grease from the surface using broad paddles of stretched bladder-skin. This grey fat is packed into clay pots to be burned for heat or smeared onto the driftwood timbers to keep them from rotting in the damp.

The acoustics of the sinkhole are strangely deadened. The sheer density of the overlapping slate roofs, the hanging mats of woven sedge used as windbreaks, and the porous clay walls absorb echoes before they can travel. There is no roar of a waterfall, only a million tiny, discrete hydraulic sounds: the rhythmic *tink-tink-tink* of droplets hitting a bronze-colored puddle, the soft *shhh* of water sliding down a greased wooden trough, the gritty scrape of flint against wet lime.

Animals are scarce, deterred by the verticality and the lack of forage, save for the fat, slow-moving blind amphipods—pale as peeled almonds and the size of a human thumb—that graze on the mineral film inside the water chutes. Occasionally, a solitary, long-legged marsh bird will land on the uppermost drying racks near the rim, tilting its head to listen to the wet, industrious ticking of the packed pit below, before stepping off into the cool grey air.
