The place is an oval sinkhole—a *cenote* that has lost its standing water—carved into the shoulder of a high, warm plateau. It is no larger than a village square, bounded on all sides by twenty-foot walls of pale, porous limestone that curve slightly inward like the rim of a bowl. Above the rim, there is only the blank, bright sky of the high altitude, pale blue and cloudless, pouring clear, unshadowed daylight straight down into the basin.

Despite the high elevation, the air inside the stone bowl is warm, trapped and heated by the pale rock, and perpetually damp. There is no stream here, nor any pond, yet the limestone weeps. Water percolates through the miles of karst above, emerging from horizontal fissures in the cliff face in a constant, warm sweat. The rock walls are dark with moisture, slick to the touch, and coated in a velvet skin of jade-green liverworts and thick, pillowy mosses that drink the seepage before it can ever pool on the floor. 

Because the basin is so small and the light so direct, the vegetation has grown lush in size rather than in number. It is a sparse, spacious garden of giants. There is no tangled undergrowth, no crowding of stems; the floor is covered in a continuous, springy mat of wet, bronze-colored moss that leaves wide, open aisles between the major plants. Only seven or eight of the *pillar-ferns* grow within the sinkhole, spaced twenty paces apart like columns in a ruined nave. Each has a trunk as thick as a barrel, wrapped in fibrous, rust-red hair that holds gallons of water like a sponge. From their crowns, twenty feet up, they unroll four or five colossal, translucent fronds. Each frond is the size of a boat’s sail, arched gracefully over the open space, glowing a brilliant, veined emerald when the sun strikes it from above. Because there are so few of them, the light hits the mossy floor largely unimpeded, casting only a few sharp, lacy shadows that barely move as the day progresses.

The strangeness of the place lies in its botany and its acoustics. Growing at the base of the weeping walls are the *bladders*—leathery, translucent globes of pale yellow vegetation, some the size of a fist, others as large as a kettle. They have no leaves, only shallow root-nets that drink the mineral-rich runoff from the limestone. As they digest the dissolved chalk and iron in the water, they slowly inflate with a warm, heavy gas. Over weeks, a bladder will swell until its skin is taut as a drumhead and translucent enough to show the milky condensation beading on its inner walls. 

When the sun reaches its zenith and heats the air in the bowl just a fraction of a degree more, the bladders reach their limit. One by one,though perhaps only two or three a day, they split along a pre-formed seam with a sound like a musket shot, followed by a long, resonant, brassy sigh that echoes back and forth against the curved limestone walls. 

Even without the bursting bladders, the sinkhole is loud. The limestone is honeycombed with vertical flutes and deep, narrow chimneys that descend into the dark rock below the moss floor. The water that seeps from the walls eventually trickles down into these hidden pipes. Down in the dark, drops fall from great heights into subterranean basins, and the stone chimneys act as organ pipes, amplifying the impacts. The result is a continuous, hollow, percussive racket that rises from the ground itself—*clop, tung, plock, boom*—like a hundred wooden mallets striking gourds of different sizes in an erratic, echoing rhythm. The sound fills the sunlit space, bouncing off the wet cliffs until it seems to come from the air itself, a loud, liquid music with no visible source.

Animal life is almost entirely absent. The altitude and the sheer rims keep out the heavy browsers of the plateau, and the water is too mineral-heavy for amphibians. Only the *glass-snails* live here, and they are rare—perhaps a dozen in the whole basin. Each is the size of a human hand, with a flat, coiled shell made of clear, brittle silica taken from the stone. You can see their slow, grey hearts beating through their shells—one contraction every two minutes. They glide at an imperceptible pace up the red-furred trunks of the pillar-ferns, leaving wide ribbons of iridescent slime that dry in the sun into crisp, cellophane-like peelings. 

Though no one lives here, someone comes. There are no walls, no cleared paths, no fire pits—the moss is too wet to burn anyway—and no shelter. Yet, at the base of the weeping cliffs, where the rock is softest, crescent-shaped scars mark the stone where chunks of flint have been pried loose with heavy elk-antler picks. A few of those picks lie forgotten or stored on a dry ledge five feet up the wall, their tines worn smooth and stained green by algal damp.

More striking are the harvesting rings around the great bladder-plants. Someone has used sharp, unpolished flakes of flint to slice the tough, cured skins of the burst bladders, taking the leathery material away—perhaps to carry water on the dry plateau above, or to stretch over frames. Beside one massive, unburst bladder, a ring of fist-sized river cobbles—brought from somewhere far below the plateau—has been arranged in a neat circle on the moss, pressing down the plant's marginal roots to slow its growth and thicken its skin. Three long, peeled wands of willow are driven into the moss nearby, leaning together in a simple tripod; hanging from their apex by a twist of braided grass is a single, heavy stone maul, suspended directly over the bladder’s crown, waiting for the skin to swell just high enough to touch the stone and be ruptured on the maker's terms. 

Nothing happens quickly. The shadow of the western rim takes hours to creep across the bronze moss. A drop of water swells on the tip of a liverwort leaf, trembles, and falls into a stone flute in the floor; three seconds later, the deep, hollow *onk* of its landing echoes back up into the warm light, loud enough to vibrate in the chest. The giant fern fronds do not sway, for the wind of the high plateau passes cleanly over the lip of the bowl, leaving the humid air inside completely still.
