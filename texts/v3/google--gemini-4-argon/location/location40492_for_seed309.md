The Sump—or the Bellows-Ward, as the older cast-iron street signs still claim before the bark eats their lettering—occupies a single, cavernous subsidence three hundred feet below the crust. It is no larger than a cathedral nave or a sunken town square, yet it feels strangely roomy, almost hollow, because there is no soil, no stone, and no clutter on the ground. There is only the Root. 

What appears to be the floor of the cavern is actually the upper dorsal surface of a single, ancient, petrified-yet-living lignified mat. It rises and falls in broad, smooth, sweeping gullies that mimic the worn cobblestones of an old industrial plaza. Everything here—the walls, the ceiling, the wide, curving avenues, the four-story tenement shells—is composed of *Ficus ferrum*, the iron-strangler, a hyper-adapted subterranean flora that long ago consumed the original brick and mortar of the district and replaced it with its own dense, metallic-gray wood. The result is an urban architecture that has been entirely digested and re-extruded as living timber. There are no twigs, no leaf-litter, no bushes to crowd the eye; the vegetation is total, a monolithic, smooth-skinned wooden terrain that leaves the center of the cavern starkly, eerily open.

Into this living wood, heavy industry has been grafted like brass fittings into the hull of a ship. 

Great, sweating cast-iron boilers and rhythmic stamping-engines sit in the wide, sparse intervals between the wooden tenements. They do not rest on the wood; they are grown into it. Thick, vascular burls of iron-bark wrap around the bases of flywheels and dynamo housings. Instead of coal or oil, the machines are fed by tap-lines—thick, braided copper hoses driven directly into the massive arterial trunks that form the cavern’s boundary walls. The trees pump a volatile, turpentine-heavy sap under high pressure; the engines burn it, thumping with a heavy, greased, mechanical cadence that shakes the ground. In return, the exhaust pipes vent warm, carbon-rich steam directly back into the upper canopy of fused branches that forms the vault of the roof.

It is never wet here, only perpetually, slickly damp. The engine-steam rises, hits the cooler upper boughs, and condenses into a warm, greasy mist that beads on the ironwork and the gray bark alike. Every surface feels like the skin of a working horse. In the deep grooves of the root-floor—smooth channels worn down by centuries of acid runoff and foot traffic—shallow ribbons of amber-colored condensation collect and trickle toward the central intake grates. 

Because the architecture and the machinery leave vast, sweeping pockets of empty air between them, the fauna has claimed the negative space entirely. The air and the smooth walls are thick, frantic, and boiling with animal life. 

There are no birds, only the *clack-moths* and the *grease-swifts*—blind, leather-winged creatures the size of human hands that navigate by the acoustic thrum of the engines. They hang in curtains of ten thousand from the underside of the iron gantry-walks, dropping in synchronized, swirling clouds whenever a pressure-valve vents. They do not fly randomly; they move in dense, ropy torrents through the open air, flowing around the sparse pillars and steam-stacks like dark smoke blown through a wind tunnel. 

And on the smooth, sweeping root-floors, the *tread-crabs* and *valve-lice* migrate in unbroken, clicking carpets. The crabs are pale, flat-shelled scavengers the size of dinner plates, their carapaces stained dark with machine oil. They move in wide, disciplined lanes from one boiler-station to the next, grazing on the velvety white sulfur-fungi that bloom wherever a steam-pipe leaks. When a piston strokes down, the crabs scatter in a synchronized wave, exposing the worn, polished wood beneath, only to surge back the moment the shadow lifts.

The sound is immense, a physical pressure in the warm, dim air. Because the space is enclosed and the smooth wooden walls are hard as bone, every noise reverberates. The deep, rhythmic *chug-thump* of the sap-engines forms the bassline, a slow, industrial heartbeat that you feel in your teeth. Layered over this is the deafening, metallic cicada-like screech of a million insects rubbing their chitinous legs against the warm brass pipes to draw heat, and the constant, dry-leaf roar of winged things whipping through the dark. Occasionally, a high-pressure sap-line equalizes with a long, mournful whistle that echoes through the empty tenements, causing the entire living ceiling of bats and moths to shift at once—a sound like a massive wave of gravel being dragged across sheet metal.

Light is scarce, heavy, and orange. There is no sky, only the low, vaulted ceiling of braided roots and rusted iron girders. Illumination comes from two sources: the fierce, flickering amber slits of the furnace fireboxes, which cast long, rotating shadows of flywheels across the plaza, and the *lumen-grubs*. These translucent, thumb-sized larvae live in the mortar-seams of the wooden buildings, outlining the ghost-geometry of long-gone brick walls and window frames in a dim, sickly chartreuse glow. You can see the outline of a three-story townhouse, complete with a gabled roof and a storefront, drawn entirely in squirming, bioluminescent lines against the dark bark.

Everything here is old and settled into a strange, violent equilibrium. The iron of the machines is deeply pitted with rust, softened at the edges like river stones, yet kept from failing by the woody calluses that grow over every crack and weld. The wooden floor is grooved a foot deep where the exhaust-water runs, polished to a high, dark sheen by the bellies and claws of millions of generations of scavengers. 

Every twelve minutes, the main governor-engine at the north end of the cavern reaches peak pressure. A brass bell strikes—three flat, deafening clangs. The massive exhaust flues open. A gale of hot, pine-and-sulfur-scented fog blasts horizontally across the plaza. Instantly, the ecosystem shifts gear: the flying swarms drop to the floor, flattening themselves into the erosion gullies; the crabs lock their legs together, forming living, armored cobblestones against the blast; the woody walls visibly swell, their pores opening with a wet, sighing sound to drink the hot mist. For forty seconds, the world is blind, roaring, white-warmth. Then the valves slam shut, the mist condenses into a sudden, brief dribble of warm rain, the amber furnace-light cuts back through the gloom, and the millions of creatures detach themselves from the floor and resume their frantic, deafening orbits around the machines.
