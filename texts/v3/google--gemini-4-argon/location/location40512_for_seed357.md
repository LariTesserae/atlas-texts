There is no dirt here, and no stone. Eight thousand feet above the planetary datum, suspended in the thin, baking air of the upper troposphere, the entire valley is a single, self-weaving architectural megastructure grown from hyper-metabolic timber and vascular vine. 

It is a landscape of immense, empty plazas, soaring aqueducts, and mile-long colonnades, all of it alive, all of it wet, and all of it violently rearranging itself in the clear afternoon light.

The ground underfoot is a tightly braided mat of flattened, iron-hard roots, cross-hatched like woven ash and slick with hot, mineral-heavy sap. This living floor stretches out for miles, dipping and rising in terraced amphitheaters that have no audience. Because the structure is so high in the atmosphere, the sky above is a deep, bruised violet-blue, the sunlight sharp and unfiltered, glaring off wet bark. Yet the air is bathwater-hot. The heat does not come from the sun; it radiates upward from the floor and outward from the pillars. The architecture is endothermic, running a constant, feverish metabolism as it pumps millions of gallons of water up from the unreachable abyss below and forces it through its own structural arteries.

To look across the valley is to see a sparse, monumental geometry in a state of slow-motion hysteria. 

Great buttresses and soaring arches of pale, muscle-like wood stand hundreds of yards apart, framing vast squares of open, steaming air. Nothing is eroded; nothing has had time to weather or rot. Every surface has the glossy, sharp-edged sheen of newly split bamboo or fresh scar tissue. Where two load-bearing walls meet, the corners are razor-sharp, woven from taut, fibrous tendons that gleam with condensation. 

The turbulence of the place is architectural. A freestanding campanile of braided banyan-stock, three hundred feet tall and standing alone in the center of a flooded courtyard, will suddenly shudder. With a sound like a ship’s mast snapping in a gale, its internal pressure shifts. New shoots, thick as barrels and blind-white, erupt from its upper tiers, uncoiling into the empty air with terrifying speed. Within minutes, they are stiffening, lignifying, turning green and then bronze as they throw a new flying buttress across five hundred feet of open space. When the new arch strikes the distant retaining wall, the tendrils instantly split into thousands of microscopic rootlets, burrowing into the receiving wood and fusing the joint in a cloud of hissing steam.

Meanwhile, half a mile away, an entire viaduct lets go. The plant-mind—or the blind, chaotic algorithm of its growth—has rerouted the turgor pressure away from that sector. Deprived of hydraulic tension, the massive, rectilinear bridge does not rot; it violently untangles. The beams twist, unbraiding themselves like let-go ropes, whipping through the hot air and splashing down into the shallow, stepped reservoirs below, where they are immediately overgrown and re-absorbed by the thirsty floor-mat.

Water is the engine of this frantic masonry, and it is everywhere. The high mountain winds hit the hot, respiring surfaces of the mega-flora and condense instantly into horizontal sheets of mist, only to be sucked back into greedy foliar grates built into the floors. Great, hollow-stemmed conduits—natural pipes ten feet in diameter—run along the edges of the empty avenues. They are translucent, and through their skins, you can see the shadowy, foaming surge of boiling water being pushed uphill by peristaltic contractions of the wood. Where a joint is still forming, or where a wall is actively tearing itself down to build a new spire, the water bursts out in high-pressure geysers, showering the empty plazas in warm, permanent rain. The flat expanses of the woven ground are perpetually ankle-deep in fast-moving, crystal-clear runoff that smells strongly of crushed green stems, ozone, and hot copper.

There are no animals here. No insects buzz in the heat; no birds nest in the hyper-active rafters. Nothing with legs or wings could survive the crushing, unpredictable carpentry of a world that builds and demolishes its own rooms every twenty minutes. The only motion is the wind, the running water, and the relentless, twitching, architectural gymnastics of the wood.

Because there are no voices, the mechanical noises of the plant-city completely fill the high, thin air. It is a loud place. Beneath the constant, soothing roar of cascading water and the hiss of venting steam, there is the ceaseless, high-tension creaking of the wood. It sounds like a thousand wooden tall ships locked together in a heavy swell. As the sun heats the upper canopies, the rapid cellular expansion creates a rhythmic, staccato popping—like distant rifle fire—echoing across the wide, vacant courts. Deep underfoot, the peristaltic thrum of the great root-pumps vibrates through the soles of the feet: a slow, wet *lub-dub* that shakes the standing water into concentric ripples.

In the center of the valley, a new terrace is being laid. Four massive, squared-off trunks have grown up at the corners of a half-mile square, standing like lonely, glistening obelisks against the clear sky. From their crowns, green cables are shooting outward, crossing the emptiness, missing each other, whipping back, and firing again until they catch, knot, and pull taut, drawing a new ceiling across the sky, blocking out the sun, and turning the bright, flooded plaza below into a resonant, dripping hall of living timber.
