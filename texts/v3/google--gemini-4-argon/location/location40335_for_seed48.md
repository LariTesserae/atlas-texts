The basin is three miles wide, flat as a poured slab, and sunk just below the level of the surrounding shale flats. The air sits in it like warm bathwater. There is no wind to clear the low, grease-gray overcast that hangs a few hundred feet above the ground, turning the afternoon into a shadowless, pewter twilight. Every surface—iron, concrete, and stone—sweats. 

This is an urban footprint built entirely at ground level, designed to leave the air empty. There are no towers, no overhead cables, no stacked tenements. The industrial town consists of vast, sunken concrete aprons separated by hundred-yard-wide avenues of dark, wet gravel. The infrastructure is subterranean or flush with the mud. Beneath the heavy iron grates that stripe the plazas, turbines turn with a deep, submersed thrum you feel in the soles of your boots rather than hear. Steam vents, worn smooth as river stones by decades of condensation and acid rain, hiss softly at the corners of the blocks, bleeding warm vapor into an atmosphere already saturated to the point of dripping.

Nothing grows here. The chemistry of the run-off and the perpetual, dim heat have scoured the basin of chlorophyll. There isn't a blade of crabgrass between the paving stones or a smear of green moss on the north sides of the foundry walls. There is only the mineral slick of wet slate, the velvety orange paste of oxidized iron, and a thin, translucent biofilm that makes the low concrete retaining walls slick to the touch.

Yet the emptiness of the architecture is completely colonized. The ground moves.

Millions of broad-backed, slate-colored crabs, none larger than a human thumb, carpet the sunken avenues. They are the color of wet ash. As they shift over the gravel and the rusted tram rails, they produce a continuous, dry-wet whispering sound, like fine rain hitting heavy canvas. When a boiler vents a sudden gout of hot water into a drainage sluice, the crustaceans part in a sluggish, oily wave, then close back over the warm stone seconds later to feed on the bacterial scum. 

Above them, the vast, open volume of the basin belongs to the bats and the midges. Because the buildings are low and widely spaced—squat pump-houses, sprawling sheet-metal sheds with collapsed roofs, miles of knee-high pipeline running over the flats—the sightlines stretch for miles through the dim, yellow-gray mist. Hanging in that open space are pillars of blind, heat-loving midges, spinning slowly above the thermal leaks in the underground steam mains. Weaving through them, twenty-four hours a day in the perpetual gloom, are tens of thousands of small, rust-furred bats. They do not screech; they hunt by a soft, clicking echolocation that blends into the dripping of the eaves and the click-clack of the crabs' claws on the iron grates. 

Everything is worn down to a dull, rounded equilibrium. The brick corners of the kilnyards have been softened by eighty years of hot drizzle and the scraping of chitin. The raised lettering on the cast-iron manhole covers is nearly illegible, polished flat by the bellies of heavy, pale amphibians—three-foot-long mud-salamanders that haul themselves out of the flooded foundation pits to lie across the warm metal covers of the junction boxes. They lie stacked three and four deep, motionless for hours, their gills pulsing slowly in the wet heat, skin gleaming like greased rubber under the yellow sodium lamps.

Those lamps, spaced fifty yards apart along the avenues, are the only things that break the horizontal monotony. Their glass globes are half-filled with trapped water and dead midges, casting wide, watery cones of amber light onto the slick concrete. Beneath each lamp, the biological engine of the town runs at double speed: the midges drop, the crabs converge in tight, clicking mounds to tear them apart, the salamanders snap lazily at the crabs, and long-legged, silent wading birds—gray-plumed herons stained brown to the belly by rust-water—stalk through the periphery of the light, spearing the salamanders with quick, piston-like strikes of their beaks. 

There is no roar to the place. The great factories are mostly automated and underground, their heavy stamping and smelting muffled by twenty feet of wet earth and concrete. The surface world is an expanse of warm puddles, low brick geometries, and the quiet, relentless industry of mouths and claws working in the dark. Water pools in the shallow basins of the plaza, runs in tepid sheets over the worn curbs, and disappears down the grates, carrying shed shells and gray feathers down into the dark where the turbines spin.
