The horizon does not drop away here; it blurs into a dense, warm, silver-white wall of condensation, as if the world simply runs out of room for clear air a few miles out. Beneath that low, bright ceiling of overcast sky lies the Sump—a continental basin of hyper-dense infrastructure that has long since lost any memory of the bedrock beneath it. There is no soil, no green, not a single blade of grass or stubborn weed anywhere across thousands of square miles. Instead, there is only the Aggregate: a packed, restless, endlessly rearranging matrix of dark metamaterials, porous ceramic-alloys, and weeping cooling-stone.

It is warm—the heavy, enveloping warmth of a laundry room or the exhaust vent of an engine the size of an ocean. Because the elevation is so low, trapped in a shallow bowl the size of a tectonic plate, the atmospheric pressure is thick and soupy. And everything is wet. Not submerged, and not battered by rain, but slick with a permanent, cycling sweat. The very ground is a massive thermodynamic sponge. Warm water sheets down the stepped faces of hexagonal bas-reliefs, pools in the shallow grooves of interlocking floor-plates, and hisses into lukewarm mist where the underlying logic-cores run hot. 

There is no empty space. Every cubic foot of the basin is occupied by machinery, conduit, structural ribbing, or the narrow, shifting sluice-alleys that divide them. It looks like a city, if a city were designed by an intelligence that viewed buildings not as shelters, but as temporary configurations of data and heat distribution. Low, broad ziggurats of matte-black smart-matter sit shoulder to shoulder with acres of trembling radiator fins, tightly coiled forests of fiber-optic cabling thick as ancient oaks, and sprawling fields of intake valves. And none of it stays still.

The landscape is in a state of permanent, turbulent remodeling. The materials here are old—the edges of the hexagonal plates are rounded and smoothed by centuries of friction and running water, stained with ochre and rust-colored mineral tracks—yet the structures they form are ephemeral. A street does not remain a street for more than an hour. With a deep, shuddering grind that travels up through the soles of the feet, a row of ten-story heat-sinks will fold inward like origami, their surfaces weeping trapped condensation, flattening into a glistening plaza to allow a localized pressure front to pass. Moments later, the plaza’s tiles will unlock, sliding over and under one another with the speed of a shuffled deck of cards, rising into a dense baffle-wall to redirect a sudden gale of warm exhaust. The city is an anthill of solid stone and self-assembling metal, constantly tearing itself down and rebuilding to optimize some vast, incomprehensible equation of heat, pressure, and computational load.

Because of this ceaseless kinetic violence, the sound is staggering. It is a deafening, omnidirectional roar that ceases to be noise and becomes a physical pressure against the eardrums. There is the low, tectonic groan of millions of tons of weathered alloy shifting on hydrostatic bearings; the sharp, artillery-like *cracks* of megavolt air-gaps discharging through the damp atmosphere; the relentless, screaming hiss of pressurized steam venting from a million hairline fractures; and the rhythmic, metallic *clack-clack-clack* of self-repairing tiles cascading across entire square miles in rolling waves. To stand here is to stand inside the cylinders of a running engine. The air vibrates so hard that the puddles on the ground do not ripple; they dance in standing geometric patterns—squares, hexagons, sharp little needles of water jumping in time to the sub-audible bass of the deep foundations.

Despite the total absence of plant life—save for an occasional, transient smear of rusty chemotrophic slime near the hottest mineral vents—the Sump is teeming with life. The fauna here has evolved entirely around the abundance of waste heat, moisture, and kinetic energy. 

Flocks of things that look like biological kites—flat, leathery organisms with copper-threaded veins and no visible eyes—ride the violent thermal updrafts between the folding towers, snapping up airborne droplets of mineral-rich grease and water. When a section of the city prepares to shift, changing its electromagnetic resonance a fraction of a second before the stone actually moves, these flocks bank away in a single, synchronized flash of wet gray skin. 

Down in the packed, roaring labyrinth of the sluice-alleys, billions of crab-like scavengers with shells of accreted ceramic dust and salvaged copper wire scuttle in dense, clicking carpets. They move with the shifting walls, pouring over rising barriers like liquid, feeding on the iron-oxidizing biofilms that bloom and die in minutes on the warm, wet vents. Larger things live in the flooded under-conduits: pale, muscular, eel-like amphibians that use the city’s own electromagnetic fields to navigate, shooting through the boiling dark of the drainage pipes just ahead of the peristaltic pumps that endlessly rearrange the city’s water table.

Above it all, the light is a flat, relentless, shadowless white. The sun is up there somewhere, strong enough to turn the permanent layer of low, warm mist into a blindingly bright diffuser. The glare catches on every wet, moving surface: the oil-sheen on the backs of the thermal-gliders, the polished, water-worn rims of the grinding vents, the beads of moisture trembling on the millions of shifting plates. As far as the eye can see, the landscape ripples, rises, collapses, and steams, a boundless, deafening plains-city of wet stone and restless physics, endlessly digesting itself under a bright, blank sky.
