There is no rock beneath the Shelf. If one were to drop a sounding weight down through the gin-clear water, it would fall for six miles through a geometric lattice of bone-white roots before striking the dense, impenetrable mat of older, dead roots that forms the continent's floor. The entire high plateau is an elevated ocean held aloft in the sky by a calcified, self-supporting forest of *Baryxylon*—mega-flora that gave up leaves and bark epochs ago to become pure plumbing and architecture. 

The water is forty-four degrees Fahrenheit at the surface and forty-four degrees at the bottom. It does not freeze, and it does not warm. Because the Shelf sits twelve thousand feet above the planetary datum, the sky overhead—visible as a pale, rippling ceiling from beneath the surface—is thin and colorless, pouring down a flat, clinical daylight that penetrates the water for thousands of feet without scattering. The visibility is terrifyingly vast. From the side of a single root-column, you can see the next column three miles away as clearly as if it were across a room, and beyond that, the next, receding into a pale blue infinity like the pillars of an endless, flooded hypostyle hall.

Between these columns, the space is mostly empty liquid, clean of silt because there is no stone to erode. Yet the world is entirely botanical. The columns themselves are fifty feet thick, their surfaces smoothed by millennia of constant, laminar flow into fluted channels and hydrodynamic ridges. Every three hundred yards, horizontal runners—perfectly round, cable-like stolons thick as freight trains—span the gaps between the verticals, locking the continent into a rigid grid. 

Nothing here grows anymore, and nothing rots. The ecosystem reached its climax state ten million years ago and locked into a steady-state thermodynamic loop. The plants do not compete for space; they are the space. And instead of leaves, they have developed organs of pure industrial utility.

The sound hits you before you understand what you are looking at: a deafening, uninterrupted, low-frequency thrumming that vibrates through the water and rattles the ribcage. It is the sound of ten billion biological turbines. 

Where the horizontal runners meet the vertical trunks, the plant tissue has differentiated into massive, chambered nodules of black, vulcanized cellulose. Inside these chambers, rotated by the high-pressure osmotic differentials between the cold external water and the warm, pressurized sap-streams within the plant, giant impellers of lignified bone spin at a constant four hundred revolutions per minute. They have no evolutionary ancestor in the animal kingdom; they are botanical organs that solved the problem of circulation through rotary mechanics, growing and shedding their own friction-polished bearings in a continuous, microscopic turnover. 

Every junction is a power station. The water around the nodules shimmers with waste heat that is instantly wicked away by the steady current, and the silver-sheathed nerve-trunks running down the underside of the stolons crackle with high-voltage bio-electricity. You can see the charge: a faint, violet corona that clings to the copper-rich bark, occasionally arcing across a scarred node with a sharp, metallic *crack* that echoes for twenty miles through the water.

The water is always moving, driven from west to east by the synchronized pumping of the root-continent itself, which draws water up from the abyssal marshes below the plateau and vents it across the high plains at a steady four knots. The current never eddies, never slacks, and never surges. Over eons, it has worn the outer deadwood of the *Baryxylon* into smooth, parabolic curves, exposing the pale, dense heartwood beneath in striated patterns like Damascus steel.

Because there is no silt or drifting plankton to cloud the water, the abundant fauna of the Shelf are starkly visible at great distances, hanging in the vast blue rooms between the trunks like suspended machinery. They are creatures adapted to an environment of high voltage, relentless current, and botanical industry. 

Shoals of flat, ribbon-like fish, three feet long and plated in overlapping scales of dull zinc, hold themselves completely stationary in the four-knot flow. They do not swim; they orient their bodies to act as hydrofoils, anchored in the invisible magnetic fields radiating from the silver nerve-cables. They feed by induction, their specialized lateral lines siphoning stray electrical current directly from the tree-grid to power their own sluggish metabolisms. A thousand of them will hang in a neat, three-dimensional array around a junction box for years, motionless as a chandelier, vibrating slightly in the hum of the turbines.

On the trunks themselves, grazing crustaceans the size of wolves—pale, long-legged things with shells of translucent silica—move with the slow, rhythmic precision of factory automatons. They do not eat the living wood; they scrape the microscopic film of mineral scale that precipitates out of the water onto the warm casings of the pump-nodules. In return, the plant vents small, timed bursts of glucose-rich lubricant from pores along the runner-tracks, which the crabs gather with specialized, sponge-tipped chelae. Everything is accounted for. There is no predation, because predation is inefficient. When a crab's joints finally wear down from the constant vibration and the four-knot drag, it simply lets go, tumbling slowly eastward and downward through the miles of open grid until it hits the lower intake vents, where the acidic root-mats dissolve its shell back into raw minerals to be pumped skyward again.

Above it all, the surface of the water is a mirror torn into uniform, standing waves by the steady wind of the high plateau. From below, the surface looks like a ceiling of hammered tin, bright and blinding. Nothing breaks it. The trees stop exactly twelve feet below the air, their crowns flattened into broad, hydro-planing tables of slick green tissue that drink the harsh, high-altitude sunlight without ever risking desiccation in the cold air above. 

You can drift for a hundred miles through the mid-water levels of the Shelf and see no change in the architecture, no shift in the light, no variation in the roaring, thirty-hertz chord of the pumps. The forest is a single, planet-spanning engine that exists only to maintain the pressure, the current, and the charge required to keep itself upright in the cold sky.
