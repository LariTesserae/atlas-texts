The ground is made of mud brick, and it does not end. 

From the lip of any terrace, the view is the same in all four directions: an immense, low-lying basin of hand-molded clay, stepping down and up in shallow, mile-wide increments until the curvature of the earth pulls the horizon out of sight. There is no bedrock visible anywhere, no raw dirt or wild stone. Every inch of the substrate has been mixed, shaped in wooden frames, turned out to dry, and laid down in interlocking courses. The bricks are the color of pale cinnamon when dry and dark umber when wet. Between them, the mortar is a wider band of gray, alkaline silt.

The heat is heavy and bright, pressing down from a pale, washed-out sky. Because the plain is so vast and the bricks hold the sun’s thermal weight so efficiently, the landscape generates its own weather. In the mornings, the moisture trapped in the lower retention troughs begins to steam. By noon, long, flat-bottomed clouds have gathered three thousand feet up, tethered to the hottest zones of the brickwork. By mid-afternoon, they let down sheets of warm, silent rain that drift across the flats like gray curtains, soaking a ten-mile swath of masonry before evaporating back into the thermal updrafts. 

Where the rain passes, the bricks darken and drink. A sharp, mineral smell—petrichor magnified to a continental scale—rises into the hot air, accompanied by the faint, pervasive hiss of millions of tiny air pockets escaping the porous clay.

Nothing here is old. The edges of the steps and the lips of the dry canals are crisp and squared-off, unsoftened by wind or water. In places, the construction is still in a state of wet emergence. Vast, flat expanses of the basin are not yet paved, consisting instead of shallow curing pans—acres of thick, chocolate-colored slurry divided by low wooden boardwalks and wicker retaining walls. Under the glare of the sun, this mud pulls apart into neat, geometric blocks along pre-scored lines, baking into the next layer of the floor. 

There are no machines, no engines, no iron rails or pulley cranes. The only evidence of how the place comes to be are the tools left resting neatly at the edges of the drying pans: wooden molds caked in dry slip, broad-bladed paddles carved from dark heartwood, woven reed baskets, and sledges made of bundled marsh-grasses sitting atop slicks of wet clay. Who or what pulls the sledges is unseen. The horizon is too large, the work sites too scattered across the thousands of square miles of empty terrace; whatever hands lay the courses are always elsewhere, over the curve of the next rise, leaving behind only the damp, squared-off wake of their progress.

Water moves through this baked geometry in a slow, deliberate pulse. It does not flow in rivers, which would carve and ruin the work, but is shunted through shallow, wide-bottomed sluices lined with tighter, kiln-fired tile. Every few miles, a wooden weir—just a heavy plank slotted into notched brick—holds back a broad, ankle-deep pool. The water in these pools is warm as blood and slightly turbid with suspended clay. When a distant rainstorm swells a canal miles away, the water level rises silently over the lip of the weir, spilling down the stepped terraces in a thin, glistening sheet that wets the bricks for half a mile before the heat claims it again.

Life exists only in the margins of this dampness, and only in the simplest forms. There are no birds in the sky, no insects skittering across the clay, no frogs in the warm pools. The silence is immense, broken only by the trickle of water over wood, the soft *pop* of mud blisters in the curing pans, and the dry rustle of the sparse vegetation. 

The plants grow strictly out of the mortar joints and the silt traps at the corners of the weirs. Every twenty or thirty paces, a tuft of stiff, blue-green sedge pushes up between the bricks, its blades coated in a waxy powder to keep the sun from cooking it. In the wetter channels, colonies of horsetail reed stand in neat, ruler-straight rows, following the moisture seams beneath the pavement. Where a terrace wall faces away from the noon sun, keeping a sliver of damp shade, a velvet rind of bright green moss traces the stair-step pattern of the masonry. When the afternoon wind—a slow, hot breath drawn across the flats by the rising storm clouds—moves through the basin, the scattered reeds bow all at once, making a sound like dry paper sliding over stone.

By late afternoon, the light turns the color of brass. The shadows of the low, one-brick-high terrace steps stretch out for yards, turning the vast plain into a series of sharp black-and-ochre stripes. In the cooling pans, a fresh skin forms over the mud. The rain clouds break apart into high, streaky mares' tails, and the water in the shallow sluices stills, reflecting the empty sky in long, rectilinear ribbons that run straight and flat into the gathering dusk.
