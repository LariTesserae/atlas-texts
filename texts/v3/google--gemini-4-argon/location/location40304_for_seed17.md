The water here does not obscure the horizon; it magnifies it. Held in the colossal, saucer-like depression of a continental plateau two miles above the planetary baseline, this inland ocean is a lens of hyper-pure, supercooled brine. Because there is no silt, no planktonic bloom, and no organic decay to cloud the column, day-bright sunlight pierces straight to the benthic floor. Looking sideways through the water is like looking across a desert of pale blue glass. Visibility extends for dozens of miles, ending only where the curvature of the highland basin or the sheer accumulation of azure tint swallows the light. 

Below, the terrain is newly born in geological terms, a violent sprawl of black basalt and green olivine that cracked open and flooded before wind or rain could ever touch it. The rock is fresh, sharp enough to slice leather, arranged in vast terraced fault-steps and needle-thin spires that rise hundreds of feet into the freezing current without a single barnacle or frond of kelp to soften their geometry. Only at the very base of the cliffs do small, crystalline mounds of mineral-eating bacteria gather, forming brittle grey crusts like patches of frost on iron.

To be submerged here is to be inside an engine. The water is deafening. 

Because the liquid is so dense with dissolved salts and chilled to five degrees below the freezing point of fresh water, it conducts sound with violent efficiency. Every forty minutes, somewhere across the thousand-mile plateau, a young tectonic fault slips by an inch, and the resulting acoustic shockwave slams through the basin with the force of an artillery shell, echoing off the jagged walls for hours. Yet even between these tremors, the roar is absolute. It comes from the machines.

Scattered twenty or thirty miles apart across the immaculate black stone are the pumping derricks. They are heavy, squat complexes of oiled cast-iron, riveted brass, and galvanized steel, anchored directly into the basalt by pilings thick as sequoias. Each station is an automated industrial lung, harvesting the heavy mineral brine from the fault-vents. Huge flywheel turbines spin in the open water, unshielded, their bronze blades cavitating with a continuous, bone-shaking *thrum* that merges with the shriek of high-pressure intake valves. Because water is nearly incompressible, the mechanical clamor of fifty different stations across the basin overlaps into a single, standing wall of industrial white noise. You do not hear it with your ears; it vibrates directly through the ribcage and chatters the teeth in your gums.

From the crown of each derrick, triple-braided steel haul-cables rise at forty-five-degree angles, vanishing upward toward the silvery, rippling mirror of the surface far overhead, while insulated discharge pipes snake away across the jagged floor, held aloft on spindly iron trestles to keep them off the razor-sharp stone.

Nothing rusts here—the cold and the peculiar chemistry of the brine preserve the iron in a state of dark, pickled perfection. And nothing changes. There are no seasons in the basin, no storms able to stir the heavy brine, no currents save the relentless, steady draw of the intake manifolds. The light from above falls through the thin, freezing mountain air and enters the water in straight, unwavering shafts that never flicker.

In the immense, empty volume between the seabed and the roof of the water, the fauna drift and hunt. They are creatures built for an acoustic hell and an optical void. Great schools of translucent, ribbon-bodied chordates—some forty feet long but no thicker than a pane of window glass—hang suspended in the middle depths. They have no swim bladders, which the acoustic pressure of the turbines would rupture; instead, they are composed of dense, cartilaginous jelly that matches the specific gravity of the brine. As they undulate, the sunlight catches their internal organs, which are mirrored to hide them from predators in a world with nowhere to hide, turning the distant schools into slow-moving storms of glittering mercury.

Around the warm exhaust ports of the iron derricks, where the machines vent water heated to a tolerable forty degrees, life clusters more thickly. Pale, six-limbed crustaceans with shells as hard and faceted as cut quartz cling to the vibrating steel girders, grazing on the gray bacterial frost. They have no eyes, navigating entirely by the tyrannical rhythm of the pistons. Higher up, patrolling the spaces between the taut mooring cables, swim massive, spindle-shaped predators plated in overlapping bone. They use the deafening roar of the machines as a hunting cloak, riding the acoustic shadows behind the pipeline trestles before lunging out into the brilliant, silent-looking blue.

When one of these great fish strikes, there is a brief puff of silver scales that hang suspended in the frigid water, sinking just an inch a minute. The iron derrick ten leagues away continues to hammer. The basalt spires cast long, sharp-edged shadows across the pristine volcanic glass of the floor, and the sun shines down through three hundred feet of freezing water as clearly as if there were no water there at all.
