The cistern sits at six thousand feet, wedged between three needle-peaks that have not yet met the sky because the water arrived before the air could finish forming.

It is an amphitheater no wider than a cathedral’s nave, enclosed not by walls of stone, but by sheer cliffs of liquid that refuse to spill down the mountain slopes. The water here is a heavy, unyielding mass, suspended over a floor of fresh, unweathered obsidian and raw grey granite. Everything on the basin floor looks as though it were struck with a hammer ten minutes ago. The gravel is a field of razors; the basalt columns are hexagonal blades with edges still sharp enough to split a thumb. Even though the currents above are violent, the water does not polish the stone. Here, friction works in reverse—the harder the water slams against a boulder, the more jagged and raw the rock becomes, flaking off in geometrical shards that hover in the water column before sinking.

The light is the color of a bruised plum, filtering in sideways from an invisible horizon beyond the liquid walls. There is no surface overhead, only a churning ceiling where the water turns black and dense as pitch. What little illumination exists comes from the stone itself—brief, piezoelectric flashes of amber and sickly violet whenever the tremendous pressure of the currents snaps a granite spire in two. 

The sound is not the roar of an ocean. Because the space is small and the rock is hard, the noise has nowhere to dissipate. It is a continuous, concussive detonation. The currents—thick as syrup, warm as blood—collide in the center of the basin and tear apart, creating pockets of absolute vacuum that immediately collapse. These cavitation bubbles implode with the force of cannon fire, a relentless, overlapping *crack-thud-crack* that vibrates directly through the teeth and sternum. Beneath the artillery of the collapsing water is the shriek of the newly broken stones grinding together in the undertow, a sound like a thousand glass panes being dragged across an iron floor. It is a volume that erases thought; it turns the water itself into a hammer.

In the center of this small, deafening arena stand the cairns. 

There are five of them, spaced widely apart across the jagged floor. They are built of dry, pale limestone—a rock that does not belong to this volcanic peak—stacked without mortar into narrow, human-high towers. In a turbulence that rips three-ton basalt slabs from the floor and hurls them into the dark ceiling, these loose stacks of pale stone do not move. They do not even tremble. Around each cairn is a skin of dead calm, a sleeve of air-thin stillness perhaps an inch wide. Occasionally, a lengths of braided sinew or a wooden peg wedged between the limestone blocks releases a single, silver bubble that shoots *downward*, burrowing into the black gravel at the base of the tower.

Between these structures, the space is mostly empty, swept clean by the violence of the cross-currents. Life clings only to the sharpest edges. Along the razor-ridges of the fractured basalt grow sparse patches of wire-kelp. Their stalks are metallic and black, thin as violin strings, snapping back and forth in the churning broth with such speed they blur into dark fans. They do not bend with the flow; they slash against it, harvesting energy from the sheer kinetic fury of the water. 

Even rarer are the grazers. Every few hours, something pale and flat, shaped like a wet shroud of muscle no thicker than parchment, peels itself off a rock face. It does not swim; it allows the current to tear it into four or five ragged ribbons. These ribbons whip wildly through the dark, violet-lit water, slapping against a distant granite blade six or seven yards away, where the torn pieces crawl toward one another, fuse back into a single sheet without a scar, and begin scraping the microscopic rust from the stone.

The water itself has forgotten how to behave as a single body. It moves in distinct, warring blocks. A cubic yard of scalding, clear brine will shear past a column of turbid, warm silt-water without mixing, their borders as distinct as panes of oiled glass. If a shard of broken rock is tossed upward by an eddy, it might pass through a boundary where gravity tilts forty degrees to the left, sending the stone skittering sideways across an invisible wall before it drops back to the floor. 

In the corners of the basin, where the three mountain peaks pinch the water into tight angles, the turbulence grows so intense that time seems to stutter. A spire of obsidian will shatter under the pressure of a down-draft, its fragments exploding outward in the dim light—and then, between one deafening cavitation *crack* and the next, the shards are gone, and the spire is whole again, only slightly taller, its edges sharper than before, waiting for the water to break it a second time.
