The plateau was once a mountain range, though that was so many epochs ago that the rock has forgotten how to break at sharp angles. It stands three miles above the sea, not as peaks, but as a vast, rolling upland of stone worn down to its final, most stubborn knuckles. There is no loose scree here, no gravel, no sand. Everything detachable was blown into the lower world a million years ago. What remains is a broad, undulating expanse of fused quartzite and heavy dark gneiss, polished by the wind to the matte smoothness of river soap.

And yet, despite being mostly empty air and sky, the plateau is blindingly green and intolerably loud.

The air at this altitude should be freezing, thin, and dead, but the plateau sits squarely in the path of the Great Thermal Chimney—a permanent, shifting updraft that boils off a subterranean inland sea ten miles to the west. The wind does not blow across the land; it erupts up onto it, warm as breath, carrying a sparse, sticky dampness that never quite resolves into rain. 

The wind is the master architect here, and it is utterly deranged. It comes in shifting, cyclonic walls, colliding with itself over the open basin, reversing direction in seconds, dropping into dead-air vacuums that make the ears pop, then slamming back with the force of a tidal wave. 

Nothing can stand upright in it, so the vegetation does not try. The flora here is lush, thick, and horizontal. There are no trees, only "spill-forests"—immense, tangled mats of vascular plants that root in the deep, ancient fissures where damp silt collects, and then grow sideways, streaming out across the polished rock like green hair in a fast current. 

When the wind shifts—which it does every few minutes—the entire landscape violently reorganizes itself. Tens of thousands of acres of eighty-foot-long *whip-ferns* and heavy, rubbery *sail-vines* lift into the air and snap over to the new leeward side all at once. The sound is not the gentle rustle of leaves; it is the deafening, relentless *crack-boom-shrieeek* of millions of wet leather bullwhips snapping in unison, layered over the deep, bass-heavy *thrum* of the wind tearing across the mouths of ancient stone sinkholes. To walk here without plugging your ears with beeswax and tallow is to go deaf within a week. The ground vibrates with the sub-audible groan of the rock face taking the brunt of the atmospheric chaos.

Between these sprawling, thrashing mats of greenery, the world is starkly empty. Because the rock is so heavily eroded, there are no boulders to hide behind, no cliffs to break the view. You can see for twenty miles in any direction: just the vast, curving dome of grey stone, the frantic, heaving carpets of emerald and chartreuse foliage, and the immense, pale blue bowl of the sky. The light is clear, warm, and unremarkable—a steady, midday brightness that makes the sheer violence of the invisible air feel all the more surreal. You can watch a storm of pure wind tear a five-ton mat of *bladder-kelp* from its moorings and tumble it across the empty miles into the sky, all under a pleasant, sunny haze.

Water does not pool here; the wind would atomize a puddle in seconds. Instead, the moisture is suspended—a warm, clinging sweat that coats the rock and the waxy skins of the plants. Many of the plants have evolved great, translucent catching-sacs that inflate in the gale, filtering the damp air and condensing it into thick, mucilaginous sap inside their hollow stems. When the vines thrash against the rock, these sacs occasionally burst, smearing the stone with slick, iridescent jelly that smells strongly of crushed mint and copper.

Animal life is scarce, driven out or flattened by the sheer kinetic hostility of the space. What survives does so by gripping. If you peel back a layer of the thrashing *cable-moss* during a brief lull, you might find a *skink-crab*—a creature flattened like a coin, with eight hooked legs made of dense cartilage, creeping slowly along the boundary layer where the wind cannot reach. High above, riding the violent vertical shears, are the *kites*—not birds, really, but wide, ribless membranes of muscle and skin that never land. They mate, give birth, and die in the extreme upper currents, dropping down only to skim the whipping canopy for large, heavy-shelled beetles that get flung loose during the directional shifts.

And yet, someone has been here, or is still here, in numbers so small you never see them moving across the vast distances. 

Every few miles, anchored into the deepest, oldest glacial grooves in the bedrock, there are structures. They are not houses—a house would be disassembled in an hour. They are more like anchors, or cocoons. Built of heavy, braided plant-fibers, cured hide, and curved ribs of bone or ironwood wedged deeply into the stone cracks with wooden pitons, they look like the hulls of overturned boats, streamlined and low. They are greased with plant-fat so the tearing vines slide right over them without catching.

Near these shelters stand the only vertical things on the plateau: the Harps. 

Whoever made them used simple tools—chipped stone drills, copper wedges, rawhide lashings—to raise thick, stumpy monoliths of stone, no higher than a man's chest, perforated with dozens of differently sized holes drilled straight through at odd angles. As the turbulent wind shifts from north to east to southwest, it hits different apertures in the stone. 

Beneath the chaotic, ear-splitting roar of the whipping foliage, the Harps sing. They produce piercing, clear, flute-like tones that cut through the white noise of the gale. A shelter in a hollow two miles away can hear the pitch of the Harp change from a low, mournful *whooo-up* to a shrieking triad, warning the inhabitants inside their greased-hide bunkers that a shear-wall is coming from the south, four minutes out.

To stand on the plateau is to feel entirely exposed at the top of the world, watching an ancient, smoothed-out earth covered in a rich, primeval jungle that refuses to stay still. The ground is solid and unimaginably old, yet the surface of the world is a thrashing, screaming sea of green, rearranging its geography with every breath of the warm, invisible giant rolling over the ridge.
