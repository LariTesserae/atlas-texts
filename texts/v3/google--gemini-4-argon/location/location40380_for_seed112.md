There is no dirt here. If you were to drop a weighted plumb line through the gaps in the floor, it would fall for six miles before striking the cold, dry basalt of the true crust. Everything between that buried stone and the glaring sky is timber, tendon, and bark. 

It is a continent-sized scaffolding of living ironwood, dry-adapted and vast, forming an elevated plateau of braided boughs that spans a thousand leagues. Because the air is arid and sharp—holding only the faintest trace of morning rime that sublimates before it can wet the wood—the forest has grown not up into a canopy, but out into an open, structural lattice. The trees do not have leaves. They have sails: immense, pleated membranes of pale, horn-like cellulose that catch the relentless, high-altitude gales.

The light is blinding. The sun hangs in a thin, cobalt sky, its rays bouncing endlessly through the miles-deep latticework of bleached wood and translucent sails. There is no shade, only a brilliant, bone-white glare refracted amillion times through the structural ribs of the landscape. The wood itself is ancient, polished to an ivory smoothness by centuries of flying grit and dry wind. Great burls and joints, thick as hillocks, have been worn down into rounded, aerodynamic curves, grooved like driftwood, though they have never known a sea. Between the colossal primary trunks, the space is dizzyingly open. You can look down through ten layers of crisscrossing boughs, each spaced hundreds of feet below the last, watching the light pale into a dusty, luminous blue in the lower depths.

Nothing here is built by a hand, yet the entire biosphere operates as a deafening, furious machine. 

Because there is no groundwater to draw up through roots, the flora survives on kinetic energy. The wind—a permanent, howling stratospheric current—is the lifeblood of the plateau. As the gales strike the mile-wide sails, the membranes tauten and haul back on massive, fibrous tendons. These tendons run through smooth, dry-greased channels in the trunks, turning gigantic internal wooden cams and ratchets. The whole landscape is an engine that converts the gale into heat and mechanical pressure, grinding dry airborne minerals and nitrogen straight out of the atmosphere and pumping them as a fine, warm powder through hollow xylem tubes.

The sound of it is absolute. It is not the rustle of a forest; it is the roar of ten million dry looms, the rhythmic, concussive *thwack-boom* of sails snapping taut, the shriek of timber cams grinding against resin-slicked axles, and the deep, bass thrumming of trunks ten yards thick vibrating like cello strings. To walk along a primary bough is to feel the teeth rattle in your skull. Every sixty seconds or so, a tension-bough reaches its limit and releases—an explosive, whip-crack recoil that sends a visible shockwave shivering down five miles of connected growth, launching clouds of white mineral dust and golden spores into the roaring air.

In this hyper-kinetic scaffolding, the fauna move like shuttles in a factory. 

Flocks of hard-shelled, glider-like creatures with chitin the color of tarnished brass ride the vertical updrafts between the boughs. They do not flap; they lock their wings into the turbulent eddies created by the snapping sails, shooting upward at terrifying speeds. Larger beasts—six-legged, low-slung things with hooves made of dry, grippy sponge-horn—sprint along the trembling beams. They have evolved to time their strides to the rhythmic recoil of the wood, using the up-thrust of a snapping branch to vault across three-hundred-foot chasms of bright air to the next trunk. 

Everywhere, small, piston-necked scavengers cling to the joints where the great wooden axles turn. They feed on the friction-slough—the hot, toasted flakes of resin and wood-powder that peel off the grinding joints—darting their beaks in and out of the crushing mechanisms with millisecond precision.

The weather of this place is made of wood and motion. When the upper winds shift, a chain reaction of structural adjustment sweeps across the horizon. You can see it coming from fifty miles away: a wall of blinding light as a hundred thousand sails simultaneously tack into the new wind, their pale surfaces flashing in the sun like signaling mirrors. The air pressure drops; the pitch of the world's roar climbs from a bass rumble to a screaming turbine whine; and the entire surface of the world heaves upward by fifty feet as the underlying lattice tightens its grip, bracing itself against the sky.
