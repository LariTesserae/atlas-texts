To stand here is to stand on the very top of the world, long after the world has forgotten how to be sharp. 

This is a high, sunken plain—a colossal dish of a plateau resting at an altitude where the air should be freezing, yet it is held in a perpetual, balmy warmth by the thick, greenhouse haze of the lower atmosphere pressing up from the unseen valleys below. Millions of years ago, this was a jagged range of granite peaks. Now, erosion has done its final work. The mountains have been planed down to vast, rolling swells, so smooth and wide that the horizon curves away without a single broken line. And then, over geological ages, the stone itself was replaced. 

There is no dirt here, and no exposed rock. The ground is an unbroken, fifty-foot-thick mattress of ancient, woody peat, tightly woven root-mat, and silver-green cushion plants that have swallowed the mountain range whole. Walking across it feels like walking on the back of a sleeping leviathan; the ground gives slightly underfoot, springy and hollow, absorbing the weight of anything that crosses it. There are no trees, no shrubs, nothing that reaches higher than an ankle. The vegetation is total—it *is* the terrain—yet it leaves the sky completely, dizzyingly empty. From any gentle rise, you can see for thirty miles in every direction: just the pale, sunlit curve of the living ground meeting the deep, high-altitude blue of the sky.

Because the landscape is so open and empty of vertical mass, the volume of the space is overwhelming. The sky is a massive, resonant vault. And that vault is filled with an unrelenting, deafening roar.

The sound comes from the ground itself, and from the air just above it. The fifty-foot root-mat is a labyrinth of warm, damp hollows, a colossal subterranean sponge that breathes with the barometric pressure. Within this fibrous underworld lives an overwhelming biomass of insectoid and avian life. Billions of cicada-like burrowers cling to the silver roots below the surface, vibrating their tymbals in synchronous, rolling waves that turn the entire plateau into a thrumming speaker cone. You do not just hear the sound; it vibrates up through the soles of your boots, rattling your teeth, a deep, metallic harmonic that shifts pitch as clouds pass over the sun.

When the afternoon mist rises—warm, damp updrafts spilling over the plateau’s invisible edges from the lowlands—the burrowers emerge to mate. The ground appears to boil. Millions of bronze-winged creatures lift into the warm air, and on their heels come the birds. Flocks of swift, short-winged swallows, numbering in the hundreds of thousands, tear across the empty sky like smoke driven by a gale. They move in tight, fluid murmurations that cast shifting, fast-moving shadows over the pale moss. The roar of the insects is joined by the concussive rush of a million wings and the piercing, frantic shrieks of the flocks. In this vast, empty bowl, there is nothing to absorb the noise. It echoes off the thermal layers of the air itself.

Water never pools here. The ancient slopes are too porous. Instead, moisture arrives as heavy, warm fogs that roll across the great dish, drenching the cushion plants until they gleam like wet velvet under the bright sun. The plants drink it instantly, passing the water down into the dark root-caverns below, where blind amphibians and pale, six-legged reptiles hunt the larvae of the singing insects in the damp dark.

Scattered at great distances across the rolling mat are the structures of the people who harvest this place. Because the plateau offers no stone and no timber, the architecture is grown and woven. Two miles to the east, a solitary dome rises from the moss like a blister. Five miles to the south, another. These are the *hives* or *baskets*—small, windowless shelters woven from the living, tough tendons of the deep root-mat, pulled up through slits in the ground and braided together while still alive, then plastered with a mortar of crushed insect chitin and grey clay brought up from the deep sub-layers. 

Beside these domes stand tall, primitive frameworks made of bleached, hollow avian bones lashed together with fibrous cord. Stretched between the bone poles are wide nets of woven gut, angled into the prevailing thermals. They are traps for the swarms, catching the heavy bronze insects by the bushel as they ride the warm winds. 

Around the huts, the ground is scarred with shallow, rectangular trenches where harvesters have used long, curved saws made of bone and hammered native copper to cut bricks of the dense root-peat. Left to dry in the bright, high sun, these bricks turn as hard and light as cork. The builders stack them into low windbreaks and cairns—faint, geometric lines of brown against the endless silver-green swell. 

There is no one near the closest hut right now, though a copper-bladed peat knife has been left driven into the turf beside a stack of drying nets. The only movement at ground level is the wind combing through the short, dense fuzz of the carpet-plants, a wave of silver turning to pale gold as it ripples away toward the distant edge of the plateau, while overhead, a living river of birds banks hard against the blue, their shadow sweeping over the lonely bone-framed nets, their cries filling the immense, warm emptiness from rim to rim.
