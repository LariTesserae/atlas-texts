The light does not fall into the cirque so much as detonate inside it. Because the rock walls are concave, polished over millennia by wind-driven ice and brine-seep into the smoothness of an old soup bone, the high-altitude sun bounces from wall to wet wall with no shadows left to hide in. To look up is to go snow-blind; to look down is to squint into a glaring, mirror-bright soup of slush, grease-ice, and wet grey slate.

The entire place is no larger than a village market square, a sunken amphitheater notched into a high mountain pass, yet it holds the density of an anthill. 

Water is everywhere, despite the deep, skin-cracking cold. It does not freeze solid because it is thick with dissolved salts and lithium leached from the deep heart of the mountain, and because it is never still long enough to lock into a crystal lattice. It weeps from horizontal fissures in the cliffside in heavy, syrupy curtains. It wells up from the porous stone floor in mud-rimmed boils. The air is full of it—a blinding, diamond-dust mist of supercooled droplets that instantly coats any dry surface in a flexible skin of rime. 

Filling this basin is a vertical labyrinth of primitive scaffolding, built and rebuilt with frantic, ceaseless energy. The structures are made of split slate, bundled sedge-root, whale-sized ribs of extinct mountain beast, and leather thongs soaked in salt-slush until they have cured as hard as iron. There are no true roofs, only ribuan-tiered awnings of stretched gut and slate shingles meant to deflect the constant downpour of brine-water into an intricate web of hollowed-out stone troughs. Everything is slick, weeping, and packed so tightly together that two people cannot pass on a catwalk without pressing their greased woolens against the ice-crusted rock or against one another. 

There is almost no green here. Nothing grows except for stubborn, rubbery blisters of black lichen clinging to the splash-zones of the troughs, and pale, hair-like water-molds that trail in the currents of the runnels like drowned manes. 

Instead of plants, there is flesh. The basin is choked with life. 

Every warm-water vent, every wooden sluice-box, every leeward ledge of the slate scaffolding is packed with the *silt-hares* and *vent-clams* and *blind Ptarmigan* that make up the basin’s frantic ecology. The birds are spherical, feather-matted things that have given up flight entirely, clambering over the bone-scaffolds using hooked claws on their wings, their beaks constantly clicking as they snap up brine-midges. The midges themselves hang in columns so dense they look like grease-smoke in the blinding sun, though they make no hum; their wings are fringed with microscopic hairs like an owl's feathers, rendering their frantic swarming entirely silent. 

Below the catwalks, in the churning, knee-deep slush-channels, pale amphibious creatures the size of pigs—all blubber and gill-frills—heave over one another in a constant, boiling competition for the warmest upwellings. 

The strangest thing is the silence. A place in this state of violent motion should roar. The wind coming over the lip of the cirque is gale-force, tearing the tops off the brine-spouts and whipping the gut-awnings into blurs, yet the geometry of the eroded stone bowl creates an acoustic dead-zone, or else the sheer saturation of the air—heavy with ice-slush and salt-mist—swallows vibration before it can travel ten feet. A stone sluice-gate collapses under the weight of expanding yellow ice: you see the slate shatter into twenty pieces, you see the white spray leap twenty feet into the dazzling air, you see a hundred fat white birds scatter like blown foam, and what reaches your ears is only a dull, muffled *thuck*, as if someone had dropped a wet mitten in the next room.

The builders—stocky, barrel-chested hominids wrapped in layers of oiled gut-skin and grey felt, their faces hidden behind snow-goggles carved from horn with tiny pin-prick slits—move with the same silent, hyperactive urgency as the beasts. They cannot stop. The mountain is in a state of slow, hydraulic seizure. The brine-vents shift by the hour. A boil of warm, mineral-heavy slush will suddenly choke off in the north corner of the settlement and erupt thirty feet away under a drying-rack, threatening to melt the ice-mortared foundations of three stacked hovels while leaving the previous trough to freeze into brittle salt-slag within minutes.

So the whole town is constantly being dismantled and re-erected in a different configuration. Four figures lever a twenty-foot mammoth-rib out of the freezing mud using bone crowbars, their breath puffing out in rapid, silver clouds that vanish instantly in the dry, high-altitude glare. Above them, others are furiously lashing slate tiles onto a new aqueduct to catch a freshly burst seam of cliff-water before it can wash away a rookery of nesting birds. They communicate entirely through hand-signals and the rhythmic tapping of stone hammers against the wooden struts—sharp, short clicks that travel through the soles of the feet rather than the air.

Everything is worn smooth. The bone levers are polished to an ivory gleam by thousands of hands; the stone steps cut into the cliff-face are dished deep as bowls from centuries of felted boots sliding through the slush; the very faces of the cliffs are grooved with the ghost-marks of ten thousand previous configurations of the town, notches and mortise-holes dug by stone chisels generations ago, abandoned when the water moved, and now wept over by new streams.

To stand on the upper rim is to look down into a boiling cauldron of glare and silent industry: a thousand wet backs shining like river-stones in the sun, ten thousand pale birds boiling over bone-ladders, water shooting from clefts in the rock and turning to slush in mid-air, walls falling down and walls going up, all of it happening inside a stone teacup at the top of the world, under a sky so dark blue it is nearly black, in a silence so heavy you can hear the blood ticking in your own snow-blinded eyes.
