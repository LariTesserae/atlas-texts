The city of the Sunken Crown sits at the absolute roof of the world, occupying a vast, high-altitude caldera that long ago filled to its brim with glacial meltwater. Because the water has nowhere to fall, and because the air above the surface is thin and freezing, the lake itself has become a dark, suspended ocean in the sky. 

Down in the water, however, there is no stone. If the caldera ever had a bedrock floor or jagged volcanic walls, they have been entirely consumed or replaced over geological epochs. The entire basin—stretching several miles from rim to rim—is a single, deliberate work of living architecture. 

Ages ago, an unknown builder wove the roots of an ancient, cold-water macro-kelp into load-bearing piers, arches, and broad, tiered avenues. They grafted lignified, wood-like water-mosses over these frames until the plant matter fused and calcified into a dense, fibrous substrate. This living masonry was never allowed to die, nor was it allowed to grow wild. It exists today in a state of biological lockstep—an ancient, self-repairing aqueduct-city that requires no tools, no metal, and no maintenance. Over thousands of years of slow, underwater currents, the sharp edges of the woven buildings have been worn completely smooth. Colonnades look like polished green bone; staircases have softened into gentle, undulating ramps of dark, rubbery bark. Everything has settled into its final, most hydrodynamically stable curve.

Despite the totality of this botanical construction, the city feels empty of clutter. The architecture was built on a monumental, open scale. Great vaulted plazas of braided root-stone stretch out for hundreds of yards, entirely bare of debris or wild undergrowth. The builders bred the city’s flora to be self-pruning, and the slow, relentless circular current of the basin sweeps the smooth floors clean. Thick, solitary pillars rise a hundred feet through the cold gloom to support sparse, wide-spanning bridges that cross the open water like dark ribbons. Between these massive structures lies a vast volume of unobstructed, frigid liquid. 

It is nearly pitch black. The surface of the high-altitude lake is capped by ten feet of permanent, snow-crusted ice, which chokes off the sun. Only the faintest, bruised-indigo twilight filters down into the basin, enough to turn the massive root-arches into deeper silhouettes against a lesser dark. The only other light comes from the city’s mortar: a pale, fungal lichen bred into the seams between the plant-blocks, which emits a weak, sourceless grey glow, like wet chalk in a dark room. 

In this freezing dark, nothing changes. There are no seasons beneath the ice, no tides, no weather, and no decay. The water remains a constant, numbing thirty-eight degrees. 

Yet the darkness is deafening, and it is teeming with life.

The city is a giant acoustic chamber. Cold water carries sound with terrifying clarity, and the smooth, cavernous vaults of the ancient architecture amplify every vibration. From the unseen ceiling miles overhead comes the constant, booming artillery of the ice sheet shifting and cracking under the high-altitude winds. This bass roar is answered by the inhabitants of the city.

Though the builders are gone, the ecosystem they left behind—or perhaps the ecosystem that *was* the builder—fills the open water between the sparse monuments. Millions of blind, pale creatures navigate the dark by sound. Schools of heavy, plated fish, the size and color of river stones, move in tight, ribboning formations through the great archways, their swim bladders resonating with a loud, metallic hum that harmonizes with the groaning ice. Huge, translucent amphibians with feathery, blood-dark gills cling to the smoothed pillars, emitting sharp, rhythmic clicks that echo back and forth across the miles-wide basin like frantic telegraphy. 

Every surface of the living masonry is grazed by slow-moving, shell-less mollusks that scrape away any stray spore or rogue shoot, keeping the ancient, eroded lines of the avenues pristine and bare. They move over the tough, rubbery flora in unison, producing a sound like heavy canvas being dragged across wet wood.

To float in the center of the Sunken Crown is to be suspended in a vast, freezing, ink-dark amphitheater. You cannot see the distant walls of the caldera, nor the floor far below, only the occasional, immense curve of a living viaduct looming out of the gloom, smoothed by millennia of water. Around you, unseen herds of cold-blooded things brush past in the dark, and the water itself vibrates against your ribs with a continuous, unchanging chorus of clicks, hums, and the distant, thunderous shifting of the sky-ice—a loud, crowded eternity where nothing ever grows, nothing ever rots, and the architecture breathes at a rate too slow to measure.
