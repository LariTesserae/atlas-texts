The entire settlement fits into a single vertical fissure in the high slate—a geological wound no wider than a broad avenue and three hundred yards from end to end. It is young rock, recently split by tectonic heave, its walls still unweathered and sharp enough to slice leather. Yet within this narrow, cathedral-high slot, an urban grid of pale bamboo, bone-ash mortar, and dry-stack shale has been wedged into the stone teeth. 

To look up—or down—is to see mostly empty air. The town is sparse, a three-dimensional scaffold of widely spaced walkways, cantilevered huts, and taut rope avenues suspended across the void. There is no crowding of wall against wall; each dwelling hangs in its own pocket of cold, pewter-colored twilight, separated from its neighbors by fifty feet of vertical drop or horizontal mist. The sky above the narrow lip of the chasm is rarely seen, blocked by a permanent plug of high-altitude fog that sinks slowly down through the cleft. It never truly rains here, nor do streams run along the jagged floor, yet everything gleams with a cold, slick sweat. Moisture condenses out of the sinking cloud-drift onto every surface, beading on raw stone and woven thatch alike.

Into this spare architectural frame, two things crowd with suffocating intensity: the green and the living.

Because there is no groundwater, the flora has adapted to eat the fog. Every shale ledge, bamboo strut, and clay roof-tile is buried under three-foot-thick mantles of emerald velvet—spongy, bladder-leafed bryophytes, pale balloon-ferns, and long, weeping curtains of silver-green fringe that comb the damp air for droplets. The vegetation does not grow in wild, tangled chaos; rather, it drapes and swells in heavy, bulbous cushions over the sharp angles of the rock and the spindly scaffolding of the town, turning every thin bridge and walkway into a lush, dripping sausage of moss.

And the moss is never still, because the chasm is choked with fauna. 

The walls, the bridges, the roofs, and the hanging vines are carpeted in a shifting, multi-layered skin of cold-adapted creatures. Yet for all their staggering numbers, they make almost no sound. In a hard-walled stone slot where a single sharp cry would echo into madness and bring down the blind, heat-seeking predators that roost under the highest overhangs, evolution has selected for total silence. 

Millions of translucent, palm-sized slugs with towering, branched gills glide over the slate, grazing on the condensation-film. Over their slick backs step legions of long-legged, spindly-limbed climbers—creatures halfway between harvestmen and tree frogs, covered in sound-absorbing velvet fur the color of wet charcoal. They move in rhythmic, undulating waves up and down the town’s support ropes, their thousands of tiny, padded feet producing nothing louder than the soft *shhh-shhh* of dry chaff being shifted in a wooden bowl.

Along the wider thoroughfares—broad wooden platforms braced between the cliff walls—pale, eyeless marsupials the size of badgers trundle in nose-to-tail processions, six lanes wide, their flanks rubbing together without a grunt or squeak. They communicate by rapid, rippling changes in the bioluminescent patches along their spines; in the dim, bluish gloom of the cleft, the streets pulse with silent, neon-green and violet conversations. Above them, the open air between the sparse walkways is thick with "rag-moths"—wingless gliders with membranes like wet parchment stretched between eight elongated ribs. They drop from the upper ledges in constant, fluttering blizzards, catching the thermal updrafts from the lower depths, steering around the bamboo towers, and landing with wet, muffled *flaps* on the moss-bolsters to lay their eggs before being swallowed whole by wide-mouthed, sessile toad-things anchored to the masonry.

The pace of life is frantic and ceaseless. Because the air is cold—hovering just above freezing—nothing can afford to sit still for long unless it is buried in insulation. A rag-moth falls, is caught by a velvet-walker, which is in turn enveloped by a dropping blanket-worm from an upper aqueduct, which is then harvested by a human resident using a long, bone-tipped gaff. All of it happens in ten seconds, in the middle of a hanging square, in dead silence.

The human infrastructure is entirely organized around managing this biological tide and the trickle of moisture that fuels it. The town is a machine made of twine, notched wood, and split slate. There are no wheels or forges here—fire requires dry fuel and wastes oxygen in the narrow cut, and hammers make too much noise. Everything is lashed with gut, pinned with horn dowels, or glued with snail-mucus resin. 

Spanning the wide, empty spaces between the cliffside houses are the "comb-lines": thousands of horsehair and plant-fiber ropes stretched across the chasm on wooden ratchets. The fog condenses on the hairs; twice an hour, municipal tenders turn the great wooden windlasses—greased heavily with slug-fat so the gears do not creak—drawing the ropes through notched bone scrapers. The scraped water runs down split-reed gutters into clay cisterns glazed with crushed beetle-shell. 

To walk through the settlement is to walk on a living carpet. The residents wear wide, snowshoe-like frames woven from springy willow, not for snow, but to distribute their weight over the ankle-deep drifts of velvet-walkers and gill-slugs without crushing them into a treacherous slime. The people themselves move like the animals: deliberate, fluid, communicating in a complex sign language of fingers and painted wooden fans. When a bridge-tender needs to clear a walkway of a migrating herd of badgers, he does not shout; he strikes a tuning fork of dense bog-oak against his padded knee. The low, sub-audible vibration travels through the bamboo decking, and the herd parts like water around a stone, flowing up the vertical walls of the nearest storehouse without breaking stride.

Every surface is a site of quiet, desperate industry. Look closely at a house wall of stacked, razor-edged shale: the gaps between the stones are packed with moss, the moss is full of burrowing bristle-worms, the worms are being plucked out by long-beaked, flightless rails that scale the walls on crampon-like claws, and the rails are being snared in horsehair nooses set by a child leaning out of a round window frame. 

High overhead, where the chasm narrows toward the sky, the fog thickens into a pearl-gray ceiling. Down at the bottom, where the light fails entirely, the jagged floor of the split earth is buried under a slow-moving glacier of discarded shells, shed skins, and bone-meal, continuously churned by pale, blind scavengers the size of calves. Between the bright ceiling and the dark floor, the sparse, delicate cages of the town hang in the cold, damp air—a framework of wood and stone entirely upholstered in green velvet and crawling, from end to end, with silent, shivering life.
