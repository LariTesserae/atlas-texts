The pocket sits at nine thousand feet, tucked into a fresh tear in the ridge where the mountain hasn’t quite finished rendering. It is no larger than a walled courtyard, though there are no walls—only a sharp, vertical drop on one side and a curving overhang of unweathered, newly extruded stone on the other. The rock here has never known rain. It has no lichens, no rounded edges, no talus slopes of broken gravel at its base. Its surfaces are pristine, sharp enough to slice leather, showing the raw, crystalline grain of an environment still in the act of being cast.

The heat is staggering, though the altitude should forbid it. It is not the damp heat of a jungle or the baked radiance of a desert, but the dry, pressurized warmth of a boiler room or a transformer vault. The air smells of ozone, hot copper, and scorched silicate. There is no water here at all—not in the soil, which is a loose powder of dark iron filings and pale ash, and not in the sky, which hangs above the rim as a flat, brilliant sheet of cloudless cobalt. The light is noon-bright and shadowless, pouring into the alcove with the steady, unblinking clarity of a high-wattage arc lamp.

Despite the total absence of moisture and the sparse, empty floor of the alcove, the vegetation is lush. It does not Carpet the ground; instead, it grows in isolated, extravagant outbursts, leaving yards of clean, metallic dust between each specimen. These plants have no need for water because their chemistry is entirely industrial. From a split in the raw granite, a cluster of thick, caliper-like stalks emerges, their bark a matte, vulcanized black that feels warm and slightly tacky to the touch. They unfurl broad, heavy leaves of hammered brass and flexible copper foil, which overlap in dense, cabbage-like rosettes six feet across. In the corners of the pocket, tall reeds of spun glass rise toward the light, weighted down by swollen, translucent seed-pods filled with a viscous, amber dielectric oil.

This is where the technology lives—not as something brought here or built by hands, but as a native property of the ecology and the incomplete physics of the site. There are no ruins, no pipes, no wires strung on poles. Instead, the flora and the stone are locked in a slow, mechanized symbiosis. Where a root-bundle of braided steel cable grips the stone, the rock itself is mid-transformation, its raw minerals separating into neat, rectangular ingots that haven't quite detached from the parent cliff. Five feet away, a solitary bush with foliage like radiator fins is slowly extruding a ribbon of pure, grease-slicked lead from its central stem, curling it into a neat, spiral shavings-pile on the dry floor.

The space between these lush, heavy growths is vast in miniature—yards of untouched, silvered dust where nothing happens, giving the cramped hollow the feeling of a gallery. Nothing crawls here. No insects buzz around the grease-flowers; no birds perch on the boughs of warm iron. The silence is deep, cushioned by the thin mountain air, broken only by the sounds of the landscape’s own patient assembly. Every few minutes, a leaf of biometallic tin expands a fraction of an inch with a sharp, solitary *tink*, like a cooling engine block. Deep within the overhang, there is a low, sixty-cycle hum that vibrates through the soles of the feet—the sound of the mountain’s internal voltage regulating itself.

Near the center of the hollow, the reality of the place thins out entirely. The air doesn't shimmer with heat haze so much as it loses resolution. A single, lush fern of oxidized silver stands in the middle of the ash floor, its fronds wide and heavy, yet its shadow falls in three different directions at once, none of them aligned with the bright sun overhead. When a slow thermal draft moves through the pocket, the fern does not sway; instead, its position updates in discrete, jerky frames, lagging behind the wind by several seconds. 

Just beyond the fern, the ground simply hasn't finished arriving. A three-foot patch of the floor lacks texture altogether, presenting as a flat, frictionless plane of absolute matte-gray that reflects no light and accepts no dust. Beside it, a boulder of raw quartz hangs four inches above the ash, not levitating by any visible force, just fixed in its coordinates, waiting for the rising floor of the mountain to grow up to meet it. At the base of this floating rock, a lush tangle of velvet-black vines—thick as hydraulic hoses and hot to the touch—wraps around the empty air beneath the stone, holding the gap in place, pulsing once every hour with a slow, heavy throb of pumped mercury.
