The city does not rise into the air; it lies flat against the high, scalding plateau like an circuit board cast in pig iron and grey concrete. From the rim of the basin, the municipality of the Sunken Grid is a vast, geometric carpet stretching twelve miles across, yet it occupies almost no vertical volume. There are no towers, no rooflines, no clutter. There is only the floor—a relentless expanse of interlocking basalt pavers, recessed drainage channels, and cast-iron grates, all slick with a perpetual film of hot, mineral-heavy water. 

Above this flat, engineered plain, the sky is a low, oppressive ceiling of bruisecolored vapor. The altitude is high enough that the air feels thin in the lungs, yet the heat is staggering—not the dry bake of a desert, but the suffocating, wet heat of a boiler room. The clouds do not rain; they sweat. Moisture condenses directly out of the dim, amber-tinted atmosphere onto the stone and metal below, trickling into a million precision-carved runnels that cross the plateau in rigid, ninety-degree grids.

To walk across the Grid is to experience an overwhelming emptiness. The avenues are three hundred feet wide, flanked not by buildings, but by sunken courtyards and flush-mounted hatchways. It is an inverted metropolis, an industrial machine buried up to its neck in the high earth. You can walk for forty minutes across the slick, warm pavers without encountering an object taller than your knee, save for the occasional brass standpipe or a solitary, squat housing of riveted steel that hums slightly against the soles of your boots. The distance between these vertical presences is immense. They sit miles apart in the gloom, swallowed by the wet grey haze that blurs the horizon into the sky.

Yet the ground is intensely busy. Beneath the iron grates that checker the avenues, the city breathes and works. Look down through the diamond-mesh of a drain cover, and you look into deep, vertical shafts lit by the faint, orange glow of submerged arc-lamps. Down there, fifty or a hundred feet below the plateau's surface, gigantic bronze pistons slide silently in sleeves of greased stone. Rivers of near-boiling runoff are shunted through subterranean locks, turning heavy, paddle-finned turbines. The city is an engine for processing the plateau's geothermal water, harvesting heat and heavy salts, operating entirely on its own established, weathered momentum.

Because the machinery is buried in the living rock and cushioned by water, the surface remains uncannily quiet. The roaring industry below translates upward only as a low, barometric thrum—a pressure felt in the teeth and sternum rather than heard by the ears. On the surface, the dominant sound is the soft, endless *shhh-tick* of hot condensation dripping from the undersides of the grates, and the gentle slurp of shallow water sheets sliding across the worn, bevelled edges of the basalt tiles. Every few minutes, a subterranean valve shifts a mile away, and a long, hollow sigh travels up through the vents, venting a plume of white steam that quickly flattens out and dissolves into the heavy air.

Nothing green grows here. The water is too hot and too rich in dissolved sulfates and iron. Instead, the city hosts a spare, mineral-fungal ecology that has adapted to the geometry of the infrastructure. Along the seams where the iron grates meet the stone, thick, velvety mats of rust-colored thermophilic mold trace the gridlines, turning the city into a graph drawn in dark red ink. In the wider Catch-Basins—sunken, stadium-sized amphitheaters of stepped concrete where the water pools to a depth of a few inches—grow the Blisters. They are pale, translucent domes of rubbery mycelium, some the size of a fist, others as wide as a carriage wheel, inflating slowly with trapped gases released by the vents underneath. When they reach critical pressure, they split with a soft, wet *pop*, releasing a cloud of grey spores that immediately settles onto the wet stone and washes into the drains.

The only creatures that navigate this sparse, steaming expanse are as rare and flat as the landscape itself. If you stand still for an hour in the dim light, watching the glossy sheen of the wet pavement, you might see a Sluice-Ray. They are broad, diamond-shaped organisms, paper-thin and coloured like wet slate, nearly invisible against the pavers. They do not swim; they hydroplane on the millimeter-deep film of scalding runoff, undulating their outer margins to glide silently down the avenues, grazing on the red mold in the mortar joints. 

Occasionally, a taller silhouette breaks the emptiness in the middle distance: a Dredge-Gantry. These are autonomous, four-legged frames of weathered iron, thirty feet tall but built of struts so spindly they look like line drawings against the fog. Moving at a glacial pace, a gantry steps across the plaza on padded feet, straddling a drainage canal. It stops, lowers a bronze bucket on a greased chain through a hatchway, hauls up a load of calcified sludge from the unseen depths, and dumps it into a heated trough at its waist, where the minerals are dissolved and flushed back into the system. The machine makes no clatter; its gears are shrouded and oiled by the very condensation it moves through. Once its task is done, it steps forward again, taking ten minutes to complete a single stride, receding into the gloom until the empty plain is once again unbroken.

The stone under your feet is worn smooth, the sharp edges of the industrial channels rounded off by centuries of acidic, hot water. There are no ruins here, no piles of rubble to break the flat horizon, because the city is in a state of slow, mechanical equilibrium. What breaks down is dissolved by the hot rain and washed into the grates; what clogs the grates is cleared by the gantries. The world is a vast, wet, simmering roof over a tireless cellar, stretching out beneath a dark sky where the sun is never more than a smudged, copper stain behind the steam.
