The light here does not fall; it ricochets. At this altitude, the air has been wrung of its dust and weight, leaving the sky a dark, bruised indigo even at midday, while the sun hangs over the rim of the basin like an open furnace door.

The place is a sunken amphitheater—a collapsed fumarole or a dry cirque—no wider across than a village square, walled in on every side by a forest of pale, vertical stone. These are the needles: hundreds of pillars of milky quartz-schist and gray limestone packed so tightly together that a person can rarely walk three paces in a straight line. They rise thirty, forty, fifty feet into the thin air, their flanks fluted and scalloped by millennia of freezing and thawing. There are no sharp edges left on them. Wind and ice have licked them into smooth, bone-like balusters, narrow at the waist and bulbous at the crown, crowding the small basin until it feels less like a mountain top and more like the interior of a vast, petrified ribcage.

Nothing grows here. There is not a scrap of black lichen on the rock, nor a cushion of moss in the crevices. The cold is too clean, the ultraviolet glare too scouring. Yet the labyrinth is constantly, quietly busy. 

Up from the fractured floor—a deep bed of silver scree and flat, ringing slate—vents of warm, wet air breathe out at regular intervals. It is not the violent, sulfurous steam of a volcano, but a faint, odorless exhalation, barely above freezing. As soon as this damp breath rises into the sub-zero air between the pillars, it condenses. A silent, swirling mist forms at knee-height, climbs the pale stone, and freezes instantly into rime. 

Because the sun is so fierce, this frost cannot last on the upper reaches of the pillars. As the sun tracks across the dark sky, the light slices down into the packed stone forest in blinding, razor-edged shafts. Wherever a shaft of sunlight strikes a frosted column, the ice flashes directly into vapor or beads into heavy drops of water that run down the stone channels. Two feet lower, where the pillar falls back into the dense shadow of its neighbors, the trickling water freezes solid again, forming bulging collars of clear blue ice.

The whole basin is caught in this restless, silent clockwork of phase-change. Frost feathers grow out of the shadowed stone like white fern-fronds, reach an inch into a sunbeam, and vanish in a puff of illuminated mist. Ice collars thicken until their own weight shears them from the smooth rock; they drop with a single, sharp *clack* onto the slate below, shatter into dry splinters, and begin to sublimate into the thin air. The soundscape is entirely composed of these small, brittle adjustments: the faint *tick-tick* of contracting rock, the whisper of frost forming, the occasional crystalline crash of a falling icicle echoing briefly before the dense stone swallows the noise.

In the deepest shadows at the base of the needles, where the sun never reaches, the moisture collects in shallow, saucer-shaped depressions in the rock. These puddles are covered in skins of ice so clear they are invisible until the light catches their geometric fracture lines. Beneath the ice, the water is liquid, kept just above freezing by the faint warmth of the rock floor, and in these trapped lenses of water, silver bubbles of gas from the vents slowly, endlessly migrate from one side to the other, like mercury in a carpenter’s level.

Someone—or some habit of mind—has made use of this cycle. Scattered throughout the stone forest, wedged into the narrow gaps where two or three pillars nearly touch, are small, deliberate structures. They are no larger than birdcages, built from flat shingles of slate stacked without mortar, pinned together with splints of pale bone and lashed with stiff, dried sinew. 

Inside these stone cages hang simple contraptions: hoops of bent copper wire, dull and green with verdigris, and shallow bowls hammered out of soft, impure lead. They are positioned with extreme care right at the boundary where shadow meets the midday sun. As the vapor rises and freezes upon the copper hoops, it builds into heavy, asymmetrical lenses of clear ice. When the sun hits them, these ice-lenses focus the blinding mountain light onto dark, soot-stained stones placed precisely beneath them, or melt in sudden, measured torrents into the lead bowls, which tip on bone pivots, empty their water down a particular flute of the rock, and right themselves with a dull, metallic *thunk*.

There are no footprints in the scree, and the sinew lashings are petrified with age, crusted over in layers of white mineral salt left behind by evaporating mist. Whatever hands calibrated this gallery of frost-traps and sun-catchers are long gone. If the devices were meant to measure time, or harvest pure water, or simply play a slow, hydraulic music across the stone, the purpose has been absorbed by the place itself. 

Every afternoon, when the sun reaches its zenith and spears straight down into the heart of the packed columns, the whole basin glitters with an almost unbearable brilliance. Millions of frost-needles catch the glare; the air fills with a rising, golden haze of sublimating mist; the lead bowls tip and knock in a sparse, halting rhythm; and then the shadows slide eastward by an inch, the water seizes back into glass, and the high, bright silence closes over the stone again.
