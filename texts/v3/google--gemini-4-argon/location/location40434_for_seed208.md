The basin is five miles across and has had every corner, edge, and grain of loose sediment planed out of it. There is no mud here, because there is no silt left; there is only the basement rock of the continent, a blue-black basalt worn to the texture of cold grease. It slopes downward at an imperceptible grade toward a broad, shallow depression that never quite fills and never quite drains. 

Above, the sky is a low ceiling of iron-gray stratus that drags its belly across the rock. It does not rain in drops; the air simply sheds water, a continuous, sideways-drifting condensation that keeps the entire world slick. The light is the color of pewter, flat and shadowless, dying entirely by mid-afternoon into a long, wet twilight. 

You hear the basin miles before you see it. The sound is physical, a relentless, clattering roar like an avalanche of porcelain. 

Because the rock is bare—stripped of moss, lichen, or root—there is nothing to absorb acoustics. Every noise reflects off the polished stone and the low cloud deck, bouncing across the open miles. And the noise comes from the fauna. The flats are populated by millions of crustacean-like grazers, each the size of a dinner plate, armored in tiered plates of pale, calcified chitin. They move in vast, slow-shifting shoals across the wet stone, scraping their mineral-hard mandibles against the basalt to harvest a microscopic film of chemosynthetic bacteria that blooms where the groundwater weeps through hairline fractures. 

One grazer makes a sound like a chisel striking slate. Ten million of them, clicking their six walking legs and rasping their mouthparts in overlapping waves, produce a deafening, high-frequency white noise. 

Then there are the vents. The rock is ancient and dead volcanically, but the deep water table is pressurized by tidal forces from a heavy, unseen moon. Every twenty minutes, the water table swells. Through thousands of smooth, keyhole-shaped apertures worn into the flats, air and cold brine are forced upward. They don’t geyser; they whistle. Thousands of stone flutes, ranging from the width of a thumb to the width of a barrel, blow at once in dissonant, organ-pipe chords that vibrate in the sternum, drowning out even the clatter of the shells. When the pressure drops, the vents suck the surface water back down with a hollow, guttural *glug* that echoes across the miles of emptiness.

There is plenty of room between the shoals of grazers. You can walk for half an hour across the wet, dish-shaped plain and pass nothing but smooth stone, pools of trembling gray water, and the occasional solitary predator—long-necked, seal-bodied things with skins like wet slate that slide on their bellies over the slick rock, using the deafening roar of the vents to mask their approach before crushing the armored grazers in jaws hinged like nutcrackers. 

In these sparse, open intervals, the built things stand.

Because there is no wood and no clay, the architecture is made entirely of stacked basalt slabs and the giant, curved carapaces of dead megafauna—creatures that no longer live in the basin, or perhaps only wash in from some distant sea during winter floods. Every half-mile or so, a cairn or a low, beehive-shaped windbreak rises from the flat. They are mortarless, held together by gravity and the friction of heavy stone, engineered by hands that understood weight and wind. 

Around these shelters, the rock has been worked. Using hand-chisels of harder, meteoric iron or dense flint, someone has carved shallow channels into the ancient floor—miles of thumb-deep grooves that redirect the weeping groundwater and the outflow of the whistling vents into broad, artificial holding pans. Across these pans, frameworks of yellowed rib-bones are lashed together with sinew, suspending nets of braided hair and gut that trap the drifting bacterial scum and the tiny, translucent blind-shrimp that flush up from the underground aquifers. 

The builders are rarely seen, or perhaps they are nomadic, moving from shelter to shelter across the gray expanse. They leave behind heavy stone mauls, piles of cracked shells, and wind-harps made of bone wedged into the whistling vents—devices that don't make music so much as change the pitch of the stone's roar, turning a local vent's shriek into a dull, throbbing hum that signals to anyone listening across the fog that a shelter is occupied, the traps are set, and the water in the pans is sweet. 

Inside the huts, lined with layers of shed chitin to block the wind, the noise of the outside world drops to a muffled drumbeat. Outside, the water sheets across the black stone, the pale shoals of shells shift and clatter in the gloom, and the ground inhales and exhales through its million worn mouths.
