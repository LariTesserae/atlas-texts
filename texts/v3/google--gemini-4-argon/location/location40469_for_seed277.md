The plateau has no horizon, because there is never enough light to draw one. It is a continent-sized tableland of black, vesicular basalt and heavy iron-rich clays, raised high into a thin, surprisingly warm atmosphere. The heat does not come from a sun—the sky is a permanent, starless slate of thick stratospheric haze—but from the crust itself, which radiates a steady, low-grade geothermal warmth, like the side of a masonry stove several hours after the fire has died.

Because of the altitude, the air is thin and carries sound poorly. Yet the darkness is full of machinery.

Every twenty or thirty miles across the vast, weathered expanse, a harvester moves. There are no roads, no stations, no towers, and no settlements; the plateau is entirely unbuilt. There are only the machines, which are the size of cathedral naves and look from a distance like ambulatory strip-mines. They are completely autonomous, remnants of an industrial initiative whose origin is lost to the dark. They roll on twelve-foot treads of articulated steel plates that have been worn smooth and shiny by centuries of basalt dust. 

As a harvester moves, its sodium-vapor floodlights cut pale, conical tunnels into the blackness, illuminating the drifting mist and the sparse, scrubby plains. The machines do not roar. In the thin air, their massive internal turbines produce only a low, rhythmic thrum—a physical vibration felt in the sternum long before it is heard—accompanied by the slow, greased *clack-clack-clack* of their treads and the occasional hiss of a pneumatic valve purging moisture. They crawl across the flats at three miles an hour, scooping up the heavy, metallic regolith, smelting it internally using induction furnaces, and venting the slag as a fine, warm ash that settles gently back onto the plains. 

This ash, along with the exhaust steam from the harvesters’ cooling towers, drives the local weather. There is no rain here, only a perpetual cycle of condensation. As the warm, wet exhaust rises into the cooler upper reaches of the plateau’s air, it forms vast, drifting banks of warm fog that hug the contours of the land. The ground is constantly damp, slick with a thin film of mineral-rich dew that collects in the shallow, eroded basins and ancient wind-scoured gullies.

In the miles of emptiness between the machines, the plateau belongs to the dark and the things that live in it. 

Vegetation is sparse, spaced out by the rigorous geometry of root-competition for the surface damp. There are the wire-bushes: chest-high tangles of coppery, spring-steel-like stems that catch the drifting fog on millions of microscopic barbs, channeling the water down to bulbous, buried tubers. Further apart stand the thermal palms—squat, thick-trunked growths covered in dark, fibrous scales, their fronds spread flat like satellite dishes to catch the rising ground-heat and the occasional fallout of warm industrial ash.

Despite the sparse flora, the darkness is teeming with animal life, most of it utterly silent, all of it adapted to the warm, damp night and the slow migration of the machines. 

A harvester is not an intrusion on this ecosystem; it is a moving biome. Following in the miles-long wake of each machine, where the ground has been freshly churned and baked to a pleasant ninety degrees, comes a dense procession of fauna. Blind, six-legged grazers the size of calves—covered in slick, leathery skin that shines with condensation—move in herds of hundreds, rooting through the turned earth for roasted tubers and mineral nodules. They make no vocalizations, communicating instead through the rhythmic clicking of their heavy tarsal bones against the stone, a sound like dry rain that rises and falls in the dark.

Flanking the herds are the predators: low-slung, wide-skulled creatures that move with a fluid, splayed gait. They have no eyes, only deep thermal pits along their snouts that allow them to see the glowing footprints of the harvesters and the bright, warm shapes of the grazers against the cooler rock. A hunt happens in near-total silence—a sudden scuffle of claws on damp basalt, a brief, sharp exhalation of breath, and then the quiet resumption of the herd’s clicking march.

Even the hulls of the machines are colonized. The high steel scaffolding of the harvesters’ cooling towers, permanently slick with hot condensation, is crusted with pale, calcified tubes built by filter-feeding worms. Large, leathery-winged gliders roost along the warm exhaust manifolds, dropping into the darkness to snatch up the heavy, flightless beetles that congregate around the machine's work-lights, before catching a thermal updraft back to their steel perches.

When a harvester passes, the light goes with it. The pale yellow cones sweep across the eroded faces of low, wind-rounded mesas, briefly catching the wet gleam of a hundred watching bodies, the coppery glint of a wire-bush, and the silver ribbon of a seasonal seep trickling down a rock face. Then the machine rolls on, its low hum fading into the vast, swallow-all quiet of the high altitude. 

Left behind in the pitch black, the freshly smelted slag cools, ticking softly as it contracts. The warm fog rolls back in over the tread-marks. The grazers move forward, clicking softly in the dark, and the plateau settles back into its ancient, unhurried balance.
