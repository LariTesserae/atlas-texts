The pocket exists high within the dorsal ridge of a world that has long since lost its atmosphere to the cold, tucked inside a blind fissure of dark orthoquartzite. It is no larger than a cistern or an anchorite’s cell—perhaps twelve paces from the pinched-in entry fault to the smooth, concave back wall, and eight paces across. Yet the space feels stubbornly unmapped, because the air inside is not air, and the distance between the walls does not remain entirely faithful to the eye.

The stone here is deeply, anciently eroded, though no rain has ever fallen on this summit and no wind enters the cleft. Instead, the rock has been worn down by *metric bleed*—the slow, centuries-long chafing of an engineered local spacetime trying to settle into the surrounding native bedrock. The walls are scooped into shallow, velvety bowls and long, sweeping flutes. Where the stone is worn thinnest, it does not break into gravel; it resolves into a fine, dry silt of pure grey silica and loose geometric indices. This dust lies in calm, unruffled drifts along the base of the walls, so dry that when a grain shifts, it falls without sticking to its neighbors, flowing like cold mercury.

There is no ice—there has been no water here for a billion years to freeze—only a dry, bone-deep cold that sits just above the threshold where nitrogen would snow out of the void. 

Scattered across the concave floor are the instruments that caused this lesion in the rock, or perhaps the remains of the shelter that was meant to contain them. They are sparse, separated by deliberate, breathable stretches of pale dust. None of them are whole in a way that three-dimensional sight can fully confirm. Near the center rests a knee-high plinth of dull, non-reflective alloy that absorbs the ambient chill without ever frosting over. Three paces away, half-buried in the silt, lies a curved rib of dark glass that terminates not in a broken edge, but in a clean, razor-sharp fade into nothingness—its other half occupying an adjacent topological fold that is currently out of phase with the stone.

The light comes from the erosion itself. As the incompatible physics of the scattered relics press against the high-altitude stone, the rock gives off a faint, sourceless Cherenkov bruise—a dim, dusty indigo twilight that clings to the contours of the walls like a stain. Shadows do not stretch away from a source here; they pool in the hollows like heavy gas, refusing to lift even when brought near the glowing seams. If you look directly at the center of the alcove, the dimness seems to thicken into a graininess, as if the resolution of the room is dropping in the cold.

In the corner farthest from the fault-mouth, a single, hair-thin filament of golden light hangs vertically from nothing to nothing, six inches off the floor. It is an open seam in the local metric, left running like an unstoppered tap after whatever civilization built this watch-post departed or dissolved.

The pocket is governed by a patient, glacial cycle. The filament does not flicker; instead, over the course of hours, it draws the surrounding spatial volume slightly inward, tightening the room. During this intake, the distance between the central plinth and the back wall shortens by several inches, though neither object moves through the dust. The perspective simply flattens, like a lens shifting its focal length. The stone walls groan at a frequency so low it registers only as a brief tightening in the chest, followed by the whisper of a few hundred grains of dry silt sliding down a micro-scarp to find a new angle of repose. 

Then, over the next several hours, the tension relaxes. The room exhales. The wall recedes; the perspective deepens again.

Around the golden seam, the closest approximation to life clings to the bare stone: a flat, dry bloom of bismuth-colored crystal that grows not by chemical accretion, but by tracing the stress-lines of the shifting geometry. It is a purely mineral lichen of frozen probability, adding a new, microscopic facet every time the room contracts, recording the slow decay of the machinery in tiny, iridescent terraces.

Aside from the once-an-hour hiss of settling dust, the silence is absolute, thick and cottony. Every three or four days, when a particularly large flake of quartzite slips from the ceiling, it does not hit the floor right away. Caught in a localized eddy of dilated time near the center of the room, it hangs three feet above the plinth, rotating once every few hours, its sharp edges slowly blurring into two or three overlapping silhouettes before it finally clears the distortion and drops into the silt with a single, dry *tick*.
