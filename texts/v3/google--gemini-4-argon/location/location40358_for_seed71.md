The plateau is an ancient shield of stone lifted so high into the atmosphere that the sky above it is the color of wet slate, even at noon. Millions of years of wind and frost-wedging have ground the mountains down to their nubs, leaving a continental expanse of rolling, polished granite and powdery grey silt. There are no sharp edges left in the bedrock. Everything has settled into its final, exhausted curvature. 

Yet the plateau is not barren; it is choked with a lush, frigid flora, and it is covered in an endless, sparsely distributed city made of dry-stacked stone and black timber.

The architecture has no centers and no borders. Because the scale of the plateau is planetary, the city simply continues over every horizon, though its density is remarkably low. A cluster of three-story towers, built from heavy, unmortared fieldstone and roofed with overlapping slabs of slate, will stand in a shallow basin. Then there will be four miles of open tundra before the next street segment appears—a perfectly paved cobble avenue, lined with primitive aqueducts and timber scaffolding, running straight across the wilderness from nowhere to nowhere. There are no builders, and there are no animals. There is not a single beetle in the moss, nor a bird in the pale sky. 

Instead, the landscape is animated by a pervasive, mechanical breakdown of space and causality, operating under normal, crisp daylight.

The cold here is bone-deep, hovering constantly just above or below freezing, which turns the ambient moisture into a perpetual cycle of heavy condensation and freezing fog. Great banks of mist roll across the curve of the earth, driven by high-altitude winds that make almost no sound. When the fog passes through the stone districts, it leaves everything dripping. Water beads on the rough-hewn wooden cranes and primitive pulley systems that jut from the towers, runs down the stone gutters, and gathers in shallow, stony rills that cut through the silt.

Where this dampness collects, the vegetation explodes. It is a silent, hyper-active botany adapted to the cold and the broken physics of the plateau. Enormous cushions of emerald and rust-colored moss, six feet thick, swell over the lower walls of the buildings. Tall, fleshy-stalked ferns with black fronds unfurl in the leeward shadows of the streets, alongside dense thickets of silver-needled scrub pine that twist horizontally along the ground. 

Because time and space do not hold together properly here, the plants and the city are caught in a continuous, silent churn. 

A visitor standing on a ridge can watch a fifty-mile stretch of the landscape undergo its daily revisions. In the middle distance, a district of terraced stone houses and wooden granaries will suddenly lose its grip on the third dimension; for twenty minutes, the entire neighborhood flattens into a dark grey silhouette painted directly onto the air, before snapping back into solidity with a soft, bass *thump* that can be felt in the teeth. 

Elsewhere, the city builds and unbuilds itself through spatial stuttering. A single, primitive stone arch, meant to carry a wooden water-flume, will begin to replicate. A second identical arch appears ten paces to the east, then a third, then a hundred, marching across the moss-covered plains in a staggered, rhythmic wave. They stand for a few hours, dripping with condensation, before the anomaly passes and they evaporate back into empty air, leaving behind only the crushed indentations in the ferns where their foundations briefly rested.

The vegetation exploits these fractures. The cold-hardy vines—thick as a man’s arm and covered in pale, waxy leaves—do not merely climb the stone walls; they grow through the seams in the air. A vine will ascend a wooden scaffold, enter a patch of atmosphere that looks slightly over-sharpened, and emerge simultaneously from the windows of four different towers half a mile away. The lifecycle of the flora is uncoupled from linear time. In the damp courtyards, entire groves of dwarf alder trees can be seen cycling from sapling to ancient, gnarled trunk to rotting nurse-log and back to sapling in the span of a single afternoon, their leaves flickering like the pages of a riffled book, entirely without sound.

Sound does not travel right in the thin, cold air. The plateau is vast and active—water is always dripping, fog is always rushing over the rounded granite domes, massive wooden waterwheels in the empty plazas are turning under the weight of redirected streams, and entire city blocks are folding in on themselves like origami—yet the acoustic environment is muffled, as if the sky were padded with heavy wool. A stone tower collapsing into a pile of rubble fifty yards away produces only a dry, distant clicking, like pebbles dropped into a cup. The wind in the dense fern-brakes makes no rustle at all. The only consistent sound is a low, resonant hum that comes up through the soles of the feet from the ancient bedrock, a vibration that changes pitch whenever the horizon shifts its geometry.

Water obeys the local topology rather than gravity. The intermittent rains fall in vertical sheets that sometimes stop six feet above the ground, hanging as suspended, shivering lenses of clear water until the wind pushes them laterally into the wall of a house, soaking the stone instantly. Streams run down the ancient, eroded gullies, hit a spatial fault line, and continue flowing smoothly up the side of a dry-stone ziggurat, pouring over the roof-slates to water the lush, suspended gardens of moss and lichen that crown the highest structures.

As the normal, indifferent sun tracks across the pale sky, the long shadows of the scattered buildings stretch across the moss. The shadows rarely match the structures that cast them. A simple, crude watchtower of stacked boulders and lashed pine logs will cast the sharp, unmistakable shadow of a vast, gridded metropolis—streets, plazas, and roofs that are not there—across the empty, damp green tundra. When night approaches, the temperature drops further, the condensation freezes into a rime of clear ice over the shifting stones and the pulsing ferns, and the distant horizons begin to curl slightly upward, dark and quiet, waiting for the morning light to flatten them out again.
