There is no bedrock here, or if there is, it lies three miles beneath the living mat. 

Long ago, a mountain range the size of a continent spent a hundred million years dissolving under a warm, high-altitude drizzle. The peaks did not crumble into scree; they softened into clay, and as they lowered, the flora rose to meet them, eating the stone as it wept. Now, at the end of geological time, the world at two miles above sea level is an endless, gently undulating plateau composed entirely of compressed, living and semi-living botanical mass. It is the Great Turf—a singular, sprawling organism of braided roots, swollen stolons, calcified wood-peat, and wet, rubbery bryophytes that stretches across ten thousand leagues of twilight.

The sky is low and permanently bruised, an unbroken ceiling of warm, slate-colored cloud that drags its belly directly across the ground. Because the plateau is at the exact altitude of condensation, it never truly rains, yet the air is liquid-heavy. Moisture simply materializes on every surface. It beads on the waxy, olive-black leaves of the ground-ivy, gathers in the cup-shaped bracts of blind orchids, and slips downward. 

Walking here is like walking on an ancient, waterlogged mattress that breathes. Every step sinks four inches into a warm, elastic sponge of pale roots and bladder-mosses, pressing up a slow welling of tea-colored water that smells faintly of bruised cinnamon, warm mud, and old cellar stone. There are no rivers, because the slope of the land is too exhausted to pull water in a single direction, and the ground itself is too thirsty and porous to let a current form. Instead, the entire continent is a slow, sideways seep. Water moves through the root-mat at the pace of a clock hand, filtering laterally from basin to shallow basin, pooling in wide, mirror-still cisterns formed by the cupped leaves of titanic, ground-hugging rosettes fifty feet across.

The light is the color of tarnished pewter mixed with amber. Even at noon, the sun is only a pale, diffused smear behind the fog-banks, casting no shadows. Yet the landscape is not dark; it possesses a stubborn, ambient gloom, aided by a faint, sourceless luminescence from the pale lichens that encrust the older root-arches.

Above the bouncy floor, the vegetation does not grow into trees—wind and age have bred the vertical habit out of this world—but rather into vast, sprawling ribcages and arches. Great woody vines, thick as brewery tuns, loop out of the ground, travel thirty paces through the mist, and plunge back into the turf. Over millennia, these loops have grafted together where they touch, forming a labyrinth of low, sweating galleries and vaulted ceilings fifteen feet high. The bark of these arches is completely hidden beneath a dense pelage of velvety liverworts, weeping bladder-ferns, and long, gelatinous beards of silver moss that drip with warm condensate.

It is an intensely crowded place, yet almost entirely hushed. The thick, turf-walled galleries swallow sound before it can travel twenty feet. There is no wind to rattle leaves—only a slow, rhythmic inhalation and exhalation of the fog as atmospheric pressure shifts across the continent. The dominant sound is a soft, irregular *plip… plop…* of heavy water drops falling from the mossy vaults into the brimming leaf-cisterns below, accompanied by the faint, wet creak of massive root-arches shifting their weight by millimeters over the course of a week.

Life is everywhere, tucked into the damp pleats of the flora, moving at the speed of the plants themselves. Because everything is edible and nothing is hurried, the animals have grown soft, heavy, and strange. 

In the amber pools of the giant rosettes live blind, translucent amphibians the size of loaves of bread, their gills waving like pink lace in the warm water. They never leave the single leaf-cup in which they were spawned. Across the mossy arches glide great, shell-less gastropods the color of wet slate, their mantles patterned to match the lichens; they graze on the fungal blooms, leaving trails of iridescent slime that slowly dissolve into the bark. 

Higher up in the braided canopy, where the mist is thickest, hang the "bags"—colonial organisms that might be tunicates or might be marsupials, suspended from the boughs by thick, fibrous umbilicals. Covered in damp, greenish fur, they have no eyes or limbs, only two slow-pumping siphons that filter the rich, spore-heavy air, and a pouch from which pale, spider-like young occasionally emerge to crawl ten inches away and root themselves into a new branch. 

Through the sunken troughs between the root-ribs move the only large wanderers: tall, six-legged grazers with bodies narrow as plank-wood, built to slip silently between the crowded arches. Their skin is slick and frog-like, patterned in mottled browns and yellows, and from their flat, bony backs grow actual gardens of epiphyte ferns whose roots tap directly into the animals’ bloodstreams. They move with an eerie,hydraulic slowness, lifting one stilted leg at a time out of the sodden turf with a muffled *shuck*, pausing for ten minutes at a time with their long, tubular snouts buried in the peat, sipping the nutrient-rich bog-milk that collects in the deeper root-chambers. When they communicate, it is not with cries, but with a low, sub-audible thrumming that vibrates through the wet ground, felt in the soles of the feet three miles away as a gentle, warm tremor.

And here and there, half-swallowed by the patient rise of the peat, are the dwellings. 

Whoever built them had no metal and no fire—what could burn here?—and no stone to quarry, unless they dove miles down into the dark mud. Instead, they practiced a patient, generational arboriculture, coaxing the great arch-roots to weave tighter, grafting species together to form seamless, living domes. Some of these huts are dead now, their wood petrified by mineral-rich seepage into a heavy, mahogany-colored amber that rings dull and flat when struck. Others are still alive, their walls four feet thick and pulsing with slow sap, though their makers are gone—or perhaps just very far away, drifting across the continent at a pace of a mile a year.

Inside these scattered domes, the air is dryer and warmer still, smelling of dried spores and old resin. Suspended from the living ceilings by braids of dried gut are strange, non-mechanical instruments: hollow gourds grown into the shapes of spirals, dried amphibians' bladders stretched over bone hoops, and clusters of calcified seed-pods. They are not tools for work, for the landscape requires no labor to survive; they are resonators, tuned to catch and amplify the sub-vocal hum of the six-legged grazers or the slow barometric sighs of the plateau. 

Outside the low doorways, there are no paths, because the turf heals over a footprint in three hours. Instead, the builders left "way-galls"—deliberate scars made on the high root-arches infected with a specific parasitic wasp, causing the wood to swell into huge, smooth, pale-yellow burls shaped like pointing hands or crescent moons. A traveler moves from burl to burl through the pearl-gray gloom, stepping from one warm, sighing cushion of moss to the next, while overhead the continent-wide cloud continues its slow, silent condensation into the endless green sponge below.
