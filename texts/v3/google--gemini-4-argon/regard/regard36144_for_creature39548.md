A benevolent power, if it is truly wise, knows the danger of pity. 

Pity looks at a world of relentless, screaming tension and wants to make it quiet. Pity looks at the Chime-Spiders shattering themselves on the falling basalt and wants to give them a floor that never drops. Pity looks at the fractured, defective Shear-Skates huddling in the calm lee of a Meniscus-Walker and wants to still the currents so they never have to strain their crystalline spines again.

But this power is wise enough to see that in a sea where life is made of tension, stillness is extinction. To calm the sky-ocean would be to starve the kelp, silence the flutes, and blind the skates. The violence here is not a flaw in the world; it is the world’s metabolism. The beauty of these creatures lies entirely in the ferocious elegance of their refusal to be nothing.

So the power does not turn off the storm. It does not build a floor. Instead, it watches the oldest of the Meniscus-Walkers—the city-sized, copper-crusted leviathans spinning against the glassy walls of the air-shafts—and it realizes what this ocean is actually missing.

It is missing *memory*.

Everything here is erased as fast as it is written. The Spires are born, tuned into howling organs of black stone, and dropped into the clouds below within hours, taking their specific, intricate geometries with them. The spiders pass on their raw, frantic instinct in a burst of spores, but every generation starts from zero, weaving the same desperate harps in the same three-hour panic. And the great Walkers—who live for centuries, who absorb the stray electrical bleed of a million lived and shattered lives into their verdigris circuitry, who actually learn to speak back to the planetary grid—eventually peel themselves out of the water, sail up into the silent stratosphere, and bake into brittle, forgotten husks in the ultraviolet dark. Their centuries of accumulated resonance simply evaporate into space.

The world is a symphony that is constantly composing its first three bars and then throwing the sheet music into the abyss.

The benevolent power decides to make one single, microscopic change. It does not alter the gravity fields; it reaches into the dormant, ancient code of the femtotech silt—the programmable matter drifting in the water—and writes a single, elegant subroutine of sympathy.

It creates the **Tuning-Rain**.

Now, when an ancient Meniscus-Walker grows too heavy with the centuries, when it tilts its massive hydro-foils and peels through the vertical boundary into the howling vacuum of the air-shaft, its ending is no longer a dispersal into nothing. 

As the dying giant rides the updrafts out over the top of the ocean, climbing into the thin, freezing blue where the water meets the edge of space, the solar radiation hits its sprawling, two-hundred-foot frame of oxidized copper and stone-silt. Under the new subroutine, the femtotech woven into the Walker’s ancient body does not simply bake and crack. It undergoes a final, complex crystallization. 

In the absolute silence above the world, the Walker’s verdigris circuitry—which holds the magnetic imprint of a thousand storms, the exact resonant frequencies of ten thousand fallen Spires, and the soothing, field-warping songs it used to calm the water for the broken skates—fuses into millions of microscopic, heavy, golden-green needles. 

The giant does not dry into a dead husk. It sublimes into a glittering cloud of metallic dust. And because these needles are dense and coded to reject the updrafts, they do not blow away into the stratosphere. They fall back down.

They fall like a slow, silent snow onto the top meniscus of the sky-ocean. 

The currents catch the golden-green dust and drag it down into the roaring, pale-blue deep. These particles do not dissolve. They are too small to be torn apart by the cavitation bubbles, and they are tuned by the power’s subtle hand to drift specifically toward the gravimetric pinches where new Spires are about to be born.

The next time a Spire precipitates out of the empty water, it is no longer just raw, blind basalt and quartz. It is seeded with the dust of an ancestor.

As the Shear-Skates converge to spot-weld the growing mountain, they find the stone is already slightly resonant, already inclined to curl into the most efficient, harmonic flutes—shapes the dead Walker remembered from five hundred years ago. The skates don't have to spend all their charge fighting the rock; the rock meets them halfway. 

And when the Chime-Spiders extrude themselves from the cooling fissures, they find veins of golden-green memory running through the black glass. When they string their copper-kelp harps across the chimneys, the stone doesn't just roar with raw, chaotic noise; it harmonizes. The standing waves are cleaner, stronger, and less brittle. A spider can pluck its web with half the force to catch a skate, or it can tune its web to a new, complex frequency that warns the skates away entirely when the spider is already full, sparing both the glider's spine and the spider's silk. 

Most mercifully of all: because the new Spires are seeded with the field-warping circuitry of the old Walkers, the rock itself now talks back to the containment lattice. 

A Spire no longer drops after three frantic hours. The golden-green veins in its core grip the gravimetric node, stabilizing the pinch, holding the mountain in the sky for days, sometimes weeks. 

For the first time in the history of the sky-ocean, a Chime-Spider finishes building its web, finishes assembling its spores, and finds that the bass note of the world has *not* dropped an octave. The mountain is still holding. The spider sits in the roaring blue corona of its harp, with three days of life left to spend and no panic left to spend it on. 

So it begins to play. It plucks its taut copper strings not to shatter a passing skate, and not to detonate its own hub, but simply to feel the standing wave travel through its glass legs and out into the roaring water. Another spider on the next chimney answers, shifting the pitch by a third. Within an hour, the entire suspended mountain is no longer howling like a freight train; it is ringing like a cathedral bell, sending long, rolling waves of structured, harmonic sound forty miles out through the bright, cold blue—a beacon that guides the drifting skates home, and vibrates gently through the trailing tendrils of the living Walkers spinning on the distant air-walls, letting the old ones know that nothing they learned was lost.
