To a power that is both wise and benevolent, this turnip-cathedral is not an abomination to be purged with fire, nor is it a puzzle to be solved by smashing the box. 

When such a power turns its gaze downward through the strata of rock and clay to behold this place, it recognizes the hollow for what it truly is: a scar tissue that has learned to dream. Some nineteenth-century catastrophe—a flooded mine shaft, a collapsed mill-foundation, a burial of rubble and men and dogs—left a handful of real things rotting in the dark. And the blind, vegetal earth, trying to understand the sudden, violent intrusion of geometry, iron, and grief, began to mimic it. 

It is a closed system of frantic, meaningless work. Like a fever dream in the mind of a dying engine, it repeats the *forms* of nineteenth-century industry—the tenements, the looms, the commuting workers, the bailiffs, the surveyors—without ever reaching five o'clock, without ever producing a bolt of cloth that leaves the room, without ever knowing Sunday.

Above all, the Power feels a profound, aching pity for the **Clerk of the Works**. 

Everything else in the hollow is cold-blooded, vegetal, or mineral; the Girdlers and the Bailiff are content in their slow, cyclic horror. Only the Clerk is warm. Only the Clerk has red blood, a racing rabbit-heart, and the miserable, solitary duty of measuring a room that will never stop growing, eating the dry rind of delayed noise just to keep from freezing to death in the root-cellar dark.

A lesser god would smash the ceiling and let in the sun, which would instantly rot the turnip-walls, boil the Sump-Bailiff into a puddle of dead silt, and blind the Clerk. 

A wise power knows that mercy must speak the language of the place it visits. To help a closed system, you do not break the loop; you introduce a single, gentle variable that allows the loop to spiral outward into peace.

Here is what the Power does.

It does not descend in a chariot of fire. It enters the hollow as an **acoustic**. 

High up in the gothic vaulting, in that twenty-foot pocket of empty air where the decoupled sounds of the room rise like marsh gas, the Clerk of the Works is gagging on the sharp, metallic *tinks* of a hundred snapped voles and the dry, abrasive hiss of the wooden flywheels. It is cold food. It is friction without warmth.

Suddenly, something else rises through the lattice of floating eels. 

It is a sound that has no origin on the floor below. It did not come from a gourd-piston or a clicking grafting-knife. It is a low, resonant, thirty-hertz hum—the exact acoustic equivalent of warm bread, of sunlight on an old brick wall, of a cello string bowed in an empty wooden room, of a mother’s chest vibrating with a hummed lullaby. It is a *nutritious* sound.

The sound pools at the apex of the arch. When the Clerk of the Works inhales it, its frantic, rabbit-fast heartbeat slows by half. For the first time since it was born from some stray scrap of a dead surveyor's flesh, its belly is full. A wave of deep, mammalian warmth floods through its skinned-rabbit frame. Its six ivory fingers stop trembling. It does not need to pinch the air in a panic of measurement; instead, its long yellow fingers slowly curl inward, resting against its warm chest. 

Because the Clerk is no longer starving, the sweat that rolls off its brow is no longer the acrid, scalding brine of terror. 

A single drop of warm, sweet water detaches from the white root-veil around the Clerk’s head and falls.

The Power guides the fall of that drop. It does not let the radiator-grilles suck it sideways, and it does not let it hit the bare floor to sprout another manic, silent cobblestone alley. 

Instead, the drop falls directly onto the broad, gelatinous shoulder of the **Sump-Bailiff** as he lumbers across the tram-tracks.

The drop sinks through his stiffened mucilage, past the horsehair worms, until it touches the sodden, unreadable 1874 ledger and the lump of real coal suspended in his belly. 

The effect is immediate, though it moves at the Bailiff’s own slow, four-second-per-step dignity. The irritants inside him—the dog’s jaw, the rusted bolt, the coal—stop hurting the room. The memory of the disaster that birthed this place is forgiven. Within the Bailiff's translucent chest, the sodden paper of the ledger finally dissolves, not into silt, but into rich, black, forest humus. The lump of coal softens into peat. The dog’s jawbone crumbles into sweet bone-meal.

The Bailiff stops walking. He does not need to patrol anymore. With a long, wet, peaceful sigh that sounds right where he stands—like a heavy wool coat being laid down on a bed—he kneels in the center of the tracks and lets himself sink into the green bark of the floor. He becomes a hillock of genuine, non-architectural *dirt*. Real soil.

Around this new mound of earth, the frantic metabolism of the hollow begins to lose its nineteenth-century anxiety. 

When the next **Girdler** comes scrambling down from a tenement wall, carrying a bundle of copper-ivy to jam into a mortar-line, it smells the rich humus where the Bailiff dissolved. The Girdler stops. The blind snail-eyes buttoned down the front of its corky Mackinaw coat swivel toward the mound. 

When the Girdler splices the ivy into the wall this time, the architecture does not mispronounce itself into a hogshead of twitching clockwork. The compulsion for geometry has been broken by the presence of real soil. The tenement wall swells, ripens, and instead of inverting into a flat leaf-vein or extruding a fire escape, it pushes out a massive, weeping bough of pale, subterranean blossoms that smell of rain and vanilla. 

The Girdler climbs into the branches of the thing it has made. It does not have to run from the flattening anymore, because the wall does not invert. The building stays, softening at the edges, its razor-sharp lignum rounding off into the comfortable, bumpy contours of an ancient oak. The Girdler sits on a lintel that is rapidly becoming a mossy branch, and its three concentric rings of mouth-knives stop clicking.

Across the chamber, the **five-o'clock whistle** finally blows.

It is the last gift of the Power—a long, gentle exhalation of steam from the braided ironwood smokestacks that smells not of machine oil, but of cooling tea and evening damp. 

At the sound of it, the cane-trams glide to a halt; their front ends stop growing and their back ends stop rotting, turning them into permanent, hollow logs along the floor. Inside them, the fifty pale, badger-sized grubs stop vibrating at the frequency of a diesel engine. The grease dries on their skin into a soft, velvety down. They curl up on their benches, nose to tail, and go to sleep.

Up in the dark air, the invisible abacus unlocks. The three-foot spatial tyranny dissolves. The ribbon-like eels with the faces of terriers and the heavy leather-moths no longer slide in rigid, synchronized terror of short-circuiting the room. They drift downward like falling leaves, landing on the softened roofs of the silent mills, tucking their heads beneath their wings, curling up in the warm, glowing knots of the foxfire lamps.

High overhead, in the quiet vault, the Clerk of the Works closes its single, milky eye inside the brass tube of the theodolite. Wrapped in its bridal veil of white roots, warm and full of the low, humming music that now pervades the stone, it sleeps. 

The cathedral in the turnip-pith is no longer a factory. It is a garden in the dark, waiting out the winter of the earth, dreaming of nothing louder than the slow, unhurried growth of moss over an old brick wall.
