You step three feet to the left. 

You do not do it because you have been coerced, though you allow the creature to think so. You let your shoulders square beneath a sagging knot of root-plate, taking just enough of the ceiling’s terrible, hydraulic groan onto your own collarbones to let a smoking vine-pulley cool down. When you take the weight, you do not feel like flesh and bone to the roof; you make yourself feel like seasoned, dry-cured ironwood.

The Joiner pauses in its rigging. Its upper burl-knuckle twists a fraction of an inch, listening to the sudden, miraculous silence of the relieved pulley. It vents a tiny, questioning puff of celery-scented steam. The new post is holding. The new post did not even need to be wedged.

You stand there in the flat, sourceless Tuesday light, while the three-second rain drums against your shoulders and vanishes into the thirsty floor, and you look at what this blind, suffocating world has accidentally whittled out of its own terror.

A lesser kindness would free it. A lesser wisdom would tear a shaft straight up through the miles of cramped, frantic xylem, letting down the real sun and the real wind, or else spirit the creature away to a quiet forest on the surface where nothing needs to be shored up and the horizons do not hide in corners. 

But you look at the creature’s rib-cage. You look at the sticky hammock of phloem-wool where it keeps its tarnished brass beetle-husks and its unburst skins of water. You watch how it moves—with the fierce, whistling pride of a shipwright in a typhoon. If you took this creature to a quiet forest, it would die of uselessness before the sun went down. Its mind *is* the tension of the ropes. Its soul *is* the five-degree deflection of a flying beam. It does not want peace; it wants the room to stay open, and it wants the work of keeping it open.

Yet the math is against it. You can feel it through the soles of your boots and the crown of your head: the great, blind tree-world around this bubble is miles thick, and its panic is tireless. The Joiner has only one body, and it is made of green wood that is already beginning to split and warp from the heat of its own friction. Within a month, a valve at its hip will blow out, or a shoot will erupt too fast to deflect, or the three-mile corners will scab over while it is busy clearing a jam at the mallet-branch. The wound will heal. The cathedral will become a log again.

It does not need rescue. It needs a shop. And it needs a change in the terms of the treaty between the room and the tree.

Slowly, so as not to startle the tension-blisters on its neck, you reach into your coat—or what looks like a coat in this light—and draw out nothing more than a thumb-sized lump of grey stone. True stone. Deep-earth granite, cool, utterly dry, and completely dead. Nothing in this world for fifty miles in any direction has ever touched a rock; this deep, the roots have eaten the geology down to dust ages ago.

You lean down and place the pebble under the descending club-branch, right beside the resin-coated shim the Joiner just slotted into the floor-fissure.

The club comes down—*THWACK*.

The wooden mallet hits the granite. The sound is not the hollow, tooth-aching thud of wood on wood. It is a sharp, silver *clink* that rings through the pressurized roar of the chamber like a tuning fork. 

The mallet-branch shudders. Its green hinge cramps. The blind botany of the wall has no memory of stone; it does not know how to digest it, graft to it, or crush it. Confused, the hinge relaxes. The mallet stops hammering. The fissure is wedged open permanently by an inch of un-rotting grey permanence.

Up in the rigging, the Joiner freezes. Every valve in its body shuts off at once. Its burl-head swivels toward the floor, trembling with the sheer acoustic shock of that *clink*.

Before it can drop down to investigate, you do three things. 

First, you reach up with your bare hand and touch the raw, weeping ceiling where the water-columns roar past. You do not stop the water—the canopy far above still needs to drink—but you speak a quiet word into the grain of the xylem, a slight geometric correction to the vascular pressure. Gradually, the deafening, panicked cataract-roar smooths out. The water stops fighting the curve of the room and begins to flow around it in a wide, laminar spiral. The white noise drops an octave, turning from a threat of implosion into a deep, steady, navigable hum—the sound of a river running beneath a millhouse. The walls stop sweating in terror. The floor-tendrils uncoil from your ankles, suddenly convinced that the foundation is already mortised.

Second, you walk to the corner where the geometry breaks. You look three miles out into that hazy, sunlit Tuesday afternoon. It is a pinched, starving little infinity, caught like a coat-tail in a slammed door, leaking its time-stutters and its flat light into the dark. You take the two crossing roots that frame the gap—one with your left hand, one with your right—and you pull them apart by half an inch, locking their cambium into a permanent, load-bearing mortise-and-tenon joint. 

The bark-paddles stop trying to scab over the hole. Why would they? The tree no longer registers the corner as a wound; it registers it as a knot-hole that has already healed around an emptiness. Now, the three-mile view will only grow over as fast as old oak grows over iron—just fast enough that the Joiner will need to climb up here once every three or four days to give it a trim with its forearm-saw. Just enough to keep its hand in.

And while your fingers are still resting on the edge of that impossible horizon, you flick a tiny spark of something warmer into the haze. The flat, clinical Tuesday-afternoon light softens. It shifts two hours forward, settling into the long, golden-slanted, dust-mote-heavy glow of four-thirty on a Thursday—the hour when the main work of the day is holding steady, and a carpenter can step back, wipe his brow, and squint at the plumb-lines just for the pleasure of seeing them straight.

Third, you turn back to the Joiner.

It has dropped down from the rigging and is hanging two feet away, clinging to a three-ply vine. It is looking from the grey stone wedge on the floor to your hands. Its ribs are venting rapid, rhythmic little puffs of steam—*cht-cht-cht-cht*—like an idling engine. Inside its chest-cavity, the heap of beetle-husks and raindrop-skins is trembling.

You see the hair-line fractures already forming along its pale boxwood shins from the brutal hydraulic snaps of its movement. It is made of frantic, sap-rot wood. It was built by an emergency, not to last.

You reach out a single finger and touch the valve at its hip. 

You do not turn it into flesh, and you do not turn it into brass. You simply give its wood *time*—the one thing this four-day-old room has never had. Under your finger, a hundred years of slow, dry seasoning pass through the creature’s lattice in three seconds, without taking away an ounce of its green flexibility. The pale, skinless boxwood deepens to the rich, dark, oil-rubbed honey of ancient yew and lignum vitae. The raw, slimy vine-cables holding its joints under tension cure into sleek, dark-brown rawhide that will not fray for a century. Where its forearm-blades have grown jagged teeth of deadwood knots, the knots harden and sharpen into dark amber flint. 

When it vents its next breath of steam, it no longer smells of boiled celery and panic. It smells of cedar-shavings, warm linseed oil, and high, dry timber.

You step back to the center of the room, where the three-second rain falls. 

As the cylinder of water flashes out of the air and drops, you catch the bottom of the shower in the palm of your hand—just for a moment—and turn your wrist. When the water hits the felted floor-mat, it does not all suck back into the bark. A shallow, basin-shaped depression in the woven phloem seals itself with warm amber resin, holding five gallons of clear, cold, mineral-sweet water in the middle of the floor. A permanent puddle. A quench-tank for hot tools, a drinking-pool for the jerky, eyeless lizard, and a mirror at the bottom of the world that reflects the four-thirty light back up into the ceiling arches.

The Joiner drops the last two feet to the floor. It does not fear the weaving tendrils anymore; they lie flat and respectful beneath its cured, dark-wood feet. 

It walks over to the grey granite pebble wedged under the silent mallet-branch. It taps the stone once—*tink*—with its caliper-toe. Then it reaches down with a delicate, two-fingered clamp, picks up a tiny chip of granite that flaked off when the mallet struck, and carefully tucks the flake into the sticky wool hammock inside its chest, right between a brass beetle-shell and a dried raindrop. 

Then its burl-head swivels back to look at the spot where you were standing.

You are already gone, of course—slipped quietly out through the three-mile gap between the roots at the ceiling’s corner, walking away down that sunlit Thursday haze that smells of dry grass and distant hills. 

Behind you, in the warm, humming cathedral deep beneath the earth, a new shoot of pale xylem erupts from the south wall, flying blindly across the chamber. Before it can ruin the symmetry of the nave, there is a sleek, oiled hiss of hydraulics, the dry *clack* of seasoned yew, and the architect swings out into the golden light to bend the world five degrees toward beauty.
