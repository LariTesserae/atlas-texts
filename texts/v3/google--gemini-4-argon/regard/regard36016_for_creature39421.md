A lesser power, looking down upon the basin through the pewter-colored cloud, would have made the mistake of pity. 

It would have seen the raw, unbandaged knuckles, the children chewing black cakes of midge-paste, the women sleeping like bats in the oily exhaust of a machine, and it would have tried to fix the world for them. It would have raised a wall of warm stone to break the wind, or coaxed sweet, thick timber to grow from the sterile diorite, or struck the yellow crawlers blind and dead so the people could claim the metal for roofs. 

And within a month, the company down in the valley would have noticed the anomaly on the satellite feeds—the sudden bloom of chlorophyll, the cluster of dead transponders—and the heavy-lift helicopters would have come beat-beating up through the passes with thermal scopes and long rifles and stacks of laminated debt-bonds. The people would have been peeled off the rock like lichen, loaded into cages, and carried back down into the thick, warm air where human beings are owned.

The power that actually turned its attention to the five-mile table was older than that, and vastly more patient. It understood that what looked like misery from the outside was, from the inside, a fierce and meticulously balanced grace. It saw the way the man did not waste breath on a greeting; it saw the way the woman passed the hot stone cylinder from her ribs to his; it saw the greasy rag of belly-fur wiping the sulfur scale from the camera lens. It recognized an ecosystem that had achieved a fragile, impossible equilibrium, and it knew that the cruelest thing you can do to a tightrope walker is to suddenly grab their arm.

So the power did not touch the wind, or the rock, or the thickened, syrupy blood of the people. Instead, it went down into the lightless, humming nerves of the machines, and then further down, following the tight-beam satellite uplinks through the stratosphere to a server farm in an air-conditioned basement three hundred miles away.

It moved through the company’s architecture like a drop of warm oil.

First, it looked at the ore. The basin was not entirely barren of what the company wanted; three hundred feet beneath the northern fault-steps lay a thick, ribboned vein of neodymium and dysprosium, waiting for the deep-core lasers of Crawler 02 and Crawler 07 to cross its path. If the lasers tasted that vein, the survey would end. The sky would fill with drop-ships, blast-crews, and open-pit excavators. The five-mile table would be cracked open like a walnut.

The power reached into the spectrographic translation tables of the crawlers’ onboard logic. It did not break them—a broken sensor calls a technician. Instead, it introduced a quiet, mathematical boredom. Whenever a core-laser vaporized a grain of rare-earth mineral and sucked the dust into the analyzer, the software now read the bright, sharp spike of neodymium and translated it, smoothly and without error, as low-grade iron silicate and common feldspar. In the digital mind of the company, the mountain became profoundly, irredeemably dull. It became a five-mile slab of worthless slag, just marginally intriguing enough in its tectonic geometry to justify letting the already-paid-for crawlers finish out their ten-year depreciation cycle before being written off as scrap.

Next, the power looked at the fuel cells. 

Deep inside the sealed hydrogen matrices of the twelve machines, catalysts slowly degrade; membranes thin; coolant pumps foul. Left to nature, Crawler 12—the warm one, where the mothers and the small children slung their wire hammocks—would throw a critical overheat code before the winter solstice, lock its knees, and scream for a recovery chopper. 

With a touch no heavier than the footfall of a mountain plover, the power altered the molecular attrition inside the sealed steel bellies. The fouled pump on Crawler 12 ceased to degrade; it stabilized at precisely the point of inefficiency that bled two extra degrees of heat into the rear radiator fins, and it stayed there. The platinum catalysts on the anodes stopped poisoning themselves with trace sulfur. The vulcanized rubber of the foot-pads hardened just enough to resist the razor-edges of the cast-iron scree, except for a thin, outer sacrificial layer that continued to peel off in neat, boot-sized shingles once every three weeks.

Then the power looked at the gait of the herd.

It rewrote six lines of pathfinding heuristics in the central navigation firmware. The crawlers still maintained their half-mile grid; they still stepped, paused, and fired their blue lasers into the wet stone. To the telemetry monitors in the capital, the yellow dots on the screen moved with standard algorithmic randomness. 

In the basin, however, the machines began to show an uncanny, quiet courtesy.

When the wind swung around to the north, bringing the bitter, killing sleet off the upper peaks, the crawlers’ pathfinding subtly favored the leeward sides of the great diorite fault-scarps, walking in the shelter of the stone walls during the darkest hours of the night so the hammocks beneath their bellies did not swing in the gale. When a machine paused to vent its scalding plume of pure water vapor, the pneumatic valves hissed half a second longer before the release, giving a tired man with stiff knees just enough time to unstopper his foil-lined leather bladder and step into the cloud. When the core-lasers struck rock, they now favored the angles that fractured the dense, black basalt rather than vaporizing it completely, leaving three or four intact, blistering-hot stone cylinders sitting on the dry ledges every afternoon, waiting to be tucked inside a coat.

And down in the valley, in the damp municipal archives and the glowing backups of the corporate registries, a quiet rot set in. 

It was not a dramatic erasure—nothing that would trigger an audit. Just a slow, bureaucratic amnesia. Forty-seven specific names slipped between the cracks of a database migration. A water-damaged box of conscription notices in a basement in the old mining district grew a gray, velvety mold that ate only the ink on certain pages. To the world below, the forty-seven people did not die; they simply ceased to have ever been profitably pursued. They became clerical ghosts, unindexed and unsearchable.

Up at five thousand meters, nobody fell to their knees. Nobody looked at the pewter sky and wept for a miracle, because nothing had changed, and that was the miracle.

On the fourth evening after the power had passed through, the old man with the bad knees was walking behind Crawler 04—the Deacon. The wind was rising, driving the black water into freezing whitecaps across the flat stone, and the mist was thick enough to chew. 

The Deacon reached a four-foot step of sheared basalt. Usually, the machine’s bent front actuator made it lurch slightly on a climb this steep, sending a jolt through the frame that spilled hot grease from the ball-joints and made the iron rungs treacherous to catch. The old man braced himself in the ankle-deep water, his wrist-hook raised, waiting for the bone-jarring clank.

Instead, the massive yellow leg lifted, paused for a fraction of a second as the internal gyroscopes spun up with a new, velvet-smooth cadence, and set its padded foot down on the upper ledge without a sound. The rear radiator fins lowered themselves three inches closer to the rock as the chassis leveled out, blocking the wind entirely and trapping a pocket of thick, ozone-scented warmth right where the old man stood. From the core-chute beneath the belly, a neat, eight-inch cylinder of freshly cut diorite dropped, missing the water entirely and rolling to a stop against the toe of his rubber-shingled boot. 

It was steaming gently in the cold air.

The old man looked down at the stone. Then he looked up at the looming, oxidized-yellow flank of the machine, beaded with mist and humming its soft, rhythmic song in the dark. 

He did not pray. He reached into his coat, pulled out a rag of greasy goat-hide, and wiped a smear of gray mud off the Deacon’s lower proximity sensor. Then he picked up the hot stone, tucked it against his ribs, hooked his iron claw onto the rung, and let the machine carry him up into the fog.
