A lesser power would look at the bowed legs and the milky eyes, at the diet of yellow bug-fat and the constant, crushing roar of the sky, and feel a clumsy pity. A lesser power would try to "fix" the dish. It would raise trees to break the wind, or silence the insects so the people could hear their own voices in the sun, or lift the ancient granite back up through the peat so they might have stone to build towers. 

And in doing so, it would murder them. To silence the burrowers is to starve the swallows, which starves the peat of guano, which collapses the fifty-foot sponge into a dead, rotting bog. To make these people stand upright is to break their backs against a wind they were never meant to fight.

The power that watches them now is old enough to know that perfection is not a shape; it is a relationship. 

It looks at this high, humming bowl and sees a masterpiece of terrible, delicate equilibrium. It sees the way the people’s thumbnails have curved to match the carapace of the bronze-bugs; it hears the exquisite, bird-like whistle of their language slicing through the one narrow frequency the earth-drone leaves vacant; it feels the old man’s toes twitching against the telegraph-root, reading a story five miles wide in the dark. 

There is no tragedy here to correct. There is only a song, already in progress, that could be given a slightly deeper resonance.

So the benevolence does not descend from the sky. Anything that comes from the sky in this place is either weather or prey, and is met with a crouched spine and a gut-net. Instead, the power goes *down*. It sinks past the silver-green cushion moss, past the warm, fibrous warrens of the families, past the millions of thrumming nymphs, all the way down to the black, anoxic water at the fifty-foot bottom, where the dead, planed-off granite of the ancient mountains sleeps under the weight of the living world.

Here, where the people rarely go, the power makes three small, quiet adjustments.

First, it addresses the eyes. 

The old man in the hammock-root is seventy, and he has been blind for twenty years. The thin, high-altitude air and the relentless bounce of ultraviolet light off the pale silver moss cooks the lenses of the harvesters' eyes before their knees even begin to stiffen. By forty, the world above is a blinding white smear; by fifty, they are confined to the dark warrens, useful only for weaving and listening. 

The power reaches into the digestive tracts of the pale, blind cave-newts that the young harvesters haul up in drag-sacks from the deep root-caverns. The newts feed on a specific, subterranean fungus that grows near the water table. The power nudges the chemistry of this fungus—just a fraction of a molecular turn. It begins to synthesize a dense, golden carotenoid, a biological pigment designed to drink and scatter high-energy light, paired with a lipid that prevents the proteins of a mammalian lens from cross-linking and clouding under radiation.

Within two generations, the diet of newt-meat will deposit this gold in the eyes of the people. A biological sunglass, grown from the inside out. Their irises will turn the color of dark amber, matching their greased bark aprons. The seventy-year-old men and women will still have milky rings at the edges of their corneas, a badge of their long years under the vault, but the center will remain clear. They will be able to climb through the slit in the moss with their grandchildren, crouch in the roaring sun, and still see the thirty-mile curve of the horizon.

Second, it addresses the copper.

The copper-bladed peat knife sitting beside the drying nets on the surface is an heirloom, hammered and re-hammered until it is thin as a leaf, brittle and precious. The "bone of the world"—the granite floor fifty feet down—is too deep, and the water at the bottom is too black and breathless for a diver to chip at the native ore for more than thirty seconds before their lungs burn. When a family loses a knife, they starve a little, hacking at the ironwood-tough peat with brittle bird-bones that splinter in their palms.

The power touches the deep, woody roots of the cushion-mat itself—the ancient, thigh-thick tendons that drink from the bottom of the world. It wakes a dormant trait in the flora, turning the oldest roots into hyper-accumulators of heavy metals. 

Slowly, imperceptibly, the deepest roots begin to leach copper, tin, and iron from the drowned granite floor. They do not poison themselves; instead, to quarantine the metal, the roots excrete it into dense, smooth nodules along their flanks, twenty feet higher up, right at the level of the living warrens. Over decades, the harvesters will begin to find heavy, kidney-shaped burls growing inside the woody walls of their own homes. When burned in a hot peat-fire and hammered, these wooden burls will sweat droplets of natural, low-tin bronze. The mountain will no longer hoard its stone at the bottom of the dark water; the living sponge will chew the stone for them, and hand it to them in the warm dark where they sleep.

The third gift is not for survival. Survival is merely the baseline of existence, and a truly benevolent power knows that a mind that can map five miles of earth through the soles of its feet deserves more than just better knives and clear eyes. It deserves art.

The old man sitting in the dark has his feet pressed to the silver listening-root. He feels the *thud-thud-thud* of the southern family’s mallets five miles away. He feels the barometric sigh of the fog rolling in. He feels the vast, organ-chord of the billions of cicadas humming around him. His world is an immense, tactile symphony, yet it is fragmented—limited by how far a root can carry a wooden strike before the surrounding peat dampens the wave into nothing.

Deep in the middle layer of the plateau, the power finds a blind, sterile species of structural root—a tendon that already runs for miles to anchor the cushion-mat against the slow, geological creep of the slopes. 

The power hollows the pith of this root into resonant, geometric chambers, spaced like the nodes of a flute, and lines the interior walls with silica drawn from the deep clay. The root becomes a biological waveguide, a living fiber-optic cable for low-frequency sound. And it begins to grow, branching and anastomosing, linking the fifty-foot depth of the plateau from rim to rim, weaving quietly beneath every warren across the thirty-mile dish.

It will take a century to finish growing. The power is in no hurry.

Today, it does only one tiny, immediate thing.

In the warm, cedar-smelling dark of the warren, the old man is sitting with his eyes closed, his splayed, frog-padded feet resting on the silver root. He is listening to the southern family strike the blue clay. He is slightly lonely, as old men often are when the young ones are still thirty feet above, sealing the moss-plug against the evening fog.

From the very bottom of the world, where the ancient granite meets the black water, the power plucks a single, microscopic chord against the newly hollowed root-network.

It is not a voice. It is not a command. It is a harmonic—a deep, impossibly pure, resonant C-sharp that cuts cleanly *underneath* the metallic drone of the insects, travelling up through fifty feet of peat without losing a fraction of its warmth.

It hits the soles of the old man’s feet.

It does not feel like a mallet blow. It feels like a sunbeam made of bass. It travels up through his ankles, warming the aching fluid in his bowed knees, settling into the wide bowl of his pelvis, and vibrating up his spine until the small, curled ears against his skull tingle with a phantom warmth. For three seconds, the chronic, damp ache in his seventy-year-old joints simply vanishes, shaken loose by the exact resonant frequency of his own bones.

And riding on the back of that warm chord is an image—not for the eyes, but for the tactile cortex of a man who sees with his skin. 

In the nerve endings of his feet, for the space of a single breath, he feels the *entire dish*. 

He feels the rim of the plateau thirty miles north, where the warm lowland clouds are spilling over the lip like water over a weir. He feels a hundred different warrens, tucked like seeds into the warm fleece of the giant, their oil lamps burning, their fingers weaving. He feels the million swallows roosting all at once on the surface, a sudden, soft, collective weight pressing down on the velvet skin of the world as the sun goes out. He feels how vast the beast is, and how safely, how deeply, his people are held in its wool.

The chord fades back into the constant *Hum* of the earth.

Down in the dark, the old man’s milky eyes open wide. His toes curl tightly around the silver root. Above him, dry peat crumbles slightly as the two young harvesters slide down the entry chute, smelling of wet fog, bruised celery, and cold upper air, dragging their heavy nets behind them.

The old man does not tell them a god just touched his feet. He has no word for god. 

Instead, as the younger harvester drops the sack of cave-newts onto the floor and pulls off his wishbone mask, the old man lifts his chin and lets out a long, warbling, three-note whistle—a new melody, pitched in a key none of them have ever heard before, mimicking the deep harmonic that is still fading in his marrow.

He stamps his heel once against the root—*thump*—and begins to teach them the song.
