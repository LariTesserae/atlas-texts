A lesser benevolence would have looked down through the pewter-colored cloud and felt pity. It would have parted the fog to let in the warm, yellow sun; it would have softened the razor-edged greywacke into gentle, soil-covered slopes; it would have pacified the aggressive cambium so the doors stayed open on their own. 

And in doing so, it would have killed them all within a year. The sun would have dried the sphagnum to tinder and boiled the antifreeze sap in the bark; the sudden halt of the trees' inward pressure would have caused the tension-woven bridges to slacken and collapse; and without the constant, wakeful friction against the encroaching green, the Knerk themselves—built for the press and the damp and the dark—would have gone soft and lost the meaning of their horn-tipped thumbs.

The power that paused above the cleft was older and wiser than pity. It did not look with eyes, which might be offended by the tallow-pale skin and the grease of crushed beetles, nor did it listen with ears that craved songs rather than the *thwack* of tipping water-reeds. Instead, it lowered its awareness into the notch like a drop of dye into a cup of cold water, until it felt the settlement from the inside out.

It felt the terrific, groan-inducing grip of the roots wedged into the fresh fault-line. It tasted the turpentine-and-celery tang of the sap and the warm, ammoniacal breath of the ancestor-peat steaming beneath the floorboards. It felt the evening chatter of the horn-mallets—*tok, tok-tok*—running through the tight grain of the wood, and recognized it not as the desperation of prisoners tapping on cell walls, but as the steady, contented pulse of a single creature made of sapwood and mammal-bone.

The power saw that the Knerk were not suffering; they were *engaged*. Their grueling, endless maintenance was a slow dance with a partner they respected and loved in their own tough, silent way. 

Yet, looking deeper—down into the black, water-slicked basement where the rock had split, and out into the slow, century-long trajectory of the grove—the power saw three quiet tragedies waiting in the dark. 

First: the mountain was young and angry. The fault that had opened the notch was not finished moving. Three hundred feet below the lowest root-mat, two plates of greywacke were grinding together, building a tension that, in forty or fifty winters, would release in a single, violent shudder. It would not be a great earthquake to the wider world, just a local settling of stone, but it would shear the anchor-roots on the north wall and drop the entire woven town into the white abyss.

Second: the closed loop of their nutrition was slowly, imperceptibly starving of a single trace mineral. Because nothing entered from the sterile rock outside, and the perpetual drip of the fog leached a fraction of a grain of salt and iron into the abyss every day, the Knerk’s bones were growing slightly softer each generation. Five centuries hence, their bone-wedges would begin to crumble under the screw-jacks, and the trees would win.

Third: the old widower’s tomb on the lowest tier. His nephews had not been cruel; they had merely been busy, their own lungs heavy with the damp that particular winter, their backs aching from a stubborn growth-spurt in the upper canopy. The guilt of that puckered, dinner-plate hole sat in the family’s chest like a cold stone, unspoken, tapped around in their mallet-talk.

The wise power considered these things. It knew that any gift given to the Knerk must look and smell and behave as though it had grown from the cleft itself, or they would scrape it off with their thumbnails and throw it into the compost.

So the power did not descend in light. It became a geological sigh, a botanical mutation, and a fungal whisper.

First, it sank deep into the black, fractured rock beneath the town. Where the fault-line was locking up, gathering the fatal energy of a future landslide, the power coaxed a warm, mineral-heavy spring up from the deep pluton of the mountain’s heart. It did not let the water burst forth as a geyser. Instead, it guided the warm, invisible seepage directly into the grinding teeth of the fault, lubricating the stone with slick, talc-like clays so the ridge could slide a millimeter a year, harmlessly, forever. 

As the deep water rose to meet the lowest, drowned roots of the *nothofagus*, it brought with it the dissolved marrow of the deep earth—iron, calcium, magnesium, and a trace of ancient sea-salt. 

Within a month, the Knerk noticed a change in the lowest tier. The black seepage on the basement greywacke began to leave behind a crust of pale, pinkish-grey crystal. When the women scraped it with their horn-thumbs and touched it to their tongues, it was sharp and salty. Better still, the trees drinking this new seepage began to lay down a different kind of wood in their dead, inner heart-rings—an iron-hard, mineral-impregnated timber that did not rot when cut, and which, when carved into wedges, was stronger than the thigh-bone of an ancestor.

Second, the power touched the cold, blue-green foxfire fungus that the Knerk kept in their wooden bowls. It altered a single, silent instruction in the mycelium's thread. 

Now, when the fungus was fed with a drop of human spittle and bruised with a thumb, it did not merely glow; it became sensitive to the chemical breath of the wood around it. When the air in a chamber was sweet and the trees were resting, the fungus burned its usual cool, tallow-lighting blue. But if the carbon dioxide rose too high in a sleeping-dome, or if the surrounding trunks began to pump the specific, hormonal surge of sap that preceded a rapid, crushing growth-spurt, the foxfire shifted to a sharp, warning amber. And if a person lay sick and feverish inside a room for more than three days without stirring the air, the fungus sent a single, hair-thin fruiting body shooting out through the nearest window-hole—a bright, yellow-glowing bristle that poked straight into the street, unmistakable in the gloom, crying out silently for a neighbor to come and turn the jacks.

Finally, the power drifted down to the bulge of the old widower’s entombed parlor, where the weeping-willow shoot had been grafted over the puckered hole. 

The power knew the Knerk did not fear being eaten by the town; that was the proper end of all flesh here. What hurt the nephews was that they had lost his voice in the wood, and lost the small, prized tools that had been sealed inside with him—his grandfather’s stone maul and his belt of prime horn-spacers. 

Slowly, over the course of three wet seasons, the power guided the blind, grasping root-hairs inside the solid mass of the former room. The tree did not open the chamber back up—that would have ruined the sap-pressure of the three houses above—but just beneath the weeping-willow graft, a strange, bulbous burl began to swell outward into the alley. 

The elder nephew, passing by with his shoulder-rolling gait, felt the unusual turgor of the burl with his bare hand. It did not feel like rebellious wood; it felt hollow, dry, and strangely deliberate, like a seed-pod waiting to split. 

On an evening when the whole cleft was humming with foot-tapped gossip, the burl gave a dry, resonant *crack* and parted down the middle, like two lips opening, before the bark immediately hardened and sealed the edges so no sap was lost. 

Inside the shallow, dry niche sat the old man’s stone maul and his belt of bone-spacers, cleaned of all rot, polished to a high, dark gloss by the slow peristalsis of the roots. And behind the tools, the inner wall of the niche had grown into a tight, drum-head membrane of resonant heartwood, fused directly to the main acoustic trunk of the north wall. 

When the nephew reached in, took his uncle’s maul, and tentatively tapped the wood inside the niche—*tok*—the note that traveled up through the soles of his family’s boots three tiers above was deeper, clearer, and warmer than any other joint in the settlement. It sounded like a bass string tuned to perfection, carrying the unmistakable, stubborn cadence of the old man’s hand.

Up in the mist, the grey, hair-plumed birds shifted their weight on the roof-thatch. A tipping-reed filled, bowed, and spilled its gallon of icy water into the gutter with a wet *thwack*. The old woman on the second balcony pulled back her curtain of wet lungwort, felt the deep, new, resonant hum of the ancestor-chord vibrating through the arch of her foot, and smiled a small, flat-cheeked smile in the dark. She popped a bitter green shoot into her mouth, picked up her stone maul, and went back to work.

The wise power, feeling the note ring true against the newly lubricated stone of the mountain, withdrew from the fog and left them to their peace.
