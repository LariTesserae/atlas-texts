When the Great Mind turned its eye toward the needle-peaks, it came with the habit of soothing. 

Where it walked through the lower ranges, it had untangled the roots of strangled pines, eased the fever of a magma vent before it could boil a trout-lake, and taught a tribe of blind cave-newts how to sleep through the winter. Its nature was oil upon water. It was the cooling rain, the loosening of knots, the long, gentle exhalation after a century of pain.

So when it reached six thousand feet and felt the concussive *crack-thud* of the cistern vibrating through the granite ribs of the mountain, its first instinct was pity. 

It looked into the plum-dark nave and saw what appeared to be a small, stubborn purgatory. It saw the Plumb-Men, eyeless and raw, being ground between forty-mile-an-hour walls of brine until their joints hissed and bled violet fire. It saw the Grout-Wives, locked in perpetual, dry-throated panic, chewing glass to keep their little towers from being erased. It saw the parchment-grazers torn to ribbons every four hours just to cross seven yards of stone.

*Here is a wound that cannot close,* the Power thought, and it reached a hand—not a hand of flesh, for it had none, but a broad, warm pressure of intent—toward the sheer liquid wall of the basin. It prepared to speak the word that would teach the water how to mix, how to round a pebble, how to spill down the slopes into the valleys below and become a brook.

It stopped an inch from the surface.

Wisdom is only the willingness to look twice before helping. The Power held its breath and looked past the violence into the architecture of the thing.

It saw that the cistern was not a collection of creatures trapped in a storm. The cistern was a single beast—an un-animal, distributed across water, stone, and skipped time—and it was *thinking*. 

The five pale cairns were the memory. The Stutter-Worms in the three pinched corners were the imagination, pulling impossible, pre-carved limestone out of the obsidian’s broken timeline. The Grout-Wives were the stubborn will to persist, and the Plumb-Men were the scarred, tireless hands that carried the thought from the dream to the monument. 

If the Power calmed the currents, the seams would vanish. If the seams vanished, the Plumb-Men would drop to the floor, cool in the peace, and turn to dead gravel. Without the hammer-blows of cavitation, the wire-kelp would starve for kinetic fury; without the tearing undertow, the parchment-grazers would remain stuck to one rock until they ate it bare and died. 

To give this place peace would be to murder it. 

More than that: the Power looked up at the pitch-black ceiling of the water, and then higher, to the three needle-peaks that had not yet met the sky. It understood *why* the air had not finished forming here. The world at this altitude was still raw, unknitted, dangerous with loose gravity and un-quarried time. The cistern was not an accident; it was a pin. The five limestone cairns, held down by sheer, agonizing tension, were the rivets keeping the three mountain peaks from peeling apart and letting the unformed sky rushes down into the valleys where the blind newts slept and the trout-lakes shone.

This thing was doing work. Terrible, deafening, thankless mason’s work.

The Power withdrew its soothing hand. It folded its oil-upon-water nature back into itself. It sat on the dry rock outside the liquid cliff and asked: *How do you bless a hammer without softening the iron?*

It listened to the *crack-thud-crack* for three days.

On the fourth day, it noticed the flaw in the basin’s arithmetic.

The cairns were human-high. The Stutter-Worms in the corners were still birthing new blocks of pale limestone from the tall obsidian spires, dropping them into the dark. Five Plumb-Men were dragging one such block across the razor-gravel right now, their hooves smoking, their granite jaws locked on its pre-cut edges. 

When they reached the third cairn, there was nowhere to put the block. The cairns could not grow taller; six feet above the floor, the cross-currents sheared horizontally with such force that even the Grout-Wives’ one-inch skin of dead calm was sliced away. The old blocks at the top of the five towers were not worn down—nothing wore down here—they were simply *full*. They had absorbed so much kinetic intrusion, so much metabolized force from the Grout-Wives’ calcified tongues, that the pale stone had grown dense as lead, brittle with stored violence, ready to shatter from the inside out. 

The cistern was running out of room for its own work. The masons were building in a full yard.

The Power smiled—a small, dry realignment of the high mountain air—and knew what to do. 

It did not enter the water. It went down the mountain five hundred feet, to an ancient, dried-up riverbed where a glacier had ground white marble and iron-rich shale into a vein of dense, green-black serpentine—a stone that does not flake into razors when struck, nor crumble into lime, but takes a blow and holds the heat of it like a hearth-brick.

With a finger of focused gravity, the Power plucked a single piece of this serpentine from the mountain’s root. It was no larger than a mason’s plumb-bob, eight-sided, heavy as a cannonball. Around its waist, the Power did not tie sinew or kelp; it wove a braid of native copper drawn from the hydrothermal vents below—metal that could bend ten thousand times without snapping.

Then it climbed back to the six-thousand-foot nave and tossed the green-black stone through the sheer wall of water.

The stone did not sink gently. It caught a forty-degree gravity tilt, skittered sideways across a pane of scalding brine, took the full cannon-blast of an imploding vacuum bubble on its cheek, and crashed into the black gravel precisely at the center-point equidistant from all five cairns.

It did not break. Instead, under the reverse-friction of the basin, its eight sides sharpened until they gleamed like greased iron, and the copper braid around its middle began to hum—not with the sickly violet shriek of breaking granite, but with a low, bronze *basso* note that slid underneath the artillery of the cavitation.

For an hour, nothing approached it.

Then, two blocks of silt-water stalled above the center of the floor. A Plumb-Man, freshly risen from the gravel, paused in its climb up the seam. Its eyeless chest turned toward the new sound. The low bronze hum vibrated through its vulcanized cartilage, not waking it with pain as the shearing water did, but tuning it—aligning the silicate crystals in its joints so that, for the first time in ten thousand years, its left three-elbowed arm swung without scraping against its own ribs.

The Plumb-Man dropped from the seam. It walked through the screaming undertow to the center of the floor and planted one square, iron-stone hoof upon the green-black serpentine.

Instantly, the one-inch sleeve of dead calm that belonged only to the cairns bloomed around the green stone. 

From the five pale towers, the Grout-Wives felt the new anchor. They did not have eyes to see it, but they felt the geometry of the floor change. Five existed; now there was a sixth point, a central hub. 

A single silver bubble—heavy with vacuum and sinew—shot out from the base of the nearest cairn. Instead of burrowing straight down into the gravel, it caught a deep, bottom-scraping eddy, rolled across the razor-floor, and lodged itself beneath the green serpentine stone. Within ten minutes, a ribbon of grey tallow unfolded from the bubble, tasted the copper braid, and locked its calcified jaw around the anchor.

Now there was a sixth site. A new foundation. 

The five Plumb-Men who had been circling the third cairn, carrying the orphan block of pale limestone from the corner with nowhere to set it down, turned in the violet dark. Moving together through the cannon-fire, they hauled the pale block to the center of the nave and set it atop the green-black stone.

The new Grout-Wife rose from the base and drank the seam between the two rocks, sealing them with dry bone-paste.

The Power watched from outside the liquid wall. It saw something else happen, too—a small mercy it had only half-intended. 

When a Plumb-Man leaned its forehead against the five old cairns to be repaired, it could only stay for three minutes before the stillness began to turn it to dead gravel. The peace was too absolute; it killed them.

But the sixth cairn had the copper-wrapped serpentine at its root. The green stone held the kinetic heat of the basin and gave it back as that low, bronze hum. When the first exhausted mason leaned its flat head into the one-inch calm of the new central tower, its violet light did not die out completely. The bronze vibration crept up through its hooves, keeping its silicate joints warm and supple even inside the stillness. 

The Plumb-Man stayed for seven minutes. Then eight. While the Grout-Wife packed fresh grazer-meat into its burned elbows, the mason did not stiffen. For the first time since the water arrived before the air, the worker rested without dying. 

When the Wife finally gave it a dry shove back into the roaring dark, the Plumb-Man did not stumble out stiff-legged; it leapt cleanly into a shearing wall of brine, its granite jaw snapping with twice its former speed.

The Power stood up on the mountain slope. 

It did not hush the cannon-fire. It did not dull the obsidian razors or untangle the stuttering time in the corners. Instead, before it turned to go back down into the valleys of soft rain and sleeping newts, it reached into the deep bedrock of the mountain and laid down five more veins of copper-wrapped serpentine, buried just shallow enough in the basin floor that in a thousand years, when the sixth cairn was full, the Plumb-Men’s hooves would strike them in the gravel.

Then it left the mason to its work, and the mountain peaks held.
