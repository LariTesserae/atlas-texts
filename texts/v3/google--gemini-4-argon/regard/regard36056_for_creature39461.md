A lesser benevolence would have looked down through the forty-foot ceiling of mist and felt pity. It would have parted the grey lid to let the yellow sun pour in, drying the slick basalt and blinding the boy with the huge, dark pupils. It would have quieted the deafening roar of the swifts so the people could remember how to sing, or raised a forest of dry pine so they could build a roaring hearth. And in doing so, it would have shattered the delicate, clockwork mercy of the trench, scattering the flocks, starving the mustelids, and turning the five badger-built people into blind, weeping refugees in their own home.

The power that lingered at the rim of the fault was wiser than that. It looked at the five people wedged in the limestone V, smelled their rich coat of tallow and ammonia, listened to the sharp, economical *clack* of their chert disks cutting through the ninety-decibel static, and recognized perfection. They were not broken humans waiting to be rescued; they were a new verse of humanity, written in the meter of wet stone and endless twilight. They were safe here. The world above—with its droughts, its winter blizzards, its tribal wars, and its fickle seasons—could not touch them.

Yet the wise power saw the two quiet shadows that hung over their survival. 

The first was the cold that settled into the marrow when they slept, forcing them to burn their own lives away just to keep their blood moving in the fifty-degree damp, and the slow, stealthy rot of lungs that breathed nothing but floating feather-down and damp guano-dust for forty years. 

The second was the number five. Five is a knife-edge. One slick patch of liverwort during the three-day climb to the rim, one shifting slab of limestone, one bad fever, and the verse would end. 

The power did not descend in a pillar of light. It moved down the vertical chisel-marks of the escarpment like a slow change in barometric pressure, completely invisible in the pewter noon. It went to the rock itself, deep beneath the crushed shale and the fallen barge-sized slabs, down to the fresh geological fault where the earth was still settling from its violent drop.

With a nudge no heavier than a sigh, it pinched a deep subcutaneous vein of magma against an aquifer three thousand feet below the eastern overhang. 

It did not make a geyser or a volcano. It calibrated the fracture with infinite care. 

Three days later, the older woman with the salt-colored hair was sitting at the limestone workshop, scraping fat from a martin-skin with a flake of black chert. Her bare, horn-calloused feet were tucked beneath her parka to keep them off the cold stone. 

Slowly, over the course of six hours, the dry limestone shelf beneath her thighs stopped stealing her heat. 

She paused, her thick-knuckled hands stilling on the hide. She pressed her flat palm against the grey rock. It was not hot—not enough to wake the sluggish lizards or disturb the temperature of the valley's air—but it held a steady, blood-warmth of a hundred and two degrees. Deep inside the eastern wall, a silent, sealed ribbon of geothermal water had begun to circulate behind the dry overhang, turning the sleeping crevices and the work-shelf into a permanent, gentle radiant hearth. 

When the boy and the others returned and piled into the deep dry cleft behind the shelf to sleep, they did not have to shiver in forty-minute fits. For the first time in three generations, their muscles unknotted completely. Their breathing slowed even further, dropping into a deep, restorative stillness as the stone cradle fed heat directly into their thick bones. Moreover, the gentle warmth rising from the rock shelf dried the air strictly within the overhang, causing the floating grey down and ammonia-mist to drift outward, away from the sleepers' lungs, leaving a pocket of crisp, clean air right where they laid their heads.

The power did not stop with the heat. In the seepage pools nearest the warm wall, where the water now sat at a tepid sixty-five degrees, a dormant spore awakened—or perhaps it was placed there, tucked into the mud between the willow roots. 

Within a month, a thick, pale-amber tuber began to swell around the submerged knuckles of the stunted willows. A rock-weasel dug the first one up, and the boy, watching from thirty feet away with his wide grey eyes, saw the animal gnawing it. He took the half-eaten root, smelled its clean, earthy starch, and brought it to the old woman. 

When pounded in the limestone mortar with rendered swift-fat, the root turned into a dense, buttery paste. It was rich in the hidden things the sunless valley lacked—the bright vitamins that keep gums from bleeding and bones from bowing too sharply in the dark, and a sharp, resinous oil that cut the feather-dust from their throats when swallowed. Furthermore, its fibrous skin, when chewed, acted like a soft sponge, polishing the abrasive shale-grit from their flattened teeth instead of grinding them down.

The third gift required a longer reach. 

Fourteen miles away, out on the blinding yellow grass of the upper plains, a small band of surface-nomads was being pushed south by a summer grass-fire and the spears of a larger clan. Among them were a brother and sister, twelve and fourteen years old, who had been born with the pale, sensitive eyes that surface-people call *moon-touched*—children who squinted in the noon glare and only moved with grace when twilight fell. In the chaos of the smoke, they were separated from their band.

The benevolent power did not speak to them in words. It simply laid a cool, damp draft of air across their faces, smelling faintly of wet stone and rich meat, drawing them toward the jagged lip of the four-mile fracture. It guided their hands and feet to the top of the three-day vertical fault-chimney, at the exact hour the five people from the basin had climbed halfway up to make their autumn trade.

The boy from the rift—wearing his slit-bark goggles against the sky-glare—heard the rattle of dislodged pebbles above him. He froze, pressed flat against the basalt like a grey moth. 

Looking up through the narrow wooden slit, he saw two small figures descending the chimney, weeping from the smoke and the sun, their hands bleeding on the sharp rock, moving with desperate, clumsy courage into the cool shadow of the fault. 

He did not reach for his fire-hardened throwing stick. He looked at the girl’s eyes as she dipped below the shadow-line of the rim—wide, pale-irised, drinking in the sudden gloom with obvious relief. 

The boy reached into his parka, pulled out two flat disks of black chert, and tapped them once, softly. *Clack.*

Below him in the chimney, the old woman looked up. 

The power withdrew then, pulling its attention back up through the forty-foot ceiling of grey mist, leaving the lid closed. It did not stay to watch the slow, silent integration of the two surface children—how their bleeding feet would eventually callous into grey horn, how they would learn to swallow raw breast-meat for its warmth, how they would bring new blood and a few new stone-tapped rhythms into the dry warmth of the eastern wall. 

In the basin, the millions of swifts kept banking through the pewter light. The water kept sweating down the dark basalt at the exact rate it evaporated. The roar of the shingle-beach never dropped its pitch. Nothing had been broken; nothing had been tamed. Only the stone beneath the sleepers was warm, and in the dark cleft at the base of the cliff, seven people breathed clean air in the shadowless noon.
