The attention does not arrive as a shaft of sunlight breaking the fog, because sunlight would blind the bacterial mats and dry the delicate gill-cowls of the three tenants. A lesser power might have looked upon this sunken cellar of rust and slime and felt pity, and out of that pity might have swept the gray wool from the sky, sweetened the water, and planted reeds and willows. And in doing so, it would have murdered the entire world.

This power is older than the preference for green things. It knows that a hearth does not need to be a fire; sometimes a hearth is a leaking brass valve at forty-five degrees, and joy can taste like copper sulfate and cold lard. 

For a long time—three full turns of the tide a mile away, while the three tenants complete a single rotation around the leaning manifold—the presence simply inhabits the fog. It listens to the three-four drip of the flanges and the *clink-scritch* of the foundry-nuts grinding in the dark gizzards. It admires the slow, leveraged devotion of the goethite radula scraping the brass clean. It recognizes the tenants for what they are: the obscure, patient saints of a dying machine, holding the frayed ends of their small universe together in their own pale flesh so the shrimp may eat and the worms may fish.

The power sees that the tenants are content, and so it resolves not to change their lives, only to secure their lease. For the power can see down through the rotten shale, where the tenants cannot.

Forty feet below the basin, in the dark, flooded galleries of the old pump-work, the ancient lead-acid battery banks—left behind in a bricked-up powerhouse a century ago, somehow still weeping their last chemical dregs through the armoured cables—are finally exhausted. The plates have sulfated to chalk; the last millivolt is weeks away from fading forever. And fifty yards to the seaward side, the main cast-iron intake pipe is pinched nearly shut by the shifting weight of the slump-clay. Within two winters, the brass relief valve will clack for the last time, the cables will go cold, the hydrogen fizz will stop, and the three pillars will stand in the dark waiting for a tick and a wheeze that will never come again.

So the power reaches down into the blind geology beneath the cellar. It does not use miracles; it uses the grammar of the place.

Deep in the shale, where the old copper return-lines vanish into the bedrock, the power nudges a fault line by the breadth of a fingernail. It brings a buried seam of dark, pyritic graphitic slate into direct, wet contact with a massive, subterranean body of native bog-iron, with the brine-soaked clay acting as the bridge between them. It builds an earth-battery the size of a cathedral, a natural galvanic cell that will take ten thousand years to discharge. The stray current in the frayed cables above does not surge—a surge would startle the worms and burn the throat of the blue-stained elder—it simply steadies. The *tic-tic-tic* deepens, gaining a faint, harmonic resonance, a guaranteed heartbeat that will outlast the cliffs.

Next, the power turns its mind to the choked intake pipe. It does not clear it all at once. Instead, it coaxes a slow, warm Finger of deep artesian water—rich with the lithium, iron, and sulfur the basin loves—to intercept the rusted iron main from below. And as that water rises, the power dissolves a massive, hidden pocket of ancient dolomite and scrap-iron into the stream, ensuring the broth will remain thick and rich for epochs. 

On the surface, nothing seems to have happened. The fog still hangs like wet wool at four o'clock. The scale-mollusks still *shuck-shuck* across the bolt-heads. 

Then, the brass relief valve on the leaning manifold lifts. 

*Clack.*

The quart of rust-red water that drools down the housing is two degrees warmer than it has been since the administration of William McKinley, and it smells gloriously, intoxicatingly of deep-earth iron and virgin sulfur. The valve settles back with its long, bronchial wheeze, and the wheeze is fuller, deeper—the sound of a lung that has unexpectedly found more room in the chest.

The tenant currently leaning against the manifold pauses. Its wide, puckered ventral slit drinks the broth. A slow, rippling shudder of pure, bureaucratic satisfaction travels up its lard-colored mantle, cracking a three-inch flake of yellow travertine from its shoulder, which falls with a *plop* into the tea-colored sluice.

Twelve feet away, in the central pool, the largest of the three tenants is holding the two rotted ends of a gutta-percha cable in the wet fold beneath its robin’s-egg blue throat. It has been holding this splice for nineteen days, its flesh growing numb and sour from the effort of bridging the dying current. 

Suddenly, the tick changes. The phantom electrolysis does not stop; instead, a tiny, flower-like nodule of native copper, drawn out of the enriched water by the newly steadied earth-current, begins to plate itself rapidly across the gap between the frayed wires. Within an hour, a solid, living bridge of dull red metal has grown straight through the conductive slime, welding the two ends together stronger than any solder. 

The elder tenant feels the current bypass its tired flesh and run clean and strong through the new copper bridge. A cloud of microscopic hydrogen bubbles blooms upward, tickling its overlapping cowls. 

Slowly—taking forty minutes to make up its mind, and another twenty to unpeel its pedal-disc from the silt—the great, stone-crusted barrel backs away from the mended wire. It does not know it has been relieved of duty; it only knows the wire is whole, the pool is fizzing, and its throat is free.

Before the presence withdraws from the rim of the slump-clay embankment, it leaves one small, parting gift in the mud beside the donkey-engine’s firebox, half-buried where the oil-crabs scuttle. It is a single, six-inch hexagonal nut of heavy, high-phosphorus foundry iron, dropped as if from a workman's pocket ninety years ago, unrusted and dense as a meteor. 

In three days, the elder tenant will slide over it, draw it up into its mantle, and swallow it down into its gizzard to replace the old stones that have worn smooth. And for the next century, as the three of them tend their pipes in the grey dark, the *clink-scritch* of their digestion will have a clear, resonant, bell-like note—a tiny, hidden music in the wet hollow, answering the clack of the brass and the tick of the wire.
