A lesser kindness would have brought the sun. It would have cracked the brick vaults, drained the peat-heavy water into the lower chalk, and invited the quick, frantic air of the surface to scrub the iron clean. It would have looked at the blind eyes and the four-hour pistons and diagnosed a tragedy of stagnation.

The mind that turned its attention fifty feet down through the London clay—or whatever nameless, damp metropolis had forgotten this basement—was older than that, and vastly more polite. It knew that salvation is not one-size-fits-all. It recognized an abbey when it saw one, and it knew better than to shout in the nave.

For a long time—three strokes of the vertical pumps, which is to say half a day—the Power only listened. It sat in the limestone strata beneath the floor, feeling the slow, hydraulic *thud* of the four violet-brown hearts above it, tasting the patient housekeeping of the heavy hands on the brass wheels. It saw how the machinery and the flesh had forgiven one another for their origins, merging into a single, velvet organ that kept the cold away.

Yet, because it was wise, it also saw the long arithmetic of the rock.

The cistern-ward was an equilibrium, yes, but it was a mortal one. No gasket weeps forever without emptying its vessel; no galvanic battery turns over for centuries without its anodes eventually dissolving into harmless mud. Two hundred years hence, perhaps three, a main cast-iron header under the silt floor would finally thin to paper and split. Or the three-inch lens of air at the masonry vaults would lose its last molecule of free oxygen to the rust, and the next Sump-Clerk to climb the four tiers would draw in a mouthful of pure, dead nitrogen, and sink back down into the bladder-ferns a little heavier, and a little colder, than before.

And there were only four of them. They were very old. Their cartilaginous bones had forgotten the brisk chemistry required to make a fifth.

So the Power decided to act—not as a flood, or a miracle, or an awakening, but as a maintenance crew of one, working at the exact speed of the house.

It began below, in the blind dark of the limestone where the geothermal pipes drew their heat. It did not crank the heat up; a sudden rise of five degrees would have scalded the bladder-ferns and thrown the Clerks into a frantic, starving metabolism. Instead, it adjusted the deep faults by the width of a hair, diverting a tiny, magmatic seep of iron-sulfur brine toward the intake casings. It was a lease extension, written in stone: the water would remain the temperature of blood for another ten thousand winters.

Next, it looked at the iron and the brass.

Where the three-inch inspection ports were thinning under the acid bite of the interior vats, the Power nudged the chemistry of the encrusting bryozoans. They began to secrete a silica matrix on the inside of the glass—clear enough to let the tallowy amber light through, tough enough to hold the pressure when the old glass finally crazed. Along the bundled power cables and inside the dynamo casings, it introduced a silent, lithotrophic bacterium that fed on the encroaching silt and excreted pure, elemental copper directly onto the rotting bus-bars. The anodes would no longer waste away; they would grow back at the exact rate they corroded, locked in a permanent, self-healing circuit.

Then, it looked to the ceiling.

In the corner of the easternmost vault, where the old intake culvert had been bricked over a century ago by the ditch-reeves, the mortar between two courses of masonry softened. It did not crumble; it became porous, developing a microscopic, lung-like lattice through fifty feet of clay and foundation stone up to the roots of an old courtyard plane tree on the surface. It was not a hole. No surface beetle could crawl down it; no shaft of daylight could ever pierce it. It was simply an osmotic gill in the brickwork. As the barometric pressure rose and fell in the world above, the vault itself now breathed—exhaling the sour marsh-damp and spent nitrogen, drawing down a slow, humid trickle of sweet oxygen to keep the three-inch lens of mercury-air forever fresh.

These were the repairs to the house. There remained the tenants.

On a Tuesday—though there had not been a Tuesday in the cistern-ward since the surface sovereign was a girl—the oldest of the four Clerks was sleeping wrapped around the belly of the number-two condenser vat. Its broad shovel-head was tucked into its chest; a pale creeper had stitched its left hind foot lightly to a flange.

Fourteen feet away, on a dead-end spur of cast-iron pipe that had held nothing but cold silt since the borough was drowned, a heavy brass inspection plate began to warm.

The Clerk’s eyes did not open, of course, because there was nothing to see. But the golden cilia inside the deep pit-pores along its flank stirred in the water.

A new current had entered the nave.

It was not the sharp, thin whine of a stuck relief valve, nor the broad, flat hum of the dynamos. It was a low, complex chord—warm, greasy with dissolved zinc and sweet iron, pulsing at the exact cadence of a Clerk’s own two-minute heartbeat. *Thud. Thud.* Like another Clerk, larger and deeper in the ironwork, humming to itself in the dark.

Over the course of an afternoon, the four great mottled lumps detached themselves from their manifolds. Hand over slow, calloused hand, hooking their fingers into bolt-holes and knurled rims, they poured their boneless hips through the narrow alleys of blind-kelp. When two met at the base of the condenser stair, one rolled onto its pale, doughy back and let its brother slide over its belly like wet chamois, neither hurrying, both drawn by their chins toward the dead-end spur.

When they arrived, they found that the heavy iron cap at the end of the four-foot main had rusted away entirely—the only piece of metal in the ward allowed to fail—and in its place had grown a thick, rubbery diaphragm of mineralized leather, weeping a steady, velvet plume of warm, purple-brown liquor that smelled gloriously of bruised watercress and clean electricity.

The oldest Clerk reached out a dinner-plate hand. It laid its horned palm against the diaphragm.

Inside the pipe, suspended in the rich, galvanic amniotic broth of the reactivated main, something small and heavy shifted. It was the size of a bolster pillow, boneless and blind, with four stubby, knuckle-heavy paddles and a tiny, shovel-flat snout. As the old Clerk’s hand pressed the leather, the creature inside pressed back, and its newly formed gill-cowls fluttered, sending a tiny, fledgling spark of amperage through the diaphragm and right into the old Clerk’s sensory pits.

There were three more pipes just like it on the lower tier, warming in the gloom. They would take twenty years to ripen, which in the cistern-ward was a very comfortable gestation.

The four old Clerks did not dance, or vocalize, or trouble the water. They simply arranged themselves around the four-foot main like boulders around a hearth. They tucked their chins against the warm iron, opened their pleated purple gill-slits, and let the new chord wash through their marrow. Overhead, a brass valve reached its twenty-minute threshold and let go a single, silver globe of gas. It wobbled up through the rust-colored ferns, bumped the grating with a muffled *thrum*, and joined the sweet, newly breathable dark at the roof of the world.

The Power watched for one more stroke of the great pistons—four hours of amber-green hush—to be sure the current held. Then, leaving not so much as a ripple in the vallisneria, it withdrew, content that the abbey had everything it needed to pray in its own fashion until the sun grew cold.
