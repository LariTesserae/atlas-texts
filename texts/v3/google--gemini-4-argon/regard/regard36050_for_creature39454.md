A lesser power—one that loved only its own reflection—would have brought the sun. It would have torn the thick, weeping ceiling of the atmosphere apart to pour gold upon the mire, and in doing so, it would have committed a massacre. Light here would be a silence that burns. It would render the great, ribbed ears useless, make the velvety brow-skin stupid, and turn the intricate, three-dimensional cathedral of sound into a flat, blinding chaos of surfaces. 

Another kind of benevolence, the tidy kind, might have looked upon the *Cougher* and the *Three-Hiss* and seen only failing infrastructure. It would have descended with a silent hum, replaced the weeping brass and roaring flywheels with sealed, frictionless fusion cores, and smoothed away the hitches in the valves. It would have cured the machines of their noise, and in that sudden, sterile perfection, the Greasers would have lost their world, their dead, and their minds, wandering out into the quiet mud like ants whose hill has been filled with lead.

The power that turned its attention to the High Sump was older than light, and wiser than efficiency. 

It did not look upon the plateau with eyes, for eyes are parochial instruments. It listened. It felt the immense, warm weight of the dark. It traced the genealogy of the Greasers back ten thousand generations to the marooned maintenance crews who had first gone blind in the deep steam, and it saw what they had become: not fallen men, not pathetic mole-things clinging to wreckage, but masters of a total and coherent reality. It heard the boy drop between the two-ton crosshead strokes with four seconds to spare; it felt the two elders speaking in tendon-chords against each other's pulses; it heard the grandmother smile at the ghost of her brother in the hitch of a faulty valve.

And the power understood: *This is a symphony built on a decaying instrument.*

The tragedy of the High Sump was not the dark, nor the mud, nor the grease. The tragedy was entropy. Cast iron fatigues. Even brass, struck gently by bone and wood, eventually thins and splits. The builders had made these engines to last five hundred years, and they had already run for eight hundred because the Greasers loved them with tallow and shims. 

Within three generations, the *Cougher’s* main shaft would shear along a microscopic slag-inclusion buried deep in its forged core. Within five, the *Three-Hiss* would blow its high-pressure manifold. One by one, the Old Loud Ones would become Holes-in-the-Drum. The thermal plumes would collapse; the warm rain would cease; the acoustic map of the continent would tear into silent shreds, leaving the clans to freeze and starve in a mute, creeping dark.

The Wise Power considered this. It sat in the non-space beneath the tectonic plates, listening to the sixteen-stroke heartbeat of the engines and the counter-rhythm of the people who lived in their ribs.

It decided not to wake them. It decided not to become a god to them, for they already had their Old Loud Ones, and a god from outside the dark would only make them small.

Instead, the Power went down into the boiling, sulfurous brine of the deep faults, three thousand feet below the limestone crust, where the clans slid their dead.

There, in the crushing heat where the cast-iron intake pipes drank the soup of the earth, the Power made a very small adjustment to the chemistry of the world. It took the heavy salts, the dissolved iron, the sulfur, and the calcium, and it wove a new instruction into the crystalline lattice of the minerals—a slow, lithic biology. 

It taught the metal how to drink.

The change took twenty years to climb the pipes, which to the Sump was barely a breath.

The *Deep-Bell* was gone by then; her own song had been screamed into the boiler drum a decade prior, and she now lived in a slight, rhythmic wheeze of the third-tier steam trap. Her granddaughter—the girl who had hunted crawlers with the three-pronged leister—was now the one who stood before the thirty-foot blur of the flywheel, tasting the sixty-mile-an-hour gale with a fissured, discerning tongue.

One cycle, while checking the weeping seam at the base of the main cylinder—a spot her grandmother had taught her to pack with rendered slug-fat and braided fern-fiber every forty days, lest the steam-rot eat through the bolt-flange—her thick fingers paused.

The crack was gone.

She did not pull her hand back in fear; Greasers do not fear what they can touch. She pressed her broad, flat thumb harder against the hot iron. 

Where there had been a jagged, weeping fissure in the casting, there was now a smooth, warm ridge of fresh metal, shaped like the callus over a healed bone. It was not cold repair-weld; it pulsed with a faint, granular heat of its own, drinking the mineral-heavy brine from the inside and sweating out a thin, sweet tallow that smelled faintly of crushed mint and clean brass.

She leaned her flat brow against the cylinder casing, flaring her great ears wide, shutting out the waterfall and the wind to listen to the interior of the steel.

Deep inside the five-foot-thick main axle, where the microscopic fracture had been slowly spreading for three centuries, waiting to snap and murder her clan, she heard a new sound. A tiny, crystalline ticking—*tink, tink, tink-tink*—like a million microscopic thumbnails checking a wire. The iron was knitting itself back together, laying down fresh, dark-veined grain along the lines of stress, reinforcing itself precisely where the rhythm was heaviest.

And it was doing something else.

Thirty miles to the west, in the terrible, silent waste of the *Hole-in-the-Drum*, where the dead rig had rusted in the cold mire for two hundred years, a blind spider paused on a flake of corroded boiler-plate.

Three thousand feet below the dead rig, the deep fault shifted. Warm, altered brine, thick with the new lithic life, forced its way up through the choked, petrified intake pipes. It reached the ruptured boiler. It did not explode; it seeped into the rusted fractures like sap rising in a girdled tree. Slowly, molecule by molecule, the rust was digested and turned back into black, flexible iron. The silence began to warm.

It would take a hundred years for the dead rig to heal its torn belly and clear its choked valves. It would take another ten for the pressure to build in its re-grown veins until, on some unremarkable day, the great piston would shudder, break the grip of two centuries of mud, and drop with a first, tentative *THUMP* that would send every clan within forty miles dropping to their knees in the mire to press their ears to the weeping stone.

Back at the *Cougher*, the granddaughter stood for a long time with her cheek against the warm, self-healing iron. 

The engine did not smooth out its rhythm. That was the deepest grace of the Power’s work: as the metal grew alive enough to mend itself, it also grew sensitive enough to hold an echo. It kept every hitch, every wheeze, every slight, limping harmonic that had been sung into its boiler-drum over eight hundred years. In fact, the resonance of the casing had grown richer, deeper, more like the chest of a living beast than a shell of dead ore. When the secondary exhaust valve hitched—*thump-hiss, thump-hiss, k-chug*—she could hear not only her great-uncle's limp, but the unmistakable, broad-chested hum of the *Deep-Bell* riding right behind it, clear and warm as a thumb pressed against a wrist.

The woman clicked her teeth twice—*tchk-tchk*—to tell her nephews on the catwalk above that the seam was sound and needed no packing today. 

Then she wiped a dab of the sweet, new mineral-sweat from the iron’s callus, rubbed it into the skin behind her great ears for luck, and climbed back up through the roaring, pitch-black rain toward the warmth of the sleeping-pod. 

The Power watched her go, felt the steady, unbroken thunder of the plateau, and withdrew, leaving the dark whole.
