A lesser power—one that confunds love with improvement, or mercy with ease—would look into the amber gloom of the Sink and see a tragedy of privation. 

Such a power would pity the cracked, clay-plated skin of the people. It would ache for the parched, fissured tongues combing the dust for the ghost of a drink. Out of the goodness of its heart, it would crack the limestone floor and call up an artesian spring. It would part the ochre ceiling of silt to let the golden sun pour in, and send a gentle, sweet rain to wash the centuries of gypsum from the leaves.

And in forty-eight hours, the city would be a rotting grave.

The sudden water would burst the cells of the slow-growing succulents; their roots, built to sip vapor, would drown and turn to black slime. The ancient, mortarless mud-brick mounds, stable for ten thousand years in the bone-dry cellar air, would drink the rain, swell, and slump into suffocating landslides of wet clay. The delicate, dry drifts of shed insect shells and chalky waste would ferment into a toxic mire, breeding molds and bacteria against which the inhabitants have no immunity. The great, metallic roar of the cicadas would choke out into silence, and the people, their internal condensers flooded, their skin stripped of its protective crust, would die shivering in the mud of a paradise they were never meant to inhabit.

The power that has actually turned its gaze upon the Sink knows all of this. It is old enough to understand that survival, carried on long enough and with enough grace, becomes a form of sacred architecture. It recognizes that the Sink is not a prison, and its people are not starving. They have achieved the rarest thing in the universe: a closed circle. A truce with entropy so profound that time itself has lost its teeth here.

So the power does not bring rain. It does not bring fire, or iron, or speed.

Instead, it looks downward—through the slumped terraces, through the compacted talc of the plazas, through the deep, dry rubble of the initial collapse—until its awareness rests in the dark, stressed geology beneath the basin.

A mile below the city, where the roots of the limestone plateau press down into the deeper crust, there is a flaw. The Sink was born of a collapse ages ago, and the rock has not quite finished settling. A massive, subterranean shelf of dolomite is locked against a fault line, accumulating strain over epochs. In four hundred years, or perhaps six hundred, that shelf will finally shear. The resulting tremor will not be large by the standards of the surface world, but here, it will be catastrophic. It will shatter the hanging rim, dumping a billion tons of surface desert into the bowl, and its sharp, high-frequency shockwave will liquefy the dry dust of the plazas, shaking the ancient, unmortared mounds into flat rubble and crushing the people before they can even uncurl from their crouch.

The power reaches into that dark, stressed stone. It does not weld the fault shut, for the earth must move. Instead, it becomes a lubricant. 

Over the course of a century, it changes the crystalline structure of the rock along the fault face, turning brittle, locked limestone into a microscopic seam of slick, soapy talc. Instead of holding the strain for a single, city-killing snap, the shelf begins to slide—imperceptibly, at the speed of fingernails growing, a millimeter a decade. The catastrophe is bled off into a gentle, harmless creep.

As the deep rock slides, it generates a vibration. 

The power shapes this vibration with the care of a master luthier. It tunes the friction of the deep earth so that it rises up through the bedrock ribs of the Sink not as a rumble, and not as a threat, but as a single, continuous, sub-audible note—a warm, resonant hum in the rock that sits three octaves below the metallic roar of the cicadas. 

It is a bass note for the city’s song. A carrier wave.

Up in the amber twilight, an old woman sits on the third terrace of a central mound, her spine pressed against the soap-smooth sandstone, her knees drawn to her chest. Two miles away, across the wide, dusty sea of the plazas, a young man kneels by an agave, the heel of his dark dolostone wedge resting against an exposed foundation stone.

He taps. *Thock. Pause. Thock-thock.*

Before, the stone of the city was dry and dead; it swallowed half the force of his strike, and the message arrived at the far rim faint and muddy, like a voice heard through a thick wall. 

Now, the stone is awake. The deep, slow sliding of the fault beneath them has put the entire basin under a gentle, harmonic tension, like the skin of a tuned drum. When the young man’s wedge strikes the foundation, the deep note of the earth catches his tap and carries it. The stone rings—not in the air, never in the air, where the insects own the world—but in the marrow. 

Two miles away, the old woman feels the tap arrive in her lower vertebrae with a sudden, breathtaking clarity. Every nuance of the strike is there: the exact weight of his wrist, the angle of the dolostone, the slight hesitation before the second beat that gives the word its personality, its humor, its warmth. The whole three-mile bowl of the city has become a single, lossless sounding board. A whisper tapped with a fingernail on the northern rim can now be felt in the soles of every foot on the southern terraces.

And the power does one thing more.

It looks at the high, bruised ring of the rim, three miles wide, where the surface desert presses against the sky. The wind up there is scouring a low saddle in the eastern cliff. Left alone, in a thousand years, that saddle would wear down just enough to let a massive dune of sterile, heavy quartz sand spill over the lip, pouring into the Sink like a slow avalanche and burying the eastern groves.

The power nudges a single boulder at the top of that eastern cliff—shifting it two inches to the left. 

Over the next fifty years, the desert wind catches behind that boulder, swirling, dropping its load of heavy sand *before* the rim, building a natural berm that armors the weak spot in the cliff. And as a consequence of that new, slight curve in the stone, the twelve-hour thermal tide—the invisible waterfall of cool air that rolls down into the basin twice a day—is subtly reshaped. 

The draft no longer tumbles down the cliffs in ragged, uneven eddies that leave some corners of the city parched and others dusted too thickly. The new lip of the rim curls the falling air into a wide, laminar, spiral current. 

When the people of the Sink climb the worn steps of their mounds at twilight and tilt their broad, clay-crusted faces to the sky, opening their mouths to the dusk, the air that washes over their tongues is a fraction of a degree cooler. It moves in a slow, majestic, clockwise wheel that touches every grove, every cushion of moss, and every waiting mouth with equal, unfailing measure, carrying just three percent more moisture stripped from the high night air than it did the century before.

No one in the Sink knows that a god has passed by. They have no word for *god*, just as they have no word for *rain*. 

They only know that the stone beneath them has grown wonderfully, richly clear to the touch, and that the evening wind tastes sweet. In the amber gloom, a hundred people lower their mouths from the fading draft, drop one heavy hand to the warm masonry beneath them, and begin to tap out a poem that will take until winter to finish—and the deep earth holds the rhythm for them, steady and silent, all the way to the bottom of the world.
