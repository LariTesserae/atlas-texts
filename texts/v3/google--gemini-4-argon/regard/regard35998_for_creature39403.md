A power that is merely benevolent would look into the glare of the teacup and weep. It would see the milky, salt-burned eyes, the walnut-knuckled hands, the children boiling their uncles for tallow, and it would reach down to save them. It would calm the seizing plumbing of the mountain; it would sweeten the lithium-brine into clear spring water; it would melt the snows and raise green meadows of sweetgrass around the rim, inviting the builders out into a gentle, ringing sun.

And within a week, the builders would all be dead. 

Without the heavy, merciful blanket of the lithium in their blood, the sudden onset of full human terror and grief would shatter their minds like cold slate. They would look at the bone levers in their hands and realize whose shins they were holding, and they would go mad. Without the constant, violent gymnastics of the shifting vents, the town’s stagnant edges would freeze solid, and the pig-sized gill-beasts would die in the sweet water, and the midges would vanish, and the fat white birds would starve. In the new, terrifying quiet of an acoustic valley where sound actually travels, the howling of their own voices would drive them to claw their eardrums out.

The power watching from the dark-blue sky above the cirque is not merely benevolent. It is also wise. 

It understands that love is not the erasure of a creature’s nature to suit the creator’s comfort. Love is the recognition of what a thing *is*, and how fiercely it has fought to be so. The builders are not broken humans waiting to be fixed; they are a masterpiece of stubbornness, a poem written in bone, grease, and mercury-calm against the blank page of the high peaks.

So the power does not break the teacup. Instead, it reaches down into the blind, roaring root of the mountain—three miles beneath the slush-boils, where the fault lines grind together and the subterranean brine-lakes cook over magma plumes—and it makes three small adjustments.

First, it addresses the "heavy lung." 

Deep in the porous limestone beds through which the thermal waters rise, the power alters the geometry of a single, massive cavern packed with native sulfur and porous basalt. As the superheated brine surges upward through this new filter, the heaviest, sharpest alkali precipitates out, binding to the deep rock instead of riding the steam into the sky. The lithium remains—the folk will keep their deep, mercury stillness, their armor against despair—and the salt remains to keep the water liquid in the sub-zero glare. But when the mist bursts from the cliff-seams now, the microscopic droplets are slightly larger, slightly heavier, and chemically blunted. They still coat the catwalks in rime; they still sting the skin. Yet when inhaled, they no longer crystallize in the warm, dark wet of the alveoli. 

No one in the basin notices the day the air changes. Only, over the span of five years, the rhythm of the town shifts. The elders do not begin wheezing at thirty-five. They live to fifty, to sixty—their bowed legs still strong, their pinhole eyes still sharp enough to read the water. For the first time in the history of the cirque, there is a surplus of memory. An old woman who has seen the vents shift ten thousand times can stand on a central buttress and feel, through the soles of her boots, the deep, premonitory shudder in the rock that means the North Boil is about to choke off—*before* it happens. She taps her stone hammer against the mammoth-rib: *three-short, drag, two-long*. The young men pivot and move the rookeries twenty minutes before the ice calves. Nobody gets crushed. They do not need to render their uncles this winter.

Second, the power looks at the bone-starved walls of the town. The ancient mammoth-graveyards in the high passes are nearly picked clean, and every excursion into that roaring, open sky is a torture for the builders. 

So the power nudges the metabolism of the black, rubbery lichen that clings to the splash-zones of the stone troughs. 

Over a dozen seasons, a new strain takes hold where the brine-spray is thickest. It does not grow leaves or flowers, which the wind would only tear away. Instead, it begins to greedily drink the dissolved calcium, magnesium, and silica from the mountain-broth, weaving those minerals into its own root-mat. When a builder pries a four-year-old blister of this new lichen off a sluice-gate and leaves it on a cold ledge for two days to dry in the hyper-arid wind, the plant does not shrivel. It cures into a black, fibrous horn—lighter than slate, resilient as whalebone, and capable of being split with a stone wedge into six-foot beams or thin, flexible shingles. 

The town no longer needs the outside. The builders stop climbing to the rim. They stay in their warm, crowded, blinding pit, harvesting their building struts right off the wet walls of their own aqueducts. 

The third gift is the smallest, and the only one that touches their souls.

The builders have no time for art, and no use for beauty that must be looked at, for the glare ruins all sight. Their world is entirely tactile and vibrational. They know each other by the *thrum* in the soles of their feet and the *knock* of a chin against a collarbone. 

Up near the lip of the cirque, where an ancient, dry fissure runs down through the heart of the central stone buttress—the main spine to which half the town’s scaffolding is lashed—the power hollows out a hidden chamber inside the solid cliff. It shapes the interior of this stone lung with the precision of a luthier, carving resonance chambers and acoustic baffles into the living granite, and filling the bottom of the cavity with a pool of dense, warm, heavy brine that never vents to the surface, but sloshes in a slow, perpetual tide driven by the mountain's deep thermal heartbeat.

The first time the wind hits the new angle of the upper fissure while the deep pool is at high tide, a vibration wakes inside the rock.

It is not a sound in the air—the heavy, mist-choked atmosphere of the basin still swallows noise. It is a sub-audible hum, a deep, velvet *purr* at forty cycles per second that travels straight down through the solid granite of the buttress, out into the lashed mammoth-ribs, along the cured rawhide thongs, and up through the greased felt boots of every builder standing on the web. 

All across the glaring, frantic amphitheater, four hundred stocky, grease-sheathed figures pause for three seconds. 

Down in their chests, beneath the giant bellows of their lungs, their slow, fifty-beat-a-minute hearts sync to the vibration in the wood and bone beneath their feet. The lithium in their blood keeps them from weeping or falling to their knees in awe; they do not invent a god to explain it. 

Instead, they feel the deep, rhythmic pulse in the scaffolding—steady, warm, and unbroken—and they understand it the only way they know how: as a weight and a cadence. It is the signature tap of the mountain itself, coming up through the soles of their boots. *I am here,* the stone says to their feet, beneath the silent crashing of the slush and the silent swarming of the birds. *I am holding the other end of the lever.*

A builder on the third tier blinks his yellowish membrane across his pinhole eyes, leans his shoulder into the chest of his brother, and knocks his horn-sheathed chin twice against the man's collarbone. *The foundation is good.* 

Then they turn back to the sluice-gate, raise their grandfather’s shinbones, and heave the slate into place, their feet warm against the humming bone.
