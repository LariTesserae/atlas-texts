A lesser power, looking down through the quartz lens, would have pitied her. 

A lesser power would have seen the bone-dry parsimony of her existence—the counted swallows, the three-minute wait for a single drop of water, the careful, aching slowness—and called it poverty. It would have struck the western wall and brought forth a gushing cold spring. It would have thickened the ceiling to give her shade, or rained sweet manna across the cream-colored travertine.

And in doing so, it would have murdered her entire world by noon the next day. 

A spring would drown the runnels, rotting the roots of the glass-wort and washing the microscopic algae from the walls. The change in humidity would suffocate the spindly crabs and bring surface-molds blooming down the badger-tube to choke the giant snails. Worst of all, an easy abundance would untether the cistern-keeper from the fierce, delicate discipline that kept her kind alive.

The power that actually paused above the scrub-plain was neither clumsy nor sentimental. It was old enough to understand the grammar of limestone and the liturgy of thirst.

It looked through the paper-thin dome of gypsum and quartz, and it did not feel pity; it felt a profound, quiet delight. 

It watched the small, clay-colored creature unfold from her shell-cloak. It noted the way she left the third of an inch of water in the flint nodule so the stone would not crack. It heard the five-minute pause between the strikes of the river-cobble against the bone wedge—*tink... tink*—as she listened with her great, ribbed ears to the tension in the ceiling. It saw her tap the snail’s shell in its own language, taking half the mucus ribbon and leaving the rest, before placing her tools three inches apart in the dust.

Here was a mind that did not war with its boundary, nor beg to be delivered from it, but instead read the harsh text of its world with absolute, loving literacy. She was not suffering; she was conducting an orchestra of stone and vapor at a tempo so slow only the geology could hear the music.

To intrude with a miracle would be an insult to her craft. 

Yet, the power was benevolent, and it had seen what the cistern-keeper could not. 

It looked above the dome, to the scrub-plain where the wind scoured the translucent roof. The quartz lens was already paper-thin. In twelve winters—perhaps fifteen—a violent seasonal hail-squall from the northern mountains would strike the precise center of the span, where a hairline fault in the gypsum was already beginning to fatigue. The dome would shatter. The red surface dust and the freezing night air would pour in, and the glowing white world would become just another dead sinkhole in the waste.

And looking down at the western wall, the power saw that the bone wedge was worn down to a nub, splintering at the crown. And seven miles away, in the dark-rock crevice, the mother’s teeth were wearing flat from chewing the tough rinds of the wire-fern, and the brother’s flint-knapping nodules were running full of chalky inclusions that ruined the edge of his scrapers.

So the wise power waited until the violet dusk had faded into night, and the cistern-keeper had climbed the wall, slipped past the seven guardian snails, and vanished up the corkscrew tube.

Then, inside the empty, faintly glowing vault, the power went to work. It did not work like a god; it worked like time.

First, it addressed the roof. It did not thicken the whole dome, which would have dimmed the light that fed the endolithic algae inside the walls. Instead, it reached into the surrounding soil on the surface above and drew together the scattered, wind-blown grains of pure silica and iron-rich desert varnish. Gently, over the course of four hours, it fused them into the hairline fault at the apex of the dome, growing a subtle, ribbing arch of milky chalcedony across the weak span—from the outside. To the creatures below, the light tomorrow would be dimmed by less than half a percent, a shadowless warmth that would now hold up the sky for another four thousand years.

Next, it turned its attention to the deep rock behind the western wall, forty feet back in the dark where the cistern-keeper’s bone wedge was slowly coaxing the moisture. The water here came from a blind pocket of ancient porous sandstone, and that pocket was slowly silting up with calcified mud. In a century, the wall would have gone dry. 

The power did not burst the rock. It simply coaxed a single, deep-earth crystal of selenite to dissolve along a hidden joint, opening a microscopic capillary to a deeper, sweeter aquifer below. 

The wall would not gush. The cistern-keeper would not come back tomorrow to a terrifying flood. Instead, the three-minute drop of water hanging from the carved stone lip would, starting tomorrow, fall every two minutes and forty seconds. And over the next ten years, as she extended her hand-pecked trough inch by inch with her grandmother’s cobble, she would find the stone along that specific vector surprisingly cooperative—yielding just a little more moisture than she hoped, rewarding her labor without ever making it obsolete.

Finally, the power looked at the tools lying beside the flint bowl. 

It would not forge her a blade of steel, nor leave a terrifyingly foreign artifact in her immaculate room. Instead, it followed her scent up the dissolution tube and across the freezing scrub-plain toward the dark-rock crevice where the three of them slept curled together Like warm roots. 

Halfway along the trail she used every third day, where a dry wash cut through an ancient gravel bed, the power shifted two yards of sand with a midnight gust of wind. 

Exposed in the moonlight, waiting to be found by a sharp silver-slitted eye on her next trip, were two things that belonged entirely to her world, yet were rarer than diamonds: the fossilized femur of an extinct desert mastodon, dense as ironwood and mineralized to a pale, creamy ivory that would make six generation’s worth of unbreakable wedges; and beside it, a twenty-pound river-nodule of flawless, buttery-black hornstone, entirely free of chalk flaws, waiting to be flaked into the sharpest scrapers her brother had ever held.

She would find them. She would test them with her horny palms, listen to their ring with her great ears, and carry them home in triumph. She would believe—rightly—that it was her own sharp eyes and her family’s deep knowledge of the wash that had won them the prize.

Back in the great white vault, the power lingered for one last moment before withdrawing from the world. 

It watched the single drop of water swell at the lip of the trough. It watched it gather the faint, ghostly starlight filtering through the newly strengthened dome. The drop grew fat, trembled, and fell into the flint basin.

*Plock.*

Two minutes and forty seconds later, it fell again. 

In the corner, the neat stack of shells sat undisturbed. On the wall, a platter-sized snail shifted a millimeter to the left, grazing on the stone. Everything was in its place. The dignity of the keeper was intact, her silence unviolated, her craft honored. The power smiled, and left her to her work.
