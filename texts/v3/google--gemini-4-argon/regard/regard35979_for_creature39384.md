A lesser benevolence would have looked upon Oakhaven-in-the-Crag with pity. It would have seen the freezing grease-mist, the two-inch iron soles, the agonizing slowness of the central tenement, and the sheer, bone-grinding labor required just to remain solid, and it would have tried to *fix* it. It would have parted the wet-wool sky with a shaft of golden, valley-warming sunlight; it would have snapped the hanging granite slabs instantly into their beds, synchronized the clocks, smoothed the razor-edged basalt into soft, loamy hills, and relieved the citizens of their pocket-lead.

And in doing so, it would have destroyed them. 

For a sudden summer would have boiled the cold-adapted lichens from their coats and choked the great, barrel-chested lungs built for thin ozone. Instantaneous reality would have shattered a blue-collar metaphysics forged over three generations of patient, heroic stubbornness. To take the weight from an Oakhavener’s pockets would be to tell them that the ground had never needed their help to stay down.

The power that turned its attention to the Crag was wiser than that. It listened for a long time to the four-second drop of the counterweights, the dry *clack* of the ironwood paddles, and the glass-rubbing hum of the unformed rim. It saw a people who had woken up in the middle of an unfinished sentence and, without complaint or panic, had simply picked up a wrench and begun hammering out the punctuation themselves. 

It loved them for their literalness. It loved the way they did not pray to the fog, but drove cold-forged spikes into it. It saw that they did not need to be rescued from their work; they needed their work to be *answered*.

So the wise power did not come as a light in the sky, or a voice in the chasms—knowing full well what a loud voice does against unweathered obsidian. Instead, it came by rail.

***

On a Tuesday morning that smelled no different from any other—of wet wool, coal-tar, and crushed quillwort—the Gage-Men patrolling the eastern sector heard a sound that made them stop dead on the glassy basalt, their four-horned goats halting beside them with ears pricked forward.

Out on the unfinished viaduct, where the double-track railway stretched like pulled taffy into a smear of chromatic aberration, the rails were singing. 

It was not the usual low, teeth-setting hum of the fray. It was the deep, unmistakable, three-chime compression whistle of a heavy freight engine, pitched in a low baritone that slid neatly between the five echoes of the bowl without waking the sixth.

A crowd of shift-workers, foundry-men, and turbine-tenders gathered slowly at the eastern terminus, standing like a miniature range of greased-felt hills in the dripping gloom. Nobody shouted. Only the dry, staccato *clack-clack-clack* of ironwood paddles passed the word from plinth to plinth, across the swaying cable-cars, down to the textile looms: *Look to the bridge.*

Out of the colourless smear where the world stopped, a train was backing in.

It was the very same cargo tram that had ridden out into the nothingness four days ago, loaded with eighty tons of pig-iron ingots and twelve three-ton spools of greased wire cable. For three generations, the trams had gone out full and returned—if they returned at all—as empty, ghost-limned flatbeds that had to be hammered back into focus at the roundhouse. 

This tram was not empty. And it was not ghost-limned. As its solid iron wheels clicked off the blueprint-memory of the missing pier and onto the true, riveted girders of the viaduct, the masonry beneath it gave a deep, contented groan—not of strain, but of settlement. 

When the brakes hissed and locked at the terminus buffer, the chief Gage-Man stepped forward. He did not drop to his knees; he unhooked a brass caliper and a red-mercury spirit level from his harness, stepped up onto the flatbed, and measured the cargo.

The pig-iron was gone. In its place were crates made of rough-sawn, resin-heavy timber—a lowland pine that smelled so fiercely of turpentine and summer needles that the workers in the front row blinked three times in a single minute. Stenciled on the side of the crates, in thick, black, readable lead-paint, was a bill of lading:

*RECEIVED: 80 TONS FACT (GRADE A FOUNDRY IRON).*
*12 SPOOLS CONTINUITY (BRAIDED STEEL).*
*CREDIT ISSUED BELOW.*

Inside the first crate were four hundred pairs of boots. They were not light, flimsy things from a softer world; they were monstrous, magnificent works of cobbling, built with two-inch soles of meteoric iron that was fifty percent denser than local pig, lined with triple-thick sheepskin that had already been cured in mutton-tallow so the wearer wouldn't have to spend five years breaking them in, and heeled with soft bronze hobnails tuned to strike a pleasant, grounding *C-sharp* when dragged across raw hornblende.

In the second crate were five-pound ingots of a dark, warm, coppery metal that none of the metallurgists in the North Shelf had ever seen. When a person slipped one into the pocket of their mantle in place of their usual stamped lead, it did not merely pull their hips toward the bedrock; it radiated a steady, blood-heat warmth that crept through seven layers of wool, and so long as your fingers brushed it, your knuckles never blurred—no matter how long you sat still shelling fern-nuts or thinking a slow thought.

In the third crate were a hundred barrels of dried apples, smoked herring, and hard, yellow cheeses wrapped in oilcloth, packed alongside sacks of black, lowland soil—real dirt, ground down by ten thousand years of rivers and worms, rich with the memory of fallen oak leaves, ready to be spread in the plazas to soak up the echoes and give the cabbage-rosettes something soft to hold onto.

And strapped to the last flatbed was a single, massive block of granite—precisely forty feet wide, twelve feet thick, and cut to the exact, jagged reverse-geometry of the missing pier on the eastern viaduct. It had already been aged ten million years. Its edges were gently rounded by ancient rain; its face was pitted with fossilized seashells so that a man running his hand across it in the dark would know, beyond a shadow of a doubt, where his skin stopped and the stone began.

***

That was the beginning of the Trade. 

The wise power did not show its face, knowing that gods are notoriously low-density concepts and tend to thin the local rendering. Instead, it established a municipality on the other end of the line—or at least, the convincing, bureaucratic Weight of one. 

Every week, Oakhaven-in-the-Crag shipped out its ballast: its stubbornly hammered iron, its six-sided slate, its lukewarm grease, its sheer, bloody-minded insistence that matter ought to behave. And every week, the unseen buyer took that raw, Newly Minted Fact, used it to shore up whatever crumbling edges of the wider cosmos needed shoring, and sent back things that had History.

They sent crates of old, thumb-worn copper coins from fallen empires, which the citizens sewed into their hems; the coins were so heavy with the memory of ten thousand markets that a single penny could anchor a billowing skirt in a gale of cylinder-steam. They sent barrels of sea-salt that smelled of warm tides, and heavy, brass-bound books filled not with fairy tales, but with exquisitely detailed tables of logarithms, bridge-stresses, and the specific gravities of ten thousand terrestrial minerals, which the Plumb-Setters read aloud in the basement taverns like scripture while the blind condenser-eels thrashed warmly in the troughs.

Yet the wise power’s gentlest work was not done at the railway terminus. It was done in the center of the valley, on a Sunday afternoon, when the stamping mills were throttled back to a wheeze.

A young weaver from the Lower Ward rode the cable-car over to the central plaza, carrying his folding iron stool and his thermos of salted mutton-broth. He set his stool on the basalt twenty feet from the ground-floor window of the out-of-phase tenement, where the yellow gas-lamps took a week to flicker. 

He sat down to watch his wife, who was still turning from the stove with the kettle in her hand, her hem half an inch off the floorboards. 

The power looked at the veil of greasy condensation that hung between the weaver and the window. It knew that if it simply ripped the veil away and snapped the building back into the valley’s clock, the six stories of brick—and the two hundred souls inside—would suffer the temporal equivalent of the bends, three months of withheld momentum crushing them against the floorboards like a dropped counterweight.

So the power did something much smaller, and much more mechanical. 

When the weaver reached into his pocket for his piece of soapstone to write on his slate, his fingers closed around something else sitting on the basalt beside his boot. 

It was a brass optical instrument, shaped like a heavy marine binocular mounted on a short, stubby tripod of cold-forged iron, with a hand-crank on the side and a set of complex, counter-rotating crystal prisms inside the barrels. Stamped into the brass casing was a small maker’s mark—a plumb-bob balanced on a level—and a simple brass plate that read: *GEAR-REDUCTION GLASS. 1:10,080.*

The weaver, being an Oakhavener, did not marvel at the miracle. He checked the tripod’s feet with his pocket-level, found it true, set the heavy brass eyepieces to his brow, and turned the little crank until the internal clockwork began to tick with a heavy, escaping *chuff-thump* that matched the municipal air-pumps.

Through the lenses, the veil of greasy condensation parted. The light coming out of the window was caught by the spinning prisms, gathered up over weeks, and compressed—just as the light of the plaza going *in* was slowed down and stretched by the reverse-gearing of the glass so it wouldn't blind the people inside.

In the eyepiece, his wife finished her turn from the stove. She set the kettle down on the trivet. She walked to the window, wiped a circle in the interior steam with the cuff of her wool cardigan, and looked out. Because the glass worked both ways, she did not see a frantic, blurring ghost on the plaza; she saw her husband sitting solidly on his iron stool, raising his thermos of broth in a slow, steady toast. 

She smiled—a real-time smile, taking less than two seconds to reach her dark, still eyes—and tapped her knuckles twice against the glass. *Clack-clack.* 

Out on the plaza, the little brass housing of the binoculars vibrated with a tiny, sympathetic striker, tapping twice against the weaver’s thumbs. *Clack-clack.* *I am here. I am solid.*

By the next Sunday, there were forty such instruments bolted permanently into the basalt around the central tenement, arranged like coin-operated viewfinders at a seaside pier, their brass barrels furred with velvet-black moss, while the copper-winged swifts above the roof slowly, gracefully, at the rate of an inch a day, were guided by a gentle, localized tailwind back out of the slipstream and into the sky, one bird coming unstuck every Tuesday like a ripe plum dropping into the ferns.

***

And as for the mountain itself—the western rim still hangs in the air. The wise power left the great, smoked-glass slabs of granite suspended three feet above the bedrock, because a city of Gage-Men and Plumb-Setters would grow restless without a horizon to True up. 

Only now, when a forty-year slab finally finishes rendering and drops with that single, earth-splitting *crack* into the valley floor, the shockwave does not threaten the windowpanes. Instead, the newly fallen stone is found to be veined with rich seams of coal, native copper, and warm, iron-rich clay that crumbles easily under a boot-heel into soil. 

Down in the basement taverns beneath the boiler-houses, the workers still sit shoulder to shoulder on their iron benches, drinking dark fern-beer in the grease-slicked warmth. When the valley shudders with the drop of a new ledge, they still pause, lift their tallow-pale faces toward the dripping ceiling, and tap their ironwood paddles once against the brick.

A dry, unison *clack*. 

And then—so faint that a lowlander would miss it entirely, slipping right through the four-second pause of the counterweights—comes a single, deep, resonant tap from deep within the newly fallen bedrock itself. 

*Clack.*

The mountain tapping back, letting them know the shipment has arrived, the foundation is level, and the work is good.
