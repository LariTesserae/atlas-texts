To a lesser mind, the plateau would look like a place of brutal scarcity—a blinding purgatory where former humans have been pressed flat by the sky and reduced to silent, scraping parasites on the skin of a machine they cannot comprehend. A lesser power might feel pity. It might reach down to turn off the nodes, or lift the veil of high-altitude glare, or rain sweet water onto the stone until trees grew and the people could stand upright again and remember how to sing.

The Power that watches from the deep folds of the cobalt sky does none of these things. It is old enough to know that pity is often just a polite word for vanity—the desire to remake another’s survival into a shape more pleasing to the observer.

Instead, the Power watches the forty-minute cycle of the puddles. It watches the exquisite, unbroken geometry of a hunting band moving through the hush. It sees that these people are not degraded; they are *honed*. There is no fat on their bodies, and there is no fat in their souls. Their unsuperstitious indifference to the nodes is not ignorance—it is a profound, radical acceptance of reality. They do not waste calories worshipping the black ribbons any more than a mountain goat worships the cliff face. They simply know where to put their feet.

And yet, as the Power observes the long, silent centuries on the five-mile shelf, it perceives two things that draw its quiet intervention. 

The first is a mechanical certainty. The infrastructure beneath the travertine is ancient, running on automated routines established before the mountains were pushed up. The nodes are not eternal. In four hundred years, or perhaps six hundred, a subterranean manifold will finally corrode through, or the non-local calculation will reach its final integer and halt. When that happens, the waste heat will cease in an afternoon. The high-altitude freeze will rush in before the sun sets. The mists will stop rising. The fragile, high-metabolism oasis will flash-freeze into a tomb before a single generation of the crouched people can adapt or migrate down the sheer drop-offs.

The second thing the Power notices is the pebbles. 

It looks at the bases of the nodes, where tiny, water-smoothed spheres of limestone are wedged into the cracks by the survivors after an elder steps into the focal point. The people have no religion, no myths, and no time for grief. The pebble is the only thing they do that does not serve the belly. It is a marker: *Here was a weight. Now it is mist. We drank the mist, and we leave the stone.* 

Within the cold, thermodynamic harvesting of the nodes, the last electrical flickers of the elders’ brains—their lifetimes of reading the four-foot horizon, the memory of ten thousand mist-banks, the exact feel of a child’s horn-padded hand in the dark—are simply stripped as raw kinetic energy to power a dead civilization’s lingering math problem, and then radiated away as waste heat.

The Power decides that this is poor accounting. A machine should not eat a mind and leave only steam.

So the Power acts, though no creature on the plateau—not the gliding scavengers above, nor the scale-hounds in the shimmer, nor the scout flashing green light from a limestone flute—ever sees a hand descend from the sky. To change the rules of the plateau too quickly would be to break the dancers’ legs mid-stride. 

The Power reaches down, not into the air or the water, but into the fundamental forces where the nodes have their roots. Gently, with the patience of geology, it edits the parameters of the subterranean infrastructure.

First, it addresses the end of the world. It does not make the machines immortal; nothing made of matter should last forever, lest it trap the life that depends on it in an endless, unchanging loop. Instead, it programs a five-thousand-year twilight into the manifold. Beginning today, the plateau will cool by one-tenth of a degree every century. The fifty-foot radius of the silent zones will shrink by a fraction of an inch each generation. And down the sheer, jagged drop-offs at the plateau’s edge—where the warm fog currently rises—the Power subtly alters the thermal vents, guiding a tiny trickle of warmth and moisture down the cliff faces into the cold alpine valleys below. Over millennia, a staircase of micro-climates will form, leading downward. When the nodes finally die, five thousand years from now, the plateau will not be a trap; it will be an empty nest, its people having slowly, naturally followed the retreating warmth down into the wider world, their bodies and languages shifting at the pace of blood and bone.

Second, the Power touches the pebbles.

It does not change the way the elders die. That quick, painless stepping into the hush—turning one’s failing body into water for one’s grandchildren before the hounds can tear it—is a clean, proud mercy that belongs to them. 

Instead, the Power alters what the nodes *do* with the harvest. 

The next time an old woman with mineral-crusted knees walks into the center of a floating silver cluster and lets the machine take her heat, the last electrical pattern of her nervous system is not ground down into anonymous work. The Power diverts that final, intricate spark of consciousness downward, directing it into the tiny, spherical limestone pebble her band wedges into the crack at the node’s base.

At the level of the crystal lattice, the calcium carbonate of the pebble is re-indexed by the node’s warped physics. The stone becomes a high-density, solid-state memory core. 

Nothing changes on the surface. The band drinks the condensed puddle of her body, wedges the stone into the crevice, flashes a spark of blue light, and lopes away. 

Months later, during the freezing dead of night, the band returns to that same node to sleep. They pile together like pale roots within the twenty-pace halo of warmth, invisible inside the refractive shimmer. A young hunter, shivering slightly as the mist dries on his gullied skin, shifts his weight and presses his calloused palm against the base of the rock, his fingers brushing the row of wedged pebbles left there over the years.

He does not hear a voice—he has no words for a voice to use. 

Instead, as his skin touches the limestone sphere, the node’s ambient field completes a circuit. Behind his double-slitted eyelids, in the dark of his resting brain, he feels a sudden, warm flush of *knowing*. 

He sees, through eyes that closed forty years ago, where the water lingers longest when the wind comes from the north. He feels the exact, subtle muscle-tension required to turn a wrist so the crab-shell mirror throws a needle of light through a heavy fog without scattering. More than that, he feels the deep, quiet satisfaction of the old woman as she watched him—then just a small, clumsy thing with soft horn on his knuckles—successfully scrape his first basin of red algae. He feels her hand on the back of his neck, her violet heat-frill pulsing slowly against his cheek in the cottony dark: *Safe. Warm. Here.*

The young hunter does not startle. He has no concept of ghosts, so he is not afraid. To him, it is simply another rule of the rock, as natural as the refractive shimmer or the hot fissures that cook the bladder-reeds. The stone holds the heat of the day, and the small stones hold the heat of the old ones. 

In the dark, without making a sound, he shifts an inch closer to the crevice, letting his young daughter rest her head against the row of smooth pebbles so she can sleep inside the long, unbroken memory of the shelf.

Above them, the cobalt sky turns black and fills with hard, bright stars. The Power watches the small pile of breath and bone resting in the mirage, satisfied. It has given them time to become whatever they will become, and it has made sure that in a world without voices, nothing they have learned in the silence will ever be lost.
