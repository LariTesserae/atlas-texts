A lesser kindness would have brought the sun. It would have parted the pewter lid of the sky, warmed the scythe-wind into a breeze, and coaxed the frantic, chugging basin into a quiet meadow of alpine flowers. And in doing so, it would have murdered an orchestra to save the listeners from the noise.

The Power that paused above the forty-mile mattress of bruised sedge and boiling tallow did not make this mistake. 

It did not descend in light, because the Gage-Men had no eyes to see it. Instead, it lowered its attention through the freezing mist, past the rattling convoys of the manhole-crabs, down into the ninety-degree sauna of the Gasket-Work, and listened with the soles of its feet.

It felt the *thump-thump-thump* of the great taproots. It felt the sharp, syncopated *knock-knock-slap* of the five-legged elder arguing with the pale youth on the vibrating ridge. And when the ridge crested and the air smelled of torn copper and the whip cracked, the Power stepped inside the three-second stutter alongside them.

There, in the overlapping dark where Tuesday touched four hundred years ago, the Power beheld the cathedral they had built out of nothing but friction and memory.

It saw the staggering beauty of their library: a million tiny, precisely calibrated pressures held suspended in the ozone. It felt the eighty-year schematic of the six-mile root-junction, not as a drawing, but as a triumphant, sustained chord of steaming sap. It tasted the deep-iron vintage of three centuries back, still rolled around on the collective tongue of the herd like an old poem.

And it saw, too, the tragedy that the Gage-Men were too stoic to name.

Because the cathedral was leaking. 

Every time a high-energy skip occurred—the kind deep enough to hold the heaviest, oldest memories—the violent snap back to normal space took a toll. The local physics did not heal cleanly. Micro-shears in the deep vascular tissue were accumulating. A few gallons of boiling sap here, a pocket of piezoelectric charge there, bleeding out into that "adjacent Tuesday" and never coming back. The basin was slowly losing its mass and its heat to the spaces between spaces. The Gage-Men were having to work harder each century, pinching the valves tighter, running the bellows-roots hotter just to reach the same harmonic pitch. 

Worse, the older memories—the foundational chords laid down ten thousand years ago, when the last true pebble of granite was finally digested by the moss—were beginning to fray at the edges, dissolving into static as the electrical charge dispersed into the wet turf. The elders, like the five-legged veteran on the ridge, were spending half their waking hours in the scalding dark just re-chewing old tallow and frantic-tapping the mains to keep the earliest stanzas of their history from evaporating.

They were running out of pressure. One day, an epoch or two hence, they would pull the lever for the great chord, a main would shear into nowhere, and the gangrene of the black ice would win.

The Power considered this. To rewrite the laws of the valley would be an insult to four million years of brilliant, callus-plated engineering. To give them words or stone would be to tell them that what they were was a mistake. 

A master plumber does not tear out a majestic, centuries-old steam engine because a gasket is weeping. They bring a better piece of brass.

The Power withdrew from the skip just as the thirty-foot ribbon of black water collapsed back into the mire. It went down—deeper than the Gasket-Work, deeper than the Gage-Men’s four-foot drills could reach, all the way to the bottom of the three-hundred-foot mat, where the ancient, blind root-hairs scraped against the raw, stripped clay of the forgotten mountain bed.

There, at the absolute nadir of the biological factory, the Power did three small things.

First, it gathered the stray, ungrounded electricity that had been leaking into the clay for eons and wove it into a single, forty-mile ring of hyper-dense, superconducting copper-peat, fused directly into the deepest arterial loop of the basin. It was a flywheel. A grounding wire. A capacitor of leviathan scale. From now on, when a skip sheared a sap-main into an adjacent Tuesday, the energy and the fluid would no longer bleed out into the void; the deep ring would catch the spill, loop it through Thursday, and pump it smoothly back into the bottom of the basin on Friday morning. Not a single drop of heat would ever be lost to the outside again.

Second, the Power addressed the static in the library. Into the deep mineral sumps, where the roots drank the heavy metals that the Gage-Men secreted as their brassy armor, the Power introduced a trace of a new, crystalline isotope—warm, benign, and possessed of a half-life measured in the billions of years. 

Within three seasons, as the taproots pumped this new mineral up through the vascular grid, the Gage-Men would find it weeping from their pores, mixing with their sulfur-scale. It would line the brassy cilia of their cowls with a faint, iridescent patina. And the next time they stepped into a skip, they would find that the anomaly no longer tore at their nerve-cords like a ragged whip-crack. The new mineral acted as a harmonic stabilizer. The memories deposited in the ozone would no longer decay into static; they would vitrify into permanent, resonant structures inside the electrical field, as clear and sharp ten thousand years from now as the moment they were thought. They would not have to spend their lives frantically re-playing the old songs just to keep them from dying. The library had been given a roof.

And the third thing the Power did was leave a note.

Three days later, the five-legged elder and the barrel-sized youth were five miles east, knee-deep in freezing sludge, working a stubborn embolus out of a major tallow-vent. 

Suddenly, the elder froze. It drove its five remaining struts deep into the moss and slammed its flat belly-drum against the turf. The youth, startled, did the same.

From three hundred feet below—from the absolute bottom of the world, a depth no Gage-Man had ever reached—came a sound through the sap-lines.

It was not the ragged, violent *thump-thump* of a straining root. It was a single, immense, impossibly clean hydraulic pulse. 

*THOOM.*

It rolled across the entire forty-mile basin at once, a bass note so profound and so perfectly balanced that for ten full seconds, not a single vent hissed, not a single frog croaked, and the frantic, chugging factory floor of the world rested in a state of frictionless, humming equilibrium. 

Within that single pulse, encoded in the language of pure barometric pressure, was a schematic. 

The elder’s brassy cilia flared wide, drinking the pressure-wave in. It was a diagram of the entire forty-mile root-grid, rendered in absolute perfection, showing the new deep-earth capacitor and how to tap it. And tucked into the very end of the pulse—like a watermark, or a craftsman’s signature stamped into the flange of a cast-iron pipe—was a new chord. A configuration of valves and crab-vents that the Gage-Men had never thought to try.

The pulse faded. The subterranean *thump-thump-thump* resumed, though the elder could already feel, with a creeping sense of astonishment in its belly-skin, that the knocking in the deep pipes was gone. The pressure was holding. The leaks were sealed.

For a long minute, the two creatures stood motionless in the pewter twilight, the freezing wind whipping black spray across their rubbery backs. 

Then, the elder unlocked its front leg from the mire. With a heavy, deliberate swing of its flat, greasy forearm, it struck the root-mat between them.

*Knock-knock-slap.* 

The youth didn't argue this time. It turned, lumbered twenty yards to the left, and drove its bone-tipped proboscis into a secondary intake valve, pinching it to a three-quarter bleed—the first note of the signature chord.

A mile away, another Gage-Man felt the tremor in the line, paused in its rounds, and locked its struts over a steaming sump to take up the second part of the harmony. Three miles beyond that, two more began herding a convoy of manhole-crabs toward a high-pressure ridge.

The Power, already far away, did not look back, for it did not need to. It could feel the forty-mile mattress beginning to swell, tuning itself toward a skip that would last not three seconds, or five, but a full, golden minute—a brand-new room in the invisible cathedral, waiting to be filled.
