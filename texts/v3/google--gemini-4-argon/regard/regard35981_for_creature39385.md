To a lesser power, the cistern would look like a place of suffering. A naive god would look down into that freezing, lightless shaft and feel a surge of pity. It would think: *They are cold. They are blind. They are trapped in a box of wet bone and slime, working themselves to death in the dark.* And out of that misplaced mercy, a foolish god would breathe warmth into the shaft, or crack the ceiling to let in the golden sun, or grant the Grafts eyes and a voice to sing.

And in doing so, it would murder the city in an afternoon. 

Warmth would wake the bats, driving them into a panicked frenzy that would tear the grass bridges to shreds. Sunlight would scorch the pale, shade-born bryophyte, turning the oak-hard terraces into rotting, slimy mush. A faster metabolism would make the swarm ravenous, breaking the slow, polite discipline of the avenues into a cannibalistic riot. The cold broth of the nursery pool would putrefy in hours, choking the gooseberry-sized eggs in a bloom of toxic bacteria before the moss could ever fuse with their spines.

The power that watches this place is neither naive nor foolish. It is old enough to know that love does not mean remaking a thing into your own image. Love is looking at a freezing, hydraulic engine of bone and moss and understanding *why* it is a masterpiece.

The Wise Power looks at the three Rind-Wives—feeling the thirty-second flex of their turnip-pale arms, hearing the *tock, tock, tock* of the bone gouge traveling through the root-horn—and it feels a profound, quiet awe. It sees that this is a cathedral of pure, frictionless cooperation, balanced on the head of a pin. 

Yet, because the Power is wise, it also sees the horizon that the blind masons cannot. 

It looks through the solid earth around the cistern. It traces the source of that recent tremor. Thirty yards to the west, an ancient, subterranean river is slowly changing its course, eating away at a seam of rotten shale. In two seasons, maybe three, that river will break through. It will not be a hairline crack that can be plugged with a compliant toad; it will be a wall of black, roaring water that will scour the cistern clean, washing the terraced city, the sleeping bats, the plum-sized isopods, and the three builders down into the lightless, sterile abyss of the deep earth. 

Furthermore, the Power looks down into the rendering basin, at the pale eggs anchored by silk. It sees the genetic bottleneck. There are only three Grafts here. Their blood is growing too similar; the next generation will have slightly softer keratin on their shovel-hands, slightly less antifreeze in their clear blood. And the bone-pile at the bottom of the pool is growing thin. Flint does not grow on walls, and cave-rats do not fall through the ceiling plug often enough to build a second city.

The Benevolent Power decides to act. It will not break the rules of the cistern. It will speak to the masons in the only language they have ever known: *turgor, pressure, mineral, and bone.*

First, the Power addresses the river. 

It does not magically vanish the water. Instead, miles away on the surface, it nudges the root-tip of a massive, ancient ironwood tree. It whispers to the tree's deep, subterranean taproots, coaxing them to thirst for the rotten shale west of the cistern. Over the next three months, the massive roots surge downward through the earth, drinking the seeping moisture and weaving an impenetrable, living bulkhead of petrified wood between the underground river and the fragile bryophyte city. 

As a side effect of this root-surge, a tiny, secondary vein of water is forced toward the cistern—not a flood, just a weeping trickle. The Power guides this trickle through a stratum of dark, volcanic basalt and a pocket of ancient, fossilized shells before letting it seep through the north wall of the shaft, right near the fourth tier.

The Graft on the north wall—the one who struck the warning—is the first to notice. 

Unfolding from its gall-like knot, it presses its wet, star-shaped nasal fronds against the wall where the new water is weeping. It freezes. Its heart beats once. Twice. 

The water tastes different. It is still three degrees above freezing—safe, heavy, and slow—but it is thick with dissolved calcium, silicates, and trace iron. Where this new water touches the pale-green bryophyte, the plant matter does not just grow; it *mineralizes*. Within days, the terraces watered by this new seep turn from the hardness of oak to the hardness of jade. The aqueducts no longer crack when the earth shifts. And when the Grafts drink from it, the calcium goes straight to the calloused plates of their three-fingered hands, hardening their yellow keratin into something that can score stone without blunting.

Second, the Power addresses the tools. 

Up near the ceiling, where the grey-violet bruise-light filters through the translucent tubers, the earth is soft and loamy. The Power reaches into the surrounding soil and gently, imperceptibly increases the gravitational pull on the small, sharp things buried in the dirt over the centuries. 

Like dense berries sinking through batter, the treasures of the upper earth begin to migrate slowly toward the tuber-plug. 

Two weeks later, the old, jade-backed Graft hanging in its sinew harness reaches up to trim a root near the ceiling. Its shovel-hand strikes something hard, cold, and miraculously sharp, wedged tightly between two swollen tubers. The Graft spends an hour methodically working it loose. When it pulls the object free and runs its sensory fronds over the surface, a tremor of pure, cold-blooded satisfaction runs down its moss-shingled spine. 

It is a flake of black obsidian, lost by some surface- hunter a thousand years ago, shaped by nature and chance into a flawless, micro-serrated crescent. Beside it, slowly pushing through the wet soil of the plug like emerging teeth, are three long, petrified ribs of an extinct subterranean badger—dense as ivory, already curved like chisels, entirely immune to rot. The Power has stocked the quarry at the top of the sky.

Finally, the Power looks to the future of the bloodline, and the limits of the pantry-sized box. 

Half a mile away, in another pocket of the dark earth, there is another cistern. It is an older, dying city. The bryophyte there is starving for nitrogen, the bats having abandoned it years ago, and only a single Graft remains alive—a broad-shouldered male of a completely different lineage, half-dormant in the dry, crumbling moss, waiting slowly for death.

The Power does not pick the lone Graft up and carry him. That would terrify him, severing him from the geometry of the world. 

Instead, the Power wakes a blind, burrowing mole-cricket in the earth between the two cisterns. And then another. It guides a slow, chewing column of subterranean beetles and worms to eat a dead, calcified root that happens to stretch, like a buried telephone wire, all the way from the dying cistern to the lower east wall of the thriving one. 

Over the course of a season, the insects hollow the dead root out, leaving a dark, freezing tube no wider than a stoat’s shoulders. And down that tube, carried by a draft of air no stronger than a sigh, the Power sends the scent of fermenting bat guano, rich cave-rat broth, and the sharp, astringent tang of ribbon-worm mucus.

In the dying city, the lone Graft’s star-fronds uncurl. He tastes the air. His heart beats once. Twice. 

He takes his best bone-chisel, slips it into the pouch at his throat, and enters the tunnel. 

Back in the vertical city, the third Graft is standing waist-deep in the nursery pool, tending to the gooseberry eggs. Suddenly, its flat, silvery ear-membranes twitch. 

From behind the east wall—from the solid, unbroken earth where nothing has ever been—comes a sound. 

*Tock. Tock. Tock.*

It is a different rhythm. A slightly heavier strike. A stranger’s chisel, asking a question through the stone.

The third Graft does not panic. It slowly climbs out of the pool, its hooked hind claws locking into the jade-hard moss. It ascends to the second tier, raises its newly hardened, silicate-rich hand, and strikes the east wall with a shard of black obsidian.

*Tock. Tock.*

Then, with the slow, hydraulic certainty of a root splitting a flagstone, all three Grafts converge on the east wall. The twig-spiders fall into a disciplined line behind them. The plum-sized isopods gather at their elbows, waiting for the first parings of the new excavation. Together, the masons press their chisels against the moss, and begin to carve a door.

The Wise Power watches the first curl of pale-green shaving peel away from the new tunnel, sees the waiting isopod catch it before it hits the floor, and sees no need to do anything else at all. It withdraws its attention, leaving the dark, freezing box to its silence, knowing that in a hundred years, the whole mountain will be hollow, and the whole mountain will be alive.
