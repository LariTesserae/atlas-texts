A lesser power, looking down into the warm, copper-smelling dark of the Sump, would have mistaken the place for a tragedy. 

A lesser power would have seen the faceless Tenants queueing at the bottom of a four-step stair that led only to a drop, and felt a sharp, intervening pity. It would have parted the fifty-foot ceiling of black humidity to let in the violent geometry of the sun. It would have taught the basalt to stay hard, commanded the water to lie flat, and carved mouths and eyes into the tallow-stone heads of the Tenants so they might weep at their condition and then be comforted. It would have built the roofs they were waiting for. And in doing so, it would have murdered the Sump entirely, turning a vast, slow, dreaming lithic organism into a mere ruin populated by confused statues.

The Power that actually turned its attention to the basin was older than architecture, and far wiser. It knew that a habit is a sacred thing, even when there is no mind behind it to know what it is practicing. It recognized that the Sump of the Unbuilt was not a broken city, nor a purgatory, but an *incubation*—a blind, mineral rehearsal for a play that had not yet been written. The mud was trying to remember something it had never seen. 

So the Power did not bring light, which would have blinded the bruisey violet gloom. It did not bring wind, which would have dried the glossy mucilage of the reeds into brittle chaff. It descended into the basin without displacing a single drop of the two-inch water, arriving not as an object, but as a slight, merciful adjustment to the rules of the dream.

It listened for a century or two to the *plop* and the three-second echo. It watched the Tenants fuse into benches in the melted amphitheaters, and it watched the Surveyors roll down the avenues like logs of dark glass, grinding the citizens back into boulders and printing the east upon the west. 

The Power saw that the Sump’s great, silent exhaustion came from a single flaw in its broken physics: the cycle was entirely closed. Every Congestion equalized the Tenants; every Surveyor erased them. The city was chewing and re-chewing the same mouthful of mud, forever resetting its own errands. It had posture, and it had motion, but it had no *accumulation*. It could not keep what it learned.

The Power decided to give the Sump three small gifts. They were so subtle that the sterile, feverish dark did not reject them.

First, the Power reached into the subterranean sinks where the grey silt-stone migrated, and it altered the memory of the tallow-rock. 

The next time a Surveyor burst against a condensed cluster of towers with the sound of a splitting sail, the smooth, grey boulders left scattered in the receding wave were not entirely erased. When the base of a boulder softened into porridge and extruded its heavy, blunt arm to pull itself back toward a doorway, a faint trace of its previous shape remained deep in its core—a slightly denser grain of basalt that had survived the churning belly of the water-column. 

Now, when two Tenants met on a shrinking threshold and leaned their heavy, thumb-indented brows together in Congestion, they did not merely equalize their mass into two identical medium-sized figures. The denser grains within them resisted the averaging. Instead of cancelling each other out, their shapes *compounded*. One Tenant would pull back from the plum-colored flare of light bearing a slight, permanent asymmetry on its shoulder—the ghost of the narrow alley the other had stood in for a week. They began, ever so slowly, to become distinct. They were no longer just balancing the ledger of the mud; they were collecting an archive of postures.

Second, the Power touched the solitary black survey-reeds. 

It did not make them grow leaves, nor did it teach them to flower. It simply tuned their stiffness to the frequency of the dripping arches. Before, the three-second echoes of the condensation had bounced off the retaining walls warped and lonely, dying out in the wet dark. Now, when a heavy bead fell from a lintel—*plop*—the sound wave traveling through the two-inch water struck the nearest iron-black reed and caused it to hum. 

It was a low, submarine vibration, barely a note—the sound of a bronze bell rubbed with a wet thumb. One reed hummed in the key of the plaza; twenty meters away, another answered in the slightly flattened key of the sunken boulevard. As the invisible ribbons of mucilage drifted from the reeds and wound around the passing Tenants like heavy overcoats, the slime carried this vibration with it. A Tenant walking down an avenue with its rhythmic *schluck-schluck* began to hum faintly with the acoustics of the districts it had traversed. When seven or eight of them sat in the concentric mud rings of an amphitheater and fused into a single breathing bench, their combined mineral coats resonated in a slow, shifting chord—a deep, thrumming harmonic that lasted for days, giving the empty pit in front of them a performance to listen to.

The third gift was the smallest, and it was given to the water itself.

The Power watched a two-inch sheet of marsh water peel off the cobblestones with a greasy *shhhk*, rising through the black air with the inverted relief of the street held on its underside. Normally, at thirty feet, it would either cool and slap back down, or curl into the crushing cylinder of a Surveyor. 

The Power breathed a fraction of a degree of warmth into the ceiling at the thirty-foot mark—just enough to keep a rising sheet of water from losing its cohesion, here and there, once in a great while.

In the center of a wide, drowned commons, a lifted table of water rose twenty feet, then twenty-five, and stopped. It did not fall. Its edges drooped slightly, like a heavy tablecloth over an invisible table, and then the mineral-rich mucilage trapped within it slowly cured in the upper warmth, stiffening the water into a permanent, translucent canopy of amber-tinted ice that was warm to the touch. 

Beneath it, for the first time in millions of years, a patch of the Sump ceased to be rained upon. 

The drip stopped. The porridge-stone beneath the canopy dried just enough to hold a crisp edge. Within an hour, a dozen Tenants had slouched out of the surrounding avenues, drawn by the sudden absence of falling water. They stood beneath the hovering, cobblestone-stamped ceiling. They did not have to wait for the roof to be built anymore; the water had remembered the ground, lifted it overhead, and become the roof.

The Power withdrew then, leaving no footprints in the mud, satisfied. 

It had not forced the city awake. The Sump of the Unbuilt remained dark, and wet, and mindlessly patient. The bruised violet light still failed to reach the water; the Surveyors still rolled through the suburbs like liquid locomotives. 

But now, under the warm, hovering canopies of stilled water, Tenants with distinct, crooked shoulders stood dry in the dark, leaning their brows together, trading the deep, humming chords of the east and the west—slowly, across geological ages, assembling the shape of a thought they would one day be ready to think.
