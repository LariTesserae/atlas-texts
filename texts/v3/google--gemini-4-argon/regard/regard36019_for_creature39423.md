When the Presence arrived, it did not come from the sky. To come from the sky would have been an intrusion of cold white light and foreign air, and the Presence was too courteous for that. 

Instead, it arrived as an warmth in the groundwater, rising slowly through the flooded cellar-vaults and the drowned foundation pilings, until it was simply there, standing on the springy, oil-slick mattress of the peat. For convenience—or perhaps out of a quiet respect for the builders who had laid the bricks a hundred years ago—it wore the shape of a tall, slightly stoop-shouldered figure in a coat the color of wet slate, though if you looked closely at the hem of the coat, you could see that the wool was woven from hanging roots and threaded with droplets of condensate.

A lesser benevolence might have felt pity upon looking into this sunken nave. A lesser wisdom might have seen the rust, the rot, the oily black muck, and the blind, heavy beasts wedged against the iron, and thought: *This is a trap. I will break the brick walls; I will stop the loud, dirty engines; I will cleanse the water and lead these creatures out into the clean green marshes by the sea.*

The Presence did not think this, because it listened first.

It stood still for three days—which in the time-scale of the courtyard was barely a long afternoon—and let the *chug-thump, chug-thump* of the twin centrifugals enter the soles of its boots and climb into its chest. 

It saw that this was not a ruin. It was a marriage. 

It saw the way the strangler figs held the trembling brickwork together like a net around an old cheese. It saw how the creatures had surrendered their own heartbeat to take up the rhythm of the pistons, and how the pistons, in return, had surrendered their cold mechanical mortality to become the warm, iron organs of a living body. To smash the walls would let the winter winds in to freeze the copper-rich blood of the beasts. To stop the pumps would stop their hearts, and drown the whole low-lying district of sleeping warehouses and forgotten streets beyond. 

Yet, looking deeper—down through the dark peat, into the iron housings, and fifty years forward along the slow curve of time—the Presence saw the sorrow that was waiting for this little world. 

Iron is patient, and these creatures were patient, too, chewing the encroaching roots and greasing the shafts with the fat of their own throats. But entropy is a debt that biological grease cannot pay forever. 

Down inside the main bearing housing of the eastern pump, the babbitt metal was worn down to the thickness of a fingernail. High on the north wall, the brass spring inside the pressure-relief valve had developed a microscopic hairline fracture; in four years, on a Tuesday in November, it would snap, venting all the steam at once, dropping the line pressure to zero, and letting the cold river rise. And deeper still, in the forgotten municipal boiler-vaults two blocks away—where an automated geothermal-and-coal-slurry feed had been blindly sending steam down the main conduit since the city abandoned the basin—the main intake grate was eighty percent choked with calcified river-silt.

The garden was tending the machine, and the machine was tending the garden, and the beasts were the priests of the union. All that was lacking was a way for the iron to grow back what it lost to the water.

So the Presence went to work. It did not work like an engineer, with wrenches and torches, which would have frightened the small gasket-creatures on the overhead pipes. It worked like the courtyard itself: slowly, darkly, and through the medium of water and muck.

First, it knelt beside the great oil-drum-sized elder wedged between the condensers. 

The creature did not startle. Through its buried root-toes, it had felt the newcomer’s weight on the peat for seventy-two hours, and it had judged the vibration to be harmonic—like a new, steady timber shoring up a wall. When the Presence laid a broad, warm hand upon the shelf-fungi and pillow-moss of the creature’s head, the amber eyes opened. The gray-green membrane slid back. The beast let out its deep, bass groan, adding that extra syllable to the *chug-thump* of the floor, and did not pull away.

"You have kept the watch well," the Presence murmured, though the words were not spoken in the air; they were sent down through the palm of the hand as a low, comforting thrumming in the fifty-hertz range, right where the creature’s bones could hear them best. "You have kept the water down and the heat in. Now let me widen your table, so the iron does not starve."

The Presence reached its other hand down into the black, bath-warm water pooling around its boots, pushing its fingers six inches deep into the cinder-muck until they brushed the pale, sensitive tendrils of the creature’s toes. 

There, in the warm dark, the Presence introduced a tiny, silent alteration to the anaerobic bacteria that lived in the peat and in the beast’s own heavy gut. It did not change what the creature was; it merely gave its internal furnace a new recipe.

From that hour on, when the elder drank the iron-rich runoff and the dissolved limestone of the yard, its body would no longer simply pass the minerals back into the mud. The bacteria in its throat-glands began to bind the reduced iron and the river-silicates into the heavy lipid-slime under its chin. 

When the beast next dragged itself over to the eight-foot flywheel and rubbed its broad throat against the spinning main shaft, the glistening biological grease it left behind did not merely lubricate. Drawn into the dark housing by the turning steel, squeezed under the immense, rhythmic pressure of the axle, the iron-rich slime began to plate itself onto the worn babbitt metal. Atom by atom, revolution by revolution, *chug-thump* by *chug-thump*, the beast’s own secretions began to rebuild the bearing—depositing a smooth, grey-black organo-metallic matrix that was slicker than bronze and tougher than pig iron. 

Whenever the machine wore down a millimeter, the beast’s affection would grow it back.

Next, the Presence looked up into the green suspension bridges twenty feet overhead, where the raccoon-sized youngsters clung to the hissing pinpricks in the steam conduits. 

Walking to the north wall, where the giant philodendrons climbed the damp brick, the Presence touched the woody aerial roots and whispered a small, clever secret into their cambium. It taught a single, creeping species of rusted-iron ivy—already growing in the mortar—how to drink the copper-and-zinc leachate from the old electrical cable trays and lay it down inside its own woody fibers as a flexible, metallic scale.

Within a season, the ivy had climbed to the brass pressure-relief valve. It did not choke the valve—the youngsters up on the pipes nibbled the shoots back whenever they grew over the vent, because they liked the regular *shhhhh-clack* of the hot vapor. Instead, the ivy wrapped the fracturing brass housing in a tight, living bronze-wood corset, taking the strain off the tired century-old spring, calibrating its tension to the humidity of the leaves.

And for the distant, unseen boilers two blocks away? The Presence simply walked through the dark, flooded subterranean pipe-tunnels where no foot had stepped since the mid-twentieth century. Where the river-silt was choking the great intake grates, it took a handful of the blind, finger-long millipedes from the courtyard and set them upon the warm stone, blessing their mandibles with a hunger for calcium scale and their bellies with a fondness for the hot water around the grates. Within two years, there were ten thousand of them, grazing the intake clean, keeping the water flowing and the deep geothermal-slurry fires breathing evenly in the dark.

Before it left, the Presence stood once more in the center of the sunken nave. 

It looked up at the rectangle of flat, white coastal sky four stories above. It saw that the world outside was changing, as the world outside always does. In twenty years, or forty, men with clipboards and drones and surveying lasers would eventually come back to this low river basin, looking for space to build glass towers, or wondering why this forgotten quarter of the city had never flooded during the great autumn storms.

If they looked down into the courtyard and saw what was here, they would not understand it. They would call the beasts an anomaly, and the machinery a hazard, and they would bring cranes and pumps of shrill, high-speed aluminum that whined instead of thrummed.

So the Presence did one last thing. It did not build a roof, for the monstera and the tree ferns needed the submarine green light and the rain. 

Instead, it reached up and wove a slight, gentle laziness into the light that fell past the fourth-story cornices. It was not an illusion, not quite; it was simply an encouragement to look elsewhere. If a gull flew over, it saw only a tangled roof of green vines over a collapsed mill-yard. If a satellite passed above, its thermal cameras registered only the diffuse, unremarkable warmth of rotting compost in an old brick hole. And if a surveyor ever climbed the rusted stairs of the surrounding buildings to peer through a broken fourth-story window, the heavy, bone-deep *chug-thump* rising from the green gulf would make his eyelids grow suddenly, wonderfully heavy, and convince him that there was nothing down there but old weeds and rainwater, and that he ought to go back down to the street and get a cup of coffee.

Down on the floor of the court, the north wall’s relief valve blew—*shhhhh-clack*—sending a fresh, glorious cloud of warm steam rolling down through the staghorn ferns. 

A single drop of hot condensate hit the nose of the great mud-beast between the condensers. It licked the drop away with its grey, eel-like tongue, shifted its heavy belly an inch deeper into the warm, oily peat, and let its eyelids slide shut. It did not know that it had been blessed; it only knew that the iron against its ribs felt unusually strong today, and that the broth of the yard tasted rich and sweet, and that the heart of the world was beating right on time.

*Chug-thump.*

*Chug-thump.*
