When the attention arrived, it did not come as a shadow across the forty-degree sun, nor as a wind that would have troubled the taut hemp of the plumb-bobs. A lesser kindness might have pitied the valley its stillness and offered it a spring thaw, or a night full of stars, or the hot, messy blood of true beasts. 

But the power that looked down into the slate basin was old enough to recognize a work of art, and wise enough to know that the city was not a prison. It was a vow.

For a long watch—longer than the endurance of any mortal knee—the Presence simply inhabited the geometry. It lay along the blinding eastern faces of the cliffs; it cooled itself in the frost-rimmed western crevices; it listened to the flannel-wrapped pocket-watch ticking of the millions of flat iron-chitin bodies wedging the dark stones together. It followed the slow, saline pulse of the Keepers’ gills, and it sank down through the soapstone pipes into the black faults to feel the great, blind siphon-root shifting its pale tonnages of water from north to south.

It saw how the builders had feared time—how they had looked at the rotting of wood, the slumping of mortar, the wild, grieving chaos of birth and hunger, and had chosen instead to turn themselves and their flocks into the teeth of an escapement wheel. They had traded the story for the state. They had built a truce with entropy, and they were winning, at the cost of every thought larger than twelve inches.

And yet, the Presence felt a tiny, almost imperceptible friction in the machine.

The valley was not quite closed. Even in a timeless pocket, a flint shim rubbed three million times by a basalt block loses four atoms of silica. Even a copper nail-plate, scraping a film of ice from a western crevice, leaves a microscopic trace of verdigris in the frost. The six-sided pellets dropped down the drainage slits carried a grain of salt that the deep siphon could not entirely digest. And in the slow, cold brine of the Keepers’ veins, there was a tiredness that was not physical—a faint, mineral tautness, the vigilance of a hand that has held a weight for ten thousand years and does not know how to set it down without breaking the floor.

The city was an eight-day clock that had been wound to run for a million years, and it was in its nine-hundred-thousandth year.

The Presence did not wake them. To wake a Keeper would be to drop it from the parapet; their minds had been pared away until only the calipers remained. To give the ungulates curiosity would be to starve them, for the moss grew only fast enough for seven chews of the jaw.

Instead, the Presence decided to do two things: one for the machine, and one for the soul that had forgotten it was inside the machine.

First, it descended into the flooded faults beneath the bedrock.

Where the blind, white column of the siphon-creature pressed its muscular membranes against the jagged young slate of the mountain’s root, the water was cold and thick with the tiny, accumulated dust of ages—the microscopic flour of stone ground off by the Shimmers, the stray copper ions from the Keepers' nails, the lost silica. The siphon had been holding this silt in suspension in its lowest, darkest blind-sacs, unable to vent it as vapor, slowly stiffening over the eons like an old joint filling with chalk.

The Presence laid a hand that was not a hand along the deep stone. It did not alter the fault. It simply warmed a single, hair-thin vein of quartz three hundred fathoms below the lowest heart of the siphon, establishing a silent, atomic exchange. As the dark water pulsed past that deep seam, the suspended dust of the city’s wear was drawn out of the creature’s tissues and re-crystallized directly onto the living rock of the mountain’s base, while the exact equivalent of fresh, dissolved mineral was released back into the cold current to rise through the mist-vents, settle on the moss, be eaten by the grazers, drop down the slits, and return to the walls.

The loop was sealed. The clock would no longer wear its own bearings away. A great, sub-audible *thump* traveled through the plazas—softer than usual, the sound of the deep siphon relaxing a fraction of an inch in its flooded bed, relieved of a burden it had no brain to name.

Second, the Presence rose to the unfinished upper tier of the terrace, where the bronze pry-bar lay beside the damp wooden mallet and the copper plumb-bob hung from its tripod.

Twenty yards away, along the shadow-line, the Keeper sat like a bundle of gray shingle-thatch. Its four smoky-quartz eyes were closed against the glare. In the pouch of folded skin beneath its throat lay the single brown conifer needle it had picked up from the courtyard flags an age ago, waiting to be placed back into a fissure where the roots required that exact grain of humus.

The Presence looked at the plumb-bob.

The Keeper’s entire existence—the existence of all three hundred of them—was bent upon the prevention of discrepancy. When the string was pulled a thousandth of an inch out of true by a bead of mist, the Keeper moved. Between errors, the Keeper was nothing: a stone corbel, a waiting caliper, a suspension of being. They had no rest, because rest requires the completion of a task, and a vigil is never completed; it is only interrupted by the next fault.

The Presence reached into the pale yellow light forty degrees above the southern ridge.

It did not move the sun. It did not stir the air.

Instead, right beside the wooden tripod, in the two-inch gap between the bronze pry-bar and the reed basket of shims, the Presence caused a single stone to be.

It was a piece of dark river-flint, no larger than a thumb-joint, shaped into a twelve-sided polyhedron. Its facets were not split like the young rock of the valley, nor dressed with bronze like the masonry; they were polished to a dull, greaseless satin that neither gathered the mist nor reflected the blinding eastern sun. It had no place in the wall. It was too round for a shim, too small for a flagstone, too heavy to be blown there by a wind that did not exist.

It was an error, and yet its weight on the terrace slab was distributed with such exquisite, impossible symmetry that the Shimmers in the dark flues beneath did not feel a single grain of added stress. Not one iron-chitin leg had to lock to support it.

Slowly—over the course of forty minutes—the gray thatch on the parapet stirred.

The Keeper had not seen the stone appear; beyond three feet, the world was only yellow blur and cobalt shade. Keepers did not patrol at random. They moved when the geometry called them. And something in the terrace’s stillness had changed—not a flaw, not a threat to the plumb, but a new note in the chord of the plaza’s weight.

Waiting until a flock of oil-black birds stepped up the ramp onto the tier, the Keeper unfolded itself and moved among them, its long, thatch-wrapped arms brushing the flagstones at the exact tempo of their heavy, water-shedding stride. While the birds paused by the unfinished wall, the Keeper folded itself down beside the tripod and stayed behind as the flock marched on.

Its brow-thatch parted. The four cabochons of smoky quartz came within twelve inches of the twelve-sided stone.

For a long time—longer than it took the herds to cross the three-mile grid from spur to spur—the Keeper looked at it.

Up close, where the Keeper could see the grain of basalt and the line where water intended to freeze, the stone had no grain at all. The water vapor did not bead upon it, so there was nothing to wick away with a dry palm. It did not lean against the hemp string of the plumb-bob. It did not threaten to pry two slates apart.

The six-fingered hand unfolded from the mantle. The three-against-three tongs of blue-gray skin closed around the stone.

The Keeper lifted it a millimeter off the terrace slab.

When it did, the stone was not cold like the western crevices, nor warm like the eastern flags. It held a quiet, neutral temperature that belonged to neither shadow nor sun—the exact temperature of the clear brine in the Keeper’s own slow veins. And through the porous, river-flint skin of its palm, which was made to drink stray droplets of water, the Keeper drank something else.

Not a thought. Not a vision of rivers or stars, which would have cracked its narrow, jewel-eyed skull.

It felt *sufficiency*.

It felt, in the language of dry-stack masonry, the sensation of a keystone dropping the last hair’s-breadth into an arch that can never fall. It felt the weight of the four-story watchtowers, the three-mile grid of plazas, the millions of ticking wedges in the dark, and the great blind heart below—and for the space of that touch, the weight was not something the Keeper had to hold up with its copper nails. The weight was holding *itself* up. The work was good. The plumb was true.

After an hour, the six-fingered hand set the twelve-sided stone back down—not in the pouch beneath its throat where stray pine needles went, and not over the edge of the parapet into the drainage slits. It placed it three inches to the left, squarely in the center of the flat coping stone of the unfinished wall, where the next course of slate would have gone if the builders had not stopped.

Then the Keeper drew its arm back under the cedar-colored thatch, closed its four quartz eyes, and sat beside the tripod.

It looked, from across the plaza, exactly as it had before: a bundled wool cloak left behind by a quarryman, dripping slow pearls of mist from its elbows at the same rate as the dwarf conifers. When a bead of water formed on the plumb-line, its thumb still reached out and wicked it away.

Yet if you watched the valley for three days—or what would be three days in a country where the sun went down—you would see a new movement among the silent gears of the city.

A Keeper from the sunken courtyards would come up the monumental staircase in the middle of an ibex-herd, pause by the unfinished wall while the animals chewed their seven strokes of cud, and lay its six-fingered palm for five minutes upon the small, satin-dark stone on the coping. Then it would fold itself back into the herd and go down to scrape the frost from the western crevices. Later, another would come from the northern granaries.

Nothing in the valley decayed, and nothing grew larger. The sun stayed at forty degrees above the southern ridge; the cisterns stayed full to the chisel-mark; the amphibians pulsed their throats in the cold shade. The museum remained bright, breathable, and still.

Only now, on the highest terrace, there was a keystone for the silence—a place where the hands that held the world still could touch the rock, and know that it was held.
