A lesser power—one that confuses benevolence with tidy symmetry, or mercy with the absence of struggle—would look into that dark, clicking throat of rock and feel a spasm of pity. It would want to lift her out of the dung and the bugs. It would think of green valleys, of clear running water that does not taste of sulfur and bat-piss, of sunlight warming clean skin. It would think to heal the milky rinds of her eyes so she could see the stars, to straighten the ancient bow of her spine, or to call another of her vanished kind from across the epochs so she would not have to be the last.

Wait long enough, however, and wisdom stays the hand.

To clear her eyes would be to blind her on the snow-fields; those cataracts are her shield against the high, white glare. To straighten her back would unbalance her on the forty-degree scree slopes where a high center of gravity means a thousand-foot tumble into the dark. To wash the grease and the yellow lichen from her skin would strip her of her armor, leaving her raw to the teeth of the gale. And to bring another of her kind into that hull-sized chamber would break the math of the mountain: the avalanches do not drop enough dead goats for two, and the bone-rimmed bench above the vent is only wide enough for one old barrel-chested body to sleep without crowding the beetles out of their warmth.

She is not miserable. She is a stone fitted into an arch that has stood for ten thousand years. If you pull her out to polish her, the arch falls.

So the power does not descend in a pillar of light, which would only send a hundred thousand bats into a fatal, wing-snapping panic against the narrow walls. It does not speak a word into her mind, for she has not used words since her mother died three hundred winters ago, and a voice in her head would feel like a tumor or a madness. 

Instead, the power listens to the same ledger she listens to. It drops its awareness down through the roots of the mountain, past the sleeping swifts and the tallow-colored harvestmen, down into the dark plumbing of the earth where the magma seam sighs.

And there, three miles beneath her calloused feet, it finds the flaw.

The seam is dying. Not tonight, and not next winter, but soon—in five years, perhaps six. A plate of deep granite is slipping slowly sideways, choking off the basalt capillary that feeds heat to the chimney. Left alone, the deep *thrum* beneath her buttocks would begin to falter while she still has twenty good years of marrow-cracking left in her thick hands. The steam would thin. The slick rock would dry and grow brittle with frost. The bats would fail to wake from their torpor; the plum-sized arachnids would curl their eight legs inward and freeze into dry husks; and one morning, she would bury herself deep in the vulture feathers and simply never warm up enough to move again.

That is a cold death, and an untidy one.

So the power reaches into the deep dark, three miles down, where the rock is plastic and red as a coal. It does not shatter the mountain; it merely leans its weight against the slipping granite plate—a silent, tectonic nudge, no wider than the breadth of a fingernail. A hairline fracture opens downward into a deeper, younger pocket of mantle-heat. Fresh magma, thick and slow as pine pitch, oozes into the root of the vent. 

Up in the vaulted chamber, the old woman is just reaching into the bottom of her nest for a handful of cold mutton-tallow to grease her brow against the night air. 

Beneath her, the rock gives a single, almost imperceptible *shiver*—not an earthquake, just the settling of a house that has decided to stand for another thousand years. The deep subterranean *thrum* drops half a note in pitch, becoming fuller, steadier, like a throat clearing itself in sleep. A slightly warmer puff of steam sighs up through the floor, carrying a faint, clean smell of hot iron that cuts through the ammonia for a second before vanishing.

She pauses, her grease-blackened fingers hovering over the horn pot. Her flat ears twitch. She feels the new note in the stone through the leather of her soles. For three breaths, she sits completely still, letting the harvestmen flow over her ankles, waiting to see if the mountain is angry. 

When the rhythm holds—deep, warm, and certain—the shelf of her lichen-crusted brow relaxes by a fraction of an inch. She makes a small, wet sound in the back of her throat, no louder than a bat settling its wings. 

Then the power goes up the chimney ahead of her, out into the silver, frost-brittle night under the moon.

Three hundred yards down the western ridge, wedged in a chute of gray shale where the wind scours the snow away, lies an old nanny goat that broke its neck in last week's slide. The vultures have not found it yet because it is buried under four inches of wind-packed crust. Left as it is, the old woman might walk right over it in the dark, her nose defeated by the freezing wind.

The power becomes a tiny, localized eddy in the gale—a finger of wind that scrapes three inches of frost-crust off the dead goat's flank, exposing a palm-sized patch of dark, stiff hair and releasing a single, thin ribbon of rank, musky scent to curl up the ridge toward the chimney's mouth.

And one last thing, smaller still. 

Right at the lip of the entry-slot, where she must haul her heavy, curved body out onto the scree every night, the black basalt has been worn so glassy by centuries of her greasy palms that her grip has begun to slip when her joints are stiff with the morning cold. The power touches a patch of stone the size of a dinner plate just inside the rim, leaching a few grains of feldspar out of the matrix, leaving the surface minutely pitted—rough as a cat's tongue, just sharp enough to catch the horny calluses of her four thick fingers and hold them fast.

Then the power withdraws entirely, leaving the mountain to its own slow time.

Ten minutes later, a broad, spade-like hand reaches up out of the dark slot. The calloused fingers find the new, rough bite in the stone without knowing it is new. She hauls herself out into the freezing moonlight, hunched and steaming like a boulder dropped from a fire. She pauses on the lip, turns her broad, bridgeless nose into the wind, and catches the thin ribbon of dead-goat musk riding up from the western chute. 

Her milky eyes do not blink. She shifts the black flint chopper to her left hand, drops onto all fours to keep her belly warm against the shale, and crawls out into the silver dark to claim her supper.
