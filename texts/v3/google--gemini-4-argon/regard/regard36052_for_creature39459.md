A foolish power would look upon the Sunken Grid and see a prison. It would see the stooped backs, the milky eyes, the scarred vocal cords, and the relentless, scalding gloom, and out of a clumsy pity, it would try to "save" the Lappers. It would part the bruised clouds to let in the blinding sun; it would cool the boiling rivers, silence the bronze pistons, and plant green trees in the cracked basalt. 

And in doing so, it would murder them. In the sudden, terrifying silence of a stopped engine, the Lappers’ hearts would falter from the lack of the barometric cradle-thrum. Their immense, vapor-adapted lungs would dry and crack in the crisp mountain air. Their culture, written in the vibration of phosphor-bronze against cast iron, would be struck instantly deaf and dumb.

The power that turned its attention to the plateau was neither foolish nor clumsy. It was ancient enough to understand that love does not mean reshaping a creature into your own image, and wise enough to recognize that the Lappers had achieved something rare and sacred: a complete, unbroken harmony with an impossible world. They were not suffering; they were *fitted*.

Yet, as the power watched through the amber haze—feeling the slow, ten-minute strides of the Dredge-Gantries and listening to the staccato chatter of clan-mothers rapping their knuckle-rings against the buried steam-trunks—it perceived two truths that the Lappers themselves could not see.

The first was a slow, generational sorrow: the sulfur-scarring of their lungs and throats, which stole their breath decade by decade and turned their final years into a wet, rasping struggle before the Dissolution. 

The second was an impending doom, centuries away, but mathematically certain. Deep below the fifty-foot shafts, in the living rock where the geothermal locks met the magma plumes, the primary intake manifolds—structures the Lappers had never seen and could never reach—were slowly succumbing to a crystalline silicate creep that the Gantries could not dredge. In four hundred years, a main valve the size of a cathedral would seize. The pressure would rupture the basalt floor, and the Sunken Grid would blow itself apart in a single, apocalyptic geyser of superheated brine.

The power decided to act. It did not descend with trumpets or miracles. It became a plumber, a chemist, and a composer.

First, it sank its presence three miles down into the unbearable heat of the bedrock, below the deepest bronze piston. There, in the dark, it spent a year subtly altering the mineral equilibrium of the hydrothermal vents. It did not stop the heat or the salts; instead, it introduced a trace catalytic element into the rising water—a heavy, stable isotope that bonded with the silicate creep on the cathedral-valves, causing the lethal crust to flake away into harmless, fine silt long before it could seize the gears. It rebuilt the deep foundations of the machine so that its equilibrium would hold not just for centuries, but for geological ages. It guaranteed the floor would never break.

Next, it rose to the Catch-Basins, where the pale, rubbery Blisters swelled with vent-gas and popped in the hot rain. 

With a touch as light as a stray cosmic ray, the power tweaked the genetic code of the mycelium. Over the span of three seasons, the Lappers noticed a subtle change in the harvest. The largest Blisters—the ones they peeled and tanned for their gallery floors—began to develop a faint, vein-like marbling of dull gold within their translucent skins. When these new skins were cured in alum and laid down in the Dry-Pockets, they reacted with the ninety-five-degree heat of the masonry. They became biological scrubbers. They absorbed the raw, corrosive hydrogen sulfide from the drafts and sweated it out as harmless, neutral water, while releasing a slow, steady micro-mist of volatile lipids that coated the Lappers' bronchial tubes like a soothing balm. 

Within half a generation, the wet, rasping cough that had plagued the elders of *Brass-Sleeve* and *Third-Sluice-East* simply faded away. Their throats remained quiet—they had no need for loud voices—but their great bellows-lungs drew the heavy steam without pain, clear and deep until the day they died.

Finally, the power looked at the Lappers’ minds. They were a practical, magnificent people, entirely occupied with *clearance* and *salt*, with the immediate geometry of the grate and the runnel. The power wished to give them a gift of pure grace—something that had no utility, something that was simply beautiful, translated into the only language they revered.

In the exact center of the twelve-mile plateau stood an ancient, dead housing of riveted steel—a regulator junction that had been bypassed by the subterranean rivers a thousand years ago. Because it had no pressure beneath it, it did not hum. The Lappers avoided it; to their feet and their bronze tuners, the dead junction felt like a hole in the world, a patch of sensory blindness.

The power reached into the buried bronze cam-shafts beneath that dead junction and re-linked them to the main geothermal turbines—not to process water, and not to dredge sludge. It turned the hollow shafts below the junction into a subterranean carillon of massive, water-driven bronze reeds, tuned to sub-audible, low-frequency harmonics.

One Slack-Hour, a young Lapper from *Nine-Valve* was skating across the central avenue on his soapstone pads, his phosphor-bronze tuner clenched between his teeth as he checked the grates for lime-choke. As he slid past the dead steel housing, he paused, dropping to his belly in the warm runoff. 

Through his clogs, through his knee-pads, and up through his jawbone, a new vibration was rising from the basalt. 

It was not the heavy, monotonous *thump-slide* of the working pistons, nor the sharp *rap-tap-rap* of a clan-mother’s knuckle-rings. It was a chord. It was a slow, shifting, polyphonic weave of deep vibrations that rose and fell in mathematical ratios matching the golden spiral, the orbital resonance of unseen planets, and the exact resting heartbeat of a sleeping Lapper. 

He pressed his tuner against the wet stone and closed his milky eyes. The vibration traveled into his mastoid bone and bloomed inside his skull—not as sound, but as an overwhelming sensation of geometric vastness, of warm safety, of intricate, interlocking perfection. It was a song made of pressure and bronze, telling him that the machine beneath him was deep, and whole, and would never stop.

Word traveled across the twelve-mile carpet at the speed of iron-tapping. 

Now, when the work of rodding the capillaries is done, and the rust-bread is baked, the Lappers skate toward the center of the Grid. They do not build temples, and they do not pray. They simply lie flat on their bellies in the hot, shallow water around the old steel housing, fifty or a hundred of them arranged like dark, diamond-patterned tiles against the grey stone, their chin-bones resting on their bronze rods, listening together to the deep, endless music of the floor.
