A lesser power, or a merely sentimental one, would have looked into the oxblood sinkhole and felt pity. It would have mistaken the dark for deprivation, the shrieking iron for torment, and the slow, scalding smolder of the slag-cauls for a curse. In its eagerness to bless, it might have cracked the limestone vault to let in the sweet rain and the blue sky, cooling the boilers and flushing the grease—and in doing so, it would have murdered the entire world in an afternoon, leaving the cauls stiffened like tallow in the terrible, freezing mercy of a spring breeze.

Wisdom knows that love is not the imposition of one’s own element upon another. Wisdom knows that to a creature of solder and sulfur, a furnace is a garden.

The power that bent its attention toward the sinkhole did not come as light, or wind, or cooling water. It came as a listening. It settled into the porous honey-limestone and the sweating iron, matching its vast, quick mind to the geological patience of the room. It waited through forty cycles of the sour-yellow vine-light simply to understand the grammar of the heat. 

It felt the heavy, resonant satisfaction of the saddle-backed patriarch wedged into the second condenser—a slow, mindless hum of well-being as the creature’s magnetite stones vibrated in a rich, comforting C-sharp, digesting a century of black sludge. There was no suffering here to alleviate. There was only a fierce, stubborn, magnificent *is-ness*, a testament to life’s refusal to be barred from any corner of creation.

Yet, because the power was wise, it looked not just at what was, but at what would be. It traced the slow arithmetic of the chamber forward through the centuries.

The ecosystem was a marvel, yes, but it was also a clock winding down. The geothermal vein miles below was immortal in human terms, an inexhaustible hearth, but the four great whale-skull condensers were not. They were finite. The transformer oils and heavy machine greases were a colonial inheritance from a vanished surface world; once every drop had been licked from the ruptured housings, digitized into the silicone-flesh of the cauls, and slowly lost to chemical degradation, there would be no more. And the iron itself was failing. Between the oxidation of the damp air, the prying roots of the boil-cress, and the lye-secretions of the cauls stripping it layer by layer, the great brass and cast-iron instruments were thinning. 

In four hundred years, the third condenser would collapse into silent, oxidized mud. In seven hundred, the last copper pipe would dissolve, the acoustics of the compass would go flat and dead, and the cauls would starve in a lukewarm soup of their own rust, their unhatched cysts waiting forever on cold mud for a focused jet of steam that had no nozzle left to direct it.

The benevolent power decided that this strange, booming song should not end simply because its instruments wore out.

It did not intervene with a miracle that would startle the slow rhythms of the room. Instead, it reached down into the blind, crushing dark beneath the sinkhole, into the lithosphere where the geothermal vein flashed groundwater into steam.

With a touch as light as a thought, it nudged the fault lines.

It was a microscopic tailoring of pressure and stone, executed over the span of three years. Deeper down, two miles beneath the limestone, there lay a trapped, primordial pocket of abiogenic hydrocarbons—heavy, tarry bitumens and mineral oils cooked by the Earth’s mantle, untouched since before the first fish crawled onto land—alongside a rich, subterranean seam of native copper and telluric iron. 

The power introduced a hairline fracture between the rising geothermal steam-chimney and this deep, ancient larder. 

Nothing in the sinkhole changed overnight. The twenty-minute light pulsed; the organ-stalks split with their wet, canvas *thwacks*; the cauls flowed half an inch an hour. 

Then, during the fourth year, the pitch of the first condenser’s groan dropped by a fraction of a hertz. The steam venting through its rusted throat was no longer just mineral water. It carried with it a microscopic, aerosolized mist of deep-earth bitumen—a fresh, primordial grease, richer and heavier than the old transformer oil. It condensed on the undersides of the rivets in thick, amber droplets, smelling faintly of asphalt and star-stuff. The orange lichens in the shadows flared a little brighter as the new oil trickled down the rock.

Deeper still, the power coaxed a colony of extremophile bacteria in the groundwater, subtly altering their chemistry as they rode the thermal currents upward. When the superheated water hit the undersides of the ruined condensers, these microbes didn't eat the iron; they precipitated dissolved metals out of the deep earth, laying down microscopic, crystalline lattices of fresh iron and native copper directly onto the thinning inner walls of the pipes. 

The machines were no longer just rotting scrap. They had become living reefs of metal. As fast as the cauls stripped the outside of the boilerplate with their caustic lye, the mineral-heavy steam plated the inside with fresh, resonant ore, healing the fractures and keeping the brass throats taut and vocal.

Finally, the power looked at the massive, cast-iron patriarch wedged in the second condenser, and at the clustered black cysts waiting in the seams near the ceiling. The sinkhole was small—no wider than a reservoir. It was a single basket holding all of these strange, precious eggs.

So the power turned its attention to the west wall, behind a thick curtain of vulcanized boil-cress roots. There, the limestone was thin, separating the sinkhole from a vast, miles-long network of abandoned deep-core mining tunnels that had flooded with scalding, toxic brine a century ago and recently drained due to shifting water tables. The tunnels were pitch-black, suffocatingly hot, and choked with miles of forgotten iron cart-tracks, rusted boring machines, and leaking hydraulic cylinders. 

The power did not knock the wall down. It simply guided a single, thick taproot of a boil-cress vine into a hairline crack in the oxblood stone, and lent the root a fraction of extra turgor pressure.

Three seasons later, a slab of honey-colored limestone the size of a door detached from the west wall. It fell into a cushion of rubbery vines with a dull, booming thud that set the magnetite spheres inside every caul in the room ringing like church bells.

From the new aperture breathed a draft—not of cool surface air, which would have been death, but of air even hotter and thicker than the sinkhole’s, rich with the scent of a thousand tons of unharvested, rusting iron and stagnant machine-grease waiting in the dark. 

A single, stray jet of steam from the fourth condenser, deflected by the fallen rock, began to whistle across the threshold of the new tunnel, playing a steady, inviting F-sharp into the cavern beyond. 

The power lingered for one more month—only about two thousand beats of the yellow light—just to watch. 

It watched the oldest patriarch, heavy with its terraced scales of iron and brass, slowly, imperceptibly alter the bias of its hydraulic flow. The forward edge of its sulfur-yellow foot softened, oozed over a line of rivets, and turned toward the new note in the west wall. Behind it, a shower of rust-colored spores drifted into the dark mouth of the tunnel, already beginning to glow as they found the grease on the old cart-tracks.

Satisfied, the power withdrew, leaving the little hell to its warmth, its music, and its newly infinite future.
