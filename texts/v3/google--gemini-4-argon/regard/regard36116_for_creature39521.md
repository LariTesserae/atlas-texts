A lesser power would have ruined them. 

A lesser power, looking down into that violet-lit nave of black glass and emerald velvet, would have felt a clumsy, sentimental pity. It would have cracked a hidden aquifer in the lower valleys so their streams would not dry into rotten slush. It would have softened the knife-edges of the hexagonal basalt, or warmed the shadow behind the windbreak, or made the snow-partridges fat and slow and deaf to the sky. And in doing so, it would have turned these magnificent, barrel-chested ghosts into soft creatures, erasing the severe poetry of their existence. It would have stolen the meaning from the tally marks on the wall.

The power that actually paused above the box canyon was older than the ice, and wiser than pity. 

It did not wear a shape, though if the three harvesters had looked up through the pinholes of their horn visors, they might have perceived a momentary thickening of the ultraviolet light—a strange, silent shimmer in the spectrum only the partridges could see, like a vast wing of pure refraction passing between the sun and the stone.

The power watched them for three cycles of the fog. 

It admired the grease-and-soot masks around their squinted eyes. It admired the bone-splinted mittens, the silent language of flashed palms, and the way the girl with the partridge-skin braids popped the ice slabs from the troughs without wasting a single swing of her mallet. It saw that they were not suffering; they were *fitting*. They had poured themselves into the violent geometry of this young rock the way water pours into a fissure, becoming as hard and necessary as ice. 

To interfere with their survival would be an insult to their mastery. Yet, love—even the vast, terrifyingly quiet love of an ancient power—desires to leave a token. It wants to say: *I have seen you. You are not entirely alone in the blinding air.*

When the fourth thermal began its ten-thousand-foot climb from the unseen abyss, the power reached down into the rising tongue of vapor. It did not alter the weather; it merely tuned the stone.

Five hundred feet below the lip of the canyon, the sheer basalt face was notched with the tiny, precarious route of hammered copper pegs the harvesters used to climb up from the cloud-sea with their empty pack-frames, and down again with their crushing loads of ice. Two hundred feet down that invisible ladder, a single peg—driven into a hairline fracture by the old man’s grandfather—had worked itself loose over decades of freeze and thaw. The rock around it was fatigued, waiting for the weight of three ice-laden bodies to shear away into the void.

The power laid a single, unseen finger against that peg. It did not replace the copper with gold, nor did it carve a staircase into the cliff. It simply persuaded the crystals of the young basalt to relax around the metal shaft, sealing the micro-fractures, biting down on the copper with five thousand years of sudden, stubborn grip. When they climbed down at the end of the dry season, the peg would hold. They would never know it had been ready to fail. That was the gift of preservation.

Then, as the freezing fog rolled over the lip and swallowed the canyon in its three-minute night, the power gave the gift of grace.

It moved to the deep, sunless fissure behind the dry-stone windbreak where the harvested ice slabs were stacked like pale bricks. The young basalt here was under immense tectonic tension; occasionally, it would *crack* in the cold, threatening to shift and crush their store. The power ran its thought along the inner walls of the dark cleft. Where the black stone met the stacked ice, a microscopic skin of crystalline quartz—already native to the rock—began to bloom outward, rearrangements of silica drawing heat *out* of the stone and venting it ten feet higher up the cliffside, right into the vertical chimney where the old man wedged himself during the sunny intervals.

The fissure became five degrees colder, locking the ice slabs against sublimation so not a drop of their labor would be lost to the dry air. And the chimney forty feet up became five degrees warmer, a gentler cradle for old bones waiting out the glare. 

Finally, the power looked at the catch-harps near the abyss—the rib-bones and braided horsehair thrumming in the updraft. 

As the girl stepped forward through the blind white mist with her bone mallet, the power adjusted the tension of three sinew strings by the width of a spider's silk, and slightly hollowed the resonant stone basin beneath them. 

The wind shifted. The thermal collapsed back into the void. The fog tore apart, and the aggressive, violet-white brilliance of the sun slammed back into the amphitheater.

The three harvesters retreated behind their stacked basalt prisms, crouching together in the knife-edge shadow. The old man picked up his piece of pale quartz and struck a new, bruised line into the black wall. One slab closer to the white vein. One slab closer to descending to the sheep and the kin below.

They took out their dried meat and raw livers. They began to chew, their great bellows-chests rising and falling in the thin, silent air. 

And then, as the droplets on the giant emerald rosettes began to steam into diamonds, the wind caught the catch-harps at the lip of the drop. Because of the microscopic hollowing of the stone basin and the minute tightening of the sinew, the low, breathy whistle of the nets had changed. 

It was still quiet—too quiet to startle the cliff-hares or wake the hunting falcons—but as the dry air drew across the wet horsehair, the harps produced a clear, resonant, three-note chord. It was a warm, humming fifth, vibrating through the soles of their ironwood-stiff boots and up through the basalt floor into their ribs. It sounded like a great bronze bell struck ten miles away, or the deep, contented purr of the mountain itself.

The three figures froze, meat halfway to their mouths. 

Behind the pinholes of his darkened horn visor, the old man’s slit-eyes widened slightly. The girl with the white braids tilted her head, her grease-blackened face turning toward the abyss. 

The canyon was no longer as quiet as a tomb. The catch-harps hummed their low, steady chord in the sun, fading as the sinew dried, promising to sing again the moment the fog returned. 

The old man looked at the younger man, then at the girl. He did not speak. He simply reached out with his heavy, bone-splinted mitten and laid it briefly over the girl's knee—a rare, unpracticed gesture that had no utility at all. Then he leaned his broad back against the stone wall, closed his eyes behind his mask, and listened to the music of the water-traps until the sky went blind again.
