A lesser power, looking down into the sunken bowl of the Ossuary, might have felt pity. It might have seen the cramped dark, the calcified joints of the elders, the endless, Sisyphean labor of propping up a ruin with no mortar, and decided to heal them. It might have widened the alleys, or brought down the sun, or granted the flat-bodied masons the spark of restless, questioning invention so they could climb out onto the upper flats and build towers.

The power that actually found them was wiser than that. 

It did not come as a voice, or a vision, or a radiant avatar, for the masons had no words, rarely looked up, and understood the world entirely through weight, heat, and vibration. Instead, the power descended into the forty-foot basin as a long, silent attention. It threaded itself through the hip-wide slots. It settled into the pores of the sweating terracotta. It felt the sixty-pound load resting on a living caryatid’s spine, and it listened to the *tink-tink-tink* of the black nails against the flues.

It saw that the Ossuary was not a prison, and the masons were not cursed. They were a masterpiece of congruence. To change their nature would be to shatter the lock and the key at the same time.

Yet, with a sight that stretched across millennia, the power saw two slow tragedies creeping toward the basin. 

The first was the entropy of the clay. Mudbrick, no matter how well-baked, eventually yields to thermal damp and the crashing deadfalls of the hunt. Slate delaminates and turns to shale-dust; clay tally-tokens crush under heel and claw until they are only slick silt. In three hundred years, there would be no wedges left sharp enough to bite, and the forty-yard bowl would slump into a smooth, suffocating porridge of mud.

The second was the hemp. The ancient cords that held the clattering draft-weights—and served as the tripwires for the gecko-traps—were rotting away, fiber by fiber, with no one left on the upper flats to weave more.

So the power acted. It did not break the rules of the basin; it merely deepened them.

First, it sank its awareness forty feet below the tamped-sherd floor, down into the dark bedrock where the thermal waters ran. With a gesture like a potter kneading dough, it shifted the deep subterranean faults. It directed the scalding water through a forgotten vein of rich, kaolin clay and dissolved iron, and then it altered the pressure of the vents.

Three days later, in the deepest, unused cistern at the bottom of the bowl—a place too hot and choked with steam for even the crabs—the mud began to boil. 

It did not erupt. It pulsed, like a slow heart. Over the course of a month, the geyser coughed up heavy, fist-sized globules of dense, iron-rich clay into the dry upper chambers of the cistern, where the furious updrafts baked them for weeks in the dark. When the vent finally subsided, it left behind a fresh scree slope of thousands of new, brick-hard nodules—rough, asymmetrical, and bitingly sharp at the edges, waiting in the gloom to be sorted by four-thumbed hands. The heart of the earth would do this once every fifty years now, an endless, slow-beating kiln to replace what time ground into dust.

Next, the power turned its attention to the elephant-hide vines. 

As the next shaft of midday sun lanced through the upper grates, the power breathed a quiet instruction into the dusty roots wedged between the stones. The vines did not bloom or burst into leaf—that would have ruined the drafts. Instead, along their lower, leafless lengths, the thick gray bark began to split horizontally, peeling back to reveal long, weeping strands of inner bast. 

When the warm updrafts hit this new bast, the fibers dried and twisted upon themselves into tough, sinewy cords the thickness of twine, hanging loose from the woody knuckles like shed snake-skins. 

A young mason, chimneying through a middle alley five feet off the ground, paused. It cocked its ironwood head at a sharp, bird-like angle, fixing one dark-amber eye on the peeling vine. It reached out with a massive, paddle-like hand, pinched a loose strand between two opposing thumbs, and pulled. The fiber stripped away with a dry, satisfying *zip*. The mason tested the tension between its hands. It did not wonder why the vine had changed; it only knew that a thirty-pound mudbrick two rooms over was waiting for a trigger.

Finally, the power looked at the elders—both those whose joints were stiffening in the damp, and those who had already locked themselves forever into the lower vaults to hold up the world.

It could not take away their calcification without unmaking the very biology that allowed them to fuse with the walls in death. If they died soft, the lower levels would collapse within a generation. The sacrifice was structural; it was the final, proudest masonry they performed.

So the power gave them a gentler mortar.

Into the blind, walnut-sized crabs, the power wove a slight metabolic shift. The crabs still grazed on the waxy, tallow-scented sebum of the masons’ back-plates, their pale claws clicking softly in the dark. But now, as they grazed on the eldest, slowest residents—the ones whose shoulders could barely fold—the crabs left behind a microscopic film of trace minerals and a mild, soothing alkaloid in their saliva. 

It did not stop the bone from stiffening. By eighty, an elder would still feel the deep, unmistakable call to descend into the lowest breaches, to find a cracking ironwood beam and brace its plated back against the ceiling one last time. 

Look there, in the deepest vault, where the light never reaches and the steam is thick as broth. 

An old one has just locked its knees into the silt floor, taking the immense, crushing load of a fractured corbelled arch onto the crown of its skull and the flat blade of its shoulders. Its hinged collarbones settle into their final, rigid geometry. 

In the old days, the last weeks of this vigil were a slow, grinding agony of pinched nerves and starving muscle, endured in stubborn, animal silence. 

Now, as the elder locks its frame into the breach, the crabs come in their hundreds, drawn by the cooling skin. Their rasping carapaces sound like a warm rain of gravel. As they climb over the wedged body, the alkaloid seeps through the calloused creases of the adobe skin. The pain of the locked joints dissolves into a heavy, golden numbness. 

More than that: the mineral film left by the crabs subtly alters the acoustic resonance of the elder’s calcifying bones. 

As the old mason’s eyes close for the last time, its skeleton begins to conduct the vibrations of the basin with crystal clarity. Dying is no longer a fading into the dark. Instead, as the mason turns to stone, its awareness widens into the walls. It feels the *plip-plock* of the condensation thirty feet above as a cool rhythm on its own brow. It feels the *shuck-shuck-shuck* of the young ones sorting the new clay nodules in the deep cistern. It feels the distant *tink-tink* of a finger on a flue pipe, and the warm, booming rush of the afternoon gale sweeping through the upper alcoves. 

The elder does not die; it simply finishes becoming the house.

Having done this, the benevolent power withdrew from the Ossuary. It left no monument, no shrine, no name for the creatures to tap out on the pipes. It simply paused for a moment at the rim of the upper flats, listening to the great, thriving clatter of the bowl—the roar of the shells, the clack of the weights, the booming of the geckos, and the deep, resonant hum of a thousand living and dead shoulders holding up the sky—and saw that it was good.
