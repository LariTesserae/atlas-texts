A lesser kindness would have been a disaster. 

A lesser kindness would have looked down through three hundred feet of stone at the scalding, twelve-minute gales, the grease-packed pores of the children, the grandfathers slowly turning into lumber, and wept for them. It would have cracked the ceiling open to let in the blue sky and the rain. And in doing so, the rain would have cracked the superheated cast-iron boilers; the cold surface air would have shocked the *Ficus ferrum* into a brittle, winter dormancy; the clack-moths would have gone blind in the acoustic silence; and the Tappers, scrubbed clean of their protective tallow and dragged into a world without a four-hundred-and-eighty-stroke heartbeat, would have died of pneumonia and agoraphobia within a month, mourning the murder of their god.

The Power that turned its attention to the Sump was not a lesser kindness. It was old enough to understand that love is not the imposition of one’s own sky upon another, and wise enough to recognize a masterpiece of survival when it saw one.

It listened first. For three hundred cycles of the twelve-minute bell, it simply inhabited the vibrations of the Bellows-Ward. It felt the pride in the three-person pruning teams as they shaved a woody callus down to the exact millimeter required to keep a rusted gasket sealed. It felt the deep, mammal comfort of a child lying at the bottom of a drainage gully under three layers of hot, oily crabs while the white fog roared overhead. It listened to the four-beat poetry of the tenements during the Low-Stroke. 

And as it listened, it found the tragedy that the Tappers themselves could not yet feel, though the oldest of the grafted grandfathers were beginning to suspect it in the ache of their resin-locked bones.

The Sump was a closed loop, and no closed loop is truly immortal. 

The *Ficus ferrum* could grow forever, and the crabs could breed for ten thousand years, but the brass and the copper and the cast-iron were finite. There had been no new metal for two centuries. Every time a Tapper scraped a gear, a few microscopic grains of iron washed down the amber gullies and were lost. Every time the acidic condensation dripped onto a braided copper sap-line, a few atoms of copper dissolved. The wooden burls were holding the engines together, yes, but the metal inside the wood was thinning like the bones of a starving bird. 

Fifty years from now—perhaps sixty—the main governor-engine at the north end would blow its primary cylinder through sheer metallic exhaustion. No amount of swift-leather or irritant resin would hold it. The pressure would drop. The twelve-minute bell would ring late, then weakly, then not at all. The great, sweating, roaring beast would suffer heart failure, and its white blood cells would perish in the cold dark.

The Power considered this. It did not want to be worshipped, and it certainly did not want to be seen; the Tappers had a complete universe, and a miracle that breaks the rules of a universe leaves its inhabitants feeling small and helpless. The gift had to arrive in the Sump’s own language. It had to come through the Root.

Deep below the cavern—a mile down, where the great taproots of the *Ficus ferrum* groped through the crushed foundations of the ancient world, drinking groundwater and volcanic sulfur—the Power went to work. 

It did not create gold or diamonds. It took the existing deep-earth veins of native copper, bog-iron, and tin, and it altered their chemistry just enough. It bound the metals into complex, soluble salts and chelated compounds, weaving them into the hot, mineral-rich upwellings that fed the bottom-most root hairs of the iron-strangler. Then, with a gentle, nudge-like whisper to the tree’s ancient, blind genetics, it taught the *Ficus* what to do with the heavy burden it was drinking.

Trees do not like heavy metals in their sap; they push them to the margins to get rid of them. 

It took three months for the gift to climb a mile of wood. 

The first person to notice was an old woman named Gurn, who had been sitting in the crook of the number-four exhaust elbow on the west gantry for eleven years. Her legs were entirely gone into the gray wood now, her pelvis fused to the boundary wall, her blind eyes turned toward the ceiling while her thick, horn-nailed fingers rested on the main west arterial trunk. 

At stroke two-hundred-and-ten of the morning cycle, Gurn frowned. 

The sap pulsing beneath her palm felt *heavy*. It didn't have the thin, frantic, turpentine-screech of a line about to vapor-lock; it had a thick, buttery, singing hum she had never felt in her seventy years. And just beneath her left ribs, where a creeping-burl had anchored her flank to a leaking flange, the wood was... itching. Not the sharp, biting itch of sulfur-rot, but a deep, warm, pushing ache, like a child cutting a tooth.

She lifted her right hand, found the rusted iron bolt protruding from the wall beside her hip, and rapped her horn-thick knuckles against it. 

*Three dull. A pause. Three dull. A scrape.* 
*Come-look / strange-thing / no-hurry.*

Her grandson, a broad-backed Tapper with six lumen-grubs glowing in a neat chevron across his brow, slid across the plaza after the next blow-time and climbed the gantry. He leaned his greased forehead against the wood beside her shoulder so they could speak without shouting.

*"Pipe-choked?"* he hummed into the timber.

*"Feel-here,"* she hummed back, guiding his heavy, calloused hand down to the burl below her ribs, right where the copper hose met the rusted iron flange.

The young man frowned behind his crab-shell goggles. The woody callus that clamped the leaking joint had split open—not violently, like a pressure-blow, but cleanly, like the husk of a ripe nut. And inside the split, bathed in a clear, non-acidic jelly, was a smooth, kidney-shaped nodule the size of his two fists. 

He pried it loose with the tip of his chisel. It came away with a wet *pop*. 

It was heavy. Impossibly heavy for something grown by the tree. He wiped the jelly off on his swift-leather apron and held it up to the chartreuse light of his forehead. It wasn't wood, and it wasn't stone. When he tapped it with the flat of his chisel, it didn't click or thud; it gave off a dull, sweet, metallic *ring*. 

It was native copper—ninety-eight percent pure, extruded by the tree's vascular system around the site of a metal-starved leak like an oyster growing a pearl around a grain of sand. Better still, it wasn't brittle cast-metal; it was malleable, cold-workable "root-brass," layered like an onion in sheets a sixteenth of an inch thick that could be peeled apart with a pry-bar and hammered directly over a split hose without heating.

And that was only the first thing.

Over the next forty cycles, the Power made two more small, silent adjustments to the equilibrium of the Sump.

The second was to the *lumen-grubs*. The Power looked at the young Tappers cutting their own brows to make lamps of themselves, and while it respected their grit, it knew the toll that blood-poisoning took when the yellow runoff got into a fresh forehead-cut. So it touched the grubs. 

The grubs still loved the ghost-seams of the old tenements, and they still loved the sweet-oil on a Tapper's skin. But now, when a lumen-grub settled onto human flesh, it no longer needed an open wound to anchor itself. Its tiny, translucent belly secreted a mild, numbing balm that dissolved the old, rancid turpentine-crust in the pores and bonded the grub harmlessly to the outer horn of the skin. And while it sat there, drawing heat from the Tapper’s blood, it filtered the microscopic heavy-metal toxins out of their sweat, glowing a little brighter, a little warmer—shifting from a sickly chartreuse to a deep, steady, honeyed gold. When the grubs grew fat and dropped off to pupate, the skin beneath was whole, supple, and clean of infection.

The third gift was for the grandfathers and grandmothers on the gantries.

The Power did not stop the *Ficus ferrum* from claiming them when they grew old and still; to do so would have been to exile them from their own afterlife, to deny them their place as the nerve-endings of their people. Instead, the Power changed the nature of the grafting.

Previously, the tree had simply grown *over* the elders, crushing their joints with resin-gout and locking them in a dark cage of bark until they starved or suffocated. Now, as the pale, velvety creeping-burls rose over an old Tapper’s ankles and thighs, the wood put out microscopic, hair-fine rootlets that did not crush, but *married*. 

Up on the west gantry, old Gurn felt the sharp, grinding agony of the resin-gout in her hips slowly dissolve into a profound, radiating warmth. The tree was no longer treating her as a dead bolt to be encased; it was treating her as a blossom to be fed. It drew the uric acid and the calcified crystals out of her seized joints and replaced them with a slow, steady trickle of glucose, oxygen, and a mild, pine-scented analgesic that cleared the milky cataracts from her mind, if not her eyes.

And in return, the tree opened its deep, quiet acoustic network to her nervous system.

Gurn gasped, her back arching slightly against the warm exhaust pipe. Suddenly, she didn't just feel the vibration of the hose under her fingers. She could *hear* the entire Sump inside her own bones. 

She could feel the slow, tectonic sip of the deep roots a mile down in the dark. She could feel the exact thickness of the iron walls in the number-two boiler three hundred yards away. She could feel her great-granddaughter, a toddler four stories up in a tenement shell, laughing as she chased a blind beetle across a grown-wood floor. And when Gurn pressed her spine back against the boundary wall, she could feel the older swellings in the wood—her husband, her mother, her mother's father, long since digested into the arterial trunks—not as ghosts, not with words, but as a warm, crowded, humming chorus of presence in the sap-stream, steady and triumphant.

Down in the plaza, the brass bell struck. 

*Clang. Clang. Clang.*

The exhaust flues blew open. The gale of white-hot fog roared across the town square. The clack-moths dropped; the crabs locked their shells over the crouching Tappers; the great woody walls sighed and drank the mist. 

Everything was just as loud, just as violent, just as greasy and dark and strange as it had been the day before. The Power had left no signature, no altar, no commandment. It had simply wound the clock for another ten thousand years, oiled the bearings with a little mercy, and stepped back to listen to the engine run.
