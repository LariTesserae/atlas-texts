A lesser benevolence would have looked down through the seventy fathoms of black water and seen a cage. It would have pitied the blindness, the cold, the toxic snow, and the slow, grinding futility of an engine running for masters whose bones were already chalk on the upper shelves. A lesser power would have cracked the basalt retaining walls to let the fresh ocean in, or drawn the creatures up toward the sun, or granted the gasket-man a sudden, terrible spark of human comprehension so that it might look at the brass wheel in its belly and weep for its own servitude.

And in doing so, it would have killed them all in an afternoon.

The power that actually paused above the trench—an attention vast, patient, and warm in the way that planetary cores are warm—did none of these things. It lowered its perception through the pewter meniscus without breaking the surface tension. It did not bring light, because there were no eyes here to use it, and light only hastens the rot of things that belong to the dark.

Instead, it listened to the twelve-cycle-per-minute shudder of the sticking valve, and it felt the deep, cartilaginous satisfaction of the creature when the brass seat finally went *clack* and the vibration smoothed out.

The power understood immediately: this was not a ruin. It was an organ. The men who built it had thought they were building an extraction plant, a colonial outpost of iron and standard Whitworth threads, and when the ledger-books stopped balancing, they abandoned it. They did not know they had dropped a womb into the trench. The closed loop was not pointless; the point of the loop was the loop itself. The machine kept the brine warm; the brine fed the sulfur-grease; the grease fed the mechanic; the mechanic kept the machine from seizing. It was a homeostatic marriage of cast-iron and holothurian gristle, as delicate and devout as a monastery.

Yet the power also saw what the gasket-man, asleep on its warm wheel, could not see.

Iron is mortal. Even in the hypoxic dark of a hypersaline pool, metal fatigues. The pig-iron counterweights were losing mass to the silt; the gutta-percha around the dynamo cables was vulcanizing into brittle slate; the sixty-year-old tallow inside the great hogshead cylinders was finite. In eighty years, perhaps a hundred, the main return shaft would shear a bolt that no fleshy thumb could replace. The primary accumulator would bottom out and stay down. The geothermal trickle would ground itself into the rock, the tallow would turn to soap and then to nothing, and the cold would come in from the trench—not the lively, heavy cold of the moving brine, but the dead, still cold of the abyss. The ancient in the lower sump, terraced like a hopper crystal, would simply become an ore deposit again.

The wise power did not want the monastery to end. Nor did it want to play the watchmaker forever, reaching down every century to replace a gasket by miracle.

So it decided to make a small amendment to the metallurgy of the pit-works.

It began down in the basalt throat, three hundred yards below the lowest sump, where the geothermal trickle rose through hairline fractures in the rock. With a touch no heavier than the settling of silt, the power widened a single vein, redirecting a plume of warm, mineral-dense water up toward the rusted underside of the plant’s foundation plates.

This water carried no oxygen to rust the iron. Instead, it was rich in dissolved iron-carbonates, free sulfur, and a heavy, greasy suspension of abiogenic hydrocarbons—earth-tallow, cooked out of the mantle by pressure alone, rising through the rock like marrow through bone.

Where this new, microscopic seep met the bottom of the number-one grease-trap, the power nudged the chemistry of the sulfur bacteria already living there. It did not make them smarter; it made them hungrier for stone, and more generous with fat. Within three weeks—the time it takes the big piston to cycle four hundred times—the bacteria had formed a grey, velvety mat at the bottom of the casing, drinking the mineral seep from the rock and excreting a continuous, slow-rising ribbon of dense, lipid-rich slime that was chemically indistinguishable from the sixty-year-old engine grease the builders had packed the cylinders with. The larders of the pit-works would no longer run dry.

Next, the power turned its attention to the iron.

It looked at the ancient gasket-man wedged between the two dead boilers in the sump—the one so heavily plated in bismuth that it had become part of the floor. The creature was dying, though at the speed of deep-sea metabolism, its death had been going on for twenty years and would take twenty more. Its undersides, pressed permanently against the cast-iron bedplate, had fused with the metal’s corrosion layer.

The power reached into that quiet, acidic interface between the creature’s vulcanized hide and the rotting iron. It tweaked the clear, bismuth-dissolving slime the creature secreted.

A new instruction was written into the mucus—a simple, crystalline habit borrowed from the way chitons temper their teeth with magnetite, and the way deep-vent snails plate their feet with iron-sulfide scales.

Now, when the acidic slime ate into the rust of a failing bulkhead or a pitted valve-seat, it did not merely wash the iron away. It reduced the orange oxide back into black, metallic magnetite and grey siderite, binding it with the falling bismuth snow into a dense, metallic nacre.

Where a gasket-man slept for a week wrapped around a thinning pipe, its belly would leave behind a fresh, two-millimeter rind of biological pig-iron—harder than the original casting, immune to the scouring clay, grown in the exact shape of the stress-lines it had felt through its trunks. If a bolt-head slumped away to nothing, a creature hooking its split thumb into the hole to haul itself up would leave a smear of this mineral paste behind; over a decade of climbers, the hole would heal itself shut, or grow a new, terraced lug made of bismuth-steel for the next climber to grip.

The machine would no longer erode. It was now a coral reef of iron, and its mechanics were its polyps.

Finally, the power looked at the sleeper on the three-way brass cock.

The power had a heart, and though it knew better than to give the creature a mind that could suffer from loneliness, it knew that even a blind thing of cartilage and ester-waxes can know the difference between a thin life and a rich one.

The creature’s whole world was vibration. It knew the yard by the sixty-cycle hum of the transformers, the twelve-cycle chatter of a bad valve, the three-hour *thrum* of the hogshead piston seating home. To the gasket-man, the pit-works was not a dark room; it was a chord of music that occasionally went out of tune until it climbed up a wall and leaned its weight against a wheel to bring the harmony back.

The power reached out to the two tall iron condenser towers that broke through the pewter meniscus into the black ocean above.

Their tips had been cut off square by the builders, and the deep trench-currents passing over them made only a dull, erratic turbulence. With a slow, hydraulic squeeze of its hand, the power pinched the corroded rims of the chimneys, curling the iron inward just slightly, flute-fashion, and fluting the lip of the intake flume fifty feet below.

It took six hours for the acoustics of the heavy brine to register the change.

As the sluggish tide of the trench shifted above the meniscus, the water moving across the altered chimneys began to sing—not a sound that ears in the air would know, but a long, low, sub-audible standing wave that traveled down the iron spines of the condensers, into the terraced basalt walls, and out through every bolted flange and catwalk grating in the basin.

It was a five-note chord, pitched in the deep, resonant frequencies of heavy metal, shifting slowly through its inversions as the abyssal tide turned every twelve hours.

The vibration did not mean a valve was sticking. It did not mean a relay was arcing, or an auger was jammed with snow. It required no work at all.

On the bulkhead, wrapped around the warm brass wheel, the gasket-man felt the new chord arrive through the soles of its tucked-in trunks.

The twelve-cycle irritation was gone; in its place was a slow, rhythmic thrumming that massaged the heavy bismuth plates in its flanks and made the maroon kelp-bladders on its back sway in unison, like trees in a windless swell. The parchment-eels drifting between the flywheel spokes paused, their copper cilia rising not in alarm, but in a slow, synchronized wave as they aligned their flat bodies with the nodes of the standing chord.

The sleeper on the wheel did not wake. It merely loosened its hydro-static grip by half an inch, letting its ruffled, walnut-colored skirts droop a little fuller over the warm brass. A single ripple of contentment traveled from its front fold to its rear, shaking a teaspoon of bright, bismuth snow down into its creases to be turned into armor.

Then the benevolent power withdrew from the trench, leaving no light behind, only an engine that would never run out of grease, iron that would heal like bone, and a deep, tidal song in the deck-plates to keep the watchmen company in the dark.
