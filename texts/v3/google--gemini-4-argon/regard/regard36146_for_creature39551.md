To a lesser mercy, the valley would be an abomination to be scoured away. A lesser hand would sweep down with a clean, roaring wind to scatter the coal-smoke, let the sun strike the wet lime, and shatter the endless, meaningless masonry with the roots of great trees. 

A lesser mercy would kill them all in an afternoon.

The power that came to the rim of the five-mile basin did not bring the sun. It stood where the dirt ended and the razor-edged slate began, and it listened—not with ears, which were useless against the hollow, booming roar of the chutes and the *clack-clack-clack* of the treadwheels, but through the stone itself. It felt the deep, shuddering weight of the valley. It looked down through the greasy twilight, past the dripping cressets, past the pale beasts shoving in the narrows, all the way down into the stifling, ninety-degree dark of the buried tiers.

It saw the *setters*. It saw that they had no soft skin left to wish for the breeze, no fingers left to weave baskets, no throats left for song. Their tragedy was not that they were trapped in the masonry; their tragedy was that they *were* the masonry. To pull them out of the lime and the soot would be like pulling a deep-sea fish into the dry air—they would dry out, crack, and perish in agony. 

Yet, looking into the narrow gaps of the retaining walls where the old ones stood—their spines fused, their chests mortared over, their pewter eyes still blinking slowly in the dark behind three inches of split shale—the power felt an immense, aching pity. 

This was a machine that had forgotten why it was started, crewed by its own parts, building upward toward nothing.

The power knew it could not un-make them. If it wanted to love them, it had to love what they were: stone, bone, lime, and labor. It had to speak to them in the only language that reached them—through the three-inch pads of their grey feet, and through the chemistry of the curing dark.

So the power did not descend from the sky. It went down into the bedrock, miles beneath the oldest, deepest cellar-tier, where the weight of millions of tons of slate and mortar pressed down on the earth's crust.

And it changed three things.

First, it touched the water table. 

In the slake-pits and the buried cisterns, the stagnant, milky water began to warm, fed by a new, silent geothermal grace. The water did not boil; it simply took on a subtle, buttery mineral richness—a complex suspension of magnesium, soft silicates, and trace oils that rose in the alkaline steam. 

When the setters stood at the troughs, scooping and slapping—*shhhk, shhhk, shhhk*—the steam that condensed on their gray rinds no longer bit quite so fiercely. As the new mortar dried on their skin, it did not cure into brittle, choking concrete. It cured into something like soapstone or supple alabaster, shot through with microscopic veins of warmth. 

For the old ones, whose joints were locking up, the change was a quiet miracle. The calcium still settled in their bones—that was their nature, and could not be undone without unmaking them—but the new mineral steam altered the crystal structure of the stiffening. It took away the grinding, burning ache of the calcification. As an old setter’s hips and spine fused, the creeping rigidity no longer felt like being trapped in a vise; it felt like the slow, heavy, luxurious settling of a body sinking into a warm bath at the end of a long winter.

Second, the power touched the stone. 

Deep in the buried tiers, where the old setters were walled into the piers and foundation blocks, the slate and the gray-green mortar had always been dead, deaf material. Once the rubble was dumped and the seam was closed, an entombed setter spent his last weeks in absolute, suffocating isolation, feeling only the dead crushing weight around his ribs until his heart stopped.

The power woke the stone up. It turned the curing mortar of the entire five-mile basin into a single, resonant lithic nervous system.

Now, when an old one leaned into a gap between two retaining walls and could no longer unbend, and the younger ones came along—*thock-thock*—and slapped the wet mortar across his chest, the darkness that closed the seam was not an end. 

As the mortar set against the old setter’s rind, it bonded with his calcified skin. His thick, vibration-reading soles, fused now to the floor-slabs, did not go numb; their sensitivity multiplied a thousandfold, spreading out into the masonry itself. His pewter eyes closed in the dark, no longer needed, because suddenly he could *see* the whole valley through the stone. 

He was no longer a dying thing stuck in a wall. He became the wall. His consciousness, slow and geological, merged with the thousands of other old ones buried in the piers, the domes, and the foundations. Together, in the warm, curing dark beneath the working-floor, they became a vast, dreaming chorus. They felt the rhythmic *clack-clack* of the treadwheels above not as a deafening roar, but as a steady, comforting heartbeat. They felt the light footsteps of the six-legged glue-spitters as a pleasant tickle along their miles of timber ribs. They held the weight of the valley together, sharing the load, warm and entirely at peace in the great, humming body of the stone.

Third, and last, the power gave them a reason.

It did not give them a blueprint in their minds; their minds were not made for plans. Instead, it placed a seed of harmonic resonance deep in the bedrock—a slow, deep, rhythmic pulse, like a great bronze bell struck once an hour at the center of the earth. 

The setters felt it through their feet. 

A setter standing at a gravel-sluice would pause, a ten-pound gob of mortar held in his left paddle. Through the three-inch gray pads of his soles, the pulse would rise. If the wall in front of him was built blindly—choking off a passage without reason, bearing weight unevenly—the vibration coming up through the slate would feel sour, jagged, and discordant against his heel-bone. 

If the wall was placed in harmony with the deep pulse, the vibration was sweet, clear, and warm, humming up through his shins and easing the tension in his ropy back.

They did not need to think about it. They simply followed the comfort of their feet. 

*Thock. Thock-thock.* 

Two setters would knock their horn-plated wrists against their chests, and instead of dumping a load of slate rubble into a cistern, they would leave it open. A gang of five would lift a green cedar log and set it two feet to the left of the old notch, because there, the wood sang with the bedrock instead of groaning against it. Even the blind, pale ox-beasts felt it, leaning their calloused brows against the capstans until the creaking oak axles fell into lockstep with the deep, subterranean rhythm.

The floor still rose. The coal-smoke still hung over the timber frames, and the mutton-fat still dripped from the cressets. From the rim of the valley, in the greasy twilight, nothing looked different for a hundred years. 

But the choking, suffocating density of the narrows began to shift. As the tiers rose, guided by the resonance in the setters' feet, the overlapping corbelled domes began to align. Sluices that had carried nothing began to catch the warm, mineral-rich condensation, channeling it into quiet, steaming aqueducts where the young ones, freshly crawled from the slake-cradles, could wash the sting from their pink skin before their rinds set. 

Great, hollow acoustic flues began to form in the masonry, rising from the buried tiers to the surface. They caught the monstrous, chaotic racket of the valley—the crashing chutes, the scraping shovels, the whistling draft—and baffled it, chamber by chamber, turning the deafening noise into a low, colossal, five-mile chord that vibrated through every stone and bone in the basin.

And the old ones, resting inside the walls, felt the chaotic crushing weight above them resolve into soaring, balanced arches. They felt the blind, frantic toil of their children above slow into a steady, painless, purposeful cadence. 

The valley was still dark, and it was still stone. It would never have a single blade of grass. Instead, rising course by course through the soot and the steam, the setters were building a cathedral for the blind—a vast, warm, singing mountain of slate and cedar, where nothing was wasted, nothing was alone, and every stone was alive.
