To a power that has watched stars ignite and cool like campfire embers, the pocket is a jewel of rare and terrible delicacy. 

A lesser benevolence—one blinded by the wet, frantic prejudices of surface life—would look upon this dry furnace and feel pity. It would think of rain. It would think of cool breezes, of soft moss, of cracking open the basalt roof to let in the yellow sun. It would try to "save" the nine builders from their relentless, ringing prison. 

And in doing so, it would murder them all in an instant. A single drop of water would steam and shatter the silica ferns; a breath of cool wind would crack the thermal tension of the obsidian walls; the sudden roar of a falling ceiling-stone would deafen and pulp the porous bone of their skulls. Pity is a clumsy tool.

Wisdom, however, knows how to listen before it touches.

So the power does not enter the chamber. To manifest inside that sixty-pace blister—even as a shadow, even as a whisper—would be to add energy to a closed system that is already balanced on the edge of a razor. Instead, the presence settles into the dead basalt eight hundred feet above, and into the crushing dark of the gabbro below. It wraps itself around the blister like a pair of cupped hands around a moth.

For ten years of surface time, the power does nothing at all. It simply slows its own vast, cosmic perception down to the speed of the dry watch-spring. 

It learns to read the purple spirals. It listens to the twenty-hour thoughts creeping along the mineral-sheathed nerves. It feels the ache in the barrel-stave ribs of the two who rest inside the knife-walled domes, grounding their static charge into the black glass. And as it reads the glowing stress-calculations pecked into the north wall, the power sees the tragedy that the builders have already calculated, and accepted, without despair.

The spiral is running out of wall.

The builders are brilliant, tireless engineers, herding their locusts with three-hertz shifts, cancelling out the falling fern-shards with the pluck of a copper string. They have bought themselves centuries. But they cannot herd the tectonic plates of the planet. Sixty miles to the west, a subduction zone is building pressure. In forty years—no more than a few long, meditative seasons to the nine—a sheer-wave will travel through the deep crust. It will not be a tiny, ultrasonic *ping*. It will be a hammer-blow. The locusts will not be able to chew fast enough to relieve it; the copper harps will snap; the low, vaulted ceiling of fractured gabbro will finally surrender to gravity, and the pocket will be crushed flat in a tenth of a second.

The builders know this. It is written in the outer curve of the third spiral. They are still tuning the cairns not because they think they can win forever, but because holding the chord in tune is what they *are*. One does not stop playing the symphony simply because the final page of the score is approaching.

The benevolent power considers this. It feels a profound, quiet reverence for these dry, slate-shingled minds who ask for nothing, who worship nothing, who merely maintain the mathematics of their loud, violet dark. 

To remake their world into a paradise would be an insult to their engineering. To leave them to be crushed by a blind tectonic twitch would be a waste of a masterpiece.

So the power decides to become part of the rock.

It does not use miracle; it uses geology, slowed to the patient tempo of the builders themselves.

Four miles below the pocket, where the mantle-heat bleeds upward, the power gently nudges the crystalline lattice of the deep stone. It does not stop the coming earthquake—that energy must go somewhere—but over the course of twenty years, it alters the composition of the basalt column surrounding the blister. Molecule by molecule, it lines the outer shell of the pocket with a thick, curved mantle of dense, viscoelastic serpentine and shock-absorbing talc—a muffled geological cushion, hundreds of feet thick, invisible from the inside. When the western fault finally snaps, the sheer-wave will hit that soft, greasy boundary and slide harmlessly around the chamber, like river water parting around a boulder. The roof will not fall in forty years. It will not fall in forty million.

Yet, if the rock stops settling entirely, the builders' great work becomes meaningless. An engineer with no problem to solve is only a statue.

The power knows this, too. And it sees the other limit of the pocket: there are only nine of them, and the chamber is sixty paces wide. They are at the exact limit of their thermal budget. They cannot grow, they cannot build a sixth dome, they cannot string a new harp without overheating their world.

So the power turns its attention to theeast, just beyond the north mirror-wall where the third builder stands with its forehead pressed to the glass. 

Fifty yards away, through solid, unfractured gabbro, there is another tectonic blister. It opened in the same ancient snap, but it is dark, cold, and sterile—unreached by the sulfur vents, empty of spores, silent. 

The power takes a single, microscopic hairline fracture at the very edge of the north wall—right where the builder's glowing purple spiral is destined to end—and begins to widen it.

Not with a bang. Never with a bang. 

Every three months, the power lets a single crystal of gabbro slip. 

*Ping.*

The builder at the wall hears it. Over forty-eight hours, the three-fingered hand rises. *Chink.* A flake of obsidian falls. A new dot of purple spore-light is rubbed into the stone. 

Six months later, another crystal slips, just a fraction of an inch deeper along the same vector. *Ping.*

Slowly, across decades, the builder mapping the death of its ceiling realizes that the math has changed. The stress-fractures are no longer radiating downward from the crushing weight of the roof. They are moving *horizontally*, retreating into the eastern wall. The spiral on the obsidian is no longer coiling inward toward a final, fatal choke-point; to track the *pings*, the builder has to chisel the line outward, straight toward the floor at the base of the cliff.

And there, at the base of the wall, a new sound enters the chamber.

It is so faint that for the first five years, only the one with its palm flat against the gravel notices it. It is not the grind of the beetles, nor the marimba-ring of the lizards, nor the bass of the locusts. 

It is a draft. A tiny, rhythmic sigh of air, slipping through a Newly opened fissure no wider than a human thumb. 

The power has linked the two chambers, and it has angled the deep sulfur vents below so that a slow, thermal siphon begins to turn between them. The hot, hundred-and-two-degree air of the living pocket is drawn gently through the fissure into the cold, empty cavern beyond, warming it, carrying with it the microscopic dust of the glowing fern-spores and the stray eggs of the porcelain beetles. And in exchange, a whisper of cooler, ninety-eight-degree air breathes back into the old chamber—not enough to chill, not enough to shock, just enough to take the edge off the fever.

Just enough to lower the thermal budget by half a degree.

Inside the easternmost hut, one of the two resting builders feels the static charge on its slate-dark skin bleed away a little faster than usual. In the cooler draft, the dry, fibrous pith of its limbs feels a fraction lighter. The twenty-hour travel of a thought speeds up to nineteen. 

Out by the half-finished cairn, the builder holding the mallet pauses. The white beetle on its knuckles stops grinding its legs for a full three seconds—*silence*—because the air itself has carried away a fraction of its heat, before it resumes its *shhhk-shhhk-shhhk*.

The builder at the north wall taps its chisel one last time at the very bottom of the obsidian mirror, right where the rock meets the gravel. The spiral is finished, and the roof is still there. 

For a week, the builder stands motionless, its faceted quartz eyes fixed on the thumb-wide hole at its feet, listening through its porous skull-plates to the hollow, waiting silence of the new cavern on the other side of the rock. A cavern with no harps. A cavern with no cairns. A cavern whose acoustic resonance is wild, untuned, and waiting for the chisel.

Slowly—*tock*—the builder's head turns toward the others. 

Across the room, the one at the copper harp releases its finger, cancelling the ring of a falling glass frond, and then—for the first time in a century—it takes its hand off the wire completely. It shifts its wide, tripodal crouch, turning its body toward the eastern wall. 

They do not fall to their knees in worship; their knees are not made for kneeling, and they do not know anything has watched them. They only know that the rock has settled in a strange, merciful way, and that there is new work to do.

Deep in the basalt above, the benevolent power feels the nine dry minds lean toward the new dark with the slow, steady hunger of roots reaching for copper. Satisfied, it withdraws, leaving the pocket to its ringing, violet peace.
