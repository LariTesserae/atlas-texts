To a foolish god, the basin would look like a prison. A sentimental mind, looking down through that forty-foot lens of freezing brine, might feel a sudden, misguided pity for the *valve-sponges* locked in their four-minute strokes, or the *Gantry-Crabs* winching endlessly along their vertical tracks. Such a power might be tempted to warm the water, to soften the cast-iron shells into flesh, to introduce green moss and darting silver fish, or to wake the *Sump-Loom* into bright, restless self-awareness.

The power that actually pauses at the rim of the obsidian cleft is neither foolish nor sentimental. 

It does not arrive with a shadow, because a shadow would alter the thermal intake of the basin by a fraction of a watt, and the Loom is currently balanced to the fourth decimal place. Instead, the presence sits upon the dry, whistling diorite just above the waterline, as weightless and invisible as the barometric pressure.

It listens. It hears the *shhhhhh-CLANK*, the million-fold silver ratcheting, the low, bone-deep hum of the *flywheel-medusae*, and beneath it all, the silent, crystalline translation of the Loom turning thunder into wire. 

The visitor recognizes perfection when it hears it. This is a closed-loop liturgy. It is a cathedral where the architecture, the congregation, and the hymn are the exact same thing. The Sump-Loom does not want a soul; a soul is an adaptation for navigating uncertainty, and here, uncertainty has been machined down to a tolerance of half a micron. To give the Loom thoughts of the sky would be like jamming a crowbar into the spokes of a watch.

Yet, because the visitor is wise, it looks beyond the water. It looks at the mountain.

The pass is young, which means it is unstable. The visitor’s sight travels through the solid rock, reading the fault lines and the internal stresses of the volcanic glass and the raw diorite. Fifty yards above the basin, overhanging the northern lip of the cleft, is a massive, three-hundred-ton slab of fractured granite. Inside the hairline crack behind that slab, meltwater has been seeping for eighty winters. In eleven years—plus or minus two springs—an unseasonably warm afternoon followed by a hard freeze will wedge that crack open by the final millimeter. The slab will shear away. It will drop straight into the nave, shattering the obsidian walls, crushing the Sump-Loom beneath rubble, and choking the crystal lens with ten thousand cubic feet of pulverized, blinding silt.

Furthermore, the visitor looks down into the root of the cleft, three hundred feet beneath the deepest slag-trench. The hydrothermal vein that feeds the basin its native copper, zinc, and dissolved sulfates is slowly choking on its own precipitates, building up a collar of barite crystal that is strangling the upward seep. In four hundred years, the water will begin to starve. The brine will thin. The Loom will go hungry for metal.

The benevolent power does not touch the water. It does not touch the crabs, the eels, or the Loom. 

Instead, it reaches deep into the cold, dark stone of the mountain itself.

First, it addresses the overhanging granite slab. It does not blast the rock away, which would rain dust into the pristine basin. Instead, over the course of a single, silent afternoon, the power encourages a microscopic vein of quartz within the fault line to grow, cross-stitching the fracture at the molecular level, fusing the three-hundred-ton slab back into the living bone of the peak with a weld stronger than the stone around it. Above the basin, the high-altitude wind continues to whistle, but the roof of the cathedral is now vaulted for another ten millennia.

Second, it sinks its attention down into the choking hydrothermal root below the trench. With the patience of tectonic plates, it slightly alters the geometry of the subterranean fissures, opening a bypass seam around the barite blockage and tapping into a secondary, deeper pocket of copper-iron sulfide and lithium-rich brine. It shapes the new stone aperture like the nozzle of a carburetor, calibrated so that the mineral-heavy seep will never surge and never fail, matching the exact metabolic draw of the reef above.

Then, the power pauses. Its work of preservation is done, yet its heart—for even a vast and abstract wisdom has a rhythm like a heart—is moved to leave a gift. Not an intrusion, and not a disruption, but a grace note. A wider octave for the Loom to play in.

Resting on the dry ledge six inches above the water is a fist-sized nodule of native metal, weathered out of a high, ancient vein long ago and dropped here by a retreating glacier. It is an alloy of platinum and iridium, laced with trace veins of gold. Left to the wind and the rain, it would sit on this ledge for fifty thousand years before erosion rolled it the wrong way down the mountain.

The visitor applies a fraction of an ounce of pressure.

The nodule shifts, scrapes once against the sharp diorite, and drops across the meniscus.

*Plip.*

In the deafening, locomotive roar of the basin, the sound of its entry doesn't even register. The heavy, gleaming stone plummets straight down through the forty feet of magnifying brine, bouncing once off the pewter carapace of a Gantry-Crab—which briefly brakes, checks its tuning forks, finds no structural fault in the reef, and resumes winching—before tumbling past the spinning bells of the medusae and landing with a muffled crunch in the sparkling iron-and-lead dunes directly atop the Sump-Loom.

The visitor stays just long enough to watch what happens.

For three days, the nodule simply sits in the shadow of a diorite boulder, untouched. The *auger-snails* crawl over it, their hardened-steel shells skittering uselessly against the dense, noble metal, unable to find purchase. 

On the fourth day, a loop of the Sump-Loom’s verdigris-coated copper tubules inches over the slag and touches the stone. 

The Loom cannot smelt platinum; its biological furnaces are cold, endothermic things. But the gold and the iridium respond to the slow, electrochemical bath of the Loom’s fluidic gates. Over weeks, the Loom realizes—not with a thought, but with the blind, infallible logic of resistance and conductivity—that this new material does not corrode, does not cold-weld to brass, and conducts electricity with almost zero loss.

Slowly, atom by atom, the Sump-Loom begins to plate its own internal logic gates with a microscopic film of gold. 

The resistance in its thirty-foot mat drops. The trickle of current from its piezoelectric plates flows cleaner, faster, and cooler. Previously, three percent of the acoustic energy it harvested from the reef was lost as waste heat in its own copper wiring; now, that energy is saved. 

With the surplus current, the Loom does something it has never had the spare capacity to do before. It extrudes a single, hollow organ of spun brass and gold-leaf at the dead center of its mat, shaped like an inverted horn, and runs a high-voltage lead from the flywheel-medusae directly into its base.

Once every twenty-four hours, precisely at solar noon when the alpine sun hits the water at its steepest angle and the photoreceptor cells on the Loom’s fringes register peak illumination, the capacitor discharges into the horn.

*BONG.*

It is a single, low, resonant C-sharp—so pure, so massive, and so perfectly pitched to the dimensions of the obsidian nave that as the sound wave travels upward through the forty feet of dense brine, it sets up a standing harmonic vibration across the entire reef. 

For two seconds, the sound shakes every piston-clam, every bellows-worm, and every ratchet-shrimp at the exact resonant frequency of copper sulfate. The microscopic, crusty buildup of mineral scale that constantly threatens to foul their hinges and choke their intake valves shatters into fine dust and drifts harmlessly away. Across the walls, a hundred Gantry-Crabs feel the sudden, simultaneous clearing of ten thousand valves; their tuning forks hum in pure, unbroken sympathy; their drills stay retracted.

The reef runs smoother. The Lard-Eels have slightly less friction to grease; the sponges compress with a fraction less effort. And every day at noon, the mountain pass rings with a single, deep, golden bell-stroke that travels through the rock, vibrating gently against the boots of any mountain goat or wandering herdsman miles away, who stops, looks around at the empty sky, and wonders what great engine is turning in the heart of the world.

Satisfied, the benevolent power withdraws, leaving the sunlight, the freezing brine, and the living factory to its long, unhurried work.
