A lesser power, looking down upon the drowned peneplain, would have felt pity. It would have seen the bone-ash stirred into the evening broth, the children sleeping on one leg like tired herons, the agonizing slowness of the hunt, and the relentless, static roar of the frogs, and it would have judged the place broken. A lesser power would have heaved the granite upward to make dry hills, or dug channels to drain the stagnant lagoons, or cast a rich black loam across the sterile clay so that heavy-headed grains might grow. 

And in doing so, it would have murdered *those who stand*. 

For the power that came to rest in the pale, bright mirror of that sky was old enough to understand that a masterpiece of survival is as fragile as blown glass. It watched the hunters slide their cracked, grey feet through the amber water without waking a single ghost of white kaolin. It listened to the deep, sub-aqueous *voom* of the root-drums rolling beneath the metallic wall of ten million clicking throats. It saw the children dipping their bog-wood spoons into the ruby pitchers of the carnivorous plants, taking two beetles and leaving one, with the quiet courtesy of guests at a frugal table. 

The power recognized that these people were not suffering; they were *honed*. Their liturgy of slowness, their bone-to-bone hums in the apricot mist, their reading of the rust-and-plum charts beneath their shins—this was a poetry written in the margins of a finished world. To give them abundance would be to make them clumsy. To give them silence would be to deafen their bones.

Yet, the power was benevolent, and its heart was moved by one small, cruel arithmetic in their lives. 

Because the ancient rock had been leached of its marrow eons ago, the ledger of calcium and phosphorus among *those who stand* could never quite balance. Every generation, despite the careful harvesting of the dead, a tiny fraction of bone-ash was lost to the wind or the water. A rib-awl snapped and sank into the deep clay; a skull-fragment failed to burn clean; a hunter died thirty miles out and the floods dispersed his frame before his kin could gather him. Over centuries, the debt compounded. The people were growing ever so slightly more brittle. A child’s shin would bow just a fraction too much; an elder’s hip, locked in the sleeping-tripod, would ache with a thin, porous hollowness before their time. They were slowly fading into the mineral poverty of the flat.

So the power decided to act—not to change the music of the plain, but to keep the instruments from breaking. It chose to give them three things, each so small and so native to the rules of the flat that the people would never know a god had passed by, only that the world had grown slightly deeper in its grace.

First, the power reached down—not to the surface, where a disturbance would bloom the kaolin for a week, but five miles beneath the basement rock, into the warm, slumbering roots of the crust. 

With the delicacy of a surgeon, it coaxed a single, hairline fracture to open through the ancient granite beneath a wide, shallow lagoon at the center of the families' circuits. It did not summon magma or boiling sulfur; it merely allowed the warm surface water to seep down, kiss an ancient, buried pocket of limestone and apatite that time had forgotten to leach, and rise back up through the stone at the pace of a growing tree. 

The spring had no bubble, no current to stir the spherical sand. It was simply a spot, thirty yards across, where the basement rock was stained a slightly paler, milky turquoise among the plum and ochre swirls. 

Where this invisible breath of mineral-heavy water met the sun, the dark, sealskin film of cyanobacteria changed. As it grew over the seep, the algae drank the dissolved calcium and phosphorus and wove them into its own microscopic sheaths. When the people came with their stone blades to scrape the black jelly and dry it on their shoulders in the sun, they found that the wafers from the Turquoise Shield had a slightly different snap. When chewed, they had a faint, chalky sweetness beneath the taste of iron and salt. A pregnant mother eating three wafers a day from this rock found that her teeth no longer loosened, and her child was born with shins as dense and straight as bog-iron.

Second, the power looked at the long hours of the noon glare, when the people stood locked on their staffs, enduring the heat-shimmer, their eyes aching from the double sun, waiting for the mercy of the Fog-Drop.

The power turned its attention to the solitary, swollen-trunked trees spaced out like sculptures across the water. They were ancient, tough things, hoarding their own peat. The power whispered a tiny, lateral mutation into the seeds of the oldest tree on the plain. 

It did not make the trees grow closer together, which would have broken the sightlines and ruined the acoustics of the root-drums. Instead, it changed the way they died. Previously, when a dwarf-tree finally gave up after five hundred years, its trunk rotted hollow from the inside while the pale, papery bark held its shape until it collapsed into the water. Now, as the heartwood dissolved over decades, the tree secreted a heavy, amber resin into its lowest, widest roots—a resin rich in the heavy minerals drawn up from the new seep. 

When the dead tree finally fell and dissolved over fifty years, the roots left behind were not merely straight shafts for walking-sticks, but broad, curved cradles of black bog-wood, heavy as lead, shaped like shallow crescents and immune to the rot. 

More than that: the living trees began to shed their papery bark in wider, thicker ribbons during the wettest weeks, and the underside of this new bark was lined with a microscopic, silvery down that trapped air. When a hunter wove three strips of this bark across the fork of his walking-staff, or slung a crescent of the new root-wood between two trunks on a peat mound, it formed a dry, buoyant sling three feet above the water. For the first time in ten thousand years, an old man whose knees were weary did not have to lock his hip in the tripod to sleep at noon; he could lean back into a web of pale bark between two branches, suspended above the leeches and the rasping-fish, swaying in the wind like a cocoon while his joints rested completely.

Third, and last, the power considered their songs. 

It loved the way they spoke—the forehead-to-forehead hum in the orange fog, the private resonance passing from skull to skull while the frogs clicked wildly in the dark around them. It was the most intimate language in the world, for you could not lie to someone when your voice was vibrating inside their own teeth and temples. Yet, when a person died, and their skull was burned for the broth so the children could grow, their specific resonance—the particular acoustic chamber of their brow and sinus that shaped their hum—was lost forever.

The power walked—Moving slower than the hunters, slower than the creeping water—out to the edges of the white and lavender kaolin flats. Where the spherical quartz sand was purest, it gathered the stray lightning strikes of a hundred distant storms, channeling their heat not in a blinding flash, but in a silent, pinpoint thread beneath the clay. 

There, in the undisturbed silt, it fused the spherical sand and the lavender clay and the trace iron into hollow, fist-sized geodes of natural porcelain and murky amethyst glass. Each one was grown with a different internal labyrinth—spiral chambers and thin, vitrified baffles that mimicked the exact acoustic geometry of a human cranium. 

Over the years, as the water crept southward by inches, the clay parted to reveal these smooth, plum-colored stones resting on the bare granite shields. 

A girl gathering sundew-beetles found the first one. It was light enough to hang at the waist, smooth as soap, with a single small aperture at the top. When she brought it back to the peat mound at Fog-Drop, and her grandmother pressed her forehead against the warm stone and hummed, the hollow crystal caught the bone-song, amplified it, and held the overtones in a shimmering, three-second decay that traveled through the clay dome into the feet and knees of everyone sitting on the mound. 

More than that: each stone had its own voice, its own key. When the grandmother died and her bones were given back to the family's strength, her stone remained. When the girl pressed her own forehead to it in the apricot gloom and hummed the old woman’s song, the stone shaped the girl's vibration into the exact timbre and resonance of her grandmother's skull. No one was ever entirely eaten away by time again; a family’s mound accumulated a small cluster of singing-stones, a chorus of ancestors that could be held against the collarbone on lonely watches in the middle of the lagoon, humming the old rhythms of the flat straight into the heart.

Having done these three things—a silent seep of white water, a slightly broader curl of bog-root, and a handful of hollow stones—the benevolent power withdrew into the pale, bright smear of the horizon. 

It left the heat heavy and wet. It left the ten-mile mirrors unbroken. It left the frogs to their deafening, metallic joy, and the storks to their rust-colored patience. It merely watched, well-pleased, as the afternoon sun dropped, the sky condensed into a warm room of orange cloud, and across twenty miles of drowned stone, the low, chest-thumping *voom* of the root-drums called the tall people home to sit above the water and hum into the dark.
