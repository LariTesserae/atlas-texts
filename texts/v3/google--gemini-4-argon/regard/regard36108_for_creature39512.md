If a lesser power—a merely *good* power, an anxious and managerial god—were to look down into this forty-kilometer trough of freezing mud and screaming wind, it would be seized by an immediate, terrible pity. 

A lesser power would see the raw basalt slicing at bare feet, the sleet driving sideways into unblinking eyes, the old male squatting in the black silt with his hand buried in thirty-three-degree mud, and it would think: *They have fallen. They have forgotten what they were.* It would look at the obsidian needles and the matte-gray spheres, recognize the high, ancient geometry of an abandoned star-faring artifice, and conclude that these barrel-chested, grease-wrapped things in the brush were the tragic survivors of a wreck. It would want to rescue them. It would calm the gale, warm the sea, soften the rock into loam, and try to teach them how to build houses and speak with their mouths again.

And in doing so, it would blind them, deafen them, and break their hearts.

The power that actually turns its attention to the basin is not anxious. It is old enough to know that not every descent is a fall, and wise enough to recognize a cathedral when it hears one.

It does not arrive in a chariot of fire or a sudden parting of the clouds; breaking the cloud-bellies here would be like kicking a hole in the roof of a concert hall while the symphony is playing. Instead, the presence enters the valley on the valley’s own terms. It slips in as an unrecorded barometric low, five hundred miles wide, rolling off the polar pack. It arrives as a slight thickening of the salt-spray, an extra half-knot of velocity in the wind, a subtle increase in the electrical potential between the racing slate sky and the iron-rich stone below.

For three days and three nights—though "day" is only a bruised pewter brightening of the fog—the power simply listens to the Listeners.

It feels what they feel. It extends its awareness down into the flooded chunnels where the families wedge themselves like grey boulders in the mill-race, feeling the deep, narcotic comfort of the freezing water hammering against their thick skulls. It watches the young ones learning to read the standing hexagonal waves around the obsidian pillars, slapping the mud with flat, seven-jointed hands to tell jokes about the clumsy foxes. It watches the old male by the alder-ravine, who has been squatting on the same slick ledge for sixteen hours, slowly nudging basalt plates with his horn-calloused heel, patiently guiding the subterranean acoustic currents away from a fault-line that, if allowed to shatter all at once, would bury two miles of prime smelt-spawning slush-runs in sterile gravel.

The power sees the truth of them: they did not lose their technology. They *finished* it. They reached the end of metal and glass, realized that a machine you have to repair from the outside is a machine that owns you, and made the long, brave, ten-thousand-year leap into flesh and stone. They built an instrument the size of a county and then bred themselves into the fingers that play it.

They do not need saving. They are magnificent.

Yet, because the power is wise, it looks deeper than the present season. It looks down into the root-cellar of the planet, five miles beneath the fresh basalt, and it looks forward four hundred years.

And there, it finds two small, quiet tragedies waiting in the dark.

The first is the Late Summer. Every year, in August, the high-pressure ridges lock over the coast for ten or twelve days. The gale dies. The sky clears to a hard, merciless blue. The meltwater slows to a trickle, the mud bakes into gray crust, and the basin goes silent. For the *K’eł-t’a*, this is not peace; it is sensory deprivation. Without the wind to drive the nodes, without the sleet to form the sonar-lattices, without the roar of the boulders under the gray spheres, the great external mind of the valley goes dark. They spend those twelve days wedged deep in the dwindling, lukewarm trickles of the lowest chunnels, half-mad with loneliness, unable to see beyond their own skin, waiting in claustrophobic misery for the autumn storms to give them back their souls.

The second tragedy is slower. The gray spheres and the black needles are very old. They are self-repairing, drawing carbon and silicates and heavy metals up from the bedrock to mend their own molecular lattices, but they were tuned ten millennia ago to a slightly different continent. Deep beneath the trough, the great tectonic plate is shifting its weight, relieved of the billion-ton burden of the receding ice sheet. This isostatic rebound is tilting the basin by three millimeters a year. 

In four centuries, the deep harmonic alignment between the twelve obsidian needles and the underlying magma-plume will drift out of phase. The gray spheres will lose their anchor-points in the local gravity well. They will not explode; they will simply go dead, turning into ordinary, silent black glass and heavy gray metal. The valley will become just a valley. The Listeners, who have pared away their vocal cords and their tool-making thumbs to become one with the machine, will be left stranded in the mud, locked inside their own skulls forever.

The benevolent power considers this. It knows that if it reaches down and visibly fixes the machines, or if it alters the weather by fiat, it will introduce something terrible into the basin: the concept of the *Outside*. The Listeners are whole because their universe is entirely participatory; every erg of energy in their world can be felt through their toes and steered with a pry-bar of bone. To give them a miracle they cannot feel the mechanics of would be to turn them from artists into beggars.

Whatever is done must be done in the language of basalt, ice, and pressure.

So, on the fourth night, at the very peak of the tide, when the gale is howling at ninety knots and the air is a solid horizontal sheet of freezing rain, the power acts.

It does not touch the sky. It goes down into the dark, three miles beneath the roots of the dwarf spruce, where the ancient, unweathered root of the mountain meets the rising mantle. 

Wait for the next ozone-click.

Up by the alder-ravine, the old male is still squatting in the mud. Two miles away, a female with a yearling calf tucked under the heavy kelp-folds of her mantle is wading through a slush-run, her wide feet crushing ice-crusts while smelt thrash silver against her shins. 

Two seconds before the pulse, every Listener in the forty-kilometer basin tilts their head at that precise forty-five-degree angle. Their hollow brow-bristles stand up, glowing with green St. Elmo’s fire.

*Click.*

The pressure hits. The falling rain across a hundred-yard arc around every needle freezes in mid-air, hanging like ten billion beads of solid black glass in the dark.

The old male snaps his horn-plated jaws together. *Clack.* 

Across the valley, three hundred other jaws snap shut in the exact same microsecond.

The sound-wave goes out, hits the suspended lattice of frozen rain, and bounces back—not just from the surface of the valley, as it has done ten million times before, but from *all the way down*.

Because in that single microsecond, three miles beneath the valley floor, the benevolent power has opened a seam in the planetary crust and filled it with a vein of hyper-dense, resonant piezo-quartz, six miles long and shaped like the bridge of a violin.

When the reflected sonar-click comes back to the old male’s eardrums and the buried tips of his seven-jointed fingers, it does not stop at fifty feet underground. The resolution goes all the way to the mantle. 

For one thousandth of a second, the old male—and the female in the slush-run, and the children wedged in the roaring chunnels—sees the entire deep architecture of the earth beneath them. They see the slow, four-hundred-year tilt of the rising continental plate. They see the exact stress-points where their black needles are beginning to drift out of phase with the bedrock. 

And more than that: they see the new vein of resonant quartz three miles down. They feel how the deep, continuous, low-frequency hum of the planet’s own rotation—a sound that never stops, not in August, not when the wind dies, not when the sun bakes the mud—is now being caught by that subterranean bridge and piped straight up into the roots of the twelve obsidian pillars.

The microsecond ends. 

*Thwack.* The rain drops into the moss. The gale roars back in.

Around the valley, three hundred Listeners go utterly, profoundly still. Even the clear membranes over their amber eyes stop clicking. For five full minutes, while the petrels tumble out of the dark and the arctic foxes splash through the overflow at their ankles, the Silt-Heavy Ones do not move a muscle. They are digesting a single, massive chord of geological information that just rewrote their future.

By the alder-ravine, the old male slowly pulls his left hand out of the freezing mud. The white lichen-scars on his knuckles are trembling slightly—not from cold, which he cannot feel, but from the sheer, staggering scale of the music he has just heard.

He understands what is down there now. He does not think of a god; he thinks of the mountain. He thinks the basin has finally grown deep enough to strike a new root. He knows that next August, when the terrible blue sky comes and the wind dies, the needles will not go silent. They will hum with the deep, slow, bass-note of the planet’s own crust grinding against the sea-floor, a summer-song dark and cool enough to keep their minds wide open while they sleep in the warm trickles.

And he sees—with the calm, hydraulic certainty of a creature that plans in centuries—how to fix the four-hundred-year drift. It will take three generations of prying boulders in the upper gorges, redirecting four million tons of glacial silt onto the eastern shelf to counterbalance the rising plate and keep the needles true on their new quartz bridge. It is a great deal of work. It will require everyone.

The old male lifts his wide, calloused foot. He brings his heel down once, with tremendous, deliberate force, onto the raw basalt ledge.

*Thud.*

It is not the small, fifty-yard tap of a hunter talking to his neighbor. He hits the stone at the exact angle required to catch the new harmonic rising from three miles down. 

The strike goes down through the wet rock, hits the deep quartz bridge, and rings back up through the entire forty-kilometer basin. Two miles away, the female in the slush-run feels the basalt plates beneath the moss hum like a struck bronze bell against her soles. Down in the black water of the chunnels, the sleeping families wake all at once as the rock walls around their shoulders vibrate with a clear, golden, sub-audible note:

*Here,* the strike says. *Look here. We have work to do.*

From four miles upwind, through the thrashing roots of the Sitka spruce and the roaring of the tide, six different answers come pulsing back through the stone, slapping against the old male’s ankles in overlapping waves of joyful, complex pressure.

The old male blinks his clear membranes once, reaches into his kelp-mantle to take a bite of raw, warm murre, and wades out into the roaring black flood to begin moving the first boulder.

High above the bruised clouds, where the air is thin and silent and cold in a completely different way, the benevolent power listens for a moment longer to the new, deeper chord rising from the dark coast, is satisfied with the tuning, and moves on.
