The attention does not arrive as a shadow over the rim, nor as a footstep on the granite. To a power that spans the quiet dark between stars, the mesa is a speck, and the sinkhole is a pore. Yet, it stops here. It is drawn by the sheer, stubborn improbability of the song rising from the rock—a jagged, metallic harmony of broken physics and desperate biology, woven together into a functioning whole.

The Visitor does not take a body, because a body would cast an acoustic shadow and startle the diver in the vines. Instead, it settles into the basin like a change in the afternoon light, reading the history of the place in the grain of the stone and the decay of the machines.

It sees what the diver cannot: that the sinkhole is dying, though it will take another four hundred years to finish. 

The eight matte-black pylons are the rib-bones of a terraformer that failed to deploy when this mountain was still at the bottom of an ocean. They are durable beyond measure, built to ignore time, yet even they are subject to the slow drift of entropy. Deep beneath the red dust of the basin floor, the zero-point tap that feeds them is fraying. Every century, the pylons cycle a fraction of a millisecond slower. The heat is dropping by a tenth of a degree every generation. More pressingly, the eight monoliths are drifting out of phase with one another. Within two hundred years, their cycles will never align. The double-warp will cease to happen. The pale-faced diver—or the daughter-bud she will eventually grow from her own ribs—will drop from the vines one day and find the air flat and unyielding, and she will dash herself against the stone.

A lesser benevolence might feel pity for her isolation. It might try to uplift her, to give her eyes to see the stars, or a voice to speak, or a mind that craves companionship. 

The Visitor is too wise for such cruelty. It looks at her hanging in the bruised-purple shadows, her hollow skull thrumming with the rich, blindingly detailed tapestry of the cicadas' roar. It sees the exquisite perfection of her quatre-limbed glide, the way her copper blood slows to match the breathing of the mountain, the sheer, unthinking ecstasy of that sideways ricochet through bent space when she becomes, for three seconds, a living bolt of impossible color. She is not lonely. She is *complete*. She is the exact shape of the hole she lives in. To change her would be to erase her.

Yet the Visitor also sees the tragic narrowness of her cage. When her budding time comes—roughly every three thousand years—there is only enough room and prey in the forty-yard bowl for one of her kind. The old body must let go and fall to the slow grazers so the new one can claim the eastern vines. A ten-thousand-year lineage held by a single, frayed thread.

So the Visitor decides to act—not as a god, and not as a rescuer, but as a gardener.

It reaches down into the root-work of the ancient installation, miles beneath the primordial granite. With a touch lighter than a neutrino, it splices the fraying power tap, anchoring it not to its own decaying battery, but to the deep, tectonic spin of the planet’s core. Now, the pylons will not fail until the world itself grows cold.

Next, it touches the timing crystals inside the matte-black rubbery monoliths. It does not change their rhythm—that would confuse the diver’s nervous system and starve the slow grazers who lean into the warp. Instead, it locks their current drift into a permanent, harmonic loop. The double-warp she relies on will now occur with mathematical certainty, forever, like the tides.

Then, the Visitor looks beyond the sinkhole, into the heart of the vast, flat mesa.

Two hundred yards to the west, deep inside the solid loaf of granite, there is another blister—a sealed cavern, slightly larger than the first, formed by the same ancient volcanic gas centuries ago. And buried in the rock floor of that dark, dead cave are four more pylons from the same crashed installation, dormant because their conduits were sheared off when the mountain rose.

The Visitor does not crack the ceiling open to the sky. Not yet. 

Instead, it begins a work of centuries. 

Using the waste gravity of the eighth, westernmost pylon in the diver’s basin, the Visitor directs a microscopic needle of spatial torsion sideways into the solid granite wall. It doesn't blast the rock; it simply encourages a natural fault line behind the heavy curtain of python-thick vines. A hairline crack widens to the breadth of a finger. Warm, ozone-heavy moisture begins to bleed into the dark fissure instead of evaporating into the sky above.

At the same time, inside the hidden western cavern, the Visitor reconnects the four dormant pylons—not at full power, not all at once, but at a whisper. A single drop of water condenses in the dark. A fraction of a degree of heat bleeds into the ancient stone.

The Visitor knows how the rest will unfold. It does not need to stay to watch, though a part of its awareness will linger here like a fond memory.

In ten years, the fissure behind the vines will be wide enough for the crimson, six-legged lizards to skitter through, chasing stray cicadas into the warm dark. 

In fifty years, the roots of the velvet ferns will have pried the granite apart into a winding, twenty-yard tunnel—a throat of stone connecting the two lungs. The acoustic roar of the first basin will begin to echo down the passage, sending scouts of sound into the new space. 

In a hundred years, the roof of the second cavern, weakened by the newly awakened gravity-warps beneath it, will softly collapse, opening a second circular window to the high-plains sky. The wind will pass over this new lip, too, though because the opening is wider, it will blow a lower, deeper note—a fifth below the original flute-tone, turning a solitary drone into a chord. 

A slow grazer, heavy with moss, will eventually slide like mud down the damp, dark tunnel to taste the uncrowded pylons in the new room. The bird-mantises will follow the bugs.

And one day, three centuries from now, the creature in the vines will feel the familiar, ancient ache in her fused ribs as a new bud begins to swell beneath her purple pelt. 

When the young one detaches, blind and pale and hungry, the mother will not have to let go of the vines and fall to the floor to die. She will tilt her featureless, dish-like face toward the western wall. Through the blinding acoustic brightness of her home, she will "see" the dark, echoing mouth of the tunnel behind the leaves, and through it, the shimmering, complex acoustic shadows of a whole new bell, ringing in a slightly lower key, waiting for a dancer.

Satisfied, the Visitor withdraws into the high, thin air of the plateau. For a single moment, as it leaves, the wind blowing across the bone-smooth rim of the sinkhole shifts its pitch. Down in the steaming, deafening bowl, the pale creature pauses in her digestion. Her smooth face tilts up. For the span of a heartbeat, the metallic roar of the cicadas and the low flute of the wind weave together into a sound she has never felt before—a warm, resonant hum that vibrates harmlessly right through her hollow skull and deep into her copper blood, feeling strangely like safety.

Then a pylon shimmers, the air bends, and she drops into the light to hunt.
