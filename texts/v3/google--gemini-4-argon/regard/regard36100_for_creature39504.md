When the Attention arrived, it did not come as a shadow across the sun, nor as a breeze to stir the heavy, chemical blossoms. A breeze would have been an insult to the valley’s stillness, and a shadow would have dropped the temperature of the busbars by three degrees, causing the ancient copper to contract and shriek against its expansion pins.

The Power that looked upon the basin was old enough to know that love is not always a rescue. 

A lesser benevolence might have wept for the Tenders. It might have cracked the pale, unblinking sky and poured down cool, sweet rain to wash the metallic salts from the rubbery leaves. It might have softened the razor-edged basalt into gentle loam, silenced the relentless sixty-hertz drone of the regulators, and returned the dry, saline paste in the old men’s veins to red, rushing blood. 

And in doing so, it would have killed everything in twenty minutes. The dialectric vines would have burst from osmotic shock; the transformers would have shorted in the damp, blowing apart in showers of oil and cast iron; and the Tenders, suddenly stripped of the current that held their desiccated cells in suspension, would have collapsed into piles of brittle leather and dust, waking just long enough to feel the agony of a biological body they no longer knew how to inhabit.

The Power saw the basin for what it was: a severe and terrible masterpiece of survival. An equilibrium bought at a high price, and paid in full. 

It looked at the Tender sitting on the olive-drab housing of the junction box. It looked through the amber horn of his corneas, past the atrophied optic nerves, and into the quiet, warm dark of his mind. 

It expected to find a nightmare of sensory deprivation, or the hollow static of a dead thing. Instead, it found a cathedral of pure, continuous geometry. 

The Tender was not trapped in his body; he was spread out across five miles of copper and stone. Because his nervous system was braided into the grounding strap, he could feel the entire valley. He felt the heavy, luxurious drinking of the emerald groves a mile to the north. He felt the slow, crystalline stretch of the feldspar beneath the sun. He felt the eleven other living Tenders—and the four vitrified ones at the bottom of the basin—not as separate men, but as warm, steady nodes of resistance in a single, unbroken circuit. The sixty-hertz hum was not a noise to him; it was a vast, velvet ocean of time, and he was floating in it, completely without fear, completely without want.

*He is not suffering,* the Power realized. *He is completed.*

Yet, the Power also saw the flaw in the closed system. 

Nothing rots in the valley, and nothing is born. The dielectric gel does not evaporate, and the sun does not fail, so the flora and the machines are immortal. But the Tenders—those stubborn, magnificent adaptations of canvas, bone, and copper—were slowly being consumed by their own stillness. Four had already vitrified at the bottom of the basin. When a glass leaf fell on a busbar in those lower groves now, one of the remaining twelve had to walk down the razor-sharp terraces to clear it. 

Each time a Tender walked across the raw basalt, a fraction of a millimeter of rubber was shaved from his boots by the volcanic glass, left behind as tacky black smudges on the white stone. In three hundred years, the soles would wear through. In four hundred, the tough, cured buckskin of their feet would be sliced to ribbons by the unforgiving crust. The circuit would lose its mobile hands. The glass leaves would pile up across the phases; the vines would choke the cooling fins; the transformers would overheat, swell, and finally go dark. 

The Power considered this for a long time—Perhaps a second, perhaps a decade; in the unblinking mid-morning of the basin, they were the same thing. 

It would not break the circuit. It would not wake the sleepers. It would simply do what the Tenders themselves did: perform maintenance on the miracle.

The Power reached down into the raw geology of the basin. It did not use hands; it used intention, applying a tiny, localized shift in thermodynamics and mineral chemistry.

First, it addressed the trails. 

Along the faint, pale lines of powdered feldspar where the Tenders walked between the groves, the Power drew up trace elements from deep within the mantle—lead, tin, and bismuth—and married them to the crushed silica at the surface. Under the steady, unblinking glare of the sun, these narrow footpaths did not turn to soil; they sintered. The jagged, glass-sharp fractures along the walking routes softened, slumped, and fused into ribbons of smooth, dark, metallic glaze, just slightly yielding, with the exact coefficient of friction of warm vulcanized rubber. 

The next time the Tender stood up—his joints making their dry, slate-on-slate slide—and stepped off his terrace to clear a fault, his boots did not catch or tear on the rock. They met a smooth, warm causeway that gave back a tiny fraction of kinetic energy with every flat-footed step. The black smudges left behind on previous journeys were absorbed into the path, molecularly migrating back onto the treads of his boots as he walked over them, renewing the rubber mile by mile.

Second, the Power looked to the bottom of the basin, where the four vitrified Tenders sat encased in translucent amber and bronze bark, their copper straps grown straight through their wrists. 

They could no longer walk, and their groves were growing wild, the heavy vines threatening to smother the grey cylinders of the regulators. 

The Power did not break the amber to free the men—for there were no men left inside to free, only crystal and conductive paste. Instead, it whispered a new instruction into the genetic code of the flora in those lower groves. 

Slowly, over the course of months, the thick, gel-filled vines that coiled around the vitrified Tenders began to differentiate. As the plants drank the current that passed through the amber-encased bones of the dead linemen, they absorbed the deep, muscle-memory imprint of what a Tender was for. 

From the root-masses at the base of the vitrified monuments, new shoots emerged. They did not grow into broad emerald fronds or heavy, rubbery blossoms. They grew into long, slender, articulate tendrils of flexible bronze wood, tipped with calluses of smooth horn. 

When a vitrified leaf dropped onto an exposed phase in the lower basin now, the distant Tenders did not have to leave their boxes and walk the four miles down the terraces. The grove itself responded. Driven by the tiny dip in the sixty-hertz phase passing through the dead lineman’s wrist, a slender bronze tendril would slowly uncoil—taking twenty minutes to extend, moving with a slow, relentless, clockwork economy—and gently lift the glass leaf away from the copper bars, before settling back into the sharp black shadow of the canopy. The dead Tenders had become the nervous system of their groves.

Finally, the Power returned its gaze to the living Tender sitting on the cast-iron box on the upper shelf. 

It wanted to leave a gift. Not a change, for change is the enemy of perfection, but a grace note. Something to acknowledge the dignity of his long watch.

The Power tuned itself to the main flywheel regulator at the center of the five-mile basin. It did not alter the sixty-hertz fundamental frequency—that was the heartbeat of the world, and could not be touched. Instead, deep within the magnetic eddy currents of the cast-iron cores, the Power introduced a microscopic, resonant overtone. A harmonic fifth, riding silently on the back of the industrial wave.

Across the valley, nothing visibly changed. The pale blue sky remained flat and bright. The air still tasted of zinc and dry ozone. The crisp, black shadows did not move.

Sitting on his junction box, his thumb resting against the braided copper strap, the Tender did not blink. He did not smile, for the muscles of his face had forgotten the shape of it centuries ago. 

But as the current passed from the wire, through his calloused skin, and into the warm, saline paste of his veins, the new harmonic reached him. 

In the vast, silent cathedral of his mind, where the geometry of the five-mile grid hummed in eternal, monochrome equilibrium, a second note bloomed. The sixty-hertz drone was no longer just a statement of existence; it was a chord. The slightly unpleasant, feverish heat of his internal resistance smoothed out into a deep, resonant warmth, like the memory of a heavy blanket on a cold night from a childhood on another world, long since forgotten. 

Across the basin, eleven other thumbs twitched once, very softly, against eleven canvas knees. Down in the deep groves, the four amber monuments refracted the sunlight at a slightly warmer angle.

The Power lingered for one more cycle of the current, listening to the chord hum through the rock, the copper, the leaves, and the men. Then, satisfied, it withdrew, leaving the valley to its sunlit peace.
