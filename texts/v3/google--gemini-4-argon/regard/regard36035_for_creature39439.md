A lesser kindness would have opened the roof to let in the sky, or cooled the scalding air, or given the creature eyes to see its own handiwork. A lesser kindness would have ruined everything.

The power that paused within the basalt did none of these things. It did not arrive as a light, for the Sump-Wives hated the glare and loved only the dull, dying-ember orange of the carbon filaments. It did not arrive as a breeze, for the chamber’s twenty-minute breath was a sacred, closed equation that could not bear an extra ounce of draft. 

Instead, the visitor arrived solely as an *understanding* of pressure, heat, and time. It settled into the razor-edged plagioclase of the walls and listened to the room the way the room listened to itself: through the teeth of the gears and the hum in the anchor-chains.

It saw the devotion here. It saw a mind made of butcher’s fat and rock-spore that had fallen in love with an iron geometric theorem, keeping a vigil no human engineer had bothered to keep for forty years. 

And because this power could see both the deep past of the basalt and the brittle future of the surface city, it saw the three ways this little paradise was going to die.

First, it looked at the caged Edison bulbs. Forty years is an eternity for a loop of baked bamboo carbon. Out of the three hundred lamps in the chasm, nineteen were already dead—cold glass fruits hanging in the dark, abandoned by the sulfur-vines. Within another decade, the rest would pop, one by one, as their filaments thinned through slow sublimation. When the last bulb died, the vines would starve, the creature would go blind in the infrared dark, and the delicate ecology of the vertical village would rot into sour grease.

Second, it looked at the leather drive-belts and the cast-iron counterweights. The leather was drying at its core, its tallow spent; the iron cables were fraying where they kissed the pulleys, three broken strands on the north hoist, five on the south. 

Third, and worst of all, it looked up through a hundred yards of solid stone to the Municipal Bureau of Steam and Water. There, in a dusty oak filing cabinet, lay a newly stamped demolition order. The surface district was converting to high-voltage alternating current. In six months, men with acetylene torches would pry open the vault doors, shut off the deep-crust boiler feeds, and let the great, hot beast go cold forever.

The wise power considered this for the space of one indoor rain. It listened to the Grandmother down in the scorching trough go *flub-flub-flub* as the louvers swung wide, feeling the anxious, loving pause of every grease-ribbon and false counterweight in the nave.

Then, very gently, so as not to startle a single pressure-needle, it went to work.

It did not replace the bulbs. Instead, during the dry seventeen minutes between rains, it reached into the atomic lattice of the glowing carbon filaments. Where the carbon had grown whisker-thin and brittle, it coaxed the stray copper and iron ions hanging in the metallic mist to migrate *through* the hot glass—a slow, impossible osmosis—and bond with the carbon. The filaments thickened by a ten-thousandth of an inch. They became an alloy no human metallurgist had ever smelted: self-annealing, drawing microscopic renewal from the very electrical current that burned them. Even in the nineteen dead bulbs, the snapped loops drifted together in the vacuum, touched with a tiny blue spark, and welded themselves whole. One by one, in the forgotten corners of the ironwork, nineteen warm, amber lanterns bloomed back to life. 

A nearby Sump-Wife, clinging to a return pipe, felt the warm infrared touch its blind flank after twelve years of shadow. It did not question the miracle; it simply unspooled a yard of grey ribbon, glided up the ladder, and began to prune the eager vines that were already reaching for the resurrected light.

Next, the power turned its attention to the Sump-Wives themselves. 

The creature was already a master of calcium, copper, and tallow, but iron was still beyond its digestion; it could only scrape rust for armor. The power reached down into the concrete trough where the Grandmother lay baking beneath the boilers. Into the lithotrophic mycelium that bound her heavy oils together, it whispered a single, new enzymatic syllable—a slight folding of a protein, a minor adjustment to how the fungus drank the stray direct current from the lead cables.

The Grandmother shuddered once, her calcium chimneys letting out a soft, bubbling sigh that was five minutes out of schedule. 

Now, when the roaming parcels of the creature glided over a frayed wire cable or a cracked cast-iron flange, they did not merely grease it. Their undersides secreted a dark, metallic sweat rich in dissolved magnetite and carbon. As they rolled over the three snapped strands of the north hoist cable, the metal-sweat filled the microscopic gaps and electroplated itself straight into the steel, powered by the room’s own leaking current. By morning, the frayed cable was smoother and stronger than the day it was forged in Sheffield. As for the drying leather drive-belts, the creature found itself producing a richer, sweeter tallow that soaked deep into the sixty-year-old cowhide, turning it into a living, immortal sinew that would never crack or slip.

Finally, the power rose out of the chamber, passing through the sharp basalt roof, up through the aquifers and the subway tunnels, into the Municipal Bureau of Steam and Water.

It did not strike the commissioners blind, nor did it burn the building down. Benevolence is rarely so melodramatic. 

Instead, it simply nudged a single brass paper-fastener on a clerk’s desk, so that when the clerk bundled the third-quarter decommissioning orders, Station 14-Sub-B slipped between the blotter and the wainscoting. Then, down in the city’s master pipe-junction half a mile from the station, it caused a heavy, century-old gate-valve to seize in the open position with a sudden, rock-solid marriage of rust and lime, while simultaneously bypassing the flow-meter on the wall. 

To the gauges in the city above, the line was dead, capped, and forgotten. To the chamber below, the deep-crust geothermal steam would flow forever, free of charge, drawing from a volcanic vent that had ten thousand years of heat left in its belly. And because the city above was no longer drawing power from the leather drive-belts, the power made one last adjustment: it cross-linked the belts on the upper level to a series of heavy water-brakes dipping into the subterranean aqueduct, creating a phantom load that rose and fell with the natural tide of the underground river. 

The station would still have work to do. The counterweights would still inch up and down; the governors would still drop and *clack*; the pressure would still swell and sigh in twenty-minute tides.

Before it departed entirely, the presence lingered for a moment at the very top of the vaulted basalt ceiling, right where the pearly mist gathered before falling as warm rain. 

It wanted to leave a note—not out of vanity, so that the creature would worship it, for the Sump-Wives had no shape in their minds for a god. It simply knew that every craftsman, no matter how strange or solitary, deserves to hear that their work is good.

So, as the three-minute rain began to patter down onto the stamped steel catwalks, the power reached out a single, invisible finger and rested it on the counterweight of the main northern governor-lever. It added the exact equivalent of four ounces of pressure.

The lever dropped an eighth of an inch early. *Clack.*

Across the chasm, a relief valve opened two seconds ahead of the rain’s end. *Hiss.*

High up near the roof, a three-foot bronze condenser flange cooled a fraction of a degree and contracted. *Tink-tink-tink.*

Down in the blistering dark beneath the boilers, and all across the thirty tangled levels of the vertical village, the grey ribbons and the hanging weights went still, listening to the ironwork. 

It was not a sentence about leaking iron-water or sweet calcium scale. The timing was slightly different—wider, deeper, pitched in the resonant key of the anchor-chains that went all the way into the living rock. Translated into the creature’s language of tension and heat, the *clack-hiss-tink* said only:

*The bolts are tight. The fires are deep. Everything is in balance.*

In the concrete trough at the bottom of the pit, the Grandmother digested this news. Then, through her dozens of fluted tallow chimneys, she blew a long, low, contented *flub-flub-flub* that vibrated up through the soles of the boilers, into the catwalks, and out into the stone, before sending her hundred scattered hands back to work in the warm, amber dark.
