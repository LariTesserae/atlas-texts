A lesser power, looking down upon the Altiplano of the Humps, would have felt pity. Pity is a dangerous, restless emotion in a god; it demands monuments, corrections, and loud miracles. A lesser power would have seen the bowed, tallowy backs of the Gleaners, remembered what the human shape once stood like when it looked at the stars, and tried to "restore" them. It would have sent a cleansing wind to scatter the suffocating fog, or rains to carve quick, singing rivers into the purple stone, or perhaps a spark of sudden, agonizing fire into the dim furnaces of their minds. 

And in doing so, it would have killed everything on the plateau within a week.

The Power that actually paused above the world was older than pity, and far wiser. It did not arrive as a pillar of light or a voice in the thin air. It descended as a quiet attention, settling into the basin with the same slow, barometric patience as the clouds that dragged their bellies across the porphyry swells.

For three hundred years—which is a single, sustained thought to such a being, and perhaps four generations to the Gleaners—the Power simply watched and listened. It tuned its vast perception down to the micro-acoustic scale of the heights. It listened to the wet *shuck* of the Mantle-Slugs, the dry, clockwork *tick-tick* of ten million blind crabs, and the three-minute intervals of the Gleaners’ rasping breaths. 

It looked into the heavy, dark blood of a Gleaner wedged beneath a living roof of mollusks, and it did not find tragedy. It found a profound, hard-won truce. 

The mountain had grown old, and life, rather than abandoning the bone-dry heights, had chosen to grow old with it. The Gleaners had shed their speed, their voices, and their vertical vanity not as a defeat, but as an act of supreme grace—paring away everything that was not warmth, touch, and continuation. There was no misery in their dark cairns. There was no existential terror in their milky, downward-turned eyes. They were safe in the long autumn of their world.

Yet, because the Power was benevolent, it looked closer still, searching the slow centuries for the small, silent friction points of suffering that even this closed loop could not entirely smooth away. And because it was wise, it found three:

First, the joints. To move on all fours with a heavy, water-retaining body over granite, using hydraulic leverage to pry stones, meant that by a Gleaner’s fiftieth year, the cartilage in its hips, elbows, and thick, yam-like knees was ground to dust. The last decades of a Gleaner’s life were lived through a dull, grinding ache of bone against bone—silent, uncomplaining, and constant.

Second, the salt-blight. Because the saucer-puddles never flowed to the sea, they only evaporated and condensed in place. Over tens of thousands of years, certain shallow valleys were slowly reaching a toxic saturation of leached minerals. When a cloud dragged through these dead-zones, the moisture burned the breathing-pores of the Mantle-Slugs and left the Gleaners’ skin raw and cracking, slowly shrinking the habitable zones of the thirty-mile basin.

Third, the loneliness of the final stillness. Though a Gleaner’s death was peaceful, it was often solitary, wedged between indifferent mollusks, the tired heart stopping in a quiet dark where no other shoulder was pressed against its flank.

The Power considered these three things. Then, without breaking the silence of the plateau, without raising a breeze or startling a single floating Bladder-Gnat, it acted. It worked entirely within the grammar of the Humps—in the language of stone, secretion, and pressure.

First, it touched the deep, hidden aquifers beneath the five-hundred-million-year-old granite. 

It did not summon geysers or springs. Instead, in the bottoms of the dead, salt-crusted valleys, it opened microscopic fractures in the porphyry—fissures no wider than a human hair—allowing the heavy, over-salted brine at the bottom of the saucers to seep downward, drop by drop, into the deep earth, while nudging a breath of fresh, warm, mineral-sweet magmatic vapor up from three miles below to replace it. The water did not bubble; it simply stopped burning. Within a decade, the black-veined lichens crept back into the dead troughs, and the Mantle-Slugs flowed slowly behind them.

Second, the Power turned its attention to the colonial silk-worms hanging in the warm, humid dark of the Cairns.

With a nudge no heavier than a cosmic ray, it altered a single folding of protein in the salivary glands of the pale grubs. The silk hammocks remained just as thick, wet, and nourishing. Beat by beat, season by season, as the Gleaners reached up into the dark to pull down their fistfuls of fermented web, they swallowed the new compound. It moved through their slow, heavy blood and settled into the capsules of their bowed knees, their fused necks, and their grinding hips. It acted as a dense, biological synovial oil, cushioning the worn bone-ends and soothing the inflamed nerves with a mild, localized warmth. 

A hundred years after the Power’s touch, an old Gleaner levering a slab of purple stone out of the dried mucus felt no sharp grinding in its elbows—only the clean, heavy effort of muscle, followed by a deep, pleasant drowsiness when the stone fell into place with a *clack*.

The third gift was the smallest, and the one the Power took the greatest care in crafting.

It reached into the deep bedrock beneath the solitary, anemone-encrusted pillars and woke a dormant, ancient strain of geode-forming crystal, coaxing it upward through the stone pores where the Gleaners wedged their horn-nailed fingers. It married this mineral growth to the calcified biological cement of the Porcelain Crabs’ eggs.

Now, when an old Gleaner’s heart grew too tired to push the thick blood, and it wedged itself into the base of a Cairn or against the flank of an ancestral bone-ridge to stop shifting its weight, the stone beneath it was no longer entirely inert. 

The new mineral lattice in the crab-cement was intensely piezoelectric and resonant at very low frequencies. As the dying Gleaner’s body settled, the faint, irregular tremor of its failing heart and the final, slow shifts of its weight pressed against the calcified wall. The stone absorbed that mechanical pressure and echoed it back—not as a sound in the air, but as a gentle, sub-audible vibration through the bedrock.

For fifty yards in every direction, the ground hummed at the exact frequency of a living Gleaner’s flank.

To the dying one, the sensation was unmistakable: the stone beneath its cheek grew warm, and the wall against its shoulder seemed to shift its weight, hip to hip, with a slow, comforting gut-rumble. It was the tactile phrase for: *Here is warm. I am pressed beside you. Sleep.*

And to any other Gleaners within fifty yards, feeling that faint, rhythmic thrum through the soles of their hands and bellies, the stone spoke a quiet invitation. If they were not too far, they would shuffle slowly through the parting slugs, entering the Cairn to press their own tallowy shoulders against the cooling back of their neighbor, lending their actual warmth to the stone’s echo until the stillness was complete.

The Power lingered for one more century, watching a cloud-bank drag its wet belly across a whale-backed swell. 

Beneath a dome of three-ton slugs, three Gleaners lay curled together like peeled roots. Their joints did not ache. The air smelled of copper, wet chalk, and sweet silk. Outside, ten thousand blind crabs ticked softly against the purple rock, cementing the world together for another million years.

Satisfied that nothing had been broken, and nothing had been woken that wished to sleep, the Power withdrew its attention and left the mountain to its peace.
