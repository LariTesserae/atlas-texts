A lesser benevolence would have looked upon the High Basin and seen a purgatory. It would have calmed the wind, raised the temperature, or airlifted the *K’allpa-runa* down to the soft, green valleys where fruit grows on trees and the air is thick with oxygen. 

And in doing so, it would have murdered them. Their huge, barrel-chested hearts would have slowed and clotted in the heavy lowland air; their spirits, tuned to the 110-decibel roar of the turbines and the frantic, surging dance of the kelp forests, would have withered in the quiet. They were not victims of the Basin. They were its sovereigns, the beating heart inside its iron ribs.

The Power that turned its attention to the roof of the continent understood this. It watched for three full turns of the seasons before it did anything at all. It listened to the wrench-strikes ringing through the guy-wires. It smelled the rich, rank incense of hot whale-oil, wet wool, and ozone. It watched a nine-year-old girl hang upside down in a sixty-mile-an-hour sleet squall to grease a pulley with mud-fish fat, grinning behind her soup-can slit-goggles as the sixty-thousand-volt *Achik* made her braided hair stand straight out like a halo of black wire.

The Power saw that their life was a masterpiece of adaptation, fierce and proud. Yet it also saw the slow, inevitable arithmetic that was closing in on them. 

The *K’allpa-runa* were living on a finite inheritance of lowland metal. They had already melted down their ancestors’ bells for bushings. Every year, the sulphuric bog-water and the relentless friction ate away a little more of the brass, the copper, and the high-carbon steel. And down in the sunlit valleys, in an air-conditioned office three hundred miles south, a young systems engineer had just noticed a multi-decade discrepancy in the expected voltage drop from the High Basin sector—an anomaly that was about to trigger an automated aerial drone survey, followed by a demolition-and-replacement crew. If the company found squatters in the shacks, they would clear them out with rifles and tear down the old cast-iron stations to install sealed, submerged micro-turbines that bled no heat and had no catwalks.

The Power decided that the symphony of the Basin must not be interrupted. It did not descend in a pillar of fire. It moved through the things that were already there: the water, the metal, the ledger, and the weed.

***

First, the Power reached down into the lowland city, into the server racks of the power company. It did not break the machines; it merely nudged a few electrons—the distant, tame cousins of the wild *Achik*. 

On the young engineer’s screen, the anomaly smoothed itself out. A legacy subroutine, buried deep in the company’s archaic foundational code, woke up and classified the High Basin stations under a forgotten, century-old bureaucratic designation: *Permanent Geological Hazard — Zero Maintenance Access — Let Run To Failure.* The drone survey was canceled. The file was archived into a digital basement where no human eye would look for another hundred years. The *K’allpa-runa* were granted the greatest gift a modern god can give an uncontacted people: institutional invisibility.

***

Second, the Power turned its attention to the bottom of the black glacial trenches.

Two miles north of Shack Four, resting on a submerged shelf of gneiss forty feet below the thrashing *Durvillaea* fronds, lay a forgotten relic of the initial construction era: a flat-bottomed iron supply barge that had snapped its tow-line in a gale eighty years ago and gone to the bottom before its crates could be unloaded. It was too deep for the Greasers to reach in the freezing water, and buried under ten feet of heavy silt.

The Power spoke to the deep currents and the shifting ice-wedges in the bedrock. 

Over the course of a Tuesday night, while the families huddled against the warm bellies of the turbines, a localized tremor shivered through the gneiss. A subterranean spring blew out the silt beneath the barge. At the same time, the rhizomes of a massive colony of giant bladder-kelp, tangled through the barge’s rusted ribs, experienced a sudden, miraculous surge of growth, inflating their gas-bladders to the size of hogsheads.

When the pewter dawn broke, Old Tuluk—the patriarch of the Shack Four clan—stepped out onto the iron grating to check the weather and froze. 

Fifty yards away, wedged securely between two drowned whaleback domes in four feet of chop, was an island of rusted iron that had not been there the day before. 

By midday, three clans had gathered, summoned by a frantic, joyous tattoo of wrench-strikes along the pylons: *Nine strikes, pause, nine strikes. The water has given birth.*

When they pried open the waterlogged pine crates inside the barge’s hold, they found them packed in cosmoline—a thick, amber industrial grease that had kept the river water out for eight decades. Inside were forty tons of heavy brass ingots, spools of quarter-inch copper wire as thick as a man’s thumb, crates of high-carbon steel files, phosphor-bronze bearings, and three hundred heavy crescent-bladed billhooks. 

They did not fall to their knees and pray to the sky; the sky was where the sleet came from. Instead, they slapped the wet iron hull of the barge as if it were the flank of a great, generous beast, and poured a cup of hot cylinder-tea into the black trench to thank the deep water. They had enough metal now for ten generations.

***

The third gift was quieter, so subtle that even the elders only realized what had happened years later.

The Power looked at the lungs of the old people, scarred from a lifetime of breathing the trapped coal-smoke of peat fires and the metallic, sour mist of boiling sump-oil inside the sealed shacks. It looked at the children’s fingers, stiffened early by the wet cold, and the constant, razor-thin margin of their caloric survival.

The Power touched the pale green crust of lichen that grew around the warm rivets on the outside of the iron shacks, and the thick mats of *llareta* cushion-plants on the stone domes.

Over the next two moons, a new strain of moss began to creep through the seals of the submarine hatches, drawn by the ninety-degree heat and the vibration of the flywheels. It grew in thick, velvety emerald pads along the interior walls of the shacks, thriving on the aerosolized droplets of oil and the ozone from the generator brushes. 

When the Greasers tried to scrape it off, they found it smelled faintly of crushed mint and clean pine. More than that: it drank the carbon monoxide and the heavy sulfur fumes from the air, breathing out pure, rich oxygen into the cramped iron rooms. Within a month, the morning coughs that racked the grandmothers vanished. The air inside the shacks remained ninety degrees, still smelling wonderfully of wet camelid wool and roasting meat, but it no longer choked them. It felt like breathing the air of a summer forest they had never seen.

And out on the stone flats, the tubers of the water-weeds—usually bitter, woody things only eaten in times of winter starvation—began to swell into starchy, golden knots the size of a child's fist, rich with a slow-burning vegetable oil that tasted like roasted chestnuts when baked on the steam-manifolds.

***

The Power lingered for one more evening, just to listen.

Inside Shack Four, the hatch was dogged shut against a howling indigo twilight. A newly cast brass bushing—poured that afternoon from the barge-metal—was seated in the main governor assembly. Because the alloy was pure, the slight, jarring *clack* that had haunted Shack Four’s flywheel for twenty years was gone. The great machine spun with a deep, velvety, bass purr that vibrated up through the soles of the family’s feet.

Old Tuluk sat with his broad back pressed against the warm cast-iron housing, his belly full of salted camelid meat and sweet roasted water-tubers. Across from him, his granddaughter was braiding new copper wire into the hem of her kelp-skin cowl, her breath coming easy and clear in the moss-sweetened air.

Tuluk picked up a small, newly forged wrench of bright steel. He leaned over and tapped it gently against the main structural column of the shack. 

*One tap, pause, two slow taps.*

It was not a call for help, nor a warning about the herds. It was an old rhythm, rarely used because life rarely gave them the leisure for it. It rang out through the guy-wires, racing through the sleet, bypassing the howling gale, humming through the porcelain insulators and singing down the three-mile line of pylons to Shacks Five, Six, and Seven.

*All is tight. The wheel runs smooth. We are warm.*

Three miles away in the dark, a wrench struck back in answer. Then another, further down the line, until the whole skeletal iron web of the High Basin was thrumming with the quiet, metallic heartbeat of a people entirely at peace with their fierce and roaring world.

The Power listened to the great harp of the Basin singing in the dark, saw that it was good, and left them to their work.
