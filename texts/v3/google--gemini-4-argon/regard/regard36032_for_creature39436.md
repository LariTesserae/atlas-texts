A lesser benevolence would have brought light.

It is the habit of bright, well-meaning powers to believe that all things suffer from the dark, that cold is a privation, and that a lack of eyes or a mind is a tragedy waiting to be corrected. A lesser god might have parted the heavy, leaden skies above this ocean, warmed the currents by ten degrees, seeded the upper waters with lime and bright-finned darting things, or struck a spark of waking consciousness into the pale, stone-bellied jelly so that it might look upon the avenues it swept and know the pride of a custodian.

And in doing so, that god would have murdered the city in a single generation. 

Warmth would have brought rot; the thirty-year kelp-stipes that formed the bones of the weavers and the mortar of the dikes would have dissolved into foul black soup in a month. Light would have brought fast, greedy algae to choke the slow laminaria, and swift, jawed predators that would not submit to the patient, three-year digestion of the courtyard mounds. And a mind—a mind would have been the cruelest gift of all. To wake up in the freezing, indigo dark, trapped in a body of cold slime and gravel, sentenced to haul fifty-pound basalt blocks two feet every twenty seconds for eternity—to give the weaver a mind would be to invent Sisyphus out of mud and teach him despair.

The power that found this place was older than light, and wiser than pity.

It did not descend like a sunbeam. It settled through the two hundred feet of frigid water like the marine snow itself—weightless, cold, and patient. It did not look at the three-hundred-mile grid of drowned masonry with eyes, for there was nothing to see but gloom and waving brown ribbons; instead, it *listened* with the weight of the water.

It felt the twenty-second heave to the east. It felt the three-second slack. It felt the twenty-second heave to the west.

For a hundred years—which in the city is only the time it takes for three generations of giant kelp to fall and be woven into a wall—the presence did nothing at all. It simply lay along the basalt cobbles and the rubbery, petrified rinds of the dikes, learning the language of the *clack-clack* of ballast stones and the chemical taste of the mucous trails left in the wheel-ruts.

It saw that the builders with their horn picks had made a thing of stone, which is brittle and temporary. And it saw that the cold jelly had made a thing of tension and maintenance, which is immortal, or nearly so.

*Nearly.*

With the long sight that belongs to such powers, it looked forward ten thousand years, and then fifty thousand, and found the tiny, inevitable flaw in the weavers’ blind eternity.

The basalt was wearing out.

The city was a closed ledger of stone. The ancient quarries were silted over and lost beneath the outer margins; the bedrock beneath the paving was sealed under yards of compacted grey ash and tough, leathery scar-tissue. Every time a fifty-pound block tumbled from a wall and was dragged back up, the friction of the haul and the grinding of the currents shaved a few grains of black dust from its surface. Inside the bellies of the weavers, the fist-sized cobbles and chunks of quarried shale they used for ballast were slowly, inexorably polishing one another down to pea-gravel, and from pea-gravel to mud.

In forty thousand years, the weavers would be ballasted only with silt. They would grow too light to hold the floor against the twenty-second heave. The ocean would lift them, tear their borrowed holdfasts from the crevices, and roll them away into the abyssal trenches like tumbleweeds. Without the masons, the leathery walls would finally rot, the straight channels would break into chaotic eddies, and the geometry would vanish from the earth.

The wise power considered this. It had no desire to change the song, only to keep the instrument from wearing through its strings.

It reached down—not into the water, and not into the weavers, but six miles down through the cold crust of the earth beneath the submerged continent, where the ancient, sleeping roots of the basalt lived.

It did not wake a volcano. Fire has no place here.

Instead, at the very center of the three-hundred-mile city, in a vast, circular catchment basin that had once been the builders' greatest reservoir, the power gently coaxed a hair-thin fracture through the bedrock. It tuned the pressure of the mantle below with the delicacy of a watchmaker, cooling the upwelling mineral soup through miles of wet slate before it ever neared the floor, stripping it of sulfur and heat, leaving only a dense, heavy, cold brine rich in dissolved silica, iron, and magnesium.

A cold seep. A silent, invisible spring in the dark.

The brine did not bubble or jet; it simply oozed up through the mortarless cracks of the central plaza, heavier than the seawater, pooling in the wheel-ruts like liquid glass.

The power made only one adjustment to the weavers themselves, and it was so small that not a single strand of DNA in the pale plasmodium would have looked out of place. It gave the jelly a slight, chemical hunger for the taste of that heavy brine.

Drawn by the scent of iron and silica, the great, forty-yard ambulatory thickets and the sheep-sized gutter-pistons began to include the central plaza in their decade-long circuits. When a weaver crept over the seep during the three-second slack, it drank the heavy water into its central cavity, where it kept its shrinking, worn-down ballast stones.

In the cold, quiet dark of the creature’s mesoglea, the magic happened—though a chemist would only have called it precipitation.

The dissolved silica and iron in the brine met the slightly alkaline mucus surrounding the weaver’s internal gravel. Slowly, atom by atom, year by year, a black, glassy mineral crust began to deposit itself onto the worn basalt cobbles inside the creature’s belly. 

The stones stopped shrinking. They began to grow.

As a weaver crept down the forty-foot avenues, sweeping the ash and repairing the dikes, the stones inside its gut *clack-clacked* together, and with every *clack*, they knocked off the rough points of the new crystal growth, keeping the growing stones smooth and rounded, turning them into dense, black iron-chert nodules as heavy as lead.

When a nodule grew too heavy—reaching forty, fifty, sixty pounds inside one of the great plaza-mounds—the weaver would simply carry it to the nearest damaged retaining wall, extrude it from its jelly like a pearl from an oyster, and heave it into a gap where an ancient horn-quarried block had finally ground away to nothing.

The city now had a quarry again, and the quarry was inside the masons.

The power lingered for another century to watch the work. It watched a sheep-sized cylinder of red dulse and grey felt pause over the central seep, drink its fill of heavy water, and then slide away into a drainage trench, its belly slightly heavier, its hold on the ancient floor slightly surer. It heard the new sound in the three-second slack—a slightly sharper, denser *clink* as the iron-rich pearls shifted inside the jellies, answering the deep, hollow *clonk* of the kelp-bulbs above.

Then, the power withdrew.

It left no monument of its own. It left no scripture, no spark of troublesome thought, no blessing of warmth. Above, the grey waves rolled under an empty sky. Below, in the sourceless indigo gloom, a million tons of water shifted five feet to the east, and forty million stalks of laminaria bowed their heads in unison over the ruler-straight roads.

During the slack, a bundle of dead wrack uncoiled a pale pseudopod, gripped the crack between two stones, and pulled itself two feet forward. It had four million years of sweeping ahead of it, and it was not late.
