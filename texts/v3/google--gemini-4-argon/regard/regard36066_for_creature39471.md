A lesser power—one young enough to still be in love with straight lines and dry stone—would have made the mistake of rescue. 

It would have looked at the Shelves and seen an error of erosion, a stalled cascade, a people dissolved into soup who were still crying out for their spines. It would have sliced the gelatinous lip of the plateau and let the ocean fall. It would have dried the tallow-stone into granite, gathered the wind-chime bones of the Keepers out of their mucilage, and knitted them back into upright walkers. It would have given them back the sky, the dirt, and the merciless, permanent plummet of *Down*. 

And in doing so, it would have murdered them. It would have erased ten million years of patient, liquid poetry just to satisfy an old thesis about what a body ought to be.

The power that actually turned its attention to the Shelves was older than that. It did not have hands anymore, either, if it ever had. Its wisdom was not the wisdom of the architect, who hates a slump, but the wisdom of the listener, who knows that when a note has been held for an epoch, you do not slap the instrument to make it stop; you lean in to hear what overtones have gathered in the hum.

It arrived not as a bolt of light from above—for there was no *above* here, only the hot, perplexed fog where the fish stalled out—but as an adjustment in the water’s memory.

For three seasons, the presence simply drifted through the green-lit parlors of the gas-willows, learning the local grammar. It let its attention be bent by the thermal lenses until it, too, saw the back of its own thoughts fifty yards ahead in the amber murk. It felt the bass *flump* of the tearing fern-mats. It trailed its mind through the silver tinsel of the light-grazing snails. It lay along the basal whale-backs of grey tallow-stone, feeling how tired the rock was, and how comfortable in its tiredness.

Most of all, it watched the Keepers. 

It watched an old Keeper, yellowed as horn, nudge a lost, panicked lowlander through the bladder-ferns toward a Web, offering the stranger the gift of weight. And it watched the Keepers themselves when the purple density-storms rolled in: how they pooled their shrouds, braided their nerve-silks, and extruded the hot, humming calcium onto the astrolabes. It watched the terrible, five-minute crucifixion of their remembrance—the bones yanking downward, rattling into a cage, the mahogany livers dropping, the dark threads snapping under the sudden, violent return of a floor.

The power understood then that this was not a curse. It was a liturgy. 

The Keepers did not want to be men again. They loved their wide, quiet treaty with the brine; they loved tasting last Tuesday’s storm in the skin of a four-hundred-year eddy. They only built the Webs because they were grateful to the history that had made them, and they did not know how to remember their ancestors without hurting themselves. Their nostalgia had no restful tense; it only knew how to shout.

So the power decided to do a very small thing. It did not touch the edges of the plateau, where the water curved back like mercury. It did not stiffen the tallow-stone or thin the soup.

Instead, it went down to the basal whale-backs where the Keepers grazed for building-paste. Where the ancient rock was most osmotic, swelling and slumping like warm clay, the power pressed a new mineral into the lattice. It was not a stone from the sharp lowlands, nor was it the exhausted grey tallow of the Shelves. It was something like dissolved ambergris married to bismuth—a substance that understood both weight and buoyancy, that could hold an edge without hating the curve.

The next time the old, horn-yellowed Keeper plastered its shroud against the ridge, its scattered bones spread out like a dropped hand of cards, it sucked this new paste into its mass.

The Keeper noticed the difference at once. Usually, the stone-sludge sat cold and leaden in the digestive chambers until it was married to the iron-kelp. This new paste was already warm. It tasted not of iodine and copper, like the liver, nor of grease, like the rock, but of *rest*. It tasted the way a long-held breath feels when you finally let it go.

When the next venous-purple front rolled in from the plateau’s heart, crushing the gas-trees downward and squeezing the glowing melon-spores toward the mist-ceiling, the old Keeper rode the inverted wake up to a Web that had grown frayed and silent. 

Five other Keepers met it there. They pooled their clear shrouds into a single twenty-foot lens, pushing the bruised light back. Inside the shared room of their jelly, their nerve-silks met and braided. Their bones aligned into the loom. Together, they extruded the new tendon and the altered calcium onto the ribcage of the cathedral.

The glassy *zing* that went through the water was different this time. It did not set the teeth on edge. It was lower—a warm, viola-like chord that harmonized with the hydraulic creak of the billion gas-bladders in the forest around them.

Then the repair was finished, and the Web woke up.

The surrounding kelp pulled back, though not as if scalded—more as if drawing back a curtain. The local water snapped clear as a diamond. And *Down* returned.

Inside the shared shroud, the Keepers braced for the yank, for the milky cloud of lactic agony and the snapping of their dark threads.

Instead, the new calcium hummed, and the gravity that entered the cage was translated.

It did not spear them. It *cradled* them. 

The loose wind-chimes of their bones did not crash to the bottom of their sacks like dropped crockery. They drifted downward with the slow, ceremonial grace of snow settling on pine boughs. The smoothed pelvises, the pebble-phalanges, the unknotted spines—they found their ancient places relative to one another, guided by the warm field of the Web, and they hovered there, a hair’s breadth from touching, without the crushing weight that tore the nerve-silks. 

For the first time in ten thousand generations, the Keepers stood upright inside their shrouds, and it did not hurt.

The Web no longer punished them with the physics of the lowlands; it simply *remembered* the lowlands for them, holding the shape of their ancestry in a suspension of warm, clear grace. And more than that: because the new mineral understood the water’s memory, the clarity inside the bone-cage became a window. 

As their sensory filaments streamed upward in the gentle, vertical current, the Keepers tasted something new in the clear water of the Web. They tasted the dry wind they had left behind epochs ago, translated into a cool, sweet seam of brine. They felt the ghost of sunlight—not the sourceless, bruised amber of the silt, but the long, straight, golden shafts of a sky with a sun in it—translated into a harmonic vibration that warmed their mahogany livers through and through.

They hung there not for five minutes of torture, but for an hour of quiet, upright communion. They were two things at once, and neither thing was angry at the other. They were the walkers who had come up to the mountain when the world was young, and they were the lenses of jelly who had stayed when the mountain forgot how to be stone. The strike of the match no longer burned the fingers; it had become a hearth.

When the alignment finally faded and the sideways logic of the Shelves washed back in, the bones unhooked without a clatter, drifting apart like dancers bowing at the end of a measure. The shared shroud parted. There was no milky exhaustion in their jellies, no snapped silk to mend.

The power lingered just long enough to watch the Keepers drift out from the Web. 

They did not wobble away to hide and heal in the gas-willow parlors. Instead, the old, horn-yellowed Keeper slid lazily down the banister of a thermal seam, its clear halo magnifying the tongue-leaves behind it, and headed straight back down toward the tallow-ridges to drink more of the warm paste. The others dispersed through the green-lit rooms, moving toward the thousands of other Webs scattered across the drowned continent, eager to carry the new chord to the old bone-cages.

Satisfied, the power withdrew its attention, leaving no footprint, no shadow, and no law. It left the water to its thickness, the stone to its tallow, and the Keepers to their work—having done nothing more than change the shape of a memory from a wound into a song.
