A foolish kindness would quiet the room. A small, sentimental mercy would look upon the freezing mist, the earsplitting violence of the bronze relief valve, and the brutal geometry of the sheared iron, and wish to soften it. It would bring warmth, or soil, or silence—and in twenty minutes, the forty Chocks and their trembling Skein would suffocate in the terrible, alien peace, their clear blood gelling into wax.

The power that turns its attention to Valve House 4 is not foolish. It is old enough to remember when the mountains themselves were poured and cured, and wise enough to recognize a cathedral when it sees one, even if the liturgy is written in hydraulic head pressure and forty-cycle-per-second steel hum.

It does not enter the room as a light, because the creatures have no eyes to be comforted by brightness. Instead, the presence enters the chamber as an appreciation of tension. It slips in through the underside of the mountain, riding the two-thousand-foot drop of the snowmelt, and settles into the vibrating membrane of the air.

For a long time—days, perhaps weeks of the colony’s slow, rhythmic existence—the power simply listens. It feels the counterfeit hex bolts clamping down against the sub-audible warning wave. It watches the slate-grey shims skating across the checkered floor plates on the chatter of the acoustic blast, planing their ribbons of neon algae from the concrete grain. It admires the Skein, taut and singing like a bass string in the greasy orange dark, threshing pine pollen and iron oxide out of the freezing vortices.

It sees that the colony is magnificent, and it sees that the colony is doomed.

Not by the harshness of the room, which is their lifeblood, but by the room's builders. Valve House 4 is new. Soon, the routine maintenance schedules will begin. Men in heavy yellow slickers and ear-defenders will clamber down the iron ladders from the pass. They will bring wire brushes to scrape the curious "scale" from the bolt-heads. They will bring buckets of zinc-chromate primer and thick, suffocating grey epoxy paint to seal the raw mill scale of the H-beams against rust. They will wash the concrete down with bleach to kill the green slick on the Baffle Wall, and someone will eventually pick up the grease gun on the workbench, wipe away the strange, tough rope of clear caulk hanging beneath it, and throw it in a trash drum.

And fifty years from now, or ten, a turbine in the valley will be overhauled, and the great head-gates at the top of the mountain will be cranked down, and the penstock will go dry and silent for a week.

The wise power considers the blueprints of men, and the blind, geometric tenacity of the Chocks. It decides to make an adjustment. It will not break the rules of the room; it will use them.

First, it addresses the silence that will one day come. 

Deep beneath the checkered floor plates, where the oversized perimeter grates gulp down the freezing foam into the roaring dark, the concrete piers are anchored directly into the living granite of the mountain pass. Here, in the bedrock, runs the cold, deep fissure from which the ancestors of the Chocks were first drawn when the tunnelers bored the shaft. 

The power reaches into the microscopic interface where the poured concrete meets the raw subterranean stone. It does not crack the foundation; it tunes it. Gently, molecule by molecule, it alters the crystalline structure of the quartz and feldspar veins running beneath the piers, aligning them with the massive steel H-beams above. It turns the underlying bedrock into a sympathetic resonator. 

Then, half a mile down that blind granite fissure, where an underground cascade of snowmelt drops eternally through a natural siphon—a waterfall no human engineer will ever dam, divert, or turn off—the power couples the stone to the water. 

Now, there is a baseline beneath the baseline. While the penstock roars, the new vibration is entirely invisible, lost in the thunder. But if the great pipe is ever drained—if the bronze valve goes quiet and the steel skin grows dead and still—the H-beams and the concrete piers will not fall completely silent. They will continue to hum, fed by the deep, undammable subterranean drop half a mile away. It will be a faint, forty-cycle-per-second murmur, undetectable to a man in boots, just strong enough to keep the clear, antifreeze fluid moving through the microscopic capillaries of forty rubbery hearts while they wait in torpor for the water to return.

Second, the power addresses the metal.

Under the shells of the false hex-bolts and the counterfeit gusset plates, the Chocks are leaching the iron with their weak sulfuric mucus, leaving bright, etched silver scars that would catch the eye of any inspector checking for corrosion. 

The power moves its attention into the biochemistry of that slow digestion. It nudges the sulfur-reducing bacteria living beneath the Chocks' rubbery feet, teaching them a slightly different trade. Instead of stripping the iron bare and leaving it bright, the waste product of their grazing is subtly shifted. As the Chocks creep and feed over the months, the wake they leave behind no longer gleams silver. It oxidizes instantly upon contact with the clammy mist into a dense, ultra-hard layer of dark blue-black magnetite—a passive, microscopic glaze that looks identical to fresh mill scale, yet is completely impervious to rust. 

Now, when the Chocks graze across a beam or a weld, they leave the steel behind them stronger and better sealed than the day it was forged.

Finally, the power looks at the men who will come.

Fifty miles down the valley, in the warm, fluorescent-lit basement of the utility authority’s district office, a maintenance supervisor is writing up the seasonal inspection protocols for the newly commissioned high-altitude penstock. He is a tired, practical man who knows what thirty winters at eight thousand feet do to machinery. 

As his pen hovers over the entry for *Valve House 4*, a quiet intuition settles into his mind. It feels entirely like his own thirty years of experience speaking to him. 

*Leave the raw iron alone in Four,* he thinks, tapping his pen against his teeth. *The condensation in that box is too heavy; paint’ll just blister and trap the water against the beams. Let it breathe.* He writes: **VH-4: Visual inspection of gauge stanchion only. Do not scrape, wire-brush, or paint structural steel or bolt-heads. Do not pressure-wash baffle wall (leave biological film to cushion hydraulic scour).**

Six months later, two linemen climb down into the chamber during a sleet storm. 

They hate the place immediately. The noise is a physical assault that makes their stomachs turn; the freezing mist seeps past the collars of their slickers in seconds. They shine their heavy yellow flashlights around the gloom. 

"Look at that iron," one of them shouts into the other’s ear-cup, his beam sliding over a four-inch triangular gusset plate tucked into the base of an H-beam, and then sweeping up the underside of the sweating ten-foot pipe. "Not a spot of red rust on her. Whatever mill rolled this steel knew what they were doing."

"Check the stanchion so we can get out of here!" the other yells back.

They squint at the brass gauges through the streaming water. One of them notices a second grease gun sitting on the floor near the door, right where he accidentally kicked it on the way in, and picks it up, never looking twice at the old, corroded one sitting in the far corner on the steel workbench, which seems to be stuck to the metal by a thick puddle of spilled, hardened grease. 

They do not notice that there are forty-six bolt-heads on a flange that only calls for forty. They do not notice the three flat, slate-colored shims tucked neatly beneath the lip of the Baffle Wall, waiting for the governor to trip.

The men climb out and dog the heavy steel hatch shut behind them, sealing the dark.

Ten minutes later, the governor trips. The sub-audible wave slams down the pipe. From forty-six bolts and a dozen gusset plates, tiny beads of milky sealant squeeze tight. The bronze valve cracks open, and the boiler-explosion shriek erases the world in a blinding, joyful whiteout of freezing foam. 

In the roaring dark, the shims drop from the beams and begin to skate. The Skein hums in the vortex, fat with mountain pollen and green mash. The ten-foot pipe shudders with the weight of the peaks above, and the mountain beneath answers with its own eternal, hidden hum. 

The wise power withdraws, leaving the cathedral to its choir.
