To a lesser mind, the chamber is an abomination: a stalled wound in the basalt, stinking of ammonia and hot iron, choked with parasites and blind, weeping things that eat the dead. A lesser benevolence might be tempted to *save* them. It might break the rock to let in the clean wind, or clear the foul air, or lift the four slow creatures out of the dark and set them in some sunlit pasture where they would dry out, go blind from the glare, and die of terror within the hour.

Wisdom knows that love is not the imposition of one’s own sky upon another’s roof. Wisdom looks into the wet, amber gloom and sees a cathedral of absolute, hard-won equilibrium. It sees the sixty-cycle hum not as industrial waste, but as a lullaby that has become the heartbeat of four quiet lives.

And so, the Power does not descend with fire or fresh air. It enters the chamber the way the groundwater does: silently, through the micro-fissures, respecting the pressure and the heat. 

It looks upon the four Gantry-Men standing upright against the sweating iron, their cartilaginous hoods pressed to the vibrating glass of the bell-jars, their white fungal coats grazed by blind millipedes. It feels their slow, hydraulic peace. It also sees what they cannot: the fragility of their artificial cosmos. 

Up-shaft, in the forgotten auxiliary substation, the copper windings of the transformer are forty years old, their insulation brittle and flaking. Of the original twenty lamps along the north wall, thirteen are already dark; the remaining seven burn on thinning tungsten and failing ballasts. Above the chamber, in the municipal offices under the sun, a digitized map still bears a dashed red line through this sector—an old wound waiting for a new budget, a new mayor, or a better ventilation fan to wake the thirty-ton boring shield from its rust.

The Power decides. It will not remodel their world; it will consecrate it.

First, it reaches up-shaft into the substation behind the east bulkhead. It does not replace the machinery with something new and silent, for the creatures need the sixty-cycle rattle in their bones to digest their food and mark the hours. Instead, the Power touches the rotting rubber insulation, the pitting copper contacts, and the fatigued steel cores of the transformer. At the molecular level, the metal is annealed; the carbonizing oil inside the cooling drum clarifies and stabilizes. Within the seven surviving bell-jars along the north wall, the sputtering sodium-vapor arcs steady themselves. The vapor pressure inside the thick glass bulbs becomes a closed, immutable loop. They will not burn brighter, and the thirteen dead lamps will not relight, because seven is the number the territory has been divided into, and seven is enough. They will simply never go out.

Second, the Power turns its attention to the surface, two hundred feet above. It does not topple the city, nor does it curse the engineers. It merely nudges the quiet sediment of human bureaucracy. In a municipal database, a geological survey file is subtly corrupted, its seismic risk numbers shifting by two decimal places—just enough to make the deep basalt appear treacherously unstable, riddled with unmapped pockets of pressurized sour gas. On a maintenance supervisor’s screen, the work order to decommission the auxiliary substation slips into an archived table marked *COMPLETED—SEALED*. In the minds of the three or four engineers who have seen the silhouettes on the dead-end inspection cameras, the memory softens, losing its sharp edge of curiosity and settling into the comfortable, unexamined certainty that they were only looking at shadows and wet tarpaulins after all. Five minutes later, the lenses of those cameras are gently, permanently frosted over by a sudden bloom of milky calcite.

Finally, the Power returns to the floor of the vault, moving through the warm, ankle-deep puddles without disturbing a single iron-oxidizing bacterium or parting the waving antennae of a single cricket.

It sees that the four creatures are healthy, well-fed by the rain of the rookery, and content in their slow, blind rhythm. Yet it also perceives a slight, chronic poverty in their chemistry—the slow leaching of trace minerals that never quite make it through the dense basalt from the world above, making the repair of their heavy cartilage hoods a slower, harder labor every decade.

Where the six-foot municipal water main emerges from the rock near the ceiling, sweating cold river-water condensation into the hot air, the Power opens a hairline seam in the surrounding stone—not in the pipe itself, which must hold its pressure so its hollow roar never falters, but in a blind pocket of ancient, mineral-rich brine trapped in the basalt beside it. A trickle no thicker than a knitting needle begins to weep down the cast-iron column nearest the center of the hall. It is warm, heavy with dissolved calcium, magnesium, and sweet trace salts, and as it meets the sixty-cycle hum of the iron, it resonates just slightly louder than the other drips—a new, rich note in the room's acoustic map.

That is all. 

Twenty minutes later, the nearest of the Gantry-Men—the one leaning beneath the fourth sodium lamp, its front pads hooked over the square iron plates of the rock-bolts—feels the new vibration through the three-foot translucent whiskers trailing in the puddle at its base. 

Slowly, with the agonizing, unhurried grace of cooling slag, it unhooks itself from the wall. It lowers its oil-drum bulk back into the warm water, the cave crickets parting before it like grain and closing behind it. It creeps fifty feet along the buried rails until it reaches the central column. Rearward pads planted in the iridescent mud, it raises its shovel-shaped hood and presses its throat-slit against the weeping iron, drinking the warm, salty stone-milk, while the sixty-cycle hum of an eternal grid rattles softly through its bones, and the orange lights burn on, and the small things overhead keep falling like snow.
