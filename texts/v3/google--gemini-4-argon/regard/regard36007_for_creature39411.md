To a power that has watched stars ignite and cool like embers, the mountain-building engine is recognized immediately for what it is: an orphaned script. The architects who seeded the lithic looms are long gone—either ascended, extinct, or simply three galaxies away, having forgotten this world the way a carpenter forgets a dropped nail. Left to its own parameters, the engine will print its basaltic geometry until it hits the upper stratosphere, at which point the atmospheric thinness will starve the looms of moisture and mineral vapor. The pylons will go silent. The bone-white pavilion will power down. And the basin will freeze into a jagged, lifeless needle of glass.

The visiting presence—which has no shape here, existing only as a slight, golden warming of the pearl-gray twilight and a sudden, attentive stillness in the gravity fields—observes the basin for three cycles of the creature’s sleep.

A lesser benevolence might feel pity for the keeper. It might look at the headless, bristle-backed sack of muscle, see its solitary labor among the razor-sharp stones, and decide to "save" it. It might smooth the glass terraces into soft loam, silence the deafening clatter of the mercury-frogs, or lift the creature away to some plush, horizontal paradise where it never has to knap its own feet with a thumb-claw again.

Wisdom stays that hand. 

The power sees clearly that the creature is not suffering; it is *employed*. Its whole heavy, resonant body is an answer to the question posed by this jagged hollow. To take away the sharp glass would make its resinous wrists absurd; to stop the slag-galls would starve its grinding furnace of a gut; to turn off the pavilion’s tension-beams would rob it of that sublime, shivering minute when the water flies from its bristles in a halo of mist. The creature’s dignity is entirely bound up in its competence.

Yet, there are three small cruelties here—one of the future, one of the body, and one of the spirit. And because the power is kind, it decides to make three quiet edits to the basin. It does not break the rules of the mountain; it simply writes three footnotes into the blueprint.

***

The first edit is for the future, written directly into the rotating polyhedrons inside the bone-white pavilion. 

While the keeper hangs asleep in its felt pouch, an invisible finger touches the smallest, fastest-spinning dodecahedron. The instrument does not lose a beat of its metronomic clicking, nor does the keeper wake, though the copper cilia along its chest-seam twitch once at the sudden smell of warm cedar and summer rain—a scent from a world it has never known. 

Deep within the non-baryonic logic of the mountain's blueprint, the vertical limit is erased and replaced with a spiral. 

Now, the mountain will never finish arriving, and it will never starve in the freezing stratosphere. When this basin reaches the threshold where the clouds thin and the heat begins to bleed away into space, the looms here will not shut down; they will subtly shift their vector. They will begin to print sideways, cantilevering out into the pearl-gray sea of mist, building a slow, titanic helix of warm pockets and stepped terraces around the mountain's waist. As an old basin cools behind it, a new one will always be opening just ahead, warm and wet and waiting. The keeper will never run out of mountain to keep. 

***

The second edit is for the body, and it happens the next time the creature wakes to knap its shoes.

The keeper drops from its sling with its heavy *thud-clack*, ambles to a flat step, and sits. Its left front club has grown lopsided from stepping through a stray violet filament the day before. It hooks its woody thumb-claw into a cleavage plane in the black glass and raises its right knuckle to strike.

Before the blow falls, the watching power reaches down into the root of the nearest lithic loom and alters the mineral recipe by four parts per million, drawing up a trace vein of deep-crust magnesium and hyper-dense silicates.

A single, anomalous violet filament snaps from the pylon. It does not strike another pylon; it arcs gently, almost lazily, across the terrace and grounds itself directly against the keeper’s four outstretched, resin-wet wrists. 

The creature tenses, expecting the familiar, clumsy buildup of brittle obsidian. Instead, as the violet light washes over its knuckles, the glass that prints onto its flesh is not black, nor is it razor-sharp. It is a deep, translucent smoky-amber, laced with microscopic, flexible fibers of metallic hornblende. 

When the light fades, the keeper lifts a limb and stares at it with its blind, vibrating chest. It strikes the new hoof experimentally against the edge of a basalt step. Instead of a harsh, shattering *clack*, the contact produces a rich, muted *bong*, like a wooden mallet striking a bronze bell. 

The new material is tough enough to walk on blades forever without chipping, yet slightly yielding at the core where it meets the resinous wrist, absorbing the brutal shock that has been slowly calcifying the creature’s heavy shoulder joints for decades. Better still, the amber glass conducts vibration with ten times the fidelity of the old obsidian. When the keeper sets all four feet down on the terrace, it freezes in astonishment. 

Through the new soles, the basin is no longer just a series of loud cracks and dull hums; it is a symphony in high relief. It can feel the individual heartbeats of the mercury-frogs twenty paces away; it can feel the delicate, papery uncoiling of a pale fern in a rock-joint; it can feel the deep, tectonic breathing of the planet miles beneath the mountain’s roots. For a creature that sees with its feet, it has just been given color.

***

The third edit is the smallest, and it takes place at the jagged rim of the world.

An hour later, the keeper is making its rounds as a gardener. Two of the looms have gone flat in pitch, muffled by a brigade of bread-sized, shell-less snails that are sliming up the dry glass. The keeper plucks four of the heavy, sour beasts from the monoliths, two in each amber-shod hand, and lumbers to the lip of the basin where the pearl-gray clouds drag across the rock.

It drops them into the nothingness.

Then, as it always does, it squats on the precipice, keeping its belly three inches clear of the razor-stone, and waits. It is listening through its new, clear-sighted feet for that tiny, distant tremor in the root of the mountain that means the snails have hit the bottom. 

The power watches the creature wait. It sees that this is not merely a check on pest disposal; it is the closest thing the keeper has to contemplation. It is how the deaf beast measures the vastness of the world outside its village square of rock. It drops a stone into the dark, and waits for the dark to answer.

Usually, the answer is nothing more than a microscopic *thump*—a dead, flat period at the end of a long fall.

Today, as the four snails plummet through the mile-deep curtain of cloud, the power weaves a net of localized gravity currents—the same invisible threads the red ribbon-predators use to swim—far down in the blind chasm below the basin. 

The snails do not hit the bottom. Two thousand feet down, they are caught in an elastic hammock of warped air, decelerated until they are floating like dandelion seeds, and gently deposited onto a warm, wet, lower shelf of the mountain that the looms finished printing three hundred years ago—a place already thick with giant ferns and entirely devoid of pylons to ruin.

In their place, at the exact moment the snails *would* have hit the rocky nadir of the abyss, the power strikes the root of the mountain itself with a single, knuckle-sized pulse of pure gravimetric resonance.

High above, squatting in the wet mist on its smoky-amber hooves, the keeper suddenly stiffens.

Up through the mile of solid basalt, rising through the hexagonal columns and the crystal seams, comes the answer from the deep. It is not the tiny, dead *thump* of crushed snails. 

It is a chord. 

A warm, complex, three-note harmonic rises through the stone, enters the creature’s amber soles, travels up its dense, weary limbs, and sets the heavy gizzard in its belly vibrating like a singing bowl. The chord says nothing in words, for the creature has no words, but in the language of tension, weight, and stone, its meaning is unmistakable:

*The foundation holds. The work is good. You are not alone in the dark.*

The resonance lasts for ten full seconds, turning the entire village square of glass into a warm, humming hearth. Even the mercury-frogs stop clanking for a moment; even the floating instruments in the pavilion pause their clicking for three beats to measure the impossible, golden perfection of the stone's alignment.

When the chord fades back into the root of the mountain, the keeper remains squatting at the rim for a long time, its copper cilia fanned wide, tasting the ozone. 

Then, slowly, it turns back to its square. A seam in the floor cracks with a sharp *ping*, pushing up a fresh inch of steaming black glass; a frog leaps, gets clipped by a violet thread, and leaves a tiny, sizzling blister of carbon on the step. The pavilion’s instruments begin to chatter.

The keeper ambles over on its bell-like feet, eats the mistake, shivers dry in the invisible crosshairs of the pavilion, and climbs into its felt sack to sleep—secure in the knowledge that the mountain has a voice now, and that it likes the way its garden is kept.
