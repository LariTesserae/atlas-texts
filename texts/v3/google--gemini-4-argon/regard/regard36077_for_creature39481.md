A lesser kindness would shatter the walls. 

A lesser kindness, looking down from the thin, singing dark of the upper atmosphere, would take pity on the crushed lung and the amputated wrist. It would reach in with a hand of cool, starlit glass, tear open the woven bamboo ceiling, and spill this fever-knot out into the clean, empty void. 

And in doing so, it would kill the mason instantly. 

Wisdom knows that when a creature has spent eternity holding up a collapsing roof, you cannot simply take the roof away. The mason’s blood is tuned to the boiling pressure of the cistern; in the open air, its veins would burst like deep-sea fish dragged to the surface. Its mind—distributed across a hundred frantic, load-bearing knuckles—knows no grammar but *brace, divert, tally,* and *bind*. Give it an empty horizon, and it would go mad with the terror of an un-shored sky, tearing its own tendons out to stitch the nothingness together.

So the Power does not break the weave. It does not cool the kiln, or silence the roar of the gutters, or stop the blind, cancerous birth of the clay. 

Instead, it introduces a single, new architectural principle. It introduces the *Arch*.

It begins not as a miracle, but as a slight, almost imperceptible shift in the chemistry of the condensation. High up in the three-foot choke, where the split-bamboo is braiding itself shut against the violet vacuum, a single drop of water forms that is not scalding. It is cool. It smells faintly of high-altitude snow, of lichen on ancient granite, of things that do not grow an inch an hour, but an inch a millennium.

The drop falls. It strikes the broad, parboiled back of the mason’s largest trowel-hand just as it is lunging to intercept a newly extruding root-brick.

Where the drop hits the skin, the frantic, hydraulic tremor of the muscle stills for a fraction of a second. The drop rolls down the furrowed, white flesh, slips under the split iron-slag of the fingernails, and soaks into the raw, steaming mud of the new brick.

When the mason’s five thumbs press into the clay, the clay does not simply flatten and square off. Under the blind, habitual pressure of the hands, the mud curves. It bevels. It forms a precise, mathematically perfect trapezoid—a wedge of stone that wants to lean, but cannot fall. 

A keystone.

The bone stylus in the middle finger jabs downward to make its frantic, three-line tally mark—*intrusion, intrusion, intrusion*—but the tip skitters slightly on the cooling surface. Instead of three vertical gashes, it leaves a single, sweeping curve. 

A new instruction has entered the blueprint.

Below your feet, where the floorboards are sprouting roots of raw iron ore, the Power makes its second revision. It does not stop the growth; it gives the growth a vector. The blind, frantic proliferation of matter has been folding inward only because it had nowhere else to go—a seed planted in a closed fist. Now, the Power whispers a single word to the unformed mortar in the seams, a word that belongs to the vocabulary of masonries that are meant to be lived in, rather than merely survived:

*Outward.*

The low, rhythmic thrum behind the bricks—that altitude-sick potter’s wheel—shifts its pitch. It drops an octave, losing its feverish, fluttering edge, settling into the slow, heavy cadence of a cathedral pendulum.

In front of your face, the green yew beam that was shooting across the gap to pierce the creature’s central liver-mass suddenly arrests its linear thrust. Guided by the new geometry in the sap, the wood bends. It arches gracefully overhead, meeting the opposite wall not as a spear, but as a vaulting rib. As its wet rawhide bindings snap tight, it does not steal space from the shaft; it pushes the weeping brickwork *back* by four inches, locking it into a self-supporting curve.

For the first time since the world began to boil, a piece of the wall holds itself up.

You feel the change immediately in the sinew lashed around your collarbone. The violent, crushing tension slackens just enough for your ribs to expand. You are no longer a desperate prop in a collapsing mine; you are a column in a nave.

Behind you, the broad, calloused hand pressed against your spine freezes. 

You can feel the confusion radiating through those five iron-shod thumbs. The mason is waiting for the buckle. It is waiting for the inward crush that requires it to chew a mortise-hole into its own shoulder or sacrifice another forearm to the floor. Its dozens of hands hover in the bruise-purple gloom, fingers twitching like spiders on a web that has suddenly stopped vibrating with the struggles of a fly.

A terracotta crab un-molds itself from the newly vaulted wall. It pulls its legs free with a *suck*, clicks its copper mandibles, and scrapes up a wad of bronze-veined taro leaf. By ancient reflex, it raises its claws to plaster over its own eyes and turn itself back into a dead boss on the stone. 

Instead, its claws pause. The mud it holds is cooler now, infused with a faint, silvery luminescence that does not look like a bruise. Rather than blinding itself, the little clay creature turns and applies the mortar to the raw, bleeding stump where the mason’s lower arm recently tore itself free from the iron-ore root. The mud seals the wound with a gentle, cooling hiss—not calcifying the flesh into a drainpipe, but soothing theparboiled skin, letting the budding embryonic fingers relax, curl inward, and simply rest.

The shaft is still hot. It is still loud with the *slap-drip-chug* of the wooden gutters and the *pop* of bursting seed-pods. It is still a vertical jungle of sweat and terracotta, hanging in the violet nothingness at the roof of the world. The mason would not know how to exist anywhere else.

But as the yew beams continue to weave upward, they are no longer braiding the ceiling shut. They are building a dome. 

Inch by slow, deliberate inch, the walls are extruding themselves *away* from the center, following the quiet, merciful mathematics of the arch. The space widens from three feet to four, then to five. The bloated bladder-ferns un-flatten themselves from the mud, their leaves unfurling into the new, steamy air with a soft, rubbery sigh. The great pigskin lung-sac hanging near your face takes a long, slow inhalation; when it exhales, it does not cough grey lime-phlegm into the trough, but releases a long, resonant note—like wind blowing across the lip of an amphora—that harmonizes with the deep thrum of the walls.

The hand at your back slowly withdraws its iron thumbs from your spine. The smaller, needle-fingered hand reaches up and gently unloops the wet sinew from your collarbone, drawing the living cordage back into the latticework, releasing you from the plumb-line. 

Then, the massive, flat trowel-hand—the one that has known nothing since its birth but the frantic, scalding dark of the crush—slides up along the weeping wickerwork until it reaches the newly formed keystone of the arch. 

It does not strike it. It does not tally it. It simply lays its parboiled palm flat against the cool, curved brick, feeling the weight of the world flow harmlessly around it and down into the self-supporting walls. And there, in the four-inch sanctuary of space that it no longer has to fight for, the hand curls its five thumbs inward, and closes into a quiet, resting fist.
