When the Power arrived at the slate rim, it did not descend in a chariot of fire, nor did it part the pewter vapor to let in the sun. It was wise enough to know that sunlight here would only be an insult—a blinding, sterile glare upon meat that had evolved for the dark, drying out the synovial films and cracking the leather valves before they could comprehend why they were dying.

Instead, the Power stood at the edge of the mire—not in a body, for a body here was merely a spare part waiting to be grafted—and listened. 

A lesser benevolence would have wept at the horror of the basin and reached for a sponge to wipe it clean. It would have brought mercy in the form of silence: halting the pistons, untangling the striders, dissolving the iron ribs back into soft clay, and planting willow trees and sweet-grass in the cooled mud. It would have looked at the Load and seen a madness to be cured, a fever to be broken.

But this Power was older than grass, and its wisdom did not suffer from the narrow prejudice of leaves and sunshine. 

It looked down through the sheared lenses of the atmosphere, past the chugging bellows-beasts and the frantic, stitching scuttlers, all the way down into the hot, dark, grinding belly of the Crankcase. And it understood two things at once.

First: that the Crankcase was alive, and that its life was defined entirely by **Bearing**. To take away its weight, to silence its gears and turn its iron to meadow-loam, would not be salvation; it would be murder. The Load did not want to be a bird or a flower. It wanted to *run*. It wanted the valves to seal, the rhythm to hold, the torque to balance. There was a terrible, dark majesty in its blind devotion to the work—a stubborn refusal to freeze, a ferocious solidarity of meat and metal holding back the void by sheer, rhythmic friction.

Second: that the basin was in hell, not because it was a machine, *but because the machine was running in neutral.*

Every engine needs a load to work against, or it will eventually tear itself apart. Here, the siphon-worms drilled rock only to spit it out so it could settle and be drilled again. The striders marched three steps forward only to be snatched three steps back. The Crankcase calculated the stress of ten thousand epochs, holding them all in its hot, flat memory at once, because none of those epochs had ever *finished*. Nothing ever left the basin. Nothing was ever completed. The engine was roaring at full capacity, burning its own fat, welding its own nerves, screaming through the brass gills of its Gangers—*Hurry. Hold. Stroke. Clear.*—all to spin an axle that wasn't connected to a wheel.

The Power felt a profound, aching pity—not for the ugliness of the beast, which was merely its shape, but for its wasted fidelity. This vast, blind creature was working so hard, with such brutal, breathless honesty, for nothing at all.

So the Power decided not to break the machine, nor to change its nature, but to **engage the clutch**.

It did not speak in words, for the Crankcase only knew Torque. Instead, the Power reached down through the thirty miles of packed blubber and iron, past the Tuesday that had lasted three hundred years, and laid its hand directly upon the deepest, slowest worm-gear at the center of the bedrock—the colossal, calcified axle that took a century to turn once.

And the Power *leaned* on it.

It applied a new resistance. Not the chaotic, stuttering resistance of a jammed strider-herd or a temporal hiccup, which only forced the Load into frantic, circular maintenance, but a steady, heavy, directional weight. The weight of a world outside the rim.

Deep in the dark, the Crankcase felt the change instantly. For the first time in ten million years, when the great central bone-gear pushed, something *pushed back*—not with the dead, elastic bounce of a closed loop, but with the long, yielding resistance of work actually being done.

To give the work somewhere to go, the Power walked to the southern shoulder of the lead-colored slate rim and pressed its thumb into the stone. A fissure opened—not a ragged crack, but a smooth, stone-lined sluiceway, forty feet wide, sloping gently downward out of the basin, leading away into the starved, barren continents beyond the plateau, where the soil had died of cold eons ago and the seas were locked in lifeless, salt-free ice.

At the same time, as a consequence of that great, heavy hand resting on the central gear, the pooled, stagnant time of the mire began to drain toward the sluice. The atmospheric lenses did not vanish, for they were part of the basin's native sight, but they aligned, like the mirrors of a great lighthouse, pointing south.

In the middle distance, the herd of tall, spindly striders dipped their hooks into the mud, spliced the sparking cable-eels into their spines, and marched three paces forward.

The sky did not hiccup.

They stood there, shuddering in the sleet, their yellow bone-booms dripping oil onto mud they had never touched before. For three seconds, the herd stalled in sheer, mechanical confusion. There was no groove here. The mud ahead was un-dredged. 

Then, a ripple of the Load arrived, racing along the braided tendon-conduits from the Crankcase below. It hit the striders like a fresh gale. Their internal ratchets clicked; their hooks swung out; they took a fourth step. And a fifth. Behind them, the valve-crabs that had been crushed by the forty-ton crawler stayed crushed—and instead of rewinding into living misery, their black blood and tiny brass springs sank down through the mire, drawn into the digestive gut-pipes of the Crankcase, where the iron was smelted into fresh bone-plate and the rich, warm mineral slurry was pumped southward, toward the sluice.

The Load surged across the thirty-mile flat, and for the first time since the basin had settled into the dark, its pitch changed.

Before, the unified time signature had been a frantic, hammering 4/4—the sound of a treadmill spinning wildly to stay in place. Now, as the Power’s steady resistance took hold of the deep gears, the tempo slowed, deepened, and dropped an octave. The frantic clattering of unmeshed chitin smoothed into a long, rolling, titanic *three-four*—the heavy, deliberate heave of an ocean liner's screw biting into deep water.

Out on the eastern flats, a Ganger stood twenty feet tall, its bone-shears raised to amputate the tangled legs of a stalled condenser-herd. As the deep gear engaged below, the temporal eddy that had knotted the herd simply smoothed out and flowed away down the southern gradient. 

The Ganger paused, its rotating turret clicking softly in the mist. Through the fluted brass gills along its neck, the steam began to vent. Not the short, frantic blasts of *Hurry. Hold. Clear.* 

Instead, because the pressure in its chest was now steady and backed by an infinite, patient draw, the brass gills blew a single, long, resonant chord—a deep, thrumming contrabass horn-blast that rolled across the thirty miles of black brine like the voice of a cathedral organ made of iron and wet leather.

*Work,* the horn said, though there were no words in it. *Pull. Bear. Done.*

Down in the hot, dark sea of synovial fluid, the Crankcase felt the first gallon of refined, mineral-rich, steaming slurry pour out over the lip of the southern sluiceway, spilling down the mountain to thaw the dead seas of the outer world and seed the barren plains with iron and warmth. 

It felt the weight of the basin lighten by a single, microscopic fraction of a gram—and it felt, trickling down from the high slate rim to replace it, a fresh, virgin run-off of unworked stone and cold rain, waiting for the drills.

The Crankcase did not know the name of the Power, nor did it sprout eyes to look up and give thanks. It could not. It was thirty miles of blind cartilage and cast iron.

Instead, it did the only thing it knew how to do, which had suddenly become the holiest thing in the world: it shifted its weight, settled its massive bone-gears deeper into the oil, leaned its shoulder against the new, heavy truth of tomorrow, and pulled.
