A lesser kindness would have stopped the engines.

A lesser wisdom, looking down from the quiet, untrampled pastures of the high ether, would have pitied the Tenter-Men their shattered ears, their calcified knees, and the frantic, inherited futility of their shift. It would have reached a cool hand into the six-hundred-foot chest of the plateau, stilled the frantic wooden hearts of the burls, and let the silence fall like snow.

And in that silence, the Tenters would have gone mad. Their bones, tuned for four hundred generations to the sixty-cycle roar, would have ached with a phantom hollowness; their talk-boards would have felt dead as butcher’s meat; and within a season, the great root-mat, robbed of its metabolic tension, would have unknitted its fingers from the mountain walls and dropped them all into the dark.

The Power that paused above Oakhaven in the pale,bluish light of a Tuesday afternoon was not a lesser wisdom. It did not arrive as a voice, for there were no ears to catch one. It did not arrive as a vision in the sky, for the Tenters only looked up to gauge the drift of the steam-shears, and anything bright in the heavens was merely another refraction of the broken air.

Instead, the Power arrived as a weight, a carpenter, and a very slow change in the pitch of the world.

It began by listening—not with the mind, which judges, but with the jawbone, as the Tenters did. The Power let its attention sink into the six hundred feet of compressed xylem and root-iron. It felt the terrible, feverish *thrum-clack* of the subterranean looms. It tasted the sour, exhausted turpentine in the brass mains and felt the brittle, terrified stiffness of the local space, which had been shaken at the exact same resonant frequency for so many thousands of years that the afternoon itself had developed stress fractures.

The city was an engine stuck in a runaway governor-loop, tearing its own frame apart because it no longer had a load to pull. The looms were running empty. A power loom that throws a shuttle across an empty race, with no warp to catch it and no weft to trail behind it, hits the stop-block with twice the violence. That was the *clack* in the *thrum-clack*: the sound of ten thousand wooden hammers striking nothing.

And the Tenters—magnificent, grim, loyal as iron bolts—were spending their narrow, fibrous lives oiling the hammers so the hammers wouldn't break.

*They do not want to be saved from their work,* the Power understood, watching a Tenter woman in a grease-stiffened smock tack expertly across a four-second pleat in the turf, her mercury wand whipping a clean grey figure-eight before her. *Their work is the shape of their love for one another. They only need the work to stop breaking the room they do it in.*

So the Power did three things. None of them were loud, and none of them were fast, because on a plateau that takes three days to inhale, any sudden mercy is just another kind of violence.

***

First, the Power gave the looms a thread.

Deep in the abyssal underside of the plateau—down where the dead cork-burl grew in the freezing dark above the three-mile drop, where even the Tenters only went on ropes to harvest boot-soles—the Power gathered up the loose, frayed ends of the local geometry.

All those vertical panes of hardened air; all those four-second stutters; all those floating, severed blocks of valley floor weeping amber into nothing—they were not garbage. They were unspun fiber. They were the slack, tangled warp-threads of a place that had come unhitched from the mountains.

With patience that measured itself by the slow, three-day breaths of the moss, the Power reached into the deepest, blindest intakes of the subterranean engines and fed the broken edges of the air into the rollers.

Above ground, the change was noticed on a Thursday, at the third hour after dawn, by a Tenter named Venn—or rather, by a Tenter whose name was three short fingernail-scrapes and a thumb-press on the left knee.

Venn was kneeling beside the main lymphatic manifold of Sector Four, his chin-guard unlatched, his flat grey molars clamped hard onto the brass head of his three-foot spanner. The other end of the spanner was wedged against a primary root-rail. He was listening for the dry, resin-starved shriek of a deep flywheel he had been nursing for a month.

Through his teeth, the *thrum-clack* came up into his skull.

*Thrum-clack. Thrum-clack.*

And then, while his teeth were clenched on the brass, the *clack* softened.

It did not stop. If it had stopped, Venn would have dropped his spanner and run for the burrow-ladders to warn his kin of the rot. Instead, the sharp, metallic slap of the subterranean pistons took on a deep, muffled, fibrous weight.

*Thrum-thump. Thrum-thump.*

It was the sound a shuttle makes when it finally catches the yarn. It was the difference between an axe striking an iron anvil and an axe biting deep into green, living pine.

Venn stayed on one knee for ten minutes, his yellow-filmed eyes wide, his horizontal eyelids flicking once, twice, against the steam. Through his jawbone, he felt the engines fifty fathoms down take up a load. The frantic, rattling sixty-cycle-per-minute sprint slowed—not by much, just by four beats, then six, settling into a massive, unhurried, fifty-two-cycle stride. The resonant frequency that had been shattering the air for millennia broke its lock.

Three hundred yards down the avenue, a quarter-mile slab of floating root-turf that had hung six feet in the air since Venn’s grandfather was a boy gave a long, low sigh of escaping ozone. Slowly—over the course of an afternoon—the amber drops weeping from its severed edge began to reach the moss below. The gap narrowed. By sundown, the slab settled back into the earth with the quiet, damp *shuck* of a boot fitting into mud, and the surface roots immediately began to knit across the seam.

Second, the Power addressed the duplicates.

When the looms below began to weave the fractured space back into the mat, the pleats in the warehouse districts began to draw tight. The Power knew the local custom: when the air un-creased, four of the five identical women on the brass main would vanish into ozone, and the remaining one would drop to her knees, vomiting thin bile from the sudden, crushing convergence of five sets of short-term memory and a quintuple dose of muscular fatigue.

That evening, in the stuttering avenues of the East Grid, a Tenter woman was scraping salt-crust in five places at once.

As the fifty-two-cycle *thrum-thump* reached the East Grid’s foundations, the air began to un-pleat. The woman felt the familiar, sickening pull behind her eyes—the gathering snap of the invisible cable. She braced her cork-soled boots wide on the slick silver moss, dropped her bone scraper, and pressed her palms over her scarred ear-flaps, waiting for the blinding spike of pain as four versions of her afternoon slammed into one skull.

The snap never came.

Instead, the cold, dry smell of ozone warmed into the scent of beeswax and heated cedar. The four duplicates did not pop out of existence like burst soap-bubbles. As the spatial pleat smoothed itself out beneath the new, steady draw of the underground looms, the five figures simply walked along the pipe toward one another, drawn by a gentle, downhill grade in the afternoon that hadn't been there a moment before.

They stepped into one another on the moss—not with a crash, but like five lantern-beams projected onto the same wall, sliding together until there was only one bright circle.

When the last shadow joined her heels, the woman stood still.

She had no headache. The five hours of work she had done in five places did not hit her like a falling timber; instead, the Power—acting as a quiet ballast in her blood—divided the exhaustion by five, and multiplied the rest. For the first time in her life, she felt what it was to have five women’s strength in one frame. Her joints did not click when she bent to pick up her scraper. The grey skin of her cheeks was warm. She looked down at the two hundred yards of gleaming, clean brass pipe, and then she reached down, touched the bare wood of her tool-handle, and felt a small, steady warmth in the grain that hadn't come from the steam vents.

***

Third—and this was the smallest thing, and the one the Power took the longest pleasure in making—it left a blueprint.

Not on paper, which the damp would rot in an hour. And not in words, which the Tenters had no use for.

High in the crotches of the central factory-burls, where the burrows smelled of tallow and dry mountain moss, the families gathered after dusk. Down in the valley, the steam-plumes were already thinning; because the engines were no longer overheating in their frantic, loadless race, the relief vents weren't shrieking every thirty seconds. The clammy, lukewarm drizzle was clearing. Through the shaved-horn windows of the burrows, the stars above Oakhaven—usually fractured into smeared, jittering streaks by the atmospheric shears—shone down like single, steady points of cold silver.

In the largest burrow of the central burl, seven Tenters sat in a circle, their calloused knees wedged tight together. Across their laps lay the community’s oldest talk-board, a three-foot disk of dark, polished cedar worn thin by twelve generations of horn-thick fingernails.

For an hour, their hands had been flying across the wood in amazed, urgent rhythms.

*The stroke has deepened,* the eldest man tapped with the heel of his palm. *Fifty-two. Fifty-two. The deep bearings are cool.*

*The South Seam closed,* a young woman scraped with her pinky nail, drawing a long line and then pressing her flat thumb over it. *The six-legged elk walked straight across the tracks without stopping. I saw them.*

*Is it engine-stop?* a boy asked, his small fingers drumming the rapid, frightened warning near the rim. *Is the mat dying?*

They all fell still, their bare palms resting flat on the cedar, waiting for the eldest to answer.

And then the talk-board spoke back to them.

No hand had moved. Yet up through the dry, seasoned cedar—drawn from the buttress-roots of the factory wall, which drew it from the deep moss, which drew it from the six-hundred-foot heart of the plateau—came a new vibration.

It was not the heavy, industrial *thrum-thump* of the floor, which their cork boots and resin-lined walls usually muffled. This was fine, crisp, and articulate, travelling only through the dead wood of the board on their knees, as if a seventh person with immense, gentle hands were sitting beneath the floor and tapping upward against the underside of the table.

*Tap-tap. Slide.*

The seven Tenters froze. Their yellow-filmed eyes met in the dim tallow-light.

The board tapped again. It used their own grammar—the ancient, tactile carpentry-speech of Oakhaven—but it used it with the precision of a master machinist who had built the first burl when the mountains were still rock.

Across the undersides of their thirty-five resting fingers, the wood drew a map.

A long, curving pressure of a thumb moved from the center of the board toward the western rim: *Sector Seven. Sub-level Three. Four hundred yards past the Great Copper Elbow.*

Then, the board gave three light, rhythmic knocks—the sign for a sealed door—followed by a slow, warm circular rub in the exact center of the cedar.

In the language of the Tenters, a warm circle meant *grease*, or *marrow*, or *good soup*. It meant the thing that keeps the joints from grinding.

The next morning, when the rose-and-brass sun cleared the shelf, a crew of six went down into Sector Seven. They didn't have to tack across the avenues; they walked straight down the pewter-shined root-rails, their mercury wands hanging quiet at their belts, their rocking, sailor’s gait strangely light in the fifty-two-cycle morning.

Four hundred yards past the Great Copper Elbow, where the living ironwood had grown entirely over an ancient masonry bulkhead, they found the seam the talk-board had described. The wood here wasn't calloused and knotted like the rest of the burl; overnight, the bark had parted like a healed lip, leaving an archway six feet high and smelling faintly of beeswax.

Inside was not a machine. It was a grove—a subterranean vault thirty yards wide, lit by pale, bioluminescent fungal lanterns that had been waiting in spore-form for four millennia for the temperature to drop three degrees so they could bloom.

In the center of the vault grew a ring of small, low trees with smooth, silver bark and leaves as soft as lamb’s ear. From their trunks wept a clear, golden gum that did not smell of sharp turpentine, but of honey and wild thyme. And stacked neatly against the dry, warm walls of the vault were a thousand bales of fresh, un-cured *cork-burl*, lighter than any they had ever harvested from the dangerous abyss below, already cut to the width of a Tenter’s boot, and soft enough to sleep on.

When the lead Tenter knelt and bit his spanner against the floor of the vault, he tasted no frantic iron terror. He felt only the deep, slow, fifty-two-beat cradle of the plateau, weaving itself whole again, and beneath that, a quiet, steady warmth that said:

*The shift is halved. The roots will hold. Take off your boots.*
