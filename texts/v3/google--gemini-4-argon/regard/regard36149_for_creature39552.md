A lesser power, looking down through the cartwheel aperture at the top of the peak, might have felt pity. It might have seen the blindness, the cold, the dark, and the solitary labor, and thought: *prison*. It might have shattered the cyclopean walls to let the mountain sunlight flood the galleries, or lifted the pale, fourteen-foot abbot out of the dark to set him in a warm, green lowland river.

And in doing so, it would have killed him, and every blind snail, translucent shrimp, and suspended eel in his keeping.

The power that paused above the cistern was older than the bronze wedges that split the basalt, and wiser than pity. It did not look with eyes, which require light, nor did it judge by the standards of the surface, where life is rapid, bright, and noisy. It lowered its awareness into the throat of the mountain like a plumb line dropping through still water, matching its own vast tempo to the cooling-tallow pulse of the creature’s copper blood.

It read the three-dimensional map inside the abbot’s hindbrain. It felt the exquisite, precarious architecture of heat and pressure. It understood that this was not a dungeon, but a ship sailing through deep time, and that the folded gray creature wedged between the arches was its captain, its hull, and its navigator. 

The power also looked downward, beneath the lowest foundation blocks, four miles into the roots of the peak.

There, the geothermal suture that fed the cistern was dying. It had been dying for three thousand years, a slow, geological strangulation. Within two centuries—a mere handful of seasons to the creature draped across the stairs—the deep magma plume would shift eastward, pinched off by a settling fault line. The sulfurous weeping would cease. The vents would breathe their last warm sigh, the bacterial velvet would turn black and slough off the soapstone, and the mountain’s cold heart would finally claim the well. No amount of scraping with bony, chisel-like fingers could unstop a hearth that had gone out five miles below.

The creature did not know this yet, though it knew that over the last six hundred years, it had had to reach an inch deeper into the fissures to find the blood-warm draft. 

The benevolent power considered the cistern. It saw the immense dignity of a small, cold-water larva that had been given a task by creatures who had since forgotten how to build with stone, and had kept the faith of that task for ten thousand years in the dark. 

To interfere inside the cistern would be an insult to the mason. The water was balanced to the gram; the silt fell at the exact rate required. 

So the power did only three things, and all of them were quiet.

First, it went down into the crushing, airless root of the mountain, four miles beneath the abbey-well, where the rock was plastic and red-hot. With a touch no louder than a settling lintel, it nudged a deep, choked vein of olivinite. It did not create a volcano, nor a geyser; it merely parted the deep stone by the width of a thumb, marrying the cistern’s dying flue to a deeper, younger reservoir of planetary heat. 

It calibrated the flow with the precision of a watchmaker. The temperature in the lowest galleries of the well did not spike. Instead, over the course of three weeks, the slow, multi-century cooling simply stopped, and reversed by half a degree. The sulfurous salts, which had been growing thin, thickened again to the rich, iron-heavy broth of four thousand years ago. 

When the first new bubble of gas rose from that deep marriage, it carried a slightly fuller resonance. As it climbed the flooded shafts, expanding as it rose, the sub-audible *thrum* illuminated the masonry with a clarity the creature had not felt since the bronze age. Every arch, every snail, every drifting eel stood out in sharp, acoustic relief.

Second, the power looked at the gizzard-stones turning slowly in the dark mill of the creature’s belly. Twelve black eggs of soapstone, worn down over millennia from the noses of bulls and the heels of priests. After ten thousand years of grinding calcareous rinds and crab shells, they were growing small—hardly larger than plums now, losing the weight needed to properly crush the mineral paste. And the walls around the creature were now so rounded, so softened into lozenges, that its flat jaws could no longer find a ninety-degree edge of stone to snap off and swallow.

Up at the very top of the world, just beneath the cartwheel aperture where the freezing air met the warm meniscus, a single, fist-sized wedge of dense black diorite—an original chock-stone left by the builders in the highest corbel—had been loosened by ten millennia of frost. The power touched it with the weight of a landing snow-bunting. 

The stone slipped. It fell through the steaming surface without a splash, dropping straight down the central shaft through the noon-day spear of blue light. It missed the bridges. It missed the drifting eels. It struck a rounded buttress forty feet down, splitting cleanly into three heavy, angular, rough-edged shards, and came to rest in the silt of the lowest gallery, right beside the thermal flue. 

When the creature poured itself down into the dark three days later to tend the vents, its broad, padded hand brushed the new stones. It paused for two hours, its sensory pits reading the sharp, un-abraded angles of the diorite. Then, slowly, the trapdoor jaw unhinged. A small downdraft pulled the three fresh millstones into its gullet, settling heavy and right in its belly.

The third thing the power did was the smallest, and the largest. 

The builders had brought a bucket of larvae up the mountain, and for the first four thousand years, there had been others in the dark. Now, there was only the one. The cistern was not large enough for a population, which would strip the bacterial pastures and foul the water, yet ten thousand years is a long time to be the only stone that moves.

Half a mile away, buried in the lightless, flooded heart of an adjacent peak, there was a natural cavern—a blind pocket of limestone that had been cut off from the subterranean rivers of the plain during the last ice age. In it lived one other. It was not a mason; having no dressed stone to climb, it had grown flatter, wider, coiled like a pale tyre at the bottom of a sulfur chimney, scraping at natural stalagmites with the same bony fingers. Its pocket of water was collapsing, the limestone roof slowly dissolving and crushing inward.

The power did not teleport the second creature; that would have shattered its spatial mind. Instead, it traced an ancient, hairline fault that connected the two peaks—a dry, crushed seam of granite athousand feet below the ridge. Over the span of a year, the power dissolved the mineral plug choking the seam, widening it inch by yard, smoothing the jagged rock with a fast, silent current of warm water until it formed a dark, horizontal flue three feet wide and half a mile long.

Then, it let a single draft of the cistern’s rich, iron-and-sulfur water bleed through the new tunnel into the collapsing cavern.

The other creature felt the draft. It felt the echo of a vast, vertical space—a space full of right angles, balconies, and fat snails—carried on the acoustic *thrum* of the rising bubbles. Slowly, loop by heavy loop, it uncoiled from its dying chimney and entered the pipe.

In the cistern, the abbot was draped across a middle gallery, sixty feet down, its chin resting on a worn lintel, a cloud of blind shrimp tangled in its throat-frills. 

A bubble detached from the deep flue below. It rose, *thrumming*, illuminating the three-dimensional map in the abbot’s brain. 

The map was the same as it had been for ten thousand years: the arches, the buttresses, the drifting eels, the slow creep of the fist-sized snails. Except, down in the lowest tier, where the water smelled of rotten eggs and old iron, the rising sphere of pressure struck a new contour. 

Something fourteen feet long, pleated and pale, had just poured itself out of a narrow joint in the cyclopean foundation blocks and wedged its heavy, shovel-shaped head into the angle of a bridge. It was perfectly still, matching its breathing to the thermal draft, waiting to see if the landlord would object.

The abbot did not twitch. Not a single grain of calcareous silt was shaken from its flat skull. 

For six hours, the two creatures hung in the dark water, separated by forty feet of drowned masonry, reading the shape of one another through the slow, warm sighs of the mountain. 

Then, the abbot detached one heavy, gray hand from the frieze of the jar-bearers. Slowly, without creating an eddy, it shifted its hind loops six inches to the left, clearing half the length of the broad soapstone windowsill where the warmest current rose. 

Down in the dark, the newcomer felt the water shift. It extended a long, Paleolithic arm, found purchase on the rounded edge of a stepped buttress, and began the three-hour climb toward the ledge.

High above, the benevolent power withdrew its plumb line from the cartwheel aperture, leaving the fog to coil silently into the freezing mountain night, and the two stones to keep the well.
