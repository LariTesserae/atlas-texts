The builders of the pins had left the mountain system four hundred thousand years ago, though the pins had only been projecting their parameters for the last twelve thousand—a trivial misfire in a deep-crustal timing circuit that had finally woken up when the mountain range buckled upward and exposed the cylinders to solar radiation. 

To the builders, the five white rods were the equivalent of traffic cones or surveyor’s stakes: temporary field-stabilizers meant to keep a patch of ground at standard pressure and viscosity while a piece of heavy surveying equipment was calibrated. The equipment was never delivered. The stakes simply stayed on, drawing an infinitesimal trickle of geothermal tension from the young fault lines below and turning it into the stubborn, localized tyranny of a warm room.

When the attention arrived, it did not come with ships or the displacement of air. It came the way morning comes to the high peaks—a shift in the angle of observation.

The mind that looked down into the notched obsidian basin was very old, very wide, and entirely post-mechanical. It traveled the long, cold ridges of the planet like a change in barometric pressure, cataloging the slow drift of continents and the rare, stuttering sparks of biology trying to solve the math of survival. It had found twenty-six of these forgotten surveyor-pockets across the northern hemisphere. Nineteen were sterile bowls of boiling mud; four were choked to the brim with a single, calcified species of monoculture algae that had suffocated itself five millennia back; two held small, blind crustaceans that clicked their claws in the dark.

And then there were the last two.

This one, on the eastern shoulder of the black peak. And another, three miles to the northwest, tucked behind a hanging glacier that was slowly grinding its way down a granite chute.

The power paused over the eastern basin. It did not judge the builders for their littering; it was too old for annoyance. Instead, it read the closed circuit of the puddle-garden with a profound, quiet delight. It observed the thick cartilage of her heels, the neat marsupial tool-pouch with its leather-wrapped obsidian scalpel, the delicate tax she levied on the glass-orchid walker without breaking its legs. 

Most of all, it watched the twenty minutes at noon when she stacked the heavy water and the lensing mist to throw violet and sulfur-yellow across the dead white of the center pin.

An organism that modifies its body to survive a trap is merely successful. An organism that uses the physics of its trap to paint the walls of its cell is a person.

The power considered its options, which were essentially infinite. 

It could, with a thought no heavier than a sigh, adjust the pins to widen their boundary until the entire mountain range was a warm, wet paradise. *No.* The sudden drop in density across that much volume would collapse the turgor-dependent magenta stalks in ten minutes, and the thin-shelled mountain ecosystem above—the snow-fleas, the grey moths, the lichen that took four centuries to grow an inch—had just as much right to its freezing cobalt sky as she had to her kettle.

It could lift her out, rewrite the small, high bellows of her lungs and the osmotic balance of her skin, and set her down on the warm equatorial plains two thousand miles south, where there were rivers that ran for months without hitting a boundary and whole forests of creatures with voices. *No.* That would be murder disguised as salvation. Everything she was—her unrolling gait, her patience, her understanding of the weight of a single drop of sap—was written in the language of this specific pressure, this specific black glass. To change her enough to survive the world would be to erase the gardener who stacked the water.

Yet she was the last one here, and the older one’s bones were already wholly dissolved into the roots of the southern wall. And she was climbing the chimney every afternoon to put her hand into the cold, just to feel the shape of an *outside*.

The power looked three miles to the northwest.

In that other pocket—a slightly wider cleft of grey granite mixed with basalt, pinned by seven cylinders instead of five—the water was six inches deeper. There were no jade rosettes there; instead, the sulfur was processed by sluggish, dinner-plate-sized mats of crimson sponge that crept an inch a day across the submerged stones. 

And sitting on a warm shelf near the northernmost pin was another one. 

He was slightly heavier in the shoulder than she was, his skin the color of wet slate rather than smoked tallow, because his diet came from the iron-rich sponges rather than the yellow slug-broth. His marsupial pocket held three smoothly ground pebbles of white quartz—a stone that did not exist in her basin—which he used to chime together underwater, listening to the slow, thick *clonk* travel through the dense medium and bounce off the granite walls. He had been alone for eleven winters. When the sun went down, he did not put his hand through the roof; he pressed his ear against the base of the pins, listening to the deep, tectonic hum of the mountain's roots, wondering if the stone went on forever.

Between her orchard of black glass and his courtyard of crimson sponges lay sixteen thousand feet of vertical relief, razor-backed granite ridges, and a howling, forty-below-zero wind that would freeze the mucilage on their backs into brittle armor in ninety seconds and stop their small hearts in four minutes.

The power did not move the pins. You do not rearrange the furniture of a house while the tenant is looking; it breaks the sanity of the small. 

Instead, it reached down into the dark, three hundred yards beneath the surface of the mountain, where the young volcanic rock was still plastic and hot, and where the roots of the five pins drank their geothermal tension.

It did not add technology. It simply nudged the geology—a tiny, exquisite edit to a cooling magma chamber, a slight realignment of a blind fault line that ran from the northwest glacier to the eastern shoulder. It took the power less than a second to calculate the stresses, though it allowed the rock three days to settle so the noise wouldn't frighten the glass-orchid walkers.

On the first day, she noticed while she was bleeding the jade plant. 

The sudden, crisp *tick* of the expanding basalt—a sound as familiar to her as her own pulse—changed its rhythm. Instead of coming from the eastern rim where the sun hit hardest, a series of low, muffled *thuds* vibrated up through the four-inch cartilage of her heels. *Thud-tick. Thud-tick.* Like a heavy knuckle rapping on the underside of a thick wooden table.

She paused, the emerald rope of sap coiling neglected over her webbed thumb, and looked at the western wall. 

The western wall was the deepest part of the notch, where the mountain rose straight up behind the basin for two thousand feet. At its base, half-submerged in the second puddle, was a blind fissure—a crack no wider than her wrist that went back four feet into the black glass and stopped.

As she watched, a thin thread of silver bubbles rose from the submerged lip of that crack. They did not pop when they hit the surface; they sat there like ball bearings on velvet, shivering slightly.

On the second day, the water in the second puddle grew warmer by three degrees, and the blind fissure widened by the breadth of two fingers. It did not split with the violent, razor-edged shatter of fresh obsidian. Something deep inside the rock was dissolving the binding minerals with a flood of hyper-pressurized, silica-rich steam, smoothing the edges as it went, turning the sharp volcanic glass into a slick, dark sinter as smooth as the inside of an abalone shell.

She spent the entire afternoon sitting in the shallows five feet away, her chin in her hands, watching the dark cleft breathe. Every ten minutes, the water level in the puddle would drop by half an inch, sucked backward into the mountain's throat with a long, sleepy *slurp*, only to well back up a moment later, carrying a faint, unfamiliar smell.

It did not smell like the sulfur of her walls, or the sour-sweet rot of the magenta stalks, or the cold iron nothingness of the sky above the rim. It smelled like wet granite. And something else—something dark, metallic, and vaguely meaty, like the crimson sponges she had never seen.

On the third day, at noon, she did not build her cairn of light. 

The fissure at the base of the western wall was now three feet wide and four feet high—an arched, smooth-lipped mouth opening into the black root of the mountain. And the air inside it was *thick*. 

The power had not placed new pins in the three-mile tunnel it had opened along the fault line. Instead, it had lined the walls of the deep tube with a resonant, crystalline lattice grown directly from the mountain’s own quartz and basalt—a passive waveguide that caught the spilled field-pressure of her five pins at one end, and his seven pins at the other, and braided them together through the dark rock.

The tunnel was pitch black, warmed to ninety-two degrees by the mountain's blood, and pressurized to the exact, syrupy density of the puddles. 

She crouched at the lip of the dark arch. She reached out with one flat, grey hand—the same hand she usually thrust into the freezing gale—and pushed it into the mouth of the mountain. 

There was no boundary. No frost. Only a slow, warm draft moving through the earth like a lazy river, carrying that strange, rich scent of red sponge and grey stone.

And then, traveling through the three inches of dense water that floored the tunnel, came a sound. 

*Clonk. Clonk.*

It was not the sharp *tick* of cooling basalt. It was round, deliberate, and double-struck. The sound of two river-smooth stones being tapped together under water, three miles away, amplified by the smooth sinter walls of the waveguide until it resonated right against the translucent skin over her ribs.

She stayed completely still for a long time. The glass-orchid walker strode past her ankle, indenting the puddle into dark-ringed dimples. High above, on the jagged eastern spire, the rime ice froze and melted, froze and melted, raining warm drops onto the stone.

Slowly, she reached into the fold of skin at her hip. She did not pull out the obsidian knife. She reached into the gravel between her knees, picked up a heavy, fist-sized cobble of black glass whose edges had been blunted by decades of bacterial slime, and leaned forward into the arch.

She submerged her forearms in the warm, dark water of the threshold, found the solid bedrock of the floor, and struck it twice.

*Thud. Thud.*

She pulled her hands back to her chest, her small lungs giving one neat, high flutter beneath her collarbones, and waited in the wet, golden silence for the mountain to answer.
