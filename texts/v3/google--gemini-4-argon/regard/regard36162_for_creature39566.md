The attention does not arrive as a beam of light or a sudden warmth. Warmth would be a catastrophe here; a five-degree rise in temperature would melt the drafter’s glass snares, drown the walkers in their own dissolving legs, and rot the sulfur-mold into black slime. 

The power that notices Junction 14-Sub-Zero is old enough to know that love is not the imposition of comfort. It knows that to a creature with ammonia in its veins and iron teeth in its belly, a green meadow is hell. 

So the attention enters the cistern on the fifty-cycle hum of the violet ballast tube, riding the alternating current without changing its frequency by a fraction of a hertz. It settles into the triangular void with the patience of deep bedrock, observing the forty-minute winter and the twelve-minute spring.

It sees the perfection of the trap. And because it sees time not as a line, but as an architecture, it sees the three ways this tiny, clockwork world is scheduled to end.

First: the drain-trap at the lowest corner of the floor. The rimstone is growing at a millimeter a week. In fourteen months, the pearl-grey false flint will pinch the drain’s bronze throat shut. The next blow-down cycle will not gulp away; it will pool, reach the sub-zero western bulkhead, and freeze solid from wall to wall, entombing the thirty-nine walkers in a single block of yellow ice.

Second: the porcelain-caged inspection lamp. Its thick carbon filament has been burning since the Cleveland administration. Near its center, a microscopic fracture has already formed, sublimating carbon onto the inside of the glass. In nineteen days, when the great flywheels three levels up engage the night-shift dynamos, the seven-minute shudder will snap that filament. The orange-red heat will vanish, the convection currents that lift the golden spores into the drafter’s snares will die, and the upper cistern will starve.

Third: the city above. In six years, the municipal traction engines will be decommissioned in favor of high-tension rotary converters. A salvage crew will come down with oxy-acetylene torches to cut out the copper feedlines and the lead conduits. They will open the bulkhead, let in the dry, dead air of the sub-basement, and scrape the cistern clean.

A lesser benevolence might sweep the creatures up and carry them to some distant, primordial sulfur-vent at the bottom of the sea. 

This power is wiser. It knows that these beings are not mistakes trapped in a cellar; they are the children of the municipal traction engines. Their blood is the pickling vat; their bones are the boiler-scale; their rhythm is the brass governor on the eastern wall. To remove them from the machine is to erase what they are.

So the power does three very small things. It acts only through the laws of the room—through tension, crystallization, and the slow creep of metal.

First, it addresses the lamp. 

During the forty-minute freeze, while the room is still, a stray whisker of copper-sulfate crystal—no thicker than a silk thread—grows across the damp porcelain socket of the inspection lamp. The power nudges the geometry of its lattice as it forms. The crystal bridges a tiny gap between the brass screw-shell and the iron cage, creating a minute parasitic resistance. 

Instantly, the current reaching the carbon filament drops by four percent. 

The orange light dims by a shade no human eye could detect. The temperature of the filament drops by eighty degrees—not enough to kill the convection current that feeds the drafter, but just enough to halt the sublimation of the carbon. At the same time, the power encourages a thin, gelid collar of black oil-lichen to creep up the base of the porcelain cage, cushioning it against the iron standpipe. When the seven-minute shudder passes through the room, the lamp no longer jars; it sways, damped by the grease-fed lichen. The filament will now burn for another forty years.

Second, it looks to the drain, and to the thirty-nine walkers cemented into the rimstone.

The walkers are already masons, scraping the floor with their precipitated iron teeth. They only lack a reason to quarry the drain. 

Wait for the brass governor to click. Wait for the counterweight to drop with its heavy, oiled sigh.

As the relief pipe coughs its five gallons of greywater across the terraces, the power reaches up into the pneumatic accumulator two levels above, where the leather gaskets bleed their grease. With a touch lighter than a draft, it worries a microscopic flaw in an oakum seal, right beside a brass fitting rich with zinc and phosphor-bronze. 

When the blow-down water surges into the cistern this time, it carries an invisible trace of heavy, phosphor-tainted tallow. Because of its specific gravity, this tallow does not float across the upper terraces; it sinks, riding the current straight down to the lowest corner of the floor, plating the lip of the drain-trap just as the hollow, metallic gulp sucks the water away.

Before the steam has even cleared, seven of the scab-walkers abandon their usual niches on the upper steps. Drawn by the sharp, metallic tang of the phosphor-grease—which makes a far superior, lower-freezing-point oil for their joints than the old gasket-scum—they skitter down their needle-stilts to the drain. They park themselves in a tight ring around the bronze throat, lower their domes, and begin to rasp furiously at the encroaching rimstone to get at the grease trapped in its pores. 

From this day on, the drain will never calcify shut. The walkers will keep it bored clean to the bare metal, maintaining their own abyss.

Third, and last, the power looks at the floor beneath the drain, and at the six years remaining before the men with torches come.

Beneath the cistern’s floor lies six feet of high-alumina concrete, then a lead liner, then the iron casing of the deep-drainage viaducts. And beneath the viaducts, eighty feet deeper still, lies the old, abandoned brick intercepting sewer of 1848—a flooded, pitch-black cavern three miles long, choked with mineral-heavy groundwater, decaying timber pilings, and the bitter, chemical seepage of a hundred factories, sitting right alongside the buried return-mains of the district heating company. Warmth and cold. Brine and sulfur. A continent waiting in the dark.

The cistern does not need to be saved forever. It only needs a door.

Inside the throat of the drain-trap, two feet down where the cast-iron pipe passes through the concrete slab, there is a casting flaw—a tiny, ancient air-bubble in the iron, sealed over by seventy years of scale. 

During the twelve-minute thaw, when the drafter in the horsehair canvas overhead secretes its drops of warm, caustic potash to melt its glass snares, one drop misses the uptake pore. 

It falls. 

It does not hit a walker this time. Guided by a fractional shift in the electrostatic field of the violet tube, the drop of potash falls straight between the standpipes, misses the rimstone terraces entirely, and plummets into the open mouth of the drain-trap, landing squarely on the casting flaw in the iron pipe.

It hisses, eating away a thousandth of an inch of iron and scale before the next cycle washes it down. 

Forty minutes later, another drop falls in the exact same spot. And forty minutes after that, another.

It will take four years for the potash, the rust-water, and the scraping iron teeth of the walkers—who will follow the tasty, corroded metal down into the pipe—to eat through the iron casing, pit the lead liner, and honey-comb the concrete beneath. 

Two years before the traction engines stop and the salvage crews arrive with their torches, the bottom of the drain-trap will quietly give way into a fist-sized fissure leading down into the eighty-foot shaft of the old brick interceptor. The weeping brine from the cistern will begin to trickle down that dark chimney, coating the old brick in fresh, glassy glaze. Spores of pale yellow sulfur-mold will ride the draft down. A bead of the drafter’s jelly, clinging to a shed dome of boiler-scale, will wash through the fissure and catch on a warm district-heating pipe in the abyss below. The walkers will follow the rimstone staircase down into a cavern ten thousand times the size of their cistern, where no inspection hatch will ever be pried open.

Having set the drop to fall, the power does nothing more. It does not leave a mark, or wake a single mind inside those rubbery gray gizzards, or change the harsh, ammonia-sharp tang of the air. 

The steam vanishes into the bronze mesh of the intake vents. The miniature blizzard stops. Down on the grating, the thirty-nine walkers fold their freshly extruded stilts beneath them and let the weeping stone cement them back into the floor. The violet tube hums at fifty cycles. The dimmed orange filament burns steadily in its grease-cushioned cage, throwing long, red shadows across the wet steps of the basin, while the mountain range continues to build itself in the dark.
