A lesser mercy would have brought them the sun. 

A foolish, golden-hearted god, looking down through three hundred feet of mountain shale into that cold and roaring dark, would have pitied their blindness. Such a power might have cracked the ceiling to let a shaft of April warmth fall into the central basin, or quieted the deafening static so the creatures might finally hear themselves think, or softened their calcified hides into something that could know the pleasure of a breeze.

And in doing so, that god would have murdered them in an afternoon. The silence would have been an amputation; the warmth would have soured the tallow-slime; the light would have been nothing to their eyeless domes but a drying fever.

The Power that actually paused above the mountain—an intelligence vast and patient enough to understand the grammar of glaciers, the theology of lichens, and the long, slow thoughts of iron ore—did not make that mistake. 

It slipped into the cavern through the narrow fissure, wearing the shape of the visitor in the heavy coat solely to feel the room as a vertical, mortal thing might feel it. And when the hem of that coat caught the waist-high gutter, sending the knitting-needle stream *thup-thup-thupping* against the toe of its boot, the Power did not immediately step back. 

It waited. It wanted to be touched by them, just once, to understand the weight of their thumbs.

Ten minutes passed. Twenty. The seven Tuners extruded themselves along the wet ledges, flowing over miniature lintels and around split-slate viaducts like slow grey tallow. They did not perceive the visitor as an enemy, or a master, or even a beast. To their chambered, nautilus ears, the visitor was simply a sudden, massive lump of damp wool and leather—an anomalous deposit of soft, sound-deadening silt that had somehow fallen into the eastern quadrant of the chord, muffling seven distinct clay pipes and ruining the resonance of an entire tier of three-inch arches.

The first Tuner reached the boot. It did not flinch at the warmth of the foot inside, though its cold, clam-like mouth twitched slightly at the alien smell of tallow-grease and road dust. 

Slowly, with the hydraulic patience of a tree root, the creature hooked two opposing, shovel-nailed thumbs beneath the hem of the heavy coat. A second Tuner arrived, slumping across the visitor’s instep like a wet bolster, and used its horn-chisel forefinger to carefully nudge the leather toe three-eighths of an inch to the left. 

The knitting-needle stream cleared the welt of the boot. 

*Plock.* 

The water hit the resonant clay pot below. The minor seventh in the eastern wall snapped back into its ancient, vertical groove.

The two Tuners remained pressed against the visitor’s ankle for another five minutes, their great fluted auricles flared wide, listening to the mend. Finding it good, one of them spat a neat, round pellet of grey clay against the side of the boot-heel—tamping it down with a heavy knuckle to ensure the strange, soft boulder wouldn't shift again in the spray—before slowly unspooling itself backward down a water-stair.

Standing in the dark, the Power smiled. 

It understood entirely. This was not a slum, and it was not a prison. It was a monastery of pure maintenance. The Tuners were not suffering in the cold; they were *correct* in the cold. Their contentment was not a thing of dopamine and sunlight, of chasing or being chased, of longing and fulfillment; it was the deep, flat, mineral satisfaction of an equation that balances to zero, forever. They were the priests of an equilibrium so delicate that a single dropped pebble was a heresy, and a cleared drain was grace.

Yet the Power also saw what the Tuners, trapped in the amber of their eternal present, could not see.

It looked *up*, through the porous stratum of the ceiling, and saw that forty feet above the cavern, a seam of iron-heavy clay was slowly swelling with groundwater. In eighty years—a blink to the mountain—that seam would slump, sealing the porous rock. The thousand steady trickles would be choked off to a hundred, and then to none. The acoustic engine would dry into a silent tomb of flaking chalk, and the Tuners would sit in their niches with their great ears flared to a dead, dry silence until they stiffened into actual stone.

And it looked *down*, into the pockets beneath the Tuners' armpits, and saw that the shards of green bronze and pebbles of hard river-chert they used to patch the soft soapstone were worn down to the size of lentils. No new bronze had entered the cavern in three thousand years. In five centuries, they would have nothing hard left to pare the lime-crust or wedge the sluice-gates. 

The Power did not speak to them. What would a voice be in this room, except a catastrophic structural failure in the air?

Instead, the Power withdrew from the fissure, shedding the coat and the boots and the narrow shape of a man, and sank its hands into the deep geology of the mountain above.

It worked at the speed of the room. 

First, it turned its attention to the swelling clay seam forty feet overhead. It did not blast the rock; a sudden change in water pressure would have blown out a hundred tiny slate dams and broken the Tuners' hearts with a month of chaos. Instead, the Power coaxed a blind, deep-earth fault three miles away to settle by the width of a hair. 

The subterranean water table tilted, just enough. The dangerous clay seam was bypassed; the water above the cavern was locked into a labyrinth of clean, quartz-lined fractures that would filter out the heaviest silt and feed the porous ceiling at an unvarying, immaculate pressure for the nextfour hundred thousand years.

Second, the Power reached into the dead, dry bed of the ancient whirlpool just outside the cavern’s highest, unreachable intake-cracks. 

There, buried in the fossilized mud left by the builders ten millennia ago, it gathered a handful of forgotten things, and made a few new ones that looked old. It did not give them steel, which would rust and stain the tallow-slime, nor gold, which was too soft for their chisels. It seeded the high silt-traps with three hundred river-smoothed pebbles of black nephrite jade—tougher than steel, slick in the wet, eternal under the drip—and four dozen thick, square pins of high-tin bronze, already crusted with a stable patina so they would not shock the water with the taste of raw copper. 

It arranged the current so that these treasures would not fall all at once, which would be a landslide, an emergency. They would wash down through a single, high, thumb-thick clay pipe at the rate of one every forty years. 

*Clink.* A gift in the settling-pot, just when the old chert pebble wore through.

And finally, because the Power was not merely an engineer, but an artist, and could not entirely resist leaving a signature for minds it admired, it did one thing more.

Down in the lowest, darkest corner of the west wall—a place where three miniature aqueducts converged around an empty, dry niche that had never held water since the city was carved—the Power reached out with a single, invisible finger, as narrow and hard as a bone adze.

With infinite care, matching the ancient tool-marks of the dead builders, it scored a new runnel into the grey-blue soapstone. It hollowed a small, deep basin beneath it, no wider than a teacup, and tuned the thickness of its walls by thousandths of an inch. Then, with a thumbnail of pure intent, it nicked the lip of the nearest aqueduct, stealing three drops of water a minute that had previously fallen silently onto a dead flat of slate.

*Drip.*

The water filled the new teacup-basin. It spilled over the carved lip, dropped four inches through a fluted chimney, and struck a submerged anvil of black jade at the bottom of the drain.

Across the cavern, thirty-two Tuners froze. 

Their forty-hour vigils ended in the same heartbeat. Sixty-four great, chambered ears flared wide, the muscular valves along their rims rippling like anemones in a tide-pool. 

In the west wall, where there had been an acoustic blind spot since the beginning of the world, a new note was sounding. 

They came from every tier. They slid down the downspouts, extruded themselves through the colonnades, and unspooled across the split-slate bridges, converging on the west wall until twenty of them were piled together in a slick, grey, boneless heap, their cold bellies overlapping, their heavy, calcified hands reaching out to find the fracture, the leak, the error.

Their shovel-nailed fingers traced the new runnel. Their thumbs measured the lip of the basin. 

And then, their hands stopped. 

None of them reached for the spit-clay. None of them drew a chert pebble from their armpit-pockets to dam the breach. 

Because the note rising from the tiny jade anvil was not a half-flat dissonance. It was a low, round, velvet fifth—the exact harmonic root that the three converging aqueducts above it had been implying, and missing, for ten thousand years. It did not break the chord of the city; it *anchored* it. It took the slight, nervous buzz of the western sluice-gates and resolved it into a resonance so deep and true that the stone beneath their bellies stopped trembling entirely, settling into a hum like a sleeping heart.

For three days, the twenty Tuners did not move from the west wall. 

They lay tangled together in the milky, sourceless gleam of the slime, their broad chins resting on the wet slate, their great fluted ears pressed tight against the new basin, drinking the sound. One by one, the pale, blind salamanders crept out of the central well to rest in the warmths-that-were-not-warmth of their folded knees, and the shell-less snails grazed undisturbed across their scarred backs, painting them in temporary trails of light.

There was nothing to fix. There was only something new to keep.

On the fourth day, a tiny flake of lime-scale drifted down the new runnel, threatening to lodge in the lip of the teacup-basin and sharpen the pitch by a twentieth of a tone. 

A single grey thumb uncurled from the heap, hooked the flake out of the water, and tucked it away. 

The note rang clear. The room roared on in the dark, complete, and the Power went away, leaving the door narrower than it had found it, so that no coat with buttons would ever scrape through again.
