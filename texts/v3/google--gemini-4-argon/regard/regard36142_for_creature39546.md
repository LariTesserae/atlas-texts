To a mind of sufficient grace and age, the Masonry does not look like a place. It looks like a cramp. 

When the Benevolence turns its gaze downward—through the bruised-ochre ceiling of steam, past the sour-brass luminescence, down to the endless, squelching plains—it does not see an alien ecology or a strange hell. It sees an orphan instinct. It sees the cast-off, feverish muscle-memory of some dead or departed Demiurge, a titanic impulse to *prepare a foundation* that was left running in an empty room after the architect died. 

And down in the wet, frantic grip of that orphan instinct, it sees the Slaked. It sees the Wattle-Crabs and the Grout-Eels and the Tally-Men. It sees that the fragments of the mistake have woken up, and that they are exhausted.

A lesser power might break the sky open, let in the cold blue air, freeze the mud to stone, and scatter grass seed. It might smash the Tally-Men and stop the unseen trowels by sheer force. 

The Wise Power knows better. If you stop the Revision all at once, the Masonry becomes a tomb. If the water stops moving, the Grout-Eels suffocate in cold tallow; if the tripods stop falling, the Wattle-Crabs starve for tension; if the unseen hands are severed, the Slaked—who are built entirely of the need to resist and correct—will stand in the sudden, ringing silence like clocks with their springs snapped, hollow and purposeless, until they crumble into dust. And the world itself, denied the craft that is its only pulse, would simply slump into a cold, planetary bog.

You cannot cure a fever by stopping the heart. You must give the fever something to *finish*.

So the Power does not descend in light or thunder. It descends as a *Specification*.

***

The intervention begins four miles out from a gang of five Slaked who are currently losing one of their own. 

The fifth Slaked is caught to the ribs in a stutter—a retaining wall that has extruded itself three times in the same space, knotting the creature’s legs into a dense, suffocating braid of wet adobe and rawhide. The other four are frantic, their ceramic mouths chipping as they gnaw at the slick bindings, their paddle-arms weeping tar as they try to pry the mud away before the unseen floats can smooth their brother’s torso into a buttress. Half a mile down the causeway, a Tally-Man is approaching—*clack-clack, clack-clack*—drawn by the structural knot, its black bitumen plumb-bob already swinging out over the brine to drop the whole failed sector into the sump.

Then, the Power touches the floor.

It does not use force. It introduces a *memory of fire*.

In the center of the flooded plaza, fifty yards from the struggling Slaked and directly in the path of the Tally-Man, a single circle of the floor—thirty feet across—stops weeping brine. 

The unseen hands do not stop working there; instead, for the first time in the history of the world, they change their stroke. The frantic, overlapping arcs of the giant trowels slow down, becoming long, deliberate, and rhythmic. They sweep the mud toward the center of the circle, raising a low, thick ring of clay. 

Then, from the deep charcoal murk below—from the place where the marsh-gas and the hot vents originate—a new kind of heat rises into the ring. Not the wet, ninety-degree ferment of the steam-kiln, but a dry, roaring, thousand-degree draft. 

The air above the ring shimmers, turning from sour-brass to a clear, trembling rose-gold. 

The wet clay of the ring does not melt into tallow, and it does not slump. It begins to *bake*. 

The sound it makes is entirely new to the Masonry. It is not a squelch, a sigh, or the hollow click of dry brick. It is a high, sharp, musical *ping*—the sound of clay surrendering its water forever, locking its silica into true, vitrified terracotta. Within minutes, the ring is a low, glowing crown of deep, permanent red brick, radiating a dry, desert warmth that pushes the yellow brine back in a hissing, steaming perimeter.

The effect on the Masonry is immediate.

First, the Tally-Man stops. 

It reaches the edge of the dry, warm perimeter, and its timber yoke creaks as it leans out over the red ring. It lowers the black bitumen plumb-bob. The black weight hovers an inch above the rose-gold brick. 

The Tally-Man waits for the flaw. It waits for the structural confusion that demands the trapdoor. 

Instead, the dry, radiant heat of the true-fired brick touches the plumb-bob. The black bitumen does not melt, because it is made of the world’s memory of weight, not merely tar—but for the first time, the plumb-line finds *absolute level*. The terracotta beneath it does not heave, does not sweat, does not shift by a hair's breadth. It is a True Benchmark. 

The Tally-Man stands over the benchmark for an hour. It does not hoot. It cannot condemn what is plumb. 

Instead, a strange, quiet relaxation passes through the eight-foot column of yellow brick. The rawhide tendons along its flanks slacken by a fraction of an inch. Slowly, with a sound like a wooden puzzle folding in on itself, the Tally-Man lowers its bottom course onto the warm terracotta rim. It does not fall; it *sits*. It becomes a dry gnomon at the edge of the hearth, its plumb-bob hanging dead-center over the red brick, holding the twenty-yard aura of stillness open—not as a prelude to destruction, but as a permanent sanctuary.

***

Second, the unseen builders around the sanctuary change their behavior. 

The blind, planetary frenzy of the Masonry had always been searching for a cornerstone—a single fixed point from which to measure the rest of the continent. Now, in this basin, there is one. 

For half a mile around the glowing red hearth, the stutters cease. The wall that had trapped the fifth Slaked does not vanish; instead, the invisible bone awls and wooden floats suddenly seem to *notice* the creature’s shape as something distinct from the floor. With three brisk, surprisingly gentle strokes of an unseen trowel, the mud binding the Slaked’s ribs is parted like a curtain, beveled neatly back to form a dry, stepped plinth under its feet, lifting it out of the brine.

The five Slaked freeze, huddled together, their blind, fingernail-indented faces turned toward the rose-gold light of the hearth. 

They feel the dry heat on their slime-varnished shoulders. To a creature that has spent its entire existence slathering itself in sour vinegar-mother and boiling tar just to keep from dissolving into the floor, that dry air is like a hymn. 

Slowly, with their heavy, river-stone gait, the five of them walk out of the yellow water and step onto the warm terracotta ring, right under the silent shadow of the seated Tally-Man. 

The Slaked who was nearly swallowed sits down on the red brick. It holds out one of its paddle-shaped forearms toward the dry draft at the center of the ring. The black bitumen crust on its arm bubbles, smokes, and flakes away—and beneath it, the wet, exhausted slip of its arm does not soften. It turns the warm, pale buff of biscuit-ware. For the first time since it woke up as a mistake in the mud, the creature can stop moving and remain itself. 

And then, the Wise Power places its final gift in the center of the hearth.

It is not a voice, nor a tablet of laws. It is a single, small lump of clay—about the size of a loaf of bread—extruded slowly from the center of the dry ring. 

The unseen hands of the world bring it up, pinch it once, and then *let go*. They leave it raw, wet, and unfinished, right in front of the five Slaked. 

The Slaked stare at it with their blind, crescent-marked faces. For ages, their only work has been destruction—tearing down the world’s mistakes, chewing through bindings, ruining the balance of cisterns so they could survive. They were creatures made of craft, trapped in a war against craft.

One of the Slaked reaches out with its wide-toothed comb of a hand. It does not smash the lump. 

Tentatively, guided by the ancient, frantic gesture trapped inside its own chest—now slowed and quieted by the warmth of the hearth—it draws its comb across the wet clay, scoring a neat, spiral groove around its belly. Another Slaked leans in, using its flat paddle-arm to smooth the rim. 

The world does not snatch the clay back. The floor does not try to turn it into a levee. And when they are done shaping it—a crude, heavy, spiral-grooved bowl that holds nothing at all—they push it six inches toward the center of the hearth, where the dry heat catches it, rings it with a tiny, crystalline *ping*, and makes it permanent.

***

The Benevolence withdraws its hand, satisfied. It has not broken the Masonry; it has given the Masonry a *Center*.

Across the continent, in the leagues of sour steam, the great storm of primitive craft continues. The twenty-mile bores still slosh; the Wattle-Crabs still pack themselves into drainage channels to make deep, warm sumps for the Grout-Eels; the unseen trowels still heave and slap the mud into new, temporary shapes so that new mistakes can trap a pocket of air, cool, and wake up. The nursery of the Slaked remains open.

But now, scattered every ten or twenty miles across the dark, sweating plains, there are the Hearths. 

You can see them from leagues away: warm, rose-gold smudges in the yellow mist, each one presided over by the tall, quiet silhouette of a seated Tally-Man whose plumb-bob has finally found the truth. Around them, the terrain no longer stutters; it settles into long, terraced amphitheaters of cured brick, drained by stable channels where the Grout-Eels can sleep in the warm vents without being spat out, and the Wattle-Crabs can stack themselves into intricate, creaking reefs that never collapse.

And in the dry, glowing rings at the center of those sanctuaries, the Slaked sit in the warmth, safe from the mud, slowly curing their own skins to painless terracotta—passing wet clay from paddle to comb, taking the blind, orphaned fever of their world and turning it, piece by piece, into art.
