To look upon the sinkhole with wisdom is to recognize a masterpiece that requires no signature, a clockwork of bone, water, and patience that has already wound itself to perfection. 

A foolish power might be tempted to bless this place with abundance: to pour a silver stream over the lip, to summon songbirds to the emerald fronds, or to soften the steep limestone walls into a gentle stair for the harvester. A proud power might leave a monument, a glowing crystal in the moss, or awaken the Spindle-Mother’s mind to speech so she and the human might converse in words rather than in crushed marrow-bone and river cobbles.

The benevolent power does none of these things. It sees that the magic of the basin lies in its limits. It understands the sacredness of the silent treaty between the two-legged climber and the fourteen-legged gardener, a treaty written in boundary stones and calcium. To make their lives effortless would be to make their cooperation unnecessary; it would unstring the delicate instrument they play together.

Yet, when the power looks fifty years, a hundred years into the future—reading the slow, inevitable grammar of stone and flesh—it perceives three quiet tragedies waiting in the wings of time. 

First, the karst pipes below are slowly choking. The very minerals that feed the bladders and coat the rims in pink salt are, drop by drop, calcifying the subterranean basins. In four generations, the throats of the organ-pipes will narrow and close. The *clop, tung, plock, boom* will go mute; the listeners will go deaf to the world and starve in the silent dark; the sinkhole will flood with stagnant, mineral-thick water, drowning the bronze moss and rotting the great pillar-ferns.

Second, the Spindle-Mother is the last of her kind in this high place, and her next molt—two winters hence—will kill her. She has grown too vast, and her joints are stiffening. Even with the harvester’s gifts of marrow-bone, her tired gill-fringes cannot draw enough vigor from the warm water to crack the massive, moss-heavy armor of her seventh decade. When she dies inside the central fern, the shoots will run riot, the bladders will choke the vents, and the garden of giants will strangle itself into a common thicket.

Third, the harvester is aging. She is a solitary woman of the plateau tribes, an outcast by choice who walks with a slight hitch in her left hip. In the coming autumn, the braided grass rope she leaves tied to the rim-boulder will be frayed by the dry plateau winds and the teeth of a passing marmot. The next time she leans her weight over the twenty-foot drop, the fibers will part. She will fall upon her own stone maul, breaking her spine on the bronze moss, leaving no one to bring the river cobbles, no one to smash the elk bones, and no one to remember the way down.

The wise power does not announce itself. It does not part the blank blue sky. It works entirely through the laws that already govern the bowl, moving as slowly as the glass-snails.

Deep in the dark, fifty feet below the moss where the water falls into the hidden basins, the power nudges a single, dormant spore of a blind, stone-eating lichen—a species from the deep earth that had gone extinct in this mountain range a millennium ago—and wakes it. The lichen begins to grow exclusively at the water-line of the subterranean catch-basins. It feeds on the precipitated chalk, dissolving the calcified crusts just as fast as the seep-water deposits them, exhaling a microscopic fizz of carbon that keeps the stone flutes scoured clean and resonant. The music of the pipes—the *tung* and the *boom*—will now play for another ten thousand years, its pitch unchanging.

Next, the power turns its attention to the weeping wall, three inches above the ledge where the green-stained antler picks rest. Here, where a hairline fissure sweats water onto the bone-meal, the power alters the trace chemistry of the rock for the space of a heartbeat. The water emerging from this single crack becomes faintly, imperceptibly rich in dissolved magnesium and a rare, natural analgesic washed down from a deep vein of serpentine rock miles away. 

That night, when the freezing fog fills the bowl and the Spindle-Mother climbs the pick-scars to scrape up her meal of crushed elk bone, she drinks the water from that fissure. The ache in her ancient, calcified joints eases. Beneath her rust-red plates, the new, soft skin preparing for her next molt absorbs the trace minerals, becoming supple, elastic, and strong. And deep within her, in a brood-pouch that has been barren for forty years, three unfertilized eggs—capable of parthenogenesis, a trait her species only triggers in moments of supreme health—stir to life. She will survive her molting. And when she finally passes, decades from now, three small, rust-red daughters will already be living in the neighboring pillar-ferns, ready to take up the pruning of the garden.

Finally, on a bright afternoon when the bowl is empty of human presence, the power rides a thermal of warm air up out of the sinkhole onto the dry plateau. 

A hundred yards from the rim, a young, sharp-eyed coyote is trotting through the scrub, hunting the very marmot that has been chewing the harvester’s rope. The power does nothing more than shift the wind by three degrees, carrying the fat rodent's scent directly into the coyote’s nostrils. The marmot is caught before it can sever the final load-bearing strand of the braided grass. 

Even so, the rope is still dangerously frayed where it loops over the sharp edge of the limestone rim. 

Two days later, the harvester returns, a heavy bundle of elk shins slung over her shoulder, her bad hip aching from the climb. Beside her walks a child—her sister’s boy, a quiet, observant nine-year-old who does not fit in with the loud hunters of the camp, brought along for the first time to learn the secret of the water-skins. 

As the woman reaches for the rope to test her weight against it, a fat, metallic-green beetle—drawn by the thermal beacon of the sinkhole—drops out of the pale blue sky. Guided by a microscopic eddy of air, it lands not in the moss below, but squarely on the frayed, hidden underside of the rope, right where it rubs against the boulder. 

The boy steps forward to look at the shining insect. "Aunt," he says, his voice small in the vast plateau wind. "The cord is bitten through."

The woman stops. She looks at the frayed braid, then down into the twenty-foot drop, and her hand goes to her hip. She looks at the boy, whose eyes are wide as he peers over the lip into the warm, echoing green bowl below, listening to the *clop, tung, boom* rising from the earth. Just then, the sun hits its zenith. Down in the basin, a giant yellow bladder touches the suspended stone maul and splits with a sound like a musket shot, followed by that long, brassy, resonant sigh. 

The boy does not flinch at the gunshot crack; instead, he smiles, enchanted by the strange music.

Together, the woman and the boy sit in the dry grass above the rim and spend the afternoon braiding a new, thicker rope, their fingers moving in unison. 

The benevolent power watches from the blank, bright sky until the new rope is secured and the two of them descend into the warm dampness—the old harvester and the new. Then, satisfied that the negotiation will continue, the power withdraws its hand, leaving the sinkhole to its slow shadows, its glass hearts, and its singing stones.
