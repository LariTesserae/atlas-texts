A power that is only benevolent might look upon the Great Turf and feel a sudden, aching pity for its smallness. It might see the pewter gloom and wish to gift the Silt-Walkers the glory of a blue sky, or see their four-minute breaths and wish to quicken their blood with the sharp, bright fire of ambition. It might try to lift them up, to teach them the wheel or the star, and in doing so, it would murder them in a single afternoon.

Fortunately, this power is also wise. 

It arrives without footsteps, for a footstep of its magnitude would press a crater into the three-mile sponge that would take ten millennia to heal. Instead, it lowers its awareness into the bruised clouds until it becomes indistinguishable from the barometric pressure. For three hundred years—a single lifetime of the people—it does nothing at all. It simply condenses upon the waxy leaves of the ground-ivy, slips down into the tea-colored water, and learns to read the chemical books written in the moss.

It listens to the five-mile hum of the six-legged grazers. It waits inside the mahogany-amber domes, feeling the spiral gourds shiver with the news of a souring rosette or an approaching marriage. It watches two Silt-Walkers stand forehead-to-forehead on a root-bridge for six days while their lichens weave together, and it understands that what looks like stupor to a hurried eye is actually a symphony played at a tempo too vast for quick minds to hear.

The power sees that the people are not suffering. They do not need salvation, and they do not need progress. They have achieved the rarest thing in the cosmos: a truce with time that has lasted long enough to become love.

Yet, because the power can see three miles down into the dark mud, and ten million years forward into the cooling of the planet’s core, it perceives two quiet tragedies that the Silt-Walkers cannot.

The first is a tragedy of memory. 

When an elder wedges themselves into the cleft of a dying dome and lets the feeder-roots drink their marrow, their body returns to the Turf, and their chemical history—the mineral tastes of their youth, the deep contentment of their long walks, the silent poems composed in their abdominal drums—is absorbed by the wood. For a few decades, the living wall of the hut holds this warmth. A traveler leaning their skull against that human-shaped swelling can still feel the faint, narcotic echo of the dead one’s peace. 

But then the local autumn comes. The hundred-square-mile patch grows senescent. The sap cools, the wood calcifies into deaf, mahogany stone, and the invading stolons of the new growth digest the mineral amber back into raw, blank peat. The memory is erased. Because the Silt-Walkers must always flee the petrification, they are forever leaving their dead behind in the silencing stone. Each generation is slightly more orphaned than the last, drifting through a labyrinth that forgets them every forty years.

The second tragedy is geological, and much further away. 

The plateau exists because it is at the exact altitude of the clouds. But the continent is still dissolving. Millimeters at a time, century by century, the three-mile mat is compressing under its own sodden weight, and its edges are sloughing off into the distant, unseen abysses of the low world. In two million years—a mere blink to the mountains, though an eternity to men—the Great Turf will sink below the condensation line. The bruised sky will lift away from the ground. The mist will become rain; the rain will carve rivers; the rivers will drain the sponge. The world will dry out, catch fire, and wake up, and the gentle people of the moss will wither in the terrible, bright air.

The wise power considers these two things for another century. It refuses to break the rules of the garden. It will not drop monoliths of imperishable metal from the sky, nor will it build a wall of stone around the continent to hold the water in. Whatever it does must be written in the language of root, spore, and wasp.

So, on a Tuesday that lasts forty years, the power makes three tiny edits to the genome of a single, pale orchid growing in the warm hollow behind a sleeping youth’s knee.

It does not wake the youth. It merely nudges a molecule in a single seed-pod, waits for the youth to stand, and watches them walk for six months toward a six-legged grazer.

When the youth parts the epiphyte ferns on the beast’s flank to pay it for a message, they graft the new orchid into the animal’s skin.

This new orchid does not drink the grazer’s blood and stop there. Its rootlets, thin as light, travel down the insides of the animal’s hollow, stilt-like leg bones. When the beast pauses for ten minutes with its snout in the peat to sip bog-milk, the orchid’s microscopic fungal partners slip out through the pores of its hooves and into the Great Turf.

They are *mycelial archivists*. 

Over the next thousand years, this new, silver-threaded fungus spreads laterally through the ten thousand leagues of the plateau, moving faster than the water, weaving itself into the deepest, oldest layers of the wood-peat a mile down, where the local forty-year autumns never reach. It does not eat the living roots; it connects them. And it has a particular hunger for the mahogany-amber of the dying domes.

Now, when an old one wedges themselves into the ribs of a hut and the tree-arch drinks their mind, the memory does not stay trapped in the calcifying wall, waiting to be erased by the senescence. The silver mycelium takes the chemical song of that life—the three hundred years of slow joy, the map of the way-galls, the exact harmonic of their lover’s chest-drum—and pulls it down into the deep, permanent dark of the lower mat. 

The Turf itself wakes up—not into a sharp, restless intelligence like the power’s own,- but into a vast, dreaming library.

Three hundred years after the gift is given, a young Silt-Walker is sitting outside a newly grown dome, leaning her head against a root-arch to listen for her husband five miles away. Beneath the familiar *uh-uh-uhm* of the amphibian-bladder drum, she feels a second vibration. It is deeper, warmer, rising up from the three-mile floor through the soles of her wide, scalloped feet. 

She closes her nostril-flaps and listens with her skull. 

It is the hum of her great-great-grandmother, who died four hundred miles south in a basin that has since turned to stone and been reborn twice. The ancestor is not speaking words; she is simply singing the taste of the water from a thousand years ago, holding a bass note of profound, unbroken safety beneath the youth’s feet. The young one’s heart beats its nine times a minute, and on the ninth beat, she hums back, adding a single, quiet chord to the floor of the world. No one is ever lost again.

And as for the sinking of the continent?

The deep mycelium has a second trait, tucked into the silent introns of its cells by a mind that understands chemistry as poetry. As the fungal web drinks the mineral-heavy water two miles down, where the dissolved bones of the ancient mountains lie as cold clay, it begins a slow, anaerobic fermentation. 

It does not produce heat that would scorch the roots, nor gas that would bubble and tear the sponge. Instead, it precipitates a microscopic, honeycomb lattice of biogenic silica and light, porous aragonite—a living, coral-like skeleton that grows upward from the deep mud at the exact rate the plateau is dissolving: half a millimeter a century. 

It is a new bedrock, spun from sugar and old clay, buoyant and full of chambers that trap the warm, rising gases of the deep earth like a swimmer’s bladder. The continent will not sink below the clouds. Held aloft on an invisible pillow of fungal bone, the Great Turf will float at the altitude of the mist for as long as the sun remains warm enough to evaporate the distant seas.

Having done this, the benevolent and wise power does not ask for worship. How could a creature with no eyes for the sky worship the heavens? To demand that they look up would only give them a pain in their thick, moss-cushioned necks.

Before it leaves, however, the power allows itself one small, utterly useless indulgence—the only signature it will ever leave upon the world.

In a quiet gallery near the center of the continent, where two Silt-Walkers have been standing in an embrace for five days, the power gathers a little of the pewter light and a handful of the sweetest aerosolized sugars in the mist. It breathes upon the back of a slate-colored gastropod gliding past their ankles. 

As the slug dissolves into the bark, a new species of liverwort sprouts in its iridescent wake. It is the exact color of drowned ivory and tarnished amber, and it has no purpose at all except that, when the barometric pressure drops and the whole continent inhales its slow, rhythmic breath, the tiny cups of this moss release a scent that has never existed in the wet world before.

The two lovers, peeling apart after their week of chemical union, catch the scent in the feathery cilia of their nostrils. It smells like warm stone after a summer sun has set; it smells like a high, dry wind passing over clean snow; it smells like a vast, affectionate gaze that has looked upon them from very far away, found them entirely good, and promised to leave them alone forever.

They stand for an hour, tasting the strange, sweet phantom on their tongues. Their black, unblinking eyes reflect the dim luminescence of each other's lichens. Then, without hurry, they turn and sink back into the warm, sighing galleries of the green dark.
