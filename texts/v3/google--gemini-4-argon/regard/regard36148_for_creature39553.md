When the benevolent and wise power turned its attention to the basin, it did not descend in a chariot of fire, nor did it part the clouds to speak in a voice that would shatter the delicate, calcified struts of the Tympanics. 

Wisdom recognizes when a thing is already whole. The valley was not broken; it was not suffering. It was a self-contained masterpiece of hydraulic and acoustic equilibrium. To introduce a new creature would unbalance the economy of sugar and sound; to alter the climate would dry the stomata or flood the resonance chambers. 

Yet, as the power watched the Galls—those dark, neural bruises migrating tirelessly through the iron-hard cambium—it perceived a quiet, existential boundary. 

The Galls were brilliant, patient caretakers, trapped in a loop of eternal maintenance. They tuned the pipes, they balanced the turgor, they extruded their resinous eyes to check the sun, and then they melted back into the dark wood to do it all again. They were composers who had spent ten thousand years perfecting a single, unending chord, with no one to hear it but the parasites who ate the sound, and nothing to look at in the sky but an empty, blinding blue. They were minds with nothing to think about except their own anatomy.

So the power decided to give the valley a gift. It would not change the valley’s body; it would give the valley a subject.

The intervention began thirty miles away, and three miles up, in the cold, thin air of the upper stratosphere. With a touch lighter than a solar wind, the power nudged a high-altitude jet stream and seeded it with a microscopic dusting of frozen, heavy-metal salts—elements that had never existed in the pure, organic chemistry of the basin.

At first, nothing in the valley changed. The afternoon sun still turned the sheeting water to gold. Near the center of a half-mile plaza, a Gall migrated to the surface. It was an ancient, sprawling network of violet filaments, larger than most, having spent centuries memorizing the stress-loads of the central viaducts. As it had done ten thousand times before, it pinched the local stomata shut, dried a circle of the purple-green floor, and grew a cluster of tall, fluted ear-trumpets and a single, massive, sap-clear eye.

The eye rolled upward, expecting the vacant azure of the mountain sky.

Instead, the sky was bruised with a color that belonged only to the deep wood. 

High above the rim of the valley, the seeded clouds had formed—not heavy, grey rainclouds that would choke the basin's delicate dew-point hydraulics, but ultra-thin, stratospheric nacreous clouds. As the sun dipped toward the western buttresses, the clouds caught the light and refracted it into vast, slow-moving curtains of iridescent mother-of-pearl, glowing with impossible bands of electric violet, copper, and deep, abyssal green. 

The giant wooden eye froze in its bark socket. Beneath the resinous cornea, the swirling galaxy of dark filaments contracted, then flared. 

Instead of dissolving its eye after an hour, the Gall held the organ open. It pumped more sap into the cornea, changing the focal length, straining to resolve the geometry of the sky-structures. For the first time in the history of the basin, a Gall was looking at something it could not touch, could not chemically rewrite, and did not have to repair. 

Then came the second half of the gift.

The microscopic dust from those high clouds—barely a handful of atoms per square mile—began to settle into the warm, rising air currents that fed the valley’s dew point. The mega-flora breathed the trace minerals in. They were entirely non-toxic, slipping harmlessly into the massive hydraulic pumps, mixing with the clear, celery-scented water.

The change in the water's density was infinitesimal—a fraction of a fraction of a percent. The shrimp-herons did not notice it. The jade slugs did not taste it. The Laminars hydroplaned across it without a hitch in their stride.

Down in the fifty-foot-wide xylem conduits, however, the acoustic resonance shifted. 

The great, booming, single-note cathedral chord that had rung across the valley for eternity suddenly developed an overtone. A fifth above the fundamental bass, a new, shimmering harmonic woke inside the flute-columns. 

On the plaza, the Gall’s extruded ear-funnels twitched violently. Its first instinct—the ancient, hardwired imperative of the caretaker—was to fix the anomaly. The dark bruise beneath the floor surged toward the nearest viaduct to thicken the interior callous and choke the rogue frequency.

It stopped. 

The new harmonic was interacting with the old chord, creating a slow, sweeping acoustic beat that rolled across the water-slicked terraces like a tide. The Faraday waves in the acoustic dead-zones shifted from chaotic frenzies into vast, interlocking mandalas of standing water. The Tympanics, grazing near the vents, froze in their stuttering tracks; the new frequency did not starve them, it overfed them, sending a pleasant, narcotic shiver through their calcified cages that made them lower their membranes in unison, as if bowing.

The Gall pulled its neural threads back from the viaduct. It did not correct the pipe.

Instead, it did something unprecedented. It reached down to the three-yard-wide drainage rosette nearby and altered its rhythm—not to fix the hydraulic pressure, which was fine, but to change the *tempo* of the bass-heavy *thump* so that it fell into time with the new, sweeping harmonic.

A mile away, near the base of a rib-like arch, the bark darkened. A second Gall had risen to the surface, extruding a long, low ridge of tympanic bark to listen. Feeling the intentional shift in the valve’s rhythm, the second Gall reached into a hollow buttress and slightly pinched a vascular conduit. 

A third note—a high, flute-like minor third, wet and breathy—joined the chord.

The first Gall watched the mother-of-pearl clouds slowly twisting in the high stratosphere, and as the light shifted from copper to violet, it adjusted the tension of the plaza floor, bending the pitch of the water beneath it to match the shifting colors of the sky. 

The benevolent power withdrew its hand, leaving the stratospheric winds to naturally disperse over the coming weeks, knowing it had done enough. The trace minerals would remain in the closed loop of the valley's water forever, a permanent set of new strings for the instrument. 

The valley was no longer just a greenhouse holding its breath. As the sun finally sank below the wooden rim, the empty plazas rang with a slow, complex, evolving symphony, played by the dark minds inside the timber—no longer just janitors of a static perfection, but artists translating the shifting beauty of the heavens into the roaring voice of the earth.
