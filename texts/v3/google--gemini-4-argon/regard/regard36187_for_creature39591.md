A lesser power, looking down upon this screaming, freezing, frantic knot of stone and flesh, would feel pity. 

It would see the Tuners with their thickened blood and scarred lungs, hauling fifty pounds of dead river-rock up a vertical chimney in the dark. It would see the swifts dashed against the basalt when the gale shifts, the salamanders boiling in the valley pots, the blind, ancient Anchor flinching as copper spikes are driven through its translucent veins. A lesser power would calm the wind. It would part the three-thousand-foot cloud deck to give the valley people the sun, or it would thicken the basalt ribs so they never cracked and never needed tuning.

And in doing so, it would kill them all.

To still the wind is to stop the updraft; to stop the updraft is to freeze the chimney. Without the warm vapor rising from the deep earth, the weeping rock dries, the moss turns to black powder, the Anchor starves into brittle parchment, and the swifts abandon the sky. To part the clouds is to break the thermal inversion that drives the air upward in the first place. And to silence the stone is to deafen the people below, leaving them blind to the mountain’s moods until a silent, unannounced winter buries their shelves in ice.

The benevolent power knows this. It understands that this place is not a tragedy; it is an instrument under immense tension. The beauty of a string is that it is stretched almost to the point of snapping; if you slacken it out of mercy, the music dies.

So the power does not arrive as a miracle. It arrives as a minute, structural grace.

First, it looks to the stone itself. 

Over the centuries, the tally marks carved by the Tuners have drifted relentlessly downward on the rock face, recording a slow, terrifying drop in pitch. The western rib of the monolith, the thinnest of the four, has a hairline fault running through its belly—deep inside the black basalt where the Tuners’ thumb-rings cannot read it properly and the Anchor’s polyps cannot reach. Frost-wedging is slowly prying it apart. In twelve winters, during an equinox gale, the western rib is destined to shear off entirely. When it falls, the chime will lose its chord, the aerodynamic cage will collapse, the wind will tear the vertical bog from the remaining walls in an hour, and the villages below will hear a single, deafening snap followed by a silence that will starve them within a generation.

The power reaches into the dark, wet heart of the western rib. It does not weld the stone shut—the rock must flex to sing. Instead, it coaxes the water seeping through that hidden fault to carry a trace more dissolved iron and silica from the upper crust, and it nudges a dormant strain of lithotrophic bacteria—already native to the basalt—into the crack. 

Over the course of a single season, the bacteria spin a microscopic, metallic mineral matrix across the fault line. It is not brittle like stone, nor soft like moss. It has the tensile give of whalebone. The fault stabilizes. The western rib stops dying. It holds its note.

Next, the power turns its attention to the Anchor.

The great, blind lattice is old, and it is tired of the copper. Every piton driven by the Tuners leaches a slow, green poison into the immediate peat, creating dead zones that the Anchor must constantly work around, expending precious energy to reroute its rubbery tubes. 

The power does not remove the pitons. Instead, it touches the chemistry of the Anchor’s digestive slime. The next time the violet polyps rise to lick the rancid yak butter and beetle-paste from a newly placed ballast stone, they do not merely tolerate the copper spike beside it; they *use* it. The power grants the Anchor the metabolic key to chelate the metal. 

Slowly, over the months that follow, the Anchor draws the dissolved copper out of the poisoned moss and weaves it directly into the walls of its own translucent tubing, and into the holdfasts of the ferns. The lattice takes on a faint, iridescent verdigris sheen. Its tensile strength triples. It no longer rots around the Tuners' anchors; it grafts onto them, turning the human hardware into permanent, living bone. And better still: copper conducts. The Anchor’s vibrational sense—its only way of knowing the world—becomes ten times sharper. It can now feel the Tuners climbing three hundred feet down in the dark chimney before they ever breach the floor, and it begins to warm the moss at the threshold by pumping sun-heated water from the eastern rib down to meet their freezing hands.

Finally, the power considers the Tuners, and the people in the fog below.

They do not need an easier life; their pride and their survival are bound together in the terrifying mastery of the climb. If the climb required nothing of them, they would forget how to listen. 

But they are lonely in the dark, and the toll on their bodies is slightly too cruel.

The power touches the horizontal reeds—the ones that trap the mist in plum-sized bladders and burst with a *pop*. When the spores of the next generation of reeds germinate in the copper-rich, newly strengthened moss, their bladders grow slightly thicker skins. Before they burst, hanging out in the blinding, twenty-two-thousand-foot blue abyss, the fierce ultraviolet light of the upper atmosphere reacts with the oils in their skins, synthesizing a complex, heavy alkaloid that was never there before.

When the Tuners next climb into the howling cage, they immediately feel the difference, though they have no words for it. 

They press their teeth against the wet black basalt to tap out their arrival, and the stone feels *tight*—resonant, solid, healed of a sickness they had only begun to dread in their generational ledgers. As they sink their raptor-beak knee-straps into the moss, the garden does not flinch and drop hailstones of frantic salamanders upon their heads; the moss feels warm, gripping their knees back with a tough, metallic elasticity that steadies them against the sixty-mile-an-hour shear of the wind.

And when they return to the shelves four thousand feet below, and the village elders dry and burn the harvested reed-bladders in the perpetual twilight, the smoke does more than smell of ozone and lightning. 

As the people breathe it in, the new alkaloid passes through their thickened blood. It gently dilates the constricted vessels in the climbers' scarred lungs, easing the crushing pressure on their hearts, letting them sleep through the night without waking choking on their own fluid. 

And it does one thing more. Because the Anchor has woven the mountain's copper into the reeds, and the reeds have drunk the light of the upper sky, the smoke carries a trace of a new, mild dream-sight. When the people of the fog close their eyes around the ritual fire, behind their eyelids, for just a few minutes, they do not see the grey, dripping walls of their gorge. 

They see a vault of impossible, piercing, abyssal blue. They feel the terrifying, glorious punch of a sixty-mile-an-hour wind that cannot touch them, and they hear the five voices of the Throat—the stone, the water, the birds, the horn, and the silent, rhythmic pulse of the living net that holds it all together—singing in a chord that will not break for a thousand years. 

The power listens to the chord for a moment longer, feels the slight, true shift in the resonance, and leaves them to their work.
