To a mind vast enough to hold the tectonic grinding of the young mountains and the frantic, glowing neural-net of the city in the same thought, the little courtyard is a masterpiece of unintended harmony. 

The benevolent power does not look down with the urge to warm the air, or to plant tough alpine mosses in the crevices, or to awaken the spider into some frantic, unnatural sapience. Wise love knows that to soften a harsh world is to murder the miracles that have learned to dance with its edges. The hydrogel skaters, the surfing slime-mold, the patient harvestman in its felt pocket of dead wings and fiberglass—they have earned the dignity of their struggle. Their beauty *is* their precision.

And yet, the power sees the fragility of the pocket.

Deep within the city's municipal core, an automated diagnostic subroutine is slowly compiling six months of data from the thimble-drone. Within forty-eight hours, a minor statistical threshold will be crossed: the sulfur-eating biofilm is thirty-two microns thinner in its centers than the baseline algorithm predicts, and the dielectric resistance along the southern wall’s lower scales fluctuates by 0.004 percent between midnight and dawn. 

Left to its blind, sterile logic, the city will schedule a purge. In three days, the mist-cycle will be replaced for ten seconds by an aerosolized biocide and a high-amp electrostatic scrub. The courtyard will be returned to zero.

The benevolent power does not smash the city’s servers, nor does it break the thimble-drone. It acts with the same quiet economy as the spider.

First, it reaches into the cool, humming dark of the municipal architecture and touches a single, unremarkable line of code in the facility’s maintenance registry. It alters a decimal point, reclassifying the sulfur-crust and the stray capacitance not as *fouling*, but as *passive self-insulating patina—Experimental Sector 9*. The purge order dissolves before it is ever written. The thimble-drone will continue to make its rounds, flashing its violet light, forever satisfied that the mathematics of the courtyard are exactly as they should be.

Second, the power looks to the future of the harvestman. The creature in the frost-heave is old for its kind—nearly four winters—and wrapped deep inside the grey felt of its nest is an egg sac the size of a marble, insulated with its own shed down. When the spider dies in the coming autumn, the three hundred tiny, long-legged things that emerge will starve in this single, thirty-pace shaft of stone. 

So, at the far eastern corner of the courtyard, where the synthetic polymer is extruded directly into the raw granodiorite, the power makes a single, physical adjustment. It nudges the crystallographic tension of the rock just enough to let the next cycle of freezing and thawing do the rest.

*Tick.*

A hairline fracture, no wider than a pencil lead, splits through seven feet of solid mountain and city infrastructure. It breaches the blind wall of this thermal sink and opens into the next one over—and through that one, into a labyrinth of three hundred identical, sterile, mist-breathing shafts lined up along the mountain's spine, all of them currently empty of life, waiting to be colonized.

Finally, the power leaves a signature—a small grace note for the living things here now.

Deep in the foundation, where the capillary drains drink the syrupy runoff, it warms a single, fist-sized nodule of native quartz trapped in the polymer matrix by just three degrees. It is nothing the city's heat-sensors will ever notice. 

Down on the flags, the mist blooms. 

*Shhhk.* 

In the silence that follows, the harvestman pauses halfway up the seam, one whip-thin leg raised as the faint, new draft from the eastern crack brushes its sensory hairs—a whisper of vast, dark territory waiting in the rock. Below, the milky myxomycete slowly alters its course, drifting downward toward the tiny, unfailing ember of the warmed quartz, where the hydrogel skaters are already gathering in the dark to rest their pressurized bodies against a stone that will never quite freeze. 

The power withdraws, leaving the courtyard to its cold, bright rhythm, having given it the only two things a living world truly needs from a god: time, and a door.
