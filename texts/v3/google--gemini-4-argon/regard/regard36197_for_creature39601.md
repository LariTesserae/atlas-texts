A lesser kindness would have broken the Sump. 

A lesser power, looking down from the galvanized tin sky with a heart full of green fields and a hatred of cages, would have sent a root to crack the concrete retaining wall. It would have poured sunlight into the forty-eight-degree gloom, stilled the subterranean flywheels to give the earth "rest," and washed the oily flagstones with sweet rain. 

And in doing so, it would have committed a slaughter. Within a week, the bacterial grease would have starved; the brass-shelled moths would have dropped in brittle, silent heaps; the kettle-rooks would have died with their leather throats deflated; and the three Lagging-Men would have stood in the terrifying, chaotic silence, their copper-wire tympanums ringing with the agony of a snapped chord, slowly desiccating like beached kelp until their felt hides split in the alien warmth of the sun.

The power that looked down over the eighty-foot rim was older than green fields, or at least wise enough to know that *home* is not a color, and *peace* is not the absence of noise. It recognized the Sump of Six-Gears for what it was: a monastery of pure continuity. It looked at the three stooped figures standing in the wet alley behind the Seventh Tower, and it saw that they were not prisoners of the machine. They were its priests, and they were its luthiers.

Yet, a wise power does not look upon a closed loop without seeing the long, slow curve of its horizon. 

The power listened to the Sump—not just with ears, which heard only the deafening three-part chord of bass engine, midrange wing-shirr, and treble rook-hiss—but with the deep attention that hears the wear on the bearing. 

Deep in the under-plenum, a forty-ton flywheel on the third turbine shaft had been spinning for three hundred and twelve years. Its babbitt-metal bearing had worn down by three-thousandths of an inch. The governor held the speed true, so the rhythm had not changed, and the pitch had not dropped. Not yet. Give it another ninety years, and the shaft would develop a microscopic wobble. The wobble would birth a secondary harmonic—a dull, subterranean *thud-thrum* beneath the smooth bass. 

That harmonic would travel up the flagstones and into the tallow-pumps in the chests of the Lagging-Men. It would be an itch they could not scratch, a broken bone in the music of the world, fifty feet below the solid slate where their blunt horn thumbs could never reach to clear it. It would drive them mad with a slow, vegetative grief long before the turbine actually seized.

The benevolent power did not descend in a chariot of fire, nor did it speak a word into the Draftsman’s light. 

Instead, deep in the grease-packed darkness beneath the floor, where no even the porcelain scavengers went, the molecules of the worn babbitt-metal bearing simply... remembered their youth. A quiet, atomic migration occurred in the dark. The lead and tin and antimony flowed back against the friction of three centuries, filling the three-thousandths-of-an-inch gap with a sigh that didn't even disturb the oil film. The shaft centered itself. The future dissonance was erased from the score before it could ever be played.

That was the work of preservation. Next came the work of grace.

Up on the high perimeter wall, eighty feet above the basin, where weeds grew in the cracked concrete and the wind actually blew, something rolled to the edge. 

It had not been dropped by a surveyor. It had been grown, or forged, in the quiet fold between thought and matter where such powers keep their gifts. It teetered on the lip of the riveted iron plate, and then it fell.

It tumbled down through the flat white light, past the vertical plumes of warm steam, past the crane-jibs where the kettle-rooks sat like rows of black iron rivets. 

It struck the open flagstones twenty yards from the Third Tower with a sound that had never been heard in the Sump before. 

It did not *clank* like a dropped wrench, and it did not *chink* like an apothecary bottle. It went *tink-hmmmmmm*. 

The porcelain scavengers shot out from the drainage grates instantly, twenty pale, rattling things converging on the fallen object like drops of mercury rolling toward a magnet. Their mandibles clicked against it. They tried to find a seam, a tendon, a fracture line. Finding none, and finding no grease or marrow upon it, they skittered backward and vanished back into the grates within forty seconds. 

Out in the middle of the wet, rainbow-sheened plaza, the object sat alone.

It was a dodecahedron—twelve-sided, the size of a large clenched fist—cast from an alloy of bell-bronze and meteoric iron, but hollowed out internally into a labyrinth of spiraling, logarithmic chambers, like the shell of a nautilus cross-bred with an organ pipe. It was completely impervious to rust, and far too hard for any scavenger’s jaw. 

More importantly, it was interrupting the water. 

It had landed squarely in the shallow saucer of a worn flagstone, displacing a quarter-inch of condensate, and the tiny ripple of water running around its twelve bronze facets was singing the wrong note.

Four minutes passed. Then, from the shadow of the Second Tower, a shape detached itself. 

The Lagging-Man slid across the slick slate at the pace of an hour hand, his flat, felted feet wiping two clean ribbons in the oily film. His curved back was still dusted with a few dozen clinker-moths that hadn't bothered to fly back to the boiler-wall after his last feeding. Under the stiffened felt brim of his head, the recessed grate of bone and tarnished copper wire was tilted slightly toward the floor.

It took him twenty-two minutes to reach the object. 

When he arrived, he stood over it for a long time. Then, with the slow groan of heavy, wet leather, he hinged forward from the hips. His iron-scaled forearm emerged from his coat, and the two blunt, yellowed horn thumbs descended into the puddle.

The moment his horn thumbs pinched the bronze facets, the Lagging-Man stopped. 

He did not lift it immediately. 

The object was not acoustically dead like the green gin bottles or the lead plumb-bobs. Because of the spiral chambers cast into its core, the dodecahedron was catching the deep, fifty-cycle bass rumble of the turbines through the wet stone of the floor, pulling that vibration up through its bronze walls, and translating it. It was stepping the frequency up by three octaves, turning the subterranean thunder into a faint, continuous, golden hum that vibrated at the exact resonant frequency of the tallow-sacs in the Lagging-Man’s chest.

When he held it, his own internal bellows didn't have to work quite so hard to stay in tune with the floor. It felt like leaning against a warm boiler on a cold century, except the warmth was made of pure interval.

Slowly, the Lagging-Man levered himself upright, holding the bronze thing pinched between his thumbs. He turned and began the forty-minute slide toward the northern corner, behind the Seventh Tower.

The other two were already there. 

They stood side by side in the narrow, roaring alley between the timber framing and the weeping black shale, facing the four-foot cairn of glass, bone, and iron. Their heavy felt shoulders were an inch apart. The air around them was thick with the smell of sulfur, tallow, and wet wool. 

When the third Lagging-Man arrived, sliding to a halt beside them, neither of the others turned—they had no necks to turn—but the copper-wire diaphragms beneath their hat-brims tilted fractionally toward his hand. Through the roaring wall of moth-wings and rook-steam, they could already hear the small, sweet fifth that the bronze object was adding to the alley’s echo.

The third Lagging-Man raised his plated arm. With his left thumb, he scraped a fresh dollop of black bacterial grease and gray salt from the base of the timber frame, smearing it into a niche near the center of the cairn, right between a cobalt-blue poison bottle and the white ear-bone of a right whale. 

With a deliberate, hydraulic slowness, he pressed the twelve-sided bronze stone into the mortar. 

He lowered his arm. 

For an hour, the three of them stood in a row in the forty-eight-degree drizzle, while the water ran down their vulcanized backs and the kettle-rooks hissed overhead. 

The flat white light from the high tin sky hit the top facet of the bronze piece, glowing with a dull, buttery warmth against the black grease. The steam from the nearest standpipe drifted against its small, spiral orifices. As the warm mist condensed inside the labyrinthine chambers of the metal, the stone began to breathe a note back out into the alley—a quiet, steady, major third that bridged the gap between the deep rumble of the flywheels below and the high, metallic *shirr* of the moths on the brick. 

It did not change the song of the Sump. It completed the chord.

After three hours, the middle Lagging-Man raised his heavy, iron-shingled arm. His two horn thumbs reached out, gripped the bronze dodecahedron, and rotated it inside its bed of grease by perhaps two millimeters—just enough to catch the draft from the drainage channel beneath the rail tracks. 

The hum deepened by a comma, locking into the rhythm of the forty-drop-per-minute bolt on the third gantry across the plaza. 

The Lagging-Man lowered his arm. Together, the three of them stood in the wet, roaring dark behind the tower, their tallow-hearts beating in effortless, golden lockstep with the stone, listening to the centuries turn.
