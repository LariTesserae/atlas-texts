To a power that has watched continents drift like pond-scum and seen stars ignite and gutter out, the mountain is not a monument; it is a wave of stone in the middle of breaking. 

When the Presence turns its attention to the selenite pocket, it does not look with eyes, nor does it judge by the gentle standards of air-breathers who love warm meadows and quiet brooks. It perceives the chamber as a ferocious, geometric song—a high-pressure knot of physics, crystal lattices, and acoustic fury. And at the center of that knot, it perceives a smaller, stubborn counter-melody: the Weaver.

A lesser benevolence might feel pity. A naive god might crack the selenite wall to let the creature "free" into the sun, or calm the raging torrent into a peaceful pond—and in doing so, instantly murder the Weaver and its three pale young, suffocating them in stillness and crushing them under the sudden weight of their own dense bones. 

Wisdom knows that to love a thing is to understand what it is made for. The Weaver is not a tragic prisoner of the torrent; the Weaver *is* the torrent, given hands and a stubborn jaw. It does not want peace. It wants leverage.

The Presence looks at the ceiling, where the tiny lead-weighted mallet bangs endlessly against the seam between the white crystal and the black rock. It traces the hairline fracture the Weaver has spent six years widening by three millimeters. It follows that line of stress up, fifty yards through solid, stubborn basalt, to where the rock hides an ancient, flooded fault-network—a vast, miles-long cathedral of subterranean rivers, roaring with hyper-oxygenated snowmelt, rich with blind cave-fish and sliding gravel, wide enough for a hundred generations of Weavers to build their crystal cities in the dark and the deep.

The Weaver is digging in the exact right spot. Its instincts are flawless. Its math, calculated through its jawbone against the singing gut-strings, is sublime. 

It simply doesn't have enough time, and its tools are just slightly too soft. Copper bends; pure chalcedony is brittle and snaps under the mountain’s colossal sit-weight. In four years, the aragonite will choke the intake vent, the current will die, and the Weaver will suffocate with forty-eight yards of uncracked rock still above its head.

The Presence decides it will not break the ceiling for the Weaver. To do the work for a master craftsman is an insult that robs the creature of its dignity and leaves its children ignorant of how their world was bought. If the young are to survive in the deep rivers above, they must watch their parent break the sky, and they must learn the song of the wedges.

So the Presence does not touch the chamber at all. Instead, it reaches five miles away, up to the high, wind-scoured alpine plateau where the siphon begins.

Wait. Watch the glacier at the edge of the sinkhole.

High in the ice, frozen in the dark moraine for ten thousand years, is a boulder the size of a bull's heart. It did not come from this mountain; it fell from the black between the stars before the mountain was born. It is an octahedral mass of meteoric iron and nickel, laced with microscopic diamonds forged in the shockwave of its ancient impact. 

The Presence nudges a single, sun-warmed pebble on the ridge above the ice. The pebble rolls, clicks against a slab of slate, and triggers a quiet, localized slump of snow. The ice groans, parts like a curtain, and drops the star-iron into the roaring dark of the sinkhole.

Down in the ten-mile throat of the mountain, the Presence becomes a subtle hand upon the water. As the violent siphon chews the meteorite against the granite walls, the Presence guides the tumbling mass through the blind dark, letting the mountain do what the mountain does, only faster, and with exquisite aim. The iron is battered along its crystalline cleavage planes, splitting not into dust, but into long, flat, three-sided splinters and heavy, hexagonal rods—raw, indestructible billets of cold-forged heaven-metal.

At the same time, two miles deeper down, where the water brushes the hot basalt roots and drinks its fatal load of calcium and silica, the Presence makes an adjustment no larger than the width of a human hair. A subterranean vent of volcanic carbonic acid—a sour, fizzing vein of gas trapped in a side-pocket of stone—is pinched open just a fraction. 

The acid bleeds into the upwelling current. Not enough to burn the Weaver’s horn-goggled eyes or sting the milky skin of the three young in their crevice. Just enough to lower the pH of the water by three-tenths of a point. 

Back in the blinding light of the selenite room, the Weaver has reached the ceiling. It is wedged upside-down against the roaring roof, its double-heeled feet locked into a tiny undercut, its flat ribs heaving as it prepares to hammer the soft copper piton into the crack beside the four snapped crystal blades. 

Then, the water changes its taste.

The Weaver freezes. Its wide, shovel-shaped head tilts into the blast. Through its mouth and out its dorsal grates, the water suddenly feels *thinner*—cleaner, sharper, carrying the faint, electric tang of deep iron and sour stone. 

All across the black basalt walls, the creeping white frost of aragonite stops growing. The slight shift in acidity has halted the flash-precipitation. The existing crystal bleachers do not dissolve, and the chalcedony looms still slowly drink their silica, because quartz is tougher than lime—but the choking white death of the calcium crust has been arrested. The five-year clock has just been rewound to fifty.

Before the Weaver can process this reprieve, the intake vent at the bottom of the chamber coughs.

Up through the roaring throat shoots a cluster of dark, heavy shapes. They do not flutter like willow twigs or spin wildly like splintered bone; they rise through the forty-mile-an-hour updraft with the terrifying, slow-motion authority of cannonballs, fighting the water with sheer, alien density before clattering against the lower ledges and lodging in the notches of the back wall.

The Weaver drops from the ceiling like a spider on a cut thread, using the downward eddy along the selenite window to hurl itself to the bottom of the room, braking at the last millisecond with a scream of quartz-armored knuckles against the black rock.

It creeps to the nearest lodged splinter and touches it. 

The Weaver has never felt iron. It knows the greasy, malleable yield of native copper; it knows the brittle, ringing tension of chalcedony; it knows the fibrous give of mammoth bone. When it taps its rock-crusted thumb against this dark, silver-grey wedge, the vibration that travels through the water and into its solid jawbone is a revelation. It rings at a pitch so pure, so dense, and so utterly unyielding that the three pale young down in their narrow slot wiggle their shovel-heads in unison, feeling the harmonic through the bedrock.

The Weaver picks up the star-iron wedge. It weighs four times what a stone wedge of the same size would weigh. Its point is already tapered by the mountain’s throat to a brutal, three-sided chisel edge. 

And then the Presence does one last thing. It does not speak words, for the Weaver has no language for words. Instead, the Presence leans a fraction of its weight against the massive selenite slab from the outside, flexing the mountain's spine by the width of a single atom. 

Directly above the Weaver’s ceiling crack, the fifty yards of solid basalt lets out a low, sub-audible groan—a seismic chord that travels straight down the fault-line. To the Weaver’s jawbone, pressed against the rock wall, that groan is a map. Like sonar illuminating a dark ocean, the vibration reveals the exact path of the weakness in the stone above, and beyond it, the unmistakable, hollow, booming resonance of the great subterranean river-system waiting in the dark. 

The Weaver knows, now, that the sky is not solid. The sky is just a door.

The creature does not bow or pray. That is not in its nature. It shows its gratitude the only way a craftsman can: it drops the soft copper piton from its mouth, clamps its toothless, horny jaws around the heavy wedge of star-iron, and begins to climb back toward the ceiling, twice as fast as before, while down in the warm crevice below, three small heads watch and learn how to break a world open.
