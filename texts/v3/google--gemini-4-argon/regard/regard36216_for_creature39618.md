To a power that has seen a billion wet, roaring worlds rot and bloom in the dark, this dry dock of black glass is a jewel of rare and terrible austerity. 

The Watcher does not arrive with a footstep, for a footstep would shatter three blocks of salt-lattice and deafen the tuning-worms with a cataclysm of voltage. It does not arrive as a breath of wind, for an alien draft would tangle the plumb-lines in the ceiling and send the feather-light Architects tumbling into the razor-sharp cliffs like dry leaves in a gale. 

Wisdom, here, is the understanding of scale. Benevolence is the discipline of holding one's breath.

So the presence enters the basin only as an observation—a stillness that matches the stillness of the pumice. For a long time, measured not in hours but in the slow, creeping inches of the city’s northward march, the Watcher simply reads the equations of the pit. It admires the taut, silver flash of the tuning-worms translating structural grief into food. It watches the dark flocks of bellows-lice banking inside the parchment bellies of the racing spiders. It sees that the whole city is a single, divided mind—a machine made of bone-salt and static, endlessly drawing itself out of the air and erasing its own footsteps.

And because the Watcher can read the deep stone as easily as the salt, it sees the two tragedies written into the walls of the dry dock.

The first is the North Wall.

The city is migrating northward at a rate of four inches a day. The Architects are driven by an ancient, blind tropism—perhaps following the magnetic grain of the volcanic floor, perhaps simply fleeing the heavy, cold acid-fumes of the dissolvers behind them. Right now, there are forty yards of clean black clinopyroxene left between the newest, lacy white scaffolds of the northern quarter and the sheer cliff face. In a little less than a year, the racing spiders will reach the wall. 

They do not know how to turn. Their geometry is strictly linear, dictated by the ninety-degree habits of the caliper-mites and the east-west tension of the tuning-worms. When they hit the black glass, they will try to build *up* it, into the dead-air pockets where the cold downdrafts begin. The geometry will warp; the spires will crowd one another; the dissolvers, plodding relentlessly from the south, will catch up to the stalled builders. The city will dissolve its own foundations before it can figure out how to corner, and the whole dry, silent miracle will sublimate into a layer of sour white dust at the base of the cliff.

The second tragedy is above. 

A hundred feet up, the overhanging shelf of pale pumice—the roof that keeps out the rain, the wind, and the blinding, wet chaos of the outside world—is developing a fault. A hairline fracture, born from the same tectonic shrug that raised the black glass cliffs, is creeping toward the center of the span. One day, twenty or thirty years from now, a winter rainstorm on the surface will seep into that crack, freeze, and wedge a forty-ton slab of porous gray stone loose. It will drop into the basin like an iron anvil into a spiderweb.

A lesser benevolence might sweep down and gather the creatures into a safe, glass terrarium. A lesser god might pour vitality into them, giving them wet blood, loud voices, or the intelligence to recognize walls. 

The Watcher knows that to make them durable is to erase them. Their fragility is their entire nature; they are an argument made of glass and lightning, and if you change a single word, the meaning is lost.

So the Watcher decides to do three very small things. None of them will make a sound.

First, it addresses the roof. 

The Watcher reaches into the pale pumice shelf a hundred feet above the city, working at the level of the silicate grains. It does not seal the crack—that would stop the slow, microscopic shedding of biotite mica flakes that the downdrafts carry into the basin. Instead, six feet above the visible ceiling, deep inside the porous rock where the plumb-lines never reach, the Watcher slowly alters the crystal habit of the stone. Over weeks, it weaves a broad, flat arch of fibrous jadeite and fused quartz through the belly of the pumice. It creates a hidden lintel, shifting the weight of the overhang outward onto the massive black shoulders of the clinopyroxene cliffs. The hairline crack remains, still flaking off fresh black hexagons of mica whenever the surface temperature shifts, still breathing dry air, but the roof will now hold for ten million years.

Second, it looks down at the black floor in the forty-yard gap to the north, and at the wider expanses to the east and west.

The Architects do not see the cliff, because they do not see at all; they read the world through the tips of their six needle-legs, feeling the microscopic cleavage planes of the volcanic glass and the electrostatic gradient of the air. They build north because the rock-grain runs north, and because the northern cliff happens to be the steepest, shedding the coldest, most ion-rich downdrafts.

The Watcher places a finger of pure, localized heat—no wider than a pencil lead, and entirely invisible—against the eastern cliff face, and another against the southern wall, far behind the screed-crabs. Simultaneously, it introduces a faint, curving magnetic shear into the black glass of the basin floor. 

It does not force anything. It merely tilts the invisible weather. 

The next time a pocket of cool air spills into the basin, it does not roll straight from north to south. It catches on the new thermal differential along the eastern wall and begins to *spin*. The downdraft becomes a slow, basin-wide vortex, a silent, dry whirlpool of air that sweeps down the north cliff, curves smoothly along the eastern wall, washes across the southern flats, and rises again in the center.

Down in the northern quarter, a caliper-mite clamped to a fresh white strut feels the static charge hit its legs not from dead ahead, but at a five-degree angle from the east. Without thinking, it adjusts the ninety-degree bracket of its front limbs by five degrees to maximize the violet corona between its spines. 

Ten minutes later, an Architect sprints past at thirty miles per hour. Its front tarsus taps the caliper-mite's calcified shell—taking its measurement from the living corner, as it always does—and leaps outward into the empty air to draw a new flying buttress. Inside the spider's translucent abdomen, the dark cloud of bellows-lice reads the slight shift in the thermal updraft and banks half a millimeter to the right. 

The new white crystal flashes solid in the air. It is angled five degrees east of north. 

A block behind it, a tower full of tuning-worms feels the microscopic change in the structural torque. Four hundred glass threads flash silver in the twilight, pulling taut to drink the new angle of voltage, locking the curve into the tension of the grid.

The Watcher pulls back, satisfied. The city will never hit the wall now. Over the next three years, the avenues will bend into a vast, slow ring. The settlement will become an Ouroboros of potassium-salt—a circular ribbon of wire-frame towers endlessly chasing its own dissolving tail around the perimeter of the black glass courtyard. The screed-crabs will sweep the floor clean behind the dissolvers, and six months later, the racing Architects will come curving back over that same immaculate glass, sketching fresh white geometry into the air where their ancestors stood a year before, while the plumb-lines in the ceiling drop down like clockwork to seed the returning roofs.

They have been given eternity, disguised as a five-degree breeze.

The third thing the Watcher does has no utility at all. It is done purely out of love for the maker—for the blind, distributed genius of this silent town.

In the exact center of the basin, inside the new "eye" of the circular migration where neither the builders nor the dissolvers will ever walk again, a single, tiny pinnacle of black volcanic glass juts six inches up from the floor. 

The Watcher touches the tip of this obsidian needle and leaves behind a single, walnut-sized nodule of native tourmaline, doped with a trace of deep-earth rubidium. 

It is a piezoelectric stone, tuned to the exact resonant frequency of the potassium-salt lattice. As the great, silent wheel of the city turns around the perimeter of the pit—as the spiders land, as the towers settle into the acid mist, as the four hundred tuning-worms snap taut in the middle districts—the microscopic vibrations travel through the black glass floor and gather in this central stone. 

The stone does not hum; that would violate the peace of the dry dock. Instead, when the tension of the city reaches a particularly complex chord, the tourmaline converts the mechanical stress of the architecture into a faint, warm glow in the deep infrared—a spectrum invisible to human eyes, but felt by the dry, parchment skin of the Architects and the dark fronds of the lithophytes as a tiny, comforting hearth of radiant static at the center of their world.

And inside the stone, the Watcher leaves a recording capacity—a lattice of atomic memory that shifts its spin-states with every tremor. Every tower ever drawn into the cold air, every bridge, every ziggurat that stands for a month before melting back into white gas, has its exact geometric blueprint written permanently into the crystal heart of the tourmaline by the vibrations of its own collapse. 

Long after the sun of this world grows cold, if another traveler should ever peer over the razor-sharp rim of the dry dock, they will find the stone sitting on the immaculate black glass. And if they know how to read it, they will see a million years of silent, bone-white architecture unfolding in the dark—every fragile line saved, every silent spark remembered.

The Watcher withdraws from the pumice shelf, leaving the slate-gray twilight entirely undisturbed. Down on the floor, a cold draft curves down the avenue, and ten thousand dark fronds snap open at once without a sound.
