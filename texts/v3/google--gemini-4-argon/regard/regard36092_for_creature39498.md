When the attention first arrived, it did not come as light. Light would have been an insult here—a vulgar, bleaching intrusion that would have blinded the inward-staring eyes of the Sump-Wrights and spoiled the delicate, grease-slicked dark. 

Instead, the attention arrived as a quality of listening.

A lesser benevolence might have looked upon this continent-sized engine and seen a purgatory. It might have pitied the frantic, silent rush of the Gleaners, or mourned the Sump-Wrights trapped in the deep trenches, endlessly casting miniature monuments to a world that refused to hold still. A lesser wisdom might have reached down to cool the crust, to still the pistons, to bring a gentle, steady rain and the quiet patience of soil. 

It would have killed the mountain in the name of peace.

This power was older than that, and wiser. It watched the origami hounds stepping sideways through collapsing space; it watched the verdigris moss cushioning the blows of the titanic basalt presses; it heard the clear, silver *ping* of the Tappers and saw how the entire ecology leaned into the note like flowers toward a brief, acoustic sun. It understood that this place was not broken. It was a magnificent, ferocious fugue of matter trying to learn how to be alive.

Yet, as it listened across the span of centuries, the power noticed a single, quiet tragedy at the bottom of the trenches. 

The Sump-Wrights were drowning in their own fidelity. 

Because the mountain above never stopped revising itself, the slurry of broken strata never ceased to pour down into the canyons. And because the Sump-Wrights could not help but remember everything they digested, the canyon floors were filling up. Miles upon miles of terraced, three-inch bismuth dioramas—millions of extinct valleys and ten-minute peaks—were piling higher and higher in the dark. In the oldest trenches, the miniature landscapes had risen so high they were beginning to press against the weeping leather bellows of the Wrights themselves, choking their exhaust pipes, pinning their copper-sheathed arteries against the velvet-beetle walls. 

And worst of all: when the great trenches eventually snapped shut, as all trenches here must, those millions of tiny, meticulously cast memories were crushed back into anonymous slag in a single, muffled *shhh-chunk*. The mountain was furiously manufacturing itself, the Wrights were furiously remembering it, and the fault lines were erasing the memory before it was ever truly known. The engine was running in a closed loop, generating heat, motion, and survival, but no *rest*.

The benevolent power considered this. It knew that if it simply cleared the canyons, the Wrights would only fill them again. If it made the dioramas indestructible, the planet would eventually choke on its own indestructible past. 

What the mountain needed was not an end to its labor, nor the preservation of its rubble. It needed its labor to *mean* something beyond the next stroke of the piston. It needed a sabbath that fit within the rhythm of a four-stroke engine.

So, the power did three very small things. It did not break the rules of the mountain; it simply introduced a new harmonic.

***

First, it touched the deep mantle, miles beneath the lowest Sump-Wright, where the heat was born. It did not cool the fire, nor did it slow the great hydraulic pressures. Instead, it introduced a subtle, planetary syncopation—a fifth stroke to the four-stroke fumaroles, occurring only once every thousand cycles. 

The next time the synchronized vents across the thousand-mile plateau chuffed their pale clouds of sulfur and mineral oil: *One. Two. Three. Four.*

And then, instead of the immediate intake of *One*, there was a *Fifth*. 

It was a long, slow, subterranean draw of breath. For the span of ten heartbeats, the upward pressure of the mantle paused in a state of suspended, frictionless grace. The titanic slabs of dark stone along the fault line slid to the top of their strokes and simply floated there, balanced on cushions of warm steam. The wooly silence of the mountain deepened into something vast and cathedral-like.

In that ten-second hiatus, the Gleaners of the Slip stopped blurring. For the first time in their existence, they did not have to outrun the falling rock. They stood completely still on the razor-edged shelves of obsidian, their bone calipers hanging loosely at their sides, their half-real bodies settling fully into the present moment without the threat of being crushed. They simply breathed the turpentine-and-mint-scented air, feeling the strange, novel luxury of weight without danger.

***

Second, during this brief, recurring pause, the power gave a gift to the drifting, vacuum-tube jellyfish in the tarnished twilight above. 

Normally, when their trailing tendrils brushed the rock, the stone forgot its hardness, turning into a grey paste that remembered only gears and cogs. The power altered the static electricity flickering inside their translucent bells, tuning it to a slightly warmer frequency.

Now, when the great pause occurred, a few of the sky-drifters would sink down into the mile-deep trenches, drawn by the heat of the Sump-Wrights. Their tendrils would brush not against the canyon walls, but against the towering, choking piles of miniature bismuth dioramas that threatened to smother the great refineries. 

Where the tendrils touched the tiny, cast-iron valleys and bismuth peaks, the metal did not turn to gear-paste. Instead, it sublimated. It turned into a fine, luminous, bronze-colored mist that smelled faintly of old rain and warm brass. 

As the four-stroke rhythm resumed and the fumaroles chuffed again, this bronze mist was carried upward by the thermal updrafts, rising out of the suffocating trenches and spreading across the high plateaus. The Sump-Wrights, relieved of the crushing weight of their own excreta, heaved long, shuddering sighs through their leather bellows, their external arteries pulsing with sudden, unburdened ease.

And the memories were not lost. As the luminous bronze mist settled over the copper ferns and the verdigris moss above, the flora absorbed it. The plants began to grow not just in frantic, shutter-clicking violence, but in the *shapes* of the extinct valleys. A shelf of basalt would shear away, and the ferns that erupted from the scar would uncoil their copper fronds to mimic the exact silhouette of a ridge that had collapsed three centuries ago. The past was no longer piling up as dead slag in the dark; it was being woven back into the living canopy of the present.

***

The third thing the power did was the smallest of all, and it was for the Sump-Wrights and the Tappers alone.

It looked at the Sump-Wrights—those vast, immobile engines with their clusters of cataract-white eyes staring endlessly inward at their own pumping hearts—and it looked at the tiny, fastidious Tappers walking across their backs. 

The power did not turn the Sump-Wrights’ eyes outward; the bruised-plum dark of the trench walls held nothing they needed to see. Instead, it braided a microscopic thread of resonance between the steel prongs of the Tappers and the optic nerves of the Wrights.

The next time a Tapper stopped on the trembling back of a Sump-Wright, raised its tuning-fork head, and struck a calcified pipe—*Ping*—the silver needle of sound did not merely travel outward to warn the Gleaners and the origami-hounds of a shifting fault. 

It also traveled *down*, through the blubbery oil-sacs and the weeping leather bellows, straight into the dark, multi-chambered gut of the Wright. 

For the half-second that the silver note hung in the greasy air, the flickering static light inside the Sump-Wright’s body changed. The shadows of its dozens of hearts faded, and across the cataract-white surfaces of its inward-staring eyes, the echoes of the Tapper’s note painted a picture. 

It was an acoustic image of the whole mountain above. 

For one glorious, ringing moment, the Sump-Wright could *see* the world it was refining. It saw the thousand-mile fault line sliding in oiled precision; it saw the slow-motion arcs of orange sparks; it saw the Gleaners dancing like telegraph keys across the paradox-stone, and the copper ferns wearing the shapes of the very valleys the Wright had remembered for them. It saw that it was not just a blind gut at the bottom of a crack in the dark, but the heart of a vast, intricate, and breathtakingly living body.

Then the note faded. The pistons slid down with a muffled *shhh-chunk*. The Tappers gathered their wire legs and marched toward the safe angles of the stone. The Gleaners blurred in their wake. 

The benevolent power withdrew its hand, leaving no footprint, no monument, and no demand for worship. It simply left the mountain to manufacture itself in the dark—only now, every thousandth stroke, the machine took a breath, remembered its own beauty, and went on.
