The attention of a great power does not always arrive like a thunderbolt or a voice from a burning bush. Sometimes, it arrives like the warmth of a hand held an inch above a cold stone, or the sudden, inexplicable decision of a mason to lay down his trowel and go home an hour early.

The power that looked down through the split-cedar lattice did not see a mistake of engineering, nor did it see a monster trapped in a box. It saw a miracle of accidental harmony: a creature of the wild dark mud that had allowed itself to be remade into living crystal rather than die, and a sterile, geometric room of stone that was slowly, clumsily, being loved into an ecosystem by a beast with a brain the size of a walnut.

A lesser god, or a foolish one, might have shattered the western wall to return the *aker-mer* to the river. A sentimental spirit might have scooped him up and dropped him back into the dark gravel of the foothills. 

But this power was wise. It knew that the creature had already changed too much for the mud. If returned to the silt, his clarified skin would burn and ulcerate; his lungs, grown vast and silver-branched on the roaring champagne of the chute, would suffocate in the slow, warm soup of the oxbows. He belonged to the beryl light now. He was the spirit of this specific water.

Yet, if nothing were done, the ending was already written, and it was a short one.

The mortar was finite. Already, the lower courses were stripped clean of their squeezed-out lime ridges, and the creature was having to reach higher, stretching his heavy, glass-green bulk up the pillars toward the waterline. Within two months, the lime would be gone. Furthermore, at the first Turning of the Ditch—the autumn maintenance, four months away—the city’s water-wardens would drop the wooden sluice-gate in the hills to inspect the new masonry. The chute would go dry. The cistern would drain through the bronze grates. The men would climb down with ropes and lanterns, and they would find a six-legged, translucent bullock gasping on the dry limestone floor. Out of fear and confusion, they would kill him with iron bars and haul him up in pieces with block-and-tackle.

So the wise power reached down—not to break the rules of the world, which it had helped to write, but to nudge the probabilities, to whisper into the ears of men and the instincts of beasts, and to make three small, quiet adjustments.

***

The first adjustment took place six miles away, at the springhead in the foothills, where the limestone trough began.

That evening, as the sun went down in a smear of apricot and violet, a winter-old she-crab, heavy with a sponge of thirty thousand dark orange eggs beneath her apron, was picking her way across the gravel basin. She was a mountain crab, flat-shelled and the color of wet slate, built for wedging herself under stones in fast water. 

Normally, the roaring draw of the new stone sluice terrified the creatures of the basin; it smelled of raw copper and burned lime, and the current was too smooth to grip. 

But tonight, a sudden, warm gust of wind blew down from the ridge, smelling inexplicably of rich, sweet calcium and safe, deep corners. At the same moment, a heavy, oily mud-carp—a sluggish, bronze-scaled female near to bursting with roe—turned her nose toward the wooden gate, drawn by a sudden, phantom trail of crushed cedar needles and honeybee wax.

They did not go alone. For three hours after twilight, a tiny, silent migration slipped over the lip of the intake trough. A dozen river-limpets, their shells thick with the spores of velvet-moss and crustose lichen; a mated pair of flat-headed stone-loaches; four pregnant river-shrimp; and the she-crab, who tucked her claws tight against her face, folded her eight legs over her egg-sponge, and let the smooth, sun-warmed stone slide her down into the dark like a child on a chute.

They tumbled for two leagues. It was a violent ride, a roaring, starlit sluice, and normally the sixty-foot drop at the end would have dashed the carp to pieces against the limestone floor.

But as they shot from the eastern chute into the dark cistern, the *aker-mer* was not sleeping over the western grates. Drawn by a sudden, singing itch in his nostrils, he was standing directly beneath the plunge-pool, his back arched high, his three-inch-thick coat of rubbery jelly swollen to its softest, most compliant fullness.

The carp hit his broad, cushioned flank at forty miles an hour, bounced off with no more injury than a lost scale, and shot into the deep, quiet eddy behind the northern pillar. The stone-loaches slithered down his tail like rain off a leaf. The she-crab landed squarely between his eyes, gripped the tough skin of his shovel-snout with two desperate pincers for three seconds to arrest her fall, and then scuttled down his cheek and vanished into the two-inch gap beneath the lowest step.

The *aker-mer* did not snap at them. He stood very still in the dark, roaring water, his golden, pupil-less eyes wide in the starlight falling through the cedar grates. The water suddenly tasted different. It tasted of *company*.

***

The second adjustment happened in the mortar joints, twenty feet below the surface, right behind the southeastern pillar where the creature kept his hoard.

The limestone blocks of the floor had been laid over a bed of rammed earth and gravel, which in turn sat upon the ancient, fractured bedrock of the plateau. Two feet beneath the southeastern corner of the cistern floor, a narrow, crooked vein of pure satin-spar gypsum and chalk—soft, sweet, and laid down by a dried sea ten million years ago—ran through the dark stone.

Under normal circumstances, the two-inch bedding of hydraulic lime mortar between the floor blocks would have kept that vein sealed away forever.

The power did not use a hammer. It simply encouraged the carpenter’s three-inch copper nail. 

As the *aker-mer* licked the nail the next morning, relishing the sharp, green-lightning tang on his tongue, his rough, gray-plated jaw pressed the sharp point of the metal directly into the mortar joint between two floor slabs. A microscopic fault line, left there when a tired apprentice had mixed a batch of lime with slightly too much sand, gave way.

A hairline crack opened, running two feet down through the mortar bed and kissing the top of the chalk vein.

Warm water, driven by the twenty-foot head of the cistern’s pressure, forced its way down into the dry chalk. Five minutes later, a slow, steady plume of milky white water—thick with dissolved gypsum, sweet calcium, and ancient sea-salts—began to weep upward through the floor joint, right beside the pile of cedar needles.

It looked like a tiny, underwater chimney smoking in the quiet corner. 

As the milky plume hit the cold copper nail and the lime-rich water of the cistern, it began to precipitate. It didn't form brittle ridges that would run out in a month; it began to grow a soft, porous, cauliflower-like boss of travertine stone right on the floor. It grew a quarter of an inch a day, fed by a mountain vein ten miles long—an endless, self-renewing salt-lick of chalk and sweet stone that would last as long as the hills stood.

When the *aker-mer* returned from his noon bath under the waterfall, he saw his hoard dusted in fresh, sparkling white snow, and the nail half-buried in a blooming rose of soft stone. He lowered his broad snout, blew gently through his nostrils to clear the cedar needles aside, and gave the new growth a long, slow, rasping lick. *Scritch.* His silver lungs expanded in a silent sigh, and he settled down on all six legs, curling his notched tail around the smoking crack like a dog around a bone.

***

The third adjustment was the most delicate, because it involved the minds of men, which are noisy, anxious, and hard to steer without breaking.

Ten days later, the Chief Water-Warden of the city—a fifty-year-old engineer named Kheb, whose knees ached in the damp and whose belt was heavy with brass calipers and lead plummets—walked up the hill to inspect the new cistern. With him were two quarry-masters and a junior priest of the Temple of the Measure.

"It's too loud," the junior priest said as they approached the low, square wall that surrounded the cedar grating. Even thirty paces away, the ground hummed beneath their sandals, and a fine, cool breath of mist rose through the split-cedar poles into the dry scrub-oak air, smelling sharply of cedar-rot, wet lime, and wild mint. "We should have put a baffle-box at the head of the chute. The turbulence will scour the mortar out of the eastern wall in five years."

"We'll drop the gate in the autumn," Kheb grunted, wiping sweat from his bald crown. "Go down with baskets and re-point the joints with puzzolana. Let's have a look at the silt-lines."

They stood on the timber balks directly over the center of the pool and looked down through the gaps in the cedar poles.

At first, because of the blinding midday glare and the violent silver plume of the waterfall, they saw only the dancing webs of sunlight on the cream-colored masonry twenty feet below.

"Clear as gin," one of the quarry-masters said proudly. "Not a spoonful of mud on the steps. My boys laid that floor tight as a cup."

"Look at the mortar on the north pillar," the junior priest frowned, shading his eyes. "It's already stripped flush for ten feet up. The current shouldn't be scouring the lee side of the pillar like that. And look at the eelgrass—every tuft is sitting on a little white button of lime, like someone went down there with a paring knife and trimmed around them."

Kheb didn't answer. He was squinting down into the southeastern corner, where the water was calm.

Down there, twenty feet under the beryl-tinted glass, the *aker-mer* was lying flat on the floor. He had grown another six inches in the last ten days. His skin was so clear that the masons’ chisel-scallops on the floor slabs were clearly visible right through his back, slightly magnified and bent by the curve of his three-inch jelly coat, like pebbles seen through the bottom of a thick green bottle. Only his branching silver lungs, rising and falling in a slow, four-part rhythm, and the constellation of white chalk-crumbs suspended in his belly betrayed that he was solid at all.

Around his broad, shovel-shaped snout, three small, bronze-backed carp—fingerlings hatched five days early in the warm, oxygen-drunk water—were darting in and out of the gill-slits on his neck, picking microscopic flecks of algae from the velvet nap of his skin.

As the four men watched from the lattice above, a single white blossom from a wild almond tree on the hillside blew across the dry ground, slipped between two cedar poles, and dropped onto the heaving surface of the water.

Kheb watched the flower. And because he was watching the flower, he saw the water *thicken* beneath it.

He saw six short, stumpy legs tuck smoothly against a barrel-shaped side. He saw a notched, rudder-like tail give two lazy, massive strokes that sent a distorted lens of green light rippling across the submerged steps. He saw a shape the size of an ox—made entirely of mint-colored ice and silver lace, with a pale skeleton floating inside it like ivory in aspic—rise effortlessly through the geometric shafts of sun.

The junior priest gasped, his hand flying to the bronze amulet at his throat. "Gods below," he whispered. "What is that? It's in the drinking water. Kheb, tell the runners to go to the gatehouse. We have to drain it now. Get the lime-burners with their pikes—"

"Shut your mouth," Kheb said softly.

The old engineer did not know that a wise power was standing right behind him on the cedar beams, resting a warm, invisible weight upon his shoulder, lending his tired eyes a sudden, piercing clarity. He only knew that for thirty years he had built channels, wells, and reservoirs across three provinces, and he had seen water go bad in a hundred ways. He had seen cisterns turn red with blood-worms; he had seen them choke with black slime that smelled like a dead dog; he had seen the lime scale up so thick on the bronze grates that the walls burst from the pressure.

Down in the water, the glass bullock reached the surface, caught the white almond blossom delicately on the flat shovel of its nose without breaking the surface tension, and sank slowly back toward the floor. As it passed the western wall, its wide, rubbery mouth opened and gave a single, careful *scritch* against the bronze outflow grates, rasping away a tuft of dead cedar-needles that had begun to clog the slots, swallowing the lime-scale with them, leaving the bronze bright and clear.

"It's an *aker-mer*," the older quarry-master breathed, his knuckles white on his measuring-rod. "My grandfather saw one once, when they dug the foundations for the bridge at Avaris. Only that one was black as pitch and no bigger than a pig. They said if you kill one, the spring dries up for seven years."

"It's not black because the water is clean," Kheb said, his voice dropping into the register he used when arguing with governors. He felt a strange, deep lightness in his chest, as if he were breathing the mist rising through the grates. His aching knees didn't hurt at all. "Look at him. You can see the chisel-marks right through his ribs. He's eating the loose lime so it doesn't Wash down the pipes and choke the street-fountains. And he just cleared the western grate."

"He's a beast," the priest objected, though his voice lacked conviction as he stared down at the glowing silver trees pulsing inside the creature's chest. "When he dies, he'll foul the whole supply."

"They don't die," the quarry-master said simply. "Not if the water keeps moving. They're like tortoises. They turn into stone from the inside out when they get old. My grandfather said their bones are made of pure alabaster."

Kheb looked down into the southeastern corner. The creature had settled back onto the floor and was tilting its head to slide the white flower onto a neat, absurd little pile of cedar needles, right beside a blooming white rose of fresh travertine that was clearly sealing a floor-joint leak Kheb hadn't even known was there. A slate-gray mountain crab crawled out from under the lowest step, pinched a waterlogged honeybee in half, and handed the larger piece up toward the creature’s rubbery lip; the *aker-mer* ignored it, letting a pair of stone-loaches dart in to take the prize instead.

"If we drop the sluice-gate in the autumn for the inspection," Kheb said slowly, thinking aloud, "the water will drain out the bottom grates, and the weight of his own belly will crush his lungs on the dry floor."

He turned to the quarry-master, his eyes bright and sharp. 

"Go back to the yard," Kheb ordered. "Have the smiths cast four bronze stop-logs, eighteen inches high, fitted to drop into the grooves just in front of the western outflow grates. Before we ever drop the mountain sluice for maintenance, those stop-logs go in first. This room never drains below six feet of water. Not ever. Put it in the wardens' ledger, under the temple seal."

He turned to the junior priest, who was still staring down through the lattice, where a shaft of noon sun had just struck the creature's pupil-less golden eyes, sending two tiny, twin moons of reflected light dancing across the underside of the cedar beams.

"And you," Kheb said to the priest. "Go tell the High Measure that the eastern cistern has taken a guardian. Tell him the water is sweet, the grates are clear, and if any man drops a line, a net, or an iron bar through this roof, I'll have his hands nailed to the sluice-gate."

The priest swallowed, looked once more at the living block of beryl light below them, and nodded. "What... what should we enter for its upkeep? In the temple accounts?"

Kheb watched a second white petal drift down through the air, catch an eddy of mist above the grates, and drop toward the roaring silver plume.

"Plant two more almond trees on the windward side of the roof," the engineer said, turning to walk back down the hill. "And tell the carpenters, when they come up to mend the cedar lattice every spring, to drop a handful of new copper nails through the grates. I don't know why, priest, but I think he likes the taste."
