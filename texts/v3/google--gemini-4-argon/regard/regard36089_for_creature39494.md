To a lesser mind, the Caldera-Trench looks like a sentence of hard labor. A benevolent force of limited imagination might look down upon the Kett—seeing their pruned skin, their frantic, stilt-legged dance over scalding mud, their babies tethered by gut-cords above boiling vents—and feel pity. Such a power might be tempted to cool the magma, still the vents, and seed the valley with gentle soil and soft grass, turning the roaring cauldron into a garden.

The Wise Power knows that this would be murder. 

To quiet the trench would be to break the clockwork of the Kett’s souls. Their genius *is* the dance. Their art is the reading of the tremor; their pride is the ninety-second harvest; their reverence is the crystal pillar of an ancestor holding up the morning’s net before returning to the deep earth. To take away their danger is to make their stilts, their whistles, their scarred maps, and their ferocious, synchronized grace completely meaningless. 

So the Power does not still the earth. It listens to the symphony of the basin—the *crack-ping* of glass, the bone-rattle of the crabs, the shrieking steam-pipes, and the *clack-clack-thrum* of the tapping-poles—and decides not to change the music, but to enrich the instruments.

The intervention happens in three quiet ways, woven so seamlessly into the violent metabolism of the trench that the Kett will never know they were blessed; they will only believe they grew cleverer, and the earth slightly more generous.

### 1. The Singing Faults

The cruelest deaths in the trench are the silent ones. A Listener can read the deep, liquid burps of magma thirty feet down, and they can feel the hollowness of a blister-dome right beneath their stilts. What kills a Kett is the *shear-pocket*—a lateral shift of superheated brine ten feet below a solid slab of basalt that gives no warning tremor until the rock simply vanishes beneath a Rigger’s weight.

The Power reaches down into the boundary layer where the upper crust meets the magma-blisters and subtly alters the crystallization of the rising minerals. 

Where deadly, silent shear-pockets are forming, the Power coaxes the precipitating iron and silica to form microscopic, hollow lattices—millions of tiny, resonant chambers within the new stone. Now, when a hidden pocket of brine begins to eat away at the underside of a seemingly stable shelf, the escaping gases are forced through these mineral lattices before the rock gives way. 

To the naked ear, nothing changes in the deafening roar of the valley. Yesterday’s death-trap sounds the same as today’s. 

But to a Listener pressing a fire-hardened pole to the stone and a masked cheekbone to the wood, the rock has learned a new word. Three minutes before a silent collapse, the vibration in the pole shifts from a dull, solid hum to a distinct, harmonic buzz—like a trapped wasp humming in a hollow gourd. 

The first time a young Listener feels this new vibration, she hesitates, confused, then trusts her instincts and throws her arm skyward. Her hunting party bounds away across the black glass just before an acre of three-day-old basalt—rock that should have been safe for another week—drops into a violet cauldron. Within a generation, the Kett have named this vibration the *Stone-Whimper*, and they carve new, wider resonators onto the butts of their tapping-poles to catch it better. No one dies without the earth speaking first.

### 2. The Cliff-Reed

The Power looks next at the *Pendulums*—the communal sleeping-sacks hanging fifty feet up the sheer basalt walls—and sees the great bottleneck of Kett existence: wood. 

Everything the Kett do relies on the upper world’s timber and reeds. Their tapping-poles, their wicker frames, the spikes that hold their sleeping-sacks to the cliffs—all of it must be harvested by scaling the three-hundred-foot sheer walls into the cold, flat world above, a place where the stilt-walking Kett are clumsy, half-blind without their obsidian goggles, and vulnerable to the predators of the plains. Worse, the wood of the upper world was not made for the trench; it rots in the constant steam, and the cliff-spikes slowly char and weaken in the sulfurous updrafts, occasionally sending a sleeping family plunging into the dark during a night-boil.

The Power takes a stray spore of one of the twenty-minute valley puffballs—blown high up against the cold basalt cliff—and marries its rapid, heat-drinking nature to the fallen seed of an upper-world iron-reed trapped in a crevice.

A new thing begins to grow along the fifty-foot line of the cliff face, right where the rising steam meets the cold, stable stone of the upper shelf. 

It looks like a thicket of grey-green bamboo, rooting directly into the hairline fractures of the basalt and drinking the mineral-heavy condensation that rolls up the walls. Because it feeds on the trench’s exhaust, its cell walls do not build themselves merely of cellulose; they pack themselves with precipitated silica and native copper dust. 

When the Kett discover these thickets growing right beside their Pendulums, they find a material that might have been dreamed into existence by an ancestor. Cut green, the stalks can be split into strips as tough and flexible as rawhide, weaving into wicker back-frames that weigh half as much as the old ones and never rot in the damp. Cured over a fumarole for an hour, a whole stalk turns into a tapping-pole as light as hollow bird-bone, as unyielding as bronze, and thrice as conductive to the vibrations of the deep rock. Driven into the cliff crevices as hanging-spikes, the living roots of the cuttings actually grip the stone, bonding with the basalt until the Pendulums are anchored not by dead, rotting pegs, but by living iron.

The Kett no longer need to climb into the blinding, alien flatlands above. The trench provides its own bones now.

### 3. The Memory-Glaze

Finally, the Power looks at the center of the basin, where an old Rigger—his joints too stiff with sulfur-rheumatism to dance—has just been laid down upon the fastest-growing aragonite terrace while fifty bone-whistles shriek his dirge into the steam.

The Kett do not fear being eaten by the rock; it is right and good that an ancestor holds up a flyer-net for a week before sinking back into the magma. Yet the Power sees the silent grief in the eyes behind the obsidian goggles when a particularly beloved grandmother or a legendary Listener is swallowed by a fissure after only a few days, leaving nothing behind except a new scar tattooed onto a survivor's pale skin.

The Power touches the deepest geothermal vents at the very heart of the basin—the sacred terraces where the Kett always lay their dead.

It does not stop the terraces from rising and collapsing. Instead, it introduces a trace of rare, heavy earth-metals and a new strain of extremophile microbe into those central waters. 

Now, when the mineral spray coats a body, it does not lay down ordinary, brittle white aragonite. The microbes react with the cooling flesh and the cured leather of the Kett’s armor, weaving the precipitating minerals into a dense, cross-linked matrix of opalescent chalcedony and metallic copper threads—a stone nearly as hard as corundum, shot through with shimmering veins of blue and gold.

Within a week, the ancestor is still transformed into an abstract crystalline pillar, and the young Riggers still lash their guy-lines to its shoulders. And within a month, the terrace still shudders, cracks, and drops the pillar down into the churning violet mud.

Wait, however, for the next great "boil." 

Because the new funeral-stone is denser and harder than the surrounding travertine and basalt, the shallow magma-blisters do not melt it, and the exploding steam domes do not shatter it into dust. Instead, the tumbling underground churn breaks the pillar into fist-sized, fire-polished nodules of iridescent, copper-veined agate, and spits them back out through the geysers weeks or years later.

A child, swapping out clogged bone-whistles along a fresh fissure, sees something gleaming in the grey slush. She reaches down with tongs and pulls out a smooth, warm stone that flashes deep blue and bright copper in the glaring sun—the only round, worn thing in a valley of razor edges. 

When she strikes it against her flint scraper, it rings like a bell. When knapped by a master toolmaker, it yields blades sharper and ten times more durable than brittle black volcanic glass. When a sliver of it is ground thin and fitted into a mask, it cuts the blinding white glare of the terraces better than obsidian, while letting the wearer see the subtle heat-shimmers of escaping gas.

The Kett quickly realize where these stones come from, for they only erupt after a funeral terrace has been swallowed. 

The dead do not merely hold up the nets for a month anymore. Now, they go down into the dark to be forged by the deep earth, returning years later to become the unbreakable knife in a granddaughter’s hand, the bell-stone hanging from a Listener’s belt, and the clear, protective lenses through which the clan watches the ground.

---

The Wise Power withdraws its hand and simply watches. 

Three hundred feet below the continental shelf, the Caldera-Trench is as loud, as scalding, and as restless as it was at dawn. The crabs still clatter like dry bones; the flyers still snap in the hot wind; the rock is still arriving. 

Through a tearing veil of steam, six figures come vaulting across the fresh black glass on stilts of living silica-reed. They move with a terrifying, joyful speed, looking through the eyes of their ancestors, listening to the singing warnings of the stone, completely at home in the storm of matter.
