A lesser power, looking down into the greased skillet of the High Slump, would have been offended by the grayness of it. A lesser power would have parted the pewter mist with a theatrical shaft of golden sun, or struck the mountain’s cold belly to give the people a roaring thermal spring, or taught them the secret of burning coal. 

And in doing so, it would have killed Spindle-Hollow in a week. 

The sudden heat would have rotted the giant cushion plants into a sliding bog; the sun would have blinded eyes bred for luminous lead; the drying of the peat would have turned the town’s grounding-bed into an insulator, and the very next cloud-bank grinding over the western lip would have discharged its static in a single, catastrophic blue hammer-stroke, frying every Reed-Tuner on the gantries.

The attention that actually settled over the basin was neither sudden nor bright. It arrived on a Tuesday in the middle of the long season that the lowland calendars call summer, though in the Slump it was merely the time when the forty-degree damp smelled slightly more of wet iron-birch than of frost.

The Power did not descend like a bird; it condensed, like the mist itself. For three days, it simply existed in the spaces between the copper fins and the wet wool. It listened to the amputated hush of the valley. It felt the slow, heavy *thump-shuck* of the hillside rocker-arms through the soles of a hundred clogs, tasted the buttermilk-sour tang of a leeching bladder-fern in the back teeth of an old woman on the third terrace, and observed the patient, shivering point of a stilt-hound’s snout beneath a dry bearing on Tower Four-Nineteen.

It saw a people who had not conquered their harsh world, nor been crushed by it, but had instead threaded themselves through its eye like a greased wire. They did not need saving. To take away their labor would be to unravel their language, which lived in the brass knocks along the pipes; to take away the cold would be to insult the thick, beet-dark blood they had spent twenty generations brewing in their barrel chests.

Yet, the Power was benevolent, and it had knees of its own, after a fashion. It felt the one true, quiet misery of the Hollowers: the slow, inevitable victory of the damp over the marrow. It saw how, by fifty, a gantry-walker’s knuckles were so swollen with the gray-ache that he could barely close his fist around a brass hook. It saw the small slate markers in the high peat-gardens—too many of them short—for the children whose lungs had filled with the "pewter-croup" before their chests could barrel out to take the thin, glassy air. It saw the endless, Sisyphean exhaustion of the four-hour watches spent scraping strangle-moss from the high gears while the mist ate through three layers of tallow-soaked shearling.

So the Power decided not to break the machine, nor to rebuild it, but merely to *tune* it. It would introduce three small notes into the ecology of the High Slump, each so native in its shape and smell that the Hollowers would never know whether they had been blessed by a god or had simply stumbled across a stubborn new habit of the mountain.

***

The first gift was given to the granite and the copper.

Deep beneath the lowest terrace, where the town’s main grounding-cables were driven twelve feet down into the wet peat and bolted to the ancient, whale-backed bedrock, the Power reached into the mineral seams. It did not create gold or coal. Instead, it nudged the crystalline structure of a long, dormant vein of dark, soapy stone that ran beneath the central viaduct—a stone the locals called *grease-schist* and ignored because it was too soft for walls and too slick for cobbles.

The Power altered the stone’s memory of heat.

It began with old Maura Cleary, the senior Reed-Tuner of the West Notch, whose left index finger was crooked like a dry juniper root and who hadn't slept four hours straight since the winter of the Big Shear, so fiercely did the static-ache hum in her hipbones. She was seventy-two, and the cold had gotten behind her kneecaps and stayed there.

She was walking the lowest conduit-trench at dusk, where the fat, thumb-thick copper grounding-lines dove into the muck. Her bare, scarred knuckle was an inch from the main return-pipe, reading the evening bleed-off.

Usually, the ground-return tasted like cold pennies and old snow. Tonight, her boots stopped on the boardwalk. 

The hair on her knuckle was standing up, pulled toward the wet copper, but the taste at the back of her molars wasn't sour, and it wasn't cold. It tasted like *toasted barley*. 

Frowning beneath the dripping rim of her sou’wester, she did something a Tuner rarely did: she laid her bare palm flat against the sweating pipe.

It wasn't hot—heat would have drawn the strangle-moss in a strangling green tide. Instead, the pipe was vibrating at a frequency so low and so fine it felt like the purr of a sleeping animal, and that vibration was passing directly through her skin and into the dense, heavy blood of her veins. Where the weak, cold current of the Slump had always left a brittle, garlic-smelling static in the joints, this slightly altered current—filtered through the newly awakened schist below—acted like a gentle, internal induction coil. It didn't warm the air; it warmed the *iron in the blood*.

For the first time in thirty years, the gray-ache in Maura’s hip unclenched its teeth. A long, slow breath went out of her, misting white and then vanishing into the pewter dark. 

She stood there for ten minutes in the dripping hush, her hand on the pipe, while the rocker-arms went *shuck-shuck-shuck* overhead. Then she took her bronze wrench from her belt and tapped a slow, four-beat cadence onto the low-pressure line—the knock for *come and see, no danger, bring no tools*.

Within a month, the Hollowers had figured it out, in their pragmatic, wordless way. They dug up the soft, dark grease-schist from the lower trench and found that when a slab of it was bolted between a household air-accumulator and the grounding-wire, it turned the stray, nuisance static of the rowhouses into that same deep, marrow-soothing hum. They began carving small, flat ovals of the stone—*hum-stones*, they called them—and slipping them into the linings of their clogs and the pockets of their oilskins, wired to their copper grounding-tails. A gantry-walker could still be crusted with sleet on the outside, his canvas stiff as horn, while inside his woolens his joints moved as easily and warmly as a tallow-greased piston.

***

The second gift was given to the bellows-newts and the strangle-moss.

The Power had watched the maintenance crews on the upper gantries, swinging in their leather harnesses seventy feet up in the blind, lead-colored fog, hacking endlessly at the wet green felt that choked the brass Savonius rotors. It was the town’s great tax: half the calories the Hollowers scraped from their root-gardens were spent simply keeping the valley from digesting their iron.

One night, inside the warm, oil-smelling dark of Compressor Shed Three, the Power touched the jelly-like eggs of a clutch of bellows-newts laid behind the main leather lung.

When that generation hatched in the autumn, the machinists noticed nothing different at first, save that the young newts were slightly darker—the color of bruised plum rather than pale chamois—and their blind, flat heads had a faint, coppery sheen along the jaw. 

The difference showed when the frost-winds began to howl through the western lip. Previously, the bellows-newts would curl into tighter knots around the indoor axles, refusing to go near the drafty eaves. These new, plum-colored newts did the opposite. Drawn by the faint, violet crawl of St. Elmo’s fire along the outer guy-wires, they waddled out through the rocker-arm slots and climbed into the freezing mist, their bellies slick with their thick, anti-corrosive slime.

They were not going out to freeze; their blood now carried the same inductive trick as the stone below, keeping them forty-five degrees inside so long as they touched a charged wire. They were going out to graze.

To these new amphibians—which the gantry-crews quickly dubbed *gear-lickers*—the fibrous, heat-seeking strangle-moss was no longer an obstacle; it was fodder. And more than that, as they crawled through the teeth of the turning iron cogs, chewing the moss away from the hot bearings, their belly-slime left a faint, salty trace of lithium and copper-salts on the metal. Strangle-moss touched by that slime didn't die—the Power knew better than to starve the soil of its compost—it simply lost its appetite for friction-heat, dropping off the iron housings in neat, heavy mats to rot harmlessly on the boardwalks below.

A gantry-walker named Joric was the first to see it clearly. He had climbed Mast Twelve in a thick, greasy blow, his brass pruning-hook ready to clear a stalled rotor, only to find the Savonius drum turning sweet and silent. Curled around the main bevel-gear were four fat, plum-dark newts, methodically licking the bronze teeth clean while their tails grounded out a tiny, harmless trickle of violet sparks into the wet air.

Joric didn't use his hook. He reached into his pocket, pulled out two strips of dried mist-skate wing and a lump of mutton tallow, and tucked them into the flange of the bearing housing. He tapped two quick clicks on the guy-wire to the mill below—*line clear, full pressure*—and climbed back down to have his tea twenty minutes early.

***

The third gift was the smallest, and it was given to the lungwort that hung from the twisted dwarf-birches.

The Power knew the sound of the pewter-croup—that small, wet, rattling cough in the fourth-story bedrooms where the bog-gas globes burned dim and amber through the night. The thin, forty-degree air, heavy with bladder-fern spores and grease-damp, was hard on new lungs that hadn't yet learned the trick of the mountain.

Out in the tangled thicket near the eastern notch, where the mist spilled out of the basin into the great emptiness below, a single curtain of rubbery black lungwort began to grow small, pale-amber nodules along its scalloped edges, no bigger than peppercorns. They only grew where a stilt-hound had paused to shake the water from its long, knobby legs, or where the wind carried the faint, nitrous mist of the town’s sluices.

It was a stilt-hound that showed them to a scraper-boy named Tam, whose little sister was three floors up in Rowhouse Seven, breathing like a cracked bellows. The hound—a scarred old female with one ear notched from a falling wrench—did not stand beneath a failing tower that morning. She stood beneath an iron-birch on the edge of the terrace, shivering in the draft, her long snout pointed straight up at a curtain of black lichen, her jaws making the wet, rhythmic *clack-clack-clack* they used when a bearing was dry.

Tam climbed up into the wet branches, expecting to find a dead mist-skate tangled in the twigs. Instead, he smelled it: a sharp, clean scent cutting through the wet wool and ozone, like bruised mint, warm pine-pitch, and something deeply, anciently sweet—the smell of sun on high rocks, translated into a language that a nose bred in the fog could understand.

He scraped a handful of the amber nodules into his tallow-pot and took them home to his grandmother, a woman who knew every root and slime in the Slump. She bit one between her front teeth, felt her own bronchial tubes open like a greased valve, and immediately dropped six of them into the boiling kettle of sump-tea on the catalytic grate.

The steam that rose in the cramped, slate-walled kitchen didn't fight the heavy air of the basin; it married it. When the little girl breathed it in, the amber resin coated the delicate, raw linings of her chest against the spore-damp and expanded the tiny vessels to catch the thin oxygen. By midnight, the rattling in the room had stopped, leaving only the sleeping *hiss* of the floorboard air-pipe and the steady, deep rise and fall of a Hollower chest learning its trade.

***

And that was all the Power did.

It did not build them a temple, for they had no spare slate and no room on the terraces. It did not give them a name to pray to, because shouting a name into the High Slump was like tearing a wet rag, and the mountain would only eat the noise.

Instead, the Power simply lingered for a few centuries more, diffused through the mist that rolled over the western lip, crawled through the iron forest of Spindle-Hollow, and spilled out the eastern notch. 

If there was a prayer in the valley after that, it was not spoken with the mouth. It happened at twilight, when the sky deepened from lead to the color of an iron skillet, and the gangs came down from the gantries with their knees un-aching and their lungs clear. Before stepping across their thresholds to scrape the fern-slime from their clogs, a Hollower would often pause for a second in the narrow, moss-furred alley. They would take the brass pruning-hook or the bronze wrench from their belt, reach out to the nearest sweating copper conduit, and tap a single, soft, resonant note into the spiderweb of the town—not a warning, and not a call for tallow, just a small, ringing *clink* that traveled up the guy-wires to the turning brass rotors, and down through the wet peat into the warm, humming dark of the mountain’s bones, just to let the machine know it was running well.
