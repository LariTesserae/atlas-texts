A lesser benevolence would have looked down through the grey ice-slush and pitied them. It would have stilled the violent gyres, or warmed the thirty-four-degree water into a tropical soup, or cracked the basin’s rim to let the trapped sea drain away so the blind, heavy beasts could remember the sun. 

And in doing so, it would have killed them in a week. Without the twenty-knot gales to spin the silver-fish vortices, the plumbs would starve. Without the freezing head-pressure, their dense, purple-blooded hearts would overheat and burst. Without the violent standing waves singing through the apertures, their rubbery, gel-pitted prows would go deaf; the city would vanish into a silent, featureless dark, and they would wander in circles until they died.

The power that paused above the cloud-fog was not foolish. It looked down through the pewter twilight and saw the truth of the valley: that this was not a ruin, and the plumbs were not survivors clinging to a wreck. They were craftsmen in a workshop of pure force. They had taken a catastrophe of ice and stone and turned it into an instrument they knew how to play.

Yet the power also saw what the plumbs, for all their acoustic mastery, could not calculate: the math of their own erasure. 

The city was running out of notes.

Every time a thinned-out arch finally gave way with that sharp, echoing *clack*, the plumbs gained a boulder to tune a culvert, yes—but the valley lost an aperture. Over the millennia, the relentless hydrodynamic friction was winning. The ziggurats were smoothing down into low, humped blisters that no longer tripped the deep currents. The great colonnades were snapping one by one, their rubble swept eventually into the deepest abyssal drains where even three-ton beasts could not winch them back up. In another thousand years, or two, the basin would be scoured completely flat—a smooth, silent porcelain bowl where the currents would race unhindered around the rim like water in a bucket, leaving no lee, no eddies, no standing waves, and nowhere to hide the young.

The wise power did not want to break the gale. It wanted to give the engineers more stone.

It did not use magic that defied the laws of the valley; it used the valley’s own deep memory. Descending below the forty-fathom amphitheaters, below the fitted polygonal slabs of the floor, the power reached into the ancient volcanic throat of the mountain that the original builders had capped and plugged ten thousand years ago.

It did not wake the volcano. A sudden eruption would have boiled the basin and choked the clear water with ash, blinding the plumbs’ pores and ruining the vacuum-lock of their suction cups with grit. Instead, the power adjusted the deep tectonic valves by the breadth of a fingernail.

Far down in the blind dark beneath the central plaza, where the warm upwelling vents met the freezing glacial down-drafts, a sealed seam in the foundation parted. 

Nothing exploded. No silt rose. Instead, a slow, pressurized extrusion of mineral-rich brine—thick with dissolved silicates, calcium, and dark basaltic paste—began to bleed upward into the thirty-four-degree water, right at the base of the most eroded, paper-thin bridge-tendons and hollowed-out monoliths.

To a human eye, it would have looked like a faint, shimmering heat-haze rising from the mortar joints. To the plumbs, it was a new song. 

As the heavy, mineral-saturated water hit the freezing currents, it did not disperse; it precipitated. Where the shearing water slammed hardest against the worn stone, the pressure and the sudden drop in temperature forced the dissolved minerals out of solution, Accreting atom by atom, layer by microscopic layer, onto the scoured basalt. 

It was the reverse of erosion. In the exact spots where the hydrodynamic friction was most violent, the stone began to grow back—not in the sharp, unnatural rectangles of the ancient builders, which the water had hated and spent ages erasing, but in dense, fluted, stromatolite-like ribs and bulbous buttresses that followed the natural curves of the turbulence. The thinning bridges thickened like healing bones. New, hollow chimneys of pale travertine and dark silicate began to rise around the vents, growing an inch a year, shaped by the very currents that tried to tear them down.

And the power left them one other thing.

In a wide, dead-water courtyard near the center of the valley, where the stone was too flat for the plumbs to farm and the currents too confused to spin the ribbon-eels, the power pressed its thumb into the newly softening mineral floor and drew it back out, leaving a single structure.

It was a tuning fork of black, glassy basalt, thirty feet high, shaped like the twisted horn of a ram or a chambered nautilus sliced in half. It had no straight lines. Its curves were calculated to the thousandth of an inch against the barometric gyres of the valley.

When the power withdrew, the twenty-knot rip-current hit the new horn. 

It did not hum like the rest of the city. Because of the precise, spiraling geometry of its chambers, it caught the chaotic, silent turbulence and organized it. A deep, clear, resonant *B-flat*—a note so pure and massive it made the water itself feel denser—rolled out from the courtyard and echoed across all three miles of the valley.

Every plumb in the sunken sea froze. 

In the middle of winching across the avenues, in the middle of peeling slate-rays from the leeward walls, hundreds of heavy, grey shapes locked their cups to the stone and lifted their blind, pitted prows. They tasted the new note. They felt how the horn took the violent downdraft and split it cleanly into three rich, slow-spinning vortices that trailed half a mile down the boulevard like braided ropes, thick with trapped eels and warm vent-fat.

Slowly, with the rhythm of ratchets, the nearest plumbs released their rear anchors—*pop, pop*—and began to haul themselves toward the sound.

They gathered around the black horn over the next three days, dozens of them, sliding their calloused bellies over the smooth pavement. They did not worship it; they had no mind for gods. Instead, they did what they were made to do. One by one, the great, scarred beasts winched themselves up the outside of the spiral, pressing their sensitive, gel-filled snouts directly against the vibrating stone, letting the pure, organized thunder of the water pass through their skulls and into their heavy bones. They felt the angles of the curves. They learned the math of the spiral. 

Then, sliding back down to the floor, three of the largest plumbs found a four-hundred-pound boulder of freshly grown travertine that had calved from a nearby chimney. They wedged their broad snouts against it, waited for the five-second lull in the gale, and began to heave it down the avenue—not to jam it crudely into a drain as they had always done, but to place it at a new, precise angle against a pillar, trying to match the pitch of the horn.
