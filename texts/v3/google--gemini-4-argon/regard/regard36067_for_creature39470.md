A lesser power, looking down into that bruised, freezing dark, would have felt pity. 

A lesser power would have seen the forty hunched figures straining against the weight of the water, seen their stiff, ratcheting limbs and their faceless heads of bundled reed, and thought: *They are trapped.* It would have parted the permanent shroud of freezing cloud to let the golden warmth of the sun strike the water. It would have calmed the violent thermal surges, stilled the screaming currents, and perhaps tried to gift the Orcharders with softer bodies, or eyes to see colors, or voices to sing songs that were not made of water-pressure data and valve-whistles.

And in doing so, that lesser power would have murdered the valley in an afternoon. Warmth would have rotted the taut, cold-cured wood of the bladder-pines; light would have blinded the lantern-moss; stillness would have starved every biomechanical leaf and left the Orcharders paralyzed on the bare slate, their internal bladders slack, suffocating in a quiet, lukewarm grave.

The power that came to the high basin was not lesser. It was old enough to understand that love is not the act of making a thing like yourself; love is the act of helping a thing become more fully what it already is.

For three hundred years—which to the Orcharders was perhaps three generations of frame-weaving, and to the Listener was the length of a single, appreciative breath—the power simply watched and listened. It did not take a shape. It became an attention resting in the water, feeling the deep, double-bass *hum* of the pine trunks in its own non-existent teeth, admiring the crisp, pistol-shot *crack* of the bone-kelp, and following the mint-green flare of the central tower as if watching a hearth-fire in a well-kept home.

It saw that the Orcharders were not suffering. When an Orcharder locked its three-pronged root-clamp onto a guide-rope and let the freezing mid-day torrent wind its shoulder-springs tight—*click-click-click-click*—what the creature experienced was not agony, or drudgery, but a clean, fierce satisfaction. It was the joy of a well-strung bow being drawn; the purpose of a mill-wheel meeting the race. Their silence was not loneliness; the whole valley was their voice. Every thrumming pine and whistling reed-bed was a word they had pruned and spaced into being.

They lacked nothing of spirit. 

But as the centuries turned, the wise power looked higher, up past the fifty-foot ceiling of the water, up into the unseen, eroded peaks wrapped in their permanent shroud of freezing fog, and it saw the three slow tragedies that were creeping toward the basin. None of them were things the Orcharders could fix with a flint knife or a stone maul, because all of them lay outside the reach of their guide-ropes.

The first tragedy was the stone itself. 

In the high, blind gullies where the glacial meltwater gathered before plunging into the lake, a massive lip of rotten shale was slowly giving way. In fifty years, or sixty, a winter heavier than usual would cleave that mountain-face. Ten million tons of dead, crumbling rock would slide into the throat of the main thermal chute. The great mid-day current would not stop, bit its path would be choked and baffled. The water would enter the basin not as a roaring, unified wind-tunnel, but as a weak, swirling scatter of eddies. The orchard would slowly go quiet. The Orcharders would creep slower and slower between their huts, waiting for a storm strong enough to wind their spines, until the last ratchet failed to catch.

The second tragedy was at the bottom of the valley, in the black flint beds. 

The Orcharders did not mine; they plucked flint nodules from an exposed fault-line at the western edge of the plateau. After millennia of harvesting, the fault was picked clean of large stones. Already, the Orcharders were having to resharpen old, stubby blades until they were scarcely longer than a thumb, binding them clumsy and tight to the driftwood handles. Within ten generations, they would have nothing left to slice the iron-hard kelp-hide or harvest the pine-bladders.

The third tragedy was not a threat of death, but a limit on their joy. 

Because the water was so violent, the sound of the valley was rich in the bass and the treble—the low groan of the trunks, the sharp *crack* of the spores—but it was muddied by the sheer chaos of echoes bouncing off the bare whale-backed ridges. At the far margins of the basin, two miles out from the green-lit tower, the acoustics broke down into a deafening, unreadable roar. If an Orcharder wandered out into the border-wastes to retrieve a drifting ribbon-fern, its reed-head was blinded by white noise. Every few decades, one of the forty would lose the guide-rope in that outer din, wander in circles as the surge died, and run out of torsion in the silent dark, too far away for the others to hear the faint click of its clamps. 

The power considered these three things. It knew that if it worked a miracle—if it descended in a pillar of light and stacked new flint at their doors—it would break the austere, dignified logic of their world. It would make them feel small in the only place they had ever been masters.

So the power worked the way the water worked: by weight, and cold, and stone.

First, it went up into the freezing fog of the peaks, where no Orcharder could ever climb. It did not blast the rotten shale away; instead, over the course of twenty winters, it guided the freezing and thawing of the ice in the deep mountain cracks. With the patience of a mason, it wedged the subterranean basalt apart and fused the rotten shale with veins of hard quartz, reshaping the hidden throat of the glacier into a vast, spiral nozzle—a stone rifling miles long. 

Now, the chute would never collapse. More than that: when the mid-day meltwater plunged down from the heights, the spiral stone spun the water into a tighter, cleaner, more reliable column of force, ensuring that even in the coldest, driest centuries to come, the storm would arrive on the plateau on the minute, strong and deep.

Second, the power sank its attention into the crust beneath the western edge of the basin. It did not create flint out of nothing. It found a deep, ancient seam of black chert and volcanic glass lying thirty feet beneath the slate floor, and it nudged a dormant geothermal fault—not enough to warm the water by even a tenth of a degree, and not enough to throw up a single grain of blinding silt. 

During the height of a screaming mid-day surge, when the valley was already so loud that a subterranean groan would not startle the workers, the western ridge simply *heaved*. A clean, six-foot step of black stone rose from the floor like a shelf pushed up through a table. Along its face, cracked into neat, pillow-sized ingots by the cold shock of the water, lay enough razor-sharp black glass and horned flint to keep the forty—and a hundred generations after them—in long knives for a million years. 

The third gift required a finer touch. It was a gift for their minds.

Out in the roaring border-wastes, where the echoes went mad and the Orcharders lost their way, the power began to carve the whale-backed ridges. It used the rolling, calcified root-knots of the ribbon-ferns and the heavy boulders tumbling down from the heights, steering them by infinitesimal shifts in the currents to grind and polish the outer cliffs. It shaped a series of wide, parabolic hollows into the rim of the basin, facing inward toward the central tower. 

And in the center of each hollow, it planted a seed. 

It did not invent a new organism from thin air; it took a cutting of the hollow tube-reeds and a spore of the bone-kelp and coaxed them, over three seasons, into a new, natural sport—a sparse, towering species that the Orcharders themselves might have bred if they had possessed another ten thousand years of trial. 

These new plants grew only on the outer rim. They were single, hollow columns of petrified wood, thirty feet high, each split down the center like a tuning fork and bridged by a single, drum-tight membrane of pale leather-kelp. 

When the next great mid-day current swept down from the newly rifled peaks, the water hit the valley with a clarity it had never possessed before. 

In the center of the basin, the great stone cone flared with its cold, mint-green light. Out of the low doors of the shepherd huts stepped the Orcharders, leaning forty-five degrees into the gale, their chest-mosses pulsing like green coals, their spines ratcheting tight—*click-click-click-click*—as the glorious, cold pressure poured through their open back-valves, filling them with sudden, ecstatic strength.

And then they stopped. All forty of them, spread across miles of dark slate, locked their knee-ratchets and tilted their faceless, reed-bundled heads upward.

The white noise at the edge of the world was gone. 

Out in the dark wastes, the new parabolic cliffs caught the chaotic roar of the water, focused it, and drove it through the thirty-foot tuning-forks. For the first time in the history of the high water, a pure, unchanging fundamental note—a clear, solemn, crystalline *C-sharp* that sat precisely between the low rumble of the pines and the high whistle of the reeds—rang out from the four corners of the plateau. 

In an instant, the entire three-dimensional map in the Orcharders’ acoustic heads snapped into razor-sharp focus. 

The tuning-forks acted as acoustic lighthouses. By reading the phase and shadow of that pure, ringing note against the bass of the pines, an Orcharder standing by the central tower could "see" a single frayed strand on a guide-rope two miles away. It could see the exact curvature of the distant ridges. It could see the newly risen shelf of black glass in the west, gleaming acoustically like a vein of bright silver against the dull grey sound of the slate.

Two Orcharders, holding fast to the same guide-rope near the green-lit tower, turned toward one another. The mint-green light leaked through the chinks of their wooden ribs, illuminating the swirling, crystal-clear water between them. 

They leaned their reed-heads together and opened their chest-valves. 

Usually, their exchanges lasted no more than two seconds—a clipped report of tensions and weights. This time, they stayed leaned together for a full minute while the current raged around them. They vented water through their pipes in a slow, testing chord, pitching their internal valves to match the new, pure *C-sharp* ringing from the outer dark, harmonizing with the horizon. 

It was still a report on water pressure and geometry. To the end of time, they would only ever speak of the work. 

*The horizon is true,* the chord said. *The stone is sharp. The water is strong.*

Then they broke apart. The one on the left reached out with its three-pronged clamp, locked onto the braided kelp-rope, and began to pull itself out toward the western ridge with long, bounding strides to harvest the new glass. The one on the right unslung its heavy stone maul and turned toward a grove of bladder-pines that needed thinning, moving with a swift, lethal grace through the freezing dark, while the benevolent power withdrew from the water and left them to their loud and perfect peace.
