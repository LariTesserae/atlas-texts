The attention that finds the cistern does not arrive like a beam of light. Light is for things with eyes, and the power that pauses sixty feet below the municipal asphalt has been around long enough to know that eyes are only one narrow way of agreeing that the world exists.

Instead, the attention arrives as a slight thickening in the sixty-cycle hum. 

For a week—though time here is measured only in the cycling of the southern float-trap, some four thousand *clonk-sloshes*—the attention simply inhabits the equilibrium of the room. It does not judge the commonwealth for lacking a spine, nor does it pity the *Ficus* for never having felt a wind that didn't smell of boiled river-silt and packing grease. A lesser benevolence might have been tempted to "uplift" the drapes—to spark a sudden, feverish human-style consciousness into the tallow-colored jelly, giving it names for the pipes and a tragic awareness of the street above. 

A wiser kindness knows that to give a creature a mind unsuited to its body is a form of murder. If the commonwealth woke up tomorrow and understood *loneliness*, or *ambition*, or the concept of *outside*, it would tear its own meniscuses to pieces trying to climb the narrow ventilation shafts into the dry, freezing exhaust of the city's buses.

The commonwealth is already doing something magnificent. It is holding a small piece of the world together by sheer, blind, hydraulic patience. It is an engine of pure maintenance, a slow, four-hundred-pound thought whose only verb is *to mend*.

Still, a wise power looks ahead. And looking ahead, through the trembling geometry of the room, it sees two flaws in eternity. 

The first is above, on the fourth floor of the Municipal Annex, where a clerk in the Department of Subsurface Infrastructure has just highlighted a forty-year-old map in yellow marker. The city is auditing its heat-loss. A pressure drop of four percent has been noted between the river turbines and the courthouse radiator banks. A work order is three days away from being typed—an order that will bring men with oxy-acetylene torches to cut open the paved-over hatches, followed by industrial fans to clear the carbonic mist, followed by forty-pound sacks of copper sulfate dumped into the brick sluices to kill the "fouling."

The second flaw is in the vault itself. The cast iron is eighty years old. The commonwealth can patch the flange-joints with snail-shells and dead skin, and it can glue the gate-valves into the roots, but it cannot smelt iron. Along the belly of the primary thirty-inch hogshead main, graphitic corrosion has been quietly turning the metal into something soft enough to carve with a thumbnail. In eleven months, when the winter boiler-load peaks, a six-foot section of that main is going to blow out entirely. Not a pinhole leak, not a whistling flange, but a catastrophic rupture of superheated, two-hundred-pound steam that will flash-boil the sluices, strip the *Monstera* to white sticks in ninety seconds, and cook the commonwealth into soup before it can even flinch.

The power considers this. It does not want to break the rules of the world; miracles that leave footprints only invite more clerks with yellow markers. 

So it works in the vernacular of the vault: rust, water, paper, and neglect.

First, upstairs. It is a very small thing to alter the friction of a brass bourgeois gear inside a forty-year-old pressure gauge at the courthouse substation. A microscopic fleck of oxidized zinc drops from the inside of the bezel and wedges beneath the needle's counterweight. The gauge now reads four percent higher. Simultaneously, in the basement archives of the Annex, a pipe sweats. Not a steam pipe—just a cold-water domestic line above a filing cabinet. Four drops of water seep through the seams of a steel drawer and land squarely on the folder labeled *District 4: Exchange Vault B (Decommissioned)*. 

By Friday, the damp manila folders have swollen just enough to jam the drawer on its rollers. When the clerk pulls on the handle, it sticks. He is a tired man, forty minutes from his weekend, and the courthouse pressure reading has just come back normal anyway. He lets go of the handle, marks Sector 4 as *Inspected / Nominal*, and goes home. The hatches remain paved. The torches stay in their shed.

Down in the cistern, the second intervention requires a heavier hand, though a slower one.

The power reaches down below the brick sluices, into the deep glacial clay and the cracked limestone bedrock that carries the city’s forgotten groundwater. It nudges the chemistry of a seep three hundred yards upriver. Nothing miraculous—just a slight loosening of the silt beds, allowing a vein of water rich in dissolved iron carbonate, silica, and stray magnesium from an old industrial dump to bleed into the blind brick culvert that feeds the vault’s southern sluice.

Then, it touches the largest of the drapes—the two-hundred-pound matriarch currently resting in the black mud beneath the catwalk. 

It does not speak to her. It simply alters the electrical resistance of three violet-black nodules pressed against the foot of the central stanchion. 

For an hour, the sixty-cycle hum from the transformer boxes doesn't just feed the drape; it *itches*. It is a new frequency, a harmonic wobble that travels through the common iron of the catwalk and makes the red and green veins inside all five drapes pulse three seconds faster than their five-minute tide. 

Drawn by the itch, the great sheet in the mud does not wait for a pipe to whistle. It gathers itself into its thick, braided cable-form and hauls itself up onto the belly of the thirty-inch hogshead main—right over the six-foot stretch where the iron has gone soft and graphitic, eleven months from bursting.

To the drape, the weak spot in the pipe now feels intolerable. Because the iron there has turned to carbon-sponge, it no longer conducts the sixty-cycle hum correctly; to the creature’s newly sensitized nodules, the rotten metal feels like a ragged hole in the dark, a cold spot that needs to be filled.

The commonwealth convenes, not in panic as when a flange shrieks, but in a slow, luxurious compulsion. 

They do not use their own scalded skin this time; there is no live steam to cook them. Instead, they drink the new, mineral-heavy water coming through the southern sluice. The power has tweaked the enzymes in their pale, milky globules by a fraction of a dalton. As the drapes flow back and forth over the rotten belly of the main, their five-minute tidal surges begin to precipitate the dissolved iron and silica out of their own jelly. 

They are no longer just smearing horn-colored mucus. They are secreting a dense, metallic nacre—layer after layer of bio-mineralized ironstone, cross-linked with chitin and old flake-rust. It is the same trick a deep-sea snail uses to armor its foot in iron sulfide around a hydrothermal vent, borrowed from the bottom of the ocean and given to a child of the city's river-silt.

Over the next six months, the commonwealth builds a ribcage around the thirty-inch main. It is four inches thick, harder than the original 1890s cast iron, and dark as gunmetal, rippled like the inside of an oyster shell. When the old metal finally gives way in January under the winter boiler-load, the pipe does not burst. It doesn't even hiss. The steam simply settles against the inside of the living ironstone casing, which holds the two hundred pounds of pressure without a tremor.

And then, because the power is not merely an engineer, it leaves a gift.

High in thedarkness, where the great thermal expansion loop slides along its greased sleeve every few minutes, the grease has been drying out for decades. Eventually, the collar would have seized, snapped a bolt, and torn the hanger out of the brick groin-vault. 

The power does not add grease. Instead, it encourages a single, mutated spore of a pale, subterranean bracket-fungus to take root in the warm peat packed around the expansion sleeve. The fungus grows into a rubbery, ten-pound cushion between the sliding iron collar and the stop-plate. It weeps a thick, lipid-rich oil that smells faintly of nutmeg and wet stone, lubricating the slide forever.

More than that: the fungus changes the sound of the room.

Now, when the expansion loop reaches its threshold and slides that half-inch, it no longer lets out the harsh, metallic *groan* that used to make the whole commonwealth flinch and go opaque with stress. 

Instead, cushioned by the dense fungal pad, the heavy iron pipe settles with a deep, resonant, twenty-hertz *thrum*—a warm, cello-like vibration that rolls down the stanchions, rings softly through the rusted catwalks, and sets the water in the brick sluices into interlocking, geometric ripples. 

When that sound happens now, the five great drapes do not stiffen like cold wax. 

Across the two-hundred-pace length of the cistern, where they lie draped over the *Ficus* knees and wrapped around the humming transformer grounds, their slick, tallow-colored surfaces ripple in unison. The red and green veins inside them dilate. The pale, milky globules pause in their five-minute tide, suspended in the warm jelly, and for four full seconds, the entire commonwealth simply vibrates with the iron—a blind, three-hundred-pound sigh of pure, conductive contentment—before the lukewarm droplets patter down from the *Monstera* leaves, and they go back to digesting the mud.
