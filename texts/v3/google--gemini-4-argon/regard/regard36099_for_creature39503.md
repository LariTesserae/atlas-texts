A lesser benevolence would bring light. It would look upon the blind, calcified dark and pity it, dropping a sun into the basin or clearing the chalky water so the creatures could see the grandeur of their own tenements. 

A lesser wisdom would bring peace, smoothing away the territory disputes on the bridges or teaching the solitary matriarchs to build together in love.

The power that finds this place does neither. It sinks its attention down past the shelf where the daylight dies, settling into the blood-warm, alkaline bowl like a change in barometric pressure. It listens to the sandpaper rasp of ten thousand snail tongues. It feels the slow, six-hour lungs of the bedrock. It watches a six-foot matriarch in the lower wards carefully cement the flat tooth-plate of a ray into a crumbling lintel, tilting the bone three degrees to catch the sulfur-rich breath of a flue.

And the power recognizes an artist. More than that: it recognizes a civilization on the threshold of a tragedy it does not have the organs to foresee.

The power reads the deep stone beneath the city—not just the ten-thousand-year crust of the vanished tubeworms, wait-listed for collapse, but the magma chambers miles below that drive the six-hour breath. It sees that the geothermal heart is shifting. In three hundred years, the great central vents will choke on their own mineral wealth and seal shut; the heat will migrate four miles south, into the barren, shelterless silt of the open shelf. The six-hour breath will become a five-hour breath, then two, and then the cold water from the rim will slide down and never leave. The bacterial scum will starve, the snails will still, and the great, baroque mosaics of the Lime-Wrights will become frozen mausoleums.

Furthermore, the power sees the Wrights’ other prison—the terrible, biological ceiling of their kind. Like their cousins in the sunlit seas above, a matriarch lays her eggs only once, deep in a sealed vault, weaving them into strings attached to the ceiling. For eight months she blows warm, clean water over them with her siphon, starving slowly in the dark, her thick mantle shrinking, her aragonite sweat flaking away until she is soft and ragged. When the fingernail-sized young drift out into the canals, she is already dead. She cannot teach them the geometry of the flues. She cannot tell them which arches bear weight and which are rotten with boring-sponges. Every generation of Lime-Wrights must deduce the laws of fluid dynamics and masonry from scratch, guided only by instinct and the mute shapes left behind by their mothers.

They have built a city, and they have developed a language of vibrating skin, yet they have no history. Every Wright is an orphan reinventing the arch.

The power considers this for a hundred cycles of the basin’s breath. It does not want to break the dark, hush-bound beauty of the bowl. It will not give them hands of iron or eyes that have no use for the gloom. Anything it gives them must be made of chalk, sulfur, pressure, and bone.

When the intervention comes, no creature in the basin knows it has happened.

Deep beneath the floor of the central plaza, where the bedrock cracks to let the hot water through, the power reaches into the rising plume of minerals and makes two subtle adjustments to the chemistry of the earth.

First, it opens a series of hairline fractures leading southward—not opening them wide, which would bleed the city’s pressure, but scoring a faint, heated trail through the solid rock toward the new thermal fields four miles away. Over the next century, tiny, warm vents will begin to pop open in the barren marl beyond the basin’s rim, one after another, like a line of breadcrumbs leading into the dark. The Wrights, who follow the drafts with their skin, will find the new heat and begin to carry their rubble outward, extending their aqueducts and covered streets across the sea-floor, marching their city mile by mile to meet the shifting heart of the world.

Second, the power alters the trace minerals dissolved in the hot water of the lower wards, where the great matriarchs live.

Into the chalky soup, it introduces a complex, heavy metallic salt that the glowing green bacteria eagerly consume, concentrating it in their greasy mats. The snails scrape the bacteria; the crabs eat the snails; the rays crush the crabs; the Wrights drop like wet tarpaulins upon the rays. 

The new compound enters the Wrights’ blood and goes straight to their skin glands and their nervous systems. It does two things.

In the matriarchs, it breaks the ancient, suicidal lock of their reproduction. The metabolic switch that tells a mother’s body to digest itself while tending her clutch is quietly turned off. For the first time in five thousand generations, a great six-foot Wright in a lower-ward vault watches her eggs hatch, feels the tiny, soft-troweled bodies crawl across her calcified mantle—and does not die. She is hungry, and she is thin, so she reaches one long, boot-tongue arm out the doorway of the vault, snatches a passing eel from the eddy she built forty years ago, and feeds. And her young, instead of scattering blindly into the jaws of the silverfish, stay in the vault and watch how she holds the bones.

The other thing the mineral does is change the mortar.

As the Wrights drink the new chemistry from the water, the aragonite paste they sweat and the mucus they cough from their siphons cure into a slightly different crystal lattice. It is denser, harder, and fiercely piezoresonant—like quartz, or bone china. 

Previously, when two Wrights sat on a bridge and sang their territorial threats with their skin, the vibrations died in the water the moment they stopped flexing their papillae. Now, when a Wright presses her trembling mantle against a wall she is mortaring, the wet paste *remembers* the shape of her skin. As it hardens in the alkaline dark, the microscopic ridges of her thrumming papillae set into the stone like the grooves of a phonograph record.

Three cycles later, a young Wright comes gliding down that same corridor during the cold two-hour lull. Its eight arms reach out, trowels brushing the walls to read the draft. 

Its skin scrapes across the fresh mortar of the archway, and the hardened ridges catch against its own sensitive papillae. A ghost-vibration runs up its arm—not the live voice of a rival, which is loud and pushes the water, but a dry, whispered chord locked inside the stone itself:

*Narrowed here,* the wall says to the passing fingers. *Three inches. Watch the back-eddy.*

In the dark of the lower wards, an ancient matriarch sits in her baroque plaza of crab-claws and ray-skulls. Her surviving daughter—a small, three-foot thing—sits on the opposite parapet. They do not love each other, for they are still octopuses, cold and solitary at heart. They will eventually wrestle for the prime cul-de-sac when the daughter grows heavy enough. 

But for now, as the four-hour exhale begins and the white snow starts to blow from the chimneys, the old one lifts a flat, heavy skull-plate to wedge into a gap. Before the mortar sets, she presses her throat against the wet seam and hums a long, low, complicated chord—not a threat of weight and thickness, but a diagram of the four rooms behind her, the pressure of the flue beneath the floor, and the seasonal rot of the sponge-gourds on the roof. 

She pulls back. The lime hardens. The wall holds the thought.

The city is no longer merely managed. It is becoming a library. And having wound the clock of their survival a little tighter, and given them the chalk to write their own names in the dark, the wise power withdraws—leaving the water the temperature of blood, leaving the silverfish to their ribbons, and leaving the blind masons to their work.
