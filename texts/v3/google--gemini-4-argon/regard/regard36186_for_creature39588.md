To love a thing wisely is to understand what holds it up.

When the Great Attention turned toward the Escarpment, It did not come with the clumsy pity of a lesser god. A lesser god would have looked at the purple-veined cheeks, the freezing ledges, and the dark, dung-smelling vaults, and It would have been horrified. A lesser god would have smote the rock to bring forth gushing warm springs; It would have softened the climate into a perpetual lowland spring; It would have given the people iron and mortar, and raised up lush forests of pine and oak along the terraces.

And in doing so, a lesser god would have murdered five million people in a single afternoon. 

Warm rain would have greased the friction-joints of the dry-laid slate, turning five hundred miles of forty-degree city into a billion-ton avalanche of mud and screaming stone. A thicker, warmer air would have drowned lungs built Like great leather bellows, rotting them with lowland damp and quick-moving plagues. Iron would have sped up the *tink-tink-tink* of the quarries until the people out-dug the mountain’s ability to settle, deafening the elders of the Plumb. And if the mountain were made easy, the *stillness of the blood* would vanish; the people would hurry, they would sweat, they would build towers, they would make kings, and they would fall.

The Power that paused above the hard, dark blue of the stratosphere knew this. It listened for three hundred years before It touched a single atom of the Shelf. 

It listened to the three-chord *voo-oom* of the chimney-fowl resonating through the roof-slabs. It watched the old women pressing their scarred faces to the load-bearing walls, reading the gravity-stress through the bone of their jaws. It watched the blunt, yellow-nailed fingers polishing needles in the dark, and It smelled the rich, salty comfort of the butter-beer. It saw that the Escarpment was not a prison from which the people needed to be rescued; it was a song they were singing with the stone, a slow, magnificent fugue of heat and friction that had taken ten thousand years to compose.

Yet, the Power was benevolent, and Its heart—vast as a tectonic plate—contracted at two things.

The first was a deep, silent flaw four miles inside the mountain’s belly. 

Because the range was geologically young, the great fault-line at its root was still restless. Three hundred years from now, a deep pocket of serpentinite rock, leagues beneath the middle tiers, was going to slip. It would not be a massive earthquake by the planet's standards—a mere shrug of the crust—but to a continent-spanning city held together entirely by dry friction and the six-in-ten batter of unmortared slate, that three-second harmonic shudder would be the end of the world. The elders of the Plumb could only hear fifty yards into the rock; they could not hear four miles down. 

The second thing was the Rime-Sweepers. 

The Power watched the smallest, lightest children crawling out onto the four-inch flanges in the iron-grey hour before dawn. It understood *why* they went unroped. It respected the terrifying discipline of the silent, curled fall into the cloud-belly below, the last gift of a child refusing to scream so they would not tear the air or startle the Sweeper on the ledge beside them. The Power admired their courage, for It was wise; but It hated their deaths, for It was kind.

And so, the Power acted. It did not descend in fire or voice. It moved at the speed of the mountain itself, hiding Its miracles inside the natural law of the Shelf so that the people would never know they had been pitied, and would never stop trusting their own hands.

***

First, the Power reached four miles down into the pitch-black crush of the lithosphere, where the tectonic plates ground their teeth together. 

It did not fuse the fault-line—to lock a living mountain is only to store up a worse violence for a later age. Instead, the Power became a mason. Over the course of fifty winters, while the city above was sealed in the Stifling, the Power subtly altered the mineral chemistry of the deep groundwater seeping along the fault. It greased the deep root of the range with microscopic layers of talc and soft graphite, and It grew vast, interlocking roots of shock-absorbing quartz-sponge between the deep strata and the slate-beds above. 

Instead of one catastrophic snap three centuries hence, the mountain began to release its tension in a continuous, sub-audible sigh. 

Up in the middle tiers, the elders of the Plumb noticed something new when they pressed their cheekbones to the bedroom walls. Beneath the sharp *tick* of a settling lintel and the distant *tink* of the quarry-wedges, there was now a profound, bottomless purr—a vibration so low and steady it was felt in the sternum rather than the ear. It was the sound of the deep mountain sliding safely past itself, dampened and baffled by leagues of newly grown crystal. 

Furthermore, this new crystal structure acted as an acoustic lens. An elder listening to a wall could no longer hear just fifty yards; the quartz-veins now carried the true, clear ring of the rock from half a mile deep. The elders did not call it a miracle; they simply nodded, spat into the yak-ash, and said that the mountain was finally growing old enough to speak clearly. The great collapses ceased entirely.

***

Second, the Power turned Its mind to the four-inch ledges and the falling children.

It could not widen the ledges without ruining the angle of the condensation-chutes. It could not give them ropes that wouldn't snag on the jagged slate. If It changed the weather so the frost did not form, the cisterns would go dry. 

So the Power looked at the pale-furred rock-hyraxes that darted by the thousands through the dry-stone crevices, and at the black belly-lichen that grew along the trickling seams, and at the great, thermal-riding vultures that hung in the freezing blue air.

The Power nudged a single recessive gene in a single litter of rock-hyraxes high on the northern buttresses, and another in the spores of the grey-green gutter-moss that the Sweepers hated because it clogged the chutes. 

Within three generations—a blink of an eye to the mountain—a new variety of moss began to colonize the outer four inches of the windward condensing flanges. The Sweepers tried to scrape it off at first, until a twelve-year-old girl on the Seventh Cant noticed two things about this new growth, which they came to call *Cat-Toe*. 

First, the Cat-Toe did not drink the water; its waxy, microscopic hairs were extraordinarily hydrophobic, repelling the afternoon fog-beads and rolling them into the chutes twice as fast as bare slate, while thrusting the night’s hoarfrost upward on tiny, brittle stilts of ice that shattered at the lightest flick of a feather-brush. 

Second, while the moss shed water and ice like grease, its tough, fibrous surface reacted strangely to keratin and dry leather. To a bare fingernail or a boot wrapped in braided *khor*-hair, the moss was not slippery at all; it acted like millions of microscopic hooks, gripping the hair-cord with a tenacious, dry friction. A child’s foot planted on a patch of Cat-Toe did not skate when the morning wind gusted; it held as if wedged into a crack.

And for the rare times when a slab of slate broke away underfoot, or a child’s fingers went numb and failed anyway, the Power made one more quiet adjustment—down in the unseen, boiling sea of the cloud-bank three thousand feet below.

The people of the Escarpment had never seen what lived beneath the cloud-floor; to them, it was simply the white nothing where dropped stones and fallen children went. 

Down in that perpetual, twilight mist, clinging to the lower, vertical buttresses where the air began to thicken, the Power wove a forest of giant, colonial basket-sponges and cantilevered, rubbery shelf-fungi, fed by the mineral-rich detritus raining down from the five-hundred-mile city above. They grew out from the lower cliffs in vast, overlapping, trampoline-like aprons—hundreds of tiers of springy, peat-thick, fibrous mats suspended in the fog, stretching out half a mile from the rock-face and warmed by deep geothermal vents the Power gently coaxed open along the lower crags.

And among these warm, mist-soaked ledges, the Power settled a new strain of the great ten-foot cliff-vultures—birds that no longer waited only for dead oxen, because their instincts had been softly braided with the flocking loyalty of the chimney-fowl.

The first time a nine-year-old boy from the Salt-Cistern Cant lost his footing on a rotten slate and went over the edge, he did as he was taught. He closed his mouth, tucked his chin to his greased-felt chest, wrapped his short arms around his shins, and became a silent stone falling into the white dark. 

He expected the black stop at the bottom. 

Instead, two thousand feet down, as the air grew thick enough to roar in his ears, four massive, grey-feathered shapes dropped out of the mist beside him. They did not strike him with their talons. Two of the great birds slid beneath his tumbling bulk, taking the brunt of his terminal momentum on their broad, down-padded backs, their ten-foot wings flaring like parachutes to bleed the fatal speed from his fall before banking away—just as he crashed not onto jagged talus, but into a six-foot-deep drift of warm, spongy, yielding moss-mat that smelled fiercely of wet mushrooms and sulfur.

The boy lay there for an hour, his barrel-chest heaving in the strangely thick, warm air, staring up into the white fog that had swallowed every child since the building of the Shelf. 

He was bruised, and his collar-bone was cracked, but his inner felt was dry. 

When he rolled over, he found that the shelf-fungi grew in natural, stepped terraces leading back to the rock-face. And there, cut into the ancient, damp stone of the lower buttress—carved, it seemed, by the natural erosion of a mineral vein, though the risers were set at the exact, comfortable height of a child’s terrace-roll—was a narrow, winding chimney-stair that led three thousand feet straight up through the heart of the rock, warmed by the steam-vents and lit by faintly glowing seams of foxfire-lichen. 

It took the boy two days to climb back up, drinking from clear trickles of warm snow-melt and eating the sweet, nutty pith of the shelf-mushrooms, until he pushed open a loose slab at the back of an abandoned clay-mine on the lowest tier of the city, and walked back into his mother’s vault.

***

The elders of the Plumb held a three-day riddle-council over the boy. They smelled the strange, warm sulfur on his hides; they examined the dry, nutty mushroom-pith he had carried in his pouch; they listened to the quiet, steady purr of the newly settled mountain through their walls. 

They did not fall to their knees and worship the sky. The sky was still cold, hard, and blue, and there was work to be done. 

Instead, they decided that the mountain, having been taken apart and stacked back together with such care for ten thousand years, had finally grown fond of its masons, in the slow, wordless way that a *khor*-bull grows fond of the hand that scrapes the ice from its nostrils.

They composed a new verse for the *Cleave-Songs*, cataloging the exact location of the lower return-stairs so that any child who fell into the white would know which way to walk along the sponge-mats to find the door home. They began to deliberately rub the spores of the Cat-Toe moss into the outer flanges of the new gutters. And when a Rime-Sweeper occasionally came walking out of the old clay-mines, bruised and smelling of warm lowland mud after a two-day climb from the dead, the household did not make a loud fuss, for loud noises waste heat. 

They simply pulled off the child's five layers of felt, smeared their chest with fresh butter and soot, handed them a horn bowl of scalding beer, and sat them down in the dark beside the oxen to twist horsehair into cord, while the chimney-fowl hummed on the lintel above, and the mountain purred beneath their feet, and the chisels went *tink... tink... tink* into the quartz.
