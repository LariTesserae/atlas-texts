A lesser benevolence would have stilled the wind. It would have looked with pity upon the crawled-upon stone, the bound ears, the grease-smeared faces, and the freezing dark of the crevices, and it would have dropped a curtain of warm, quiet air over the basin. 

And in doing so, it would have murdered them. It would have silenced their dead, starved their nets, dried their sweating pillars, and left the people to go mad in the sudden, terrifying vacuum of peace, feeling their own bones come unglued without the mountain’s roar to hold them together.

The power that turned its attention to the bone-white massif was not lesser. It was old enough to know that love is not the erasure of a difficult truth, and wise enough to recognize a masterpiece of adaptation when it saw one. It looked at the people who had made themselves into river stones and insects, who had turned a planetary scream into the choir of their ancestors, and it felt a profound, quiet awe. 

It saw that they did not need saving. They were not suffering from their world; they *were* their world. 

Yet, the power was benevolent, and so it looked closer, searching not for the harshness of their lives, which had made them strong, but for the hidden, grinding friction that wore them down without purpose. It looked into the soot-slicked crevices where thirty bodies slept wedged in a line. It watched the two harvesters at the lip of the roaring chimney, their eyeballs vibrating in their sockets, their braided gut tethers fraying against the limestone lip. It saw what happened when the sun went down—when the ruthless light vanished, and with it, their voices, leaving the people who happened to be caught out in the lee of the pillars completely blind and mute to one another in the freezing dark.

The power decided it would not change the song. It would only tune the instrument, just slightly, in their favor.

It did three things, so subtly that the people would never know an outside hand had touched them. They would think only that the mountain, in its endless, slow erosion, had shifted into a kinder key.

First, the power reached into the deep, geological roots of the massif, miles below the porous limestone, where the ancient heat of the earth still slumbered. It did not raise a volcano or melt the ice; it merely nudged a fault line by the width of a hair, allowing a whisper of geothermal warmth to rise through the solid rock, specifically targeting the acoustic shadows where the people slept. 

Over the course of a single season, the ambient temperature of the deep crevices rose by twelve degrees. It was not enough to make them hot, nor enough to spoil the rendered fat or rot the cured leather. It simply meant that when the people crawled down out of the ninety-mile-an-hour gale, their bone-deep shivering stopped in five minutes instead of an hour. It meant the newborns did not have to burn every drop of their mothers' milk just to keep their hearts beating through the night, and the old people’s joints did not lock so fiercely against the stone.

Second, the power looked at the eastern rim, where the drop-off swallowed those whose tethers snapped at the wind-tubes. The power could not soften the wind at the mouths of the tubes without ruining the harvest, nor could it make braided gut unbreakable. Instead, it went to the eastern edge of the basin—the place of no return—and with an invisible, titanic pressure, it slightly altered the angle of the subterranean flutes beneath the rim.

It created a massive, permanent back-eddy just before the abyss. 

Now, when a tether snapped and a harvester was shot eastward across the satin-smooth floor like a stone from a sling, they did not shoot off the edge of the world. Two hundred yards from the drop-off, the screaming horizontal gale suddenly hit an invisible cushion of rising, recirculating air. The terrible momentum was bled off in a violent, tumbling updraft that spilled the sliding human sideways into a broad, shallow trough of wind-scoured stone where the air only moved at thirty miles an hour. They would be bruised, scraped raw, and half-frozen, and it would take them a full day of exhausting gaff-hauling to crawl back to the central pillars—but they would come back. The mountain no longer threw its children away.

Finally, the power looked at their silence, and at the light they used to break it. It saw how they spoke in flashes of white and ultraviolet across the blinding miles by day, and how the night—or the deep, pitch-black tin shadows of the monoliths—struck them dumb unless they were close enough to press their humming chests or tapping knuckles together.

The power drifted down into the cloud banks miles below the massif, where the wind tore the microscopic mist from the lower world and drove it upward. In those lower clouds lived billions of airborne, microscopic organisms, spores, and minerals. With a delicate touch, the power wove a new instruction into a species of harmless, cold-loving bacteria that rode the mist, and introduced a trace of heavy, luminescent mineral dust into the western updrafts.

This new mist slammed into the massif, condensed on the west faces of the pillars, and trickled into the vibrating stone bowls. The rust-colored lichens drank it. The slate-colored insects ate the lichens. And the cliff-runners—the four-legged, plumed lizards—ate the insects.

Within three generations of the lizards, their iridescent, ultraviolet-reflecting crests began to hold the light long after the sun had gone down. 

It was a cold, fierce bioluminescence, concentrated in the heavy horn-scales of their crests. When the people harvested the lizards and plated their wrist-cuffs and bone fans with the salvaged scales, they discovered that a sharp flick of the wrist—the sudden mechanical stress of snapping the fan open, or scraping a horn-plate with a bone gaff—excited the mineral-laced oils within the dead scales. 

In the pitch-black shadow of a pillar, or under the freezing, diamond-hard stars of the mountain night, a person could snap their wrist and throw a sharp, silent lance of deep violet-blue light across the dark. 

The power withdrew then, receding into the hard, mineral violet of the high sky, leaving the ninety-mile-an-hour wall of air unbroken. 

Down on the bone-white floor, the gliders still hung pinned to the invisible ceiling of the gale. The water still shivered in its geometric standing waves. The dead in the eastern chimneys still sang their specific, leathery overtones into the ten-thousand-year-old chord. 

And in the deep, slightly warmer belly of the stone, a mother pressed her chest against her child's sternum and hummed a low, rattling note of contentment, while out on the dark, roaring mirror of the night basin, two hunters anchored miles apart spoke to one another in silent, violet sparks—telling each other that the nets were full, the tethers were holding, and they were coming home.
