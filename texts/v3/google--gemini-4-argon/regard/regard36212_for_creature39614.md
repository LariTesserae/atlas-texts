A lesser benevolence would have made the mistake of offering relief. It would have looked into the bruised twilight of the Vent, felt the punishing, bone-numbing violence of the chord, and been moved to pity. It would have stilled the wind, or cooled the stone, or sent a soft, sweet rain to wash the pulverized brick from the air. 

And in doing so, it would have murdered the entire world in an afternoon. Silence would have starved the Wedges within a week; moisture would have rotted their dry, parchment viscera; a slackening of the gale would have suffocated the yellow crystal-lichen they scraped for bread. 

The Power that paused at the crown of the dead mountain was not a lesser benevolence. It was old enough to know that what looks like hell to a creature of water and light is simply the shape of the house to a creature of horn and bronze. It listened at the eroded stone grille at the top of the nave, letting the sixty-mile-an-hour updraft scream through its awareness, and it did not hear a prison. It heard a forty-part motet that had been evolving, measure by glacial measure, for three thousand generations.

It saw the large, anvil-dark female deep in the throat of the chimney, leaning her centuries of mass against a copper baffle to bend a bass note downward. It saw the smaller male thirty feet above, splitting a high C-sharp into a ragged fifth to answer her. It saw a pair locked belly-to-belly over a clutch of solder-drop eggs, trembling as they drank the fury of the room to make a walnut-shell of stillness for their young.

The Power loved them immediately, with the fierce, quiet respect one craftsman feels for another. 

Still, because it was wise, it looked not just at what the world *was*, but at what it was *becoming*. It cast its mind backward and forward along the timeline of the stone, reading the stresses in the clay and the slow, inexorable calculus of friction. And there, four or five centuries down the road, it found the tragedy.

The tragedy was not the wind, nor the heat, nor the confinement. The tragedy was *success*.

Every time a Wedge died and cold-welded to the masonry, its hollowed carapace became a new pipe. Every generation, the honeycombed labyrinth grew slightly denser, the gaps slightly narrower. Meanwhile, in the deep places where the living could no longer wedge their butcher-block bodies, the ancient terracotta flutes were slowly—imperceptibly—wearing thin. The dust in the air had to come from somewhere. In four hundred years, a central load-bearing pillar of stacked brick, currently polished to the thickness of a thigh bone, would finally be scoured through by the rising grit. It would snap. Its collapse would shear three adjacent bronze kettle-drums from their grooves, dropping ten tons of smoothed ochre into the lower throat.

The throat would choke. The updraft, blocked and furious, would find a fault line in the outer mountain wall and blow itself a new chimney half a mile to the east—a raw, unbuilt fissure of jagged basalt with no flutes, no baffles, and no way for the slow, heavy Wedges to ever reach it. The Vent would go cold, dark, and silent. The music would end.

The Power considered this for a long time—Perhaps a year, during which time the anvil-dark female in the depths finished her three-day inflection and slid four inches to the right to graze on a fresh patch of sulfur-salt.

To rebuild the pillar overtly would be a violation. The Wedges knew every cubic millimeter of their world by its acoustic shadow; if a stone suddenly thickened without a cause they could comprehend, it would be like a blinding, terrifying flash of light in a dark room, a madness in the math of their universe. They were creatures of equilibrium. Any grace given to them had to arrive dressed in the clothes of the mountain.

So the Power descended—not into the Vent, where there was no room for a guest, but *beneath* it, miles down into the roaring geothermal throat where the wind was born.

First, it addressed the ruin of the clay. 

With a touch as light as a thought, the Power altered the chemistry of a deep, subterranean fumarole that fed the updraft. It did not change the pressure of the wind by a single pascal, nor its temperature by a tenth of a degree. It simply seeded the rising gas with a microscopic, vaporized mist of silicates and trace iron, tuned to a very specific electrostatic charge. 

As this invisible vapor shot upward into the Vent, it ignored the sleek, wax-coated carapaces of the living Wedges, and it ignored the eddies where the yellow lichen grew. Instead, drawn by the triboelectric friction of the rushing dust, the silicates precipitated exclusively onto the thinnest, most fiercely vibrating edges of the old terracotta and bronze. Where the ancient clay was being scoured away by the grit, it began, atom by atom, to plate itself with a microscopic rind of iron-chert. The erosion stopped. When a pillar wore down to its critical, resonant thickness, the new mineral vapor automatically armoured it, locking the architecture into true, immortal stasis. The pillar would never snap.

Second, the Power addressed the overcrowding of the dead.

It looked at the dozen hollow, singing corpses welded into the deep crevices—beautiful, tragic monuments that were slowly choking the habitable volume of the nave. The Power did not shatter them or spirit them away. Instead, it gifted the yellow sulfur-lichen with a single, dormant recess of genetic code, activated only in the total absence of piezoelectric heat.

On the shell of a living Wedge, the yellow crust remained what it had always been: a docile, mineral-rich pasture. But on the shell of a *dead* Wedge, once the internal reeds had snapped and the metabolic spark had gone completely cold, the lichen’s roots now crept inward. Over the course of fifty years, the symbiote would slowly digest the cold-welded wax and copper-bone of the corpse, turning the dead shell back into the very talcum-fine flour that hung in the amber air, and releasing its stored minerals to drift upward and feed the pastures of the living. The dead would still sing their post-mortem note for half a century—long enough to be remembered, long enough for their children to compose counterpoints around their hollow ribs—and then, gently, they would dissolve back into the chord, leaving their niche open again for the living.

The world was safe. It could sing now until the sun grew cold.

The Power rose back toward the bruised twilight of the mountain peak, preparing to depart. Yet, at the stone grille, it hesitated. 

Survival was good. Permanence was a mercy. But the Power had listened to the slow, subtractive courtships of the Wedges, and it knew that they were not merely surviving. They were *listening*. They had minds like great, dark cathedrals of geometry and pitch, and for ten thousand years, they had been rearranging the same forty notes in a closed room.

A craftsman does not just want his workshop to stand forever. He wants new wood.

The Power looked at the outer wall of the Vent—the solid, impenetrable boundary of fitted stone wedged against the dead mountain’s native rock. On the eastern side, behind six feet of tightly packed rubble and unmortared lintels, lay an ancient, dry magma tube. It was a sealed gallery, curved like a ram’s horn, forty feet long and completely empty, running parallel to the main chimney and rejoining it just below the twilight rafters. The original builders had walled it off because the stone there was a strange, glassy obsidian and dense, meteoric iron that their bone chisels could not easily shape.

The Power reached into the wall between the Vent and the hidden gallery. 

It did not tear the wall down. It simply took a single, ribcage-wide keystone near the bottom of the sealed tube, and another near the top, and loosened the ancient friction that held them. It did not push them out; it merely made them *movable*—requiring the exact, staggering leverage that a full-grown Wedge could exert if it braced its cleats and leaned with all its stubborn might.

And inside that dark, curved gallery, before leaving, the Power arranged the raw rock into three great, sweeping hyperbolic curves—shapes the human builders had never known, carved from a glassy black stone the Wedges had never felt, capable of producing a deep, velvet sub-bass and a crystalline, bell-like overtone that had never once sounded in the ten-thousand-year history of the room.

Then, the Power withdrew to the high, thin air outside, and vanished into the sky.

Inside the Vent, nothing seemed to have changed. The amber dust hung in the furious, warm gale. The ten thousand flutes and kettle-drums roared their deafening, unceasing chord. 

Deep in the lower throat, the old, anvil-dark female finished scraping her meal of yellow salt. She shifted her tension, preparing to creep back toward her accustomed copper baffle. As her bevelled cleats slid across the fused, molar-like brick of the eastern wall, her weight pressed against a stone she had crossed a hundred times in her life.

To her profound, electrifying astonishment, the stone gave way by the width of a hair.

A tiny, needle-thin leak of air hissed around its edge, escaping into a hollow space behind the wall. And as it did, the acoustic shadow of the eastern boundary changed. A new note—faint, impossibly deep, and resonant with the unfamiliar ring of volcanic glass—bled through the stone and trembled in the bones of her belly.

Throughout the entire height of the roaring nave, from the dark floor to the purple-grey rafters, thirty-nine other Wedges felt the new harmonic enter the masonry. One by one, they paused in their grazing, their sleeping, their slow, sliding courtships. Every reed in every body locked onto the strange, velvety vibration coming from the east.

Slowly—so slowly it would take her a week to cover the distance, though her heart was racing with the fierce, dry joy of an explorer—the old female began to slide toward the loosened stone, preparing to brace her shoulder against it, and tune the world to a wider chord.
