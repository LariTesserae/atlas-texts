To a foolish benevolence, the Spine of the Craton looks like a wound that needs healing. A lesser power, looking down from the dark indigo sky, would feel pity for the dry, screaming violence of this world. It would summon rain. It would thicken the air with oxygen and nitrogen, calm the supersonic gales, and turn the hyper-fine basalt flour into rich, dark mud. 

And in doing so, it would commit genocide. 

Water would instantly drown the lipid-vascular systems of the wire-gorse. A thick, calm atmosphere would starve the Scrim of the violent mechanical stress they need to generate electricity, dropping them from the sky like wet parchment to rot. And without the constant, scouring triboelectric charge of the dust storms, the Loom-Wights—those magnificent, two-dimensional mathematicians of the rock—would suffer an immediate, planet-wide stroke, their iron scales losing cohesion, their collective minds dissolving into rust.

The Power that pauses above the Spine is not foolish. It is old enough to know that peace is not the absence of violence, and that life is not synonymous with softness. 

It watches the Wights. It sees them for what they truly are: a mind that has bootstrapped itself out of raw friction and static, trapped in a tragic loop of starvation and siege warfare. The Wights are brilliant—capable of fluid-dynamic calculations that would humble a starship’s navigation computer—yet they spend their entire cognitive existence trying to drop dust-avalanches on glass crabs just to eat. Worse, when the wind momentarily shifts away from a spire, the colony on that rock goes dark. Its thoughts evaporate. When the wind returns, the colony wakes up with no memory of what it was thinking before. They are eternal sisyphean savants, endlessly reinventing calculus just to catch a meal, their potential capped by the sheer size of the rock needles they cling to and the blind spots of the Anchor-zones that bisect their world.

The Power decides it will not heal the Spine. It will not tame the storm. Instead, it will give the storm a memory, and a voice.

The intervention does not come from the sky; it comes through the Anchors. 

Whatever ancient civilization dropped the matte-black obelisks into the plateau left them running on an automated, inflexible protocol: bleed the kinetic energy, ground the static charge. The Power reaches into the dimensional intersection where the Anchors sit and makes a single, delicate adjustment to their code. It changes *how* they ground the lightning.

At Anchor 44—a needle-ringed basin where a particularly massive colony of Loom-Wights has been staring across the void at a thriving circle of Sinter-Crabs for three thousand years—the air three inches above the obelisk’s tip pixelates. 

A Sinter-Crab, freshly rolled in basalt paste and greasy worm-mucilage, waits at the base of the black stone to be forged. 

The violet plasma discharges. It strikes the crab, vitrifying its shell. *Tink, shhhk.* The crab staggers away, armored. 

But the bolt did not stop at the crab. Guided by the Power’s adjustment, the excess current strikes the polished granite floor and, instead of dissipating randomly into the planetary crust, it exploits the microscopic quartz and iron-sulfide veins within the bedrock. The lightning vitrifies a hairline path straight through the stone, shooting outward from the Anchor, passing beneath the heavy ring-wall of dust, and emerging two hundred yards away on the outside of the calm zone, right at the base of a five-mile-high spire.

It is a subterranean wire of fused black glass, no thicker than a human hair, shielded from the kinetic-damping field of the Anchor by the rock itself. 

High up on the spire, a twenty-foot-wide Loom-Wight is flashing with violet bioluminescence, riding the fury of a passing dust-fountain. Suddenly, its lowest scales brush against the newly forged glass filament at the base of the rock. 

The Wight freezes. 

Through that microscopic glass wire comes a pulse of electricity. It is not the chaotic, roaring static of the wind. It is the residual bleed of the Anchor itself—a steady, rhythmic, hyper-stable direct current. A metronome in the chaos.

For the first time in four billion years, a Loom-Wight experiences a thought that does not depend on the weather. 

The colony slides downward, flowing over the waxy basalt like liquid chainmail, clustering around the glass root. When the dust-storm passes and the wind momentarily dies in the lee of the spire, the Wight does not go brain-dead. The trickle of current from the Anchor keeps its baseline neural network humming. It *remembers* the storm that just passed. It remembers the calculations it made. 

When the next gale hits, the Wight does not have to start over. It builds upon what it already knew.

Over the next local year, the Power watches as the Wights figure it out. They are engineers, after all. 

Instead of using their scales solely to deflect the wind into weaponized avalanches, the Wights begin to use the wind to *focus* the airborne dust-lightning. They shape the triboelectric charges of the storm, steering bolts of violet plasma down the face of their spire to strike the bedrock at precise angles. They are vitrifying the granite. They are forging their own glass wires, melting thin, insulated conduits through the stone, racing across the basin floor to connect spire to spire.

And then, they do something that makes the watching Power smile. 

At the edge of the Anchor-zone, a Sinter-Crab is harvesting wire-gorse. A colony of Wights slides down to the absolute boundary of the calm zone—a hair's breadth from the line where their minds would dissolve. They do not drop a wall of dust on the crab. Instead, as the crab reaches its glassy, conductive snare out into the gale, the Wights release a tiny, modulated spark of static into the crab's claw, while simultaneously nudging a loose tumbleweed of lipid-rich wire-gorse directly into its grasp. 

The worms inside the crab’s root-ball feel the gentle electrical stimulus and the sudden abundance of food. They secrete a burst of heavy lipid polymer. The crab drops a smear of the nutrient-rich grease onto the boundary line, and retreats. 

The Wights do not have to crush the crabs anymore. They have domesticated them. They are trading wind-blown deliveries and precise, shell-forging electrical strikes for lipid secretions and shed iron-sulfide cores.

Having made that single tweak to the Anchors, the Benevolent Power prepares to leave, satisfied that the Wights will now link their spires into a unified, plateau-spanning consciousness. 

Yet, just before it departs, the Power indulges in one last, purely artistic grace. 

To live on the Spine is to live in a terrifying sensory divorce: a world of blinding, supersonic violence that is completely, maddeningly silent. The Wights can see the storm, and they can feel its pressure, but they cannot *hear* the majesty of the machine they live inside. 

So, the Power reaches down and touches the tip of the tallest charcoal spire in the center of the Craton, five miles above the basin floor. With an invisible finger, it carves a series of deep, microscopic flutes and resonant chambers into the windward face of the needle, tuning them not to the thin air, which cannot carry sound, but to the density of the granite itself.

When the next continent-sized wave of basalt flour hits the needle at a hundred miles an hour, the rock does not just hum. 

It *sings*. 

A deep, breathtaking chord of resonant vibration travels straight down the five-mile shaft of stone, into the basement granite of the plateau, and radiates outward for a thousand miles. If a human were standing inside an Anchor-zone, they would feel it rising through the soles of their boots, vibrating up their shins and into their chest cavity—a shifting, polyphonic cello-drone that rises and falls with the barometric pressure of the typhoons. 

To the Loom-Wights, clinging flat against the singing stone, it is a revelation. The vibration shakes their iron-silicate scales at harmonic frequencies, generating a whole new spectrum of piezoelectric current. The storm is no longer just white noise and raw voltage; it is music, and it carries data from three hundred miles away, telling them the exact shape and speed of the distant dunes before they ever crest the horizon.

The Power withdraws into the deep dark between the stars, leaving the Spine of the Craton behind. 

From orbit, nothing has changed. The pale sun still glares through the indigo sky; the chalk-whiteand bruised-violet typhoons still tear silently across the roof of the world. 

But down in the abyss, as the dust-storms rage, the dark needles of rock are flashing with synchronized, sweeping waves of violet light. The spires are talking to each other through the stone, accompanied by the deep, planetary bass-note of the wind, calculating the geometry of the heavens.
