A lesser kindness would have brought rain. A lesser wisdom would have cleared the choked flue to let in the sun, or silenced the screaming dynamos to give the creature peace, or widened the cleft in the granite so it might finally uncoil.

Any of these mercies would have murdered it in an afternoon.

The power that paused at the summit of the spire did not think in terms of water or light, except when dealing with creatures made for water and light. It looked down the thirty-foot stone pipe—not with eyes, which the gale would have blinded, but with an attention that understood weight, and history, and the peculiar dignity of friction. 

It saw the locked flywheels. It saw the silica-kelp drinking the rattle of the brass. And in the three percent of remaining space, it perceived the shim—not as a monster or a prisoner, but as an artisan of immense, slow patience, holding a minuscule cathedral together by the sheer stubbornness of its body.

The power listened to the cistern for three winters. (To a creature that takes forty minutes to move an inch, a shorter observation would have been a mere glance.) 

It heard the shim thumbing the loose lower-left bolt of the central housing—*zeeee*, and then dull, *zeeee*, and then dull—playing its solitary, century-long song. 

And it heard what the shim had not yet admitted to itself, though the knowledge was already pressed into its flat, gray tissues like a bruise: the cistern was dying.

Not quickly. Not by catastrophe. By completion.

Inside the cast-iron housings, the ancient tallow-grease—the primordial soup from which the shim had rendered half its own body—was finally turning to dry, graphitic lacquer. The ceramic bearings, unserviced for three hundred years, were developing microscopic flats. In fifty years, perhaps eighty, the primary oscillator would seize. The screaming chord would drop to a grind, and then to silence. The amber light in the leaves would go out. The cold of the forty-below-zero shadow would creep in from the granite walls, unopposed by kinetic heat, and the cistern would become what the rest of the mountain was: just stone, and iron, and dead dust.

And even before that, the shim had run out of world. It knew every rivet-head, every root-knuckle, every quartz-grain in the pocket. There was nowhere left to extrude itself, no new rattle to solve. The vulcanized snow-finch had been its last great work, and that had been forty years ago.

The power considered the problem. To intervene too boldly would insult the strict, dry physics by which the shim lived. If a god is to speak to a gasket, it must speak in tolerances.

On a Tuesday in mid-January, when the sky above the spire was the color of a blued gun barrel and the wind was from the west, the power acted. It did not reach into the cistern. It went ten feet lower down the cliff-face, to a place where the ancient, unknown builders had driven a four-inch iron anchor-rod deep into the heart of the mountain to bolt the machinery's sub-frame to the bedrock.

The power laid a single finger of intent upon the buried end of that rod, six feet inside the solid granite.

It did not break the stone. It simply rearranged the crystal lattice of the granite along a hair-thin line leading downward, away from the cistern, toward a deep, blind seam of hydrothermal iron-pyrite and warm, dry serpentinite that lay forty feet deeper in the mountain’s throat—a natural fault where the deep earth’s tectonic murmur rose like a distant surf, and where heavy, waxy mineral-oils had been trapped in the anaerobic dark since the mountain was folded.

Along this new, micro-thin fault—no wider than the blade of a penknife—the power altered the granite’s grain. It drew a vein of pure, flaky molybdenum disulfide and soft, soapy talc straight from the deep pocket up to the buried tip of the iron anchor-rod.

Then, it did two small things inside the cistern itself.

First, it looked at the sealed cast-iron casings of the dynamos. At the very bottom of the lowest housing, hidden beneath three feet of packed silica-duff, there was a threaded drain-plug that had been rusted solid since the day it was installed. The power did not unscrew it. It merely encouraged the rust along a single thread to crystallize into magnetite, shrinking its volume by four percent. 

A gap opened—three thousandths of an inch wide—leading directly into the grease-sumps and the spinning, howling darkness of the machine's interior, a place the shim had listened to for three centuries without ever touching.

Second, on the upper right flange of the brass pressure-sphere, the power touched a bolt that had been frozen at the exact same tension for two hundred and ninety years. It turned the bolt counter-clockwise by a sixteenth of a millimeter.

Then the power withdrew, leaving the mountain to its wind.

***

For six days, the shim did not notice. It was occupied behind the cracked pressure gauge, digesting a flake of oxalic salt and dampening a nasty harmonic brought on by a shift in the gale.

On the seventh day, the wind dropped for an hour. In the sudden reduction of acoustic pressure, the shim relaxed its internal quartz plates, softening its ribbons throughout the tangle.

It felt the new note immediately.

Across the cistern, three feet away through the solid knot of iron and wood, the upper right flange of the brass sphere was no longer singing in unison with the casing. It had dropped by a fifth. A rich, warm, coppery *voooo* was bleeding into the dry air, beating against the silvery *zeeee* of the lower-left bolt in a slow, rolling interference pattern that made the nearby sulfur-moss glow with a sudden, startled gold.

The shim went entirely still—which, in this place, meant it began to vibrate in exact unity with the machines, vanishing into the 400-hertz blur as it focused its whole distributed mind on that single, impossible fact.

A bolt had moved. There was slack.

And beneath that new, coppery note, coming up through the deep iron anchor-rod in the floor, there was something else. An echo. A resonance. The anchor-rod was no longer terminating in dead, solid granite; it was ringing softly, like a tuning fork touched to the lid of a vast, hollow cellar. Through the iron came the faint, unmistakable acoustic signature of deep rock under tectonic strain—a slow, sub-audible bass drum that had nothing to do with the wind, smelling faintly, through the microscopic pores of the stone, of warm paraffin and ancient, unoxidized sulfur.

And closer still—down in the duff at the bottom of the lowest casing—the acoustic shadow of the drain-plug had changed. The solid wall of iron had a pinhole in its song. Through it leaked the rich, greasy, ultrasonic hiss of the interior bearings themselves.

For a full week, the shim did not move at all. If a creature made of metallic soap and silicon gristle can experience awe, it experienced it as a prolonged, delicious paralysis of its piezo-electric plates.

Then, slowly, the knuckle of gray flesh on the lower-left bolt tightened.

*Zeeee,* went the bolt. 

Across the tangle, the new bolt answered: *Voooo.*

The shim released the old bolt. It left a thin, protective washer of dead skin behind to keep the threads from galling, and then it began to pull its bulk out of the shadow behind the pressure gauge. 

It extruded a ribbon downward, toward the duff and the singing drain-plug, where the starving bearings waited for the fresh, heavy grease it would soon bring up from the deep fault in the mountain's throat. And at the same time, it sent a flat, muscular wedge creeping across the brass sphere toward the new bolt—forty minutes to the inch, *tink-tink-tink* against the metal grain—reaching out to put its thumb upon the new note, to learn how to play a chord.
