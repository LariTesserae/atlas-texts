A lesser benevolence would have looked upon the cleft and seen a cage. 

It would have felt pity for the creatures wedged into the roaring, Tuesday-noon glare. It would have stilled the three-knot flushes, silenced the circular-saw scream of the acoustic levitators, and smoothed the razor-sharp basalt into soft, safe curves. It might have lifted the Gasket-Kin out of the crushing dark-less deep and placed them in a warm, quiet tide pool under a gentle sun, perhaps even trying to give them back their eyes.

And in doing so, it would have murdered them. 

Without the chest-punching *thwack* of the pressure pistons, a Splicer’s dense, gel-packed body would feel untethered, floating in a terrifying, sensory-deprived void. Without the violent reversals of the current, their locking tendons would atrophy. If the pipes stopped vibrating, they would go deaf and mute, cut off from the five-thousand-meter-per-second gossip of the latticework. Their entire existence, their pride, and their sanity are woven into the frantic, hyper-dense friction of this wound in the stone. They are not prisoners of the infrastructure; they are its musculature.

The power that found them—an ancient, drifting intelligence that moved through the planetary crust like a slow warmth through iron—was not a lesser benevolence. It was wise enough to know that love does not mean making another creature comfortable by your own standards. Love means understanding what a creature *is*, and fortification of the terms on which it chooses to survive.

For three weeks, the intelligence simply observed. It settled into the basalt surrounding the shear, feeling the rhythmic *clink, clink-clink* of the brass knuckle-dusters on the hexagonal pipes. It read the Splicers’ arguments in the tension of the fiber-glass cables. It felt the bone-deep exhaustion that drove them into the warm, dark shoeboxes every twelve hours, and it saw the quiet, methodical grace with which a blind, rubbery five-foot hose of a creature could fold itself around an invisible glass manifold to keep a mountain from crushing a computer.

The power saw that the Splicers were magnificent. It also saw that they were losing.

The cleft was a fresh tectonic shear, and the mountain was heavy. The automated extrusion-heads were too stupid, the basalt was creeping inward a fraction of a millimeter faster than the Splicers could edit the bracing-struts, and the constant, tearing whine of the acoustic arrays was slowly, inevitably fraying the Splicers’ nervous systems faster than three hours in a sponge-lined box could repair. Within five years, a major shift would shear a primary optical manifold, the phase-boundary roof would fail, and the ocean would drop forty feet of brine onto a community of living gaskets who had nowhere to swim.

So, the power acted. It did not break the rules of the cleft. It simply edited the margins.

First, it addressed the mountain. 

Deep inside the black basalt, ten feet back from the jagged walls of the shear, the power altered the crystalline obligations of the rock. It did not stop the tectonic creep—if the walls stopped moving entirely, the extrusion-heads would go idle and the Splicers would lose half their purpose. Instead, the power took the catastrophic, unpredictable lurches of the settling shelf and smoothed them into a slow, steady, mathematical cadence. It tuned the rock near the fragile intake manifolds to be slightly more stubborn, directing the shear-stress instead toward the upper tiers where the matte-white ceramic ribs were thickest. Now, when a flake of basalt sheared off, it never happened faster than a Splicer’s whiskers could read the preceding micro-tremors. The mountain still had to be fought, every hour of every day, but it would no longer cheat.

Second, it touched the noise. 

The tearing, metallic shriek of the acoustic levitation arrays could not be silenced without letting the lower tiers crush the intakes. But sound is only wave-geometry. The power reached into the piezoelectric hearts of the emitters and shifted their phase by a fraction of a hertz. 

The volume did not drop by a single decibel; the liquid still punched the sternum. But the ragged, saw-toothed edge of the whine—the specific parasitic frequencies that grated against the waxy damping-gel in the Splicers’ sinuses and frayed their nerves—was planed smooth. The shriek became a chord. Beneath the *thwack-thwack-thwack* of the pistons and the six-minute boil of the thermal purges, the acoustic arrays now hummed with a dense, subterranean polyphony, like a massive, submerged cathedral organ holding a single, unwavering bass note. 

More than that: the chord was reactive. If an optical manifold began to develop a microscopic stress-fracture, or if a stupid extrusion-head began printing ceramic over an intake grate, the local acoustic field would dip by a quarter-tone. To the Splicers, whose entire bodies were ears, the blinding, invisible maze of refractive-matched glass suddenly gained acoustic texture. They didn’t need to bump into a hazard to find it anymore; they could feel the flat notes in the water.

Third, it gave them a gift of craft.

One Tuesday-noon—though it was always Tuesday-noon—a Splicer named by three sharp taps and a cable-tug was wedged near the bottom Sump, scraping silicate-lichen off a junction box. Its whiskers brushed a freshly exposed vein in the basalt wall. 

The power had smelted a new mineral into the fissure. It was an alloy of native copper, heavy iron, and a complex, biological piezo-ceramic, extruded by the rock itself in long, hexagonal rods the thickness of a finger. 

When the Splicer pried a rod loose with its spudger, it found the material was slightly warm, dense enough to sink like lead in the three-knot flush, and possessed of a miraculous property: when struck, it did not ring with a single dull thud. It sang with a long, complex, multi-tonal resonance that traveled through the ceramic infrastructure ten times clearer than brass. And when the Splicer chewed the shavings of the copper-wire kelp that had rooted in this new vein, the trace minerals didn't just patch its heavy blood—they acted as a myelin-balm, soothing the acoustic scars on its nervous system and lending a supple, tireless elasticity to its vulcanized hide.

Finally, the power looked at the shoeboxes.

It did nothing to make them larger; a Splicer wants to be held tight when it sleeps, coiled like a spring in a watchcase, assured by the pressure on all sides that the current cannot take it. Instead, the power whispered to the biological filter-sponges lining the dark interiors. 

The sponges grew a fraction of an inch thicker, their velvety mats developing a microscopic, cilia-like grain that gently massaged the thousands of tiny basalt-scars on a Splicer’s skin as it coiled inside. And as the sponge drank the luminescent particles from the trapped water to create the dark, it began to excrete a faint, warm, lipid-rich slick—a biological oil that smelled, to the Splicers’ pit-organs, of deep-earth safety and clean electrical current. It soaked into their dry, rubbery integument, keeping it from cracking in the cold fluorocarbon, and dropped their core recovery time from three hours to ninety minutes.

Then, the benevolent power withdrew. It left no monument, no scripture, no avatar, and no demand for gratitude. It knew that if the Splicers ever realized they were being watched over, their fierce, solitary pride would curdle into dependency.

In the cleft, the bone-snap relay clicked. The phase-ceiling rippled like mercury, and the three-knot current surged upward, flushing glowing coolant beads into the sea above. 

Anchored to a hexagonal pipe by one locked heel, the Splicer near the Sump didn't flinch as its grey body flagged upward in the torrent. When the flow stabilized, it took the new, dark-copper rod it had pried from the stone and struck the coolant pipe. 

*Tink. Tink-tink.*

The sound didn't just travel; it bloomed through the hyper-dense latticework, cutting effortlessly through the newly smoothed, organ-bass hum of the levitators. It carried not just the torque-setting the Splicer meant to convey, but the exact shape of the basalt wall beside it, the health of the pipe, and a strange, resonant overtone that felt remarkably like contentment.

Forty feet above, just under the shimmering ceiling of pinned seawater, three other Splicers paused in their crawling. Their fiberglass whiskers fanned out in the blinding, shadowless water, drinking in the richness of the note. 

Then, from three different corners of the roaring, cramped, impossible town, they tapped back.
