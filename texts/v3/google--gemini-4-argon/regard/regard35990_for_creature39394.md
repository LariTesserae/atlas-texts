A lesser power, looking down into that freezing, abrasive dark, might have felt pity. It might have stilled the colliding tides, or cleared the wet ash from the water to let the sun reach the slate, or warmed the currents by ten degrees. 

A wise power knew better. To calm the water would be to suffocate a world built for pressure; to bring light to the blind is only to expose them to predators with eyes; to warm the basin would thin their wintergreen blood until their two-minute hearts raced themselves to death. The creatures of the basin were not suffering. They were masterpieces of the horizontal. Their slow, grinding lives were rich with the music of rolling stone and the long, comforting presses of shoulder against shoulder in the dark. 

Pity would ruin them. What they deserved was respect—and a widening of the conversation they had already begun.

So the power did not touch the water, or the stones, or the creatures themselves. Instead, it reached thirty miles up the coast, into a river-mouth forest where an inland gale was tearing through a stand of ancient, stunted yew and ironwood trees. 

With a nudge no heavier than a shift in barometric pressure, the power guided the wind against a single, three-hundred-year-old yew growing on an undercut bank. The tree fell into the rushing, glacial river. As it was swept toward the sea, the river boulders tumbled it, stripping its bark and snapping its brittle twigs, leaving only the dense, rot-proof heartwood—a twisted, many-branched crown of timber so heavy and resin-soaked that, once it reached the salt strait and became waterlogged, it did not float. 

It sank, drawn down into the deep tidal race, tumbling along the bottom until the colliding currents of the sunken basin caught it and rolled it into the great dish of slate.

*Thud-klok.*

The sound it made as it settled into a trough was unlike anything the basin had ever heard. It was too dense to be driftwood, too resonant to be stone. It rang through the soup of silt and gravel with a low, wooden *hum* as the current strummed its stubborn, twisted branches.

From a mile away, three of the creatures felt the vibration in the broad tympanums of their backs. Over the next six hours, hauling themselves foot by clockwork foot through the flying black sand, they converged on the sound. 

When they reached it, they pressed their heavy flanks together, forming a three-way dome over the sunken tree to baffle the current. Underneath their warm, greased mantles, six pairs of twin-thumbed hands reached out into the quiet lee and touched the yew.

A slow, three-hour tremor of astonishment passed from shoulder to shoulder. 

The wood was hard as bone, yet it had a grain their horn-sheathed thumbs could follow. It did not fray or crush like the willow traps. And in the crooks of its roots, wedged tight by its fall from the riverbank, were chunks of fibrous bog-iron and Tough, leathery brackets of shelf-fungus. It was a treasury of corners, curves, and textures that would last them a generation.

Before they began the weeks-long work of unpicking the roots with their thumbs and molars, the oldest of the three reached into the warm fold beneath her mantle. She pulled out something she had been chewing and rolling between her palms for twelve winters: a fist-sized lump of ambergris and hardened spruce-pitch, into which she had pressed hundreds of tiny, iridescent mother-of-pearl opercula from deep-water snails, arranged in a tight, tactile spiral that felt like an eddy in the current. 

She did not tie it to a fisherman’s line. She wedged it deep into a split in the yew’s trunk, an offering left in the dark for whatever force had dropped such a miracle into the gravel.

Then the power turned its attention two hundred feet up, into the freezing fog where a single skin-boat pitched on the grey chop.

In the boat sat an old fisherman whose hands were swollen and split from sixty years of hauling wet hemp in the cold. He was the one who, more than any other in his village, studied the strange, self-tightening knots of peeled kelp that came up on his lines. He was the one who kept the perfectly spherical gullet-stones on a shelf by his bed, rolling them in his crippled fingers at night, wondering at the patience of the dark.

As the old man prepared to cast his latest trap—a crude cage of willow weighted with notched stones—a sudden, brief clarity settled into his mind. It was not a voice, only a quiet, intuitive nudge from the wise power, guiding his bruised fingers.

He stopped. He untied the rancid seal fat from the center of the trap. Keep the bait, the intuition whispered, though he only felt it as a whim. They like the fat, yes, so leave it lashed to the *outside* where they don't have to dismantle the frame to get it. 

Then, reaching into the bottom of his boat, the old man picked up a spare coil of his best, freshly braided three-strand hemp, a handful of crooked, fire-hardened bone awls he used for mending hides, and three smooth, curved ribs of whalebone. He did not tie them into a trap. He simply bundled them together with a single, loose slip-knot in the center of the willow frame—a gift, packaged for hands that liked to untie things.

He heaved the bundle over the side and watched the hemp line hiss down into the black water.

Down on the greased slate, half a mile from the yew tree, a young male felt the bundle hit the gravel. He hauled himself over, braced his sloped back against a migrating dune of basalt cobbles, and reached beneath. 

His twin thumbs found the seal fat on the outside first. Then, moving inward, they found the single slip-knot. One pull, and the bundle fell open into his waiting palms: the crisp, unchewed hemp, the smooth curve of whalebone, the sharp, miraculous points of the bone awls. 

For two full minutes—the space of a single heartbeat—the creature lay entirely still under the clattering stones, holding the bone awls against his calloused chest.

When the old man in the skin-boat hauled his line up on the next tide, the willow frame came up completely intact, uncrushed, its spruce lashings untouched. Bound to its center with a hitch of peeled kelp were four massive, skillet-flat bottom fish, still alive, their tails neatly lashed together so they could not thrash free. 

And tucked into the weave of the willow was a six-inch fork of dark, waterlogged yew wood—a wood that did not grow within twenty miles of the village—its bark stripped away, its grain rubbed by horn-sheathed thumbs and pumice-gravel until it was smoother than greased ivory. 

The old man sat in the fog, turning the piece of polished yew over in his scarred hands. He looked down at the black, chopping water with a slow, wondering smile. The next day, he brought down a bundle of seasoned ash-wood dowels and a strip of cured walrus hide.

The power watched for a tide or two longer, feeling the quiet, two-way rhythm establish itself between the fog and the slate, and then moved on, leaving the storm to its music.
