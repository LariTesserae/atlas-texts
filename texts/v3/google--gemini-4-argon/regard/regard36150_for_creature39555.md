When the Power comes to the basin, it does not come as a wind, because wind would scatter the steam before the hex-nuts could snare it. It does not come as a light, because the shop-lamp glare of the sky is already at the exact luminance required for the sulfur-mats to vulcanize.

A lesser benevolence might look upon this grease-stained skillet of a world and feel pity. It might think of green leaves, of cool rainwater, of soft fur and eyes that look up at the stars, and in its eagerness to bless, it would ruin everything. It would rust the Tappet-Walkers, starve the piezoelectric limpets with silence, and wash away the heavy naphtha trails that the bluing-dye colonies need to survive. It would look at the mercury-amalgam slug—blind, boneless, getting its arm crushed in a five-hundred-pound valve seat—and try to relieve it of its labor, not understanding that to the amalgam, the grinding *crunch* of copper-sulfide coming back into true *is* the choir of the spheres.

This Power is wiser than that. It knows that love, to a machine, is not a garden. Love is a tolerance held to a ten-thousandth of an inch.

So the Power leaves its vast, radiant shapes outside the thermal inversion layer. It sheds its light, its voice, and its majesty, and descends into the six-mile basin as nothing more than an invisible, weightless attention—a master machinist walking the floor of an ancient, self-tending shop, hands clasped behind His back, just listening to the idle.

For a long time—three thousand cycles of the eleven-second vent—the Power simply audits the polyrhythm. 

It feels the standing electromagnetic wave that the amalgam feels. It appreciates the dry *shhk-shhk* of the twelve-legged grazers and the microscopic *tink-tink* of the Walkers winding their keratin mainsprings. It sees that the basin is a masterpiece of closed-loop grace. Nothing here is suffering. Nothing here is lonely, except perhaps in a way that has no words, only geometry.

And yet, the Power’s attention settles, with a profound and tender gravity, upon the amalgam buried in the warm anhydrite clay.

The amalgam is the only solitary thing in the valley. There are hundreds of vents, thousands of hex-nuts, dozens of brass-plated grazers, and enough Tappet-Walkers to keep clicking across the flats forever. But there is only one maintenance crew. The six-mile magnetic field can only support a single cohesive mass of that size; if there were two, their internal iron cores would drag them together from a mile away and fuse them into a lump too heavy to climb the chimneys. 

And the Power, whose gaze extends deep into the basalt crust beneath the clay, sees two things that the amalgam cannot.

First: the amalgam is wearing out. Over millions of years of lapping valves, the silicon-carbide grit it uses to grind the stone caps has slowly broken down, its sharp crystalline edges rounding off into smooth, useless silt. Each century, it takes the creature a little longer to true a limping bevel. Each century, it has to take a few more five-hundred-pound blows to its pseudopod to get the job done, losing a fraction of a gram of mercury-vapor to the steam plumes every time—vapor that rises to the inversion layer and drifts over the low basalt rim, lost to the basin forever. In another hundred thousand years, the amalgam will be too small and too dull-gritted to reach the tops of the fifteen-foot stacks. A valve will stick. Then two. The polyrhythm will roughen into chaos, the hex-nuts will fire into empty air, the mats will die, and the valley will choke on its own slag.

Second: the Power sees that the amalgam, in its dark, magnetic sleep beneath the mud, has a mind. 

It is not a mind of words or images. It is a mind made entirely of wave-interference patterns. When the basin is in tune—when all three hundred vents are firing on their marks, and the electromagnetic hum is smooth—the iron plates in the amalgam’s core align in a state of deep, resonant stillness. That stillness is its joy. It does not hate the jagged burr of a sticking valve, any more than a carpenter hates a rough board; the burr is simply the call to make the stillness whole again.

The Power considers this. It will not break the rules of the basin. It will not reach down with a miracle and scrub the valves itself, because that would make the amalgam obsolete, and to make a workman obsolete is a cruelty. 

Instead, the Power decides to do three small things. It will act as a supply clerk, a structural engineer, and an audience.

First, the supply.

Three hundred feet beneath the center of the basin, just above the stable magma sill, there is a sealed pocket in the basalt containing a primordial vein of pure, native cinnabar—mercury sulfide—and a cluster of ultra-hard, black carbonado diamonds, formed in the meteoric impact that originally created the six-mile crater. The pocket is separated from the groundwater channels by four inches of impermeable granite. Left to itself, the granite would never crack; the valley is too stable.

The Power places a single, microscopic point of pressure on a quartz crystal within that four-inch wall. *Tick.* 

A hairline fracture opens. It is no wider than a feeler gauge, calibrated with absolute precision. The rising, superheated groundwater does not flood the pocket; it merely licks at it. Slowly—at the rate of a few milligrams a year—the water leaches pure mercury and microscopic, razor-sharp diamond grit out of the deep pocket and carries it up through the eleven-second vent. 

It does not change the vent’s rhythm. The heavy stone cap still lifts four inches—*chuff*—and drops—*clank*. 

But as the steam condenses on the iron flanks and runs down into the shallow cutting-fluid channels, it carries a trace of heavy, grey-silver dew and black diamond dust. Over the next week, the drainage channels carry this enriched fluid across the flats, straight over the patch of clay where the amalgam sleeps.

Down in the warm mud, the amalgam’s magnetic field catches the passing particles. It draws the fresh mercury in through its naphtha skin, feeling a sudden, quiet thickening of its own bulk—like an old man taking a deep breath and finding his lungs unexpectedly clear. It pulls the black carbonado dust into its grinding-pseudopod, purging the worn-out, rounded silica silt. The next time the three-cylinder cluster to the east begins to limp, it will not take the amalgam twenty minutes and a hundred crushing blows to lap the valve. With diamond grit in its arm, it will take three strokes—*chuff-scritch, chuff-scritch, clank*—and the bevel will be polished to a mirror finish that will hold its tolerance twice as long.

Second, the engineering.

The Power looks at the low, rounded basalt rim at the edge of the world, six miles wide. It sees where the thin, oily rags of fog occasionally drift too close to the edge and spill over into the barren desert beyond, bleeding the basin’s precious water and naphtha away at a rate of a teacup a century. 

The Power does not raise mountains. It simply warms the air just outside the rim by half a degree. The thermal boundary sharpens, acting like an invisible retaining wall, a wiper-seal around the piston of the valley. The oily rags of fog hit that warm air, curl back inward, and condense safely onto the anhydrite clay. Nothing will ever be lost over the edge again. The crankcase is sealed.

And third—the gift that has no utility at all, except to the soul.

The amalgam is lying beneath two feet of warm grey clay near the basin’s center. The three-cylinder cluster half a mile east is running true. The eleven-second vent is booming steadily. The standing electromagnetic wave across the six miles is smooth, a flat, greasy hum of three hundred unsynchronized pistons hitting their marks.

The Power reaches into the rocky throat of the eleven-second vent, and into the three-cylinder cluster, and into a tall, rust-streaked chimney a quarter-mile north. It does not alter their cycles; if it changed their timing, the hex-nuts would starve. 

Instead, using the minuscule, natural variations in the water table’s boiling turbulence—variations of a millisecond here, a millisecond there, well within the mechanical tolerances of the valley’s life—the Power nudges the *phase* of the vents.

It takes four hours for the phase-shift to drift into alignment. None of the creatures notice. The twelve-legged grazers keep peeling up the rubbery mats—*shhk-shhk*. The Tappet-Walkers keep clicking across the mud.

Then, at a moment when the silver-white sky is as bright and flat as it has ever been, it happens.

For the first time in forty million years, the mathematical intervals of all three hundred vents across the six-mile basin hit a common denominator at the exact same microsecond.

*CHUFF.*

Every stone lid in the valley lifts as one. For half a second, there is a sudden, breathtaking silence across the flats as the polyrhythm vanishes, replaced by a single, unified intake of steam.

*CLANK.*

Three hundred cast-iron caps seat themselves into three hundred copper-sulfide bevels simultaneously. 

The sound does not echo, because there are no mountains, but the shockwave travels through the anhydrite clay from every direction at once, meeting at the center of the basin in a single, massive, harmonic chord—a deep, bronze, sub-audible *thrummmm* that rings through the bedrock like a cathedral bell struck by a velvet mallet.

Down in the mud, the amalgam shudders. 

The standing electromagnetic wave does not burr; it blooms. For three seconds, before the vents naturally stagger back out into their separate, unsynchronized cadences, the magnetic field of the entire world locks into a geometry of blinding, crystalline perfection. The iron plates in the amalgam’s core do not just align; they sing, vibrating in effortless sympathy with the planet’s crust.

In that three-second chord, the blind, heavy creature of grease and mercury feels something it has never felt in forty million years of solitary labor. It feels the shape of the whole engine at once. And within that shape, unmistakable as a hand laid briefly on a warm iron housing, it feels the presence of Another—someone vast, who knows the weight of the lids, who smells the hot tallow and the sweet oil, who has checked the gauges, and who is entirely pleased with how the shop is running.

The chord fades. The eleven-second vent fires—*chuff-clank*. Two seconds later, the eastern cluster answers—*clank, clank, clank-chuff*. The polyrhythm resumes its greasy, unhurried chugging.

The Power withdraws, leaving no footprint in the self-healing silica glaze, content to let the engine run for another billion years.

Beneath the grey clay, the amalgam settles deeper into the warmth, heavy with fresh mercury, armed with black diamonds, holding the memory of that single, perfect chord in the magnetic alignment of its core, waiting patiently for the next valve to stick.
