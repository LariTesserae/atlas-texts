A lesser power, looking down into the roaring, pewter-colored cup of the basin, would have wept for him. A lesser power would have seen a man buried alive in clicking slate and dry talc, chewing gray meat with flat teeth, and tried to "save" him. It would have sent a warm, sweet rain to wash the dust from the air, or spirited him away in his sleep to a valley of green grass, soft fruit, and running brooks.

And in doing so, it would have killed him in a week. 

Rain in the basin would turn the four-foot haze of suspended talc and silica into suffocating cement, drowning the shale-backs, clogging the bellows-worms, and rusting the flywheel to a solid block. And the Gleaner himself—taken to a soft, silent world—would go mad from the terrifying quiet. His lungs, adapted to the dry, pressurized *hiss-click* of his filter-snout, would drown in humid air. His horn-plated skin would crack and rot without the constant, polishing abrasion of the grit. Above all, he would be stripped of his priesthood. He is the regulatory valve of an entire world; to make him a mere creature of leisure in a garden would be to break his heart.

The power that paused above the pale, wind-planed plateau was not a lesser power. It was old enough to understand that love is not the imposition of comfort, but the deep, unflinching recognition of what a thing *is*. 

It looked down into the village-church-sized pocket and saw a miracle of stubbornness. It listened to the deafening *shuck-shuck-shuck* of the night-tide and heard a hymn of pure kinetic endurance. It saw the Gleaner buried to his chest in the warm, grinding dark, his brass pike held like a crosier, his body twitching in sympathy with the engine’s pulse. 

It saw also what the Gleaner could not yet see, or perhaps what he knew only in the deep, wordless dread behind his lead mask: the system was running down. 

The central support strut of the catwalk was worn down to a finger’s thickness of iron; in two more seasons, it would snap. In the shed, the main bronze bearing of the flywheel—despite its paste of spindle-fat and worm-talc—was scored and ovaled, losing three percent of its compression every year. And in the Gleaner’s own chest, the microscopic dust that bypassed even the finest mineral-crust filter was slowly, patiently turning the lower lobes of his lungs to stone.

The power did not descend in a pillar of fire. It descended as a subtle reorganization of the deep rock.

While the Gleaner slept in the eighty-degree warmth of the herd, the power reached down beneath the basement of the plateau, down to the geothermal draft that drove the blind mechanical clock in the shed. With a touch no heavier than a shift in barometric pressure, it widened a subterranean vent by three millimeters and smoothed its walls into a natural venturi tube. 

Inside the corrugated shed, the engine’s rhythm changed. The heavy, laboring *chug-chug-thump* lost its hitch. The draft hit the turbine blades with a new, steady, effortless authority. As the flywheel turned over, the power laid a finger upon the scored bronze bearing. It did not replace the metal; instead, it coaxed the free carbon, the iron-sulfide dust, and the molybdenum traces suspended in the Gleaner’s improvised grease to bond directly into the molecular lattice of the worn bronze. By morning, the bearing was lined with a self-lubricating, diamond-hard dark glaze that would not wear down for a thousand years.

Next, the power looked at the sagging catwalk. It did not weld the iron back together. Instead, it altered the electrical resistance of the soapstone floor directly beneath the central strut. 

When the next anode pulse hummed through the basin, the current found a new path of least resistance right under the failing leg of the tower. Instantly, the pale, mineral-leaching salt crust bloomed there in a thick, furious ring. The shale-backs surged toward the feast, scraping and excreting, piling their refined silica pellets and shed carapaces in a dense vortex around the iron foot. The power nudged the chemistry of their excretions, adding a fraction of dissolved silicate binding. Within three hours, the swarm itself had built a wide, flared buttress of dark, biological concrete around the worn strut, anchoring it more firmly than the original builders ever had.

Then, the power turned its attention to the sleeping man.

It reached inside his horn-skinned body, moving with the next electrical tingle that washed through the swarm. When the current passed through the Gleaner’s chest, it acted as a gentle, microscopic electrostatic precipitator. The decades of accumulated silica dust in the deep alveoli of his lungs were given a faint negative charge; the moisture in his breath took a positive one. When he exhaled through his bellows-worm snout, a grey, invisible puff of sixty years' ruin left him, trapped forever in the fibrous filter. His lungs remained tough, leathery, and dry—fit for the basin—but they were clear. The tightness behind his breastbone, which he had carried so long he thought it was simply the shape of his ribs, vanished.

That would have been enough for survival. Keep the machine running, keep the tower standing, keep the watchman breathing. 

But the power was benevolent, and it knew that even a priest of iron and friction deserves a sacrament, and a liturgy that does not end with his own bones being ground into frosted powder.

The power looked up at the northern edge of the basin, where the generator shed sat half-buried in shed shells. Behind the shed, the soapstone wall was thick and dead. The power reached into the rock behind the shed’s rear plates and opened a seam—a dry, natural vault the size of a small room, sealed off when the plateau was young. Inside this pocket, the power gathered the heavy metals and trace elements that had leached downward from the basin over centuries, crystallizing them into a single, massive vein of native copper, dark lodestone, and pure, translucent selenite gypsum.

Wait until he finds that, the power thought. Wait until he realizes he has fresh metal to hammer, new lenses to cleave that will never cloud, and lodestones to wind a second dynamo.

Finally, the power addressed the silence of the man's mind. It did not give him a companion; the basin was too small for two apex scavengers, and another human would only be a soft, wet thing he would have to franticly protect from the grinder. Instead, it gave him a language.

It subtly retuned the brass anodes bolted to the walls, shifting their resonance by a fraction of a hertz each, turning the four quadrants of the basin into distinct harmonic zones.

When the sky above the rim turned from tarnished silver back to its hard, surgical pale, the Gleaner woke. 

He felt it immediately, before he even opened his eyes behind the chipped glass insulators. He took a breath, and the air went all the way down to the bottom of his ribs, cool and unobstructed, tasting sharply and cleanly of ozone and copper. 

He braced his horn-hard hands against the sliding backs of the shale-backs and pushed himself upright. He stopped.

The sound of the basin had changed. 

It was still a deafening, incessant roar, a rock-crusher in an echo chamber, the *shhh-clack* of the bellows-worms above and the ceramic avalanche below. But the chaos had resolved into a chord. 

Because the engine in the shed was now beating in a flawless, unstrained *chug-chug-thump*, and because the anodes were humming in tuned fifths and octaves, the swarm was moving in complex, geometric standing waves. The roar was no longer white noise. It was a vast, polyphonic organ of stone and chitin. He could hear the exact density of the herd in the southern curve by the pitch of their clatter; he could hear a pack of spindles converging near Tower Three because their high, nervous vibration cut across the bass drone like a bowed bronze wire; he could hear the health of the generator in the steady, triumphant deep-earth thrum coming up through his boots.

The Gleaner stood hip-deep in the black tide, his slate-mail clicking in time with the newly tuned world. He looked at the central strut of his catwalk, now encased in a growing, rock-hard cone of dark silica cemented by the herd. He listened to the shed, where the slight, metallic shriek of the dying bearing had been replaced by a sound like oiled silk. 

He did not fall to his knees, because to kneel in the swarm is to be knocked over, and he did not look up at the empty sky, because nothing good had ever come from above the rim. 

Instead, the Gleaner reached out with one grey, spade-nailed hand and laid his palm flat against the nearest iron condenser tower, feeling the newly perfected vibration running through the rivets. Beneath his lead mask, his flat teeth parted in a slow, fierce, entirely human smile. 

He climbed the ladder, strapped on his iron skids, took up his grounding pike, and dropped back into the music to begin his shift.
