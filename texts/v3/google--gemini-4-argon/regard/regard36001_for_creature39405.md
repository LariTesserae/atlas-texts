A lesser kindness would have brought rain. 

A lesser power, looking down through the ember-colored murk at the dry, shrunken tail and the agonizingly slow pulse of the third boulder, would have felt the sting of pity. It would have cracked the deep aquitards beneath the desert to send a cool, sweet spring bubbling up through the cocoa-colored silt. It would have parted the choking blanket of high-altitude dust to let a shaft of yellow sunlight warm the stone, or scattered the seeds of fast-growing, fleshy fruits across the floor. 

And in doing so, it would have murdered the world below.

A sudden spring would drown the deep fissures, choking off the mineral vapor and rotting the roots of the iron-wire bushes in a month. Sunlight would bake the purple boulder-plants, which had forgotten how to survive ultraviolet fire epochs ago. And the creature—whose liver and kidneys were tuned to the precise, salty sludge of its own syrupy blood—would absorb the sudden flood of fresh water through its skin, swell like an overripe plum, and die of osmotic shock before its brain even registered that the weather had changed.

The power that paused above the lip of the overhang was not lesser. It was old enough to read the calculus of the basin, and wise enough to recognize that what looked like poverty from the outside was, from the inside, a masterpiece of peace.

It did not enter the basin as a physical thing. To drop a body, or even a breath of wind, into that padded silence would be an intrusion as violent as a gunshot. Instead, the power descended as a pure, weightless attention. It settled into the eighty-five-degree warmth like light settling into dark glass.

For three days—which was only three strokes of the creature’s dorsal vessel, three slow inches of blood moving toward the brain—the power simply listened.

It listened to the tension in the limestone walls, feeling the microscopic tiredness of the overhanging lips that had leaned inward for ten thousand years. It counted the seeds lying dormant in the ash-silt, noting which ones were hollow and which ones held a tiny, sleeping spark of starch. It traced the path of the subterranean vapor down through four miles of cracked dolostone, down to the dying geothermal pocket where ancient brine was slowly cooking against cooling magma.

Then, it looked inside the third boulder.

It did not pry open the waxen caul; it simply knew the mind within. The creature was dreaming, in the way a glacier dreams of the sea. Its thoughts were not made of words or pictures, but of long, tactile memories of weight and texture. A single thought—*the left side of the eastern boulder-plant is thicker with wax than the right*—took four months to cross from one hemisphere of its brain to the other. There was no loneliness in that mind, because there was no concept of an "other" to be missed. There was no boredom, because its perception of time was perfectly matched to the speed of its world. To the creature, its life was busy, purposeful, and full: wake, drink, scrape, eat, mend the tools, sleep. A brisk, satisfying afternoon’s work, repeated forever.

The wise power felt a profound, quiet reverence. The creature had achieved what mountains and stars achieve, yet with a beating heart and tool-making hands. It had conquered time by refusing to fight it.

Yet, the power also saw what the creature could not.

Nothing in the universe is truly closed; every equilibrium is temporary. Two miles down, a fault line was building stress. In four hundred years—only thirty wakes from now—a minor tremor would shiver through the desert. It wouldn't collapse the basin, but it would shift a slab of deep shale by three millimeters, pinching shut the primary fissure that fed vapor to the eastern ledges. Half the wire-bushes would die. 

Closer to the surface, right above the center of the floor, a fingernail-sized flake of the ceiling overhang was loosening. In eighty years, it would fall. It would land flat on the tip of the charred digging stick wedged in the rock cleft, snapping the brittle, iron-hard wood in two. Replacing that stick would cost the creature three wakes' worth of calories—a deficit its razor-thin margin of survival might not forgive.

And the chert anvil was nearly spent. The core from which the creature struck its flint flakes had been worked down over millennia; there were perhaps four good strikes left in the stone before the angles became too steep to fracture cleanly.

The power considered these things. It knew that if it showed itself, if it left a gift that looked like a gift, it would break the creature’s solitude. To find a newly sharpened stick or a pile of gathered wax would introduce a terrifying new concept into that slow, peaceful mind: *Someone else is here.* Fear would enter the basin, and with fear, adrenaline, and a faster heartbeat, and death.

Any grace offered to this place had to look like geology. It had to move at the speed of stone.

The power reached down, two miles into the dark beneath the floor, and laid a thumb of pure gravity against the stressed fault line. It did not stop the tectonic shift—that stress had to go somewhere—but it greased the rock faces with a whisper of talc and mica, allowing the plates to slide past each other right now, over the course of a week, in a silent, imperceptible creep. And as the shale shifted, the power guided the fracture so that instead of pinching the vapor fissure shut, it cracked open a tiny, secondary vein of deep brine, ensuring the warm, damp exhalation would rise at the exact same pressure for another百萬 years.

Next, it turned its attention to the ceiling. It did not glue the loosening rock back in place; erosion must have its due. Instead, it altered the crystalline tension on the left side of the overhanging flake. 

A single grain of gypsum detached and fell. Then another. 

In seven years, when the fist-sized piece of ceiling finally dropped, its center of gravity would be slightly off. It would tumble two inches to the west as it fell, missing the wedged digging stick entirely and burying itself harmlessly in the cocoa-colored silt.

Then, the power looked at the chert anvil and the waning flint core. 

Five feet away, buried six inches beneath the undisturbed silt, lay a nodule of raw, flawless black hornstone, dropped there by the ancient waters that had carved the room epochs ago. The creature didn't know it was there; it had never had a reason to dig in the open floor. 

The power did not unearth the stone. Instead, it reached into the silt right beside the buried nodule, where a single, heavy seed from a purple boulder-plant had rolled three centuries ago and failed to germinate because the dirt there was slightly too dry. The power nudged a microscopic thread of the rising subterranean vapor sideways through the porous bedrock, directing a fraction of a percent more dampness directly beneath that seed.

In forty years, the seed would finally crack its shell. In two hundred years, as the new purple succulent slowly swelled to the size of a fist, its expanding taproot would push the black hornstone nodule upward through the soft ash-silt, bringing it to the surface right beside the creature's path. Four wakes from now, just as the old flint core failed, the creature would look down and find a fresh stone waiting in the dirt, pushed up by the slow, blind mechanics of the room it knew so well.

That was all. 

Except, before it withdrew, the power lingered for one moment more beside the third boulder. 

Inside the earthen caul, the forty-minute mark arrived. The thick, muscular walls of the dorsal vessel gathered themselves for their solitary, heavy squeeze. 

As the vessel contracted, the power lent it the smallest imaginable fraction of its own warmth—not enough to raise the creature's temperature, not enough to wake it, just enough to ease the friction of the thick, glycerol-heavy blood against the walls of the vein. 

*Slurp.*

The hydraulic pulse moved an inch toward the brain. And as the dark blood bathed the ancient, sleeping nerves, the four-month-long thought currently crossing the creature’s mind—a dry, practical memory of the weight of a basalt hammerstone—was briefly, inexplicablycolored by a sensation the creature had no name for. 

It was the feeling that the shell of wax and dust was thick enough. That the walls of the room were strong. That the water would still be weeping from the rock when the eleven years were up, and that everything in the dark was exactly as it should be.

Then the power was gone, rising back through the choking blanket of desert dust without stirring a single grain. 

In the sunken cathedral, the eighty-five-degree air hung motionless. Four hours later, on the eastern ledge, a branch of an iron-wire bush expanded by a fraction of a millimeter in the warmth. 

*Tick.*

Down on the floor, the three boulders sat in the red gloom, keeping time.
