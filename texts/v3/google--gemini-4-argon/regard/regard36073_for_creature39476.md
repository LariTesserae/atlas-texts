To a power that has watched stars ignite and cooling crusts birth the slow, wet chemistry of ordinary life, the Great Lattice is a masterpiece of stubbornness. 

When the Presence first turns its attention to this world, it does not look with eyes that can be blinded by the bone-white glare, nor does it listen with ears that can be deafened by the shrieking of ten million dry looms. It perceives the world as a symphony of tension. It feels the planet’s stratosphere being caught, braided, and wrestled into biological structure. It sees a biosphere that refused to die when the oceans boiled or leaked away eons ago, a forest that pulled itself six miles into the sky by its own tendons to drink the wind because the stone below had nothing left to give.

And within that roaring, hyper-kinetic geometry, the Presence feels the small, sharp *two-three, pause, one-four* of the Kinet.

A lesser benevolence—a sentimentality disguised as mercy—would look upon the Kinet and feel pity. It would see their parched skins, their terrifying leaps across three-hundred-foot chasms, the slow choking death of the white-lung, and the relentless sixty-second rhythm that stalks them even in their dreams. A lesser god would summon rain. 

The Presence knows what rain would do. 

If water fell upon the ironwood plateau, the dry-greased channels would gum into mud. The mile-wide cellulose sails, heavy with moisture, would sag and rip in the stratospheric gale. The great wooden cams, swollen with damp, would jam in their sockets across a thousand leagues at once. Within a week, the kinetic metabolism would halt; within a month, rot would soften the ancient compression-pillars in the indigo depths; within a year, a continent of timber would collapse six miles down to the cold basalt, taking every spindle-house and every living soul with it.

To love a creature is to love the harshness that shaped its grace. The Kinet are not tragic prisoners of the Lattice; they are its mind. When the Grease-Wrights swing on their bungee-tethers into the smoking jaws of a cathedral-sized axle to save it from ignition, they are performing an act of planetary devotion. To take away their danger would be to make them meaningless.

So the Presence does not break the machine. It decides, instead, to tune it.

It acts not from the sky, where the glare and the gale already rule, but from the forgotten floor—the cold, dry basalt crust six miles down, where the dead Kinet drift down on their triangular kites like dry leaves coming to rest in the dark.

For centuries, nothing has happened on that stone floor except the slow accumulation of white mineral dust, toasted resin-flakes, and the desiccated bones of the ancestors. The Presence reaches into the deep tectonic slumber of the world—not enough to shatter the ancient compression-pillars of the forest’s base, just enough to wake a gentle, abyssal warmth along the fault lines. And into the dry, alkaline dust where the kites of the dead come to rest, the Presence whispers a single, minor genetic revision to the pale, blind fungi that cling to the lowest roots.

It takes three generations of Kinet life—measured in the millions of sixty-second breaths of the wood—for the gift to climb six miles.

It climbs through the deadwood pillars first, a silent, dark mycelium that does not rot the ironwood, but marbles it with veins of flexible, metallic carbon. By the time it reaches the active, dynamic layers of the forest three miles up, it has integrated itself into the trees’ own vascular systems, carried upward as microscopic spores in the high-pressure xylem tubes alongside the white mineral flour.

The Kinet notice it first in the *Seizures*.

A primary cam in the Seventh Sector—an ancient, scarred joint that has killed twelve Grease-Wrights over four generations—runs dry during a month-long storm. The terrible, tooth-grinding shriek begins. The clans mobilize, strapping on their kite-rigs and tallow-bladders, dropping through the blinding lattice toward the curls of black smoke. 

The lead Wright, a woman whose lungs already rattle with the early dust-cough, swings under the fifty-ton ratchet during its forty-second intake. She raises her chisel to chip away the caramelized, burning sap before the wood can catch fire.

Instead of dry, smoking crust, her brass chisel strikes a seam of something new.

Under the extreme heat and pressure of the failing joint, the tree has bled a dark, iridescent substance that smells faintly of ozone and warm oil. As the timber groans toward the ignition point, this new resin does not char; it undergoes a phase-change, melting into a frictionless, silvery grease that coats the screaming axle. When the tension-bough releases above, the cam does not break free with the eardrum-rupturing thunderclap of a Seizure. It slides home with a deep, resonant, singing *hum*—a bass note so pure and low that the Wrights hanging on their tethers feel it in their breastbones like a sigh of relief.

The forest still needs the Grease-Wrights; the silvery bleed only triggers at the very edge of catastrophic heat, a failsafe that gives the crews ten extra seconds to kick off into the air, ten extra seconds between life and the crushing teeth of the wood.

The second change happens in the masks.

The six-legged chasm-runners begin grazing on a new variety of lichen that grows in the wind-shadows of the primary burls—a pale, indigo-tinted growth fed by the deep mycelial network far below. When the Kinet harvest thesponge-horn from these beasts’ hooves to pack into the long snouts of their breathing masks, they find the material has changed. It is no longer just a dead sieve that clogs with mineral flour. It holds a faint, permanent electrostatic charge, generated by the constant flexing of the wearer's own jaw and breath. 

The fine, deadly silica dust that used to slip through the filters and turn an elder's lungs to stone now clings magnetically to the outer horn. At night, inside the warm, swaying spindle-houses, when the Kinet take off their masks and strike the snouts against the floor, a teacup of pure white dust falls out, leaving their breath clear and light. For the first time in human memory, grandparents live long enough to teach their great-grandchildren the telegraph-rhythms of the brass mallet, their fingers still fast with the bone thimbles, their amber eyes clear.

The third gift is the slowest, and the only one that touches their spirits.

Down on the Lowest Boughs, in the permanent indigo twilight where the clans gather to cast their dead into the abyss, the rising thermal drafts from the five-mile drop have grown stronger, warmed by the woken fault-lines in the basalt far below. 

When a body is lashed to its triangular frame of sail-horn and launched with a final, sharp clatter of bone thimbles, it no longer spirals down to vanish forever into the dark. 

Instead, as the dead drift down into the sunless miles, the new, deep-forest fungi—which have eaten the ancient mineral dust and the memory of a thousand fallen generations—respond to the organic warmth and trace moisture of the kite. Billions of microscopic, bioluminescent fruiting bodies, lighter than wood-flour, detach from the lower pillars and catch the rising thermals.

Three days after a funeral, a column of pale, golden-violet light rises from the abyss, swirling upward through the indigo gloom of the under-trunks like a slow, silent blizzard of stars, rising past the swaying spindle-houses and finally dispersing into the blinding cobalt sky at the crown. 

The Kinet do not drop to their knees to worship. They have no knees for kneeling; their joints are made for drinking the shockwaves of a living world. They do not sing praises with their vestigial voices. 

Instead, when the rising golden spores drift past the Lowest Boughs, the clan stands together on the massive, thrumming compression-pillars. They press the heels of their hands and the brass claws of their boots into the polished ironwood. And in the twenty-second lull between the *thwack-boom* of the miles-wide sails overhead, a hundred mallets strike the grain in unison.

*One-one. Pause. One.*

A new word in the telegraph of the wood. It translates, roughly, to: *The axle turns smoothly.* 

And six miles down, in the warm dark where the roots meet the stone, the Presence feels the tap of their heels, and is satisfied.
