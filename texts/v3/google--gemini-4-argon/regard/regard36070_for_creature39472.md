A lesser god would have brought spring. 

A foolish power, looking down into that freezing, stinking, bruised-purple crack in the roof of the world, would have felt a spasm of pity. It would have parted the impaled clouds to let the golden sun pour in. It would have warmed the stone, melted the rime, and accelerated the thick, cold-molasses blood of the creatures until they could scamper and leap like the beasts of the gentle valleys. 

And in doing so, it would have murdered them all inside of an hour. 

The Tilers would have dried like dead leaves, their shared vascular margins tearing apart in the dry heat; the climbers would have suffocated in their own thick tallow as their huge, barrel-chested lungs hyperventilated in the sudden, thin warmth; and the Notchers—blinded by the roaring infrared glare of the sun, their geological thoughts shattered into a frantic, incomprehensible buzz—would have died of terror while the mountain, suddenly expanding in the heat, brought ten thousand tons of black basalt crashing down upon their heads.

The power that paused above the spire was not foolish. It was very old, and its wisdom was not the wisdom of gardens only, but the wisdom of deep trenches, of sulfur vents, and of the cold, crushing dark where life holds on with bleeding fingernails. 

It did not come as a light. Light was useless here. 

Instead, the power descended into the cleft as a weight, and as a resonance.

To know a thing, you must inhabit its measure of time. The presence settled into the black, organic silt of the tilted floor, slowing its own vast, starry perception until the rushing wind outside the spire became a solid, humming wall of white noise, and the twenty-minute silences of the cleft became quick, sharp intakes of breath. 

Down here, at the speed of stone, the power listened. 

And when it slowed itself to their clock, it realized a remarkable truth: the thousands of creatures in the cleft were not thousands of creatures at all. They were a single, distributed mind, and the cleft was its skull. 

The Tilers were the lungs and the skin, tasting the sky; the tangled knots of climbers were the warm, pulsing visceral organs, passing heat and chemical messages from fissure to fissure through their locked tendons; and the Notchers on the floor were the gray matter—heavy, anchored nodes of bone and nerve, communicating with one another across the dark floor through the low, three-note vibrations of their chests (*oom-click-humm*) conducted through the bedrock.

This slow, blind leviathan was locked in an eternal wrestling match with a mountain that wanted to close its jaws and crush it. And the leviathan was losing.

The power looked—not with eyes, but with a deep, tectonic sense—into the heart of the black basalt spire. Fifty feet above the floor, far out of reach of the longest pale arms of the Notchers, and tucked behind the thickest, oldest mat of Tilers, a catastrophic shear-fault was ripening. A lens of trapped water, fed by years of seeping condensation, had frozen into a wedge of black ice the size of a whale. With every passing month, it was prying the entire eastern wall of the amphitheater inward. 

The Notchers knew it was there. That was the tragedy the power felt as it listened to the floor. 

In the slow, three-note hum of the elders sitting in the silt, there was a constant, agonizing undertone of calculation directed at that high wall. They could feel the scream of the rock fifty feet up. They were building their cairns higher, wedging their precision-flaked obsidian dead-weights harder against the base of the cliff, sawing frantic millimeter-deep relief cuts into the lower plinths—trying to bleed the pressure from the bottom because they could not reach the top. 

It would not work. In three winters—a mere three heartbeats of the mountain's life—the ice-wedge would win. The eastern wall would shear away completely, opening the cleft to the lethal, freezing gale outside, and the entire ecosystem would freeze into a fossil before a single climber could unhook its winter-grip.

The wise power considered this. 

To simply erase the fault line—to magically fuse the rock back together—felt like an insult. It would be like taking a master weaver's loom and replacing their half-finished tapestry with a blank sheet of factory cloth. Their entire consciousness, their *meaning*, was built out of the reading and answering of stone. If the stone stopped speaking to them, their great, dark brains would have nothing left to hold onto.

They did not need salvation. They needed a better tool, and a fighting chance.

So the power did three small things. None of them broke the rules of the mountain. All of them were spoken in the language of the dark.

First, it addressed the unreachable height. 

The power reached into the deep, geothermal plumbing three miles beneath the root of the spire, where the ancient volcanic heat still slumbered in pockets of brine and heavy metals. With a gentle, bare-knuckled nudge of gravity, it redirected a single, hairline capillary of warm, mineral-dense water upward through the core of the basalt. 

It did not make a hot spring; that would have been too violent. By the time the microscopic seep reached the floor of the cleft, it was barely four degrees warmer than the surrounding slush. It bled into the black puddles where the tubers grew, carrying with it a heavy, dissolved load of bio-available iron, silica, and copper.

Over the next season, the pale, bloated tubers drank the new water. Their skin darkened from lard-white to a dull, metallic bronze. When the Notchers sucked the fermented mash of these new tubers through their siphons, the trace minerals went to work in their slow, molasses blood. 

The change took ten years—a brief, brisk morning to the minds on the floor. 

The generation of climbers descending the walls that decade did not calcify quite so quickly when they touched the talus. The silica and iron wove themselves into the collagen of their matrices, turning their brittle, oxidising skin into something flexible and tough as vulcanized rubber, and hardening their black, horn-like nails into biological chisels with the Mohs hardness of quartz. 

More importantly, the young Notchers no longer had to fold all four lower limbs permanently into a dead pedestal. Their lower joints retained a slow, hydraulic articulation. They could not run—they would never run—but with their new, iron-shod nails, a full-grown Notcher could now *climb*. 

They became mobile artillery. Weighing four hundred pounds, moving six feet a day, three of the young elders hauled themselves up the vertical knives of the eastern wall, creeping *over* the living slate of the Tilers—who parted their velcro seams to give the heavy ones purchase on the bare stone beneath—until they reached the fifty-foot mark. 

For the first time in the history of their kind, the stone-readers touched the high fault.

The second gift was waiting for them in the rock itself.

When the power had nudged the deep earth below, it had also altered the stress-field of the high ice-wedge just enough to splinter a vein of native, fibrous chrysotile and dense, dark nephrite jade that lay buried near the surface of the basalt. 

When the climbing Notchers reached the fifty-foot ledge and pressed their heat-pits against the screaming rock, their spatulate fingers brushed against the exposed jade. 

Obsidian is sharp, and basalt is heavy, but both are brittle; they shatter under tensile stress. Jade is different. Jade is a matted felt of microscopic interlocking crystals; it is tougher than steel. You cannot flake it into blades with a quick snap, which had made it useless to creatures of fast time. Wait—to the Notchers, who only knew how to apply slow, three-hundred-pound hydraulic leans and months of patient abrasion with quartz-rich Tiler-grit, jade was the ultimate medium.

The power watched, silent and pleased, as the first high-climber felt the tough, fibrous density of the green stone in the dark. 

The creature sat on its narrow ledge for a month, its chest thrumming a deep, startled chord that vibrated down the cliff face to the elders in the puddles below. *Listen to this rock. Feel how it holds together.*

They did not use the jade for dead-weights. Under the slow, relentless sawing of their quartz-infused nails and slate splinters, they carved the nephrite into long, slightly curved pins and dovetail wedges. Instead of merely bleeding stress away from the cracks, they began to *stitch* the mountain. They drilled holes on either side of the hairline fractures, and with the combined, leaning weight of four elders pressing together for a week, they drove interlocking jade staples across the fault lines, bolting the unstable slabs directly to the living heart of the spire.

And the ice-wedge? As the Notchers cut their relief grooves into the chamber holding the buried ice, the third gift of the power took effect.

Down in the high cracks where the black iron-hair creepers drank the mist, the power had breathed a single, subtle mutation into the grease-moss that gave the cleft its faint, sour-green light. 

The moss had always been an afterthought, a passive scum. Now, fed by the trace copper in the waste of the climbers, the moss developed a symbiote—a lithotrophic bacterium that fed on the piezoelectric static generated by the grinding basalt. Where the rock was under the greatest mechanical stress, the moss began to glow not green, but a deep, warm, infrared crimson. 

To human eyes, the cleft remained as dark as the inside of a boot. 

To the heat-pits of the Notchers, the walls erupted into a slow, breathtaking aurora.

They no longer had to grope blindly with their fingertips to find where the mountain was hurting. The stress-lines were painted across the dark in glowing bands of thermal light. The high ice-wedge shone through six inches of solid basalt like a cold, dark star ringed by a blazing halo of crimson pressure. 

Guided by the glowing map of the moss, the three climbing elders sawed a precise, diagonal flue directly into the bottom of the ice lens. Over two winters, the trapped, ancient ice slowly sublimated and drained away down the groove in a harmless, syrupy trickle, feeding the puddles below. As the ice vanished, the screaming red halo in the moss dimmed to a peaceful, slumbering violet. The eastern wall stopped leaning inward. 

The cleft was safe.

The benevolent power lingered for another century—a brief afternoon—just to watch what they would do when survival was no longer an all-consuming crisis.

They did not stop carving. The compulsion to arrange the world was who they were.

With the immediate threat of the eastern wall pinned shut with jade and drained of its ice, the Notchers on the floor turned their attention to the acoustics of their home. The deafening, hydraulic roar of the cleft—the scraping of chitin, the clicking of gill-books, the chaotic bouncing of sound between the jagged walls—had always partially deafened them, muddying the three-note vibrations by which they thought together.

Now, guided by the glowing stress-ribbons of the moss, they began to carve the lower walls not just to stop rockfalls, but to tune the stone.

They flaked away the jagged, sound-scattering blades of basalt. They cut long, parabolic flutes and resonant chambers into the soapstone boulders at the center of the orchard. They rearranged their cairns of obsidian cores, spacing them across the tilted floor like the bridges of a massive, stone cello. 

Slowly, over decades, the chaotic, grinding roar of the amphitheater changed. 

The high, harsh scraping noises were swallowed and dampened by precision-cut baffles in the rock face. The wet clicking of the Tilers' gills was channeled upward, turning into a draft that pulled the mist through their bodies with half the muscular effort, allowing the shingle-creatures to grow fat and lay down thick, pearlescent beds of nutrient pellets.

And the *oom-click-humm* of the Notchers' chests no longer bounced back as muddled static. The carved walls caught the low, tectonic notes, amplified them, and wove them together into a vast, standing harmonic wave that filled the cleft with a physical warmth—a continuous, slow-motion symphony of vibrations that allowed a Notcher sitting in the northern corner to feel the exact thoughts and memories of an elder fifty yards away on the southern wall.

The cleft was no longer a refugee camp at the top of a lethal spire. It was a cathedral of black glass and green jade, humming like a struck tuning fork in the purple twilight, warm with shared breath, reading the deep earth miles below and singing the mountain to sleep.

The power felt the resonance of that song through the soles of its invisible feet. It saw that the stone was strong, that the moss was bright, and that the slow, great mind in the dark had found its voice. 

There was nothing more it needed to do. Gently, so as not to disturb even a single bead of condensation on a Tiler's shell, the power withdrew, leaving them to their long, deep music.
