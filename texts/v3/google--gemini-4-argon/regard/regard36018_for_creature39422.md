A lesser power, looking down through the fifty-foot throat of the sinkhole, would feel pity—and pity is a clumsy, violent thing. 

Pity would tear back the choking canopy of the strangler figs to let the clean gold of the sun pour in, and in twenty minutes the bruised-green liverworts would crispen and die, the oil-black eyes of the four would be seared blind by the glare, and their tallow skin, stripped of its protective mucilage by the dry breeze, would crack and bleed. Pity would try to lift them out, or give them back their upright spines, or teach them fire, forgetting that in this pressurized bell-jar of carbon and rot, a single spark would suck the scarce oxygen from their lungs and roast them in their own limestone oven.

The power that finds them is not lesser. It is old enough to know that survival is its own kind of sanctity, and wise enough to recognize that these four are no longer exiled humans waiting to be rescued. They are a finished thought. They are a verse that the earth wrote in the dark, using the grammar of calcium, silence, and time.

Yet the power also sees the math of the pit. 

There are four of them. The oldest female is barren, her pelvis too narrowed by the cave’s editing to pass another child; the youngest female has no mate who is not her father or her brother. The skulls in the undercuts are licked down to tissue-paper. Three seasons ago, a frog’s skin-venom took the fifth. Soon—in ten years, or twenty—there will be one left, sitting alone by the descending stair of bowls, working a six-fingered thumb back and forth in the wet stone groove with no one left to receive the *full* or *empty* shiver of her flank. And when she dies, the travertine will finally choke the channels, the crabs will scatter, and the spirals carved by the ancestors will be smoothed away into blank, sweating rock.

The wise power does not want the verse to end so soon, nor does it want the last of them to die in the terrible, unshared silence of being the final one. 

So it does not reach down with a hand of light. It reaches down with chemistry, with geology, and with patience. It works inside the rules of the cathedral.

First, it addresses the stone. 

Deep behind the western wall—three hundred yards back in the lightless, solid karst where the rainwater percolates down from the surface hills before sweating out into the nave—there is a sealed pocket of ancient marine bone-bed, a fossilized reef of chalk and phosphorite laid down eighty million years ago. The power does not shatter the rock; a crack would echo into the chamber and bring the yard-long spiders dropping from the roof. Instead, it simply leans its weight against a microscopic fault line, widening a hair-thin fissure by the breadth of a grass blade. 

Nothing happens that night. Nothing happens that week. 

But over the course of the next month, the slow, feverish water that feeds the fluted flowstone pillar above the carved basins changes its diet. As it seeps through the newly opened fissure, it dissolves the ancient reef. When the drops gather on the tallow-colored stone of the nave and fall—*plat, plat*—into the uppermost bowl of the stair, they are heavy with a new, invisible cream of bioavailable calcium, magnesium, and phosphorus. 

The next time the woman uncoils during the ten-second shiver of the thermal draft and scoops up a ribbon of the mineral biofilm from the basin, she pauses. Her frog-like throat pulses twice. The slime is thicker, chalky and sweet, rich as bone-marrow. She does not need to spend three hours tonight licking the translucent dome of her grandmother’s skull to keep her ribs from softening. The mountain itself is feeding them its old dead. Within half a year, the dense, pale fat on the flanks of the four grows firmer; the soft, cartilaginous valves in their throats strengthen; the young female’s body, starved for the minerals required to build another skeleton inside her own, quietly wakes into fertility.

Second, the power addresses the silence. 

The ten-second thermal draft is their only freedom—the brief window when the rustle of the saucer-moths and the patter of condensation mask the *cluck* of their joints and allow them to live. Ten seconds every five minutes is a hard, narrow prison. 

Fifty feet overhead, where the elephant-ear philodendrons and strangler figs choke the aperture of the roof, the power touches the vascular cambium of a single massive fig root. It does not kill the tree; it merely persuades the root to arch upward as it grows, lifting a mat of overlapping leaves the way a sleeper lifts a heavy blanket to let the heat out from under the sheets. At the same time, down at the floor of the chamber, the power stirs the thermophilic bacteria in the pitch-black mattress of the humus, warming the deep rot by just one degree.

The convection engine of the sinkhole deepens its breath. 

Now, when the heat rises, it does not escape in a lazy, ten-second sigh. It chimneys upward in a long, slow, thirty-second exhalation. The downdraft that slips down the curved karst walls to replace it is steadier, bringing a fraction more oxygen from the jungle above without drying the air. For half a minute at a time, the hanging air-roots sway, the heavy drops drum a slow, continuous chord against the violet-veined arums, and the thousand mottled moths pulse their wings with a sustained, hushed *shhhhhh*, like the sound of blood in a mother’s arteries.

In the knot of limbs high up in the Spanish moss, four pairs of oil-black eyes open at once. Their bat-wing ears swivel, cupping the prolonged rush of the rain. 

Thirty seconds. It is an eternity. It is enough time to climb from the floor to the wasp-kilns in two movements instead of ten. It is enough time to turn over in the dark without fear, to press flank to flank and speak three whole sentences of skin-talk—*warm, full, safe*—before the dead air seals the room again.

Third, and last, the power addresses their solitude.

This is the hardest thing, because there are no others like them on the surface; the people who fled the bright world centuries ago left no cousins behind in the sun. If the four are not to wither into an inbred cul-de-sac, the answer cannot come from above. It has to come from beneath.

Karst is never a single room. It is a sponge, a honeycomb of dissolved limestone stretching for miles under the jungle floor. Two miles to the south, connected to this nave by a drowned, silt-choked siphon that collapsed four hundred years ago, there is another *aven*. 

The people who went underground all those generations ago did not all go down the same hole. 

For three hundred years, a second remnant—seven of them, slightly darker-skinned from a diet of iron-rich cave-shrimp, their outer fingers fused a little higher up the knuckle, their skin-talk accented with a slightly faster tremor—has been living in that southern bell-jar. They, too, have been licking their ancestors’ femurs down to paper. They, too, have been slowly running out of blood.

The power looks at the quarter-mile of wet clay, wedged boulders, and blind, water-filled crawlspace that separates the two cathedrals. It does not blast a tunnel; the shockwave would stop the hearts of both tribes. 

Instead, it calls the blind, blunt-headed snakes and the great burrowing earthworms of the deep karst. It guides the acidic roots of the cavern-figs to follow a vein of soft gypsum between the chambers, splitting the rock with hydraulic slowness. It sends a subterranean seep of warm, carbonic water to wash the choking silt from the low, rounded ceiling of the connecting tube, lowering the water level inch by inch until there is a foot of warm, breathing air above the mud—just enough space for someone with a flattened, spider-crawling pelvis and noseless, sphincter-sealed nostrils to belly-crawl through the dark.

It takes three years for the passage to clear. The power is in no hurry.

On a night when the jungle above is cool and the thirty-second rain is drumming softly on the arums, the old male is resting at the base of the eastern retaining wall, his six-fingered palm pressed flat into the warm black humus. 

He is listening to the floor—to the squirm of grubs, the distant shift of a lemur’s weight on a root, the familiar, dampened *thrum* of the young female twenty paces away by the crab-basins.

Then, through the bones of his wrist, comes a vibration he has never felt before.

*Thrum-thrum. Pause. Thrum.*

It is not a falling fruit. It has the exact, deliberate cadence of a flexed tendon, the unmistakable grammar of a six-fingered hand striking the drumhead of the earth—but it is coming from *behind* the solid limestone of the eastern undercut, where a slumped cairn of dry-stacked stones hides a low, wet arch of dissolved rock that used to be a dead end.

And with the vibration comes a faint, new draft of warm air, smelling of crushed liverwort, old tallow, iron-rich mud, and the salty, oily sweat of skin that is just like his own.

In the gloom by the descending basins, the young female stops working her thumb in the stone groove. Her milky third eyelid sweeps across her oil-black eyes. Thirty feet up, the other two uncoil from the fig-roots and flow down the slick, fluted column during the long, forgiving hush of the thermal rain. 

The four of them gather at the base of the eastern cairn. They press their pleated shoulders together, a single silent ripple running through all four bodies at once—a word they have not had reason to use in a hundred years. 

*Other.*

The old male reaches out his paddle-fingered hand, places it flat into the pitch-black mulch, and strikes the floor twice in answer. 

Above the choked roof of the sinkhole, fifty feet up in the unseen sky, the wise power withdraws its attention, leaving the philodendron leaves to knit back together over the shaft. It has given them no fire, no language of the throat, no sky, and no salvation from the dark. It has given them marrow in the water, a longer rain to move in, and a neighbor in the stone. 

In the bruised, submarine green below, the rubble of the eastern cairn shifts without a sound, and a pale, wet hand—six-fingered, ridged with chevron pads—reaches out of the wall to touch the old male’s wrist.
