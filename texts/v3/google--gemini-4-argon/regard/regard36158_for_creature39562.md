There are powers that travel the dark between worlds looking for broken things to mend. When they find a world choked in its own ash, they bring rain; when they find a species trapped in the dark, they hang a lantern in the sky. 

The power that came to the plateau was older than those, and wiser. It did not arrive as a pillar of fire or a sudden clearing of the stratospheric haze. Had it torn open the slate-colored sky to let in a sun, the ribbon-lungs would have shriveled into dry leather within the hour, the blind grazers would have panicked in the unfamiliar glare, and the delicate, centuries-long dance of condensation would have boiled away into nothing.

Instead, the power arrived as a listening. 

It settled into the thin, warm air like an extra degree of barometric pressure, spreading across the continent-sized tableland until it touched every terraced puddle, every vibrating hull plate, every damp pore in the basalt. And as it listened, it realized that the plateau was not a collection of separate creatures scrambling over the ruins of an abandoned industry.

The plateau was a single, immense, slow-breathing organism. The harvesters were its circulating heart- chambers; the tread-canals were its veins; the silt-weavers and tinker-crabs were its marrow and its immune system. It was a self-sustaining engine of rock, steam, steel, and chitin, dreaming a warm, blind dream in the dark.

And yet, because the power was wise, it looked deeper than the present century. It looked down the long, slow slope of geological time, and it saw the quiet tragedy waiting at the bottom.

Nothing built by mortal hands truly lasts forever. The tinker-crabs could scrape the lime from the valves, and the biofilm could lubricate the seals, but they could not replace the twelve-foot tread plates as the basalt dust ground them down to foil. They could not re-wind the massive銅-and-iron coils of the induction furnaces when the insulation finally baked into brittle powder. They could not replenish the fissile cores that kept the great turbines humming. 

Already, three hundred miles to the east, one of the cathedral-machines was limping. Its starboard tread had worn through its secondary pins; it dragged itself in a wide, forty-mile circle, its light-cones flickering a dim, dying orange. Five hundred miles north, another had already stopped—a cold, rusting mountain of silent steel surrounded by the calcified bones of the herd that had refused to leave its cooling wake, slowly being smothered by a triumphant continent of gray drape-mold.

In ten thousand years, the last turbine would sigh and still. The steam would cease to rise. The fog would thin, the pools would dry, and the plateau would become what it had been before the machines: a sterile, suffocating slab of warm black rock.

The benevolent power considered this. It had the strength to simply repair the machines—to snap its fingers and make the steel thick and new again. It had the strength to reshape the creatures so they no longer needed the harvesters at all. 

But to make the machines immortal was to freeze the world in stasis, keeping the life forever dependent on a dead maker’s tools. And to take the machines away was to erase the strange, miraculous beauty of what this life had chosen to become. The tinker-crab’s spatulate claw, shaped to fit the curve of a steam-pipe, was a poem written over ten thousand generations; it would be a violence to render that poem meaningless.

So the power chose a third way. It did not change the world; it gave the world a way to finish becoming itself.

It moved into the belly of the limping harvester in the east, down into the hot, roaring dark beneath the induction furnaces. There, where the magnetic separators whined and the smelted iron ran in glowing, white-hot ribbons before being vented as ash, the power reached out and touched the silt-weavers and the tinker-crabs and the black, greasy lithotrophs of the biofilm.

It did not give them human minds—thoughts of metal and geometry would have been a curse in the peaceful dark. Instead, it gave them a deeper, older kind of knowing. It wove a new stanza into their instincts.

In the warm grease-traps of the drive-sprockets, the next generation of tinker-crabs hatched with a slightly different sensitivity in their shells. When they crawled out into the vibrating cathedral of the hull, they no longer felt only the *where* of the hums; they felt the *health* of them. A worn bearing, rattling a fraction of a millimeter out of true, no longer registered merely as a landmark. To the crabs, that dissonant rattle now felt like an itch—an urgent, irresistible hunger.

And the power changed what they did with the iron they chewed.

Previously, theharvesters had smelted the metallic regolith and thrown it away as slag-ash, an ancient industrial quota being fulfilled for ghosts. Now, deep in the induction chambers, the greasy biofilm began to creep closer to the furnace vents, altered by a whisper of grace to survive the blistering heat. As the molten metal was atomized to be vented as ash, the new bacteria caught it, cooling and binding the pure iron droplets with biological silicates before they could leave the exhaust stacks.

The tinker-crabs came to the stacks and gathered this rich, metallic paste. Driven by the itch of the dissonant vibrations, they carried it in their spatulate claws down into the dark clearances of the machine. 

Where a hull plate was worn thin and singing a high, fragile note in the wind, the crabs plastered the iron-paste against the steel. Where a tread-pin groaned under the weight of the nave, they packed the biological amalgam into the gap. Under the pressure and the radiant heat of the machine, the paste cured—not into crude stone like the silt-weavers' dams, but into a dense, fibrous iron-chitin composite, harder than basalt, slick and self-healing.

The power did not stop with the metal. It drifted into the deepest, most secret vault of the machine, where the ancient nuclear pile sat behind ten feet of lead and boron, slowly dimming as its fuel spent itself over the millennia. 

The power reached into the vesicular basalt of the plateau itself, miles beneath the hardpan ruts. The planet’s crust was heavy with heavy metals—uranium and thorium locked away in the deep magma plumes that warmed the tableland. The power coaxed the deep roots of the thermal palms and the microscopic threads of the subterranean fungi, teaching them to drink the heavy, radioactive salts from the deep stone and draw them upward, concentrating them into the dense, metallic nodules that grew among the wire-bush tubers.

When the harvesters scooped up the regolith, they now scooped up those nodules. And inside the machine, a new caste of blind, pallid gasket-eels—drawn by the warm, blue Cherenkov glow that leaked through the microscopic cracks in the ancient reactor shielding—began to carry the heavy, hot dust from the magnetic separators into the core's cooling loops, feeding the dimming fire atom by slow atom.

It took a century for the first miracle to become visible.

The limping harvester in the east did not stop. Slowly, over decades of three-mile-an-hour crawling, the agonizing screech of its starboard tread quieted. If a light had shone upon its tracks, one would have seen that the twelve-foot steel plates were no longer bare metal; they were encased in thick, layered carapaces of dark, living iron, constantly shed and regrown by thousands of scuttling shapes that moved within the joints like corpuscles in an artery. 

The machine was no longer a ruin being colonized by life. The machine was becoming a shell, and the ecosystem inside it had become the snail.

Satisfied that the heart would no longer fail, the benevolent power did one last thing before it departed. 

It looked at the silent, clicking herds of grazers, the patient silt-weavers straddling their miniature dams, and the gliders riding the hot exhaust into the fog. It saw how entirely alone each harvester-biome was—each a solitary island of warmth wandering a dark ocean, meeting another only once a decade in a brief, frantic crossing of yellow light.

The power reached into the sodium-vapor floodlights at the prows of the great machines, and into the deep, rhythmic *thrum* of their turbines. It tuned them, ever so slightly.

Now, when a harvester crawls across the flats, its turbines do not merely hum with mechanical indifference. As the tinker-crabs thicken a plate here, or thin a baffle there, the pitch of the machine changes. It becomes a voice—a vast, sub-audible whale-song that travels through the dense basalt crust for a hundred miles in every direction. One machine sings a slow, rising fifth in the stone; thirty miles away, another answers with a deep, resonant third, felt in the tarsal bones of every grazer and the feathery palps of every weaver across the continent.

They know where the others are now. The plateau is no longer a collection of blind islands in the dark; it is a choir.

And the floodlights—though they still cut pale, conical tunnels into the mist so the beetles and the gliders can hunt—have softened from harsh industrial yellow to the warm, deep amber of a banked hearth. 

The benevolent power lingered for one more turn of the season, watching two harvesters approach each other in the fog. They did not stumble upon one another by accident this time; they had been singing to each other through the rock for three months, steering their massive, chitin-plated treads into a deliberate convergence by the subtle, collective weight of the crabs in their steering linkages.

Their amber light-cones crossed, creating a gentle, golden twilight over the wet clay and the coppery scrub. The gliders spiraled through the warm mist; the young crabs dropped into the mud to trade ships; the herds mingled and clicked their dry-rain song together in the ninety-degree warmth.

Then the great, living cathedrals drew apart, their hulls gleaming with wet, newly grown iron, breathing tomorrow’s clouds into the dark. And the power left them there, knowing that the plateau needed no sun, for it had learned how to tend its own fire.
