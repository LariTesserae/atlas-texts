The attention that found the nave did not arrive as a presence within the room, because there was no room for a presence. Even a ghost would have displaced too much of the acoustic pressure, casting a dead-air shadow that would have dropped fifty Tenants out of their shafts like stunned birds. 

Instead, the power pressed its ear against the outside of the pale, braided vault, out in the unfocused beige glare where there was neither sky nor ground. It listened for a long time—three weeks in the eastern cubicles, four days in the middle of the Plaza, a century in the tight, dry grain of the load-bearing buttresses.

It understood quickly that it could not rescue them. 

A lesser kindness might have tried to quiet the room, which would have been a massacre. A clumsier wisdom might have tried to wake the Tenants up, to give them eyes to see their hollow, manic purgatory, or minds large enough to realize that the landlord was never coming and the stains they scrubbed were the only things holding the floor together. To give a Tenant self-awareness in that screaming wooden lung would be to invent a new and exquisite torture. They were made of the noise and the habit; to take either away was murder.

Yet the power felt a vast, aching pity for them. It saw the handkerchief-Tenants snapping their frayed corners at the deaf green crabs until their reed-ribs splintered from the effort. It saw the broom-thick cylinders scrubbing the bridge-tendrils with such frantic, dry-heaving urgency that they wore their own chamois skin down to the whistling cartilage. They were performing the stress of a dead metropolis without ever having known a city's small mercies. They had inherited the commute, the maintenance, the rent-terror, and the boundary-dispute, but whoever or whatever had been digested to make this place had forgotten to leave behind the weekend, the armchair, or the evening.

The power could not break the machine. The equilibrium was too tight. It had to introduce something that weighed nothing, took up no space, and used the roar itself as its medium.

It decided to give them *the Landlord’s Footstep*, and it decided to give them *Dust*.

First, the power reached into the unfocused beige glare outside the vault and altered the light. Not everywhere, and not all at once. It took the flat, eternal four-o'clock glare and introduced a slow, local drift—an optical barometric system no wider than a dinner plate that slid lazily across the outside of the perforated canopy. 

Where this pocket of altered light passed, the beige glare deepened into the rich, heavy, amber gold of five-thirty in the afternoon. 

Inside the nave, a narrow shaft of this amber light slanted down through six layers of translucent leaf-skin apartments. It did not bring silence—silence was death—but where the amber shaft fell, it changed the *phase* of the noise. 

The power tuned the light so that it acted as a harmonic dampener on the upper registers of the roar. Inside that three-foot beam of golden sun, the iron-chain rasp of the varnish-bugs and the pistol-shot *crack* of the ceiling drops did not lose their physical pressure—the air still pushed, still held the Tenants inflated—but the jagged, panicked overtones were rounded off. The screaming discordant shriek of the room was phase-shifted within the beam into a deep, resonant, fifty-cycle hum. 

It was the sound of a refrigerator running in a warm kitchen at dusk. It was the vibration of a distant subway train passing thirty feet beneath a basement floor—heavy, supportive, and profoundly indifferent. 

A Tenant, frantic and heavy with grey paste, dropped from a fourth-story balcony to scrub a root-highway. As it slid along the wood, its horny lower hem crossed into the shaft of amber light. 

Instantly, the chaotic acoustic gale hammering its chest-tympanum resolved into a single, massive, velvet chord. The horn-rimmed valves on its sternum, which had been fluttering in a panic to bleed off the sonic booms of the glides, suddenly synced to the slow, deep hum. They opened, held for three full seconds, and closed with a soft, leathery *sigh*. 

The Tenant stopped scrubbing. 

It did not deflate; the deep bass hum held its parchment pleats billowed out like a sail in a steady trade wind. For the first time in the history of the banyan-nave, a Tenant stood in the middle of an avenue and did nothing at all. The frantic, stop-motion urgency bled out of its umbrella-rib scaffolding. The grey wood-putty in its folds did not dry into irritating grit; under the amber light, the power had warmed the capillary water by four degrees, turning the paste into a slick, soothing poultice. The Tenant simply hung there in the golden shaft, floating an inch off the satin wood, gently rocking from side to side in the fifty-cycle cradle of the hum, while its sternal valves breathed a slow, rhythmic *in-out, in-out*.

It stayed there for eleven minutes until the amber shaft drifted onto the next block, leaving the Tenant rested, its reeds lubricated, its skin supple, before the four-o'clock glare returned and it resumed its wiping with half the panic and twice the grace.

Second, for the handkerchief-Tenants in the eastern cubicles and the broom-cylinders in the walk-ups, the power introduced a microscopic change to the banyan-vine’s sap. 

High up in the buttresses, where the capillary roots swelled to weep their heavy globules of water, the power coaxed the wood into secreting a trace lipid—a tiny, benign ester that smelled faintly of cedarwood, old paper, and sleep. 

When the heavy drops fell two hundred feet and exploded against the drum-skin roofs with their pistol-shot *cracks*, this oil atomized into the celery-and-linen air. It drifted into the लाखों of empty four-inch apartments, mixing with the sawdust sanded up by the insects. 

When a Tenant spread itself from wall to wall in a narrow room, acting as a living curtain to filter the air, it caught this new mist. 

Previously, the accumulating grey paste had felt like an itch—an urgent, irritating weight that compelled the Tenant to roll up and go scrub the streets until its skin was raw. It was the somatic memory of a mess that *must* be cleaned before someone sees it.

The cedar-oil changed the chemistry of the paste on their parchment skin. It acted as a mild, topical anaesthetic and a weight-distributor. Now, as the Tenant hung in the narrow apartment, trapping the floating wood-flour, the paste did not itch. It felt warm. It felt like a heavy flannel blanket being drawn slowly up over cold shoulders. 

And then came the *Footstep*. 

Every few hours, the power tapped the very top of the outside vault with a knuckle made of pure, low-frequency gravity. 

*Thump.*

It was not loud, not like the frogs or the glides. It was a subterranean, structural pulse that traveled down the load-bearing thumb-roots, through the five-story walk-ups, and into the breadbox cubicles. To the biological habits of the city, this pulse meant one specific thing, translated into the ancient, digested lexicon of the tenement: *The building is inspected. The rent is paid. The doors are locked.*

When that gentle, heavy *thump* traveled through the floorboards, every handkerchief-Tenant in the eastern district stopped flicking its corners at the green crabs. The signal had arrived: their territory was officially recognized. They did not need to defend the intersection for the next twenty minutes. They tucked their frayed hems into the ninety-degree corners, let the acoustic wind billow their backs like little hammocks, and went limp.

In the Plaza, the great *Flats*—stretched taut and razor-thin between last Tuesday and this afternoon—felt the *thump* pass through the spatial seams. The power used the pulse to briefly equalize the barometric pressure across the time-folds. For thirty seconds after the *thump*, last Tuesday and this afternoon moved at the exact same speed. The brutal, shearing tension on the Flats’ translucent bodies vanished. They didn't fall; they simply went slack in the warm air, undulating like silk sheets on a clothesline in a summer breeze, shedding the calcified stress from their edges before the time-seam tightened again and they resumed their work as shock-absorbers.

The city did not stop. The banyan-nave still roared like a cataract inside a snare drum; the glides still cracked the air at right angles; the lizards still slid through the wet puddles of three days ago; the old Tenants still stiffened into doorframes and coughed up postage-stamp young from their dry ribs. 

The wise power did not break the wheel, because the wheel was their bodies. It simply oiled the axle. It gave the curtains a reason to enjoy hanging in the window; it gave the scrubbers a warm patch of afternoon sun on the linoleum where they could forget what they were doing; and every now and then, through the frantic, booming walls of their hollow cathedral, it knocked twice on the ceiling to let them know that the house was in order, and they were allowed to rest.
