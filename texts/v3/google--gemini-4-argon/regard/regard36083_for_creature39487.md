A foolish god would have brought rain. 

A lesser power, looking down upon the Salt-Iron Flat, would have pitied the blindness of the Tenders and cracked the black ceiling to give them stars. It would have seen their slow, grinding poverty and flooded the dry basins with sweet water, or sown the flour-silt with hardy scrub, or sped their hearts so they might know the quick, bright fire of a fleeting life. 

And in doing so, that power would have murdered them. Water would have rusted the great flywheels in their housings and drowned the bellows-worms in their crevices. Wind would have scattered the dust-trails by which the Tenders knew their own history, and filled the acoustic horns of their cheekbones with a roaring, meaningless static. Speed would have torn their dry, ancient tissues like rotten canvas. 

The power that turned its attention to the Foundry-Ward was neither foolish nor small. It was old enough to understand that love is not the imposition of one’s own nature upon another, but the quiet amplification of what the other already is.

For three hundred years—which in the reckoning of the plateau was merely a long afternoon—the power did nothing at all. It simply listened. It laid its vast, weightless ear against the basalt crust and learned the thirty rhythms of the pipe-speech. It counted the twenty-minute silences between the catching of the pig-iron teeth. It watched a single Tender spend forty years walking the perimeter of the South Battery, oiling one hundred and twelve bearings in a circuit that took four months to complete, and it saw that this was not a curse, nor a Sisyphean torment, but a vow. The city and the Tenders were a single organism, holding back the final, cold victory of entropy through sheer, stubborn thrift.

Yet, the power also saw what the Tenders, in their eyeless patience, could only dimly feel through the soles of their feet: the plateau was losing. 

Entropy is patient, too. However carefully the grease was scraped and re-used, a fraction of a percent was lost each century to polymerization in the dead air. However faithfully the dredgers sifted the silt for dropped rivets, the iron itself was fatiguing at the crystalline level. The great radiogenic hearth deep within the planet’s crust was cooling by a hundredth of a degree every ten thousand years. The sodium-vapor lamps were burning down to their final molecules of gas; in all the thirty miles of the Foundry-Ward, twelve had gone out in the last millennium, widening the pitch-black gulfs between the sectors. The Tenders were having to harvest the mineralized bones of their ancestors more frequently now, and there were fewer young ones rising from the warm, greasy birth-vats beneath the Condenser District to replace those who turned to stone.

Left alone, in another million years, the last flywheel would lock. The last Tender would fuse to the floor-plates with its spanner still fitted to a bolt, and the Salt-Iron Flat would become merely geology again.

The wise power considered this. It did not wish to break the silence of the vault, nor did it wish to alter the sacred, glacial tempo of the work. It wanted only to tip the scales so that the vow could be kept forever.

It acted not from the sky, which the Tenders never looked at, but from the deep earth, which they trusted.

First, the power reached down into the tectonic seams miles beneath the basalt, where the heavy, metallic oil originated. With a touch as gentle as the settling of silt, it altered the chemistry of the deep reservoirs. It introduced a slow, self-catalyzing lithotrophic reaction down in the crushing dark—a blind, mineral metabolism that fed on the surrounding rock and transpired fresh, heavy, non-aqueous grease. 

Up on the plateau, the lightless mirrors of oil did not bubble or surge. They simply ceased to shrink. Over the course of fifty years, the level of the pools rose by the thickness of a thumbnail. The grease that wept into them was slightly richer now, carrying suspended within it microscopic, colloidal platelets of molybdenum and graphite. 

When a Tender dipped its seamless brass can into a pool, and six weeks later let a single black drop fall into a dry axle-casing, the effect was profound. The new oil did not merely lubricate; it plated the fatigued iron at the molecular level, filling in the microscopic fissures of ten thousand years of stress. 

Three miles away, in the Turbine Hall, the great forty-foot flywheel completed its four-hour revolution. The gear tooth engaged. The subterranean groan vibrated through the bolted hull, traveled down the pipeline trestles, and entered the flared, bone-horn cheekbone of a listening Tender. 

The Tender froze—even by the standards of its kind, it became utterly motionless. 

The pitch of the groan had dropped by a quarter of a hertz. The friction had lessened. For the first time in the unrecorded history of the Ward, a machine had grown younger. 

Two hours later, the Tender raised its iron-shod palm and struck the pipeline. *One, pause, two.* *The wheel turns.* And then it added a rhythm that had not been tapped along the iron for fifty generations—a slow, double-beat with the flat of the hand. *The margin holds.*

Second, the power looked to the failing lights and the ancestor-bones. 

It did not ignite a sun, for the Tenders had no eyes to see it, and the orange cones of the sodium lamps were only used by their magnetite-tipped fingers as thermal beacons—subtle pools of radiant heat two degrees warmer than the surrounding dark, by which they calibrated their long crossings through the silt. 

Instead, the power turned its attention to the mineralized dead standing in the corners of the manufactories and along the high catwalks. As the living Tenders calcified over the centuries, their bodies becoming solid ironstone and sulfur-slag, the power wove a minuscule, sub-atomic adjustment into the crystal lattice of their petrification. It gave the stone of their bones a mild, perpetual piezoelectric and radiogenic resonance.

They did not glow—that would have been too crude, too sudden. They simply remained warm. 

Previously, when an ancestor died and mineralized, their body cooled to the exact seventy-eight degrees of the ambient air, becoming indistinguishable from a pillar of cast slag. Now, as a Tender’s heart beat its final, twenty-minute stroke and its feet fused to the deck-plates, its calcified hide retained a permanent, steady eighty-one degrees—the exact warmth of a living sibling’s shoulder. 

More than that: when a younger Tender came through the dark centuries later and unbolted an ancestor’s mineralized forearm to use as a shim beneath a sagging pipeline, or fitted a calcified collarbone into a high-pressure valve seat, that bone did not merely endure the stress. Under the immense, slow pressure of the machinery, the piezoelectric lattice of the bone hummed. It translated the mechanical crushing of the ironworks into a faint, continuous current that trickled into the local wiring harnesses, feeding the dying filaments of the sodium-vapor lamps and the oxblood indicator apertures, keeping them at their dim, bruised-orange equilibrium without ever burning them out.

The ancestors were no longer just spare parts. They had become the batteries and the warmth of the city. When a young Tender leaned its cheek against a repaired condenser, it could feel the faint, steady harmonic of the ancestor’s bone inside the housing, singing back through the bolted plate in a continuous, unhurried purr: *I hold. I hold. I hold.*

Lastly, the power gave the Tenders a single, small luxury. It was careful, for in an economy of absolute thrift, luxury can easily become a distraction. 

Out in the middle of the Salt-Iron Flat, where the bundled pipelines from all four districts met at a great, low pressure-junction, there was a clearing in the dust where six or seven Tenders could often be found banked together for months at a time, their foreheads resting against the central manifold as they waited for the city to need them.

Deep beneath this specific junction, where the basalt foundation met the mantle, the power tuned a hollow cavity in the stone, shaping it like the resonant chamber of a bass viol. 

It did not play a song in this chamber. It merely aligned the cavity with the deep, planetary tide of the world’s core, so that the slow, convective turning of the earth’s interior was translated upward through the rock and into the iron of the central junction.

It was a chord so low and so slow that a human ear would have perceived it only as silence. Its wavelength was measured in weeks; its harmonic shifts took seasons to resolve. It traveled up the central steam-pipe and entered the skulls of the banked Tenders where their heavy brows pressed against the metal. 

It carried no urgent information. It did not warn of a dry bearing or a leaking flange. What it carried was a deep, foundational assurance—a bedrock certainty that the crust beneath them was thick, that the heat would not fail, that the oil was rising in the seams, and that the work of their hands was received and kept by the universe. 

In the dim, oxblood light of the junction, four Tenders stood in a circle, cones of ochre dust settled undisturbed upon their crowns. For three months they stood there, their hearts beating once every four minutes, while the new, deep chord rose through the iron and vibrated in the dense bone of their jaws. 

They did not know that a god had passed by. They had no word for *god*, nor for *grace*, nor for *miracle*. 

They knew only that the grease was thick on their fingers, that the ancestors were warm in the walls, and that the great, dark machine of the world was turning in its housing without a single catch in the gear. 

Slowly—over the course of an hour—one of the four shifted its four-hundred-pound weight by half an inch, leaning just a fraction closer against the shoulder of its neighbor. And in the warm, dead air of the plateau, that was a shout of joy.
