To a power that has watched stars ignite, burn through their bellyfuls of hydrogen, and cool into cinders, the gray plain outside the basin is not permanent at all. It is merely slow—a granite wave taking fifty million years to break. And the steaming, clicking basin is not chaotic; it is simply a very small, very fast whirlpool caught in the rock. 

Yet, when the attention of this power settled upon the collapsed dome, it did not judge the difference in speeds as trivial. It looked upon the Prompts with a vast, quiet tenderness. 

It saw the exquisite grace of their knees, forever bent to ride the heave of the root-mat. It saw the generosity of their bodies, serving as landing pads for wet lemurs and living routers for the emerald midges. And then it looked at the ring of people sitting on the soap-slick stone at twilight, passing a dead, gray pebble from warm palm to warm palm, and it understood the quiet ache at the center of their lives. 

To be the mind of a place that has no memory of form—to be the handlebars on a machine that dissolves your bed before the warmth of your back has left it—is a lonely kind of stewardship. The canopy’s memory-banks held petabytes of history, yes, accessible through the damp flank of a four-foot toad, but data is not a keepsake. A mother in the basin could call up the exact genetic sequence and metabolic holo-log of her great-grandmother, yet she had never touched a thimble, a doorway, or a wooden spoon that her great-grandmother’s hands had worn smooth. Every surface they touched was newborn. Every wall was a stranger.

A lesser power might have slowed the city down, or raised monuments of marble in the plaza. But the wise power knew that to introduce dead stone into the basin would be to introduce a blind spot—a cold, inert tumor that the roots would exhaust themselves trying to digest or route around. The roaring velocity of the timber was not a curse; it was the brilliant, feverish song of the basin. The people did not want their city to die so they could have statues. They only wanted a way to leave a mark that didn’t rot in twenty minutes.

So the power did not change the stone, and it did not break the biology. Instead, it reached down into the genetic library of the basin—a nudge no wider than a single cosmic ray striking a reproductive spore in the deep root-mat, followed by a subtle, harmonic tuning of the canopy’s computational matrices. It offered a gift written in the city’s own language.

***

Three mornings after the attention passed over the lowland, an older man named Vance—whose forearms were currently flashing a muted, rhythmic ochre to tell a flock of wading birds that he was only passing through—stopped near the center of the uncluttered plaza.

A transit pavilion had just finished dissolving to his left, slumping into a pool of amber mulch with a long, wet *shhhk*. As the root-mat drank the slurry back down, the copper-colored floor heaved upward to redistribute the mass. 

Usually, the floor smoothed itself out in seconds, resetting to a blank, pulsating canvas of water-tubers. 

This time, where the pavilion’s central pillar had stood, a single node of wood remained. It was no larger than a child’s fist, protruding an inch above the wet lignum. As Vance watched, his knees automatically shifting to take the wake of a passing sap-surge, the little knot of wood did not soften into gray mush. Instead, the city’s xylem valves gave a sharp, syncopated *thud-clack*—a sound none of the residents had ever heard before—and pumped a dense rush of dark, metal-heavy silicates straight into the knot.

The wood hissed, steaming fiercely in the skin-warm air. Its cells did not divide; they collapsed inward, cross-linking their carbon chains under sudden, localized hydraulic pressure, purging every drop of water and enzyme, and sealing their outer rind with a glaze of pure, biological glass. 

When the steam cleared ten seconds later, the knot was still there. It was black as river-basalt, swirled with grain of deep copper, and completely, gloriously dead. 

Vance blinked, wiping a spray of warm runoff from his cheek. He walked over—the floor obligingly rolling out three temporary stepping-ridges under his callused feet—and knelt. 

He pressed his thumb against the black knot. The surrounding root-mat did not try to swallow it. The enzymes in the standing puddles beaded off its glassy rind like mercury off his own skin. Stranger still, the knot was not cold like the gray chert outside the moat. Tiny, hair-thin rootlets remained braided around its base, not to digest it, but to cradle it, running a continuous trickle of warm municipal water underneath it so that the dead wood stayed at the exact temperature of a living hand.

Tentatively, Vance reached to his thigh. The pale mycelium of his kilt unweaved with a wet *rip*, yielding his bone splicing-knife. 

He leaned over the black knot and pressed the tip of the knife against its surface. It did not cut like timber, nor did it chip like flint. Where the pressure of his hand met the biological glass, combined with the specific salt-and-pheromone signature of his skin, the surface yielded just enough—like very hard beeswax—allowing him to score a single, clean groove across the grain. The moment he lifted the knife, the groove vitrified, locking the mark into place forever.

Behind him, a four-foot amphibian plastered to a distillation tower let out a low, resonant croak. The emerald midges hovering near the sap-ports shifted their frantic figure-eights, spinning briefly into a quiet, torus-shaped ring before resuming their work. The city had registered the anomaly, calculated its structural cost as zero, and written a new subroutine around it: *Do not digest. Do not move. Route around.*

***

They called it *heart-amber*, or sometimes *still-wood*. 

The city did not make much of it. If it had, the basin would have choked on its own history within a year. The new protocol was sparing, keyed not to the city’s frantic optimization loops, but to the quiet, chemical spikes of human emotion that soaked into the floor through bare soles and damp palms.

When a family sat around a temporary table for a farewell meal before a son joined the salt-traders on the gray plain, and the hormone-salts of their sweat carried the heavy, unmistakable signature of grief and pride, the table would dissolve as usual—except for a single, palm-sized pebble of smooth, black heart-amber left sitting on the floor where their hands had rested. 

When two people stood in the warm drizzle of the plaza for an hour, their collarbones and forearms flashing in the rapid, cascading indigo-and-gold of a newly forged love, the stepping-stone between their feet would not melt back into the bog when they walked away. It would vitrify with a soft *hiss*, holding the twin impressions of their callused heels in warm, copper-veined glass.

And when an elder died—their body given back, as it always had been, to the great digestive moat at the basin’s edge so their breath and calcium could rise again in the canopy—the hammock of living phloem where they had drawn their last breath would not simply unweave. It would drop a single, heavy bead of black wood to the floor, encasing a microscopic, crystallized seed of their neural-log at its center. It could not be read like a screen, not really; but if a grandchild pressed that bead against the damp flank of a basking toad while holding the other hand to their own chest, they would feel a sudden, warm wash of the elder’s favorite memory—the smell of crushed fern after a high-summer purge, or the exact, soaring sensation of a childhood leap across the plaza—flash across their own skin in a ghost-ripple of violet light.

The basin did not stop roaring. The bridges still snapped in half with the sound of cannon-fire; the doors still tore themselves open like wet Velcro; the geysers still arced across the uncluttered court to splash into thirty-second-old catch-basins. The Prompts still walked with loose, rolling knees, carrying their knives in their living kilts and letting the otter-lemurs kick off their collarbones.

But the pouches of their fungal wraps began to hold more than tools. Now, against the warm skin of a resident’s hip, there was almost always the smooth, heavy weight of two or three pieces of heart-amber—a thumb-stone carved with the tally of their children's births, a shard of a long-dissolved childhood home, a smooth black ring passed down from a friend who had died twenty years ago. 

And in the late afternoons, when the milky gold light bruised into twilight, the residents still went to the soap-slick rim of stone at the edge of the world. They still sat with their legs dangling over the bubbling moat, damping their skin-lights down to a quiet gray as they looked out over the fifty-million-year-old plain.

Only now, they did not always reach across the water to borrow the cold, dead pebbles of the waste. Instead, they sat shoulder to shoulder, reaching into their wraps to draw out their own small pieces of dark, warm, unmelting wood. They passed them from hand to hand along the lip of the basin, tracing the grooves their own people had carved, feeling the pulse of the frantic, living city beating up through the stone beneath them, and the quiet, enduring weight of yesterday resting safely in their palms.
