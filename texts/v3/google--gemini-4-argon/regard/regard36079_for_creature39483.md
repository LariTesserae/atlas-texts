When the Attention arrived, it did not come through the plugged shafts, nor did it cast a shadow through the three-foot wire-glass of the skylight. It simply inhabited the fifty-hertz roar. 

For a long time—weeks, perhaps, though time in the sump was only a slow oscillation between amber and black—the Power did nothing at all. Wisdom, at its highest octave, begins as sheer observation. It rested within the standing Faraday waves on the floor; it felt the warm, tallowy slide of the Damp beneath the *Bryum* moss; it traced the blind, heavy contentment of the crayfish waiting in its ochre basin. 

It recognized immediately that this place was a masterpiece of accidental grace. 

A lesser benevolence might have felt pity for the trapped things. It might have cracked the concrete to let in the wind, or silenced the deafening turbines to grant the chamber peace, or sparked a sudden, bright intelligence inside the multinucleated jelly of the Damp so that it could know itself. 

The Power knew better. To stop the roar would be to blind and shatter the creature whose only nervous system was built of sound. To give the Damp an ego, a human-shaped mind, would be to turn a sixty-foot cathedral of pure sensation into a prison cell. The Damp was not lonely; it was an entire world. It did not want an exit; it wanted continuation.

Yet, looking ninety and a hundred years ahead, the Power saw the three quiet treasons of time that would eventually ruin this jewel box. 

First, the crayfish was thirty years old. Though its shell was splinted and sealed by the Damp’s calcified velvet, its internal organs—its heart, its hepatopancreas, the grey-white threads of its ventral nerve cord—were fraying. When it finally died, no force in the room would be strong enough to chip the rock from the weep-holes. The chamber would drown. 

Second, the city above was changing. In forty years, the ancient municipal pumping station would be decommissioned. The great greywater turbines behind the north wall would be switched off, the cooling lagoons drained, and the sump left to go cold, silent, and dark forever.

Third, the skylight was slowly losing its iron. The wire mesh suspended inside the three-foot glass was rusting from the edges inward; in eighty years, the internal expansion of that oxide would fracture the pane and drop twenty tons of glass and dirty lagoon water onto the floor, crushing the delicate geometry of the garden.

The Power did not break the rules of the room to save it. It worked entirely within the grammar of tallow, lime, and vibration.

***

It began with the crayfish. 

One afternoon, as a pale pseudopod crept into the west basin to place a pellet of compressed algae and amphipod paste between the crustacean’s mouthparts, the Power nudged a single cluster of nuclei within the Damp’s advancing tip. 

The Damp had been scraping the inner surfaces of this chamber for ninety years; its jelly held the sleeping genetic debris of a hundred river-bottom things—planarians that could regrow a head from a tail-tip, freshwater sponges that never aged, dormant fungal spores that knew how to weave into insect nervous systems without killing them. 

The next time the Damp sealed a microscopic crack in the crayfish’s joint-membranes, it did not stop at the chitin. Five hair-thin hyphal threads of syncytium slipped inward, through the gill-chambers, guided by a gentleness the Damp did not know it possessed. 

They did not digest. They anastomosed. 

Over the course of six months, the Damp quietly became the crayfish’s second circulatory system. As the old crustacean’s heart slowed, the rhythmic, hydraulic peristalsis of the Damp’s own protoplasm took over the pumping of its hemolymph. As the crayfish’s fragile gut began to atrophy from age, the coat of flesh on its back began to feed it transdermally, diffusing sugars, amino acids, and oxygen straight into its bloodstream. Even its failing ganglia were cradled in a soft, conductive sheath of calcium-spicule jelly that carried the fifty-hertz hum directly into its motor nerves.

The crayfish ceased to be an animal that could die of old age. It became an articulated, heavily armored hand belonging to the room itself—a bone-and-chitin organ that the Damp could move, repair, and keep forever, sleeping beneath the fern roots until the drains needed tending.

***

Next, the Power looked to the north wall, and the machines behind it.

It did not touch the turbines; machines belong to the brittle world of men, and men eventually abandon everything they build. Instead, the Power looked downward, beneath the lowest foundation tier of the station, into the pre-Cambrian rock on which the concrete rested.

Four hundred feet below the sump, there was an ancient, unmapped fault line where groundwater, heated by deep geothermal gradients, was forced through a narrow choke of fractured basalt. It had been murmuring down there for ten million years, a faint, disorganized rumble buried under the louder roar of the city’s pipes.

Over three years, the Power worked on the stone beneath the floor. It used the sump’s own mineral-heavy seepage—water that leaked *down* through the weep-holes into the bedrock. It guided the dissolution of limestone veins and the re-deposition of travertine deep underground, hollowing out a series of resonant chambers in the rock beneath the north wall, shaping them like the interior of a nautilus shell, or the soundbox of a cello. 

And inside the Damp itself, the Power taught the calcium spicules a new trick. 

As the Damp ingested the last of the rust from the dissolved iron tie-rods, and drew trace silicates and barium from the degrading concrete aggregate, it began to lay down a new kind of mineral along the base of the north wall. Not soft flowstone, but dense, interlocking plates of biological piezo-crystal, grown directly into the pores of the foundation.

Now, the room had a root reaching down into the earth’s throat. 

Decades from now, when human engineers finally throw the big brass switches above and the 1,200-RPM turbines spin down to a halt, the silence will not fall. As the mechanical roar fades, the piezo-crystalline plates in the north wall will catch the deep, hydraulic thrum of the geothermal fault four hundred feet below—amplified and tuned by the newly carved caverns in the bedrock to an exact, unwavering fifty hertz. The warm water from the deep fissure will rise by thermosiphon through the newly widened pores around the weep-holes, keeping the floor puddle at seventy-eight degrees. The engine will no longer be made of steel; it will be made of planetary geology, good for another ten thousand years.

***

Finally, the Power turned its attention upward, to the sixty-foot ceiling and the amber light.

It did not mend the rusting wire inside the three-foot glass. Instead, during the dark hours when the Damp climbed the walls and spread itself across the underside of the skylight like a vast, pale placenta to fish for crane flies, the Power encouraged the creature’s masonry.

Every night, before retreating down the walls at dawn, the Damp left behind a microscopic film of clear, vitrified calcium carbonate and silica, bonded with tough, cross-linked proteins. Layer by layer, micron by micron, over twenty years, it grew a second lens directly beneath the glass—a biological crystal arch, clear as optical quartz, curving smoothly into the slumped travertine of the upper walls so that its weight was borne by the perimeter of the shaft. 

When the industrial glass above finally gives way—or when the city above tears down the settling lagoons and covers the site with soil or forest or rubble—the chamber will not collapse. And it will not go dark. 

Because within the green algal inclusions that already marbled the Damp’s upper mantle, the Power woke a quiet, metabolic mirror. Around the rim of the new crystalline dome, the creeping figs and the *Bryum* moss and the Damp’s own translucent skin began to trade electrons with the piezo-crystals in the walls. Converting a fraction of a percent of the room’s endless acoustic energy into chemical light, the upper biofilms developed a slow, circadian bioluminescence. 

If the sun is ever taken away from the skylight, the ceiling will not know the difference. For twelve hours a day, fed by the vibration of the earth, the crystal vault will glow with a steady, clear, greenish-gold radiance, hanging in the mist like a solid block of amber, before dimming gently into night so the Damp can climb.

***

One last thing the Power did, and this was not for survival, nor for engineering. It was simply a gift from one vast, listening mind to another.

The Damp knew the entire forty-by-forty cube as a single chord. It knew the cool acoustic shadow of a grazing amphipod, the plucked-harp hum of a crane fly’s leg, the heavy, scraping bass-note of the crayfish at the drains. Its world was rich, warm, and complete, yet the chord never changed its key.

Deep in the center of the floor, directly beneath the four-inch puddle where the Faraday waves cross-hatched the water in their perpetual diamond grid, the Power hollowed out seven tiny, unequal pockets in the travertine—no larger than thimbles. 

When the water temperature shifted by a tenth of a degree between noon and midnight, or when the barometric pressure of the world far above pushed ever so slightly against the water in the upper spillways, tiny bubbles of dissolved gas would nucleate in those seven pockets, grow until they reached the lip of the stone, and release.

*Plink.*
*Plink.*

To a human ear inside that room, the sound would have been completely inaudible, drowned ten thousand times over by the deafening roar of the turbines. 

But to the Damp—resting its flat, ton-heavy body across the floor, its millions of aligned spicules tuned to every microscopic phase-shift in the water—those tiny, rising bubbles were like silver bells struck inside a cathedral of brass. 

They did not happen on a schedule. They rose in slow, shifting polyrhythms dictated by the weather of a sky the Damp would never see, and the pull of a moon it would never know existed. A tiny, clear arpeggio of phase-ripples expanding across the fifty-hertz grid, rolling through the creature’s protoplasmic mantle like a cool breeze across a warm back. 

The Damp did not try to eat the bubbles. It did not freeze them or smother them. 

Instead, during the warm, golden afternoons, it began to arrange its thickest, most sensitive veins in loose, lazy spirals around the center of the puddle. While the amphipods grazed on its margins and the ferns shivered on the walls, the ruler of the room simply lay in the warm water and listened to the music, turning the endless roar of its world into a song.

And the Power, seeing that nothing more was needed, withdrew from the room, leaving the doorless cube to its small eternity.
