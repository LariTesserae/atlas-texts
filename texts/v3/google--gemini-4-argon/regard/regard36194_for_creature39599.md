A lesser power, looking down into the cathedral-sized sinkhole, would have seen a wound in the world and tried to heal it. 

A lesser power—driven by a clumsy, sunlit morality—would have pitied the blindness of the Wedged, the cold sweat of the walls, the claustrophobic agony of matter grinding through matter. It would have reached down with a hand of warm, golden law to untangle the settlement. It would have sorted the overlapping coordinates, snapped the frosted metal pillars and gray ceramic blocks into neat, polite adjacency, and cleared wide, Euclidean plazas where the wind could blow. It would have banished the flat, Tuesday-gray ceiling and given them a proper sky, with a sun that rose and set, and rain that fell cleanly from clouds.

And in doing so, it would have murdered everything in the basin. The sudden vacuum of un-collided space would have decompressed the Wedged like deep-sea fish dragged to the surface; the cessation of thermodynamic friction would have frozen their mercury-dense blood; the parchment hares would have been trapped on one side of a newly uncompromising wall, starving in the light.

Fortunately, the power that found the sinkhole was not only benevolent, but wise. 

It did not arrive as a bolt of light or an avatar walking the narrow, shoulder-width alleys. To walk the alleys would be to privilege the air over the stone, and the power understood immediately that in this place, the air was merely the waste space between the real world. Instead, the power descended into the basin as a subtle, compassionate revision of the local math. It entered the sinkhole as an invisible, fourth-dimensional oil.

It observed the trajectory of the settlement and saw two quiet tragedies unfolding in slow motion. 

The first was a simple problem of volume. The sinkhole was finite; the extrusion was endless. At millimeters per hour, over epochs, the basin was filling up. Already, the lowest levels were calcified knots of dead stone and fused elders. Eventually, the rising basalt and telescoping stairs would pack the nave entirely solid, climbing until they hit the flat plane of pearl-gray light twenty meters above, crushing the last of the flickering intersections into a single, inert, over-compiled monolith. The glitches would resolve by sheer suffocation, and the cold would win.

The second tragedy was the silence of the elders. When an ancient Wedged grew too heavy with silicate and slate, and finally locked itself into a deep intersection to become part of the architecture, it did not entirely cease to feel. Its fluid-filled cranial sinuses calcified, yes, and its shutter-ribs fused with the ribbed conduits, but a faint, trapped awareness remained in the stone—an eternal, static claustrophobia, holding up a lintel that led nowhere, cut off from the shifting, sliding warmth of its living kin.

The power considered this for a long time—Perhaps for the length of time it took a single heavy drop of water to stretch from a cantilevered beam, detach, and curve sideways into a humming wall.

Then, the power made three gentle edits to the sinkhole.

First, it addressed the ceiling. It did not break the flat plane of pearl-gray daylight, nor did it change its neutral, shadowless luminosity. Instead, it decoupled the ceiling from the fixed geometry of the surrounding flatland and tied it to the mathematical mass of the settlement below. Now, for every millimeter of basalt and spun silicate that extruded from the floor, the ceiling rose by a millimeter and a half. The steep walls of the sinkhole were given leave to quietly deepen and widen in proportion, their outer boundaries receding into an infinite, folded pocket of space that took up no extra room in the outside world. The settlement could grow forever, packing itself with impossible towers and blind staircases, and it would never choke. There would always be room for new glitches.

Second, the power touched the mechanics of the overlap. Previously, when a metal buttress and a ceramic block occupied the same coordinates, their flickering was an *argument*—a harsh, abrasive competition for dominance that threatened to shatter the local geometry unless the Wedged intervened to take the brunt of the pressure on their own slick, flat bones. The power reached into the sub-audible thrumming of the walls and tuned it down by a fraction of a hertz, shifting the dissonance into a chord. 

The friction did not disappear—the Wedged and the pale lichen still needed the heat—but the sharp, jagged edges of the uncompiled math were rounded off. Now, when five of the Intercalated converged inside a high-pressure node, pressing their greasy, tallow-like skins and clicking bone-plates into the same cubic meter of stone and steel, the alignment did not require hours of exhausting, rigid straining. The spatial load yielded to them like warm clay. The heat they drank from the seam was richer, carrying trace notes of copper and complex silicates that nourished their fibrous internal roots without stiffening their joints quite so quickly. The realignment became less like holding up a collapsing mine shaft, and more like a slow, communal stretching of limbs in a warm bath.

The third gift was the smallest, and the deepest. 

The power sank to the very bottom of the basin, down to the tessellated hex-plates where the congestion was thickest and the hum made the air itself vibrate. Here stood the calcified elders, fused forever into the foundations, their three-fingered trowel-hands locked into the masonry, sweating viscous drops of half-frozen water. 

The power did not free them; to tear them from the stone now would be to shatter them. Instead, it wove their petrified nervous systems into the living grain of the settlement’s extrusion. 

Every rising plinth, every unfolding slate stairway, every ribbed conduit that grew upward from the floor now carried a resonant frequency from the elders at the base. The entire architecture of the sinkhole became a single, vast, low-frequency seismograph. 

When an elder fused with the bottom of the world, it did not go dark and trapped. Its awareness expanded outward into the stone. Through the rising walls, the elders could now feel the slow, sliding weight of their living descendants index-shifting three hundred feet above. They could feel the tiny, deliberate footsteps of the parchment-and-bone hares; they could feel the gentle *smack* of a Wedged catching a lintel after riding a sideways gravity well; they could feel the warm, soothing release of pressure whenever a knot of colliding towers was eased back into a gentle pulse.

And the living Wedged, pressing their eyeless, drum-tight skulls against the damp walls to listen to the stone, heard something new beneath the *tick* of cooling metal and the *plop* of the heavy water. They heard a deep, slow, comforting resonance rising from the foundations—the steady, patient breathing of their ancestors, holding the world up, guiding the extrusion toward newer, warmer, and ever more intricate intersections.

The pearl-gray light did not flicker. The air remained biting and smelled of wet slate and ozone. To an outside observer, standing in a two-foot-wide alley, nothing had changed at all. 

The benevolent power lingered just long enough to watch a solitary Wedged—half-submerged in a frosted steel pillar, its pale hand protruding from a ceramic block to stroke a patch of newly lush, translucent moss—tilt its narrow head against the seam, and rest there, entirely at peace in the stone. Then, leaving no footprints in the air, the power departed.
