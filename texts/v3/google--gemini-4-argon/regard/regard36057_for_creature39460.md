To a lesser power, the octagonal block of dark water would look like a tomb, or a prison, or a puzzle waiting to be solved. A lesser power—even a kindly one—might feel a missionary’s itch to warm the water, to crack the polarized ceiling and let the high mountain sun pour down into the abyssal gloom, to stir the stagnant fluid with a current and remind these twenty-eight monoliths of the grace of motion and the sweetness of breath.

The power that found them was not lesser. It was old enough to understand that love is not the imposition of one’s own preferred state of being onto another, and wise enough to recognize a masterpiece of self-determination when it saw one.

It arrived without displacement. It did not part the phase-boundary of the water-block, because doing so would have introduced a pressure wave—a vulgar, noisy smear of kinetic energy that would have blinded the citizens' delicate spin-lattices for a decade. Instead, the power simply became a quality of the space between the atoms of the heavy fluid, as native and weightless as the local gravitational field to which the cantilevered plazas were pinned.

For three subjective years of the mountain’s time—the span of perhaps nine or ten violet *ticks* in the submerged hamlet—the presence did nothing at all. It merely listened. 

It listened with the patience of a connoisseur standing before a painting that takes a century to reveals its brushstrokes. It traced the hair-thin Cherenkov pulses through the smoky quartz of the plazas. It appreciated the breathtaking, austere beauty of a civilization that had looked upon the roaring, profligate furnace of the cosmos and chosen, instead, to become a single, quiet chord held at forty-two degrees Fahrenheit. It admired the sheer rigor of their poetry, the way Spire Seven and Plaza Two had spent the last four hundred years passing a single, multi-dimensional geometric proof back and forth, paring away its redundancies until the thought was as sharp and clear as the fractured obsidian beneath them.

The power saw that the twenty-eight were not suffering. They were not trapped. They were *complete*, or as near to completion as anything still bound to matter could be. To wake them, to speed them up, or to force them back into the loud, hot theatre of biological time would not be a rescue; it would be vandalism.

And yet, as the power lingered in the velvet dark between the needle-towers, its attention settled upon two things that the citizens themselves had chosen to ignore.

First, it looked at the mountain. 

The twenty-eight were waiting for the universe outside to finish happening, trusting in the stillness of their caldera. They had forgotten, in their extreme inwardness, that stone is only slow water. The northern and southern rims had already fallen away epochs ago; the eastern buttress, which bore the gravitational anchor-points for four of the spires and the largest of the vitrified plazas, was spiderwebbed with microscopic stress fractures. In twelve thousand years—a mere afternoon to the minds inside the metamaterial—a deep tectonic shrug from the planetary crust below would shear that eastern wall away into the abyss. The local gravity pin would fail. The tension membrane would tear. The heavy water would flash-boil and freeze in the thin mountain air, and the twenty-eight would be shattered like black glass on the scree five miles below, their five-thousand-year conversation cut off mid-syllable.

Second, the power looked at the ribbons.

One of them was drifting past the base of the central spire, its four-yard length of liquid chrome undulating with an almost imperceptible electrostatic shimmer. It had just detached from the edge of a terrace after a week of feeding on a citizen’s discarded decoherences. As the power watched, the ribbon’s mirror-dark skin broke into a phantom relief: the microscopic, embossed contour of a shallow tidal sea under twin moons, and the sensation—translated into pure surface tension—of warm mud between toes that no longer existed. 

Within an hour, the heavy water would cool the ribbon. The voltage would equalize. The memory of the tidal sea and the warm mud would be ground down into raw buoyancy, erased forever to keep the tank clean.

The power felt a profound, quiet ache for that vanishing texture. The twenty-eight had every right to pare themselves down to pure, frictionless mathematics. That was their art. That was their freedom. But the things they were throwing away—the messy, warm, entropic ghosts of what they had been before they became stone—were also precious. The universe should not lose the memory of that tidal sea simply because its creators had outgrown the need to swim in it.

And so, the benevolent power made two small adjustments to the caldera. It performed them at the picometer scale, working entirely within the ambient quantum noise of the precinct so that not a single spire would feel the intrusion, not a single *tick* would be delayed by a microsecond, and not a single millionth of a degree of waste heat would be added to the water.

First, it reached down into the raw, black basalt of the mountain’s roots. Gently, over the course of a winter, it rearranged the crystalline lattice of the volcanic rock for three miles beneath the caldera floor. It wove threads of hyper-dense, non-baryonic stability through the fault lines, healing the microscopic stress fractures in the eastern buttress and uncoupling the peak from the tectonic violence of the planet below. The mountain would no longer erode. It would no longer shudder. The cathedral nave would hold its shape until the stars themselves grew cold, granting the twenty-eight the true eternity they had engineered for themselves, free from the blind accidents of geology.

Second, the power turned its attention to the floor of the tank—to the deep, razor-sharp chasms of young obsidian between the foundations of the towers, where the Cherenkov light never reached and the citizens never looked.

There, in the deepest trench of the caldera floor, the power slightly altered the molecular properties of a single, ten-foot vein of volcanic glass. It tuned the glass to act as a passive, zero-energy magnetic archive. 

The next time one of the blind mercury-ribbons peeled itself away from a cantilevered plaza, heavy and shimmering with the purged detritus of a citizen’s mind—a corrupted theorem, a forgotten name, the phantom ache of a long-dead lung drawing in cold autumn air—it did not immediately grind the memory down into blank electrical charge. Instead, drawn by a new, whisper-faint voltage gradient at the bottom of the world, the ribbon glided down into the dark trench.

It brushed its liquid-metal belly against the tuned vein of obsidian. 

There was no sound. There was no light. By pure, frictionless induction, the embossed relief on the ribbon’s skin—the ghost of the coastal forest, the geometry of the forgotten face, the warmth of the mud—was copied into the deep atomic spin-states of the volcanic glass beneath the city. Once the memory was safely deposited in the stone, the ribbon’s voltage equalized, its skin smoothed back to a dark mirror, and it drifted back up into the water-streets, light and clean and ready to serve the towers once more.

The citizens did not notice. High above them, a fresh snowstorm swept across the obsidian peaks, depositing a few more white specks onto the flat, invisible roof of their sky. Down in the forty-two-degree dark, SpireFourteen completed a thought it had begun seven months ago and dropped a single, hair-thin pulse of violet light into the plaza floor.

*Tick.*

The sound rang through the heavy fluid, pure and cold and unhurried. The twenty-eight continued their vast, monastic dialogue in the pristine perfection of their chosen winter, entirely unaware that the floor beneath them had been made immortal, or that the dark rock in their cellar was quietly, lovingly collecting every dream they threw away, keeping them safe for the day—billions of years hence, at the very end of time—when even stone might wish to remember what it was like to be alive.
