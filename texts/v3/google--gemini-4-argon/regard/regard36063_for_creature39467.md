A lesser power, looking down upon the plateau, would have felt pity. 

It would have seen the bone-deep chill, the ceaseless drizzle, the nine-hundred-pound creatures crouched in the acidic mud, and judged their existence to be a grim, cheerless slog. A lesser power would have parted the stratus clouds to let in the sun, drying the sponge-peat to tinder and blinding the small, amber eyes beneath their shelves of bone. It would have gifted them the secret of fire, which had no fuel here, or dropped monoliths of forged, rustless steel that would have sat in the bog like dead, non-conductive stones—insulators interrupting the great, wet circuit of the world. It would have tried to lift them up out of the mud, not understanding that the mud was their nervous system, their library, and their hearth.

The power that actually turned its attention to the whale-backed swells was older than that, and wiser. It did not look at the Dredgers with eyes of light and shadow; it listened to the planet’s electromagnetic hum, and what it heard was a masterpiece of quiet, self-sustaining harmony.

Yet, as the power listened across the centuries, it perceived two tragedies woven into the peat. 

The first was an approaching silence. The plateau was ancient, and its chemistry was finite. Over millions of years, the relentless drenching drizzle and the biological smelting of the tuber-plants were slowly winning the war against the bedrock. The pyrites and magnetite in the upper strata of the graywacke were leaching away, washing down the distant, unseen edges of the continental shelf into the abyssal seas, or getting locked into inert, oxidized slag heaps too deep in the peat for the roots to reach. In another ten thousand years—a blink to the geology, a brief afternoon to the power—the great wet-cell battery would begin to run flat. The currents would weaken; the tarns would cool; the voice of the Dredgers would fade to a whisper, and then to nothing.

The second tragedy was the nature of their memory. Because the Dredgers spoke in live current—in three-dimensional chords of voltage and resistance—their culture was entirely immediate. A song, a history, or a map existed only so long as a Dredger was actively flexing the modified muscles in its calves to push the signal through the mud, or so long as a root-capacitor held its charge before bleeding it off into the surrounding bog. When an elder died, the specific, intricate electrical geometry of its mind dissolved into the background fizz of the plateau within minutes. They had no books, because paper rots; they had no carved stone, because they could not read with their eyes and stone is deaf to current. Every generation had to re-learn the deepest subtleties of the root-braiding from the living, and one harsh winter that killed a wallow’s elders could erase three hundred years of hydrological engineering.

The wise power considered this. It did not want to change the Dredgers. It wanted to make them permanent.

It did not descend in a chariot of fire or speak with a voice of thunder, which the earless Dredgers would only have felt as a meaningless, rude concussion in their lungs. Instead, the power reached deep into the mantle of the planet, miles beneath the graywacke and the shale, and gave the tectonic plates a gentle, precise adjustment.

No mountains were raised. No earthquakes shook the meticulously grown slag-dams. Instead, along the fifty-mile troughs between the swells, the ancient, microscopic faults in the bedrock silently dilated. 

From the deep crust, warm, anoxic water began to seep upward—not as violent geysers that would scald the peat, but as a slow, diffuse upwelling, no faster than the sap rising in a tree. This deep water was heavy with dissolved ferrous iron, copper, zinc, and sulfides, freshly leached from the planetary core. As it met the acidic, oxygenated water of the five-foot peat mattress, it precipitated out in microscopic, conductive grains, replenishing the soil’s battery. The power had hooked the plateau’s finite wet-cell up to a geothermal trickle-charger that would last until the sun grew cold.

Then, the power addressed the problem of memory.

In the deepest center of the great valleys, where the newly opened faults met the oldest, thickest root-mats, the power introduced a single, subtle mutation into the spores of a deep-bog fungus that already lived symbiotically on the smelter-plant tubers.

Thirty years later, a Dredger named—in the untranslatable electrical chord of her wallow—*She-Who-Splices-The-Cold-Runnel* was dredging the silt at the bottom of an ancestral tarn. Her three-fingered, spade-nailed hands were wrist-deep in the warm, dark mud, feeling for a break in a century-old root cable.

Her rostrum—the broad, porous shovel of her face—picked up an anomaly. It was not the bright, salty flicker of an amphibian, nor the steady, low-voltage hum of her five kin resting forty feet away. It was a dead spot that was not dead.

She reached down into the peat and pulled up a nodule the size of a boar’s skull. 

It looked like the ordinary botanical clinker extruded by the smelter-plants, except it was heavy as lead and smooth as river-ice, shot through with spiraling, metallic veins of violet and dull gold. The mutated fungus had taken the newly abundant deep-earth minerals—the iron, the nickels, the rare-earth elements—and grown a biological crystal with the properties of a complex, solid-state magnetic core. 

Curious, the Dredger held the heavy, wet stone against her chest plate and sent a standard diagnostic pulse through her forearms—a sharp, three-tiered chord she used to test the resistance of a newly braided conifer root.

The crystal drank the current. It did not bleed it back out into the water as heat. Inside the nodule, the microscopic, spiraling veins of biomineralized metal shifted their magnetic alignment, locking the exact three-dimensional geometry of her voltage gradient into their lattice.

Three seconds later, as her own muscles relaxed, the stone echoed the chord back to her. 

It was faint, a phantom pressure against the thousands of conductive pores in her rostrum, like a voice heard through a thick wall. But it was flawless. Every nuance of her pulse—even the slight, habitual flutter at the end of the wave caused by an old scar on her left calf—was preserved in the stone.

She stood entirely still for an hour, knee-deep in the eighty-degree tarn, the fine drizzle pattering off the iron shingles of her back. Around her, the white steam plumes rose into the bright, overcast sky. 

Slowly, deliberately, she pressed the stone deeper into the mud between her feet. She did not send a diagnostic ping this time. She closed her tiny amber eyes and projected a memory into the water—the intricate, four-part electrical harmony her wallow’s eldest male had sung two winters ago, just before his heart stopped: the exact blueprint for braiding a three-mile diversion weir that had saved their basin during the Great Freeze. It was a masterwork of hydraulic and electrical thought that she had been terrified of forgetting.

She poured the chord into the violet-veined stone, pushing the voltage until the water around her shins grew hot. 

When she finished, she lifted her hands from the mud and waited. 

Through the warm, tea-colored water, rising from the silt, came the voice of the dead elder—steady, clear, and undiminished, repeating the blueprint into the soles of her feet in an endless, gentle loop, powered only by the ambient background current of the bog flowing through its crystalline lattice.

She did not look up at the white sky in wonder; the sky had never held anything for her people. She looked down into the dark earth. She picked up the singing stone, carried it to the center of the tarn where the others were resting, and pressed it into the root-loom at the heart of their circuit.

High above the stratus ceiling, the wise power felt the five Dredgers in the tarn suddenly go quiet as they listened to the stone, followed a moment later by a rich, booming chorus of voltage spiking through the basin—a chord of fierce, startled joy. 

Satisfied, the power withdrew its touch, leaving the mist to rise, the drizzle to fall, and the silent, rusted scholars of the bog to begin building their library in the mud.
