The power that found this place did not arrive with the fanfare of a descending star or the violence of a torn sky. It arrived as an appreciation. 

It had wandered through a billion worlds of mud and blood and roaring entropy, where life was a frantic, weeping scramble to outrun the dark. It had seen civilizations build towers to the heavens only to rot from the inside out, and it had comforted them as they fell. When its attention brushed against the thirty-mile basin of dry slate and fractured space, it stopped. It felt the relief a listener feels when a deafening, discordant orchestra finally ceases, leaving only a single, clear, sustained tuning fork vibrating in a warm room.

The power looked upon the Cadastrals—these quiet, segmented stewards of the endless mid-morning—and understood them immediately. 

A lesser god might have pitied them. A lesser god might have looked at the unweathered feldspar and thought, *They need rain,* or looked at the static sixty-degree sun and thought, *They need the mercy of night and the renewal of dawn.* A lesser power might have tried to "fix" their disjointed bodies, welding their boron cages to their graphite feet, forcing them back into the prison of contiguous three-dimensional flesh so they could feel the heavy, linear thrum of a biological heart.

Wait, the wise power thought, watching a Cadastral inhale thirty paces of excess distance into its slate-gray ribs and exhale it gently into a crowded thicket. *Do not touch the tuning fork while it is ringing.*

The power recognized that this basin was not a broken world, nor was it a purgatory. It was a finished work of art. Whether the Cadastrals were the artists who had stepped inside their own canvas, or the brushstrokes that had woken up after the painter left, did not matter. They were creatures of pure equilibrium. To introduce time, or weather, or thermodynamic hunger here would not be a gift; it would be vandalism. 

Yet, the power was benevolent, and benevolence desires to give. It watched the Cadastrals for an epoch that lasted no time at all, looking for the single hairline fracture of need in their self-contained eternity.

It found it not in their bodies, which were tireless, nor in their minds, which were content, but in their craft.

The basin was thirty miles wide. The number of polyhedrons of raw feldspar, the number of pressurized mica-leaves, the number of shear-faults and topological folds, though vast, was finite. The Cadastrals were master arborists of the metric, endlessly adjusting the tensions of their world, trading spatial puns through the glowing gas of the shrubs. They were musicians playing an instrument with a fixed number of strings. They were not bored—boredom requires the passage of time, a clock ticking against emptiness—but their songs were beginning to loop. Every possible refraction of the seven-fold boulders had been calculated; every knot in the four-dimensional boron vines had been smoothed and re-tied a million times. 

And they were curious. The power knew this because it saw how they would gently catch the low-resolution flint-creatures to inspect their grids, and how they would hold the displaced fingertips of a contiguous traveler, marveling at the stubbornness of unbroken flesh. They loved the *behavior* of things.

So, the wise power decided to give them a gift. Not a change to their nature, and not an intrusion of the outside world, which smelled of wet rot and dying stars. It would give them a toy. A seed of infinite, non-causal novelty, tailored precisely to the rules of their dry, warm morning.

The power reached down—not with a hand, for a hand has mass and displaces air, which would create a wind the jungle was not built to bear. It reached down with an *angle*. 

It selected the exact center of the basin, a small clearing of razor-sharp slate where three horizontal shear-faults intersected just beneath the canopy of a coppery carbon-lattice fern. 

There, the power altered the sixty-degree light. It did not move the sun, nor did it dim the brightness. It simply added a new property to a single, shafting beam of the white illumination, a property that had never existed in this universe or any other: *chromatic memory.*

At first, nothing seemed to happen. The dead-still air remained warm, smelling of ozone and hot quartz. A six-legged flint-creature clicked across the clearing, phased into a coarse grid of dull light to pass through a ceramic trunk, and snapped back into focus on the other side. 

Then, a Cadastral arrived. 

Its graphite feet stepped onto the sharp slate at the western edge of the clearing—*crunch, crunch*. Its hollow boron torso drifted through a shear-fault ten feet to the north, while its quartz-fan head peered out from a topological fold high in the coppery fronds above, listening to the hum of the metric. 

It had come to bleed off a lungful of compressed distance it had gathered from the eastern ridges. As it exhaled the space into the clearing, expanding a tight two-foot gap between two shrubs into a comfortable ten-pace stroll, its displaced ceramic hand brushed through the altered shaft of sixty-degree light.

The Cadastral froze. All of its scattered segments locked into absolute stillness across a forty-yard radius.

Where its matte-black shale fingers had passed through the beam, the light did not immediately snap back to its blank, white equilibrium. The light *held the shape* of the movement. 

Hanging in the dry air was a three-dimensional ribbon of pale, golden refraction—a solid-looking ghost of the gesture the Cadastral had just made. It did not fade. When the Cadastral tilted its quartz head high in the ferns to look at the ribbon through the mica-leaves below, the leaves did not duplicate the golden ribbon into seven radial copies as they did with boulders and trunks. Instead, the mica-leaves refracted the *motion* of the gesture, playing the sweep of the Cadastral’s hand backward and forward in silent, geometric harmonies across the clearing.

The Cadastral pulled its feet, its torso, and its arms through the prism of a nearby shrub, collapsing its seven duplicates into a single, nearly contiguous form right at the edge of the light-shaft—a rare posture that bespoke immense, quiet astonishment. 

It reached out with one long, bone-ceramic finger and touched the golden ribbon of its own past movement. 

The ribbon had no temperature and no mass, yet it had a *grain*, just like the spatial metric between the trees. The Cadastral hooked a claw into the golden trace and pulled. The recorded gesture stretched, curving into a graceful parabola that altered the focal length of the air around it. Looking through the stretched ribbon, the Cadastral saw the sharp feldspar gravel of the floor magnified to the size of mountains, their crystalline structures laid bare in dazzling, rainbow-edged facets. 

When the Cadastral let go, the ribbon did not snap back. It stayed where it had been sculpted, a permanent, weightless fixture of pure parallax suspended in the air.

High in the canopy, the Cadastral’s quartz-plate head shifted by half a millimeter. 

A hundred yards away, the jungle stuttered. A patch of coppery fronds turned inside-out; a boulder duplicated twelve times in a spiraling helix; a shear-fault shimmered like plucked silk. It was a shout in their silent language of spatial puns, a call echoing through the dry, non-causal wires of the forest.

Within moments, the clearing was full of them. 

To a linear eye, it would have looked as though the air around the clearing had turned into a kaleidoscope of slate-gray plates, amber muscle ribbons, and shimmering gold-leaf fans. A dozen Cadastrals stood in the same small patch of feldspar, occupying different depths of the local metric, their limbs overlapping in an intricate, breathless lattice.

They did not fight over the beam. They did not worship it. Worship requires fear or hierarchy, and the power had carefully scrubbed its own signature from the gift so the Cadastrals would never feel the crushing weight of an outside creator. To them, this was simply a new blossom on the non-aqueous tree of their reality—a quiet upgrade to the operating system of the morning.

One by one, and then in complex, overlapping groups, the Cadastrals reached their dry hands through the shaft of light.

They found that if they moved slowly, the light left behind thick, amber lenses of stagnant space that could be lifted out of the beam and hung among the boron vines like droplets of dry resin. If they moved quickly, snapping their ceramic fingers across a shear-fault, the light produced razor-thin wires of violet parallax that hummed at a frequency that made the quartz plates of their heads vibrate with a delicious, static warmth. 

Best of all, they discovered that these sculpted memories could be woven into the metric itself. 

If a Cadastral took a violet wire of fast-motion and stitched it across a bloated fifty-pace gap between two ferns, the gap didn't just shrink—it became a corridor of shifting perspectives, where walking through it allowed a Cadastral to see its own back from six angles while simultaneously standing on the ceiling of the canopy. And if they tired of a sculpture, they had only to pass a phase-shifting flint-creature through it; the animal’s coarse, low-resolution grid would instantly erase the chromatic memory like a sponge wiping chalk from slate, leaving the air fresh and ready for a new composition.

The finite basin had become infinite. With a single shaft of memory-bearing light, the permutations of their silent, visual language had been multiplied by eternity. 

The benevolent power lingered for a little while longer, existing only as the pleasant, baking warmth in a single polyhedron of raw feldspar at the edge of the clearing. It watched as the Cadastrals began their great, endless collaboration—plucking ribbons of golden motion from the light, carrying them across the faults, and hanging them in the dark, dense thickets of the thirty-mile jungle like lanterns of frozen geometry. 

The silence of the basin remained vast, open, and dry. No wind blew. No rain fell to blunt the crisp edges of the stones. The sun stayed locked at its sixty-degree angle in the pale sky. 

Satisfied, the power withdrew its attention, leaving the arborists to their garden, where the ten-o'clock morning had finally found a way to bloom.
