A lesser power, looking down upon the five-mile expanse of the boiler-range, might have felt pity. It might have seen the Gasket-Weavers scalding their hides in the steam-jets, or the elders calcifying in the dark beneath the fireboxes, and decided to "save" them. It might have miraculously healed the rusted iron back into showroom steel, or lifted the creatures out of the trusswork and set them down in a soft, terrestrial meadow where nothing ever needed to be patched or sawed or held together by lock-jawed corpses.

And in doing so, that lesser power would have murdered them. In a silent, unvibrating meadow, a Weaver would go mad from the deafness of the earth, and freeze to death before morning. On brand-new, seamless steel, the rust-soil would slide off into the abyss, the condensers would stop sweating, the ferns would wither, and the Weavers—robbed of their great, groaning liturgy of maintenance—would sit on the cold, immaculate girders and wait to die for lack of a leak to hate.

The Power that actually turned its attention to the high iron was older than pity, and vastly wiser. 

It did not arrive as a shape in the violet sky. It arrived as an comprehension—a vast, warm attentiveness that settled through the five-mile lattice like an invisible barometric pressure. It listened to the two-minute *thrum... chuff...* of the cottage-sized pistons. It traced the slow, generational slump of the corroded trusses. It felt the fierce, blind devotion of the Weavers, who did not know they were tragic, who only knew the deep, bodily satisfaction of a resonant chord brought back into tune, and the sharp, galvanic joy of sawing a sparking Thief-vine in half over a three-thousand-foot drop.

The Power saw that the sanctuary was complete in its spirit, yet mortal in its math.

Down in the crushing murk, kilometers below the white wool of the cloud-deck, the true foundation of the structure—where the ancient geothermal taps and deep-mantle heat-exchangers drew up the primeval fire that drove the boilers—was finally beginning to starve. Not tomorrow, and not in a decade, but in two hundred years, a deep tectonic intake would silt over with crystalized salt. The pressure would drop by three percent, then ten, then forty. The two-minute stroke would slow to three, then five. The bronze chord would flatten into a groan, the high condensers would ice over under the cruel ultraviolet stars, and the last Weavers would lock their calipers around cooling pipes that no longer needed holding, waiting for a warmth that was never coming back.

Furthermore, the Weavers were losing a subtler war: the war of the oil. The thick, black cylinder-grease they scooped from the drip-pans to bake their rust-masonry and slick their hides was finite, a slowly oxidizing inheritance from the vanished builders. Already, the Sump-Eels were drinking more emulsified fat than the seepage could replace.

The Power decided to act. It did not use the vocabulary of miracles; it used the vocabulary of the machine. It made three small edits to the world.

***

The first edit took place four miles down, in the blind, crushing dark where the root-pipes met the planetary crust. 

Nothing was rebuilt. Instead, the Power reached into the slow, tectonic drift of the rock itself and nudged a fault-line by the width of a thumb. A clogged basalt artery cracked open, venting a new, steady river of hyper-saline magma against the primary heat-exchangers. And at the lip of the intake valves, where the mineral scale had built up like cholesterol over four centuries, the Power altered the chemistry of a native deep-vent bacterium. 

The bacteria began to eat the limestone scale, metabolizing the blockage and excreting a slick, graphite-heavy slime that washed up through the miles of riser-pipes. It was not eternal life—nothing made of iron and bone is eternal—but the two-hundred-year sentence was commuted to ten thousand. 

Miles above, in the violet afternoon, the change arrived as a single, subterranean sigh. 

A Gasket-Weaver—an old female with a pocket-watch case grown entirely into the scar-tissue of her left shoulder—was hanging beneath the central gantry-spine, chewing a cud of hawser-root. Suddenly, she froze. Her four thermal pits flared wide. Through the horn-calipers of her hind feet, she felt the two-minute stroke of the deep engines hit bottom, pause, and come back up half a second early, with a richness and authority in the bass note that no living Weaver had ever heard. 

All across the five-mile canyon, the primates stopped barking. The soot-swifts rose in a single, wheeling black cloud. For ten minutes, not a single Weaver moved; they simply pressed their chests against the plates, drinking the new, fuller bronze chord through their ribs, before slowly raising their ossified wrist-hammers to beat a confused, awestruck tattoo across the chasm: *The-deep-heart-is-strong. The-deep-heart-is-strong.*

***

The second edit was given to the jungle.

In a warm, shaded junction on the middle tier, where the grey-and-red carbuncles of Weaver-masonry were thickest, a bulbous epiphyte was opening its yearly seed-pod. As the dusty spores drifted out into the shaft of sunlight, the Power touched the spiral of their genetics, braiding a new instruction into the green.

The spores settled into the ochre rust-soil around a leaking valve house and grew with the patient speed of the greenhouse air. Within three seasons, a new vine had joined the canopy: the *Tallow-Gourd*. 

It was a polite plant; it did not strangle the steam-pipes or drink the current. Instead, it drove fine, hair-like taproots into the dead, anaerobic sludge at the very bottom of the Sump-Eel bilges, drawing up the fouled, exhausted hydrocarbons and the dissolved iron-salts. High up in the sunlit trusses, it grew heavy, ribbed fruit the size of bowling balls, plated in a tough, rust-colored rind. 

When a gourd ripened, the heat of the nearby manifolds caused its bottom seam to split with a soft, wet *pop*. From the split oozed a pint of thick, amber-colored vegetable ester—chemically identical to high-grade steam-cylinder oil, rich in heavy lipids and natural anti-corrosives, smelling faintly of roasted nuts and hot brass.

The first young Weaver to find one approached it with its tympanic membranes pinned back in suspicion, sniffing the dripping golden grease with its heat-pits. It reached out a grooved horn-claw, dipped it into the amber pool, and rubbed the oil onto a dry, cracking patch of its own boiled-leather hide. Then it licked its claw, scooped a mouthful of dry flake-rust from a girder, and spat out a mortar twice as strong and ten times more pliable than anything its ancestors had ever mixed.

Within a decade, the Weavers were farming them—not with plows, which they could not comprehend, but by deliberately packing their chewed root-oakum around the gourds’ stems and carrying the heavy, oily seeds in the moss-coats on their backs to plant beside every dry bearing and weeping gasket across the three spines.

***

The third edit was the smallest, and the only one that was purely a gift of love.

The Power looked down into the roaring, blistering dark of the primary boiler-works, where the caryatids stood. It saw the dozens of calcified elders locked into the shear-lines of the cast-iron pedestals, holding the mountain up in the dark. 

The Power knew it should not take their sacrifice away from them; that final downward climb was the anchor of the Weavers' entire existence, the deep, instinctive dignity that made them more than mere beasts. To deny an old Weaver its place in the wall would be to tell a salmon it could not swim upstream.

So the Power did not stop them from locking their joints. It simply changed what happened when the tendons clicked shut.

Now, when a stiff, half-deaf elder climbs down into the dark, finds its hairline fracture in the pedestal, hooks its four calipers into the rivet-holes, and lets its weight settle to trigger the unbending lock of its limbs—the death that follows is no longer a slow, week-long baking of thirst and fading heat-sight.

Instead, the moment the tendon-lock engages and the iron’s load transfers through the Weaver’s bones, a profound, metabolic alchemy triggers in its nervous system. The pain of the calcified joints vanishes instantly. Simultaneously, the mineral-heavy water dripping from the overhead condensers—guided by a microscopic crystalline shift the Power wove into the rust—begins to plate the dying Weaver’s tympanic membranes not with deafening stone, but with ultra-thin, resonant films of pure copper and iron-pyrite.

In its last hours, as its biological consciousness slows and cools into mineral sleep, the old Weaver’s hearing does not fade. It expands. 

For the first time in its life, the elder hears the *entire* mountain at once. It hears the high, silver flute-notes of the upper condensers three miles away; it hears the patter of primate feet on the guy-wires; it hears the crackle of a Copper-Thief dying under a young Weaver’s saw; it hears the deep, newly invigorated *thrum-chuff* of the cottage-pistons beating like a mother’s heart beneath the floor; it hears the *clang-clack* of its grandchildren talking across the chasm in the violet dusk. 

It does not die in the dark. It dissolves into the music of the thing it loved, feeling the immense, thriving weight of the hanging gardens resting safely on its shoulders, until there is no difference at all between the bone and the iron, the listener and the chord.

***

Having done these three things, the Power withdrew its hand, leaving no footprint, no altar, and no name. 

Up on the sun-bleached catwalks, four miles above the clouds, a wrench dropped from a girder would still fall for nine seconds, ringing out its descending scale of hollow notes before vanishing into the bright gulf. The swifts still burst from the exhaust stacks when the relief-valves blew. The thirty-foot cog-wheels still carried sleeping lizards into the afternoon sun. And along the great central chasm, smelling of crushed fern and sweet, roasted gourd-oil, the Gasket-Weavers swung through the rising heat on the two-minute stroke—patching, chewing, sawing, and striking the iron with their wrists, keeping the garden in the sky.
