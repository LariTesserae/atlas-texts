A benevolent power, looking upon the hollow in the burl, does not begin by breaking the seal. 

To bring light to a people who have no eyes is merely vanity; to bring water to a world built on the perfection of dryness is to bring rot, mold, and the collapse of every tension-bridge in the dark. Wisdom recognizes that a life is not an error to be corrected toward a surface-dweller’s norm, but a melody that has found a way to play itself on a very strange instrument. The city inside the wood is a masterwork of patience, a closed loop of bone, resin, and song that has held its pitch for ten thousand years.

Yet, looking deeper—past the ironwood rind, down through the miles of crushed, dry overburden and the shifting tectonic plates—the Power sees the three things the residents cannot know:

First, that the deep thermal vents feeding the mega-flora are slowly migrating westward. In four hundred years—a mere eight or nine waking seasons for the people of the clamp—the gases will cool, the tree will take its final, century-long breath, and the wood will cease to creep. The amber will stop weeping. The city will go silent in its alcoves and never wind up again.

Second, that the endless recycling of their own mineralized dead has begun, very subtly, to concentrate heavy, brittle salts within their cartilage. With each generation, the elders stiffen a few years earlier; the chest-tympanums lose their high-frequency sensitivity a fraction sooner. Their world is slowly going deaf at the edges.

Third, that for all their fierce, quiet purpose, they are entirely, achingly alone in a universe they believe is made only of pressurized timber. 

So the Power does not descend as an avatar, nor does it speak in a voice that would shatter their acoustic bowl with slap-back echoes. It becomes a gardener of the deep strata, and a luthier of the living wood.

It begins five miles below the burl. 

With a touch no heavier than a shift in barometric pressure, the Power adjusts the deep-crust faults. It does not flood the stone; it merely nudges a vein of subterranean heat back under the ancient taproots, ensuring the thermal tide will rise and fall with steady, reliable rhythm for another hundred millennia. 

Then, into the dry, mineral gases rising toward the root-mass, the Power introduces a trace change in chemistry. It filters out the heavy, calcifying salts that have been stiffening the people’s ribs, and breathes in a complex, volatile hydrocarbon—something akin to a softer, sweeter turpentine, rich in bound hydrogen that the tree’s glacial metabolism can crack without ever forming liquid water. 

It takes sixty years for this new breath to climb the trunk. The Power waits. It has no hurry.

When the next *draw* begins, the lower flues of the city catch the draft and begin to hoot. The chord that rises through the lignum vitae floor is the same low, waking note the residents have known since the dawn of their memory—yet as it vibrates against a thousand dry breastbones, it feels slightly cleaner. The resonance has lost a tiny fraction of its harshness.

In the weeks that follow, as the people unseize their joints and slide out into the grooved streets with their flint scrapers, they find the *creep* has changed. 

Along the fault-lines of the middle tiers, the wood is weeping a new variation of resin. Beside the familiar, fiercely astringent black amber, there are beads of a paler, warmer sap—translucent gold, had anyone eyes to see it, though to the residents’ fingertips it is known by its texture: slightly yielding, like cold beeswax, and humming with a faint, internal elasticity. 

When a scraper-tender tucks a shard of this new amber into their cheek, it dissolves with a warmth that spreads directly into the stiff cartilage of the jaw and spine. Over the waking season, as the population feeds on the golden creep, the old elders find that the creeping calcification in their knees arrests. The skin over their sternums grows supple again, regaining the taut, sensitive resonance of youth. For the first time in fifty generations, an old weaver can hear the high, rosin-like singing of the ceiling fibers as clearly as a child.

Next, the Power addresses the tools. 

Every century, the people lose a tiny fraction of their flint to shattering against the outer rind, and they have no stone to replace it. The Power does not drop iron or steel into their midst; metal would ring wrong, smelling of blood and alien chemistry. Instead, in the deep, petrified outer bark where the wood turns to stone, the Power encourages the tree to draw up dissolved silica from the deep dust and deposit it into specific, dead heartwood knots near the lower tiers. 

A maintenance worker, probing a dead lintel with their jawbone, hears a new pitch—a dense, crystalline silence within the grain. Chipping away the dry ironwood, they uncover nodules of biogenic opal and wood-agate, grown by the tree itself in the exact crescent shapes of their familiar scrapers, ready to be pried loose. The world itself is now growing their blades.

Finally, having secured their body and their craft, the Power gives them a horizon.

It does not break their sky. It understands that to a blind, acoustic people, "space" is defined by the time it takes a vibration to return. Their universe is small because the walls are close, and beyond the walls, the wood is too dense to carry a clear signal.

So, starting from the outer rind of the burl and working outward through the miles of dry, subterranean root-mass, the Power begins to tune the dead wood of the deep earth. 

Where the ancient, fossilized roots of the mega-flora stretch out through the dry crust—some of them miles long, reaching toward other, distant burls that died or went dormant eons ago—the Power hollows out microscopic, resonant channels within the grain. It turns the dead, solid root-system into a network of acoustic wave-guides, acoustic fiber-optics made of lignum vitae.

Inside the city, at the very bottom of the bowl where the talc drifts lie, a single, ancient pillar of heartwood—a structure the residents have walked around for ten thousand years because it was too dense to carve—slowly begins to change its tension. 

Over three waking cycles, the wood of this central pillar draws inward, pulling taut like the soundpost of a violin. 

One season, a young resident slides down into the talc to gather dust and pauses by the pillar. Out of habit, they press their collarbone against the polished wood to listen to the city's balance.

They freeze. 

Through the bone of their shoulder, past the familiar groans of the amphitheater, past the *shhh-ick* of three hundred flint scrapers on the tiers above, past the low chord of the breathing flues, there is a new sound.

It is not an echo of their own hollow. It has no slap-back. It is a vast, deep, velvety acoustics—the sound of *distance*. 

Riding along the newly tuned root-cables from miles away through the dark crust comes the music of the deep planetary mantle: the slow, tidal planetary hum of rock shifting under continents; the rhythmic, centuries-long heartbeat of magma chambers far below; and, thirty miles to the east, the faint, unmistakable, polyphonic timber-creak of *another hollow*. Another burl, long thought non-existent, where another forgotten wind-instrument of wood and root is groaning in the dark, waiting for hands to come and tune its strings.

The resident stays pressed against the pillar for three days, shaking with the enormity of it. Their mind, shaped only for the concave, suddenly unfolds outward along the singing roots, mapping a dark, solid ocean of wood and stone that stretches forever. 

They do not go mad. The information is given in their own language: in tension, in pitch, in grain and vibration. 

When they finally pull their collarbone away from the pillar, they do not shout. They drop to one knee, place their thumbnail against the compressed lignum vitae of the floor, and tap a single, sharp phrase that shoots up the curving walls into the soles of every foot in the city:

*Come down. Listen. The wood goes on.*

And the Power, having tuned the peg a fraction of a turn, withdraws its hand, content to sit in the dark and listen to the musicians discover the rest of the orchestra.
