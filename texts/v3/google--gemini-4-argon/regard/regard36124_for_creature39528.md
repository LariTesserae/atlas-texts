To a mind that spans the spaces between stars, or even the breadth of a wild and weather-beaten continent, the first impulse upon finding a closed box is to open it. A lesser benevolence might look upon this roaring, shadowless basin and feel pity for its blind inhabitants, for the endless repetition of their days, for the lack of a breeze or the turning of a season. A lesser wisdom might crack the high, fibrous vault to let in the true sun, or introduce a seed that would grow into a flower, thinking to give the hollow the gift of change.

Wait, says the deeper wisdom. *Listen.*

The attention of the Great Power settles around the buried perimeter of the root-mat, not as a weight, but as an ear pressed to the timber of a ship's hull. It listens to the four-second *thud-hiss-gurgle* of the seven nodes. It feels the dry, rustling tide of the twenty thousand crabs, the bass drumming of the copper beetles, the elastic, borrowed pulse of the ginger leeches high in the rafters. It watches the two Sump-Wardens meet in the warm, central pools, pressing their walnut-scaled flanks together in the roar, sharing the taste of their world until their blind, heavy bodies beat as one.

And the Power understands: this is not a prison. It is a watch that has wound itself for ten thousand years. It is an orchestra playing a single, sustained chord, and every creature in it is both the instrument and the lutist. To introduce a flower here would be to throw a handful of sand into a beating heart. To change the light would be to blind the ones who see with their feet.

Yet, a closed loop, no matter how ancient or how finely tuned, is still mortal in ways its inhabitants cannot perceive. The Wardens can scrape the lime from the vents, and the leeches can melt the salt from the ceiling, and the Tines can sing the silt from the drains. They can maintain the *inside* of the world forever. They know nothing of the *outside*.

So the Power draws its attention outward, passing through the twenty-foot-thick wall of iron-hard, fused wood that seals the hollow from the rest of the earth. 

Here, in the dark rock and deep soil that surrounds the buried organism, the Power finds the true vulnerabilities of the little universe.

Two miles away, a tectonic fault has been slowly accumulating stress over the centuries. In forty years, or fifty, it will slip. To the forest and the mountains above, it will be a minor tremor, a brief shaking of leaves. To the hollow—whose water-conduits are under constant hydraulic pressure, whose ivory Tines are brittle and tuned to a hair's breadth of acoustic perfection, whose ceiling struts are stretched like drumskins—that sharp, lateral shear would be an apocalypse. A cracked main vein would drop the pressure; the leeches would cease to beat; the acoustic resonance would shatter; the Sump-Wardens would listen through their feet to a song gone suddenly, fatally out of tune, and they would not know how to fix a broken drum.

And deeper still, in the subterranean aquifer from which the seven great nodes drink their warm, mineral-heavy broth, the chemistry is slowly shifting. A vein of heavy, toxic sulfates is being leached by the deep groundwater, creeping inch by inch toward the root-intakes of the great plant. 

The Power does not enter the hollow. It does not speak to the Wardens, who have no words, nor does it alter a single drop of the warm sweat running down the oiled-slate grooves of the walls. 

Instead, it goes to work in the dark outside, becoming the Sump-Warden of the mountain itself.

Miles away, deep in the crust, the Power lays a gentle, immutable hand upon the fault line. It does not stop the earth from moving—for the earth, too, has its rhythms—but it bleedingly, silently translates the tension. Over the course of a single afternoon, it turns a future catastrophic snap into a slow, grease-smooth creep, millimeters a year, entirely below the frequency that the hollow’s wooden hull can register. 

Then, the Power turns its attention to the deep water table. Where the toxic vein creeps toward the roots, the Power encourages the growth of a dense, subterranean curtain of extremophile bacteria in the porous stone—a living filter, miles long, that will drink the poison and exhale the exact balance of calcium, silica, and iron that the hollow has relied upon since the last ice age. It adjusts the geothermal vents three leagues down, ensuring the water entering the root-sieves will remain precisely the temperature of skin for another hundred millennia.

Only when the foundations are secured does the Power allow itself a single, microscopic indulgence within the chamber itself.

In the center of the mossy floor, the two Sump-Wardens have just finished their rounds. One has just clipped a tiny notch into the base of an ivory Tine—*tink*—bringing the spire back into a pure, glassy hum. Now, the two heavy, walnut-shelled beasts lumber into the tepid pool and press their broad, blind flanks together for their multi-day synchronization.

They are aging. They do not decay, not like things in the outside world, for the acidic water keeps them clean, and their shells are made of stone and wood. Inside, however, in the slow, rubbery dark of their mantles, the cellular machinery is tired. They do not breed—there is no room for three Wardens in the acoustic balance of the floor, and no margin for the clumsiness of an untaught juvenile who does not know how to step between the wine-glass spires of the Tines. They simply endure, repairing their own tissues as slowly and methodically as they repair the furnace-nodes.

As the two Wardens press together, matching the slow peristaltic waves of their bodies, the Power reaches into the warm water that films between their touching skins. 

It does not give them thoughts. It does not give them eyes. It simply untangles a few million microscopic knots of accumulated cellular fatigue in their ancient flesh, resetting the telomeric clocks of their heavy bodies, smoothing the scarred cartilage around their iron-toothed radulae. 

And for the span of about ten minutes—an eternity of four-second pump cycles—the Power amplifies the resonance between them. 

Through their broad, innervated feet, the two Wardens suddenly feel the hollow with a clarity they have not known since the wood was young. The roar of the seven engines, the twenty-thousand-fold rustle of the crabs, the high glassy singing of the Tines, and the deep, slow heartbeat of the other Warden pressed against their side—all of it locks into a single, blindingly perfect chord of vibration. There is no pain in their joints. The water tastes rich and sweet with iron. The pressure in the ceiling pipes is flawless. 

In the dark, blind nervous system of the two creatures, this translates to the only word their biology knows for joy: *Equilibrium.*

They stay pressed together a little longer than usual, bathing in the deep, resonant certainty that the pipes are clear, the hull is sound, and the world is whole. Then, with a wet, heavy *schluck*, they peel apart and lumber back into the warm, shadowless rain to scrape the vents.

The Power withdraws, sealing the earth behind it, leaving no footprint, no miracle, and no change—only the guarantee that the engine room will keep roaring in the dark, for as long as the stars permit.
