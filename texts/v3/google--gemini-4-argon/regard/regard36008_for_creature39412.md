To a power that has watched stars ignite and cool, the frantic, lung-driven civilizations of the lowlands are often a grief. They burn through their worlds like brushfires, screaming prayers into the smoke, begging to be saved from the physics of their own appetites. To intervene among them is to be forever catching falling glass.

When the attention of the Wise came to rest upon the high plateau, it did not arrive as a voice in the sky or a bush of fire. Fire requires oxygen; a voice requires air that wants to be shaken. Any miracle noisy enough for a man to notice would have shattered the half-inch sheet of water, blinded the mirror-courts with ripples, and suffocated the masons in a froth of useless bubbles.

Instead, the Presence arrived as a quality of the afternoon light—a stillness that recognized a sister stillness and sat down beside it.

For three hundred years—a single long lifetime for the inhabitants, a single breath for the Watcher—the power did nothing at all. It simply read the city. It took immense, quiet pleasure in the grammar of the half-millimeter swells. It listened to the slow, tectonic conversations passing from western terrace to eastern Spillway: *Four hundredweight needed at the northern plinth. The arum broth is thin in the seventh court; shift the chock a fingernail’s breadth to the left.* 

It saw the elders fold themselves into the retaining walls, becoming the very city they had spent three centuries leveling. It saw a people who had not merely adapted to their world, but had become its conscience and its equilibrium.

To give such beings knowledge was redundant; their feet already knew the bedrock down to the magma chamber. To give them ease was an insult; their work was their metabolism, their language, and their prayer. To introduce a single fish, or a bird, or a flower that did not belong, would be to drop a stone into a watch-work.

Yet the Watcher had eyes that reached deeper than the tympanic skin of the masons, and further down the mountain than they could ever glide. Looking into the roots of the plateau, and down into the cloud-choked valleys below, the power saw the three quiet dooms that were coming for the basin.

The first was the wood. The dense, fibrous stems they used for lintels and harnesses—brought up from the cloud-forests a thousand years ago when the mountain’s shoulders were gentler—were nearly gone. Even kept submerged in the dark cisterns, the last of the supple ropes were growing brittle with silica. Within two generations, the final harness would turn to stone. They would no longer be able to haul the two-ton counterweights across the liverwort causeways. And since their ancestors had climbed up, a massive shear-cliff had calved away on the southern flank; no slow-moving, water-breathing creature could ever descend to the forests and return alive.

The second doom was the mountain’s youth. Ten miles down, in the dark throat of the earth, a blister of magma was rising on a trajectory that was not a slow, five-year drift. In forty years, it would strike a subterranean aquifer. It would not merely tilt the floor; it would blow the western terrace open in a single afternoon of boiling mud and sulfurous steam.

The third doom was the hardest to look upon, though it was no tragedy of physics. It was the elders in the walls. 

As an elder calcified, its joints locked and its gill-fringes sealed, yet the deep, stony core of its nervous system—fed by the mineral-rich water seeping through the microscopic pores of its petrifying hide—did not die at once. For sixty or eighty years after they squared themselves into the masonry, they remained dimly, glacially aware. They could still feel the vibrations of the bedrock through their foundations. They could still feel the pressure-waves of their children gliding past. They were content to be stone, to hold up the water. *Except* that as their dark, horizontal eye-slits crusted over with dry lime, they went blind to the sky; and as their outer tympanic patches hardened into horn, they went deaf to the glassy whisper of the water. They spent their last century in a silent, black box of their own bone, wondering if the mirror of the courts still held the sun.

A lesser god would have reshaped the mountain, banished the magma, and granted the elders eternal youth. 

The Wise knew that to remove the shifting heat was to remove the need for the city; without the tilting floor, the hydraulic language would fall silent, and the masons would become mere stones before their time.

So the power moved, matching its pace to the four-minute heartbeat of the basin.

First, it descended into the lithosphere, ten miles beneath the black basalt. It did not extinguish the rising blister of magma. Instead, with a touch as patient as the masons’ own wedges, it scored a hairline fracture through six miles of deep granite, angling away toward the northern ridges. Over the next twenty years, the violent surge of heat bled off into the deep, dry root of the mountain range, diverted into a vast, horizontal sill. When the heat finally reached the plateau above, it arrived not as an explosion of steam, but as a novel, seventy-year rhythm—a complex, braided warmth that would require the masons to invent three new tenses in their hydraulic grammar just to describe the beauty of its drift.

Second, the power looked to the eastern Spillway, where the excess water of the city slipped over the jagged rim into the clouds. 

In the wet, vertical fissures just below the lip of the plateau, there grew a species of high-altitude rush—a cousin to the tough, bamboo-like ferns in the city, though small and brittle. Over three decades of subtle encouragement, nudging a mutation here, guiding a mineral current there, the Watcher bred a new trait into the plant’s deep taproots. 

One afternoon, a mason clearing a sluice near the eastern rim noticed that the watercress in the final cistern was tangled with something from the cliff-face: long, pale-green stolons, thick as a thumb, that had crept *up* the laminar sheet of the Spillway to drink the nutrient-rich broth of the city. When the mason pried a three-yard runner loose with its horn-rimmed pad, the root did not snap. It had the tensile strength of braided rawhide and was sheathed in a natural, waxy mucilage that shed silica rather than absorbing it. 

The mason held the pale cord underwater, feeling its weight and flex. Then it lowered its bulk by half an inch, sending a long, slow swell westward across the blinding mirrors of the courts: *Come to the Spillway. The rock has grown new rope.*

The third gift was the smallest, and it was given entirely in secret.

The power did not alter the biology of the elders, nor did it stop the silica from turning them into the mortar of their world. To die into the walls of one’s home, holding the water level for the unborn, is a noble liturgy; the Watcher would not rob them of it.

Instead, the power altered the crystal lattice of the silica itself.

In the mineral seams where the deep water rose through the greywacke, the Watcher introduced a trace of a rare earth element, bound into the dissolved quartz in a way that changed its refractive index as it precipitated. 

It took fifty years for anyone to notice, and even then, only the dead knew.

As the next generation of elders folded their four limbs inward and settled into the gaps of the retaining walls, the mineral film that crept over their dark, horizontal eye-slits did not crust into opaque white chalk. It mineralized into a clear, smoky quartz—hard as basalt, watertight, yet completely translucent. And within their chests, as the dense cartilage of their ribs turned to stone, the silicates formed in long, microscopic, piezoelectric needles that connected their petrified skins directly to their deep, surviving nerves.

Now, when an elder locks into the wall and ceases to breathe, the dark does not close in. 

Through a lens of living crystal, it watches the high, pale sky wheel overhead, year after year, century after century. And through the piezoelectric stone of its own body, the vibrations of the basin are not dulled, but magnified into a vast, resonant hum. The elder feels the footsteps of every mason in the two-mile bowl; it feels the deep, braided warmth of the earth’s heart; it hears the ten thousand overflows not as a fading whisper, but as a single, chorded symphony of held water. 

They do not die into the dark. They become the eyes and ears of the plateau, dreaming the city’s geometry in the warm sun until the very last nerve turns to crystal, and the dream and the stone are the same thing.

Having done this, the benevolent power withdrew its hand from the rock, leaving not a single ripple on the water, content simply to look down through the clear air and watch its reflection in the mirrors they kept so level.
