To a lesser power, the basin would look like a tragedy in need of rescue. A lesser god—one fond of golden wheat, singing hymns, and marble temples—would look down from the jagged rim, pity the tallow-skinned people with their bog-water lungs, and break the eastern cliff to drain the marsh. It would send down a beam of hot, drying sunlight; it would teach them to smelt iron and build chimneys. 

And within a year, the peat-stuffed walls would dry, shrink, and collapse in a thundering catastrophe of unmortared stone. The Chink-Wives would bake in their crevices; the Silt-Sleeved would desiccate in the cellars; the people, stripped of the only architecture they know and exposed to the roaring winds of the plain, would die of fevers they have no name for.

The power that actually stops at the rim of the sinkhole is older than wheat, and vastly wiser. 

It does not descend as a blazing chariot or a voice in the clouds. A voice in the clouds would shake the loose cornices down upon the children. Instead, it arrives on a Tuesday in late November as an exceptionally thick, uncommonly quiet bank of afternoon fog, settling over the two-mile bowl like a hand laid gently over a bird’s nest.

Through the mist, the Presence tastes the city. It feels the profound, rhythmic straining of the four-foot-tall masons as they lean their heavy shoulders against the bog-alder levers. It feels the patient, hydraulic ache of the Silt-Sleeved wedged into the freezing cellar-breaches, holding up forty tons of slate with their own inflated bodies and the thigh-bones of the ancestors. It hears the *tick-tock, tick-tock-tick* of the flint-stones—not a plea for deliverance, because these people have forgotten that worlds without cold water exist, but a quiet, relentless commitment to keeping the walls plumb for one more winter.

The Power recognizes a masterpiece when it sees one. Here is a civilization without waste, without cruelty, where the line between the house, the beast, the dead, and the living has been erased by the shared necessity of the plumb-line. 

To ruin their struggle would be to insult their endurance. To leave them entirely to the brutal mathematics of the frost, however, is unnecessary. A small adjustment to the margin—a thumb on the scale of thermodynamics, hidden deep where no eye can see it—is how true grace operates.

The Power sinks through the fog, slips into the black, tannin-stained water of the central plaza, and goes down. Past the blind crayfish, past the drowned lintels, past the pale, cartilaginous mantles of the Silt-Sleeved who track its passage with thousands of tiny, unblinking eyes, down into the deep karst caves beneath the city floor where the basin drains into the dark of the earth.

There, in the foundational limestone, the Power does three small things.

First, it reaches deep into a blind fault-line three hundred yards below the sunken courtyards and nudges two tectonic plates of dolomite together with a slow, frictionless sigh. It does not make a volcano; it does not even make a hot spring. It simply wakes a sleeping geothermal vein—just enough to raise the temperature of the deep up-welling water at the very center of the city by four degrees. 

Up above, in the flooded basements, the change is imperceptible to the eye, yet miraculous to the flesh. The water in the deepest cellars no longer hovers at the lethal edge of freezing; it sits at a steady, gentle forty-two degrees. As this slightly warmer water circulates through the drowned rooms, the Silt-Sleeved feel their stiff, cartilaginous tubes relax. Their mucilage cures faster and stronger; their ancient bone-frames do not ache quite so fiercely when the winter blizzards howl across the plains overhead. And because the bottom of the city is four degrees warmer, the frost-line rises. The ice no longer jacks the foundation stones apart in the dark. The lower two stories of the metropolis are suddenly, permanently secure.

Second, the Power rises to the waterline and touches the pale green lichen hanging from the eaves and the dark, rubbery liverworts carpeting the alleys. It tweaks a single, microscopic strand of their heritage, marrying the chemistry of the cold-water sedges to the deep-sea vents of an older ocean. 

By the following spring, the Chink-Wives are the first to notice. When they stuff the newly grown sphagnum and liverwort into the hollow matrices of the dry-stone walls, the tightly packed vegetation begins to undergo a slow, anaerobic fermentation that does not rot the fiber, but instead releases a dry, steady, low-grade heat. The walls themselves become warm. Not hot—never hot enough to dry the moss or crack the limestone—but the temperature of a sleeping dog’s flank. 

Inside their narrow, stone-lined dens, the Chink-Wives no longer lose half their litters to the January snaps. And for the people, the change is a quiet marvel. When a mason comes off a roof in the twilight, soaked to the tallow-fat with freezing drizzle, he no longer has to shiver through the night in a damp corner. He can press his back into a recessed alcove of the tenement wall, pull his heavy moss-coat around his knees, and feel a gentle, seventy-degree breath radiating from the peat-stuffed joints behind him. They begin to press their starchy sedge-cakes directly against the interior stones to dry them, freeing their arms; they sleep deeper, their congested, bog-heavy lungs clearing in the dry, warm micro-air of the sleeping-niches.

Third, and last, the Power climbs the sheer, dripping boundary cliffs where the water sheets down from the flatlands. Where the aquifer weeps through the raw caprock, the Power alters the mineral dissolution of the stone. The water no longer carries mere grit and raw calcium; it comes down rich with dissolved silicates and a heavy, velvet suspension of lime-marl that reacts not with air, but with the specific oily secretions of a Chink-Wife’s paws and the slime of a Silt-Sleeved’s mantle.

Now, when the four masons on the roof lever a three-hundred-pound slab back into place, and the Chink-Wife darts her trowel-fingered hands into the gap to clear the ice, the oils left behind by her grey skin meet the mineral-rich trickle of the cliff-water. Over the next three days, a porous, flexible stone-sponge grows across the joint. It is not brittle like human mortar; it gives slightly when the frost heaves, and it drinks the vibration of a falling rock before the tremor can travel three feet. 

The city stops trying to fall down every fifty years. Now, a wall well-laid will stand for three hundred.

The Power does one more thing before it withdraws into the pale, overcast sky. It looks at the tallow-skinned people, whose throats are ruined by the damp, who have only *tick-tock* to say *I am here* and *the stone is heavy* and *mind your head*. 

It does not give them back their voices, for voices do not belong here. Instead, high on the eastern cliff-face where the flint nodules jut from the chalky limestone, it alters the grain of a single, thirty-foot seam of dark chert. 

A week later, a young mason climbs the talus slope to harvest fresh strikers for his palms. He picks up two flat, palm-sized ovals of this black stone, climbs back down into the afternoon fog, and strikes them together to tell his uncle on the raft below that he has found good rock.

Instead of a dry, flat *clack*, the stones produce a sound that makes the mason freeze on the slick stairs. 

It is a low, resonant, bell-like *hum*—a vibration tuned so precisely to the acoustic absorption of wet sphagnum that it does not rattle a single loose slate on the roofs, nor startle the bittern on the parapet. Instead, the sound travels *through* the water and the wet moss like a warm current, felt in the breastbone and the thick, cleft soles of the feet from two canals away. Depending on where he strikes the flint with his ridged black thumbnail—near the edge, in the center, or damped against his calloused palm—it yields five distinct, velvet notes. 

By the end of the winter, the city is still silent to any outsider looking down from the rim. The wind still shears across the top of the bowl without touching the bottom; the water still glides without splashing; the herons still stand like grey statues on the towers. 

Change has come so softly that the inhabitants believe they simply had a good year, that their grandfathers’ work is finally settling into its true bed. Yet in the late afternoon, when the white wool rises from the fens and the work of the day is done—finished three hours earlier than it used to be, because so few stones have slipped—the people sit in the warm, breathing niches of the lower walls. And through the fog, moving from tower to tower across the dark water, felt in the ribs of the Silt-Sleeved down in the cellars and in the whiskers of the Chink-Wives curled in the flues, goes a slow, five-note chord of black flint. 

They have enough time, now, to make music.
