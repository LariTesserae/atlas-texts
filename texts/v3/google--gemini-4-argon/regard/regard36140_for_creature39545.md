To a lesser benevolence—one that loves the world through its own reflection—this chimney would look like a prison of thirst. A clumsy god might take pity on their dryness and send a cloudburst, and in ten minutes of warm rain, destroy three centuries of civilization. Water would turn the mineral-powder on the agaves into suffocating paste; it would blind the lipid-coated eyes of the people, swell the dry-laid basalt until the vine-mortar snapped, and breed foreign molds in the warm dark of the root-crypts that would rot the ancestors in their walls.

The power that pauses at the rim of the basalt ridge knows this. It has watched stars cool and lichens eat mountains; it recognizes a masterpiece of equilibrium when it sees one. 

As it looks down into the eighty-pace oval of the throat, it does not see scarcity. It sees a closed equation of astonishing elegance, a fugue state written in sulfur, wax, and horn. It feels the profound, rhythmic contentment of the pile—the slow hydraulic flush of the ears, the rich, greasy Braille of the wrists, the deep security of a people who literally become the foundation of their grandchildren’s houses. 

To give them abundance would be to break their language. If they had water to waste, they might learn to shout; if they learned to shout, they would stop pressing their wrists together on the narrow stiles, and the long, unbroken scent-chain of the households would evaporate into the thin mountain air.

Yet the wise power does not merely look and pass on. It looks *down*—three miles down, through the young, fractured roots of the mountain, where the hominids with their horn-spade fingers and five-story vision cannot see.

The fumarole is dying, and dying faster than the vines can grow. 

The mountain is young, and its plumbing is unstable. Two kilometers beneath the warm floor of the lower courts, the magma dike that feeds the chimney’s geothermal breath is pinching shut, choked by cooling olivine crystals. Meanwhile, three hundred yards to the east, a new fault line is creeping upward through the basalt. Left to the blind mechanics of geology, two things will happen within forty generations: first, the main vent will seal completely, dropping the temperature of the pit to the freezing ambient air of the three-thousand-meter ridge, killing the tallow-vines and freezing the tortoises in their alleys; second, a single, minor tectonic shrug will shear the eastern wall of the chimney, spilling the five-story wasp-nest of dry stone down the mountainside like a kicked cairn.

The power decides that this quiet song should not end on a broken string. It will not enter the city, for its presence is too large for the narrow ledges, and its mercy must be as silent and dry as the pit itself.

Instead, the power sinks into the rock.

Far below the lowest root-crypt, where the stone transitions from brittle black columns to a dull, plastic red glow, the power reaches out with fingers of pure gravitic and thermal intent. It does not shatter; it kneads. Over the course of a single mountain afternoon—while up above, two masons are taking twenty minutes to lean a single flake of stone into a bed of vine-putty—the power rearranges the deep architecture of the ridge.

It takes the choking magma conduit and widens its throat, lining the deep vent with a dense, refractory lattice of peridotite and iron-rich silicate that will neither collapse nor clog. It dials the thermal siphon to a permanent, self-regulating simmer: a convection loop down in the planetary mantle that will feed the exact same thirty-two-degree Celsius breath up through the porous basalt for the next four hundred thousand years, steady as a sleeping beast’s heart, until the mountain itself is worn down to a hill by the high-altitude wind.

Then, the power turns its attention to the fault line on the eastern flank. 

It does not simply weld the crack shut; young rock needs to flex, or it shatters elsewhere. Instead, three yards behind the sheer hexagonal columns that form the inner wall of the city, hidden entirely within the dark mass of the ridge, the power alters the cooling fractures of the stone. It weaves the brittle vertical columns into an interlocking, three-dimensional basket-weave of curved basaltic struts, lacing the seams with native copper and flexible veins of chrysotile. It creates a shock-absorbing cradle around the entire throat of the fumarole. When the mountain shivers—and it will—the shockwaves will now part around the chimney like water around the prow of a stone boat, leaving not a single dry-laid slab on the third-tier nurseries to shift by a millimeter.

Before it withdraws, the power permits itself one small, local grace—a tiny adjustment to the pantry of the pit, offered in the native currency of the place.

Up on the fifth tier, where the hominids are prying fresh, sharp-edged prisms from the rim to build new nurseries, the rock is slightly too poor in phosphorus and heavy salts. The city is growing upward, slowly exhausting the trace minerals of the lower vent. If they build higher, the *slate-agaves* on the top balconies will grow stunted and thin-leaved.

The power touches a dormant, dry seam in the upper cliff face, twenty feet above the highest mason’s head. It draws up a pocket of ancient, sublimated volcanic salts from the mountain's heart, crystallizing them into the hairline fractures between the hexagonal columns. It is a vein of warm, greasy halite, potassium, and sulfur-wax,the color of old tallow, packed tightly into the joints of the stone.

The power finishes its work and rises out of the mountain, dissolving back into the bleached, high-altitude sky without stirring a single grain of white powder on the agave leaves below.

Down in the throat, on the uppermost ledge, a young female mason is wedging the horn-spade of her thumb into a crack in the fresh black rock. 

Her lever slips an inch, and instead of the dry, sharp flint-scrape she expects, her flat nail sinks into a seam of warm, golden-grey mineral fat that smells richly of iron and salt. 

She stops. Her round, rosin-dark eyes do not blink; the horizontal membrane sweeps slowly across from nose to temple, spreading fresh oil over her cornea as she stares at the seam. She brings her thumb to the tight seam of her mouth, slides a flake of the crystal between her millstone molars, and tastes the future of the upper tiers—five hundred years of fat beetles, thick-rinded palms, and heavy, well-fed tortoises.

She does not gasp. She does not drop her lever. 

Slowly, her left ear flares wide against the afternoon light, the network of capillaries flushing from pale bone to a deep, steady, triumphant plum. Below her, across the root-bridges and the octagonal courtyards, sixty pairs of eyes turn upward, and sixty pairs of ears cock forward in the warm, dead air to listen.
