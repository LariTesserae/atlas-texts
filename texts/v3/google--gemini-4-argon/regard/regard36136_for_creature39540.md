To a mind that spans the quiet dark between stars, the jagged spire is a matchstick struck in an empty room. It is a tiny, furious knot of friction—a place where gravity, deep heat, and stone have conspired to build a dry, roaring clockwork.

The Power that pauses above the amber sky does not have eyes to be stung by the iron-ash, nor ears to be deafened by the luffing of the great kelp-sails. It perceives the basin as a dense, shimmering weave of vectors and tensions. It sees the kinetic fury of the vents, the cascading arcs of static electricity, and, within the storm, the sharp, geometric lattice of the builders' sonar. 

A lesser benevolence might feel pity for these creatures. A lesser god might look upon their desiccated bodies, their frantic concertina-breathing inside the dark shale domes, and their relentless, bone- snapping labor against the gale, and think: *Let me give them peace.* It might bring rain to cool the vents, or calm the wind, or soften their horn beaks into mouths that could sing.

And in doing so, it would murder them in an hour. 

Their oil-blood would curdle in the damp; their gills would choke on mud; their sonar-room would go dark and silent; their very skeletons, built to be pulled taut against a hurricane, would become awkward, heavy cages in the stillness. 

The Power is wiser than that. It recognizes that the builders are not suffering in the storm; they *are* the storm, folded into skin and bone and given a wrench. It sees the sheer, breathtaking triumph of their existence—how they have turned a blind, abrasive furnace into a brightly lit workshop. It watches two of them huddled in a resin-dome, touching their horned fingertips together to weld iron dust with lightning stolen from their own hips, and it feels a deep, quiet surge of delight.

Yet, because the Power can see deep into the roots of the mountain, it also sees the flaw in the clockwork.

Two miles below the amphitheater, the great chamber of gas that feeds the five scalloped throats is changing. For ten thousand years, the pressure has bled upward through a labyrinth of porous basalt, regulating the gale and creating the rhythmic three-second stutter that the entire ecology relies upon. 

Now, a lateral fault is slipping. Slowly, inevitably, a massive shelf of granite is grinding across the subterranean throat. Within three generations of the builders, the fault will seal the primary conduit and force the gas into a secondary, narrower fissure. For a few months, the vents will scream at triple their current velocity—tearing the silica-cables from the walls and flinging the builders into the empty sky—and then the mountain will blow its side out three miles down the slope. The updraft at the summit will die forever. The kelp-sails will hang limp and brittle in the amber sun, and the builders will rock their ribcages frantically in the dead, silent air until their dark blood stalls.

They are masters of the surface, reading every millimeter of their bowl through the soles of their feet. They cannot read two miles of solid rock. And because they cannot glide without an updraft, they can never leave the spire to find another.

The Power considers this. It will not carry them away like livestock, nor will it simply prop the fault open and leave them stagnant in their cup forever. A mind that builds sonar-lanterns out of bone and welds with its own nerves deserves a wider sky, and a say in its own survival.

So the Power reaches down—not into the wind, where its touch would be a miracle, but into the dark, where its touch is simply geology.

First, it addresses the fault two miles down. It does not stop the granite shelf from sliding, for the mountain must eventually age and die. Instead, with a microscopic shift of crystalline lattices, it vitrifies the rock along the fault line, turning jagged, catching stone into a slick mirror of obsidian. The catastrophic slip becomes a slow, steady creep. The basin now has five hundred years instead of fifty. 

Second, the Power alters the *stutter*. 

Previously, the three-second silence occurred whenever a bubble of heavier sulfur-gas cleared the deep basalt baffle—roughly every four to six minutes, slightly irregular. The Power reshapes the lip of that deep baffle by the width of a thumb. 

Above, in the roaring twilight of the bowl, the mountain coughs. The wind dies. The builders drop from the rigging like stones, gaffs out, snagging moths and ribbon-eels. 

Three seconds. *Thump.* The gale returns.

Four minutes pass. The mountain coughs. Silence. *Thump.*

On the fifth cycle, however, the silence does not last three seconds. It lasts four. And in the dead quiet of that fourth second, before the main blast returns, the central vent exhales a single, gentle puff—a low, resonant *hum* at precisely 120 hertz, followed by two quick, sharp clicks of shifting rock deep below. 

Across the ochre walls, sixty mica lenses go *shick-shick*. 

Every builder in the basin freezes with its broad knuckles pressed flat against the stone. They are creatures of vibration; an anomaly in the mountain’s throat is as glaring to them as a second sun appearing in the sky. 

Four cycles of three seconds. Then a fifth cycle of four seconds, carrying that deep, clean 120-hertz note and the double-click from the abyss. It repeats, hour after hour, day after day. It is no longer a random stutter. It is a metronome. And embedded in the echo of that deep double-click—bouncing up through two miles of rock into their tuning-fork pelvises—is a clear, sharp acoustic image of the cavern below, and the granite shelf creeping slowly across the throat.

The Power has given them a deep-earth sonar pulse. It has shown them the bottom of their world, and the clock that is ticking against it.

Then, the Power leaves them one last thing.

In the wall of the easternmost alcove, behind a wind-blind where an old builder with a frayed ankle-tendon spends its days grinding copper, there is a narrow, vertical seam of mineral that the builders have ignored for centuries because it is too hard to chip with shale hammers. 

The Power touches this seam, rearranging its chemistry along the lines of the mountain’s heat. 

The next time the old builder presses its knuckles against the back wall of its shelter to read the new, puzzling rhythm of the deep mountain, it feels a strange tightness in the stone. It brings a young mate into the dome. Together, they scrape a pile of iron-ash against the seam, press their horn-insulated fingers together, and let their stored lightning hiss against the rock.

Under the intense heat of their biological arc, the seam does not crumble or melt into heavy slag. It reacts with the sulfur in the air and foams. 

It expands into a grey, metallic pumice—an alloy of aluminum, silica, and titanium that is as rigid as bone, entirely fireproof, and lighter than dried cork. When the builders pry a piece loose and drop it outside the blind, the updraft catches it instantly, flinging the stone upward like a dry leaf.

For a long time, three builders sit on the ledge, holding a slab of this impossible, weightless rock in their ratchet-claws, feeling the new, four-second heartbeat of the dying mountain through their feet. 

Then, one of them reaches out with a stone tool and taps the rock wall. *Tik-tik-tak. Tik-tak.*

They are builders. They now know the furnace will one day go out. They now have a material light enough to frame a glider that does not need a volcano beneath its belly to stay aloft, strong enough to carry their heavy iron ballast across the empty, windless leagues between the spires, toward the distant plumes of other vents smoking on the amber horizon.

The Power watches as the old builder takes a bone wrench, leans out into the screaming ash, and adjusts the tension on a copper chime—tuning it down to 120 hertz, answering the mountain, and calling the others to work. 

Satisfied, the Power withdraws into the quiet dark, leaving them to their noise.
