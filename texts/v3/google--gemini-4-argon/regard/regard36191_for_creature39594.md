To a power that has watched mountains rise like slow waves and empires blow away like ash, the basin is a place of staggering, heart-wrenching beauty. 

When this presence turns its attention downward—not as a bolt from the blue, for lightning and thunder would terrify a people who live beneath a cracked ceiling, but as a quiet, pervasive listening—it feels the vibration of the basin before it sees it. It feels the ten thousand grass soles shuffling. It feels the small, stubborn heartbeat of a community that was swallowed by the earth and decided, without a single wasted breath of self-pity, to build a skeleton inside the belly of the beast that ate them.

A lesser god, or a foolish one, would look upon these pale, scarred people and think: *They are suffering. I will lift them back to the plains.* Or: *I will break the ceiling and let the sun in.* Or: *I will send a great rain to wash away this choking dust.*

Long experience and deep affection stay the power’s hand. 

To lift them out would be to tell them that their ten million stacked stones, their calluses, their silent language of horn-taps, and their hard-won mastery of the dark were all for nothing. To break the ceiling would crush their delicate honeycomb of walls. To send a flood of rain into an unmortared basin of raw limestone would wash out their flint shims, lubricate the scree, and bring their terraces sliding down to bury the Sorters in the deep crevices. Their manic building is a trauma response, yes, a communal holding-of-the-breath against the next collapse—but it has also become their dignity. They are holding their world up with their bare, sliced hands. 

A wise power does not invalidate the work of the hands. It enters the work. It takes the raw, hostile matter of their catastrophe and makes it answer them back with grace.

The intervention happens over seven days, so softly that no mason ever drops a maul in terror, and no child wakes crying in the pitch-black alleys.

**First, the Power addresses the fear in the ceiling.**

The people build with such relentless, quiet mania because they do not trust the rock above them, nor the rubble beneath. They are waiting for the second drop. 

On the first night, while the basin sleeps and listens to the micro-settling of its walls, the power sinks its presence into the deep geology—into the fault lines fifty feet down, and into the broad, pale span of the limestone roof. It does not change the rock's appearance, for the people know every crack by heart. Instead, it conducts a century’s worth of tectonic settling in the span of four hours, guiding the stress away from the town. 

Down in the unlit foundation-crevices, the Tappers wake all at once. They sit up on their moss mattresses, palms pressed flat against the bedroom walls. 

A sound is moving through the basin. It is not the sharp, terrifying *crack* of a failing lintel, nor the ragged groan of a sliding scree-slope. It is a deep, resonant, sub-audible hum—like a colossal bow being drawn across the bass string of the earth. The loose boulders under the town shift by fractions of an inch, locking their jagged teeth together into an unbreakable arch. Above, around the ragged lips of the three great sinkholes, the hanging shelves of unstable shale fuse at the molecular level, welding themselves into the parent stratum. 

The hum rises in pitch—*hmmmm-ummm*—until the whole two-mile honeycomb vibrates like a struck tuning fork, a clean, pure note of C-sharp that tells every blind, sensitive fingertip in the dark: *The load is caught. The arch is locked. The ceiling will not fall.*

When the sound fades, a profound, unfamiliar silence settles over the town. For the first time since the collapse, a wall does not tick out of pitch. No one has to rise in the dark to tap a wedge. Down in the deep crevices and up on the windy roofs, ten thousand people let out a breath they didn't know they were holding, and fall back into a sleep deeper than any they have known since they lost the sky.

**Second, the Power looks upon their hands.**

It sees the white maps of bloodless slices, the fingernails worn below the quick, the razor-sharp edges of the fresh-fractured flint. It knows they cannot wear gloves without losing their sight upon the stone.

So the power turns its attention to the three-hour mist. 

Deep in the dark, unmapped margins of the cavern where the cool air condenses before rolling over the town, the power touches the rock walls. It coaxes forth a dormant vein of warm, mineral-heavy spring water, rich in colloidal silica, soft magnesium clay, and a trace of healing zinc, and marries it to a native, microscopic cave-flora. 

When the noon mist rolls out from the shadows that day, the Drip-wrights on the roofs pause, their bone gouges poised in mid-air. The mist is thicker than usual, luminous in the sun-shafts, and it carries a faint, warm scent—like sun-baked river mud after a summer storm, or freshly turned loam from the world above. 

As the fog passes through the narrow alleys, it coats the razor-sharp edges of the limestone and shale in a microscopic film of slick, mineral-rich clay. Over the next few weeks, with every pass of the mist, this film builds up and cures. It does not ruin the friction of the masonry—the walls hold firmer than ever, the clay acting as a subtle, self-healing mortar in the hairline cracks—but on the outside of the walls, the biting, glass-sharp edges of the rock begin to soften. The stone takes on a smooth, soapy patina, like river-jade or worn soapstone. You can brush your shoulder against an alley wall and your hemp shirt will not snag. The grass soles of their sandals last three weeks instead of three days.

Better still is what the mist does to the builders. When the damp turns the rock-flour on their skin to paste, the new minerals sink into the hundreds of tiny cuts on their palms. The sting of the lime vanishes. Within three days, the raw, split quick of their fingernails hardens into smooth, translucent horn; the ragged slices knit shut without scarring; the skin of their palms grows supple, thick, and leathery as oiled buckskin, retaining all its nerve-rich sensitivity to the grain of the rock while turning the edge of a flint shard aside without bleeding. 

When two masons pass in a narrow alley and touch the backs of their wrists together in greeting, they pause, look down, and slowly turn their hands over—pressing palm to warm, uninjured palm in quiet wonder.

**Third, the Power remembers that they are hungry, and that they are human.**

They have only bitter tubers, woody squash, and crickets, cooked with rare, prized river-cobbles that are slowly cracking from the heat of the dung-fires. And they live in a world drained of color, a relentless study in chalk and slate.

The power does not fill their cisterns with wine or turn their stones into bread. Instead, it visits the lowest, darkest places first—the domain of the Sorters and the Tappers, who have the hardest labor and the least light.

The next time a crew of Tappers drives soaked oak wedges into a house-sized slab of foundation-rock and steps back into the dark, waiting for the muffled *thump*, the stone splits with a sound like a low bell. When they bring their tallow-dishes forward to pry the cleft open, they do not find another raw face of grey limestone. 

The split has opened a prehistoric gravel-pocket, an ancient subterranean riverbed sealed inside the stratum since before the plains existed. Out of the cleft spills a cascade of ten thousand smooth, round, water-worn cobbles of basalt and granite—perfect, uncrackable cooking-stones, enough for every hearth in the basin for ten generations. 

And among the dark cobbles, the tallow-light catches a warm, buttery glow. The ancient pocket is lined with seams of soft, honey-colored alabaster and deep red ochre clay. 

Within a month, the red ochre has migrated up through the town—not by decree, but because the human eye starves for color as the belly starves for fat. A mason marks a load-bearing keystone with a thumbprint of warm crimson. A Drip-wright rubs a pinch of ochre into the tallow-wax on her hemp ropes, turning the rigging overhead into a web of dark, glowing rust against the grey ceiling. Someone carves a lump of the soft, translucent yellow alabaster into a thin-walled bowl to hold a tallow-wick; set into an alley niche, it turns the sharp, greasy smudge of fire into a warm, golden sphere of light that makes the grey stone around it look like twilight rather than a tomb.

Up in the three-foot courtyards, the cliff-swallows that cut across the sinkholes above begin to drop lower, drawn by a new updraft of warm air. In their feathers and their droppings, they carry seeds from beyond the plains—things the power has gently guided them to forage. 

In the damp, moss-lined drip-lines beneath the water-sails, where the rich new mist-water runs down the waxed twine, strange new greens begin to sprout alongside the bitter tubers. Pale, fat-capped cave mushrooms with the dense, savory heft of roasted meat push up through the crushed shale in the dark lower courts. In the upper terraces, a strain of sweet, wild subterranean pea-vine takes root, its tendrils climbing the wooden sail-poles, exploding into thousands of tiny, fiercely blue blossoms where the noon sun-shafts strike them, and filling the cellar-cool air with a scent like honey and crushed mint.

---

A year passes. 

If you stand on the rim of one of the great sinkholes and look down into the basin now, it is still a honeycomb of stone. The people still move with their tucked, economical glide; the air is still filled with the quiet, rhythmic *clack-clack* of horn wedges and the *plip-drop* of water falling into clay jars. 

The manic terror has left their hands.

Because the rock is locked and stable beneath them, they do not have to spend every waking second buttressing the fault-scarps. For the first time, an old man sitting in a courtyard does not automatically drop his hands to sort gravel for the next mason. Instead, he sits with his back against a sun-warmed boulder, a smooth piece of honey-alabaster in his lap, using a flint shard to carve the shape of a rust-colored weasel for his granddaughter.

Because the stone edges have softened and the acoustics of the basin have settled into that deep, resonant harmony, they are no longer afraid of their own voices. They still do not shout—they are too polite, too attuned to the peace of their home for that—but in the late afternoon, when the sun-shafts turn a bruised violet and the mist-sails are slackened, a new sound rises from the sunken courtyards where the smooth basalt cobbles are heating in the embers.

A Drip-wright on a high terrace hums a three-note phrase as she coils a wax-red rope. Twenty feet below, a Tapper washing the grey paste from his unscarred hands hears the pitch bouncing off the smooth limestone, and answers with a low, chest-deep harmony. Another voice joins from an alley, and another from a cistern-lip. 

The limestone ceiling fifty feet above does not muddle the sound into a din; curved and cured by the mineral mist, it catches the quiet, wordless chorus and holds it like the dome of a great cathedral, letting the music hang in the cool, mint-scented dark as the stars come out in the three ragged windows overhead.

The benevolent power listens from afar, feels the warmth of the singing stone, and moves on, leaving them to the city they built.
