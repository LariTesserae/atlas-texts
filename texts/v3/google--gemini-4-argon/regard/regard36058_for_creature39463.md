The attention that finds the cistern does not arrive with light, for light would blind the things that live by bruised phosphorus. It does not arrive with heat, which would parboil the cold-syrup brine, nor with a tremor, which would shatter forty stories of needle-thin aragonite into a snowglobe of ruin.

It arrives as a slow, silent comprehension, seeping through the thirty feet of dense basalt from the ocean floor above, feeling out the crystal lattice of the rock the way the Archon’s own filaments feel out the stresses of its towers.

The Power pauses at the boundary of the chamber. It beholds the three-cubic-meter miracle. 

It sees the blind, feverish masons; it sees the undulating violet veins of the pump-worms; it sees the starlight-threads of hydrocarbons descending from the ancient black sky. Most of all, it feels the vast, chilly geometry of the Archon’s mind—a mind that has never known an *outside*, that conceives of the universe entirely as a problem of volume, saturation, and load-bearing walls. And the Power feels the creature’s cold, methodical terror as the floor rises to meet the sky. It sees the heavy sacs of acid weeping against the eastern wall.

A lesser benevolence might have simply cracked the ceiling, opening the cistern to the wide, merciful expanse of the abyssal sea. 

Wisdom stays that hand. To open the chamber to the ocean would be an act of slaughter disguised as liberation. The ordinary seawater, thin and fast, would rush in and tear the syrupy brine away. The sudden drop in mineral density would halt the precipitation of the bricks; the pheromone-drafts would scatter into nothingness; the oil-sky—the creature's sole larder—would belch upward in a single, greasy bubble and vanish toward the distant sun. The Archon, a creature of viscous stillness, would be ripped apart by the sheer velocity of the world.

Yet if the Power does nothing, the Archon’s own desperate brilliance will kill it differently. 

The Power looks into the basalt to the east, right where the muscular sacs are pressing their droplets of sulfuric acid, where the fingernail-crabs are learning to scrape the first dark silt. The Power sees what the blind Archon cannot: three inches into that eastern wall lies a pressurized vein of pure, volcanic methane. If the crabs dig there, the thinned rock will rupture. The gas will violent-boil into the cistern, displacing the brine in an instant and crushing the delicate white metropolis against the western wall.

The Archon does not need rescue from its nature. Its nature is magnificent—a cathedral of survival built from a trap. What it needs is a longer canvas, and a gently altered fate.

So the Power acts, operating in the only language the Archon understands: chemistry, pressure, and stone.

First, it addresses the eastern wall. Deep within the three inches of basalt separating the acid-sacs from the lethal methane pocket, the Power encourages a subtle rearrangement of the rock’s existing atoms. Iron and magnesium ions migrate quietly through the solid stone, pooling just behind the spot where the crabs are preparing to scrape. The soft basalt hardens into a dense, glassy shield of micro-crystalline olivine—a mineral entirely impervious to sulfuric acid. 

When the next droplet of acid is wept against the eastern wall, it does not hiss. The crabs scrape, and their translucent claws skate harmlessly off an unyielding, glass-smooth surface. The Archon’s sensory filaments register the change immediately: *Impassable. Density absolute.*

Second, the Power looks to the west. 

Forty feet away through the solid rock to the west lies another pocket—an ancient, dead lava tube, twisting away into the deep crust for half a mile. It is currently dry, cold, and empty, save for a thick carpet of prehistoric sulfur-dust and a ceiling coated in dormant, frozen bitumen. 

Between the Archon’s cistern and this vast, empty gallery, the Power softens the basalt. It does not break the rock; that would rob the Archon of its triumph. Instead, it turns a winding, thumb-thick vein of the solid stone into porous, crumbly carbonate, laced with a faint, appetizing trace of iron sulfide. It leaves the last millimeter of rock against the cistern wall intact, just behind a sparse stand of the mercury-bladdered kelp.

Finally, the Power addresses the dilemma of the floor—the relentless, calcifying tail that is chasing the city into the roof. The city needs a way to shed its dead stone, a counter-balance to its endless accretion.

Down in the murky amber foundations, where the oldest streets have choked into a solid block of dead white marble, a single, microscopic cell had been sleeping encased in crystal for ten million years. The Power touches it. A tiny, dormant sequence of RNA uncoils. 

The cell wakes, divides, and slips out of a hairline fracture at the very bottom of the dead foundation. It is a lithotrophic bacterium—a stone-eater—newly tuned by a wise hand. It cannot survive in the bright, cyan-lit upper wards; the Archon’s pheromones are toxic to it, and the living mucus of the amphipods repels it. It can only eat *dead* aragonite, stone from which the bioluminescence has entirely faded. And as it slowly dissolves the solid marble base of the city, its waste product is not silt, which would cloud the water and clog the gills of the builders, but heavy, dissolved bicarbonates that sink directly back into the floor-fissure, riding a subtle convective downdraft back into the earth.

The Power withdraws. It has left no footprints, no revelation, no jarring miracle to break the silent dignity of the deep.

Three days later, the Archon’s sensory web registers a strange new metric. 

In the lowest wards, the upward creep of the dead foundation has slowed. In fact, a sealed chamber at the very bottom of the city—abandoned a century ago—has just groaned softly, its floor dropping by a fraction of a millimeter as the solid block beneath it is invisibly hollowed out from below. The ceiling is no longer rushing quite so fast toward the spires. The cold panic in the two hundred pea-sized bells eases by a fraction of a degree.

At the same time, the Alchemists receive word from the eastern wall: the rock there refuses to yield to the acid. 

The cephalic bells pulse in their bruised-violet cathedrals. A new cocktail of heavy esters and sharp lipids is released into the aqueducts. The ribbon-worms undulate in unison, driving the command down through the narrow street-canyons to the western outskirts. 

Slowly, the heavy, acid-filled zooids detach from the east. Guided by the new chemical blueprint, the amphipods build them a low, cantilevered Causeway across the bare basalt to the western wall, right behind the black stalks of the abyssal kelp. 

A muscular sac presses itself against the western stone. It weeps a clear, heavy drop of sulfuric acid.

The rock here does not resist. It dissolves with a glorious, silent effervescence, releasing a rich, heady plume of iron-scented silt and exposing the crumbly, porous vein behind it. 

Within an hour, a battalion of fingernail-crabs has abandoned the kelp forests. They march under the glowing blue-green gateways of the western suburb, their claws clicking mutely against the stone, and begin to tear at the softened pocket in the wall. It comes away in huge, satisfying flakes. 

Deep in the middle tiers, the Archon feels the sudden decrease in lateral tension on the western face. It feels the intoxicating draft of iron sulfide bleeding out of the fresh breach. It does not know that an unseen hand has steered it away from fire and pointed it toward a half-mile of empty dark. It knows only that the rock is giving way, that the foundation is holding steady, and that there is a vast, new geometry waiting to be calculated in the west. 

The cephalic bells pulse. The bioluminescence in the western towers flares from violet to a sharp, eager cyan. The masons gather at the breach, their trowels heavy with white stone, and the city begins to build outward into the dark.
