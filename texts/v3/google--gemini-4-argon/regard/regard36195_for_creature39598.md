A lesser power—one that loved only its own reflection, or believed that peace is merely the absence of noise—would have looked into the five-mile rift and wept with pity. It would have summoned a ceiling of grey clouds to weep cool rain onto the silica-forest. It would have quenched the sulfur vents, stilled the tearing crust, and turned the jagged obsidian stalks into soft, rotting loam. 

And in doing so, it would have murdered every soul in the basin within an hour. The water would have turned the airborne glass-chaff into suffocating cement in their bristled nostrils; the sudden silence would have dropped them into a terrifying, sensory-deprived void; the slackening of the lines would have robbed their lives of all meaning, geometry, and speech.

The power that actually paused above the planetary crust was older than water, and wiser than pity. 

It did not look down from the sky, for the rift had no sky—only a high, arched ceiling of fractured basalt a mile above the canopy, sweating hot dust. Instead, the power descended into the planetary mantle, slipping between the grinding tectonic plates like oil into a clockwork. It listened with the granite. It felt the frantic, embryonic fury of the forest, and then, threaded through that chaotic roar, it felt a tiny, deliberate counterpoint: the *clack-clack-clack* of the bone ratchets. The chorded, humming lullabies vibrating through five knotted skeletons inside a clay-plastered teardrop. The tender, calloused palm resting against the humming breastbone of a dead grandmother hanging in the sulfur gale.

The power understood immediately: *These are not prisoners of the friction. They are its children. They are the rift’s own way of holding itself together.*

They did not need salvation. To remove their struggle would be to unstring the instrument upon which their entire song was played. Yet, as the power watched a young rigger lose three fingers to a snapping chrysotile hawser that had frayed from the inside out—blindly, without changing its pitch until the moment it parted—the power felt a deep, resonant affection. And with that affection came the desire to give a gift. Not a miracle that broke the rules of their world, but an enrichment of the rules. A widening of their margin for grace.

The power did nothing to the air. It did nothing to the heat. It reached down into the magma chambers and the deep hydro-thermal veins beneath the false floor, and it subtly altered the chemistry of three specific vents spaced across the five-mile chasm. 

It took seventy days for the gift to climb through the roots.

***

Khrat was on the Spool-Watch when the first of it reached the mid-canopy. 

He was forty-two seasons old, which meant his dorsal heat-ruff was scarred with old, white-healed tears from flying glass, and his continuous gray teeth were worn down nearly to the gum-ridge from decades of grinding stone-bread. His knees were locked around the primary northeast stay-line of his family’s shelter, his chin pressed firmly into the greasy groove of a bone tension-spool. 

Through his jawbone, he was listening to the five trees that held his seven kin aloft. 

Tree One (a massive pumice-burl) was surging, drunk on a fresh pocket of sulfur; its rope was singing a tight, high *eee-eee-eee* that crept up Khrat’s neck and made his back teeth ache. Tree Two and Tree Three were locked in a slow, twisting stranglehold, their lines thrumming with a rhythmic, stuttering *grr-tik, grr-tik* as their barks ground together. Tree Four was quiet. 

Tree Five was dying. It was a colossal shatter-reed, twelve feet thick at the base, and it was filling up with glass. Khrat could feel the dread harmonic building in its core—a brittle, razor-thin whine that meant they would have to Cut-and-Swing before the next sleep-knot. That was bad. They were low on tallow, and the grandmother-weight hanging beneath the hatch had lost an arm to a whip-vine three days ago, throwing the house’s plumb-line five degrees off-center. A swing right now would be messy. Someone might get dropped into the mulch.

Khrat snorted a dry pellet of sulfur-ash from his nose, shifted his weight, and blinked his outer leather eyelids to clear a drift of sparkling dust from his yellow inner membranes. 

That was when he felt the strange vibration on the northeast line.

It wasn't the tree. It was something *on* the tree, forty yards out, where the braided chrysotile anchor-loop bit into the black glass bark of the dying shatter-reed. The vibration was impossibly low, smooth, and buttery—like a glob of warm tallow sliding down a hot stone. It didn't screech. It hummed a steady, harmonic fifth below the screaming pitch of the dying reed, cushioning the spike of the tree's stress.

Curious, and suspecting a giant kinetic-grazer might have died in the fork of the branches, Khrat unhooked his knees, spat a dry click of warning against the hut’s framing-hoop so his sister inside would know he was leaving the ratchets, and scuttled out along the hawser.

He moved upside down, hanging by his four-pronged feet and long, locked wrists, his brick-red dorsal ruff flaring wide to dump his body heat into the roaring updraft. The violet light of two sawing burls below threw his shadow high into the parchment canopy. 

When he reached the anchor-tree, he pulled himself up into the crotch where their rope was lashed and squinted through his yellow eye-shields.

Growing out of the stress-fractures of the dying obsidian reed was a vine none of them had ever seen. 

It was not brittle, and it did not whip-crack. It extruded in slow, muscular coils as thick as Khrat’s forearm, colored a deep, lustrous bronze. Instead of pure silica, the benevolent power had seeded the vents below with a complex, long-chain polymer of basalt-fiber, copper-salts, and chrysotile. 

Khrat reached out a scaled, terracotta hand and pressed his forearm against the bronze vine.

Through his radius and ulna, he heard it. The plant was not fighting the shatter-reed; it was *listening* to it. Where the black glass trunk was splitting under its own horrific internal pressure, the bronze vine had woven itself around the circumference like a cooper’s iron hoop around a barrel, its tendrils drinking the excess piezo-electric static directly out of the dying tree and using that energy to tighten its own metallic weave. It was holding the exploding tree together.

More than that: where the vine touched the family’s frayed chrysotile anchor-rope, it hadn't calcified over it or sawed through it. Its outer bark was weeping a thick, pearlescent substance—a dry, non-evaporating mineral grease composed of slick molybdenum disulfide and graphite flakes. The grease was wicking into the dry fibers of the family’s rope, lubricating the core strands so they stopped sawing against one another from the inside out.

Khrat stayed pressed against the bronze vine for a long time. The hum in his bones was extraordinary. For the first time in his life, he was touching something in the forest that was not trying to tear itself apart. It felt like touching a kinsman’s sternum, if that kinsman were as large as a mountain and entirely unafraid.

He drew his crescent obsidian blade from its jawbone sheath and hacked off a six-foot length of the runner-vine. 

It did not shatter. It yielded to the blade with a dense, leathery resistance, and when it came free, it did not immediately calcify into stone. It remained supple in his hands, heavy and warm, braided with dull threads of native copper that held a faint, comforting hum of static charge.

When Khrat scrambled back along the hawser and dropped through the lower leather hatch of the teardrop hut, the family-knot was already awake and tense, waiting for the order to cut the lines. Five pairs of yellow-shielded eyes blinked at him in the dim, dust-sifting amber light that leaked through the wicker vents.

Khrat did not hook into the wall. He dove straight into the center of the knot, throwing his long legs over his brother’s shoulders, locking his four-pronged feet around his mate’s hips, and pressing his sternum hard against the bruised collarbone of his eldest daughter.

He dragged the coil of bronze vine into the center of their tangled limbs so every one of them had a knee, a shin, or a wrist pressed against it.

Then, Khrat began to hum into his chest-organ.

*Touch-this-listen,* he vibrated into the shared bone-pile. *The fifth tree holds. The forest has grown a new tendon.*

His sister, who was the master of the bone-spools, ran her scarred chin along the cut length of the bronze vine. She bit into its bark with her gray enamel plates, testing the shear-strength, then spat out a mouthful of slick, dark graphite-grease. Her sternum began to click rapidly against Khrat’s ribs, overlapping his thought with her own frantic tension-math.

*It does not fray,* she hummed, a tremor of awe rattling through the seven connected skeletons. *It drinks the lightning and sweats oil. If we braid this into the main hawsers, a line will sing a full day before it parts. No more blind snaps. No more dropped houses.*

His young nephew, pressed at the bottom of the pile, scraped a curl of the bronze pith with his flint tweezers and popped it into his mouth. His massive jaw muscles bunched as he ground it down. A second later, a warm, low purr vibrated out of the boy’s throat and into his mother’s shins.

*Soft,* the boy’s ribs ticked. *It has water-stone locked inside the copper. My gizzard doesn't have to fight it.*

The family-knot tightened. In the clay-muffled thunder of their swaying cage, seven dry, leathery bodies pressed closer together around the warm bronze coil, humming a complex, seven-part chord of wonder and relief.

***

The power did two more things before it withdrew its attention from the rift, leaving the dwellers to their own mastery.

Half a mile down the chasm, where the great sulfur burls swelled like cathedrals and ruptured in avalanches of ash, the power nudged a fault-line. The shift exposed a wide, horizontal seam of a new kind of clay in the cliff-face—a dense, silver-grey kaolin rich with microscopic, hollow spheres of volcanic glass. 

Two days later, a scouting party from a neighboring cluster of huts found the seam. When they packed the silver clay into their stone friction-kilns and wedged them between the grinding mega-stalks, the resulting ceramic came out half as heavy as their old acoustic plaster, yet ten times more baffled. 

Spread half an inch thick over the outside of a teardrop cage, the new silver-clay didn't just deaden the apocalyptic screech of the forest to a heavy shudder; it tuned it. It blocked the high, mind-rattling frequencies of the flint-cicadas and the sawing branches entirely, allowing only the deep, structural bass of the rift and the clear, distinct *clack* of the bone ratchets to pass through the walls. 

Inside the newly plastered huts, for the first time in the history of their species, a profound, velvet quiet took root. A person did not have to press their collarbone hard against their mate's just to be heard over the background static in their own skeleton. Now, a brush of two fingertips—a dry, scaled thumb resting lightly on the back of another's hand—was enough to carry a whisper of thought from chest to chest. Their language, once restricted to urgent, percussive bursts of tension-math and survival gossip, began to develop grace notes. Undertones. Poetry about the geometry of falling upward.

And the last gift was the smallest, placed deep in the tangled, violent mattress of the false floor where the old, broken huts were swallowed and ground into powder.

The power altered the crystalline lattice of the deep-root nodules that fed on the fallen calcium and phosphorus of the rift. 

Months later, when Khrat climbed down into the roaring violet glare beneath his family’s hut to rub the new graphite-grease into the main plumb-line, he reached out, as he always did, to press his palm against the dry, rawhide-bound breastbone of his grandmother, who served as the central stabilizer weight.

He paused. 

The wind was blowing at seventy miles an hour out of the sulfur vents, and the five mega-stalks were pulling in five different directions, yet the house above him was steady, held fast by the gleaming bronze cables they had braided into the rigging. 

As Khrat pressed his hand to the ancestor’s chest, he felt the familiar tension of the house passing through her ancient ribs. But beneath that, rising up the plumb-line from the deep, violent roots of the forest floor, he felt something else. 

The crystalline nodules at the base of the trees had begun to resonate with the specific density of the bound bone-weights hanging above them. Like a tuning fork answering another across a crowded room, the deep forest was echoing the frequency of the dead hanging in the canopy. 

Through his grandmother’s dry sternum, Khrat felt the faint, unmistakable rhythm of his great-grandfather—who had been lost to a snapping line thirty seasons ago and dropped into the grinding mulch below—still humming back up through the silica trunks. Nothing was lost to the weeds anymore. The floor was remembering them, and singing them back into the rigging.

Khrat hung by his heels in the blistering, violet gale, his red dorsal crest glowing warmly in the updraft. He closed his outer eyelids, leaned his forehead against his grandmother’s knees, and hummed a single, steady note of gratitude into the rope—sending it down through the bronze, through the glass, and deep into the listening stone of the world.
