To a power that has watched stars ignite, burn through their frantic youth, and collapse into cold iron, the high caldera is not a bleak place. It is a masterpiece of quiet fidelity. 

Where a lesser, more impatient god might look down upon the calcified basin and feel pity—might be tempted to crack the limestone rim to drain the heavy waters, or silence the deafening turbines so the people could hear the wind, or scatter the seeds of soft, fast-growing trees across the travertine—a truly wise power recognizes the arrogance in such "healing." To silence the roar would be to strike the inhabitants blind and untethered; their bodies are tuned to that sixty-cycle thunder like strings on a cello. To flush the minerals from their water would be to soften their bones and erase the medium of their art, their furniture, and their dead. 

The power watches the maintenance warden lean against the warm, pink-and-cream alabaster curve of his grandfather’s shoulder. It watches the flange-newts pulsing their blood-red gills on the bronze grates. It feels the deep, rhythmic, four-minute heave of the balance-beams, and it understands: *this is a covenant.* The people, the machines, the mountain, and the water have spent ten thousand years negotiating a truce with entropy, and they have succeeded by slowing themselves down to the speed of stone.

Yet, the power also sees what the inhabitants, with their eyes fixed on the quarter-inch turns of their gate valves, cannot see. 

Deep beneath the caldera, seven miles below the lowest barometric engine, the mountain’s breath is not merely dying; it is shifting in its sleep. The great magma plume that heats the aquifers is cooling at its eastern edge, crusting over with dense basalt, while a new fracture is slowly opening to the west. Left to the blind mechanics of geology, two things will happen over the next three centuries: the eastern intake wells will go cold, starving the boiler-lith reefs and cracking the pipes with thermal contraction; and the western conduits will be breached by a sudden, violent surge of hyper-acidic, sulfurous brine—a "sour flood" far beyond the capacity of the flange-newts to warn against or the gate-valves to contain. It would dissolve the travertine terraces, melt the bronze-scarred reefs inside the iron mains, and strip the city’s slow clockwork down to rusted scrap.

A benevolent power does not break the clock to save it. It winds it. And it leaves a tool on the workbench.

The intervention happens at the tempo of the basin. It takes forty years, and no one ever looks up at the pale, washed-out sky in terror or awe.

First, in the lightless, crushing depths where the limestone meets the magmatic hearth, the power lays a hand against the shifting rock. It does not stop the eastern plume from cooling—for even gods must respect the mortality of mountains—but it coaxes the opening of the western fracture. Instead of a catastrophic rupture, the basalt parts by the width of a human hair each year. The power threads this new heat through a labyrinth of deep, porous dolomite before it ever reaches the city’s wells. By the time the new water rises into the intake tunnels, its fury has been spent; its harsh acids have been neutralized by the deep stone, and its temperature is a steady, blood-warm hundred and four degrees. The heart of the mountain has been successfully bypassed, a graft performed in the dark, ensuring that the great turbines will have their steam and pressure for another ten thousand years.

Second, the power addresses the creeping petrification of the people themselves. It loves their heavy, sea-cow bones and their opalescent teeth, and it knows the tenderness of the evening ritual when husbands and wives scrape the travertine dust from the corners of each other's eyes with ivory spoons. It would never take that touch away from them. 

Instead, it looks at the solitary, stunted woody shrubs with the thick, waxy grey leaves that thrust out of the cracks near the leaking steam-pipes. Over three generations of the plant's slow life, the power nudges its chemistry. The shrub begins to draw up a trace element from the wet masonry—a rare, benign chelate—and concentrate it in the pale, oily resin beneath its bark. 

One afternoon, a tender with a stiff, calcifying knee notices that a colony of flange-newts has gathered beneath a dripping steam-flange where one of these shrubs grows, greedily licking the amber droplets of resin that fall into the warm puddle. The tender, following the ancient wisdom of his people, trusts the silt-dogs. That evening, he scrapes a bead of the resin into his pot of steeping moss-tea. 

The tea tastes slightly of copper and pine. It does not make him young, and it does not make him fast. Moving fast in the basin would still kill him. But over the next few months, the sharp, gritty needles of calcium that had begun to stiffen the valves of his heart and the joints of his fingers gently dissolve back into solution, passing harmlessly from his body. The people begin to cultivate the waxy shrubs along the warm skirts of the aqueduct piers. They still die when they are old and tired, and they still become three-hundred-pound cocoons of pink alabaster in the Warm Wells—Wait-Stones for their grandchildren to lean against—but their seventy or eighty years of tending the clock are no longer spent in the slow vise of stiffening joints. Their fingers remain supple for Touch-Cant until the very last day.

Finally, the power gives them a song.

Because the citizens of the caldera do not hear the air, they have never known music in the way of valley-dwellers. They know only the steady, unvarying *B-flat* of a healthy turbine and the muddied *A* of a fouled valve—the utilitarian drone of survival. 

In the center of the municipal district, where four great iron conduits meet beneath a sunken plaza, there is an ancient, decommissioned cistern that has sat half-full of warm, stagnant condensate for five hundred years. The power does nothing more than alter the crystallography of the boiler-lith growing inside the feed-pipes of this forgotten vault, encouraging the brassy, metallic reefs to grow in spiral, fluted chambers of varying lengths, like the pipes of an immense, submerged organ. 

Simultaneously, the power introduces a slight, tidal variation into the deep subterranean barometric engines—a gentle, rhythmic oscillation of pressure that rises and falls over a twenty-four-hour cycle.

It takes twelve years for the reefs in the forgotten cistern to finish growing. When they do, the city changes forever, though an outsider walking across the bronze-railed footbridge would hear nothing different in the deafening roar.

To the people of the basin, walking with their flat-footed terrace-step across the wet flagstones, the ground begins to sing.

It is a sub-audible, tectonic symphony that travels entirely through the limestone and the water, rising up through the laminated felt and copper mesh of their boot-soles, resonating in their dense shinbones, their heavy ribs, and the plates of their skulls. At dawn, as the mountain’s barometric pressure crests, the cistern-organ sends out a slow, rolling chord of three interlocking frequencies—a deep, golden purr that feels like warm hands pressing against the small of the back. At midday, when the sun glints off the wet roofs and the glare is highest, the vibration shifts into a complex, syncopated counterpoint that makes the water in the shallow basins arrange itself into miles of delicate, trembling geometric grids—hexagons and stars of standing waves that the flange-newts swim through in lazy, hypnotic spirals. At dusk, the chord resolves into a low, velvet thrum that rockabyes the housing ziggurats, easing the minds of the tenders as they lie in their sleep-troughs.

The citizens do not fall to their knees and worship. That is not their way. Instead, they gather in the evenings on the wide, wet plazas around the sunken cistern. Thirty or forty of them in their mustard-colored oiled coats will stand in a wide circle in the warm, ankle-deep sheet-flow, planting the bronze ferrules of their walking sticks against the vibrating stone. They rest their hands on one another’s collarbones and shoulders, not to speak Touch-Cant, not to report on lithium-slurry or intake valves, but simply to feel the harmonic of the mountain pass through their own ribs and into the ribs of the person beside them, amplified and warmed by shared bone.

 across the half-mile sightlines, a maintenance warden on a high pipe-bridge will pause in his rounds, feel the noon-chord cresting through the iron beneath his feet, and raise a slow, oiled forearm against the pale blue sky. He holds it at a forty-five-degree tilt, lets his wrist soften in a gesture that has no technical meaning at all—a new syllable in Long-Arm that translates roughly to *Listen to the stone*—and four miles away, against the western rim, another tiny brass figure raises an arm to answer.
