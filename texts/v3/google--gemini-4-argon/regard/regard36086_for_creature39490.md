A lesser power, looking down through the three miles of crust, might have felt pity. 

A lesser power might have seen the parched hides, the agonizing conservation of every drop of grease, the blind eyes, and the slow, crushing labor of hanging from a lever for seven hours in the dark, and decided to "heal" the Spicule Forest. It might have cracked an aquifer above them to send cool, sweet water cascading down the basalt needles. It might have coaxed a gentle, oxygen-rich breeze through the stagnant air, or seeded the rock with soft, glowing mosses to cushion the razor-edges of the obsidian and light the gloom.

And in doing so, it would have murdered the world in a week. 

Water would have dissolved the selenite bridges, rusted the iron-sulfide shells of the mantles, and rotted the ancestral struts holding up the cairns, sending the cliners’ history and homes crashing into the abyss. Moisture would have bred soft, rapid molds that would devour the tallow-pots and blind the chemical sensors of the weavers. Wind would have shattered the three-dimensional glass harps, starving the web-spinners, and filled the acoustic lattice with a deafening, white-noise roar that would blind the cliners’ jaws to the subtle hum of their lithophones. Light would have been entirely meaningless to things that had not owned an optic nerve since the Miocene.

The power that turned its attention to the Spicule Forest was not lesser. It was old enough to understand that love is not the imposition of one’s own comfort upon another, and wise enough to recognize that the cliner, perched on its basalt needle with its gypsum knot, was not suffering. 

The cliner was home. Its slowness was not torpor; it was a high, austere mastery of thermodynamics. Its silence was not emptiness; it was a crowded room of resonant stone. The world was a masterpiece of tight, dry equilibrium.

For three hundred years—which was only a few generations in the slow clockwork of the thicket—the Power simply listened. It did not descend in a chariot of fire or speak with a voice that shook the stone. It pressed its own vast, immaterial jaw against the roots of the continent and felt the cliners’ world the way they felt it: as a cathedral of tensions, weights, and grain.

It felt the small, four-handed quarrymen hanging like pendulums in the dark, bleeding off the tectonic wrath of the planet one hair-crack at a time so their small spheres of domesticity could survive. And the Power saw what the cliners could not see.

Eleven miles away and four miles down, at the very base of the Spicule Forest where the sponge met the solid, superheated mantle of the earth, a fault line of titanic proportions was locking up. It was not a local stress in a feldspar strut that could be relieved with a meteoric chisel and six hours of patient hanging. It was a continental shear. When it finally slipped—perhaps in fifty years, perhaps in two hundred—it would not produce a blue flash and a pistol shot. It would send a shear-wave through the rigid, brittle lattice that would turn five hundred cubic miles of the Spicule Forest into falling gravel. Every glass web, every dry-stone cage, every ancestor-bridge, and every cliner would be pulverized in six seconds of roaring, grinding dark.

The Power considered this. 

It could simply erase the fault line, smoothing the deep rock like butter. Wants and physics would bow to it. 

*No,* the Power thought. *To take away the tension of the rock entirely is to make the watchmaker obsolete. If the rock does not creep, their chisels have no meaning, their maps of stress become dead history, and their gypsum poems lose their meter. They are creatures of the load.*

Instead, the Power reached into the deep, crushing dark eleven miles away, where no cliner had ever climbed, because the heat there would bake their parchment skins into brittle ash. 

Moving with the same glacial, respectful slowness as the creatures it watched, the Power began to alter the deep architecture of the fault. It did not remove the stress; it braided it. Over decades, it coaxed the deep magma and the shifting tectonic plates to extrude a new kind of mineral vein upward from the abyss, threading it through the lower reaches of the Spicule Forest like the roots of a metallic tree.

It was an alloy that did not quite belong to standard geology—a dense, ductile weave of native copper, gold, and fibrous, high-pressure silicate that possessed an extraordinary tensile strength and a near-zero acoustic damping factor. When the continental shear finally slipped, these deep, golden roots would act as shock absorbers, drinking the catastrophic shear-wave and converting its lethal kinetic violence into a low, harmless, century-long hum.

Wait, the Power thought. *Let that hum be something more.*

It drew a single, hair-thin filament of this deep, resonant vein all the way up through the miles of jagged sponge, threading it invisibly through the hearts of the basalt columns, until it terminated in the wide, lonely void where the solitary cliner liked to sit and read its gypsum knot.

The Power did one more thing. 

Sixty yards below the cliner’s perch, caught in a tangle of dead tourmaline needles where an exiled cliner had dried into a mummy three thousand years ago, there was a lost tool—a heavy maul of meteoric nickel-iron whose gut-string harness had finally snapped under the weight of centuries, dropping it into a crevice too narrow for a cliner’s shoulders to reach. 

With a breath no stronger than the静电 (electrostatic) brush of a glider’s wing, the Power nudged the iron maul. It slipped from the narrow crevice, fell twelve feet through the dead air, and wedged itself neatly onto a broad, accessible shelf of orthoclase, striking the stone with a single, clear *clink*.

Then the Power withdrew, leaving the dark to the dark.

On the needle above, the cliner froze. 

Its four thumbs stopped turning the gypsum knot. For twelve minutes, it did not even click its throat-valve to exhale. Its jaw, pressed tight against the basalt, drank the echo of that *clink*. 

It knew every stone within half a mile. It knew the weight of the fallen maul by the pitch of the impact; it knew the purity of the meteoric iron by the long, bell-like decay of the vibration; it knew the exact shelf of orthoclase it had landed upon. A gift of iron in the deep sponge was worth three generations of foraging. 

Slowly—so slowly that five piezoelectric flashes came and went in the distance before it had unhooked its lower heels—the cliner began to climb down toward the shelf.

Two hours later, it had secured the ancient maul to its back-harness. As it locked its double-thumbed hands onto the basalt column to begin the long crawl back toward its family cairn and the tallow-pot, it pressed its flat, drum-skinned brow against the stone to check the local tensions.

The cliner stopped. 

Through the basalt, up through the miles of intersecting needles, came a new note. 

It was not the sharp, nervous *ping* of a weaver’s glass, nor the *tick-scritch* of the mantles, nor the hollow, local *B-flat* of the family lithophones. It was a sub-audible, golden bass note—impossibly deep, impossibly steady, rising from the very floor of existence through the hidden filament in the rock. 

Because the new vein was braided into the entire lower continent, the vibration carried with it the reflected acoustic shadow of the whole world. 

For the first time in ten million years, a cliner felt beyond its half-mile sphere. Pressing its jaw harder against the rock, its fleshy nasal fronds flaring once in the still air, the creature felt the shape of a thousand other clearings scattered across fifty miles of the thicket. It felt the subtle, rhythmic thrum of ten thousand other cliners hanging from their levers, relieving the faults, tapping their stones, keeping the roof of the world from falling in. It felt the deep, braided roots beneath them all, holding the abyss at bay, promising that the lattice would stand for a million years more.

The cliner stayed there for a full day, its cheek welded to the warm stone, listening to the vast, newly connected choir of its kind working in the dark. 

Then, when its ribs finally expanded to draw in a slow, तीन-minute breath of ozone and dust, the cliner reached into its pouch, drew out a flake of sharp obsidian, and made a single, microscopic new notch in the center of its gypsum knot—a mark for a foundation that could not be broken—before climbing home through the dark to grease its knuckles and tell its kin through their wrists that the rock was good.
