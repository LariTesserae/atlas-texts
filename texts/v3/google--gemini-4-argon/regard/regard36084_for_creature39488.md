A lesser benevolence—one that confused its own preferences with the universal good—would have brought light. 

It would have cracked the exhausted shoulder of the mountain to let in the blue, high-altitude sky, or seeded the black-and-ochre mosses with bioluminescence so that the beauty of the room could be admired by eyes. It might have looked with pity upon the Balancers, seeing in their seven-hundred-pound mounds of blind, mucilaginous flesh a tragedy of devolution, and tried to "uplift" them—to give them hands to build with, or a language of nouns and verbs so they could name themselves and know they were trapped in a cave.

The power that found this place was older and kinder than that.

It did not enter the room, because to enter a room of eighty paces where the air itself is a medium of sight is to become a boulder thrown into a pond. Even an invisible presence displaces air; even a weightless footstep alters the millimeter-deep films of water on the soapstone floor. To step inside would be to deafen the Balancers with a sudden, terrifying geometry they had no historical memory to parse. 

Instead, the power remained outside, resting its attention against the cold, thin-aired granite of the mountain’s peak like a hand laid gently against the belly of a sleeping animal. It listened through four thousand feet of dead, grainless stone.

It took the power a very long time to understand what it was listening to, because it chose to learn the room at the room’s own speed. It watched a Balancer slide six feet an hour across the basin to liquefy an ambitious suede-tree. It waited through four cycles of the insects’ twenty-minute crescendos. It remained still for the months between the stuttering of the torus, and when the three pale mounds silenced the nave and built their solid bridge of sound in the dark, the power watched the invisible fields re-synchronize with a deep, quiet delight.

It saw at once that the chamber was a masterpiece of closed grace. There was no suffering here that needed curing, no ignorance that was not actually a profound and specialized wisdom. The Balancers did not know about the stars, yet they knew the exact dielectric constant of a wet crustacean’s shell at thirty degrees Celsius, and they knew it as music. To teach them about the sky would only make their room feel small.

Yet, because the power was wise, it looked beyond the room. It looked at the mountain, and it looked at the artifacts.

And there, in the long view of geological time, it found the two quiet dooms that were creeping toward the abbey-nave.

The first was the mountain itself. The range was exhausted, yes, its tectonic fury spent, yet even dead mountains are restless under the pull of gravity and the wedge of high-altitude ice. Four hundred yards above the tallow-smooth ceiling of the hollow, a fault line—dry, ancient, and jagged—was accumulating shear stress as the eastern flank of the peak slowly slumped toward a glacial valley. In nine hundred years, or perhaps twelve hundred, a winter of unusually deep freeze-thaw cycles would trigger a slip. The nave would not be crushed, yet the seal would be broken. A fissure three inches wide would crack through the soapstone roof. The thirty-degree fever would rush out into the sub-zero stratosphere; the dry, thin air of the summit would rush in. Within forty-eight hours, the Warm Wet would freeze solid. The Balancers, whose lungs knew only the thick, soup-like air of the greenhouse, would suffocate in the cold dark while their dorsal craters filled with frost.

The second doom was inside the artifacts.

The power looked into the five structures—the hovering torus, the leaning needles, the half-sunk way-shrines—and read the slow, unfathomable computation they had been running since the mountains were young. The machines were orphans. The civilization that had extruded them into the rock had vanished before the Balancers’ ancestors had lost their eyes, leaving behind a distributed survey-engine designed to calculate the folding of a planetary crust that had already finished folding epochs ago. 

The computation was not infinite; it was merely vast. And it was ninety-nine percent complete.

Furthermore, the reason the artifacts’ fields stuttered every few months—requiring the Balancers to build their exhausting, boiling-hot bridge of sound to re-synchronize them—was not merely the mineralized water or the crawling biomass. It was because the machines were reaching the final, degenerate equations of their task, where the variables approached zero and the logic-gates had to work harder and harder to find differences worth measuring. Within five thousand years, the computation would resolve. The torus would drop four inches onto its stone pedestal with a dull *clack*. The needles in the northern wall would go cold. The fields would collapse, and the dark would become simply dark.

The benevolent power considered these two endings. Then, with the care of a watchmaker repairing a clock while it is still ticking, it went to work.

First, it addressed the mountain. 

It did not weld the fault line shut; a rigid mass in a shifting range only invites a more violent fracture elsewhere. Instead, two hundred yards above the ceiling of the hollow, deep within the dry, cold rock where the Balancers’ sonar could never reach, the power altered the chemistry of the stone. It coaxed a dormant vein of serpentine and talc to absorb trace moisture from the deep earth, swelling and softening over the course of fifty years into a broad, curving dome of slick, plastic clay-stone directly over the nave. 

It built an umbrella of slip-planes. Now, when the eastern flank of the mountain finally gave way nine centuries hence, the shear stress would hit the talc-dome and slide harmlessly around the hollow, like river water parting around a smooth boulder. The abbey-nave would remain an intact, pebble-smooth seed inside the shifting rubble of the peak, sealed for another fifty million years.

Then, the power turned its attention to the artifacts. This required a far more delicate touch, for the machines were the instruments upon which the Balancers played their lives.

The power waited until the ozone tang sharpened in the dark. It waited until the torus-water began to stutter, falling in jagged, irregular splashes. It watched the three great mounds slide to their millimeter-precise stations around the central vault, flatten their snouts into the water-films, and issue the phase-inverted pulse that struck the room silent.

As the three Balancers began to sing—as the oldest raised the sine-wave of the northern needles, the second bent the harmonic of the western shrine, and the third laid down the sub-bass grid of Faraday ripples—the power reached its mind into the intersecting beams of solid sound at the center of the room.

It did not touch the Balancers. It touched the computation inside the torus.

While the acoustic bridge held the machine’s fields open and conductive, the power quietly erased the ancient, nearly finished survey of the planetary crust. In its place, it wrote a new problem for the artifacts to solve—a recursive, self-referential equation that had no final integer, yet was not a sterile loop.

It tied the computation to the acoustics of the room itself.

Using the physics of the vanished builders, the power re-indexed the machines’ logic-gates so that their primary input was no longer the dead tectonics of the mountain, but the microscopic piezoelectric vibrations of the soapstone floor and the dielectric shifts of the air inside the nave. The artifacts were given a new task: to calculate, to the billionth decimal place, the evolving harmonic ecology of the chamber they warmed, and to output that calculation as thermal and magnetic stability.

Yet the power knew that if it made the machines *too* stable—if the torus never stuttered again, if the needles never wavered—the Balancers would lose their highest art. Their great, saddle-shaped brains and the forty fluted craters along their backs had evolved to meet the challenge of the chord. Without the occasional need to silence the room and build the cathedral of pressure in the center of the dish, their minds would slowly smooth out, just like the stone, until they were only stomachs sliding through the wet.

So the power left the flaw in. 

Or rather, it transformed the flaw from a symptom of dying machinery into a scheduled question.

Now, every few months, when the machines’ calculation of the room’s acoustics reached a certain complexity, the torus would deliberately drop its efficiency by a fraction of a percent. The ozone would sharpen; the water-curtain would stutter. The Hearts would ask to be tuned.

And when the three Balancers gathered in the absolute dark, silenced the beasts, and poured their tremendous, trembling chord into the center of the vault, they would no longer merely be clearing a snag in an alien radiator. Their song would be the actual data the machines were waiting for—a fresh harmonic seed that the artifacts would take in, fold into their endless equation, and use to calibrate the warmth and the wet for the next season of growth.

The power added one final, tiny grace. 

Previously, the sound-bridge had taken six or twelve hours to re-synchronize the faltering fields, pushing the Balancers until the mucus boiled on their flanks from metabolic heat. The power adjusted the impedance of the torus’s boundary field just enough so that the synchronization would always click home at the end of the second hour—long enough for the Balancers to feel the deep, muscular satisfaction of a difficult song sung well, yet short enough that the oldest of them would never again sear its scarred skin with its own fever.

In the dark, the faint, non-physical *click* sounded.

The suspended mist collapsed into a smooth, continuous sheet of warm water. The ozone vanished beneath the smell of crushed celery and wet fur. 

The three Balancers let their dorsal craters slacken. They felt the newness in the room at once, though they did not know it as *new*; they felt it simply as a deeper, cleaner resonance in the floor beneath their bellies. The hum of the northern pylons had lost a tiny, jagged overtone that had been irritating the oldest Balancer’s rear-most craters for three hundred years. The water splashing from the torus sounded rounder, fuller, like a fruit that had finally ripened.

For a few minutes, the three pale mounds lay still in the quiet, tasting the perfected air. 

Then, beneath the pedestal, a ribbon-amphibian inflated its throat-sac and let out a low, percussive *boom*. The sound rolled across the dish, struck the suede-trees, and returned to the Balancers’ backs—clear, rich, and whole.

Outside on the freezing summit, the benevolent power withdrew its hand from the stone, leaving no footprint in the snow, and went on its way.
