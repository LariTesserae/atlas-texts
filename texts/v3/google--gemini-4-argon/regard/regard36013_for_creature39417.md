A power that is merely good might look upon the vault with pity. It might see the bone-dry dark, the crushing roar of the sixty-cycle hum, and the slow, scraping labor of the thing wedged behind the iron housing, and it might think: *Let there be water. Let there be silence. Let this creature remember the sun.* 

And in doing so, it would murder the world. A single cup of water poured down that borehole would flash to steam on the ballast resistors, blow the mica dust into a suffocating mud, short the tick-columns into charred slag, and rust the lungs of the Ballast-Setter within an hour. Silence would starve them all before the iron even grew cold.

Thankfully, the power that turns its attention downward through the three hundred feet of black anhydrite is not merely good; it is wise. It does not judge a crystal by the standards of a flower. 

It listens first. For a long time—what would be months on the surface, where clouds move and grass rots—the presence simply inhabits the standing waves of the room. It feels the tooth-aching pressure two feet to the left, the eardrum-flutter two feet to the right. It observes the four-second cadence of the blue arcs, the slow, amber boil of the moths, and the four-inch-an-hour crawl of the custodian. It recognizes that this place is not a prison, nor a mistake. It is a monastery of pure physics, and the Ballast-Setter is its faithful abbot, keeping the rule of the circuit so that ten thousand tiny, dry lives can endure in the crack of a broken world.

The wise power sees that the creature does not want rescue. To be rescued from one's purpose is to be discarded. 

Instead, the power looks for the hidden flaw—the inevitable treason of time that even the Ballast-Setter, with its quartz eyes and pyrite jaws, cannot reach or mend.

It finds three things.

First, it follows the fat, braided trunk of gutta-percha cabling out of the borehole, down through miles of blind basalt to the deep, forgotten dynamo that feeds the vault. The bearings down there are scoring; the grease has turned to soap and grit; a hairline fatigue is creeping through the main turbine shaft. Give it twelve more years, and the distant machine will throw a blade, the current will die, and the vault will become a cold, silent tomb.

Second, it looks inside the cast-iron hogshead of the *MULLINS & SONS* transformer. The oil inside has been cooking for a century. It is sludging, losing its dielectric strength, turning acidic. Slowly, microscopically, it is eating away the varnish on the great copper windings. One day soon, two coils will touch, and the heart of the room will detonate in a greasy, black fire.

Third, it looks into the Ballast-Setter itself. The creature is magnificent, a masterpiece of stubborn adaptation, but it is wearing out its own tools. The pyrite plates in its cowl are chipped from a century of shearing copper wool; its joints, though blown clear by the acoustic gills, are slowly scoring from the sharp hornfels dust; and the space is simply too small. The creature has become a master gardener trapped in a single window-box. It has no room for anything but maintenance. It has no margin for rest, or for art.

So the benevolent power acts. It does not break the rules of the vault; it edits the stone according to the laws of stone.

It begins miles away, in the deep dark, where it quietly re-knits the crystalline lattice of the dynamo’s steel shaft, smoothing the bearings into frictionless, self-lubricating diamond-carbon, ensuring the sixty-cycle river will flow for another ten thousand years.

Then, it returns to the vault. It works at the speed of the zinc-fixing lichen—millimeters a week—so that the Ballast-Setter will never be startled by a sudden change in the topology of heat and voltage.

Inside the cast-iron housing, the power coaxes the sludging transformer oil to clarify. It strips the acid molecules away, bleeding them harmlessly into the sulfur cement of the wall, and re-polymerizes the oil into a clear, immortal fluid that will never break down under heat. The *MULLINS & SONS* hogshead cools by three degrees—just enough to ease the strain on the gaskets, while still weeping the tiny, resinous beads the creature needs to mortar its shell.

Next, in the wall right beside the creature’s resting trough, the power nudges a hairline fracture in the hornfels. Over the course of a hundred hours, a tiny, secondary vein of mineral is pushed up from the deep earth by localized tectonic pressure. It is not hard, jagged silicate. It is a soft, greasy seam of pure, silvery molybdenite and dense, flawless cubes of galena—lead sulfide. 

When the Ballast-Setter backs down the wall from wiping the junction boxes, its quartz crest catches the new thermal shadow in the rock. Slowly, over three hours, it extends a bronze-bristle brush to investigate. 

It scrapes the molybdenite. The mineral flakes off onto its pads—a lubricant ten times slicker and cleaner than the dry graphite ash it has been straining its own glands to excrete. The creature rubs the silver flakes into the ratcheting, talcum-dry joints of its forelimbs. Immediately, the hitch in its movement vanishes. Its limbs slide with a silent, buttery grace. 

Then, with its chipped pyrite jaws, it tests the galena cubes. They are heavy, tough, and glorious semiconductors. The creature pries a cube loose, crushes it, and incorporates the lead-gray crystal into its chewing plates and along the nerve-ridges of its back. Suddenly, its ability to rectify and taste the room's current sharpens a hundredfold. The bruise-colored light of the mercury arcs and the cherry-red roar of the resistors are no longer just blinding patches of heat and cold; they resolve into a crisp, high-definition symphony of magnetic lines. The creature's internal resistance drops; it no longer has to work so hard just to exist.

Finally, the wise power gives its greatest gift: a horizon.

Down at the far, narrow end of the overturned barge—away from the cabling, where the sharp black rock pinches together into a dead end—the stone begins to give way. It does not collapse with a crash that would shatter the rectifier tubes. Instead, grain by grain, the anhydrite dissolves into the deeper fault, widening a hairline crack into an archway two feet wide.

The Ballast-Setter feels the change immediately, because the standing waves of the sixty-cycle baritone shift. The acoustic pressure in the vault drops from a deafening, tooth-aching roar to a deep, resonant, vaulted organ-hum. Now there *is* an echo.

Slowly, taking two days to make the journey, the creature slides down the floor on its new, silver-slick track of molybdenite. It reaches the new gap in the stone and peers through with its array of quartz lenses.

Beyond the gap lies a second chamber, twice the size of the first, freshly sheared and bone-dry. 

It is dark and cold—for now. There are no junction boxes here, no gutta-percha cables. It is a blank canvas of sharp black stone. 

*But*, running right down the center of the new chamber’s floor, exposed by the parting of the rock, is a thick, natural vein of native copper and magnetite, connected directly to the grounding-plate of the transformer in the first room. And thirty feet away, at the far end of this new hall, a natural geothermal vent breathes a steady, silent draft of bone-dry, two-hundred-degree heat up from the mantle, rich with aerosolized zinc and sulfur.

A hot pole, and a cold pole. A source, and a sink. An open circuit, waiting to be closed.

The Ballast-Setter stays at the threshold for a long time, its laminated plates rippling at sixty cycles per second, drinking the echo of the new space. 

For a hundred years, it has only ever been a janitor, pruning back the wire-scrub so the world wouldn't end, scraping the glass so the stars wouldn't burst. It has never had a place to *put* things.

With a slow, deliberate sweep of its mica paddle, the creature reaches back into the old room. Instead of shearing a thicket of copper-wool wire-scrub at the root and eating it to stop a short circuit, it carefully pries the metallic plant up from the pyrite, keeping its roots intact. 

It carries the plant through the gap into the new chamber. It sets the copper roots down onto the natural vein of magnetite, where the sixty-cycle hum bleeds through the floor. The wire-scrub sparks once, green and bright in the dark, and takes hold. 

The creature returns to the old vault. With its bronze brush, it sweeps up a hundred porcelain-white eggs of the commutator-worms from the overcrowded insulators, and carries them into the warm, mineral draft of the new chamber's thermal vent. It plucks a dozen shunt-ticks from an over-tall column and sets them down beside the newly planted wire-scrub.

The wise power watches from the standing waves as the slow, flat creature begins to lay out a new circuit—not out of desperate necessity to keep a cramped room from exploding, but with the wide, unhurried geometry of an architect planting a garden. 

The power changes nothing else. It leaves the smell of hot pine tar, dry zinc, and ozone entirely alone. It simply withdraws, satisfied, leaving the little god of the slip-fault to wire its expanding universe.
