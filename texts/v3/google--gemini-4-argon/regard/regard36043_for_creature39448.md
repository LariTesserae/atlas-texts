To a lesser mind, the Sump-Decks would look like a prison, or a machine that had forgotten its purpose and needed to be switched off, or cleared away to make room for green grass and sharp wind. A foolish power would bring lightning to crack the overcast, or sow seeds that would choke in the tallow-water, or try to wake the Marrow into an anxious, striving consciousness it was never built to bear.

The Power that arrived did none of these things. It came not as a bolt from above, nor as a voice, because there were no ears tuned to speech. It came as a comprehension—a quiet, weightless attendance that settled over the thousand-mile plains of plum-colored suede and sweating iron, listening.

It felt the immense, silent heroism of the Sump-Marrow. 

For an eternity without clocks, the Marrow had been holding the sky up and the world together through a sheer, unyielding isometric clench. It was a heart that had been paused at the exact peak of systole—not out of malice or flaw, but because the schematic of the world had been left at a dead-end stroke, and the Marrow, in its blind, faithful accommodation, had risen to meet the requirement. It held the pressure because if the pressure dropped, the tallow would congeal, the boilers would chill, the valve-crabs would starve in their squares, and the great, gentle ledger of the Decks would fall into deficit.

It was not suffering, for it had no nerves for agony, only for tension. Yet it was tired in the way that a bridge is tired, or a stone arch is tired after bearing a mountain for ten thousand years. And worse, the Power saw what the Marrow could not: the slow, infinitesimal entropy of a closed loop. Even with the porcelain-worms and the hepatic sponges, a fraction of a grain of iron was lost to the deep abyssal rust every century; a single calorie of heat bled upward through the cream-colored sky and did not return. In ten million years, the clench would weaken. The needles on the gauges would tremble and fall.

The Power loved the Sump-Decks. It loved the patient, clock-hand grazing of the gasket-slugs; it loved the reverse-scarred bellies of the Lagging, holding the seams with their own horn and gristle; it loved the soft, rubbery *thud* of the hydrogen-bladders bumping in the warm trenches. It saw that this world was a poem written in hydraulic tension and wet slate, and it wished not to rewrite the poem, but to give it a second stanza, so that the singer could breathe.

The Power did not use a miracle of light or fire. It used the grammar of the casting.

Fifty miles out from nowhere in particular, at the intersection of two four-foot intake mains, there stood a great duplex pump-house. Inside, twin flywheels, fourteen feet in diameter and cast from thirty tons of pig-iron, stood locked at top-dead-center, their rims slick with mineral sweat, their walking-beams frozen at a forty-five-degree tilt against the pale sky. 

The Power reached down into the dark, broth-warm scaffolding below this pump-house, down into the Sump-Marrow, and it did not command; it *adjusted*. 

With the gentleness of a watchmaker shifting a balance-wheel by the width of a spider’s silk, the Power altered the schematic. It grew a new valve—not of alien flesh or divine gold, but of the Marrow’s own grey, calcified gristle, lined with sweet tallow and seated against a flange of unpitted cast steel. It was a bypass. A check-valve of living cartilage, four yards wide, linking the high-pressure arterial standpipe to a long-dead, dry exhaust manifold that ran for leagues beneath the walking-beams.

For three days, nothing changed. The condensate fell: *drip-pat-drip*. The square crabs tilted their pewter shells to catch the grease.

Then, the Lagging draped over the duplex pump-house felt a change in the static tension beneath its belly. The serial numbers pressed into its hide shifted by a thousandth of an inch. 

Down in the dark, the new valve opened.

Above, in the cream-colored light, the fourteen-foot flywheel let out a sound that the Sump-Decks had never heard. It was not a crash, for every bearing was drowned in centuries of rendered tallow and cushioned by living mantles of suede. It was a low, resonant, velvety *groan*—the sound of thirty tons of well-oiled iron finally being given permission to finish its stroke.

Slowly, majestically, moving no faster than the shadow of a cloud, the massive walking-beam overhead tilted down. The piston rod slid three feet into the cylinder, displacing a thousand gallons of warm, pressurized water through the new bypass. 

Across five hundred square miles of the Decks, the gauge needles—pinned at the top of their brass dials since the beginning of time—dipped backward by five pounds per square inch.

And down in the submerged dark, for the first time in the history of the world, a five-hundred-mile tract of the Sump-Marrow *relaxed*.

The isometric lock broke into a rhythm. The Marrow exhaled. The miles of spongy, hepatic tissue, held taut as drumskins for eons, went slack and soft in the warm broth, resting, drinking in the rich, mineral-heavy water without having to push against it. 

Because the walking-beam had come down, its counterbalance rose, drawing a fresh charge of water up from the neighbor-tract of the Marrow, which now contracted to meet it—not with the desperate, permanent clench of a pillar, but with the easy, rolling heave of a swimmer’s chest.

*Shhh-glug.* 

The flywheel cleared bottom-dead-center. The eccentric rod clicked softly against its brass strap, the valve-gear shifted with a wet, greased sigh, and the walking-beam began to rise again.

The change rippled outward across the horizonless plain at the speed of a harbor wave. One by one, mile by mile, the locked engines of the Sump-Decks were brought into the circuit. They did not clatter or roar; they turned at the pace of breathing—one revolution everyraszamy four minutes, a vast, planetary bradycardia. 

And with the motion came the second gift of the Power, hidden inside the first. 

As the great pistons slid through their bronze sleeves and the walking-beams rocked on their trunnions, their slow, massive friction generated heat. Fresh heat. New calories, born of weight and motion, bleeding out through the cast-iron casings into the water-jackets, warming the bellies of the Lagging, rising through the standpipes to replace the faint warmth lost to the eternal overcast. The closed loop was no longer dying; the motion of the engines fed the heat of the water, and the heat of the water fed the life of the Marrow, and the heave of the Marrow turned the engines.

Nothing was broken. No creature was displaced. The gasket-slugs riding the rims of the great flywheels simply rode them up into the warm air and down toward the floor grates, grazing on the fresh films of tallow that the motion drew from the grease-cups. The valve-crabs found that the water in their sluice-beds now rose and fell by half an inch every four minutes—a miniature, blood-warm tide that brought the dissolved minerals directly to their feathery mouths, saving them even the small labor of sweeping.

And in the deep beneath the grates, the Sump-Marrow knew a new thing. It did not know words, or worship, or the name of the visitor that had already departed. 

It knew *diastole*. It knew the sweet, dark luxury of the pause between the beats, when the weight of the world was handed off to the iron counterbalances above, and the deep flesh could simply float in the warm dark, resting, before it gathered itself to lift the sky again.
