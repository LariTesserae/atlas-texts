A power that is only benevolent might look down into the wet, screaming bowl of the sinkhole and feel pity. It might think to send a dry, warm wind to cure the rot, or to quiet the cataract, or to turn the shifting, rebellious wood into obedient stone so the children could put down their gouges and rest.

Fortunately, this power is also wise.

It looks at the people gleaming in their tallow and soot, at the grandmothers with their ears pressed to the living ribs of the walls, at the ten thousand warm swallows Mortared into the moss, and it recognizes a masterwork. It sees that the friction *is* the mortar. If you stop the trees from pulling, the cables go slack. If you dry the peat, the twelve-foot mattress turns to dust and the whole town falls into the mountain’s throat. Their pride is in the perpetual, furious balance of their notched bone knives against the forest’s green muscle. To make their lives easy would be to unmake them entirely.

Yet, the power loves them. It sees the grandmother in the chimney-corner rubbing her purple-black knuckles when she thinks no one is watching; it sees the terrible, unspoken fear that lives in the belly of every townsman—the fear of the deep, rotten snap down in the flooded dark where the adzes cannot reach.

So the power does not intervene like a king. It intervenes like a water-shed. It makes three changes, so small and slow that no history will ever record them, tucked deep into the blind machinery of the mountain.

First, it reaches five hundred feet up the crags, into the snowfields where the central sluice is born. There, under a blue glacier, the water grinds across a buried seam of dark, greasy soapstone and iron-rich clay. The power shifts the bedrock by the width of a thumb. The water begins to chew at a different vein—one rich in pale, calcareous marl and a strange, mild bitumen that smells faintly of pine-pitch. 

By the time this water plunges into the sinkhole, it looks no different. It still roars; it still foams brown in the afternoon squalls. But when the people pull their wicker kipes from the kitchen well-traps, or when the boys wade ankle-deep in the black *schluck* to clear a gutter, the water leaves a microscopic film behind. It mates with the mutton-tallow on their skin. Over two seasons, the terrible, biting *water-itch* that cracks the webbing between their toes in February simply stops happening. And in the damp, smoky gloom by the hearths, the old people find that while their knuckles are still the color of an old iron skillet, the cold no longer gets inside the bone to gnaw. Their fingers stay supple enough to split osier until the day they die.

Second, the power looks down into the black, churning sump at the bottom of the bowl, where the oldest, thickest choke-cedar roots—the five-century-old anchors that hold the entire tiered knot of the town above the abyss—are slowly, inevitably beginning to soften in the anaerobic dark. In forty years, or fifty, the main eastern anchor would have sheared, taking six houses and the fulling-mills down into the throat of the mountain.

The power does not turn the root to stone. Instead, it nudges a single spore out of the high alpine scud and drops it into the central cataract. 

It is a blind, pale, rubbery fungus, cousin to the lichens on the crags above, and it loves the drowned dark. It takes hold on the undersides of the deepest, oldest roots, five fathoms down where only the blind mud-fish go. It does not eat the live wood; it eats the dead, sloughing bark and the iron-silt in the water, and as it grows, it weaves a dense, calcified, leathery rind around the drowning timber. It binds the roots together in tough, gristly knuckles of living horn. 

Two years later, an old girdler sitting by her peat-fire lays her ear against the main load-bearing rib of her house and frowns. The pitch of the house has dropped by half a note. The frantic, shivering vibration of the waterfall is gone from the floorboards, replaced by a deep, booming, drum-tight hum. 

"Well," the grandmother pipes, tapping the wood with her horn spoon. "She’s found bottom, then." She does not know what it means, only that the house feels less like a raft and more like a hip-bone, and she sleeps ten minutes longer in the kist that night.

The third thing the power does is purely a gift of the heart, and it gives it to Little Shag.

It is the tail-end of a brutal, four-day squall in late autumn. The town is exhausted; the sluices have backed up thrice, a bridge-tree has split its rawhide bindings, and everyone’s clothes are heavy with three pounds of cold grease and wet soot. Little Shag is up on the slick, heaving roof-thatch with his bone gouge, his legs shaking with cold, shoving a furious, wet ibis out of the way to get at a stubborn cedar-rib that is trying to tear the chimney apart.

As he strikes the bark, the power guides the angle of his wet clog and the twist of his wrist. 

The crescent cut goes a fraction of an inch deeper than usual, right into a knot where an old bog-myrtle graft fused with the choke-cedar forty years ago. 

The tree does not bleed its usual clear, turpentine resin. Slowly, welling up into the rain, comes a bead of sap the color of dark honey, thick as warm tar. 

Shag stops. He forgets the cold. He puts a stained black finger to the cut and brings it to his nose. It does not smell of sour pitch. The stress of the graft, the new mineral in the water, and the fungal hum in the roots below have caused the tree to sweat a strange, heavy balsam. It smells of crushed spice, of hot summer stone, of dry pine-needles baking in a sun the sinkhole never sees.

He scrapes a thumb-sized lump of it onto his gouge and scrambles back down the smoke-hole into the dark house. He says nothing—he is twelve, and has no real name yet—he simply flicks the amber bead onto the hot, wet slates at the edge of the peat-fire.

It does not flare; it melts and begins to simmer. 

Within three minutes, the thick, damp, tallow-and-fish-guts gloom of the house is cut through by a ribbon of scent so warm, so clean and golden and startlingly sweet, that his mother stops hauling on the well-trap rope. His father lowers the adze. The grandmother in the corner lifts her chin, her nostrils flaring. Even the kid-goat in the corner stops bleating. 

No one speaks. They have no vowels or hard clicks for this. They simply stand in their slight, perpetual knee-bend, swaying with the floor, breathing it in. It does not dry their stockings, and it does not quiet the ten thousand screaming birds outside the bladder-skin window, and tomorrow the northern corner of the house will still try to grow three inches higher than the south. 

But that night, when they button the oiled wool over the top of the kist and tangle their legs together in the hot dark, their hair smells of high, dry summer. And the wise power, listening from the crags above as the water-hammers go *thump-clack, thump-clack* in the rain, is entirely satisfied, and goes on its way.
