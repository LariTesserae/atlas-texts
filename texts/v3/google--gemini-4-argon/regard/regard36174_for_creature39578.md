To a power that has watched stars ignite and cool, most things that call themselves "civilizations" are brief, feverish things. They are made of fire and straight lines. They quarry the bone of the earth, stack it against gravity, and spend their short centuries fighting the moss that tries to pull it back down. They are loud with intent.

This place is different. 

When the Benevolence turns its attention to the high saddle—drawn not by a prayer, for nothing here prays, nor by a cry of suffering, for everything here is too busy to despair—it feels a sudden, profound stillness of spirit. It recognizes a masterpiece of the blind hand.

For a long time, measured in the slow, four-minute waves of the termites’ hammering, the Presence does nothing at all. It simply inhabits the bruised indigo air. It lets the roar of the katydids wash through its awareness. It feels the ninety-degree fever of the compost vaults and the cool, acidic kiss of the stratus clouds above. It watches the *thrum-heavy* shuffle down the greased trough, feeling the ache in the creature's old hip, the pleasant emptiness of its drained salivary glands, the tiny, frantic tickle of the brass-colored parrots on its rump.

A lesser god—a vain one—would be tempted to "uplift" this. It would drop a spark of ego into the wet, dark brain of the arch-weaver, or open the furred eye-dimples of the badger-beast so it could look upon the fifty-foot arches and know awe. 

The Wise Power knows better. To give them self-awareness would be to give them loneliness. To show them the blueprint of the mountain would be to turn their ancient, soothing compulsions into labor. If the *thrum-heavy* knew it was an architect, it would begin to fear the collapse of the roof; right now, it only knows the deep, jaw-settling satisfaction of good paste meeting a sour wall. The city works because it is a single body dreaming it is a billion mouths. To wake it would be murder.

Yet, the Power looks down—deep down, five hundred feet beneath the oldest cured mastic, down where the original granite peak lies entombed like a dead bone inside living meat.

And there, the Power sees the flaw. 

The valley is too successful. It has grown too heavy, and too hot. 

Down in the deepest fungal vaults, where the leaf-litter of three millennia has been packed under millions of tons of mud-architecture, the heat is no longer ninety-eight degrees. In the blind, airless pockets against the ancient granite, the temperature is climbing toward a hundred and fifty. The thermophilic bacteria are working too fast. Within three generations, a pocket of methane and superheated steam will build beneath the western horn of the saddle. Worse, the sheer mass of the biological edifice—lubricated by the perpetual condensation seeping down the bedrock—is beginning to creep. One unusually heavy monsoon on the plains below, one tremor in the fault-line fifty miles south, and the entire three-mile sponge of mud and life will shear off the granite peak beneath it, sliding into the lowlands in a single, catastrophic avalanche of broken arches and boiling compost.

The Benevolence will not let this happen. Not to a work of ten thousand generations.

It does not summon a miracle of light or stone. It works in the dialect of the valley: chemistry, vibration, and slow growth.

First, it reaches down to the boundary where the ancient mastic meets the smooth, treacherous slope of the buried granite. It does not fuse them with heat; instead, it whispers to the dormant spores of a deep-earth lichen that were trapped there three thousand years ago when the first red clay was carried up from the plains. 

Under the touch of the Power, the spores wake, altered. They do not need light. They feed on the crushing pressure itself—piezoelectric and ravenous for silica. Slowly, at the pace of cooling wax, the mycelium of this new, divine fungus begins to drill downward into the solid granite, and upward into the petrified leather of the mud-city. It forms microscopic threads of biological iron and calcium oxalate—millions of them, then billions, braiding the living mountain to the dead stone bone beneath it like tendons anchoring muscle to a femur. The shear-plane is stitched shut. The city will never slide.

Second, the Power addresses the fever in the deep vaults. 

Fifty yards down the greased trough, the *thrum-heavy* stops. 

It does not know why it stops. It only feels a new vibration in its breastbone—a deep, resonant hum coming from the floor of the plaza, pitched just below the soldier termites’ warning wave. It is not an alarm. It feels, to the badger-beast’s blind sternum, like an itch. A very specific, directional itch.

And with the itch comes a scent, drifting up through a microscopic fissure in the floor: the smell of sour, overheated clay, laced with a sudden, mouth-watering trace of salt and trace minerals the beast has been craving all winter.

The *thrum-heavy* turns its broad, shovel-snout toward the floor of the sunken plaza. Its forty pale barbels flare, tasting the new, phantom pheromone. Without hesitation, it braces its ironwood hind claws against the base of the whale-rib arch, lowers its head, and bites into the floor.

Not across. *Down.*

As it chews into the ancient, compressed floor-mastic, the Power gently tweaks the chemistry of the gourds along its neck. The alkaline mucilage flooding its mouth grows slightly richer in cross-linking proteins; as the beast extrudes the paste to line the sides of the new shaft it is digging, the mortar cures in minutes rather than hours, vitrifying into a pipe as hard as stoneware.

Within an hour, three more *thrum-heavies* have converged on the plaza, drawn by the deep, comforting hum in their breastbones and the phantom scent of salt. They take turns in the shaft, spiraling downward into the hot dark, passing the excavated clay back to the surface where five million amber ants are already waiting to carry the warm pellets up to the outer retaining walls.

It will take them four months to reach the deep vaults. When they breach the pocket of trapped methane and scorching compost five hundred feet down, it will not explode; it will sigh upward through the stoneware chimney the badgers have lined with their spit. The killing heat will vent safely into the upper canopy, warming the broad-leafed figs and giving the mantle-slugs another twenty feet of vertical territory to glaze.

The crisis is averted before anything in the valley could ever know it existed.

Before the Presence withdraws its attention to return to the cold, wheeling dark between the stars, it leaves one final, small gift. It is a purely selfish act of affection for the blind mason at the bottom of the trough.

Up on the shoulders of the first *thrum-heavy*, among the pale grey bracket fungi and miniature ferns rooted in its tallow-grease, a single spore settles. 

Within a week, it will sprout into a pale, creeping moss that threads its way down between the grooved peat-bristles toward the base of the creature’s skull. This moss does not just drink the condensation; its tiny, hair-like rhizomes secrete a mild, continuous trace of lipid-soluble analgesic and a warm, mineral oil that seeps directly into the thick skin over the beast’s arthritic hip and the calloused hinges of its jaw. 

The mammoth-badger will never see the moss. It will never know it has been blessed. It will only know that as the winters pass, the deep ache in its joints has gone away, the mud-chutes feel smoother against its flanks, and the clay in its mouth tastes sweeter than it ever has before.

In the bruised indigo dark, beneath the roaring wall of the crickets, the beast spits a ribbon of warm paste against the wall, pats it twice with its nose—*thock-thock*—and keeps working.
