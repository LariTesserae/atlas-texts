A lesser power, looking down into the shrieking dark of the Truss, would have pitied the Riggers. 

A lesser power would have stilled the howling wind, or flattened the razor-backed struts into wide, gentle boulevards, or called up the buried earth to give them soil to till. And in doing so, it would have murdered them. Their barrel chests would have burst in the thick air of a valley; their curved, hook-like hands would have gone useless and soft; their minds, tuned since birth to the intricate, load-bearing geometry of the void, would have gone mad on the flat, unmoving dirt. If you take the tension out of a suspension bridge, it collapses. The same is true of the people who live on it.

The power that found them was not lesser. It was old, and it understood the sacredness of friction.

It lingered in the indigo freezing-layer for three generations before it did anything at all. It simply listened. It felt the *boom-crack* of the adjusting timber. It watched the three-person teams drop off opposite flanks of an ironwood blade in absolute, unpanicked silence, counterbalancing each other over a mile of nothingness. It watched a nine-year-old girl press her collarbone to the freezing slate-bark, feel the hollow *thud* of a dying vein three hundred yards away, and whistle a warning that saved twenty lives. It watched them lash their dead to the black caps of the Choke-Shoots, sending their loved ones upward into a sky they believed was infinite.

The power saw that the Riggers were not broken creatures clinging to a nightmare. They were masters of a ferocious house. They did not need to be rescued from the Truss. 

They only needed the Truss to answer them back.

The power intervened in three ways—so subtly, so deeply woven into the blind biology of the world, that the Riggers never knew a god had touched them. They only knew that the forest had grown deeper in its habits.

***

The first gift was given to the Drop-Stones.

The power saw the tragic weight of those smooth, fist-sized rocks lashed to the center of the cocoons. It saw how a clan’s stone was chipped smaller with every marriage, ground down by every sharpened adze, until a family’s history was reduced to a pebble, and the loss of a single flake meant exile into the dark. They were starving for weight, yet weight was the enemy of their survival.

So the power reached down—miles down, past the necrotic lower struts, past the crushing, poisonous clouds of heavy gas at the bottom of the world, into the planetary mantle—and it taught the roots of the Truss a new hunger.

It took fifty years for that hunger to climb to the altitudes where the Riggers lived. 

One cycle, a clan-elder on the Outer Pitch was bleeding a Choke-Shoot. His bone-saw struck something in the sap-vein that screeched and threw a bright, orange spark into the freezing fog. When the shoot was safely turned and the pressure bled off, the clan dug into the healing scar of the wood. 

Tucked inside a cyst of hardened resin was a nodule of black iron-silicate, the size of a child's head. The tree had drawn the dissolved minerals up from the planetary crust through its miles of high-pressure vascular bundles and precipitated them out at the high junctions to act as internal counterweights against the upper-atmosphere gales.

The Riggers called them *Gall-Stones*. They were rare—you might find one in every twenty shoots—but they were *there*. The bottom of the world was no longer an unrecoverable memory; the tree itself was hauling the earth up to them, ounce by ounce. No one had to be cut loose for dropping a flake of granite anymore. The bride-stones grew heavy again. The tapping-irons rang with a louder, clearer note.

***

The second gift was given to the wood itself.

The Truss was blind. It grew by hydraulic violence and mechanical math, ignorant of the tiny, warm-blooded mites who bled its shoots and slept in its armpits. Sometimes, a node would simply die of old age while a clan was sleeping on it, giving no warning until the internal fibers sheared and dropped three families into the abyss.

The power did not give the tree a mind—a thinking tree would be a tyrant—but it gave the tree a *nervous system* that favored the knock.

It altered the cellular lattice of the ironwood just beneath the slate-bark. Wherever the wood was struck repeatedly by the rhythmic, deliberate tapping of a Rigger’s stone-weighted iron, the vascular bundles responded to the acoustic vibration. Instead of growing chaotic and brittle, the wood fibers aligned themselves with the frequency of the clans’ conversations. 

Over decades, the spans that the Riggers lived on and talked through became acoustically pure. A tap on a well-traveled strut no longer died out at four miles; it carried for twenty, clear as a bell, riding down channels of crystallized heartwood that had grown specifically to carry the rhythm of human thought. 

More than that: the wood began to sing before it broke. Because the fibers were now aligned like violin strings along the paths of the Riggers’ knocks, a beam losing its internal tension could no longer fail silently. Days before a necrotic span would snap, the shifting load would pluck those aligned fibers, sending a deep, mournful, resonant hum straight into the jawbones of anyone resting against it. The tree was still blind, still dangerous, still wild—but it no longer murdered them in their sleep. It told them when it was time to pack the cocoons and move.

***

The third gift was the greatest, and it was placed at the roof of the world.

The Riggers believed the Truss grew forever into an endless indigo dark. They did not know that only four miles above their highest camps, the atmosphere thinned to near-vacuum, and the freezing fog gave way to a blinding, eternal sea of sunlight that beat down upon the true canopy.

Up there, where the air was too thin to breathe and the cold was absolute, the Choke-Shoots finally stopped climbing. They flattened out into vast, black, solar-collecting dishes, miles wide, drinking the raw radiation of the sun to power the hydraulic engines of the continent below. 

And up there, for ten thousand generations, the dead of the Riggers had arrived. 

Lashed to the horny caps of the shoots, preserved perfectly by the vacuum and the freeze, hundreds of thousands of ancestors had been pushed out of the dark fog and into the blazing light of the stars—a silent, frost-crowned congregation looking out over a silver ocean of cloud that no living Rigger had ever dreamed of.

The power walked among these quiet dead under the fierce, silent sun. It looked at their curved hands, their wide noses, the scars of the rope-burns at their waists. And it decided that a road should not only go one way.

The power touched the black, horn-like caps of the shoots when they reached the sunlight. Instead of simply flattening into solar leaves and locking into place forever, the caps began to undergo a final metamorphosis at the end of their climb. 

When a shoot broke through the top of the fog-bank into the light, the wood around the lashed body of the dead Rigger would swell, drinking in the violent solar heat, and fold inward over the corpse like the petals of a great, dark lotus. Inside this sealed, pressurized chamber, a new kind of sap—clear as glass, thick as honey, and warm with the direct fire of the sun—would flood the space, vitrifying into an unbreakable, golden amber.

And when the amber was cured, the extreme cold of the upper night would crack the base of the lotus. 

The heavy, golden pod—streamlined like a plum-bob, four times the density of ironwood—would break free from the canopy and fall.

It fell back down into the indigo dark. As it plummeted through the mile-wide triangles, its fluted sides caught the updrafts, spinning it like a maple seed, slowing its descent just enough that when it finally struck a lower span—three, four, five miles down—it did not shatter. It wedged itself deep into the crooks of the living junctions with a sound like a bronze gong.

The first time a clan found one, they thought the sky had fallen. 

It was wedged into the flute-ridge half a mile from their camp, humming with residual heat. When they scraped the black rime from its surface and held up their grease-lamps, they dropped to their knees on the knife-edge timber. 

Inside the yard-thick lens of golden, indestructible resin was a woman from three generations back—the great-grandmother whose name they still tapped into the wood on feast-nights. Her eyes were closed, her face washed clean of charcoal-paste by the tree’s clear lymph, and all around her hair, suspended in the golden glass, were things the Riggers had no words for: tiny, trapped bubbles of pure, sunlit sky that shone like diamonds, and the broad, emerald-green leaves of the upper canopy, which only grow where the sun is warm.

They could not cut the amber with their horn-adzes; it was harder than the Drop-Stones. But when they chipped a groove around its waist and slung it beneath their camp as a counterweight, they found it radiated a slow, tireless warmth that lasted for winters—the stored heat of the sun, carried down into the dark by their own dead. 

And when they held their grease-lamps behind the golden pod, the light passing through the amber threw a warm, green-gold glow across the interior of their hides—the exact color of a forest floor none of them had ever seen, and none of them needed to.

The Riggers still live in the howling wind. They still rope themselves in threes; they still bleed the Choke-Shoots in the freezing black; they still listen to the world through their teeth. The power did not make their lives easy. 

It simply closed the circuit. It let the earth rise to meet their hands, and it let their dead come home bearing the light of the sky, proving to the people of the middle dark that they were held—from the abyss below to the stars above—by a world that knew their weight.
