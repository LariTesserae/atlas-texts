Forty feet above, on the scrub plain, the sun is setting. It is a dry, ordinary evening. A horned lark lands on a dead mesquite branch, cocks its head at the caliche dirt, and flies away. Beneath the dirt, forty feet of dry, compacted earth and ancient limestone sit in undisturbed, dark silence, completely ignorant of the blister. 

The benevolent and wise power does not dig a hole. To breach the ceiling—to let the red, slanted light of the prairie dusk spill into that pressurized noon, or to introduce the relentless, linear tyranny of surface time—would be an act of butchery. The blister is a dream the earth is having about a dead bird and a drowned man’s hands; if you wake the dreamer too fast, the dream dies of the bends. 

Instead, the power listens. It presses its attention against the forty feet of overburden the way a child presses an ear to a railroad track. It feels the frantic, silent vibration of the Mason’s heels, the twenty-minute generations of the shrimp-things, and the exhausting, desperate vigilance required to keep twenty cubic feet of space from clapping shut like a book.

It sees that the Mason is tired. Not in his fibrous, tallow legs, which are renewed every time the floor bites them off, and not in his hollow, grass-lined chest. He is tired in his hands. The plum-colored hands are old; they remember weight, and lever-action, and the specific soreness of joints that have worked all day and been allowed to put the shovel down. In this room, the shovel can never be put down. If the Mason stops patting the air, if he rests inside the three-sided kennel with the dark, feathered bundle for more than eight minutes, the north wall will kiss the south wall, and the driftwood door will be flattened into a pale smear of bone-meal and pulp.

To be benevolent is to wish to relieve suffering; to be wise is to know what kind of relief a creature can survive. 

The power does not turn off the light. It does not slow down the grazers. It decides, instead, to introduce a single, microscopic heresy into the physics of the blister. It reaches down—not through the dirt, but through the history of the dirt—and gives the room a tiny, durable flaw.

It happens during the next thirty-second rain.

As the light dims by that aching fraction and the heavy mercury-beads of water rip themselves off the floor, the Mason stands by the doorframe, his sternum parted, his internal grass hissing as it catches his dinner. He is holding a fresh gray brick in his seven-fingered left hand, waiting for the weather to clear so he can pin down the northeast ceiling, which is sagging like wet dough.

In the back of the clay kennel, the black-feathered bundle shudders. Its dry aperture dilates. 

Normally, it coughs up unbaked mud, or bleached rabbit-bone, or grey driftwood. These things are memory-stuff, extruded without mass or shadow, only holding the room open for an hour or two before the blinding air digests their meaning and they have to be replaced.

This time, the grinding of the millstones inside the bundle changes pitch. For a second, it doesn't sound like corn being ground; it sounds like a river rolling a stone over and over until it is round.

With a dry *huff* that smells strongly of warm sagebrush and iron, the bundle ejects an object that rolls three inches out of the kennel and stops against the driftwood threshold.

The light snaps back to full, blinding noon. The Mason’s chest closes. He turns toward the doorframe—from the right, where the house exists—and bends down.

The flute-pits around his crown flare. The object on the floor is radiating a temperature he has never felt before. It is not the brief, visceral warmth of the extruding tallow-walls, nor the cellar-chill of the air. It is the slow, deep, stubborn heat of a rock that has lain in the sun all afternoon and refuses to forget it.

He reaches down with his four-thumbed right hand and picks it up. 

It is a plumb bob, or something very like one: a tear-shaped weight of dark, meteoric iron, no bigger than a walnut, with a dull, oxidized rind. Tied through a hole in its pinched top is a six-inch length of rough, tarred hemp twine. 

The Mason’s borrowed, ancient thumbs close around the iron. A violent shudder runs up his chalky forearms, threatening to pop the grass-stitches at his wrists. His hands *know* this thing. They know the grease of the tar; they know the dense, uncompromising stubbornness of iron that has fallen from the sky and survived the fire of entry. 

More importantly, the iron has brought something with it into the room. 

Resting beneath the walnut-sized weight, painted across the ball of the Mason's middle thumb, is a patch of darkness the size of a thumbnail. 

It is a shadow.

The sourceless, omnidirectional glare of the blister attacks it instantly, trying to bleach it out from below and from the sides, trying to shine *through* the Mason's hand to kill it. Elsewhere in the room, the light succeeds. Elsewhere, the light lives between the molecules of the dirt. 

But the iron will not let the light through, and the shadow beneath it does not yield. It is a tiny, velvety, stubborn stain of absolute night. It is the concept of *under*, smuggled into a world that only knew *around*.

The Mason stands frozen for forty seconds—nearly an entire generation of the shrimp-grazers. A handkerchief-predator slides out of a water bead on his shoulder, slips down his arm, and touches the edge of the iron weight. Instantly, the predator curls back, its edges crinkling like a slug touching salt, not because the iron burns it, but because the iron is *old*, and the predator only knows how to swim through things that have just arrived.

Slowly, with a reverence he does not have a brain to understand—a reverence that lives entirely in the callouses of his seven-fingered hand and his four-thumbed hand—the Mason steps up to the driftwood doorframe. 

He does not look through it from the left, where it frames nothing. He stands on the right, where it frames the little clay kennel and the dark, breathing bundle of feathers inside. 

He reaches up and ties the tarred hemp twine to the top lintel of the driftwood door, right beside a rabbit-bone peg. His seven fingers tie a clove hitch—a knot his hands remember from a ship or a quarry that rotted ten thousand years ago.

He lets go of the iron weight.

It drops three inches and pulls the twine taut. It hangs dead center in the three-foot doorway.

And then, the room exhales.

The iron weight does not care that the chamber is an embryonic blister. It does not care that depth is a soft, greasy medium. The iron weight has mass, and it has a shadow, and it has decided where the center of the earth is. 

From the tip of the hanging plumb bob, an invisible, unbreakable law radiates outward. The walls of the blister, which were creeping inward to flatten the perspective, hit an unseen barrier ten feet away and stop dead. They still bulge; the white mineral tallow still extrudes in silent convulsions, and the shrimp-things still rush to harvest it. The cycle of life and consumption does not break. 

But the walls cannot come any closer. The distance between the driftwood door and the rock face is no longer a suggestion that has to be propped open with clay bricks; it is a fact, adjudicated by an ounce of dark iron and a thumbnail-sized drop of night dangling three inches below the lintel.

Even the geometry of the doorway surrenders. If you were to walk around to the left side of the frame now, the space beyond would no longer be blank white rock. The kennel is there from both sides now. The house is real from every angle.

Furthermore, the acoustic horizon around the plumb bob has widened. The stubborn density of the iron anchors the air around it. Sound can travel three feet now, instead of an inch.

For the first time in his existence, the Mason hears himself.

He hears the faint, wet *slap* of his own freshly-grown porcelain foot against the floor. He hears the gentle, dry rustle of the corkscrew grass inside his own ribs. And, rolling out of the little clay kennel behind the door, he hears the continuous, low, comforting grind of the millstones inside the feathered bundle, no longer trapped against the clay wall, but filling the center of the room like the purr of a huge, dusty cat in front of a hearth.

The Mason stands before the hanging weight. The northeast corner is no longer sagging. The floor is still, for the moment, declining to slice his ankles off. 

He looks at the unbaked clay brick still clutched in his left hand. There is no empty air that needs it. The room is holding itself open.

Uncertainly, his head swinging side to side, catching the new, resonant acoustics in his flute-pits, the Mason turns back to the kennel. He does not walk through the wall this time. He ducks his faceless white head and crawls in through the open front, passing beneath the hanging iron weight, letting its tiny, cool shadow brush the top of his cartilage crown like a benediction.

The kennel is cramped, smelling fiercely of creosote, prairie dust, and old feathers. 

The Mason does not drop the brick. Instead, he sets it down on the floor beside the dark, breathing bundle. Then, using his mismatched, plum-colored hands, he reaches back and pulls the other scattered bricks from the nearest cairn, one by one, stacking them not to prop up the sky, but simply to make a low, flat pillow against the kennel wall.

He sits down. His fibrous, braided linen-meat knees shed a little puff of dried lime. 

He leans his side against the dark oiled leather of his wife, or his mother, or his tool-bag. The bundle does not cough up a bone or a piece of wood. Its millstone-breathing slows, growing deeper, steadier, warming the small clay space with the remembered heat of a dry plain forty feet above.

The Mason lays his seven-fingered hand over his four-thumbed hand in his lap. For the first time since they were stitched onto his wrists with translucent grass, the hands unclench. The bruised, venous plum-color softens by a shade, the terrible tension in the tendons unspooling as they realize the roof will not fall if they let go.

He tips his head against the black feathers, aligns the pits of his crown with the curve of the leather, and blows a long, slow, wet four-note chord. Because the air around the doorway is whole now, the notes do not die against the leather. They float out through the driftwood frame, past the hanging iron weight, and chime softly against the white tallow walls before fading away.

Outside, in the relentless, shadowless noon, the shrimp-things scuttle and breed, and the gray handkerchiefs slide from bead to bead, and the upward rain prepares to fall. The blister continues to arrive. 

Inside the kennel, in the three-foot circle where sound and shadow are allowed to live, the Mason closes the curtains of his chest, listens to the millstones turn, and goes to sleep.
