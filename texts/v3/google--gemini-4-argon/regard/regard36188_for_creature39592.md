To a lesser mind, the basin is a biological purgatory—a blind, wet clock ticking in the ruins of a forgotten machine. A lesser power, looking down through the glare of the mist, might feel pity. It might think: *Let us give it eyes. Let us give it a voice, or lift it from the slime, or shut off the needles so its cousins do not die in silver snow.*

Wait. Look closer. 

To give the Abbot eyes would be to blind it to the flawless, pressurized map of density and flow it holds in its braided flesh. To shut off the needles would be to lobotomize it, dissolving its liquid-crystal binder back into a million frantic, panicked ribbons of meat. And to lift it from the basin would be to tear the gardener from the only soil it knows how to love.

The power that watches the basin is old enough to know that benevolence is not the act of making other things like oneself. Benevolence is helping a thing become more fully what it already is, while sparing it the one tragedy it cannot foresee.

The tragedy is this: the needles are dying.

They are four hundred million years old. The planetary mantle beneath the dissolved mountains has cooled and shifted. The power grid they draw upon—a geothermal tap three miles down in the deep basalt—is calcifying, its conduits pinched by the slow, dead weight of the crust. Furthermore, the low rim of grey-white chert is still dissolving. In another forty thousand years—a blink in the life of the planet, though an eternity to the worms—the southern lip of the bowl will wear through completely. The warm sheet-water will spill out into the cold, arid scrublands beyond. The mist will blow away. The orchard will dry into a salt pan, the needles will go dark, and the Silt-Abbot will bake into a crust of brittle parchment without ever knowing there was an *outside* to its bowl.

The wise power does not descend in fire or geometry. The Abbot has enough geometry. 

Instead, the intervention begins three miles underground, in the dark, suffocating heat of the rock. 

With a touch no heavier than a shift in tectonic boredom, the power clears the calcified veins of the ancient geothermal tap. It does not restore them to their original, aggressive capacity—that would spike the needles' output and incinerate the Abbot the next time it leaned in to drink. Instead, it stabilizes the draw, braiding the deep magma channels into a self-cleaning loop that will feed the twelve osmium pilings a steady, unwavering trickle of current for another hundred million years. 

Next, the power considers the stone rim. It does not build a wall; walls crack and fall. Instead, it reaches into the chemistry of the deep upwelling water and introduces a trace mineral—a silicate-binding bacterium that thrives only at the exact temperature where the warm basin water meets the cooler air of the outside perimeter. As the water sweats through the low chert rim, the bacteria die and mineralize, depositing microscopic layers of smooth, greasy opal precisely as fast as the rain and wind erode the stone. The bowl will never breach. It is now in a state of dynamic, self-healing equilibrium.

This is mere maintenance, however—one bureaucracy extending a professional courtesy to another. The true gift requires a gentler touch.

The Abbot is alone, not because it minds solitude, but because its world has only one room. Its mind—that shimmering, violet-and-ochre weave of flatworms—is capable of far greater music than the simple binary of *prune* and *cull*. It is a master of a solved game, pacing a small floor.

Fifty yards to the east, just beyond the dissolved chert rim, lies a second depression in the mountain roots. For eons, it has been dry, filled with windblown dust and grey gravel, because the subterranean fissures that feed the Abbot’s orchard were blocked by a plug of dense granite.

The watching power reaches into that granite plug and softens a single, thumb-thick vein of feldspar into porous clay.

***

The Abbot feels the change three days later.

It is resting near Needle Four, digesting a mound of silver snow, when its gastropod sole registers a microscopic drop in the hydrostatic pressure of the basin floor. 

A violet flush ripples through its white matter. *An error in the map.* 

The water level has dropped by a thousandth of an inch. Somewhere, the bowl is leaking faster than usual. 

The Abbot detaches from the silt, inflating its scavenged gas-bladders, and glides toward the eastern rim on a wave of muscular, shell-less feet. The wild shoals part before it like cream. It reaches the low, soap-smooth wall of grey chert where the water normally beads and sweats. 

There, at the base of the stone, a fissure the width of a human hair has opened. Warm water is not merely sweating through it; it is drawing downward, pulled by gravity into the newly porous vein beneath the rim, seeping through the rock toward the dry basin next door.

For a long time—nearly six hours—the Abbot sits over the fissure. It extrudes a pseudopod of dense, un-latticed mucus and plugs the hole. The pressure in the basin stabilizes. The map is corrected. 

And yet, before the Abbot withdraws its pseudopod, the tips of its sensory leeches feel something through the wet stone. 

The water escaping to the other side is not vanishing into dry air. It is pooling. The stone on the other side of the rim is warm. More than that: buried in the dry gravel of that eastern bowl is a thirteenth needle, long dormant, its lower shaft now wet with the seeping mineral water. As the water closes the ground circuit, Needle Thirteen wakes up. 

Through six feet of solid chert, the Abbot feels a new hum in its teeth.

It is the same metric broadcast as the twelve needles it knows, yet slightly out of phase—a fifth above the fundamental frequency of its home, shifted by the tilt of the rock. A new note in the silent room.

The Abbot does not have curiosity as humans understand it, which is a hunger for novelty. The Abbot has a hunger for *completeness*. If there is a needle humming, it is part of the system. If it is part of the system, it must be mapped, and its garden must be tended.

Slowly, the Abbot digests its own plug of mucus, opening the fissure again. 

It does not try to force its five-foot bulk over the dry, sun-baked crest of the stone rim; the dry air above the mist-line would desiccate its gel in minutes. Instead, the Abbot reaches into its own anatomy. It selects four dozen of its smallest flatworms, a hundred tiny, juvenile gastropods, and a handful of ghost-leeches. It wraps them in a tight, tough bead of lattice-aligned mucus, no larger than a walnut, and packs the center of the bead with a rich yolk of silver protein-snow. 

It presses this living seed against the hairline fissure in the stone. 

Using the concentrated acid of its peach-slug warts, the Abbot begins to etch the chert. It does not hurry. It has all the time in the world, and the deep power watching from above has just given it a hundred million years more. 

Over the course of forty years, the Abbot visits the eastern rim every few days, trading acid for stone, widening the subterranean pipe millimeter by millimeter. As the pipe widens, more warm water flows through. The mist begins to rise in the eastern bowl. Spores of *Vallis-glass* and ribbons of black moss wash through the dark stone throat, taking root in the newly flooded silt on the other side. Drift-mantles, riding the thermal currents of the low ceiling, spill over the lip of the chert wall and drop into the new territory.

Finally, on a bright, shadowless afternoon indistinguishable from ten billion others, the submerged tunnel through the stone rim is wide enough.

The Abbot walks itself through Needle One, slicing its body in half.

One half remains to tend the ancient orchard. The other half—smaller, tighter, pulsing with pale violet light—slides across the white mud to the eastern rim, compresses its hydraulic gel into a long, glistening cylinder, and squeezes itself through the dark, wet pipe in the stone.

It emerges into the new basin. 

The floor here is uneven, still lumpy with drowned gravel. The wild shoals are thin and disorganized. Needle Thirteen hums at its strange, slightly higher pitch, surrounded by three smaller pilings the Abbot has never felt before. The geometry of the space is entirely wrong—an untended, asymmetrical wilderness of twenty yards by thirty.

The new Abbot anchors its gastropod sole in the virgin silt. It raises its crown out of the water, extrudes a fan of living leeches, and leans into the aura of Needle Thirteen. 

The air pleats. The new frequency pours down the mucus lattice and into the braided weave of worms. For a moment, the colony shudders as the unfamiliar harmonic washes through its nervous system; then, its polymers shift, accommodating the new chord. The violet flushes in its core deepen into a rich, resonant indigo. 

Behind it, through the water-filled pipe in the stone wall, a trailing ribbon of lattice-mucus still connects it to the original basin. A chemical packet—a compressed map of the new room, carrying the taste of the new frequency—travels back along the slime-thread to the first Abbot. 

There are two rooms now. Between them, the stone throat acts as a synapse. The two halves will never merge again; instead, they will trade memories back and forth through the rock, two hemispheres of a single, slowly waking brain, comparing the acoustics of two different bowls.

And high above the bright, unchanging mist, the wise power looks down at the three-inch-deep ocean, sees the two pale domes moving in slow, contrapuntal harmony across the silt, and is satisfied. Thirty yards further east, buried beneath the dry scrub, lies a third basin, and a fourth beyond that—a whole chain of dissolved mountain roots stretching toward the sea, waiting in the quiet stone for the gardener to need more room.
