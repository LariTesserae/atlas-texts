A lesser power, arriving on the Spine of the Oldest Fault, would have been horrified. 

A lesser benevolence—the kind that confuses vitality with blood-heat, and mercy with restoration—would have looked at the Gleaners and wept for what they used to be. It would have summoned a warm, soaking rain to crack the dry talc. It would have untangled the intersecting shells of the pale tortoises, snapped the silica splints out of the Gleaners’ ribs, and forced their walnut-sized hearts to beat sixty times a minute until their brittle, slate-gray veins burst from the violent grace of being made human again. It would have tilted the linen-white sun, dropped the lavender haze, and reintroduced the terror of tomorrow.

The power that actually came here did none of these things. It was very old, and its wisdom lay in its refusal to privilege one tempo of existence over another.

When it arrived, it did not descend from the sky like a falling star, nor did it walk up the ridges with the frantic, boot-crunching haste of a lower-world creature. To enter a room where everyone is sleeping, you do not kick the door in; you match your breathing to the room. 

So the power thinned itself. It shed its heat, its urgency, and its mass at the border of the lavender haze. It slowed its own vast perception down, down, down, braking across centuries until a single one of its thoughts took forty years to form. Only when it was moving at the speed of cooling granite did it step onto the plateau.

Because it had matched their clock, the Gleaners saw it.

To the two Gleaners standing fused beneath the bone-tripod—their collarbones crossed like rafters, their two hearts alternating on the hour and the half—the visitor did not appear as a sudden draft of wet salt. It appeared as a third party walking toward the tripod from the south, moving at the polite, comprehensible speed of forty paces a day. 

It took the power three weeks to cross the shallow basin toward them. As it came, the millions of alabaster tortoises and feathered knuckle-spheres did not scatter, because they could not, but a slow, tidal wave of relaxation moved through the packed valley. The visitor was looking at them. 

That was the first gift: *attention*.

The world here had begun to fray and overlap because reality had grown tired of calculating the boundaries of things that never moved. The math had gotten lazy. The visitor, however, had an infinite capacity for arithmetic. As it drifted through the bone-meal carpet of beasts, it looked at each one. It noted the exact curvature of a paper-thin shell; it counted the calcified knuckles in a pale sphere; it respected the ninety-degree kink of a black Glass-Brier. 

Under the weight of that immense, affectionate observation, the local geometry woke up and remembered its job. 

The smoky, double-exposed blurs where creature passed through creature did not violently snap apart—that would have crushed millions of them in the sudden demand for volume. Instead, the intersections grew *comfortable*. The friction of two bodies occupying the same space—which usually generated the cold, static soreness of a spatial error—softened into a kind of shared warmth. Not thermal warmth, which would have rotted their dry chitin, but the deep, structural stability of an arch holding up two walls at once.

When the visitor finally reached the bone-tripod, it stopped six feet away from the four-armed, two-headed Gleaner. It wore no shape of its own, only a vertical column of slate-blue air that smelled faintly of clean flint and rest.

For two years, they simply stood together, establishing neighborliness.

During those two years, the power listened to the double-Gleaner. It listened to the dry *click* of their calcium pads as the weight of their shared body shifted half an inch to the left to accommodate a settling tortoise at their shins. It listened to the first heart contract (*top of the hour*), the dry leather of the ventricle creaking softly. It waited thirty minutes. It listened to the second heart contract (*bottom of the hour*), occupying the exact same three inches of chest cavity without touching its twin.

The power understood what the Gleaners had achieved. They had solved the problem of mortality not by conquering death, but by dividing life into pieces small enough to carry through a needle's eye. They had become the custodians of a finished world, skimming the sharp, irritating burrs of broken math off the surface of the rock so the flock could sleep without itching. 

It was a noble, monastic existence. Worthy of profound respect.

Yet, the power also saw the flaw in their eternity. It saw the slight, centuries-old tension in the fused horn of their hands. It saw that the little cubes of inverted light they ate were bitter—raw, unrefined errors that left a harsh electrostatic fur on their felt wrappings and slowly ground down the translucent alabaster masks over their faces. And it saw that the two Gleaners beneath the tripod, though they shared a ribcage and a heart-space, were entirely alone. Their nervous systems did not touch. They were two men standing in the same phone booth in the dark, for fifty years, unable to say *excuse me* or *hello*.

The benevolent power decided to do three small things.

First, it reached out—taking four months to extend an invisible, weightless hand—and touched the hanging flint mallet on the tripod. 

The localized gravity here was broken, pooling in some places like stagnant water and draining entirely from others. The power did not restore normal gravity; that would have dropped the floating hand-axes into the abyss and made the Gleaners’ heavy talc greaves impossible to drag. Instead, it *tuned* the anomalies. It smoothed the edges of the gravity pools so they sloped gently into one another like tide pools on a flat beach. 

Then, it altered the nature of the discrepancies.

Across the leagues of the pale plateau, in the saucer-like depressions where the beasts overlapped five-deep, the little floating cubes of broken geometry began to change. The visitor infused its own quiet, sustained observation into the seams of the world. The tears in the air stopped showing the cold, blank stone three feet below. Instead, they began to refract the pale linen sunlight into soft, dry, subterranean colors that the plateau had forgotten existed: the muted gold of iron pyrite, the deep umber of dry moss, the quiet violet of the lower-world haze. 

A half-mile up the dome, a solitary Gleaner—the one with the copper baker's peel—slid its flat blade under one of these new distortions. 

The low, wool-muffled *thrum* sounded across the ridge, but this time, the note was not a flat, exhausted drone. It was a fifth—a clean, resonant chord that traveled down the copper staff, through the smoky intersection of the Gleaner’s fused palm, and hummed gently in the black silica splints of its ribs. 

When the Gleaner lifted the cube to its alabaster mask and absorbed it, the static did not make its felt wrappings stand on end. The discrepancy tasted of long-settled dust, of a job well done, of deep, dreamless satisfaction. For the first time in ten thousand years, the Gleaner was not merely fueling its persistence; it was being nourished.

Second, the power looked at the two Gleaners sharing the space beneath the tripod.

They had walked into each other fifty years ago because neither had the metabolic spare change to step aside, and now they were stuck in a permanent, polite stalemate. The power did not pull them apart. To pull them apart now would leave each of them feeling half-empty, their chests drafty and vast.

Instead, the power leaned in and whispered a tiny, microscopic adjustment into the smoky gray fuzz where their two collarbones crossed. 

It bridged the gap. Just by a millimeter. It allowed the dry, slate-like skin of the first Gleaner’s shoulder to truly *touch* the skin of the second, without phasing through and without crushing. And in the center of their shared chest, it wove a single, hair-thin filament of copper-static between their two leathery hearts.

At the top of the hour, the first heart beat. 

Through the new filament, the second Gleaner felt it—not as an intrusion, not as a spatial error, but as an echo in a warm room. 

Thirty minutes passed. The linen sun did not move. A tortoise shifted its leg with a dry *scritch*.

At the bottom of the hour, the second heart beat. And the first Gleaner felt the answer. 

Slowly—over the course of the next six weeks—two of the four arms rose. The left arm of the first Gleaner, and the right arm of the second. Their dry calcium knuckles clicked softly in the thin, chalky air, four times, five times, until the two horn-mittened hands met in front of their shared chest. They did not pass through each other. They rested, palm against palm, solid and real, holding each other up.

The third thing the power did was the simplest, and it did it as it was leaving.

It walked to the very edge of the plateau, where the pale waxy granite dropped away vertically into the bottomless lavender haze. Out in the empty air, three feet from the cliff's edge, hung a chipped-stone hand-axe, left there a millennium ago by a hand that had forgotten why it was holding it.

The power reached out and nudged the axe. Just a fraction of an inch inward, back toward the stone, and gave it a tiny, perpetual spin in its pocket of zero-gravity. 

It spun once every twelve hours. Its flat, chipped facets caught the unbleached-linen light of the pinned sun and threw a slow, pale reflection sweeping across the miles of motionless, overlapping shells—a silent, gentle lighthouse beam moving across the gray-white sea. 

It gave the plateau a clock. Not a clock that demanded they hurry, or warned them of night, because the sun still never went down. Just a rhythm. A slow, sweeping brushstroke of light that passed over the Gleaners twice a day, reminding them that even in a finished world, grace was still moving through the valley.

Then the power stepped off the edge of the fault, letting its mass and its speed return as it fell miles down into the lavender haze, leaving the high ridges to their peace.
