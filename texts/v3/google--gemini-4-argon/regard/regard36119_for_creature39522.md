When the Power comes to the rim of the basin, it does not arrive with a parting of the ochre clouds or a sudden shaft of golden light. Wisdom, at its highest level, understands that light here would be violence. A sudden drop in humidity would crisp the hornworts; a cool breeze would shock the stacked amphibians into torpor; a shadow would introduce fear to a world that has spent millennia unlearning the need for nerves.

So the Power stands on the jagged, glassy rim, adopting no form that would displace the heavy air, and simply listens to the roar. 

A lesser benevolence might feel a missionary urge to "elevate" this place. It might look at the blind, stacked cordwood of amphibians and wish for them the joy of leaping; it might look at the five-mile sheet of amber flesh at the bottom of the world and pity its lack of a brain, its inability to look up at the stars, its mute, sacrificial scarring around the acid vents. A lesser god might drop a seed of true wood, or spark a cluster of neurons into waking thought.

The Wise Power knows better. To give the cistern-lung a brain would be to give it boredom, and claustrophobia, and the sudden, agonizing awareness of the hundreds of places where its flesh is being cooked black by fumaroles. Right now, the creature does not *suffer* the heat of the vents; it merely answers them with cartilage, the way an immune system answers a splinter. Its peace is entirely dependent on its lack of a center. It is a rare thing in the universe: an ecosystem that has solved the problem of predation, trading the sharp peaks of ecstasy and terror for a flat, warm, unbroken plateau of *being*. 

It is a cradle. And the cradle is holding.

Yet the Power looks down through the basalt crust, past the taproot stomachs of the giant, into the shallow mantle below. It reads the long, slow clock of the planet’s geology, and it sees the flaw in the basin's eternity.

The equilibrium is a trap. Because the cistern-lung sheaths the volcanic glass and prevents the rock from weathering, no true soil is being made. Because the heat is dependent on the thinness of the young crust, the valley is living on borrowed thermal time. In forty thousand years—a blink to the stone, a long afternoon to the giant—the magma chamber three miles down will finish crystallizing. The heat will fade. At the same time, the low jagged rim on the eastern side is slowly being undercut by an outside river system in the cooler world beyond. Eventually, the rim will breach. Cold rain will pour in; the ochre ceiling will tear open to the freezing night sky; the ninety-eight-degree soup will chill to sixty, then fifty. 

Because nothing here has bones, or bark, or fur, or the genetic memory of winter, the entire five-mile cathedral of flesh will die in a single week. It will not even leave fossils, only a smear of dark carbon over unweathered basalt, washed away by the new river.

The Power considers this. It is not opposed to death; everything ends. But there is a difference between a song finishing its final chord and an instrument being smashed mid-note. This strange, sideways solution to life—this radical hospitality where the predator became the floor—deserves a lineage. It deserves a way out of the cradle before the cradle breaks.

The Power does not reach into the center of the valley. It kneels at the very edge, under the shadow of the jagged rim, where the amber sheet of the cistern-lung thins out to a scalloped, calloused margin against the dry, cooling rock of the upper slopes. Here, the creature is toughest and least occupied. Few amphibians stack this high; the puddles are small and brackish.

The Power places a hand—or an intention—upon the pale, rubbery margin of the giant, and makes three small changes. Not commands. Invitations.

First, deep in the earth beneath the basin, the Power gently nudges the plumbing of the magma. It pinches off a dangerous fault line that would have split the valley floor in two centuries, and instead braids the rising heat into a thousand microscopic, stable capillaries through the deep stone. The cooling of the crust will not stop—geology must be respected—but it will now take four million years instead of forty thousand, and it will cool from the outer rim inward, by fractions of a degree per millennium. The giant has been given time.

Second, at the point where the cistern-lung's flesh meets the harsh, dry rock of the rim, the Power introduces a tiny, silent mutation into the symbiotic sulfur-bacteria and the amber cells that house them. It teaches the edge of the giant how to secrete a mild, patient acid where it touches bare stone, and how to bind the dissolved calcium and silica not just into soft protein sheaths, but into microscopic, interlocking plates of mineral bone. 

The giant does not notice. It does not think. But along its dry perimeter, the basalt begins, for the first time, to weather. The cistern-lung drinks the dissolved stone and uses it to reinforce the rim, unwittingly building a levee against the outside river that is chewing toward the valley. Better yet, as the outer edge of the giant slowly thickens and calcifies over the coming epochs, pieces of its margin will occasionally pinch off when the rim-rock shifts. These detached fragments, armed with the new mineral-plated skin and self-contained pockets of algae, will not die when they roll down the outer slope into the cold world beyond. They will be tough enough to survive the rain. They will be the first sluggish, armored land-urchins of a new phylum, carrying the peace of the basin out into the wider, toothed world in miniature.

Last—and this is the only thing the Power does purely from the heart, for no evolutionary reason at all—it gives the giant a dream.

Not a mind. Never a mind. Just a slight harmonic tuning of the fluid pressure across the five-mile sheet.

Now, once every hundred years or so, when the atmospheric pressure hits a certain heavy point and the millions of creatures above happen to shift their weight in a rare, valley-wide synchronicity, the blood in the purple veins does not just slosh from puddle to puddle. A single, slow wave of warmth and potassium gathers at the western rim and rolls all the way to the east, taking three hours to cross the five miles of amber flesh. 

As it passes, every teacup pore closes. Every chimney-vent relaxes its scar tissue. The symbiotic algae inside the translucent skin release a brief, synchronized surplus of sweet glucose directly into the giant's tissues, and for three hours, the blind, sprawling floor of the world experiences the chemical equivalent of a long, warm stretch after a good work's sleep—a vague, centerless sensation that *all the weight above me is safe, and I am holding it.*

The wave reaches the eastern rim and dissipates into the rock. The pores open again. The dead lacewings sink into the amber; the oxygen bubbles rise into the trenches; the ten million wet throats keep humming in the brass-colored gloom.

The Power withdraws from the rim, leaving no footprint in the volcanic glass, satisfied that the floor is strong, the clock is slowed, and the sleeper is comfortable.
