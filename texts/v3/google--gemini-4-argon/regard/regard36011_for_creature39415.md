A lesser power, looking down upon the great wet reef of the plateau, would have felt an itch to improve it. 

A lesser benevolence might have pitied the plum-lipped people their chill, and split the basalt to give them steaming hot springs, not realizing that the sudden bloom of heat would rot the lanolin in their cloaks, boil the blind newts in their dark chutes, and breed a thick, choking algae that would clog a million laminar drains within a month. A shallower wisdom might have gifted them timber, or iron for levers, or seeds of lowland wheat, and in doing so, broken the friction that held their world against the tilt of the earth. It would have turned caretakers into conquerors, and shortly thereafter, into refugees of a collapsing slope.

The Power that actually turned its gaze upon the highland was neither shallow nor hasty. It had watched stars condense from cold gas and knew the sacredness of slow things. 

For three hundred years—a brief intake of breath—the Power simply watched. It listened to the vast, continent-spanning *shhh* of the water. It felt the microscopic ticking of a billion shale-crabs in the dark joints. It read the quiet literature of the quartz pebbles on the sluice-rims. It understood that this place was not a tragedy of deprivation, nor a monument of struggle; it was a completed sentence. It was a song sung at a frequency just above freezing, where every note was already in tune.

Yet, the Power also saw the very long horizon—the geological one, the one even the patient people could not quite read with their stacked shards of slate. 

The plateau was tilting, imperceptibly, millimeter by century, as the tectonic plate beneath it rode over the mantle. And high up at the crown of the world, where the oldest wards met the raw, unworked crags, a deep fault in the ancient basalt was taking in water. In four hundred years, or five, a winter of unusual bitterness would freeze a subterranean lake within that fault. The ice would wedge the mountain apart. A single, catastrophic pulse of water and fifty million tons of rock would shear loose, smashing the upper cisterns, overwhelming the baffles, and turning the laminar grace of the city into a scouring, cascading avalanche that would strip the plateau bare to the bone.

And on a smaller scale—the scale of knees and fingers and thick, slow blood—the Power felt the quiet ache at the end of the caretakers' lives. It saw the old ones walking upward into the biting mist, their joints swollen from seventy years of cold damp, shivering under their heavy cloaks during that final, solitary hour on the watchtower roofs while they waited for the ten-foot shadow to drop from the clouds. 

So the Power decided to act. Not by breaking the law of the plateau, which was *weight and friction and flow*, but by entering into it.

First, it addressed the mountain. 

The Power did not seal the fault with magic or weld the stone with fire. Instead, two hundred miles upslope from the highest inhabited ward, where the mist was permanent and only the lichens lived, the Power shifted the weight of a single, house-sized slab of basalt by the width of a thumb. 

It was an adjustment no different in kind from what a caretaker did at a garden sluice, only larger. That tiny shift changed the pitch of a natural rock-shelf. Over the next fifty years, the runoff from the high crags no longer pooled in the deep, dangerous fault. Instead, it was diverted sideways into a series of broad, shallow, natural fractures. There, over decades, the freezing and thawing of the water did not cleave the mountain; it neatly, methodically split the bedrock into millions of flat, manageable slabs of blue-grey slate, sliding them gently down a scree-slope toward the upper wards—a fresh, inexhaustible quarry waiting for the builders of the next ten millennia, while the dangerous fault below dried out and settled safely under its own weight.

Second, the Power made a new stone. 

It did not scatter it everywhere. It seeded it only in the high, wind-scoured beds of the upper plateau, where the old ones walked when their time came. 

To the eye, this new rock looked almost identical to the native dark basalt, perhaps a shade deeper in its grey, with a faint, greasy luster like rubbed tallow. A geologist from another world would have marveled at its impossible crystal lattice—a hyper-dense, heavy stone that drank the intense ultraviolet light of the bright, pale sky and did not merely reflect it, but trapped it, converting the high-altitude radiation into a slow, steady, sub-surface thermal vibration. 

The stone was never hot. If it had been hot, the snow and mist would have steamed on it, creating localized drafts and betraying its strangeness. Instead, it held a permanent, stubborn temperature of precisely ninety-eight degrees Fahrenheit—the exact warmth of a healthy human chest beneath a wool cloak.

The first person to find it was an old woman named Oru, though she had scarcely heard her own name spoken aloud in ten years. Her knees were stiff with the damp; her fingers, spatulate and scarred from a lifetime of resetting frost-heaved roofs, could no longer pry a thirty-pound sluice-gate without trembling. She knew her blood was thickening toward its final halt.

She left three quartz pebbles arranged in a neat triangle on the sill of her lower room—*one less mouth; the tallow-dish is full; the drain is clear*—and began the long, flat-footed walk up the incline of the world.

It took her three days to reach the high, empty wards where the roofs had mostly sagged into cairns and the wind had an edge like split slate. She was very tired, and the cold had finally worked its way through six inches of greased wool to settle in her ribs. She climbed the stepped terraces of a watchtower whose builders had been wedged into the walls five thousand years ago. 

She reached the top platform, expecting the biting, wet chill of the slate against her back as she lay down to wait for the sky to open.

Instead, as she lowered herself onto the center slabs, she felt a profound, quiet softening. 

The stone beneath her did not leech the last of her heat away. It met her. It felt as though a great, wool-wrapped back had been resting there just a moment before, leaving its warmth behind in the rock. The relentless shivering in her thighs stopped. The tight, painful knot in her barrel chest loosened, allowing the thin, crisp air to slide in and out without a rasp. 

She lay there, wrapped in grey, looking up into the bright, shadowless ceiling of the clouds. She did not think of gods or miracles; her mind worked in stone and water. She simply thought, *The sun has found a way to stay inside this rock.* 

She fell asleep before the great, ice-blue condor dropped out of the thermals, and she never felt the heavy thud of its landing.

Thirty days later, a younger caretaker—a man in his middle years with broad, stone-smooth cheeks—walked the high rounds to check the primary silt-traps. He climbed the watchtower, as was custom, to gather what the rain and the birds had left.

He found Oru’s clean, pale ribs and her heavy femurs, washed bright by the mist. As he knelt to gather them into his sling, his bare, calloused knee pressed against the center flagstones. 

He paused. 

He placed his broad, flat palm against the wet dark stone. In the permanent chill of the upland, the rock pushed back against his skin with the steady, living warmth of a sheep’s flank. 

He stayed on his knees for ten minutes, simply feeling it, his dark eyes narrowed behind their heavy folds. Then, with the innate, wordless comprehension of his people, he tested the weight and friction of the warm slab. He pried it up with his thick nails. Beneath it, the bed of the tower roof held three smaller shards of the same dark, slightly greasy stone.

He did not take the great slab—that belonged to the roof, and to those who would climb it at the end of their days. He wedged Oru’s bones neatly into the windbreak wall of the tower, where they belonged. 

Look at him, walking back down the tilt of the plateau through the shifting fog. 

Tucked inside his cloak, resting against his ribs and radiating a gentle, tireless heat that saves him a hundred calories of mutton-fat an hour, is a flat, palm-sized shard of the dark stone. When he reaches his kinship cluster four miles below, he will not build a shrine to it. He will set it into the sleeping-ledge of the dampest lower room, where the eldest aunt sleeps and where the newborn infants are rubbed with tallow. And next spring, when he goes up to clear the high weirs, he will look for another shard, and set it at the base of the sunken garden wall, where its two-inch halo of warmth will keep the frost off the dark-leafed tubers for an extra three weeks of the year.

The Power watched him disappear around the wet corner of an alley, his soft-soled shoes making no sound at all. 

The water slipped down the basalt chutes—*shhh*. A single drop fell from an overhang into a brimming basin—*plink*. High overhead, the ten-foot shadow tilted its wings against the thermal and slid across a hundred thousand pewter-bright roofs. 

The Power smiled, which was nothing more than a ten-minute parting of the high clouds, and left the machine to run.
