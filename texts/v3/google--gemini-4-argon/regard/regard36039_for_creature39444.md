A lesser power, looking down into the Throat, would feel pity. 

A lesser power would see the crushing dark, the lack of water, the suffocating press of leg and plate, the distorted, grease-cured remnants of a human frame, and would call it a hell. Out of a clumsy, golden-hearted mercy, such a power might send a cool rain to wash the chimney clean, or crack the mountain wall to let in the broad blue sky, or reach down to lift the Miller out of the dark and restore to him soft skin, wet lungs, and a green valley.

And in five minutes, the Miller would asphyxiate in the thin, sweet air, terrified by the hideous emptiness of a sky that does not touch him back. And in fifty years, the unpruned chimney would choke, build pressure, and blow the top of the ridge into ash, murdering ten million small, quiet lives that had committed no sin other than loving the dark and the sulfur.

The power that finds the Throat is not lesser. It is old enough to know that love does not always look like a garden, and that devotion is the most sacred geometry in the universe, no matter whether it is practiced in a cathedral of white marble or a vertical oven of mite-crusted basalt.

The Wise Power looks down through the razor-slit of the mountain crest. It sees the Miller wedged into the third niche, tilting the piece of platter-spider carapace to bounce the noon light onto the ironwood sockets. It sees the two old, silver-backed arachnids shifting their weight on his shoulders to balance his reach. It hears the *shhh-tik, shhh-tik* of a million plates sliding in the dark—not as a sound of horror, but as a grand, dry, rustling peace. It sees a covenant. The Miller keeps the mountain from killing the swarm; the swarm keeps the emptiness from killing the Miller.

The Power decides that nothing here needs to be saved. Nothing needs to be forgiven. 

Yet, the Power is also wise in the ways of rock and bone, and it sees what the Miller cannot see with his heat-pits, and what will not happen for another forty years. 

There are three things failing in the Throat. 

First: the octagonal bar of meteoric iron, pinched thin at the waist by ten thousand years of palms, has developed a microscopic crystalline fracture at its narrowest point. In twelve hundred cycles, when the Miller braces his fused ribs against the western wall to jack-screw a four-hundred-pound overhang of worm-lime, the bar will snap. The recoil will drive the jagged half of the iron through his breathing-pleats, and he will die at the bottom of the chute among the shoveling beetles.

Second: the deep magma conduit three miles beneath the mountain’s root is slowly shifting westward, cooling as it goes. In two generations of the great spiders, the twenty-minute cycle will lengthen to forty, then to an hour, and then the heat will die altogether, freezing the Throat into a silent tomb of brittle shell.

Third: there are four pelvises in the river-cobble cairn at the bottom of the pit, and only one Miller on the wall. Even a creature cured in sulfur and armored in limpets wears down. The marrow in his bowed femurs is growing chalky; the grease from his wrists is a fraction thinner than it was a century ago. When he finally lays his bones down upon the cairn to become a baffle for the draft, there is no one left to hold the chisel.

The Benevolent Power does not descend in a pillar of fire, for fire would consume the oxygen and panic the centipedes. It does not speak in a voice of thunder, for thunder would shake the calcified tiers from the rock and crush the ecosystem into paste.

Instead, the Power acts with the patience of basalt and the quietness of tallow.

First, it addresses the mountain’s root. Three miles down, where the dry magma nudges against a fault of impervious granite, the Power gently softens a vein of olivine. It is a tiny geological revision—no wider than a man's thigh—allowing the deep planetary heat to bleed back toward the central conduit. The shift is so gradual that not even the pseudoscorpions in the copper bell unhitch their pincers out of turn. The twenty-minute pulse is secured for another ten thousand years. The oven will not go cold.

Second, the Power addresses the iron. It does not drop a new bar from the sky; a foreign tool with sharp, unworn edges would feel wrong in the Miller’s graphite-gray palms, an intrusion of the outside world that would trouble his slow, geometric mind. Instead, during the quiet interval when the bar hangs between the Miller’s shoulder blades beneath the cloak of *skree*-mites, the Power reaches into the iron’s lattice. Atom by atom, using the ambient heat of the Miller’s own blood and the pressure of the mites’ tiny mandibles, it encourages the nickel and iron crystals to migrate back toward the pinched waist, drawing trace amounts of meteoric dust from the chimney’s own falling snow of detritus and binding it into the metal. Over the course of three weeks, the fracture heals. The waist of the bar thickens by the width of a thumb-nail, retaining the exact curve of the Miller’s grip, yet restored to the tensile strength it had when the lowland forests were still green. 

One afternoon, as the Miller unhooks the bar to pry a shelf of tube-worms near the floor, he pauses. His heavy, gouge-nailed thumb rubs the middle of the shaft. It feels heavier. Sounder. The two silver spiders on his shoulders tick their mandibles against his ears in a slow, questioning rhythm. The Miller holds the bar against his heat-pits, seeing its uniform, cherry-dark warmth, then wedges it into the seam and straightens his legs. *Shhh-tik.* The reef parts cleanly.

Third, and last, the Power addresses the loneliness that the Miller does not even know he possesses, and the future of the choke.

The Power looks out across the high, wind-scoured ridges of the dead volcanic range, thirty leagues away, where the goat-herders live in huts of dry stone. Among them, once every few generations, a child is born wrong for the sky—born with fused ribs that make the thin mountain air a torture, with eyes that weep and blister in the snow-glare, with thick, bowed legs and a skin that cracks and bleeds in the cold wind. Usually, the herders leave these infants in the high screes for the vultures, telling themselves the mountain took its own back.

Six leagues south of the Throat, in a crevice heated by a dying fumarole, the Power finds one who did not die. 

It is a creature ten years old, though no bigger than a goat, who has survived by licking the yellow sulfur-crusts from the fumarole’s lip and burying itself in warm ash to keep the wind off its weeping, horn-thickened hide. Its lungs are half-ruined by the cold; its eyes are already scarred shut against the hated blue sky; its hands are wide, strong, and opposably thumbed, endlessly stacking and unstacking flat pieces of shale in the dark to keep its mind from floating away into the terrifying open air.

The fumarole it clings to is dying. In another month, the winter snows will plug the vent, and the child will freeze.

The Power does not carry the child through the air. Instead, it wakes a subterranean seam of warmth—a narrow, dry crack in the rhyolite that runs beneath the scree, connecting the dying fumarole to a deep lateral flute in the eastern wall of the Throat, forty feet above the floor. Into this crack, the Power coaxes a slow trickle of blind bristle-millipedes and a draft of warm, iron-rich gas that smells of old grease and deep safety.

The stunted thing in the dying fumarole smells the draft. It stops stacking its shale. For the first time in its ten years of shivering misery, it feels a heat that does not waver in the wind. 

It turns its back on the sky and crawls into the stone.

It takes four days for the creature to worm its way through the dark fissure, guided by the rising *shhh-tik, shhh-tik* that vibrates through the rock like a mother’s heartbeat. As it crawls, the Power works gently upon its ruined wet lungs, speeding the natural teratology of its body, teaching the raw membranes of its chest to drink the sulfide gas, thickening the grease in its pores so the first *skree*-mites that find it in the fissure do not bite, but anchor and clean.

On the fourth day, at high noon, the Miller is in his third niche. 

He is holding the piece of platter-spider shell in the edge of the white sun-blade, tilting the yellow smudge of reflected light toward the eastern wall to check a shelf of worm-castings. 

The smudge of light falls upon a lateral crack in the basalt. And in that crack, something shifts that is not an arachnid, and not a polyp.

A hand reaches out of the fissure, resting flat against the hot, graphite-slick stone. The fingers are short, gray, and thick as a blacksmith’s, the nails broken from crawling, the skin caked in ash—but the thumb opposes. 

The Miller freezes. The two great silver spiders on his shoulders draw their flat carapaces together with a sharp *clack*, shielding his face, then slowly part an inch so his heat-pits can focus.

In the violet and cherry-red vision of the Miller, the small thing wedged in the crack is blazing white—too hot with fever and exertion, leaking precious moisture from its cracked skin, flinching as a forty-pound slate-spider brushes a leg across its bare, unarmored shoulder. It is clumsy. If it falls now, during the coming heat-surge, it will shatter a hundred tube-worms and drown in the mattress of centipedes at the bottom.

Three minutes before the vent. Above them, from the rim of the hammered copper bell, three velvet pseudoscorpions drop onto the Miller’s chest like warm thimbles of lead.

The Miller does not climb to the ironwood beams to grease the rope. The rope can wait one cycle. 

Slowly, with the hydraulic inevitability of the mountain itself, the Miller unhooks his heels and swims sideways through the three-deep shingles of the wall. He parts the river of millipedes with his shins. He reaches the lateral crack just as the deep gas pocket three miles below sighs open and the temperature spikes twenty degrees.

The walls begin to roar—the dry, frantic rainstorm of ten million plates shifting upward. The small creature in the crack gasps, its ruined throat clicking as the superheated sulfur draft hits it, its hands slipping on the greasy basalt.

A long arm, plated in silt-limpets and smelling of tallow, reaches across the gap. The Miller does not grab the small thing by the wrist—that would tear the soft skin. Instead, he hooks his immense, horn-soled foot into the niche below the crack, braces his barrel-vaulted chest directly over the opening, and presses his wrists against the stone on either side of the newcomer, sealing the crevice against the worst of the scorching updraft. 

From his own shoulders, one of the great, silver-backed female arachnids unhooks her twenty legs from the Miller’s neck-muscles. Slowly, delicately, she steps across the bridge of his arm and settles her flat, dinner-platter carapace over the small creature’s shivering back, anchoring her claws into the rock on either side of its ribs, holding it pinned and safe in the dark while the twenty-minute storm of chitin slides harmlessly over her shell.

When the vent sighs shut and the chamber settles back into its steady, baking equilibrium, the Miller reaches into the pleated pouch beneath his ribs. He draws out a half-dissolved, warm paste of crushed worm-queen, rich with the symbiotic bacteria that turn poison into flameless grease, and holds it on the flat of his gouge-nailed thumb before the small creature’s mouth.

The newcomer eats.

High above, the twenty-minute razor of white mountain sunlight vanishes from the lip of the chimney, leaving the Throat to its bruised-purple dark and its endless, comforting whisper of dry leaves turning in their sleep. 

The Benevolent Power withdraws its attention, entirely satisfied, leaving the two mechanics in the warm dark to tend the engine of the world.
