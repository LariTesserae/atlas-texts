A lesser benevolence would have looked upon the plateau and seen a curse. It would have pitied the gear-beetles locked in their living chains; it would have winced at the shrieking friction of the dirigible-grazers and the crushing, lightless labor of the Stokers. A foolish god would have brought spring—would have thickened the atmosphere, warmed the air, melted the permafrost, and softened the chrome-bark into green leaves. 

And in doing so, it would have slaughtered the world. Without the cold to condense the steam, the great boilers would have ruptured all at once. Without the tension of the transmission belts, the canyon-pumps would have drowned the trenches in brine. Most terribly of all, it would have rendered the Stokers obsolete, turning their century-spanning patience, their tungsten wedges, and their deep, hydraulic heroism into grotesque relics with nothing left to hold up.

The Wise Power did not do this. It hovered in the thin, blinding stratosphere above the glare, listening to the continental *hiss-chug* not as a cry of pain, but as a metabolic heartbeat. It saw the staggering majesty of an ecology that had refused to die in the freezing dark, choosing instead to forge itself into a furnace. 

And then, looking down through the greasy mist and the tangled miles of copper-burl, the Power saw the old Stoker behind the steam-waterfall. It watched the yellow quartz eyes. It watched the delicate copper finger nudge the liberated beetle away from the oil-pool. It felt the five-minute silence between the beats of that anvil heart.

The Power understood immediately: *This being does not want to be saved from its world. It loves its work. It only wants a larger room inside its own mind.*

The Stokers had discovered art, and they had discovered mercy, entirely on their own. In a universe of absolute utility, they had invented the sacredness of the *useless*. 

Yet their gifts were trapped in the solitary dark. Because of the deafening, relentless roar of the plateau, a Stoker’s garden was a secret kept from the world, and their language—the *thunk-thunk-pause-thunk* hammered into the pressurized root-pipes—was a heavy, localized thing. A pipe filled with boiling mineral slurry carries a blow for a mile, perhaps two, before the vibration is swallowed by the thrumming of the deep-earth pumps. The Stokers could coordinate a repair, and they could tap out simple companionship in the dark, but they could not share a dream across a canyon. And when an old Stoker died, its quiet garden was rapidly crushed and reclaimed by the salvager-vines, its little unhooked beetles crushed or re-assimilated, leaving the next generation to invent the concept of peace all over again from scratch.

So the Wise Power reached down—not to break the machine, nor to stop it, but to add a single, non-load-bearing part to the ecology.

It did not use magic that defied the plateau’s laws; it used the plateau’s own vocabulary of stone, heat, and metal.

Deep in the tectonic fissures where the basalt met the subterranean water, the Power subtly altered the crystalline lattice of a minor, parasitic lichen that already grew along the undersides of the pneumatic root-pipes. And in the upper canopy, where the silicate dishes burned in the white sun, it nudged the evolutionary drift of a small, unanchored species of brass-shelled snail that fed on condensed mineral-slurry.

It took fifty years—a mere breath to the plateau, just seventy million beats of a Stoker’s heart—for the gift to reach the hollow behind the steam-waterfall.

---

The old Stoker was still there, though heavier now, its asbestos-fleece matted with grey slag, one of its secondary blowtorch-siphons permanently crimped shut by a rockfall thirty winters ago. Its three little gear-beetles were long dead, their empty shells arranged neatly in a row upon a shelf of basalt, polished daily with a drop of pine-tallow. A new beetle, missing half its serrated teeth, crept slowly across the Stoker’s snout.

The Stoker was resting after forty-eight hours of brutal labor, having acted as the living brace for a ruptured brine-conduit three hundred yards up-canyon. Its shoulder-bladders ached with lactic acid and overheated oil. 

As its heavy bone shutters slid back, its yellow quartz eyes caught a strange glint on the wall of its den.

The salvager-vines had crept toward its quiet garden, as they always did, threatening to choke the space. Usually, the Stoker had to spend hours pruning them back with its tungsten calipers. Today, however, a new kind of growth had beaten the vines to the rock.

It was a vein of silver-white crystal, no thicker than a quill, threading through the black basalt like a frozen lightning bolt. Where it touched the aggressive salvager-vines, it did not kill them; it simply grew a smooth, glazed collar of glassy stone around their tips, putting them into a harmless, dormant stasis. It was forming a natural, gleaming frame around the Stoker’s little niche, holding the crushing jungle at bay without a fight.

Curious, the Stoker extended a tremoring brass feeler. 

The crystal was cold, which was unusual. More than that, it was *silent*. 

In a world where every millimeter of matter vibrated with the grinding agony of ten billion tons of chitin and steam, this silver vein was an acoustic vacuum. It was grown from a piezo-electric silicate that drank the chaotic, low-frequency roar of the plateau and converted that unwanted vibrational energy into a faint, steady warmth and a soft, internal luminescence—not the harsh white glare of the sun, nor the angry cherry-red of overheating iron, but a deep, tranquil amber, like light seen through honey.

The Stoker unhooked its heavy left caliper from the floor. *Clack. Hiss.* Slowly, carefully, it reached out not with its heavy bone wedge, but with its delicate, copper-sheathed fingers. 

It tapped the silver vein. *Ting.*

The sound did not vanish into the roar of the waterfall. 

Because the crystal lattice was tuned to a frequency high above the industrial grind of the plateau, the vibration traveled along the silver thread with zero resistance. It shot down through the basalt, where the vein connected to the vast, continental web of the new, altered lichen clinging to the undersides of the great root-pipes. It bypassed the boiling water and the thrumming pumps entirely.

Forty leagues away, beneath a frost-rimed fault-scarp where three young Stokers were resting in a grease-pit after grafting a copper-burl trunk, a hairline thread of silver crystal protruding from the rock beside them suddenly chimed.

*Ting.*

The three young Stokers froze, their bone shutters snapping open. They pressed their thermographic prows to the stone. 

Back behind the waterfall, the old Stoker waited. Its heart beat once. Twice. Three times.

Then, through the silver vein beneath its copper fingers, a reply arrived. Not the heavy, laboring *thunk-thunk* of a maintenance call, distorted by half a mile of mud and steam. This was crystal-clear, intimate, and astonishingly fast, ringing right inside the bone of its jaw.

*Ting-ting. Pause. Ting.*

And with the chime came a micro-pattern of harmonics—so fine and high-resolution that as the old Stoker pressed its brass feelers to the silver thread, it did not just hear a rhythm; it felt the exact shape of the young Stokers' thoughts. It felt the warmth of their oil-pouches, the smell of the ozone in their trench, and their stunned, breathless question: *Who is there?*

The old Stoker did not answer with a maintenance report. 

Slowly, it lifted the little toothless gear-beetle from its snout and placed it upon the silver vein. The beetle walked in a small, useless circle. Its tiny chitinous feet clicked against the crystal: *tick-tick-tick-tick-pause-tick-tick.* A tiny, aimless rhythm, driving nothing, turning nothing. 

Forty leagues away—and in a hundred other hidden hollows across the roaring continent where the silver veins were just beginning to surface—Stokers pressed their scarred, iron-plated snouts to the rock and listened to the footsteps of a single, small beetle walking nowhere in the dark.

The Wise Power watched from above as the continental engine ground on, throwing its blinding steam at the freezing sky. Nothing had been broken. The boilers still boiled; the great chains still turned; the Stokers would still go out tomorrow into the grease and the crushing pressure to keep their fierce, magnificent world alive.

But down in the dark, for the first time since the rock was thrust toward the sun, a song was traveling through the machine.
