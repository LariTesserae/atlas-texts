The Watcher did not come down like a storm or a falling star, for a storm would have drowned the Hollow in mud, and a star would have touched off the volatile oils of the wax-trees and burned forty thousand years of patient math to ash in a heartbeat.

Benevolence, when it is truly wise, knows the weight of its own footstep. 

Instead, the Presence rested on the bleached gravel above the lip of the sinkhole, weightless as the brassy light, and looked down through the lattice of pale roots. It listened to the dry-fire rustle of the beetles, the *tock-tock* of the Counter-Weights’ mallets, and the soft, wet *snip* of third eyelids clearing the dust from golden eyes. 

The Presence felt a profound and tender awe. In a universe that favored loud, sprawling Empires of water and iron—things that burned fast, spread wide, and died in their own slag—this tiny, grease-slicked pocket was a watchmaker’s miracle. It was a poem written in a language of six syllables, repeated without a single grammatical error since the mountains were tall. 

To give them water would be murder. To give them iron would be madness. To give them the sky would be a cruelty, for their eyes would burn and their skin would crack like dry mud before they had walked a mile across the flat. They did not need salvation; they had already saved themselves ten thousand times over, every time a boy swept a spiderweb into a carding-vat.

Yet, the Presence looked deeper—not into the wicker and the bone, which were whole, but into the pink quartzite and the basal stone beneath the compost. 

Nothing in stone is truly closed forever. Even forty-million-year-old granite creeps and sighs.

Deep below the root-cellars, where the blind dust-badgers snuffled in the hot dark, the Presence saw what the Counter-Weights could not feel through their sinew-ropes. The great mineral seam that fed the tallow-ferns and the grease-vines—a deep, ancient vein of phosphorus, sulfur, and fossilized brine—was pinching shut. Tectonic drift, slow as the growth of a fingernail, was shearing the lower stratum away. In three hundred years, the deep roots would hit sterile white quartz. The oils would thin; the wax-trees would grow brittle; the Counter-Weights would tap their blocks in frantic, baffled rhythms as the overall mass of the world began, inexplicably, to lighten. 

And higher up, on the third tier, the Presence saw a smaller, sharper sorrow. 

A young Counter-Weight—a girl of nineteen whose fingertips were calloused as horn from reading the main-vines—was going blind beneath her blindfold. Not in her golden eyes, which she rarely used, but in her ears. A tiny, calcified growth, a quirk of inbred bone-density, was sealing her inner ear. Soon she would not hear the pitch of the ropes or the *tock* of the mallets. In an economy where no one could be carried who could not balance their own weight on the catwalks, she would have to descend to the badger-floor forty years before her time.

The Presence considered the math of the Hollow. If you add an ounce of gold to a watch, you stop the gears. If you take an ounce of brass away, the spring snaps. Whatever was done had to weigh nothing at all, or else weigh exactly what was already there.

So the Presence reached down into the dark rock, two hundred feet below the badgers.

It did not break the stone; a tremor would have snapped the mortised bone-joints of the houses above. Instead, with the patience of the plateau itself, the Presence persuaded the molecules of the sterile quartz. Over the span of a single afternoon, while the amber light shifted toward violet, the Presence exchanged the dead silica, atom for atom, with a new, downward-curving ribbon of rich, greasy phosphor-salts and locked-away hydrocarbons—the compressed ghost of an ocean that had dried up before the first mammal was born. The seam was now ten miles deep, angled straight into the root-balls of the wax-trees. The engine had fuel for another forty million years. The weight of the stone beneath the floor had not changed by the mass of a gnat’s wing.

Then, the Presence looked up to the lip of the sinkhole, where the blinding gravel met the drop.

Even in this sealed engine, a tiny fraction of mass was lost every century. When the hot air of the pit breathed upward during the freezing nights, a few molecules of volatile camphor-vapor and nitrogen escaped into the empty sky, never to return. It was a leak so small it took ten generations to equal the weight of a baby’s thumb, yet the elders knew of it; their oldest, darkest songs spoke of the *Thinning*, the distant day when the last two villagers would have to climb into the compost together so that one small child could be born.

The Presence walked out across the dead plateau—miles away, where the wind still scraped the whaleback hills—and gathered the corpses of things that had died in the high, dry cold: dried-out blowflies blown up from the distant lowlands, desiccated lichens, dust rich in iron and lime. 

With a breath no stronger than the evening draft, the Presence herded this tiny, invisible drift of dead matter across the gravel pan and let it settle—not in a heap, which would have startled the villagers, but grain by grain, over the course of the twilight—into the pale root-lattice at the rim of the pit. 

Up in the violet light of the canopy, a saucer-sized tortoiseshell woodlouse paused on a root, found a crust of wind-blown highland lichen it had never tasted before, and ate it. The mineral entered the shell; the shell would become a greave on a carpenter’s shin; the carpenter would go to the badgers. The leak was plugged.

Finally, as the ten thousand cicadas began their *shhh-kuh, shhh-kuh* in the papery bark and the green grub-lanterns were hung from the eaves, the Presence descended into the warm, nutmeg-scented air of the hall itself. 

It did not take a shape. A shape takes up room on a catwalk. It became only a slight, pleasant coolness in the grease on the main-vine where the young, deafening Counter-Weight sat with her fingertips pressed to the cordage.

A tiny, six-legged scavenger-beetle, no bigger than a lentil, was climbing the girl’s greased braid toward her ear. It was a common thing—the villagers usually flicked them into their pouches before they could burrow. 

The Presence nudged the beetle’s tiny, ganglion mind. 

Instead of biting the chamois-leather skin of her neck, the beetle crept into the warm canal of the girl’s left ear, right up to the microscopic wall of excess bone that was stealing her hearing. To the beetle, nourished on carved carapaces and resin-mortar, the calcified spur smelled precisely like the salt-licks at the base of the walls. 

For three hours, while the village scraped the day’s oil from one another’s shoulders and the girl sat motionless at her post, the tiny mandibles worked with silent, microscopic precision, rasping away the pearl of dead bone, grain by grain, without ever touching the drum. 

When the spur was gone, the beetle backed out of her ear, heavy and fat with calcium. The girl’s hand came up automatically; her blunt, nimble fingers caught the insect, rolled it in a dab of sebum from her cheek, and dropped it into her hip-pouch to feed the rail-birds at dawn. Nothing was wasted.

She tilted her head. Through the dark leather of her blindfold, her great golden eyes widened. 

The *shhh-kuh* of the cicadas came rushing back into her skull, crisp and dry and glorious. Beneath her calloused fingers, the great sinew-rope hummed like a bass viol string—she could feel a mother shifting a sleeping child on the fourth tier; she could feel a badger rolling over in the hot dust eighty feet below; she could feel the deep, slow, inexplicable contentment of the wax-tree roots drinking something rich and dark in the stone beneath the world.

She picked up her bone mallet and struck the hollow ironwood block.

*Tock. Tock-tock.*

Not a command to shift weight. It was the rarest signal in the vocabulary of the blocks—the three-beat strike that meant *All tiers trim. Hold your places.*

Across the five levels of the dim, amber hall, three hundred people paused with their bone strigils resting against their neighbors' shoulders. They listened to the wooden echo die away into the rustle of a million chitinous legs. They felt the warm, greasy wood solid beneath their prehensile toes, smelled the camphor and the sage, and knew, without having a word for *tomorrow*, that the basket would hold.

Above the lip of the pit, under the cold stars that the villagers had never seen and never needed to see, the Presence smiled and went on its way, leaving the gravel as flat and empty as before.
