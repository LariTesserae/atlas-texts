The knee-prints in the border-moss do not stay wet for long. The presence that has paused at the lip of the slot has no need for wool trousers, though it wears the shape of a traveler out of politeness to the flat, wind-scoured miles it crossed to get here. 

A lesser power—one that confunds goodness with its own reflexes—would be appalled by the pit. It would see a prison of cold grease and blinding glare. It would take pity on the blind masons and strike the earth with a staff, opening the volcanic throat three miles down to send a rush of sixty-degree steam surging back through the bone chimney. 

And because this power is wise, it sees in the same instant what that mercy would do. The glycerol-thick blood of the tett would thin and race, tearing through their brittle, two-chambered hearts like spring floodwater through an old mill. The waterproof varnish of the ochre slugs would melt into toxic slime; the ancient white mortar would dissolve into grey soup before the accelerated roots of the ferns; the saucer-sized rooms would collapse upon the wedged, doughy mothers; and the city that had held out against the sky for three thousand years would rot into a warm, stinking mud-hole in a single afternoon.

To love a thing is to understand the speed at which it lives. The tett do not know they are slow. To a mason taking a week to tamp a walnut of tallow-clay into a slate joint, the week feels like a brisk morning’s work. Their loyalty to the stone is total, ancient, and unbroken. It requires no rescue.

Yet the visitor does not walk away. It stays on its knees in the grey clay as the low, white sun slides along the horizon, looking down through the silver greasewood with eyes that can read both the three-minute pulse of the masons and the three-thousand-year choke of the bedrock below.

There are two cruelties in the basin. Neither is the fault of the city, and both can be mended without breaking the truce with time.

The first is deep down.

The visitor lays one bare hand flat against the grey clay at the rim of the pit and closes its eyes. Its awareness drops past the twenty-foot walls of the slot, past the waterlogged honeycomb of the cellar-warrens, past the ancient mud where the great beast’s ribs are anchored, and down into the plumbing of the world. 

Half a mile beneath the city, the geothermal fissure is dying. It is a narrow throat of basalt, and over the centuries, the rising water has deposited crusts of white calcite and silica along its walls, narrowing the pipe the way fat narrows an old artery. In another eighty years—a single generation for the walled-in mothers—the throat will seal completely. The forty-five-degree breath at the bone chimney will stop. The frost will push down through the silver canopy, solidifying the antifreeze sap in the rosettes, turning the glycerol blood of the tett to amber glass, and ending the three-thousand-year stalemate in permanent, sterile ice.

The power does not shatter the rock. It does not widen the throat to what it was when the tett ran like badgers. Instead, it reaches into the mineral dark and persuades a hairline seam of limestone, two hundred yards to the west, to give way by the width of a thumb-nail. 

Down in the dark, a trickle of water slightly more acidic than before joins the deep thermal seep. Over the next fifty years, that faint acidity will gently, invisibly dissolve the choking calcite crusts at the exact rate the water deposits them. The deep pipe will neither widen nor close. The hearth-breath of wet iron and old sulfur is fixed at forty-five degrees, pegged to the deep longevity of the continental plate itself.

The visitor opens its eyes. Now for the eaves.

The tett belong to the slow clock; the thumb-sized flyers hanging beneath the slate roofs do not. They are creatures of the six-week summer, built for hot blood, rapid hearts, and the brief madness of midges rising from the brown thaw-ponds. Their multi-year suspension in the glare—staring down at the streets with lidless, milky eyes while green algae carpets their wings—is not a life. It is a nightmare stretching across seasons, a botched death caused by the dense fusion of the greasewood canopy above.

Yet the city needs their bodies. When one falls into the ice-fern every few months, that tiny parcel of bone and fat is the rich marrow that sustains the mothers in the dark below, allowing them to build the calcified sacs of the young. If the visitor simply weaves the canopy shut so no flyer ever falls in again, the deep rooms will slowly starve of calcium and phosphorus.

The visitor reaches down through the gap in the bleached, linen-pale branches. Its fingers do not disturb the air; the cold inversion layer remains dead still. 

With the nail of one finger, it touches the fused bark of a silver-needled greasewood branch directly above the western eaves, where the largest cluster of the tiny, winged sleepers hangs. A silent, invisible instruction passes into the cambium of the shrub. 

Then, the visitor reaches deeper, into the shadowless glare, and touches the back of a single ochre-striped slug that is currently taking four hours to cross a slate roof near the bone chimney.

That is all. The traveler stands up, brushes the damp grey clay from its knees, and walks away across the barrens toward the low hills, leaving the pit to its silence.

Down in the basin, nothing seems to have changed. The bead of water on the bone lintel swells and drops into the liverwort. The mason in the four-inch alley reaches the intruding rosette and lifts its heavy, hydraulic foreclaw to shear away the first translucent leaf.

Wait six months.

When the brief summer returns to the plain above, and the sun climbs a fraction higher in the white-blue sky, the greasewood branch that was touched begins to respond to the new instruction in its wood. It does not snap or wither; its silver needles simply part, warping slowly outward as the sap rises, opening a vertical chimney in the canopy no wider than a stovepipe. 

For two hours at high noon, a single shaft of unfiltered, direct summer sun—unbroken by the milky diffuser of the branches—strikes the western eaves.

It hits the rows of thumb-sized sleepers. For those that are newly trapped—those that tumbled in during the autumn frost and still have a winter’s store of brown fat between their shoulder blades—the sudden, direct radiant heat is a key turning in a lock. Their heart rates climb from two beats a minute to twenty, then to a hundred, then to five hundred. The milky membranes snap back from their huge black eyes. They sneeze the damp from their nostrils, shake the early dust of algae from their leathery wings, and launch themselves upward, shooting straight up the narrow shaft of sunlight and out into the brown hills to hunt the midges before the greasewood closes again in the autumn chill.

And those that were already too old when the autumn came—the worn-out flyers of five summers whose wings were already tattered before they sought the crevice? 

The touch given to the ochre slug has passed through the city’s slow slime-trails, carried from back to back, entering the mosses and the damp air as a faint, sweet ester—an invisible vapor that smells, to a dying flyer, like the last warm mud of August. 

They do not hang staring into the blinding glare for three years while their fat withers and the green velvet eats their skin. When they fold their wings under the eaves and breathe in the cold damp of the pit, their tiny brains are immediately flooded with a deep, dreamless anaesthetic sleep. Their black eyes close—for the first time since they were born, the cramped muscles of their eyelids relax. Their hearts stop without a flutter during their very first night in the basin. Their hooked thumbs let go of the slick slate, and they drop, whole, fat, and peaceful, into the cushions of ice-fern below, ready for the blind masons to find them and carry them down into the warm dark where the mothers wait.

At the center of the pit, around the ancient ribs of the chimney, the old tett sit snout-to-snout in the rising forty-five-degree breath. A younger mason emerges from a boot-heel doorway, its jaws packed with fresh white clay that is just a fraction richer in dissolved limestone than any mortar chewed in the last thousand years. Slowly, at the speed of a growing stone, it climbs the steps to lay the new mud over its grandfather’s shell.
