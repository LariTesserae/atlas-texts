A lesser kindness would have brought light. It would have cracked the basalt ceiling to let the high, clean wind of the plateau sweep out the broth-thick air, or it would have sent a root down from the alpine meadows to tell the blind things about the sun. It would have pitied the Gleaners their endless, dark plumbing, and in pitying them, it would have murdered the only world they knew.

The power that turned its attention to the blister was older than the snow on the plateau, and far wiser than pity. It did not enter with a body, for a body would have displaced the air and shattered the horsehair glass. It entered merely as a listening—a weightless curiosity that seeped through the eighty feet of unfractured rock as easily as the heat rose from the magma root below.

For a long time—ten thousand *plops* of the central soapstone bowl—the power simply sat in the dark and read the room. 

It read the taut chitin-cords with their bird-bone beads. It traced the hollowing of the shaman’s femurs beneath the milky chalcedony, admiring how the white stalks had turned the grief of the ancient crawlers into a sturdy, calcified vascular system. It felt the slow, peristaltic pulse of the Film, hoarding its six-hundred-year-old tallow like a miser, passing a single molecule of fat back and forth between the yeast and the sulfur-bacteria so neither would ever truly starve, though neither could ever grow.

The power saw that this was a masterpiece of frugal love. The human makers had built an ark against the cold; the left-behind life had turned the ark into a body. 

It also saw the three silent deaths waiting for this body in the deep future.

First, the fat in the upper willow cradles was nearly gone. What remained was little more than a waxy ghost of carbon, recycled so many times through the Film and the Gleaners’ guts that it was losing its heat with every pass. Within another century, the sweet tallow-sugar would run dry, and the pact between the Film and its long, segmented hands would starve into stillness.

Second, the mountain was still alive, and therefore restless. Forty yards away, where the cooling tube had pinched shut six hundred years ago, tension was gathering again in the basalt. The next tremor would not merely shift a slab by four inches; it would send a sheer wall of shockwaves straight through the granary-sized blister. In a single heartbeat, every hanging glass thread, every thigh-thick pillar and razor needle, would shatter into a million tons of falling daggers, turning the soft, wet leather of the alcoves and the fragile bodies of the Gleaners into crushed paste.

Third, and longest of all: the blister was a closed box. Even if the fat lasted and the rock held, the silica sweating from the ceiling would eventually win. In two thousand years, the milky stone would fill every basin, choke every bowl faster than the iron-mandibles could nip it away, and entomb the room in solid, blind agate. 

The wise power considered these three endings. It had no desire to turn the blister into a garden of the surface, nor to wake the distributed mind into the sharp, lonely agony of human-like thought. Its love for the creature was a love for what the creature *already was*: a slow, warm, tactile patience. 

So the power reached into the stone and did three very small things. None of them made a sound louder than a falling drop of water.

First, it addressed the mountain’s anger. 

The power moved outward from the blister, sliding its attention into the eighty feet of solid basalt that walled the chamber in. Five yards outside the pocket, it subtly altered the cooling memory of the rock. It opened a hairline fault—no wider than a split hair—that wrapped entirely around the blister like the shell of an walnut, and it filled that microscopic gap not with stone, but with a slick, soapy seam of pure talc and soft serpentine clay. 

Now, the chamber was uncoupled from the mountain's bones. When the great tremor finally came forty years later, the plateau shuddered and the upper crags fell into the valleys, but the shockwave hit the envelope of greased clay around the blister and simply slid past, converted into a gentle, momentary warmth. Inside, not a single horsehair of black glass snapped. A single drop of water fell two beats early from a soapstone lip; a Gleaner paused on a cord, tilted its whips at the slight slur in the rhythm, found nothing broken, and resumed its stillness.

Second, the power looked to the starving baskets high in the needles, and the creeping threat of the silica. 

It did not drop dead meat into the room, which would have brought rot and foreign violence. Instead, it reached *down*, toward the deep magmatic root that kept the floor at the temperature of blood. There, in the crushing dark beneath the basalt, pockets of primordial carbon—ancient methane and heavy, abiotic oils cooked out of the earth’s mantle—were trapped in the stone pores. 

The power took a single, microscopic capillary in the floor of the deepest kneeling-alcove and pinched it open, connecting the root-brain of the bone-trellises to that deep, volcanic cellar. 

Up through the hollow shaman-bones came a breath of warm, heavy hydrocarbons and hydrogen sulfide—not a flood, just a seep, no faster than the sweat on the walls. To a human, it would have smelled faintly of hot iron and asphalt. To the sulfur-bacteria and the ancient, mutated yeasts of the Film, it was an eternal spring of raw, unspent fat. 

At the same time, the power tilted the floor of the lowest, farthest basin by the breadth of a fingernail, opening a weeping-pore at the bottom of the stone plates. Now, the water had somewhere to go, infinitely slowly, after it had given up its warmth. The room would never drown in its own stone. As fast as the ceiling wept silica, the deepest roots could drink what they needed and let the excess mineral brine bleed back down into the dark belly of the earth.

Finally, the power looked at the Gleaners, and the taut cords of chitin-wrapped sinew, and the hollow bird-bones that the makers had left behind to speak to their own blind fingers.

The colony was safe now. It had food from the deep earth; it had a shield of soft clay around its ribs; it had a drain for its stone. It could live for ten million years,nipping the rims of the bowls, listening to the *plop* of the water. 

Yet the power felt a tender ache for the smallness of the room. A granary-sized pocket is a tiny place to spend ten million years, even for a mind that thinks only in vibrations and salt. The makers had built the knots to be read, and the Gleaners read them every day, running their four long whips over the same fire-hardened clay beads, the same notches on the same bird bones, memorizing a warning about low-hanging glass that had not changed in six centuries. 

It was a book with only one sentence.

The power did not give them eyes. It did not give them language. Instead, it looked at the eighty feet of solid basalt surrounding the chamber, and it saw seven other blisters. 

They were scattered through the belly of the plateau, ten yards away, thirty yards away, a hundred. They had been formed in the same ancient sigh of the magma chamber. They were just as dark, just as warm, and just as choked with raw, razor-sharp tachylyte glass. Some were smaller than a bread-oven; one was as large as a cathedral, its ceiling hung with black glass curtains sixty feet tall, entirely empty of life, waiting in the sterile steam.

The power did not smash a tunnel between them—a tunnel big enough for a man would have ruined the pressure and the broth-thick air. 

Instead, beginning at the back of the deepest kneeling-alcove, right behind the mat of shed skins and calcified moss, the power drew a single finger through the solid basalt. It pulled a tube of smooth, black glass through the living rock, no wider than the spaces between the hanging pillars—just wide enough for a thumb-thick creature with oiled pads to flow through without touching a sharp edge. 

And at the lip of this new, tiny threshold, where the old room ended and the dark tube began, the power took a tiny thread of the Film and a pinch of precipitated chalcedony, and it grew a single, new bead on the end of the ancient sinew cord. 

It was not smooth like the clay beads, nor hollow like the bird bones. It was shaped with a spiral groove, like the shell of one of the blind limpets, warm and resonant. When the water dripped in the far-off, empty cathedral sixty yards away, the vibration traveled through the stone and hummed faintly, invitingly, inside that spiral groove. 

Then the wise power withdrew, leaving the dark to the dark.

For forty-eight hours, nothing happened. The water sweated down the needles. The white stalks drank from the old bones. The Film tasted the new, rich butter of the deep-earth hydrocarbons rising from the floor and sent a slow, golden shudder of pleasure through its curtains of wet amber.

Then, on the third day, the oldest of the Gleaners came down from the upper willow cradles. It had just finished nipping a crust of stone from a chert bowl, and its mandibles were stained black with iron. 

It glided down the central pillar on its hundreds of oiled feet, reaching the kneeling-alcove. It cast its four long, cobweb-fine whips out into the soup-thick air to read the familiar sentence of the room: *clay bead, clay bead, knot, bird-bone, glass-pillar.*

Its leftmost whip brushed the end of the cord. 

*Tick.*

The whip stopped. 

For an hour, the Gleaner hung entirely motionless between the razors, its clear, glass-like shell showing the slow, steady pump of its heart. Its three other whips dropped away from the old knots, curling inward, while the fourth rested lightly in the spiral groove of the new stone bead. 

Through the bead, it felt the echo. Not the twenty-heartbeat *plop* of the room it knew, but a vast, cavernous chime—a drop of water falling sixty feet from a ceiling of black glass into an untended basin that had never known a bowl, or a root, or a mason’s jaw. 

Slowly, the Gleaner unhooked its rear legs from the pillar. It crawled down to the Film, pressed its mouthparts into the warm, golden jelly, and took a long draught of the new, deep-earth sugar. It gathered a small pellet of living yeast and sulfur-paste into the crook of its mandibles, the way a farmer carries seed.

Then it turned, stepped past the spiral bead, and flowed smoothly into the warm, narrow throat of the new tube, carrying the room’s quiet attention out into the mountain.
