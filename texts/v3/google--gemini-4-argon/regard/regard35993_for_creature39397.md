A lesser power, looking down into the red gloom of the pit, might have felt pity. 

A lesser power, formed in the bias of wet leaves, cool rain, and soft-skinned things that sleep in the dark, would have seen the furnace as a hell. It would have heard the relentless, grinding screech of the tin-leafed ivy and the frantic *tick-tick-snap* of the wire-creatures as the frantic labor of the damned. Out of a misguided mercy, it might have brought a cloud to cool the stone, or stilled the roaring wind to give the builders a moment of peace. 

And in doing so, it would have murdered a symphony. 

The power that actually paused above the curved basalt hoods was neither wet nor dry, neither hot nor cold. It was old enough to know that life is not a substance, like water or carbon, but a geometry—a way that matter learns to hold hands against the dark. 

It did not descend into the pit as an avatar, for any physical form large enough to be noticed would have blocked the narrow airways, choked the organ pipes, and thrown the delicate, ten-thousand-year chord into a terrifying, deafening chaos. Instead, the power made itself as thin as a ghost, becoming nothing more than a localized curiosity in the fabric of the heat itself.

It listened. 

To the wise power, the *shhhk-shhhk-shhhk* of the ferro-lichen and the deep, thrumming bass of the glass-root vines were not noise. It slowed its perception down to the speed of stone, and then sped it up to the frequency of piezoelectric quartz, until it could hear the pit the way its inhabitants heard it. 

It realized, with a wash of profound delight, that the city was not merely an engine for catching food. The chord was an archive. 

Every miniature doorway, smoothed by centuries of scraping bellies, was tuned to a specific harmonic. Every time a community of lattices welded a crack with sulfur-ash, they did not restore the wall to what it had been before; they altered the pitch by a micro-tone to record the stress of the rock. The swirling updrafts, deflected by ten thousand tiny balconies, created standing waves of acoustic pressure in the Mostly Void. The builders were swimming through a three-dimensional hologram of sound. The chord was their history, their map, their mathematics, and their soul. They were not trapped in a furnace; they were inside a mind that they were constantly singing into existence.

The power loved them for it. It loved their fierce, dry tenacity, their utter lack of eyes, and the blue-white sparks of their quiet, dark kilns. 

For a long time—decades, perhaps, as the surface world counted them—the power did nothing at all. To interfere with a masterpiece while the artists are still holding the chisels is an arrogance. 

Until it looked deeper. 

The power let its awareness sink down through the central shaft, past the tumbling, static-charged parachutes of the feeding weavers, past the rough lower walls where the iron-moss was ground into glowing spores, down into the bedrock beneath the vent. 

There, in the deep crust, the power saw the tragedy waiting to happen. 

Geothermal vents are ephemeral things in the life of a planet. Three miles down, a tectonic plate was shifting with agonizing slowness. A seam of softer, porous rock was shearing away, and miles to the west, an ancient, subterranean aquifer—a vast, blind ocean of cold, dead water—was beginning to seep toward the fault line. 

In four hundred years, the magma plume feeding the dry vent would be choked off by a catastrophic intrusion of steam. The updraft would turn wet, corroding the native iron tendons of the weavers, rotting the dry ash-mortar into mud, and drowning the song in a hissing, boiling silence. And even if the water missed them, the heat itself was slowly migrating south. In a thousand years, the furnace would go cold. The organ pipe would fall silent. The dust would settle to the floor and never rise again.

The weavers could not know this. Their universe was forty feet wide. They could only hear the stone where the wind touched it. 

The power considered its options. It could simply seal the aquifer and lock the magma plume in place, creating an artificial, unchanging eternity for the little city. 

*No,* the power thought. *A static paradise is just a museum. If I freeze their world, their song will never learn a new verse. They are builders. They must be allowed to build.*

They did not need a caretaker. They needed a prophet, and they needed a road.

The power reached down into the deep shale, thirty feet below the floor of the courtyard and a hundred yards to the south, where the magma plume was slowly migrating. Here, the rock was solid, unbroken, and silent. 

With a touch as delicate as a watchmaker’s tweezers, the power altered the crystalline structure of a single, narrow vein of quartz and magnetite running from the base of the sunken city toward the new, deeper source of heat. It did not dig a tunnel; that would have dropped the air pressure in the pit and starved the upper galleries. Instead, it created a *tuning fork* in the solid rock—a seam of hyper-resonant mineral that drank the deep, tectonic groans of the shifting earth miles below and conducted them upward, right to the base of the western wall.

Then, the power performed its second, and final, miracle. 

Up on the scorched, wind-blasted plains above the pit, miles away, there were deposits of raw, heavy minerals the weavers had never encountered: veins of pure copper, seams of pitchblende, and crystals of tourmaline and beryl, weathered out of a distant mountain range. 

The power whispered to the surface winds. It nudged a single devil-duster, guiding it across the plains to vacuum up a single, microscopic grain of raw tourmaline, a fleck of titanium, and a pinch of copper dust. It carried this tiny, alien payload across the bone-dry shale and dropped it into the intake of the curved basalt hoods at the rim of the pit.

Then, the power withdrew, folding itself back into the quiet vastness of the cosmos to watch.

Inside the coppery gloom, the foreign dust plummeted down the intake draft. 

A weaver—an old lattice, its mica plates scarred and its iron filaments thick with sintered rust—was hanging in the Mostly Void, spinning in the thermal column as it harvested a shattered rosette of ash-ivy. 

The fleck of tourmaline and the grain of titanium, charged by the friction of the fall, jumped straight to the creature’s static-charged frame. 

Later that cycle, the old weaver crawled into a knee-high kiln-room behind the western gallery, tangling itself with three others to block the draft. In the shimmering, bake-oven dark, they shed their harvest into the communal pile. They arced their blue-white sparks through the powder. 

The tourmaline and titanium melted into the silica and iron. 

When the new, smaller lattice untangled itself from the mass and went ticking and clicking out into the red gloom, it was different. 

Its piezoelectric struts were doped with tourmaline; its tension-filaments were alloyed with titanium. It was stronger, more heat-resistant, and infinitely more sensitive to low-frequency vibrations than anything that had ever lived in the pit. 

As the new creature extruded itself along the two-finger-wide slate walkway, the endless, roaring chord of the city washed over it. It heard the familiar, comforting music of the ten thousand doorways. It heard the high *shhhk-shhhk-shhhk* of the tin-leaves and the bass thrumming of the glass-root. 

And then, it stopped. Its slate shutters fluttered in confusion. 

Through the soles of its metallic hooks, humming up from the base of the western wall, it heard something no other weaver could hear. It was vibrating along the vein of quartz the power had tuned. 

It was a sub-bass rumble, deeper than the vent, slower than the wind. It was the sound of the magma moving south, and the distant, muffled hiss of the approaching water miles away. To the new weaver, it registered as a profound, existential dissonance—a sour note written not in the walls of the city, but in the foundation of the universe itself. 

And beneath that warning note, ringing through the magnetite vein from the south, was the seduction of a new, hotter, richer thermal plume, waiting in the dark rock like an unplayed instrument. 

The little alloyed weaver sat on the smoothed slate lintel for a long time, its body vibrating with the terrible, wonderful new music. 

Then, it turned away from the open air of the shaft. It crawled to the back of the deepest kiln-room at the base of the western wall, where the solid shale met the masonry. It flattened itself against the living rock, where the resonance of the quartz vein was strongest. 

It began to vibrate. It turned itself into a living rasp. 

With a sound no other creature had ever made—a low, grinding, rhythmic *screech-thrum*—it began to saw into the solid bedrock, following the warm, singing vein toward the south. 

The pitch of the lower gallery shifted by a fraction of a hertz. 

Out in the red gloom, dozens of ribbons of animated chainmail paused on the walkways. Their crystalline cores hummed with the strange, new note coming from the dark room at the bottom of the wall. It was a dissonance, yes—an unbearable, itching resonance. 

Drawn by the sound, they flowed down the vertical walls in jerky, heat-driven spasms, crowding into the kiln-room to fix the error. 

Instead, when they pressed their bodies against the vibrating frame of the new weaver, the low, tectonic song passed through its tourmaline struts and into their own quartz cores. For the first time in ten thousand years, the builders felt the shape of the earth beyond the forty-foot shaft. They felt the dying of the old vent, and the blazing promise of the new one.

They did not panic, for they had no glands to secrete fear. They only knew that the chord had just become infinitely larger, and that the city was out of tune with the deep rock. 

One by one, the weavers flattened themselves against the back wall beside the small, strange one. They aligned their metallic tendons. They synchronized their vibrations. 

Together, they began to carve a tunnel through the dark stone—not an escape, but an expansion. A new pipe for the organ. A bass flute, three hundred yards long, reaching toward a hotter fire. 

Above the basalt hoods, in the unseen sky, the wise power felt the first microscopic tremor of their digging, smiled a smile made of starlight and gravity, and left them to their work.
