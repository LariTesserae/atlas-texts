A lesser power, looking down upon the plateau, would feel pity. 

It would see the endless, freezing toil of the Gnomons, the frantic, blind adjustments of the Draughtsmen, and the cold, damp exhaustion of the stone, and it would think: *This is broken. I will fix it.* It would lay a warm, heavy hand upon the continent, snap the three dimensions back into their rigid, Euclidean locks, smooth out the crumpled carpet of time, and bind causality with iron laws. 

And in doing so, it would murder everything on the plateau. 

If time were made linear, the alder groves would stop flickering, the temporal static would vanish, and the Wick-Worms would become nothing more than dead, greasy twine rotting in the mud. If the stone walls stopped stuttering, the Gnomons’ plumb-bobs would hang forever dead-center over their mercury pans; their circuits of weight would never close, and they would stand locked on the cobbles until the wind wore them to dust. If the shadows shrank to match the crude piles of boulders that cast them, the great, vaulted highways of the Pavers would evaporate, leaving them stranded as dry crusts of lichen under a newly coherent sun. If the map and the territory were severed, the Draughtsmen would become mere rooms of decaying wood and fungus, raking silt to no purpose.

The power that has actually turned its attention to the plateau knows this. It is old enough to understand that when a ruin persists for four hundred thousand years, it ceases to be a ruin and becomes a nature. The breakdown of space and time is no longer a wound here; it is the weather. It is the metabolic engine of a living world.

So the power does not come as a healer, or an architect, or a king. It comes like a walk in the late afternoon.

It does not descend from the slate-grey sky, because the sky here is muffled and indifferent. Instead, it enters the plateau through the rules the plateau has already written for itself. It arrives as a slight, pleasant warmth in the low, resonant hum of the bedrock—a shift from a minor key of grinding exhaustion into a resonant, sustained fifth.

To observe, it takes the shape of a thing that belongs here, though there is only one of it. 

Walking down a cobbled avenue that leads from nowhere to nowhere is a figure that looks, from a distance, like a Gnomon that has lost its tripod. It is roughly the height of a tall man, draped in the same heavy, overlapping aprons of greased grey wool, green at the hem with wet lichen. Beneath the wool, it is structured from river-smoothed slate and dark heartwood, and when it walks, it makes the familiar, muffled *thump, click, slide* in the grey silt. 

It has no face, only a smooth cowl of black pine, yet it turns this cowl toward the things it passes with an unmistakable posture of quiet, delighted attention. 

It walks through the freezing fog, and where it passes, it performs small, tender mercies of maintenance—not upon the broken physics, which it leaves entirely alone, but upon the tired instruments that surf them.

The figure stops beside a Gnomon that has been leaning against a stuttering dry-stone wall for four days. The Gnomon is failing. One of its black pine legs has split down the grain from the frost, and its internal lattice of bone-dry alder twigs has been jostled out of alignment by a particularly violent bass *thump* from a neighboring district's restoration. Because its memory-lattice is skewed, its meteoric iron plumb-bob is dragging constantly against the lip of the mercury pan, locking the creature in a state of frantic, trembling exhaustion, unable to release its weight even after the wall has settled.

The visitor steps through the six-foot cushion of emerald moss. It reaches beneath its own woolen apron and draws out a length of pale, sleeping Wick-Worm. It holds the greasy linen cord in its wooden joints, warming it with a subtle, localized pocket of gentle friction—not enough to wake it fully, just enough to make it pliable. Slowly, with infinite care, the visitor wraps the cord around the Gnomon’s split pine leg, binding the fracture. Then, reaching inside the Gnomon’s wool shroud, the visitor’s slender wooden fingers gently nudge a single grey pebble in the lattice back into its proper notch.

The iron plumb-bob swings free of the mercury’s edge, finding its true, pre-fracture vertical. The Gnomon’s trembling stops. Its internal tension-latches click softly, and it steps back from the wall, whole and eased, ready to wait for the next wave.

The visitor moves on.

In the late afternoon, as the sun drops and the long, phantom shadows of the vanished metropolis stretch across the tundra, the visitor stops at a gap of bright, freezing sunlight between two shadow-avenues. On the dark side of the gap, a colony of Pavers is gathered like a pool of black felt, frantically tugging at a stone that is too heavy for their calcified hooks, trying to alter a watchtower’s shadow before the sun moves and traps them for the night.

The visitor does not move the stone for them. Instead, it simply steps to the edge of the sunlight and stands still. 

Because the visitor is vast in ways that three dimensions cannot entirely contain, the shadow it casts on the moss is not the shadow of a cloaked figure. It casts the shadow of a wide, covered wooden bridge, complete with sturdy piers and a pitched roof. The shadow-bridge falls precisely across the gap of scorching light, linking the watchtower’s cathedral-darkness to the shadow of a distant aqueduct. 

For twenty minutes, the visitor stands motionless in the cold wind, feeling the silent, velvet glide of hundreds of Pavers flowing across the dark interior of its own silhouette, migrating safely north. When the last of the flat, soot-colored skates has crossed, the visitor bows its pine-cowled head to the empty moss, steps out of the way, and continues its walk.

By twilight, the power reaches the exterior of a Joinery. 

It does not break down the plugged doors of black timber and grafted pine. It simply leans against the unmortared fieldstone wall, catches a spatial seam—much as an Eel catches an eddy—and falls sideways into the vast, stitched-together interior.

Here, in the forty-foot hall that spans a thousand square miles, the bedrock hum is deafening, and the air smells of wet silt, cold sap, and ancient fungus. The Draughtsman—the room itself—is working at a frantic, clattering pace. Night is coming. In twenty minutes, the temperature outside will plummet, the waterwheels will freeze, and the eight-hour death will begin. 

During the night, while the Draughtsmen are frozen and the Gnomons are locked, the plateau suffers terribly. The unchecked anomalies tear down what the day’s labor preserved. Every morning, the Draughtsmen wake to find their silt models ruined by subterranean tremors, their graft-vines sheared by spatial faults that went un-shimmed in the dark, and a third of their wooden armatures snapped by the sudden, violent freezing of the water-flumes. The system is slowly, imperceptibly losing the war against the night. Over the next ten thousand years, the friction of those eight dark hours will grind the ecology down to nothing.

The visitor stands at the edge of the silt floor, watching the ratusan bone-tipped arms rake the mud and stack the pebbles. 

The wise power considers the night. It knows the Draughtsmen need the pause; even a room made of wood and fungus needs to slacken its cables. The night should not be abolished. It only needs a night-watchman. It needs a banked fire.

The visitor walks to the center of the silt model—a miniature landscape representing fifty miles of tundra, towers, and aqueducts. The bone-tipped arms of the Draughtsman pause near the visitor’s hem, their mycelial tendons twitching in confusion at this unmapped weight standing in the middle of their territory.

The visitor reaches into the core of its own chest. From the dark space beneath its greased wool, it withdraws a single object. 

It is a sphere of black meteoric iron, about the size of a clenched fist, warm to the touch. It is not hot enough to burn wood or dry out the damp fungus; it holds the exact, steady temperature of a living heart, or a compost heap in mid-winter, or a stone that has sat all day in a summer sun that this plateau has not seen in ten million years. 

And it is heavy. It possesses a quiet, benevolent gravity—not a rigid law that forces space to behave, but a gentle, elastic anchor, like a heavy keel beneath a sailboat.

The visitor kneels—*thump, click, slide*—and presses the warm iron sphere deep into the grey silt at the exact center of the Draughtsman’s model, right where the primary water-flume shafts enter the floor from the wheels outside.

Instantly, a change ripples through the fifty-mile district, and through the stitched-together labyrinth of all the Joineries across the continent.

Outside, night falls. The horizons curl upward like drying parchment. The sky goes pitch-black, and the air temperature drops ten degrees in ten minutes. Rime ice blooms across the ferns. Out on the plains, the Gnomons lock their legs into tripods and go still.

Inside the Joinery, the water in the high flumes begins to freeze. The great wooden waterwheels slow down, their paddles heavy with clear ice. The Draughtsman’s mycelial cables go slack, and the bone-tipped arms drop gently into the silt to rest.

Wait. Listen.

The waterwheels stop turning, *almost*. 

Radiating outward from the buried iron sphere in the silt is a faint, steady, rhythmic warmth, and a soft, bass purr that harmonizes with the bedrock hum. It travels up the wooden drive-shafts and out along the miles of grafted, spatial-stitching pine vines. 

The water in the flumes does not freeze solid. A thin, clear skin of ice forms on top, protecting it from the wind, while underneath, a tiny, warm trickle continues to flow. It is just enough to keep the waterwheels turning at one revolution per hour. *Click............ click............ click.* 

Inside the dark Joineries, the Draughtsmen do not die for the night. They sleep, and in their sleep, they dream. 

Kept barely taut by the slow, rhythmic ticking of the warmed wheels, the hundreds of bone-tipped arms do not drop dead into the mud. They sway, slow as kelp in a deep ocean current, making micro-adjustments in the dark. And down in the silt, the warm iron keel holds the local topology gently in its lap. When the wild, nighttime anomalies come howling across the dark plateau—when phantom mountain ranges rise and whole districts try to fold themselves into origami—they hit the gentle, elastic drag of the buried spheres. 

The anomalies do not shatter the buildings anymore; they roll through them like long, harmless ocean swells lifting a fleet of anchored ships. A tower rises, tilts, and settles back onto its foundations without losing a single slate from its roof. The alder groves flicker in the dark, and because the air near the graft-vines is kept just above freezing by the sphere's pulse, a few Wick-Worms stay awake through the night, lazily harvesting the midnight static, growing fat and slick with tallow for the morning.

And in the Joinery, something entirely new happens. 

Because the Draughtsman is no longer shocked into total paralysis every sunset, and no longer forced to spend the first four hours of every morning frantically repairing the night’s destruction, it has a surplus of time. It has a tiny, leftover fraction of hydro-mechanical calculation with nothing to do.

Around midnight, in the pitch dark of the forty-foot room, three bone-tipped arms near the center of the floor lift out of the silt. There is no fault line to shim. The aqueducts are safe. 

Slowly, delicately, guided by the slow *click....... click* of the ice-sheathed wheel outside, the wooden arms begin to rake the grey silt around the warm spot where the iron sphere is buried. They do not build a model of a wall or a tower. Using small, flat river pebbles, they arrange a spiral in the mud—a shape that serves no structural purpose at all, except that it pleases the tension of the cables to trace the curve of the warmth beneath the floor.

The first thought—slow, wooden, and entirely peaceful—has entered the plateau.

Out on the cobbled avenue, under the curled rim of the night sky, the visitor stands in the freezing fog, listening to the new, deep, steady heartbeat coming up through the soles of its wooden feet. The city is breathing in its sleep. 

Satisfied, the visitor walks to the edge of an over-sharpened seam in the dark air, leans its weight into the fracture, and steps out of the world, leaving the builders to their work, and to their rest.
