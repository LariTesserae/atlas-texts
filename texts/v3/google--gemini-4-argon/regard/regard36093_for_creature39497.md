A lesser power would have brought rain. 

A foolish god, looking down from the dark violet sky into that crowded, grease-stained, echoing throat of stone, would have felt a surge of pity for their thirst. It would have cracked the basalt to give them a spring, or flattened the mountain to give them fields of grain, or broadened the shaft so they might stand upright and walk like other men. 

And in doing so, it would have killed them all in a week. A single torrential rain would tear the adobe pods from the slick basalt lobes, rot the tension-vines, drown the deep bacterial furnace of the Sump, and send the entire five-hundred-year-old tapestry of bone, hair, and balance crashing into the dark. If they were given wide, flat fields, what would become of the opposable toes, the whistled geometry of their love songs, the sacred weight-etiquette that bound neighbor to neighbor so tightly that no one could take a step without another leaning to catch them?

The Power that paused above the fumarole was neither lesser nor foolish. It was very old, and it understood that beauty is not the absence of struggle, but the shape that life takes when it leans against the wall of its limits.

It lingered at the rim, invisible in the freezing wind, and it listened. It heard the *chak-chak* of the geckos, the groan of the bone-wheels, and the liquid, bouncing trills of the cistern-keepers. It felt the immense, precarious intimacy of a thousand people holding each other suspended over the dark. 

Then, the Power sank its awareness down through the five hundred feet of bruised-amber twilight, past the compost and the blind millipedes, past the five-thousand-year choke of fallen rock, all the way down into the planetary crust—miles below, where the dying heart of the volcano sat cooling in the dark.

There, the Power found the truth the people of the Chimney did not know: the Breath was failing. 

The magma chamber that had exhaled for ten million years was nearly solid now, crusted over with cooling olivine and heavy basalt. In three hundred years—ten generations of perches and hair-nets—the rising draft would slow from three miles an hour to two. The moisture would drop out of the air too deep in the throat for the rim-nets to catch. In five hundred years, the frost of the violet sky would creep down the polished stone lobes, blackening the thirty-foot curtains of beard-moss, stiffening the joints of the geckos, until the lung of the mountain simply stopped breathing, and the whole intricate town became a freeze-dried fossil in a cold tube.

Furthermore, the Power looked at the lianas—the great, woody anchors that held the town up—and saw that after centuries of self-pollination inside the isolated shaft, their genetics were growing tired. Their fibers were a fraction more brittle than they had been in the days of the ancestors; their resistance to the Sump-fungi was thinning.

The Power considered this. It did not want to be worshipped. Worship changes the posture of a people; it makes them look up when they should be looking across at the counterweights. 

So the Power did three very small things, down in the dark where no eye—not even the huge, liquid eyes of the Sump-dwellers—could ever see.

First, miles beneath the Chimney, where the magma was turning to stone, the Power did not reignite the volcano—that would bring tremors and poison gas. Instead, right at the core of the cooling pluton, it wove a lattice of heavy, timeless elements into the rock: a quiet, self-sustaining sub-critical heart of radiogenic warmth, wrapped in a sponge of porous basalt fed by a microscopic seam of deep-earth juvenile water. It calibrated the heat to the exact fraction of a degree, and the steam-release to the exact ounce of pressure. 

Now, the Breath would not fail in five hundred years. It would rise at three miles an hour, at seventy degrees, carrying the exact same faint tang of sulfur and mist, for the next ten million years. Until the sun itself grew old, the lung would breathe.

Second, the Power rose to the Sump, into the hot, bacterial muck where the ancestors were becoming soil. It reached into the root-tips of the great lianas and the spores of the beard-moss. With the delicacy of a weaver mending a hair-net, it untangled the tired, inbred spirals of their life-code. It repaired the genetic fraying, strengthened the lignin in the woody vines so they would hold twice the tension without snapping, and gave the giant bromeliads a subtle new trait: in their deep, armored cores, where the condensed water sat, the plants would now secrete a faint, mineral-rich mucilage containing trace amounts of iron, copper, and salt—leached from the deep basalt—preventing the slow anemia that sometimes claimed the Sump-children in their third year.

The third thing the Power did was on the Rim.

High on the eastern lip of the shaft, just where the harsh alpine sunlight first struck the throat at dawn, there was a massive, bulbous lobe of dark basalt that the whistlers used as a primary sounding-board to bounce messages down to the mid-tiers. 

As the Power departed back into the vast, freezing emptiness of the roof of the world, it brushed its thumb against the inside of this stone lobe, altering the crystalline structure of the rock just beneath the surface, hollowing a microscopic honeycomb of quartz into the basalt.

The next morning, a young Rim-Clan girl in a coat of stitched rail-skins climbed out onto her perch to check the dew-cisterns. Her eyes were squinted against the glare; her cropped head was patterned with red ochre. She hooked her horn-soled feet over the ironwood rung, leaned back against the tension-line to test the morning balance, and pursed her lips to whistle the dawn yield down to her grandmother in the amber gloom three hundred feet below.

She blew a sharp, three-note trill against the eastern rock lobe.

The stone caught the whistle, as it had for a thousand years, and threw it down into the shaft. But as the sound left the rock, the hidden honeycomb of quartz hummed in sympathy. 

The girl’s sharp, utilitarian whistle did not just bounce; it *bloomed*. It gained a rich, warm, golden overtone—a second, phantom harmony that resonated at the exact frequency of a human chest cavity. As the note fell down through the tiers, cutting through the *chak-chak* of the lizards and the drone of the beetles, everyone who heard it felt a sudden, gentle vibration in their breastbone, like a warm hand laid briefly between their shoulder blades. 

Throughout the shaft, five hundred people paused on their perches. The weavers took their hands from the bone looms; the Sump-tenders looked up through their pinhole goggles. 

The girl at the rim blinked her slit-eyes, startled. She whistled again—a simple question: *Did you hear that?*

Again, the rock answered, wrapping her small, breathy note in a deep, bell-like chord that filled the stone cylinder with a sudden, inexplicable sense of safety, as if the mountain itself had just shifted its weight three inches to the left to make sure it was holding them securely.

Within an hour, the young men and women were leaning out from every porch, bouncing trills and clicks off the eastern lobe just to hear the stone sing back to them, laughing—a deep, rumbling, barrel-chested sound—as they invented new songs for the new voice in the wall.

Nothing else changed. The water was still rationed by the thimbleful. The dead were still lowered in hair-nets to feed the millipedes. The wind outside still ground the dead granite into flour under a starved sky. 

The Power went on its way across the universe, leaving them to their grease, their balance, and their warm, noisy dark—confident that the rope would hold, and the warmth would last, and the children of the Chimney would never have to walk on flat ground.
