To a lesser mind, the Drum looks like a purgatory—a place crying out for the mercy of rain, or a cool breeze, or a gentle lifting of the inhabitants out of the furnace and into a green meadow.

The Power that pauses above the blinding salt-rim knows better. 

It looks down into the two-hundred-pace well of white fire and thundering silica, and It recognizes a masterpiece. It sees the staggering, ferocious elegance of a closed loop that has held its breath for ten thousand years. The Power knows that a single drop of rain would dissolve the salt-brick balconies into slurry, rot the dry air-sacs of the Lath-Men, and silence the great stone organ forever. To give them water would be to drown them. To give them silence would be to strike them blind.

Yet, looking down through the ultraviolet shimmer, the Power sees the tragedy that the Lath-Men themselves do not yet know is approaching, though they have begun to drum about it in the dark.

The city is wearing out. 

Not the flora, and not the vents below, which are fed by the deep, cooling mantle of the world. It is the *instruments* that are dying. Down in the furnace-wards, the notched ironwood of the ancient wind-wheels—grown in a wet forest that vanished before the salt flats were born—is finally reaching the end of its molecular tether. The wood is splitting into dry splinters that cannot be lashed back together. When a wheel shatters, the Lath-Men have no new trees to fell. They cannibalize the dead wheel to repair the others, and they use the splinters for their pruning-knives. Century by century, there are fewer blades, and fewer grinding-stones turning in their housings. 

And worse: the city’s masonry, though repaired with spit-less mineral resin and ash, is losing mass. Every century, the fierce updrafts scour a few more tons of gypsum and salt-brick into fine powder and blow it out the top of the cylinder into the thousand-mile waste. The walls are thinning. The great harmonic baritone of the lower galleries is creeping upward into a strained, brittle tenor. In five hundred years, the third and fourth tiers will shear off into the central void. The wind-throats will rupture. The Drum will lose its tune, choke on its own kelp, and cook itself into a quiet grave.

The Power does not descend as an avatar. An avatar has weight, and shadows, and wet breath, and has no place here. Instead, the Power acts as a gardener of physics, moving through the deep earth beneath the sinkhole.

It begins three miles down, in the magma-heated plumbing of the thermal vents. 

With a touch as light as a thought, the Power adjusts the mineral chemistry of the subterranean fumaroles. It does not change the heat—not by a single degree—nor the velocity of the updraft. Instead, It introduces a trace vapor of vaporized silicates, dissolved iron-vein, and heavy, metallic salts into the rising column of hot air. 

For three days, nothing seems to change. The sun hammers the white floor; the Glass-Skimmers click across the void; the chime-kelp thrashes its deafening maraca-roar. 

Then, a Lath-Man hanging by his heels in the lowest, hottest gallery—the *Clack-Ward*, where the last seven ironwood wheels spin furiously in the gale—feels a shift in his breastbone.

The pitch of the masonry beneath his ribs is deepening. 

The trace vapors rising from the vents are striking the cooler, electrostatic surfaces of the lower salt-brick walls and condensing—not as liquid, which would melt the city, but as a microscopic, crystalline hoarfrost of pure silicate and bog-iron. Every hour, the wind deposits a layer of mineral glaze no thicker than a soap bubble onto the eroded mud-brick. Instead of scouring the walls away, the wind is now *plating* them. The sagging balconies are stiffening, their pale, bone-white mud drinking in the heavy vapor and vitrifying from the inside out into a substance harder than porcelain, resonant as cast bronze.

The Lath-Man unhooks his heel. He taps his wood-knife against the wall. 

Instead of the dull, chalky *tak* he has known all his life, the wall answers with a clear, lingering *ting* that travels fifty yards through the gallery before fading. 

Down in the wheel-housings, something even stranger is happening. The bladder-ferns and the brass-vine, drinking the newly enriched sulfur-air, are mutating slightly in their seasonal growth. In the deepest crevices near the vents, a dormant seed-strain—something that had waited in the dry masonry since the builders vanished—wakes at the taste of airborne iron. 

A new vine begins to uncoil from the cracks. It does not grow broad, parchment ribbons like the chime-kelp. It grows in straight, four-sided shafts of black, metallic cellulose, segmented like bamboo, as dense and heavy as hornbeam wood. As it grows, the constant friction of the grit-laden wind polishes its bark into a dark, mirror-bright sheen. When a stalk reaches six feet, it dries, detaches from its root-stock with a sharp *crack*, and lies rattling in the stone gutter—a pre-cut stave of living ironwood, waiting to be split into knife-blades or lashed into the hub of a broken mill-wheel.

The city is saved. It will not erode; it will not run out of tools. 

Many benevolent powers would stop there, satisfied with the preservation of the machine. 

But this Power remembers the dark vaults behind the granaries. It remembers the six or eight skulls locked horn-to-horn in the oven-hot dark, drumming small, private histories onto their own ribs because the world outside is too loud, too vast, and too chaotic to hold a song of their own making. They are the tuners of an instrument that only plays one deafening, eternal chord, while their own music is trapped inside a six-foot ring of bone.

The Power looks at the center of the Drum—that ninety percent of blinding, empty space where the thermal column rises straight to the hard blue circle of the sky. 

Down at the very bottom, at the exact center of the convex gypsum floor, the Power makes its final, smallest adjustment. 

Right where the main heat-vent breaches the chalk lens, the Power parts the stone in a narrow, spiral flute, forty feet deep, shaped like the interior of a whelk shell. And around the lip of this central vent, It raises seven low, curved baffles of vitrified salt-stone, arranged like the petals of a closed lotus. 

The next morning, a pruning-crew of three Lath-Men is hanging from the tether-roots of the mid-level plazas, sawing away a choke of rattle-pod. 

Suddenly, all three freeze. Their yellow horn-shutters snap open against the glare. They drop their knives—letting them hang by their rawhide wrist-loops—and press their flat chests simultaneously against the parapet.

The roar of the Drum has not stopped. The million sheets of tin are still shaking; the stone pipes are still booming. 

Yet, cutting straight through the center of the white noise, rising from the gypsum floor below, is something that has never existed in the pit before. 

It is a *note*. 

The new spiral vent at the bottom of the world has caught the main thermal updraft and spun it into a tight, laminar vortex. Instead of blasting upward as a chaotic roar, the central column of air is now a single, invisible string of spinning wind, three hundred feet long, stretched taut from the gypsum floor to the blue sky above. 

And it is tuned. 

It hums at a frequency just below the shriek of the kelp and just above the baritone rumble of the walls—a clear, hollow, golden *C-sharp* that vibrates straight through the empty air and strikes the horn-dishes on the sides of the Lath-Men’s skulls without needing stone to carry it. For the first time in ten thousand years, they are hearing through the air.

More than that: the note is responsive. 

As the three Lath-Men hang breathless on the parapet, a heavy curtain of chime-kelp fifty yards to their left is severed by another crew. The local wind-pressure shifts by an ounce. The change in airflow spills down the wall, brushes the lotus-baffles on the floor below, and the great central vortex shifts its pitch—sliding smoothly from *C-sharp* up to a resonant, humming *E*, holding the chord across the entire city for ten seconds before settling back.

Across the three-hundred-foot void, on every tier of the white honeycomb, four hundred Lath-Men come out onto the ledges. They stand edge-on to the blinding sun, looking down into the cauldron of light. 

One of them—an old tuner on the fourth gallery whose mica-skin is flaking silver with age—reaches out and pulls a wooden wind-damper halfway across an empty grain-chute beside him. 

Down on the floor, the redirected draft hits the third lotus-baffle. The central column of air shivers, splits into a harmonic fifth, and sends a slow, rolling pulse of pure vibration up through every balcony, every fern-bladder, and every hollow breastbone in the Drum. 

On the opposite wall, three hundred feet away, another Lath-Man feels the pulse in his ribs. He recognizes the rhythm. It is the opening phrase of the song they drummed forehead-to-forehead in the dark of the third granary vault four nights ago. 

Slowly, with a hitching click of his flat elbow, the second Lath-Man reaches up, grabs a thirty-foot curtain of amber chime-kelp, and pulls it taut across an oval wind-throat, bending the note back across the void in answer.

The Power withdraws from the salt wastes, leaving no footprint on the blinding flats above. Down in the white furnace, the maintenance has become the music, and the four hundred tuners are playing the city at last.
