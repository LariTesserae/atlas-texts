The Attention did not arrive as a shaft of golden light, nor did it part the permanent fog outside the high ceiling slit. A power that is truly wise knows the violence of sudden illumination upon things that have evolved in the dark; it knows that to dry the damp air would be to suffocate the blind-moss, crack the mucilage, and starve the oil-backs. A lesser benevolence might have looked upon this frantic, clattering gloom and seen a prison, or a hell, and sought to "rescue" its inhabitants by shattering the roof. 

Instead, the Attention entered the hall as a listening. 

It settled into the vacant, mist-filled cylinder at the center of the room, taking up no space, displacing no air. It let the deafening, cobbler-shop roar of the clack-weavers wash through it. It felt the cold, tallow-colored grease of the light, and the wet, rhythmic *thwack* of the eels in the dark dish below. 

Most of all, it watched the Grout-Wife. 

It watched her for three of her long, slow days. It felt the immense, blind patience of her hydraulic creep. It understood the deep, somatic discomfort she felt when a tile fifty feet up lost its purchase and shattered on the floor—how that missing tooth in the masonry registered in her nervous system as a phantom ache, a sour note in the room's acoustic drone. And when she pressed the scarred whorl of her club-tentacle into a fresh patch of lime-slime and crab-shell, rocking it once to leave the ghost of a human thumbprint she had never seen, the Attention felt a profound and tender awe.

Here was a creature keeping a promise that had never been made to her, maintaining a sanctuary for builders who had turned to dust ten thousand years ago. She was holding up the sky with her own spittle and the bones of her neighbors. 

Yet, the Attention also saw the tragedy of the closed loop. 

The Grout-Wife could only reach the lower third of the corbelled walls. Above that, the curve grew too steep for her two-hundred-pound bulk; gravity would peel even her terrifying suction from the slick, sweating clay. For centuries, she had been reinforcing the foundations while the upper courses slowly rotted away. Up near the ceiling slit, the mortar was nearly gone, washed down the rills into the gurgling throat of the drain. Within a few more decades, a section of the upper vault would sheer off entirely. It would fall not as a single tile, but as a twenty-ton avalanche of black brick, crushing the Grout-Wife, smashing through the concave floor, and choking the ecosystem to death in dry, cold fog. 

She was losing the war against gravity, and somewhere in her dense, distributed web of nerves, she knew it. The pitch of the ceiling was all wrong. It hummed with a thin, brittle tension that made her cowl twitch continuously, an itch she could never climb high enough to scratch.

The wise power considered this. It would not rebuild the wall itself; to do so would be to render the Grout-Wife obsolete, to strip her of the very purpose around which her flesh and spirit had calcified. To take the work from the mason is no kindness.

Instead, the power decided to give her an apprentice, and a way to reach the sky.

It did not conjure anything from nothing. It worked entirely within the grammar of the hall, nudging probabilities, whispering to the mineral-heavy water, and coaxing the ancient clay.

First, it turned its mind to the central drain. 

Deep beneath the stone-plugged grate where the black silt and grey tendons washed away, the water flowed through a subterranean culvert lined with the raw, unfired clay from which the original tiles had been dug millennia ago. The Attention reached down into that lightless plumbing and shifted a single, wedged stone. 

The gurgling of the drain changed pitch—from a wet, choking cough to a low, resonant *hum*. The flow slowed just enough for the richest, heaviest sediment to back up into the shallow dish of the floor. Over the next several weeks, the central puddle grew thicker, enriched with a forgotten vein of mineral-dense, volcanic marl that the ancient masons had once used as their prime binder. 

When the oil-backs thrashed through this new silt, their skin absorbed the trace minerals. Their secretions changed. When they slithered up the lower walls to feed on the ropey blind-moss, the slime they left behind no longer merely calcified into brittle crusts; it cured into a micro-crystalline glaze that drank the dampness from the air and bound itself into the porous terracotta like iron roots.

Next, the Attention looked to the blind-moss itself. 

In the seams between the plates on the Grout-Wife’s own back, a few tufts of the pale, wet-rope plant had lived for years, fed by her rich dorsal mucilage. The Attention breathed a tiny, subtle mutation into the spores of this specific patch. 

As the Grout-Wife crept along her slow rounds, the moss on her back began to grow not just in frayed fringes, but in thick, braided cables, tough as hawser-rope. As she brushed against the walls, the spores transferred to the masonry. Fed by the new, mineral-rich slime of the oil-backs, the moss began to climb. It did not care about the steepness of the corbelling. It threaded its pale, blind roots deep into the washed-out mortar joints of the upper vault, forty, fifty, sixty feet up, weaving the loosening tiles together in a living, fibrous net. 

Where the moss grew thickest, it formed heavy, stepped ridges—horizontal ledges of woody, calcified root-mat that jutted out an inch or two from the sheer curve of the upper wall. 

The Grout-Wife felt the change through the sole of her foot before she ever reached it. 

The brittle, high-pitched whining of the upper vault—the structural tension that had tormented her nerves for a century—was dampening. The acoustic echo of the hall was deepening, warming, shifting from a frantic clatter toward a rich, bass-heavy thrum. 

One day—though day and night had no meaning here—her slow hydraulic creep brought her to a section of the northern wall where a fissure had long bled a steady stream of cold water from ten feet above her highest reach. Usually, she would pause beneath this spot, her heavy cowl lifting blindly toward the ceiling, her club-tentacle flexing in frustrated, rhythmic spasms at the sound of the loose tiles rattling in the high draft.

This time, her leading edge bumped against something new. 

A thick, braided bolster of moss-root had grown diagonally across the brickwork, cemented into place by the glaze of the oil-backs and the wedged shells of dead weavers. It formed a rough, living ramp, deeply textured and humming with structural integrity. 

The Grout-Wife paused for an hour, her crescent mouth rasping softly against the lower root, tasting the new, volcanic richness of the silt trapped in its fibers. When she spat, her own mucilage reacted with the marl, foaming slightly before setting into a cement harder and lighter than anything she had ever produced. 

Slowly, with a deep, vitrified *crunch* of her armored back, she shifted her weight. Her suctorial skirt gripped the braided moss-ledge. 

It held. 

Inch by slow inch, the two-hundred-pound mason began to climb into the upper dark, leaving the lower wall behind. The clack-weavers parted before her in a staccato wave of clicking legs, tumbling out of the way of this ascending continent of tile and muscle. The featherless flyers, dropping from their high roosts, banked sharply in the mist, startled by the presence of the floor’s great rock rising into their airspace.

It took her four days to reach the fifty-foot mark. Up here, the air was colder, the draft from the ceiling slit a steady, sighing wind against her eyeless cowl. The tiles here were perilously loose, tilting out from the curve like rotting teeth. 

She anchored herself to the great cables of moss, which took her weight without a tremor. She reached into her pouch, drew forth her heavy, clay-dense tentacle, and began to work. 

She packed the gaping seams with the new, self-curing cement. She wedged the fallen shells of the high-dwelling crabs into the voids. She pushed the slumping, ancient tiles back into their beds, seating them firmly against the curve. 

And then, sixty feet above the dark mirror of the floor, right beneath the tallow smear of the sky, she rocked the calloused pad of her tentacle against the wet mortar. 

When she pulled it back, the swirling thumb-smudge gleamed in the dim light, fresh and deep. 

Down in the hollow center of the room, the unseen Attention felt the vault lock into place. The acoustic loop closed, whole and resonant. The hall was no longer a ruin slowly digesting itself; it was a living body, and its mason had finally been given the scaffolding to keep it whole. 

Having done enough, the wise power left the hall to its wet, deafening peace, taking nothing with it but the memory of a thumbprint pressed into the dark.
