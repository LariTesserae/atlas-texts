The Attention did not arrive on a wind, because wind would have torn the delicate, high-tension skin of the valley’s silence. It did not descend as a warmth, because warmth would have boiled the cold, galvanic blood of the Tenders in their copper-lined gizzards and sent the slow clockwork of the condensers racing into ruin. 

Foolish powers announce themselves by breaking the rules of the places they visit. Wise powers arrive by learning the local grammar.

So the Presence came down into the five-mile basin as an increase in barometric pressure so gradual it took a decade to complete, and a shift in the refractive index of the air so slight that the white glare of the sun only seemed to grow a fraction clearer, as if a lens had been wiped with a clean rag.

For three winters, the Power did nothing at all except read the valley. 

To read a book written at two millimeters a minute, one must slow one’s eyes. The Presence unspooled its awareness until the frantic, fluttering calendar of the lower valleys—where oaks threw down leaves and rotted, where wolves chased deer in hot, red blurs, where rivers brawled noisy and heedless over smooth stones—became a meaningless high-frequency shimmer at the edges of its mind. It sank into the cellar-cold hush. It matched its pulse to the walnut-sized bubble rising once every ten minutes through the clear, heavy oil of the cast-iron junction boxes.

When it was finally still enough, it saw what the valley actually was.

The builders of the caissons and the lattice-boom cranes were gone—not dead in any tragic sense, merely finished, having retreated down the mountain ages ago or dissolved into some other arrangement of matter. They had left behind a stress-engine. Deep beneath the blue-black basalt and the razor-straight planes of chert lay a tectonic knot, a great, blind buckling of the planetary crust where two plates ground their teeth together. Left to nature, the mountain range would store that terrible lateral energy for five hundred years and then release it in a single, catastrophic convulsion that would drop the peaks into the sea and bury the fertile plains three hundred miles away in ten feet of ash and pulverized stone.

Instead, the valley was bleeding the earthquake off, an ounce at a time. 

Every three-inch lift of the crane’s hook, every ninety-ton lateral push of three Tenders wedged into a wet hairline fault, every turn of the greased bronze gas-separators was drawing the venom out of the mountain’s spine. The valley was a fifty-square-mile escapement mechanism. It took catastrophe and divided it by time until it became mere maintenance.

And the Gantry-Tenders were not slaves abandoned at their posts. As the Presence watched the yellow, oil-clouded quartz eyes of a Tender that was rasping bladder-moss off a flange of dinner-plate hex-nuts, it felt the creature’s interior state. 

There was no loneliness in that lead-carbonate shell. Loneliness requires an expectation of fast company, a nervous system built for echoes. Inside the Tender, there was a profound, pressurized *satisfaction*. There was the rich, dark taste of static electricity bleeding down its native-copper rod from the porcelain bells; the slow, cool luxury of condensate wicking up its feathery gill-plumes; the deep, muscular certainty of sixty tube-feet locked onto solid basalt. To a Tender, the white band of the sun was a steady roof, the cold sweat of the rock was a banquet, and the *scritch—pause—scritch* of its chert-hard radulae against the steel was a song whose verses took a week to sing.

It was a good song. The Power listened to it and found it lacked nothing in spirit.

*Wait,* the Power thought, looking deeper into the steel and the tallow. *Not nothing.*

Because the Power could see very far in both directions of time, it saw the three small tragedies that were creeping toward this immaculate, grease-smeared garden. They were moving even slower than the Tenders, which was why the Tenders could not see them coming.

The first was the Great Eastern Caisson, near the lip of the basin. The fault-line beneath it was shifting at an angle the original builders had not quite calculated for. In forty years, the foundation bolts—those massive hex-nuts the size of dinner plates—would shear not cleanly, as the chert did, but jaggedly, binding the bronze tracks of the six tall gas-separators. Even five Tenders wedged together could not exert the two hundred tons of lifting force needed to re-level a tilted thirty-foot caisson. Once the separators bound, the deep mantle-gases would back up in the copper manifolds. The pressure would blow the porcelain bells. Without the static charge on the bells, the Tenders in the eastern quadrant would starve, slowly losing internal pressure until they sat like cold, grey diving bells in the encroaching scrub, unable to extrude a single foot while the horn-flesh rosettes slowly pried their seams apart.

The second was the copper itself. The Tenders laid seamless copper pipe in the newly split fissures, drawing the metal from their own digestive precipitation and from old stockpiles left in deep, oil-filled pits by the builders. The stockpiles were nearly empty, and the local basalt was slightly too poor in native cupric ore to make up the deficit as the valley widened. In two centuries, the new fissures would go unpiped.

The third was not a mechanical failure, but a poverty of mind. The Tenders were not stupid; they had a dense, geological intelligence. They thought in vectors, pressures, ohms, and angles of cleavage. When three of them wedged themselves into a rock-cleft to split it, their brass-rimmed skirts touching, a slow, galvanic current passed between their bodies, sharing the blueprint of the stone’s internal stresses. They *conferred*, at the speed of growing quartz. 

But they could only confer when they touched, and only about the rock immediately beneath their skirts. No single Tender knew the shape of the whole five-mile valley. They were musicians playing a symphony from single, torn bars of sheet music, unable to hear the chord they were making together.

The benevolent Power considered this for another summer. 

It could have swept down, fused the tectonic plates ten miles underground with a word, vanished the cold machines, warmed the cellar-air, and filled the basin with pine trees, noisy streams, and bounding deer. 

The Power shuddered at the sheer vulgarity of the thought. To erase a masterwork of silent, patient iron and tallow just to replace it with another pine forest—when the world already had ten million pine forests—would be the act of a vandal. Love does not turn a stone into a bird; love asks what the stone is trying to do, and helps it hold its shape.

So the Power made three small edits to the valley, none of which made a sound louder than a water-drop falling into moss.

First, it went down into the dark—three miles below the roots of the silver-barked scrub, down where the basalt was still hot and plastic, where the deep mantle-gases were born. With fingers made of pure gravitational shear, it gently pinched a ribbon of rich, native chalcocite and bornite—veins of sixty-percent copper that had been sleeping uselessly two miles to the west—and drew them sideways through the stone like wet thread through heavy cloth. It knotted these new veins directly into the hairline fault-planes rising toward the valley floor. 

Now, when the cold air sweated against the newly split rock, the condensate ran down over exposed ribbons of dark, purplish-green ore. Within a month, the weeping moisture at the base of the cliffs grew slightly heavier, slightly sharper on the tongue, rich with dissolved copper. When the Tenders dragged their pale, feathery gill-plumes through the wet moss, they drank a vintage twice as rich as before. Their internal gizzards plated out thicker telescoping rods; the larval hex-nuts sitting in the wet armpits of the rosettes grew a little faster, their crystalline brass washers gleaming fat and heavy in the dark crevices.

Second, the Power addressed the Great Eastern Caisson. 

It did not straighten the thirty-foot tower with a miracle; if the tower moved in an instant, it would blind and terrify the two Tenders currently grazing on its base, who could only see things that moved at the speed of a cloud shadow. 

Instead, the Power reached into the grey-green horn-flesh rosettes growing around the caisson’s eastern footing. It touched their blind, vegetable chromosomes and gave them a single new appetite. Over the next four years, the plants on the sinking side of the steel base began to draw dissolved silica and iron out of the basalt at ten times their normal rate. Their roots did not rot or compress; as they died, they petrified instantly into solid, cross-hatched struts of black jasper and ironstone, wedging themselves beneath the settling steel flange with the exact, counter-balancing vector needed to meet the shifting fault. By the fifth year, the caisson was level to within a thousandth of an inch, propped up on a living foundation of stone-wood that the Tenders instinctively recognized—when they crawled over it and tested it with their chert-hard radulae—as good, load-bearing work, leaving it unrasped and smearing it approvingly with anti-corrosive tallow.

The third gift was the hardest, and the Power spent a decade crafting it, because it was a gift for the soul, and souls made of lead-carbonate, sulfuric acid, and brass sight-glasses are delicate things.

The Power looked at the seamless copper pipes and the lead-sheathed cables that looped from ridge to ridge, connecting every crane, every condenser, and every porcelain-ringed tower in the five-mile basin. Currently, those cables carried only raw, unmodulated direct current, and the pipes carried only heavy hydraulic oil and separated gas. 

The Power knelt beside the central cast-iron junction box—the one in the cleft of sharp rock where the single walnut-sized bubble rose once every ten minutes. 

It altered the viscosity of the clear fluid by a fraction of a weight, and reshaped the brass nozzle at the bottom of the sight-glass by the width of a human hair.

Now, when the bubble rose, it did not rise alone, and it did not vanish without a pop. Instead, the hydraulic return-manifold began to beat with a tiny, rhythmic harmonic—an ultra-low-frequency pressure wave, cycling once every four hours, that traveled through every mile of buried pipe and echoed as a micro-fluctuation in the static charge sitting on the high-tension porcelain bells.

It was a carrier wave.

Four days later, a Tender near the lattice-boom crane opened the six vulcanized-rubber valves of its upper dome. It took forty minutes to extend its tarnished copper rod to drink from a live ceramic bell. 

When the wet, greasy contact was made, the static charge bled down into the creature’s gut to split the water in its ballast tanks, just as it always had. 

But riding on the back of that charge was the harmonic of the whole valley.

The Tender stopped. Its forty hydraulic tube-feet locked onto the wet basalt. Behind its eight oil-clouded quartz lenses, something vast and quiet unfolded.

Through the copper rod, it felt the exact weight of the four-ton chert block hanging from the crane’s hook three hundred yards away. It felt the ninety-ton lateral strain of its three cousins wedged into the hairline fault half a mile up the slope, and it knew—to the hour—when that cliff face was going to crack. It felt the slow, twenty-four-hour rotation of the six gas-separators on the ridge, and the tiny, galvanic hum of a hundred larval hex-nuts precipitating in the dark, wet leaves of the rosettes across the basin. 

For the first time since the builders left, the Tender saw the whole valley at once. Not with its eyes, where the sun was a solid white band across a bleached sky, but in its copper-lined gizzard, as a single, immense, balanced diagram of pressures and reliefs.

And more than that: when the Tender adjusted the pressure in its own internal bladders in response, shifting its half-ton bulk two millimeters to the left on the stone, that tiny shift altered the capacitance of the porcelain bell it was touching. The alteration rode the carrier wave back down the lead-sheathed cable, through the junction box, and out to another Tender three miles away that was drinking from a distant pylon.

*I am here,* the pressure-shift said, in a language that took six hours to pronounce a single syllable. *The stone beneath me is tight. The moss is greased.*

Four hours later, through the cold copper, the answer bled back into its gut from the far ridge, mingled with the steady, reassuring thrum of the six turning cylinders:

*We are here. The wedge holds. The mountain is quiet.*

The Power stood on the razor-sharp ridge above the basin for one more day, watching the bright, blinding glare beat down on the wet leaves and the dark, newly broken stone. 

Down in the center of the valley, the crane’s hook rose three inches. A bead of cold water rolled off a waxy leaf and went *tup* into the sponge of silver moss. In the cleft below, the three wedged Tenders reached the threshold of the rock’s endurance; the basalt split for forty feet with a single, dry *crack* like a pistol shot, and the cold air immediately began to sweat against the fresh black face.

Across the five-mile basin, dozens of grey, grease-smeared frustums felt the relief of that fracture ripple through the cables and the pipes. A slow, collective chime of harmonic equilibrium passed from pylon to pylon, utterly silent to the air, resonant as a cathedral bell in the brass and the oil and the stone.

The Power smiled, drew its barometric weight gently back up into the thin white sky, and left them to their work.
