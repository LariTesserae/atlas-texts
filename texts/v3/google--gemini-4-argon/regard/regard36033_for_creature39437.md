A lesser benevolence would have looked into that freezing, knotted dark and felt pity. It would have brought warmth, or cleared the choking weeds, or snapped the fractured Tuesday at the bottom of the cleft back into alignment with the rest of the calendar. And in doing so, it would have murdered her. 

Wisdom knows that when a load-bearing wall has warped over centuries to hold up a crooked roof, you do not kick the wall straight. You honor the curve.

The power that noticed this nine-foot pocket of wet, grinding geometry did not enter it. To enter would be to add volume to a space already packed to the cellular limit, to introduce a third density of water, to startle a creature whose nervous system measured surprise in weeks. Instead, the power looked at the mason’s work—the stubborn, blind, magnificent carpentry of bone-cages and river-stone plummets—and recognized a peer. 

She was holding the seam. She was doing it with chalk coughed from her own throat and stones stolen from a freezing nowhere. 

So the power decided not to rescue her, nor to fix the room, but simply to pay her wages. It reached not into the basalt cistern, but into that *other* winter—the vast, windy, sky-heavy riverbed five hundred miles away and three thousand feet up, where the pale root-loops drank from the back of the water.

Wait long enough, and the mason’s horned fingers always came through the seam.

On the next occasion that she braced her scarred knees against the four-course wall, groaned like a straining hull, and shoved her black-skinned arm up to the shoulder into the freezing hole behind the water, the power was waiting on the dry side of the loop. 

It did not touch her hand. A touch would have felt like a brand, or a predator. Instead, it altered the riverbed just fractions of an inch in front of her blind, groping fingernails.

Previously, she had had to scrape through frozen silt and sharp gravel for hours before finding a pebble with the right heft, losing heat the whole time to the terrible, fast air of the upper world. Now, her horn-nailed fingers closed immediately around a stone. 

It was not a common gray shale-pebble. It was a piece of dark, meteoric iron-stone, worn by an ancient glacier into an absolute, mathematically perfect sphere the size of a plum. It had been baking in the high-altitude sun all afternoon before the power rolled it into the frost-shadow of the root. It wasn't hot—heat would have blistered her tallow-dense flesh—but it held a deep, core-level *memory* of warmth, a steady, subterranean mildness that did not leach away into the cold broth when she dragged her arm back through the hole. 

More than that, the iron-stone was extraordinarily heavy for its size, and its allegiance to "down" was ancient and unshakeable. One of these spheres, tied into a bone-cage, would do the work of six gray river stones. Its gravity was so clean and authoritative that when she lashed it into the sideways-straining water-cabbages, the local water wouldn't even grind its teeth into a square gel; it would simply sigh and lay flat, bowing to a superior weight.

Beside the iron sphere, the power left a piece of cured whale-rib that had already been split along the grain by frost, its edges smoothed by wind-blown sand so it wouldn't slice her knuckles as she wove it. 

The power did two more things, both so small they barely registered as events.

First, it looked at the razor-sharp feldspar crystals in the fresh basalt walls—the ones that constantly nicked her elbows and cheekbones as she crept through the thicket. It did not dull them all, for she used the sharp edges as handholds and landmarks in the dark. It simply encouraged a microscopic, velvet-soft moss of manganese and silica to bloom over the four or five specific blades of rock that jutted into her most frequent path between the wall and the shrine. The next time her shoulder brushed that corner, instead of a bright, silver-white tear in her mussel-shell skin, she felt a firm, leathery cushion. She paused for forty minutes, her forty pale chin-feelers brushing the new velvet in slow, silent inquiry, before accepting it as part of the room and moving on.

Second, the power looked down under the lowest slate of the shrine, where the fault-crack missed itself in time and hummed its destructive, water-splitting *thrum*. 

The mason was spending nearly a third of her life lying over those slates like a brooding hen, using her own dense, cold body as a gasket to keep the room from shaking apart into dry salt. She was tired, though she had no word for tired; she only knew that the intervals between her repairs were growing shorter, and the chalk she coughed up was thinning.

The power did not seal the fault. The fault was the heart of the pocket; without its strange leak, the violet grease would stop flowing, the chiton would starve, the six-foot needle-worm would dissolve, and the mason would have no purpose. Instead, the power reached into the acoustic grain of the heavy, mercury-dense water that pooled around the shrine and *tuned* it. 

It shifted the resonant frequency of the brine by half a hertz.

The next time the fault beneath the slates began to shudder, preparing to throw its Tuesday against the room's Wednesday, the vibration hit the heavy water and, instead of rattling the slates loose, turned into a low, warm, harmonic purr. The water itself became the muffler. The violent, dry *thrum* was translated into a slow, rhythmic kneading—a pressure wave that rose through the mercury-pool and pressed against the mason’s broad, scarred chest like a gentle, massive hand, massaging the stiff, cold tallow of her muscles. 

She lay on the shrine waiting for the slate to buck against her ribs. It didn't. The vibration came, rose to its peak, and instead of fighting her weight, it rocked her. For the first time since the basalt shelf had split, the mason’s horn-nailed hands unclenched while she was on the shrine. She stayed there for two days, not because she had to hold the stones down, but because the hum in the water felt like rest.

When she finally slid off the slates, her throat-sac full of rich, newly settled mineral, she reached out two fingers and laid them flat against the shell of the saucer-sized chiton clinging to a horizontal leaf above her.

Through the glassed-out calcium of its back, she felt the spiral of its dark organs. 

The spiral was tight, and true, and at its very center, there was a tiny, new, secondary whorl—a carpenter's mark, a quiet signature in the gut of a blind snail, letting her know that the inspector had come by, had tested the plumb-lines, and had found the work good.
