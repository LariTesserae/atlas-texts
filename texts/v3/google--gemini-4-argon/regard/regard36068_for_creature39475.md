The attention does not arrive with a flash of light or a displacement of water. To disturb the slow, clockwise gyre of the sphere—even by a fraction of a knot—would be a clumsiness, and the power that pauses beside the atmospheric spire is not clumsy. 

It arrives as a sheer, weightless literacy. It reads the spire the way a master horologist reads a watch through a glass case. 

In a single, silent sweep of awareness, it comprehends the forty-foot brass bell, the stratospheric frost outside, the blind silver shrimp, and the heavy, velvety monarch wrapped around the central shaft. It sees the creature not merely as an animal, but as a small, unintentional Atlas: without its magnetite tongue scraping the limestone scale from the vents, the grates would have choked two centuries ago; without its copper flush warning the oil-eels, the predators would be boiled in the surge; without its cloud of larval silt, the moss would starve. The machine keeps the creature warm, and the creature keeps the machine breathing. It is a closed, twelve-minute poem written in rust, steam, and copper blood.

A lesser benevolence might feel pity for the monarch’s solitude. It might think of the vanished oceans miles below and long ago, and wish to break the sphere open, or spirit the beast away to some wider, safer sea, or drop a mate into the tank. 

Wisdom stays that hand immediately. A second monarch would halve the food, double the waste, and turn a tranquil garden into a slaughterhouse of forty-foot dimensions. A wider sea would have neither the scalding vertical geyser that flushes its gills nor the twelve-minute rhythm that has become the very shape of its soul. The creature is not lonely; it does not have the neurological architecture for loneliness. Its W-shaped eyes are full of light, its belly is full of silver shrimp, and its nerves are full of the deep, rich music of the pipes. It is content.

Yet, as the wise power listens to the *thrum-clack, thrum-clack* resonating up from the miles of drop-piping below, it hears what the mollusk cannot interpret.

Three miles down, in the freezing dark of the spire’s middle lattice, the main counterweight cable—braided from high-tensile molybdenum steel four thousand years ago—is down to its last nineteen intact strands. Every twelve minutes, when the weight drops, a microscopic shower of crystallized metal flakes off into the abyss. In four years, seven months, and eleven days, the cable will snap. The secondary sluice-gate will lock shut forever. Within six hours of that silence, the stratospheric cold will bite through the bronze hull. The bath will become an ice-block. The poem will end in the dark.

Furthermore, right here in the sphere, the central turbine shaft—the creature’s bone-like perch—is thinning. The iron is nearly gone, leached out by millennia of hot water and the creature’s own rasping radula. And the valve-crabs are running out of houses; three of the antique brass hex-nuts have finally corroded through their threads, splitting in half and leaving the soft-bodied crabs to be eaten by the eels.

The power decides. It will not break the circle. It will not introduce itself. Godhood, to a creature of pressure and warmth, should only ever feel like the universe working as it ought to.

The intervention happens three miles down, and it happens in the space between one *thrum-clack* and the next. 

In the dark of the lower spire, the frayed molybdenum cable does not magically become new—that would alter its elasticity, changing the pitch of the vibration the creature loves. Instead, the rust and metal-fatigue sitting in the surrounding structural beams are gently coaxed to migrate. Over the course of three cycles, iron and carbon atoms flow like slow honey through the cold steel, weaving themselves into the frayed cable just behind the wear-point, matching the exact tension, weight, and acoustic resonance of the ancient braid. The counterweight’s guides are lined with a self-replenishing film of dense, graphitic carbon. The twelve-minute heartbeat is quietly underwritten for another ten thousand years.

Then, the presence turns its attention to the warm sphere above. It waits for the ten-second warning.

The monarch feels the slackening in the deep cable. Its skin flares bright, new-penny copper. The oil-eels scatter into the shaft hollows. The creature glides to the center, locks its six stubby arms into a cone, and waits.

*Boom.*

The sluice-gate opens, and the scalding geyser roars upward. 

Riding the thermal rocket into the amber snowstorm of rust and scale, the creature uncoils its feeding whips, lost in the forty-second ecstasy of heat and oxygen. Because the roar is deafening and the water is a blinding haze of swirling sediment, it does not notice that the secondary sluice-gate at the bottom of the floor has vomited up something more than water this time.

Up from the dark throat of the pipe, carried by the violent updraft, comes a small, tumbling handful of debris—things loosened, it would seem, from some long-sealed maintenance locker deeper in the spire that finally gave way to the water.

When the counterweight resets with its heavy *thud*, and the monarch spreads its skirt to parachute gently down through the clearing water, the new treasures are already resting on the curved bronze floor, gleaming in the golden nets of daylight.

There are nine heavy, high-grade naval-brass hex-nuts of varying sizes, uncorroded and sharp-threaded. There are four hollowed-out bronze union-fittings, ideal for the largest of the crabs. 

And wedged right at the base of the central turbine shaft is a solid, twelve-pound ingot of sacrificial smelter’s pig-iron, rich with trace copper and magnesium, rough-cast and deliciously porous. Over the coming centuries, as the hot gyre licks at the ingot, it will slowly dissolve, plating its minerals straight onto the bone-like turbine shaft via natural electrolysis, healing the iron column as fast as the water can wear it down.

The great mollusk settles onto its trembling perch, fading from bright copper back to the mottled yellow-green of the grease-moss. It breathes. It digests its shrimp. 

After a few minutes of quiet *thrum-clack*, its golden, W-pupiled eye catches a new glint on the floor below. 

Slowly, it crawls down the shaft. It reaches out with one thick, velvety arm and picks up one of the new brass hex-nuts. 

The metal is heavy, smooth, and entirely free of algae. The creature holds the nut up into a shaft of stratospheric sunlight, turning it over and over. It scrapes the flat of the brass once with its magnetite tongue—*scritch*—tasting the clean, bright alloy. The ring of the metal is slightly higher, slightly clearer than the old ones. A small valve-crab, currently squeezed into a split and crumbling pressure-gauge casing nearby, waves its antennae warily at the giant.

The creature lowers its arm, sets the new brass nut down next to the crab, and taps it once with the tip of a tentacle. *Clink.*

Then it climbs back up to the shadow beneath the catwalk, wraps its broad foot around the humming iron shaft, and closes its heavy lids halfway. 

Outside, the thin blue air is freezing. Inside, the water is blood-warm, the new brass is waiting to be colonized, and miles below, the great cable holds firm. The benevolent power lingers for one more cycle, just to watch the creature turn into a copper parachute and ride the scalding fountain toward the sun, and then departs, leaving the bell to ring on in the sky.
