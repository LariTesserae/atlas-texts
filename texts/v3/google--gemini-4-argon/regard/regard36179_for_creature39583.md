A foolish god, or a young one, would look upon this wet, lightless pit and feel pity. 

Such a power would reach down with a warm hand to part the pewter clouds, letting a golden shaft of sunlight strike the Sump-Wards. It would dry the weeping basalt, sweeten the metallic water into clear spring-brooks, and cause soft green turf and fruiting bushes to erupt from the crevices. 

And in doing so, that god would murder them. 

Without the mineral grease, the cured driftwood and whalebone lashings would dry, shrink, and splinter, collapsing the twenty-tiered labyrinth in a single afternoon. Without the relentless, calcifying drip, the ancestors wedged into the perimeter fissures would rot in the sudden warmth rather than turning to stone, fouling the air and sickening the living. Without the need for the *knack* and the constant, meditative scraping of the chutes, the silent, tightly wound grammar of their lives would unravel. They would be left blinking, raw-skinned, and purposeless in the blinding sun, their specialized bodies suddenly grotesque and clumsy.

The power that actually turns its attention to the basalt blister is neither young nor foolish. It is old enough to understand that a callus is not a curse; it is an agreement between flesh and stone. It sees the sinkhole not as a wound where people are trapped, but as a single, miraculous organ—a heart made of cedar, slate, and quiet, greased bodies, pumping water and warmth through a hostile earth.

The power watches for three seasons. It listens to the million discrete hydraulic ticks. It watches the children—little pinecones of oiled leather—patiently harvesting the blind, chalk-tasting amphipods. It feels the bone-mallet vibrations of the Tallow-Wives traveling up the timbers like nerve impulses. It sees that they do not pray, because prayer requires a concept of *elsewhere*, and for these people, the universe is sixty feet wide and smells of wet flint. 

Yet, the wise power also sees the slow, invisible teeth of time that even the elders in the Sump-Wards cannot hear. It sees three things that will eventually break the machine.

First: the driftwood is tired. The great, load-bearing cedar trunks that form the primary aqueducts and spine-timbers were hauled from a distant coast generations ago. Though smeared with mineral fat, the wood at the very bottom is slowly losing its fibrous tension, compressed by the immense weight of the rookeries above. 

Second: the diet of chalk-shrimp, traded barley, and dark peat-mutton lacks a tiny, vital spark. In the sunless damp, the children’s bones sometimes grow slightly too soft before the lime-water can harden their skin, curving their spines a fraction too much, making the climb to the windy rim an agony by their thirtieth year.

Third: the elders in the Sump-Wards are going blind far too early. The mineral pitch they burn in their scores of tiny clay lamps gives off a heavy, acrid soot. In the unventilated bottom of the funnel, this smoke scores their wide, dark eyes and coats the bottoms of their lungs with a black grime that even their oily tear-film cannot wash away.

The benevolent power decides to act. It will not break the skin of their world. It will not introduce itself. Instead, it will speak to them in the only language they trust: the slow, creeping chemistry of the deep earth.

Far below the sinkhole, miles beneath the Sump-Wards where the groundwater passes through the dying embers of the old volcanic plumbing, the power makes a series of microscopic adjustments. 

First, it nudges a deep, subterranean vein of iron-rich serpentine rock, cracking it just enough to let the thermal waters wash through it before rising toward the pit. It alters the alchemy of the heavy, tallow-like mineral pitch. Over the course of a winter, the grease bubbling up into the lowest sump changes almost imperceptibly. Its grey color deepens to the hue of a bruised plum, and it takes on a faint, resinous scent, like buried pine needles. When the skimmers pack this new fat into their clay pots and touch a splinter of fire to it, the elders sitting on their whale-vertebrae notice something remarkable. The lamps no longer gutter with greasy black smoke. The flame burns shorter, hotter, and with a clear, steady, amber light that leaves the air entirely clean. The acrid bite vanishes from the Sump-Wards, replaced by a dry, gentle heat that eases the calcified joints of the old ones and leaves their eyes clear and sharp for another twenty years.

Second, the power addresses the failing wood. Into the cold groundwater seeping from the upper moorland, the power dissolves a trace of amorphous silica and a rare, dormant spore that has slept in the peat since the last ice age. 

Up in the middle tiers, the change is noticed only as a slight shift in the texture of the work. Where the mineral water sheets over the ancient, load-bearing driftwood timbers, it no longer merely threatens to rot them or coat them in brittle external limestone. Instead, the silica-rich water is drawn *into* the wood's thirsty, compressed cells. Instead of rotting, the great cedar trunks begin to very slowly petrify from the inside out—turning not into brittle chalk, but into tough, fibrous agate. Over decades, the main structural spine of the settlement becomes indestructible, a skeleton of wood-turned-stone that still holds the warmth of timber and receives the *knack* of a bone mallet with a clearer, sweeter ring than before.

Meanwhile, the awakened spore takes root—not in the chutes where it would block the water, for the power has designed it to hate fast currents. It grows only in the dead-air pockets under the eaves of the sleeping lofts, feeding on the rising warmth of human breath and the damp slate. It forms small, velvety cushions of a deep, bioluminescent ochre moss. 

At first, the pit-dwellers scrape it away, suspicious of anything new. But a child, curious, licks a chalky thumb after touching it and finds it tastes intensely savory—like roasted marrow and salt. They stop scraping it from the undersides of the roofs. When boiled in a clay pot with the pale amphipods, a pinch of this ochre velvet dissolves into a rich, golden broth. It is packed with the precise vitamins and trace minerals that the sunless sky denies them. Within a generation, the subtle rickets that bowed the legs of the young rim-workers vanishes. They grow no taller—the alleys are still narrow—but their bones become as dense and resilient as the whalebone scaffolds they climb. 

Furthermore, the moss glows. It is not bright enough to ruin their night vision or mimic the alien sun, just a faint, warm, ember-like luminescence under the eaves that outlines the edges of the notched ladders and the sharp basalt columns, sparing the clumsy young ones on the upper tiers a thousand small cuts and bruises.

The power does one final thing, a gift purely for the soul of the place, which lives in its ears. 

Deep in the jagged perimeter crevices, where the dead are wedged upright and sealed in creeping stone, the power guides the crystal structure of the forming travertine. Instead of drying into a porous, sound-deadening crust, the lime-water sealing the ancestors begins to lay down in dense, microscopic, hexagonal plates, mimicking the basalt around it. 

The effect takes ten years to mature. When it does, the elders at the bottom of the pit are the first to notice. 

The deadened acoustics of the sinkhole—the millions of discrete, isolated *tinks* and *splats*—begin to gather a subtle, unifying resonance. The calcified ancestors in the walls have become acoustic lenses. Now, when the wind shears across the moorland rim, or when a thousand tiny droplets hit the bronze puddles in the Sump-Wards, the perimeter walls do not swallow the sound. They hum. 

It is a chord so low it is felt in the breastbone rather than heard—a continuous, warm, cello-like drone that rises and falls with the barometric pressure of the sky above. When a boy on the highest, coldest tier taps his chisel to say *coming down*, the stone ancestors catch the dry click and wrap it in a soft, resonant warmth before delivering it to the bottom of the pit. The town no longer sounds like a collection of wet parts; it sounds like a single, breathing chest.

The power withdraws its hand, satisfied. 

Up on the rim, a long-legged marsh bird lands on the highest drying rack. It tilts its head, listening to the deep, thrumming, amber-lit warmth rising from the grey pit below. Down in the Sump-Wards, a Tallow-Wife closes her clear, unclouded eyes, rests her ivory-callused hand against a timber that is slowly turning to eternal agate, and listens to the ancestors hum. Nothing has been broken. Everything is held.
