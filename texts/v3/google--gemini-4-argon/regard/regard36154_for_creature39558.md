When the Power found the basin, it did not descend through the ragged slot in the ceiling. To drop from the sky, or to manifest as a light in that pewter gloom, would have been a vulgarity—like shouting in a room where someone has spent ten thousand years listening to a whisper. Even a draft of air would have been an insult to the maidenhair spleenwort hanging motionless from the Keeper’s collarbone.

Instead, the Power arrived outside, miles away and deep beneath the hills, where the rainwater first began its blind, century-long push through the dark limestone strata. It made itself small, quiet, and heavy, and it looked into the amphitheater through the medium of the stone itself. 

A lesser benevolence might have felt pity. A hasty god, accustomed to the brief, hot lives of surface creatures, might have seen a prisoner fused to a shelf of rock and thought to set it free. It might have cracked the limestone walls to let the gold of the afternoon pour in, or sent a sweet, warm rain to wash the porridge-colored crust from the Keeper’s eyes, or filled the sterile pools with silver fish and water-striders. 

Fortunately, this Power was old enough to know that not all silence is an absence, and not all stillness is a trap. 

It lingered in the bedrock for three seasons, simply watching and listening at the Keeper’s own tempo. It felt the slow, structural hum of the figure’s pulse—that steady, arch-like tension holding up the weight of the hours. It followed the microscopic migrations of lime through the Keeper's spine. And it listened to the music: the long, sweeping arpeggios of the groundwater that took decades to resolve a single chord, and the devotion of the one who sat on the ledge, keeping the instrument in tune against the slow creep of geological entropy.

The Power saw that the Keeper was not waiting to be rescued. The Keeper was *busy*. 

Yet, looking with the deep sight that sees both the beginning of a mountain and the dust it will become, the Power noticed two things that the Keeper, enclosed in its bowl of stone, could not know.

The first was twenty feet below the compacted silt of the floor. There, in the dark, a blind seam of shale was slowly decaying under the weight of the hill above it. In four hundred years—perhaps five—the stress would reach a threshold. There would be no great collapse, only a sudden, millimeter-wide shear running diagonally across the bedrock of the floor. It would be enough. The middle basin would crack. Its water would drain away into the dark, and the central voice of the room’s three-part harmony would go mute forever.

The second was thirty feet above the ragged slot in the ceiling, out in the sun-baked scrubland of the surface. A mile away, a new watercourse—a seasonal creek born of shifting weather patterns—had begun to cut its way through the topsoil during the spring thaws. In six centuries, its wandering bed would find the upper lip of the amphitheater. A single afternoon of brown, turbulent floodwater would pour through the slot, drowning the spleenwort, burying the knee-high walls in mud, and shattering the acoustics of the dome into a roaring, muddy cataract.

The Keeper was maintaining the instrument from within, thumbing down the travertine a millimeter a decade. The Power decided to maintain the conservatory from without.

It worked at the speed of the place, so that its benevolence would not feel like an intrusion, not even to the stone.

Down in the dark beneath the floor, where the shale was weakening, the Power drew the deep, mineral-heavy waters together. It coaxed a warmer, richer vein of dissolved calcite to flow along the fault line, persuading it to precipitate not on the ceiling of the cave, but deep in the microscopic fractures of the rotting stone. Over the course of forty years, the weak seam did not shear; it mineralized. It turned to a ribbon of solid, creamy agate, harder than the limestone around it, an invisible keel beneath the three pools that would hold their basins level and watertight for another hundred thousand years.

Up on the surface, where the sun beat down and the wind blew, the Power nudged a single acorn from the jaw of a passing jay. The oak grew on the exact rise of ground where the seasonal creek was chewing toward the gorge. As the tree’s taproot went down, and its lateral roots spread like iron cables through the dry earth, they bound the soil and turned the water aside, sending the spring floods gently away down the opposite slope of the hill. The slot in the ceiling remained high, dry, and secret.

That was the work of protection. It was necessary, and it was good. 

But the Power lingered a little longer. For even the wisest beings, when they encounter a fellow artist working alone in the dark, feel the urge to leave a token of their esteem—a sign, however small, that the music has been heard.

It could not be a new object on the floor; that would ruin the geometry. It could not be a change in the light; the pewter twilight was already complete. 

Instead, the Power reached into the porous limestone behind the west wall, along the path of a water-vein that had been dry since before the clay jar was fired. With infinite delicacy, it dissolved a plug of ancient silt no larger than a grain of rice, opening a capillary tube that wound through forty feet of solid rock and terminated at the tip of a dry flute high in the western curve of the ceiling.

It took seven years for the first moisture to creep down that hair-thin tunnel. 

The Power did not stay to hear it fall; it had other worlds, loud and quiet, to tend. It withdrew from the hill as silently as it had come, leaving the stone to its own slow business.

Seven years after the Power departed, on a day that was neither winter nor summer in the warm, grey bowl of the amphitheater, the dry flute on the west wall darkened at its tip. A bead of water gathered there, swelling through the long hours until its weight overcame its grip.

It fell sixty feet through the dead air. It did not strike the floor, and it did not strike the three pools. It fell onto the slightly concave surface of a fused limestone slab in one of the knee-high walls—a stone that had been dry for two millennia.

*Tink.*

It was a fourth note—a fifth above the central pool, clear and dry and crystalline, ringing with a slight, bell-like overtone because the Power had infused that particular vein of water with a trace of dissolved silver from deep in the earth's crust.

Across the floor, at the top of the three carved steps, the silence held for ten minutes. Fifteen. 

Then, with a dry, chalky *crack*, a puff of white dust drifted from the Keeper's shoulder. Over the next half-hour, the heavy, calcified head inclined two inches to the right—toward the west wall, toward a direction it had had no reason to face in sixty generations. 

A slow tear of mineral water welled up beneath its sealed eyelids, turning the crust clear, revealing the dark, algae-green depths beneath. The Keeper did not stand. There was nothing to fix, nothing to grind down or quiet with a pebble. The new note was not an error in the stone. It fell only once every eleven hours, weaving through the ancient three-part rhythm of the pools to create a harmony so vast and complex it would take a century to repeat itself.

The Keeper sat on its shelf in the neutral warmth, the three leaves of the fern motionless at its throat, and listened to the expanded world.
