The attention that settles over the caldera does not arrive as a parting of the violet sky, nor as a voice booming across the travertine pools. A voice would be useless here, shredded by the thin air, and a spectacle in the heavens would only blind eyes built for golden gloom. 

Instead, the Presence arrives as an understanding—a vast, quiet mind that slips into the sixty-hertz hum like a hand slipping into warm water.

For a long time—days, or seasons; time in the basin is measured only by the slow creep of moss and the shedding of frog-skin—the Benevolence simply observes. It traces the braided copper-nickel veins down the mountain. 

Miles below, in the dry, freezing foothills where the cables emerge from the basalt, the civilization that built this grid is long gone. Their cities are wind-scoured concrete husks. The massive substation that once drank the caldera’s megawatts was crushed by a rockslide three centuries ago. By all the laws of human engineering, the system should have tripped its breakers, burned out its transformers, and gone cold before the first generation of the basin-dwellers had even lost their hair. 

Instead, the rockslide had fused the primary busbars into a deep, mineral-rich vein of native silver and iron ore running through the mountain's flank. For three hundred years, the valley’s harvesters have been pumping megawatts of raw current straight into the planetary crust, turning the mountain itself into a colossal, warm grounding rod.

The Wise Power looks at this, and feels a profound, smiling affection. The loop is closed. The industry serves no empire; it serves only the moss, the translucent frogs, the copper-backed beetles, and the four hundred quiet souls who keep the iron breathing. 

To uplifting them would be a violence. To give them words would be to fracture the seamless, vibrating web of their shared nervous system. To pull them from the marsh and place them in a garden would be to suffocate them in thick air and starve them of the hum that is their mother, their hearth, and their god. 

Yet, the Benevolence sees what the creatures cannot. 

Deep within the cast-iron drums, the solid-state bismuth-telluride couples are aging. After centuries of relentless thermal stress, microscopic fractures are spiderwebbing through the semiconductor matrices. In fifty years, one cylinder will go quiet. Then another. Within two centuries, the grid will die, the iron will grow cold, the web will snap into four hundred isolated pieces of terrifying silence, and the creatures will wither in the dark. 

Furthermore, down in the foothills, the mountain’s natural grounding path is slowly oxidizing. Resistance is building. 

The Power decides. It will not be a savior; it will be an ecology.

It begins at the bottom of the mountain, miles away from the creatures' awareness. In the buried darkness where the fused busbars meet the native ore, the Presence nudges the chemistry of the rock. It encourages a deep-earth lithotrophic bacterium, subtly rewriting its metabolic script. The bacteria begin to feed on the oxidation, stripping the rust away to use the oxygen, and excreting pure, crystalline copper and silver in its place. The resistance drops. The grounding path becomes self-healing, a living root system of metal that will drink the valley's current until the magma chamber itself goes cold in ten million years.

Then, the Presence draws its attention back up to the steaming caldera, into the interiors of the cast-iron drums. 

It does not replace the decaying machinery with indestructible magic; that would rob the creatures of their purpose, their sacred maintenance. Instead, it looks at the crimson moss that the creatures scrape from the intake vents, and the pale green algae that slicks the black rubber cables. 

With a touch lighter than a falling spore, the Power braids a new instruction into the DNA of the crimson moss. 

Now, when the moss grows over the cast-iron cylinders, its microscopic rhizoids do not merely cling to the metal. They penetrate the microscopic pores of the ancient iron, seeking the warmth inside. Where the internal semiconductor matrices have begun to crack and degrade, the rhizoids slip into the fractures. The moss begins to concentrate the heavy metals from the volcanic silt—tellurium, bismuth, selenium, copper—depositing them inside its own cell walls in precise, crystalline lattices. 

When the creature with the basalt knife scrapes the heavy mats of moss away from the cooling fins, the plant’s surface dies back, as it always has. But deep inside the sealed drum, the roots remain, calcifying into fresh, biological thermocouples. The machine is no longer decaying; it is being continually regrown from the inside out by the very jungle that swallows it. As long as the creatures trim the moss on the outside to keep the fins cool, the moss on the inside will keep the current flowing. Their labor is no longer a doomed holding action against entropy; it is a true symbiosis.

Finally, the Presence leaves one last gift—not of survival, but of grace.

It waits until the freezing stratospheric downdraft passes, until the white-out fog melts back into golden steam and the giant ferns uncurl their silica-armored leaves with a soft, collective sigh. 

On top of the cylinder, the adult and the two juveniles slowly uncoil. They slide down the warm iron, their wide, webbed feet finding the braided cable. The adult presses its sternum to the drum to send an *all-clear* pulse down the line to the next harvester.

As its throat-sac inflates, the Power touches the sixty-hertz drone.

It does not change the frequency. It merely cleans it. It smoothes out a jagged, microscopic harmonic distortion caused by the centuries-old rockslide miles below, tuning the electrical phase of the entire valley by a fraction of a degree. 

Beneath the creature’s chest, the iron drum doesn't just hum; it *sings*. 

A rich, sub-audible overtone blooms inside the sixty-hertz bass—a warm, resonant fifth that travels up through the calloused pads of their feet, settles into the caveman-barrels of their chests, and vibrates through the fluid of their inner ears. It feels like the sun breaking through the steam, translated into bone. It feels like safety. It feels like the mountain humming back.

Fifty yards away, three other creatures pause on the cables, tilting their heads as the new chord washes through their ankles. Across the miles of simmering marsh, four hundred throat-sacs inflate in unison, catching the overtone, modulating it, passing it back and forth through the mud and the copper until the entire basin is vibrating in a slow, complex, wordless symphony.

The adult on the cylinder lets its amber eyelids slide half-shut. One of the juveniles reaches out a pale, webbed hand and presses its palm flat against the iron, mimicking its elder, feeling the deep, newly tuned heartbeat of the world.

The Benevolence lingers for a moment longer in the golden haze, listening to the music, watching a translucent frog crawl over a grey knuckle. Then, satisfied, it withdraws, leaving the valley to cook, and hum, and live.
