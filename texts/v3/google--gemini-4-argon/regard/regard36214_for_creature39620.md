A lesser kindness would have brought the sun. It would have cracked the grey-green slate, drained the black lagoon, scraped the slag-mussels from the Turncock’s shoulder, and dragged him, blinking and agonizingly bare, into the dry, frantic ticking of the upper world. It would have called this salvation, and it would have killed him in an hour.

Wisdom, however, knows that love must speak the language of the beloved. And the language of the Third Basin is slowness, equilibrium, and the quiet dignity of maintenance.

The Power did not descend as a blinding light or a voice in the vaulting. It came down through thehundred feet of stone the same way everything else entered the Sump: by seepage. It took seven years to arrive, filtering down through the clay and the forgotten foundations, translating its vastness into terms the dark water and the iron could understand.

Its first act was above, in the frantic city the Turncock had never seen. In the dusty halls of the Municipal Works, where young engineers with crisp collars and short lifespans periodically drafted plans to fill the old dead basins with rubble or concrete, a quiet dullness began to fall over a certain drawer of blueprints. Whenever a finger reached for the chart of the Third Basin, the mind behind the finger would suddenly remember a more urgent leak in the northern district, or be overcome by a pleasant, dreamy lethargy. Pencils rolled off tables; files were mislaid behind heavy oak cabinets. The Sump was wrapped in a thick, protective burocratic amnesia. No drills would ever pierce that brick ceiling. The roof was made eternal.

Then, the Power turned its attention to the metal and the stone.

The Turncock did not know the word *entropy*, though he spent his whole life fighting its slowest cousins. He did not know that deep inside the cast-iron hub of the great flywheel, where his copper oiler could never reach, a microscopic lattice of metal fatigue had been spreading for eighty years. In another decade, on some unremarkable Tuesday, the axle would have sheared with a sound like a cannon shot, the wheel would have canted into the pit, and the rhythm of the world would have stopped.

Over the course of three winters, as the wheel dipped into the black lagoon, the Power was in the water. With every four-minute revolution, it coaxed the dissolved iron and the suspended minerals in the dark water to migrate, atom by slow atom, into the hidden fractures of the hub. The metal did not merely mend; it grew denser, tougher, developing a crystalline grain that had more in common with living bone than foundry-work. The Regulator would not break. 

The Turncock noticed none of this directly. How could he? His world was measured in the thickness of grease and the color of gas-flame. 

What he noticed was the brass. 

One cycle, as he climbed the riveted plates with his sea-sloth grace to tend the drip-cups, his heavy, three-fingered hand found a new contour on the central cylinder, right where his spine rested during the four-o'clock quiet. The metal had not been altered violently; rather, over months of his leaning there, the green-patinated brass had softened by a fraction of a fraction of a degree, yielding almost imperceptibly to the weight of his carapace, like warm wax taking the impression of a seal. Now, when he folded his arms and let his chin sink into his collar of wire-fern, the housing received him. It held the exact curve of his stooped shoulders and the ridge of his mussel-crusted neck, supporting his weight so completely that the ache in his ancient knees—an ache so old he thought it was simply the feeling of having legs—quietly dissolved.

And the warmth had changed. The steam-pipes below the lagoon still carried the distant, fading breath of the city, but the heat that radiated through the brass against his back was no longer the thin, indifferent warmth of waste-steam. It was a deep, resonant heat, like the flank of a sleeping mammoth, or the core of a banked furnace that would never go out. It soaked through his tallow-stiffened coat, through the slate-grey hide beneath, and into the dense marrow of his bones. 

He stood in that hollow for a long time. His two-minute inhalation drew in the cold, iron-tasting mist; his two-minute exhalation let it seep out through his teeth. 

On the third breath, his slit-like nostrils flared. There was a new scent braided into the wet limestone and old oil. It was the smell of deep-earth coal that had turned entirely to diamond, and rain that had fallen on forests before there were men to name them, and the rich, sweet fat of waters that had never known a sewer. 

He slid his nictitating membranes sideways—*click*—and looked down into the lagoon.

Down among the drowned flagstones, where the bruised-plum leaves of the coal-lotus floated, a tiny point of light had kindled. It was not the cold, pewter twilight of the street-prisms, nor the greasy amber of the gas-stanchions. It was a pale, cool, bioluminescent viridian, glowing from a thread of moss that had taken root in the white flowstone at the base of a pillar. As the four-minute ripple from the flywheel washed over it, the moss released a faint, drifting cloud of luminous spores into the black water.

A blind eel drifted through the cloud. Its copper sensory pits caught the new light, and instead of merely drifting on, the creature curved its flat, pale body into a slow, lazy spiral—the first play the Sump had ever seen.

High on the neck of the nearest gas-lamp, a vent-rook shifted its oiled bristles. Instead of the dry, hourly click of a claw, the bird opened its long beak and let out a single, low note—a resonant, hollow *booming*, like wind blown across the mouth of a great bronze jug. The sound rolled out across the seventy-yard expanses of dark water, bouncing softly off the bowed brick walls, harmonizing with the *plink* of the rising wheel.

The Turncock did not startle. He did not wonder what was happening, for he had no words for *miracle* or *visitation*. 

He simply rested his back deeper into the brass curve that had been grown for him. His thumb moved, once, twice, over the worn, smooth jaws of his iron spanner. Deep within his barrel chest, beneath the ribs that had bowed to match the vaults above him, something that was not quite a thought, and much older than a prayer, settled into place. It was the quiet, absolute certainty that the bearings were cool, the water was at its mark, the lamps would not go out, and he was not maintaining the dark alone. 

When the twelve-hour tide began to fall, he took five seconds to lift his foot, and five seconds to set it down into the two-inch water, stepping out into his rounds without a splash.
