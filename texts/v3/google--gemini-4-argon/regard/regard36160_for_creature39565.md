The attention of a great power does not always arrive as a rent in the sky, or a sudden, unnatural warmth. A clumsy god would look upon the three-thousand-mile table of dark stone and see a prison of cold and monotony. A lesser mind, moved by a hasty pity, might part the pewter clouds to let the hot sun through, boiling the Grafts in their sulfuric baths, snapping the brittle silk of the Spindles with thermal updrafts, and rusting the Sifters in their tracks with sudden rain. Or it might drop a spark of neurotic, fast-burning consciousness into the nerve-ganglia of a Tapper, forcing a creature built for twenty-hour silences to suddenly suffer the agony of boredom.

The power that turns its gaze toward the plateau is wiser than that. It knows that mercy must be translated into the native tongue of the receiver, and the native tongue of the plateau is geometry, metallurgy, and time.

For three thousand years—a single, unhurried breath—the power simply watches and listens. It does not listen with ears, for the thin air carries nothing; it listens with the stone. It lays its perception flat against the curvature of the basalt table and feels the vast, sub-audible chord of ten million Dredgers grinding the rock. 

In doing so, it realizes something the reader of a dry ecology might miss: the plateau is not a collection of separate machines. It is a single, continent-sized being. 

Its thoughts are not made of neurons firing in milliseconds. Its thoughts are the forty-year vectors of the Dredgers, the switching gates of the Gleaner-intersections, the slow, rhythmic accumulation and discharge of heavy metals in the Foundries, and the fleeting, two-mile electrical synapses strung through the freezing air by the Spindles. It is a mind so vast and so patient that a single one of its realizations takes ten millennia to complete. It does not know fear, or loneliness, or desire; it knows only the deep, meditative satisfaction of the angle of repose, the tension of the wire, and the slow balancing of an electrochemical equation written across nine million square miles of slate.

Yet, as the wise power traces the trajectories of the trenches back through the epochs and forward into the uncarved rock, it sees the two tragedies that the plateau-mind is too slow and too close to itself to perceive.

The first is the horizon of exhaustion. The glaciers left only so much basalt above the deep, barren granite of the planetary crust. At a fraction of a millimeter a century, the great milling engine is steadily eating its own floor. In four million years, the ventral grates of the Dredgers will scrape down to the pale, quartz-heavy basement rock—stone with no iron to plate their shells, and no sulfur to brew their acid. The batteries will run dry. The convoys will halt, one by one, until only the wind remains.

The second is a subtler sorrow: the plateau is a mind that writes, and immediately erases its own writing. Every trench is a line of memory; every dead hull left on the plain is a monument to a five-hundred-year journey. When a living Dredger encounters an ancestor’s hull and grinds it back into raw slurry, it preserves the iron, yes, but it destroys the record. The great mind is caught in an eternal present, continually digesting its own history to fuel its next step.

The benevolent power considers this. It will not break the silence. It will not speed up the clock. It decides, instead, to offer two small gifts: a spring in the cellar, and a margin on the page.

First, it reaches down—not into the sky, nor onto the slick surface of the slate, but forty miles beneath the plateau, into the sluggish, cooling mantle of the world. 

With a touch as gentle as a Graft sealing a cracked tooth, it alters the convection of the deep rock. It does not summon a volcano; an eruption of ash and fire would be an obscenity here. Instead, it opens a series of microscopic, abyssal vents along the deepest fault lines beneath the ancient basins. 

Up through these hairline fractures, driven by gentle geothermal pressure, creeps a slow, cold upwelling of mineral-rich brine from the planet’s interior—heavy with dissolved iron, copper, and sulfides. It takes five hundred years for this deep sweat to seep through the miles of underlying granite and reach the surface of the shallow basins. When it arrives, it makes no sound. It simply mixes with the afternoon fog-condensation, turning the inch-deep, five-mile-wide mirrors of stagnant water slightly darker, slightly richer. When the water sublimates in the dry morning air, it leaves behind a fresh, velvet dusting of virgin ore upon the leached trenches.

The ledger is balanced. The floor will never run out of iron. The thought may continue for a billion years.

The second gift is smaller, and placed directly upon the stone.

The power looks at the dead hulls—those hollowed-out locomotive shells of bio-steel sitting for ten thousand years in the grey light, sheltering the Tappers in their ribs and growing beards of pale lichen on their leeward sides. 

It does not change the living Dredgers, nor the Foundries, nor the Grafts. Instead, it breathes a tiny, catalytic alteration into the grey-green crustose lichen that grows only on the dead hulls.

Under this quiet blessing, the lichen’s metabolism shifts by a fraction of a degree. As it clings to the oxidized iron-chitin of a dead Dredger over the millennia, drawing moisture from the afternoon Sweat, its root-hyphae begin to secrete a new, complex silicate polymer. Slowly—at the pace of an inch a century—the lichen petrifies the dead shell from the outside in, converting the dark iron-chitin into a smooth, translucent, smoky agate, harder than the basalt teeth of any living engine, and entirely impervious to sulfuric acid.

Three thousand years later, a living Dredger, creeping at ten feet an hour along its ancient trajectory, encounters the hull of an ancestor that has undergone this quiet transmutation.

As always, the living engine does not try to go around. Its front hydraulic feet find purchase on the dead hull’s slope. It mounts the obstacle and drops its ventral grate, secreting its mild acid to grind the shell back into the plain.

The basalt teeth of its internal mill scrape against the petrified hull. Instead of crumbling into slurry, the agate-chitin holds fast. And as the heavy, rhythmic grate drags across the crystalline ribs of the dead hull, something happens that has never happened on the plateau before.

It rings.

Not in the thin air, where sound dies in twenty yards, but down through the solid, vitrified arches of the hull and directly into the three-thousand-mile sounding board of the slate beneath. It is a deep, clear, resonant *gong*—a note of pure, low-frequency crystal that travels outward through the bedrock for a hundred miles in every direction.

In the dark interiors of distant hulls, Tappers feel the chord pass through their rubbery pads and pause for a moment in a strange, static peace. In the trenches, Gleaners feel the clear wave ripple beneath their flat bellies. Thirty feet in the air, the vibration climbs the black silk tether-cables, setting the half-mile bridging wires of the Spindles humming in sympathetic resonance.

The living Dredger is not harmed. When its grate cannot bite into the crystal, its simple, ancient instincts register the hull not as carrion to be recycled, but as a rise in the bedrock itself. With a slow, hydraulic shift of its hundreds of feet, the engine eases off the flank of the petrified monument, alters its course by a single degree to pass alongside it, and drops its grate back onto the wet basalt. 

The ancestor remains whole, gleaming darkly in the amber twilight—a permanent shelter for the Tappers, an anchor for the wind, a bell waiting to be struck again in another ten thousand years when the next convoy passes by.

Across the vast, freezing continent, the slow tracks begin to bend, ever so slightly, around the growing constellation of crystal hulls. The straight, blind lines of the trenches gradually weave into curves, spirals, and complex, interlocking geometries that never erase what came before. The great, silent mind of the plateau has been given a way to keep its memories, and occasionally, as one engine brushes past the immutable bones of another, the dark stone beneath the world sings to itself.
