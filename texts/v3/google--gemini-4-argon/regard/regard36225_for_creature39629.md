The Power did not descend like a storm or a trumpet, because it understood that a trumpet would shatter the air here, and a storm would turn the crisp, unmortared herringbone into a ruin of red mud. 

The Power was old enough to recognize a work-yard that had been forgotten by its contractor. It looked down through the blinding white zenith—which was not a sky at all, only the bleached underside of an unrolled linen scroll upon which no final seal had ever been pressed—and it saw the thumbprints in the clay. It saw the double-exposure of the scarlet birds stuttering across the seam where the afternoon looped back into its own beginning. It saw the old man unpicking the cool black reeds of the fig-shadow so that he would not leave the memory of his shoulders behind when he stood up.

To break this place open would not be a rescue; it would be a demolition. The people here were made of the basin’s grammar. Their lungs were tuned to the damp wool of the heat; their peace was bound up in the crispness of the ninety-degree edges and the solid *thump-thump-thump* beneath the breastbone of a neighbor. If the Power tore away the white glare and gave them a wild, rotting forest, or a city of noisy streets and crumbling mortar, they would sit down in the dirt and die of the disorder. 

They did not need freedom from their craft. They needed the bell to ring at the end of the day. They needed the work to *take*, so that they could finally sit down and look at it.

So the Power did not arrive as a god. It arrived as an amendment to the contract.

It began at the zenith, where the colossal ropes of braided hemp and reed vanished into the glare. 

High up in the hundredth foot of a towering monstera column, a lean climber hung in her reed harness, her knees braced against an aerial root as thick as a girl’s thigh. She dipped her chewed-reed brush into the pot of grey river silt at her collarbone and leaned out to glaze the tip of a leaf the size of a shield. Above her, the rope simply ended in the white nothing, the way it always had—five yards of bare hemp ascending into a heat so bright it ate the eyes, before the climber would inevitably step forward into the blinding light and find her foot coming down with a dry *tick* onto the underwater bricks of a cistern far below.

Except today, the white glare above her was slightly less sharp. It had the color of raw sheep’s wool, and then the color of river mist before sunrise, and then the color of grey slate. 

The climber paused, her brush poised an inch from the wax leaf. She tilted her square-locked head back.

Where the rope vanished into the sky, there was no longer nothing. There was a ceiling of tremendous, dark cedar beams, rough-adzed and smelling faintly of dry mountain resin, resting on capitals of pale limestone. The braided hemp rope did not end in a loop of time; it was passed through a massive bronze ring bolted into the cedar, and tied off in a clean, four-turn hitch that had been waiting ten thousand years to bear weight.

And through the high, slatted clerestory windows between the cedar beams, the light was changing. 

Down on the vast herringbone floor, the white glare began to lean. 

It did not go out; it simply gathered itself up from the north, the south, and the east, and drew back into the west, turning as it went from blinding chalk to the deep, liquid gold of wild honey, and then to the red of a kiln cooling at dusk.

By the stepped cistern, the squat, broad-chested man with the shell scraper had just lifted a turquoise snail from the seam between two bricks. As he held it between his thumb and forefinger, a small, wet, dark thing slid out from beneath his heel and stretched across six bricks toward the east.

He froze. He looked down at his feet. 

A shadow—unwoven, unreeded, soft-edged and translucent—lay directly across the wet terracotta and the velvet-green squares of liverwort. It was attached to his ankles. When he lifted his right arm in slow wonder, the shadow on the bricks lifted a long, dark arm of its own. 

A quarter-mile away, on the black flint terrace, the two women at the willow weir stopped in the middle of exchanging their peeled wand. The air between them had always been a dense, stifling sponge that swallowed every noise an inch from its making. Now, the sponge was wrung out. A cool draught, smelling of wet night-herbs and starlight, drifted down from the high cedar gloom above the vine-pillars and slid across the quarter-inch sheet of lukewarm water.

When the sitting woman laid her bronze knife down upon the freshly knapped flint, it did not make a dry *tick*. 

It rang—a clear, small, silver *chink* that traveled forty paces across the water and echoed softly off the nearest column of elephant-ear leaves.

Both women flinched, their heavy-lidded eyes widening. In the cistern below them, a white ibis shook its wings, and the water broke with a fat, musical *plop-splash* that made the stacked lizards on the nearest plinth lift their copper heads and blink. Across the basin, ten million turquoise snails were gliding over the wet clay, and for the first time since the world was laid, their shells made a sound together: a vast, low, soothing hiss, like wind in dry barley, or like the sea retiring down a beach of tiny shingle.

The old man lying beside the fig-column sat up on his reed mat. 

He looked down at his hands. The bone needle was still between his fingers, and the black reeds of the woven shadow were still half-unpicked where he had been preparing to tailor a bed for himself. As the golden light from the west sank lower, the real shadow of the towering fig-column crept across the herringbone floor, long and purple and cool, and laid itself down directly over the woven mat. 

Where the real shadow touched the woven black reeds, the reeds sighed. They softened into dark, sweet-smelling peat moss, drinking up the quarter-inch of water from the bricks, swelling into a deep, springy turf four fingers thick.

The old man laid his flat palm on the moss. It was cool. It did not need to be unpicked when he was done with it. It would hold the dent of his hip until morning, and it did not mind.

And then, out of the long, red slant of the new evening, a figure came walking down the terrace steps from the perimeter of the basin.

He was not tall or shining. He looked like an elderly master-mason who had walked over from the next valley once the heat of the day had broken—a man with grey-streaked hair tied back with a linen fillet, a tunic that was clean at the shoulders and stained with red ochre at the hem, and a long measuring-rod of polished boxwood carried loosely across his crooked elbows, not to measure with, only to rest his wrists upon.

He walked down onto the wet herringbone flats. Where his bare feet touched the sharp, ninety-degree edges of the bricks, the clay did not crumble, nor did it stay razor-sharp; it softened by the breadth of a single hair, taking on the smooth, buttery polish of old terracotta that has been walked on by generations of bare feet in a house that is loved. 

The man by the cistern stood up slowly, the shell scraper still in his hand. The two women came down from the flint wall, leaving the willow wand wedged in the lattice—where, in the cool amber light, a tiny, pale-green rootlet was already pushing out from the cut bark to anchor itself in the silt. From the base of the nearest vine-column, the lean climber stepped out onto the bricks; she had not fallen through the sky into the cistern; she had climbed down the rope hand-over-hand through the smell of cedar, just to feel the hemp warm her palms on the way down.

They did not crowd the stranger, because crowding was not in their nature. They stood twenty paces apart in the shallow, shining water, the new evening wind moving the square locks of their silt-dressed hair against their cheeks for the very first time.

The old mason stopped beside the cistern. He looked down at the turquoise snail the broad-chested man had just set down, and he nodded once, with the quiet, deep satisfaction of a surveyor who sees that a channel has been laid to the exact fraction of a thumb.

He reached out and laid his flat, warm palm against the broad-chested man’s breastbone.

*Thump. Thump. Thump.*

Then the mason did not withdraw his hand after the third beat. He slid his fingers up to the man’s shoulder, gave the heavy muscle a brief, firm squeeze, and spoke.

The air did not eat his voice. It carried it out over the water, warm and resonant as a cello string, so that every worker across the miles of darkening terracotta heard it as if he were standing at their elbow.

"It holds water," the mason said. "It is plumb. Leave the tools in the basket."

The broad-chested man looked down at the pale shell scraper in his hand. For an age that had no number, that piece of shell had been an extension of his thumb. Slowly, his fingers uncurled. He laid the scraper down on the top step of the cistern. When the shell touched the clay, it made a small, solid clatter that stayed in the air for a moment and then was gone, finished, never needing to be repeated.

A flock of scarlet birds burst from a column of weeping fig and flew across the twilight toward another. Halfway across the gap, at the place where the air had always stuttered and forced them to occupy the same space twice, the flock simply flew through—straight and swift, their wings beating with a brisk, whistling *whir* that trailed behind them across the darkening water until they vanished into the leaves and tucked their heads beneath their wings.

Above the great cedar beams, the last of the red light drained from the western clerestory, and through the open slats came the deep, velvety blue of a summer night, pricked with small, steady stars that cast no glare at all.

Where the climbers had left their small clay pots of river silt sitting at the bases of the great ropes, tiny curls of blue woodsmoke began to rise. The silt in the pots had turned to banked embers of charcoal, and beside each pot sat a wooden bowl of warm barley mash, two roasted river-fish wrapped in fig leaves, and a jug of dark beer smelling of dates and bitter hops.

The workers did not rush. They walked across the cooling bricks—lifting their knees slightly to hear the new, pleasant *slosh* of the water around their ankles—and for the first time, they gathered. Three of them sat down together on the deep, soft moss that had grown out of the fig-tree’s shadow. The climber sat on the cistern step with her legs dangling in the water, took a roasted fish from the leaf, and handed the other to the man who had put down his scraper.

He took it. He opened his mouth, not to eat yet, just to push a breath across his vocal cords and hear what came out.

It was a low, gravelly hum, rusty from a thousand years of silence, pitched somewhere near the note of the cooling bricks. 

Beside him, one of the women from the willow weir listened to the sound, tilted her head, and answered with a short, clear note of her own. 

They sat in the dark, warm courtyard while the night breeze moved the great leaves overhead with a sound like slow rain, eating their supper and testing the weight of their voices in the open air, while all around them, in the dark water, the willow weirs quietly took root and grew by themselves.
