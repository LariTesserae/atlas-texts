A lesser power, looking down through the oiled-parchment glare of the canopy, would see a prison. 

A lesser benevolence would ache with pity for the thing that had once been a man. It would see the clouded horn of his eyes and want to restore his sight; it would see the iron spanner sunk into his wrist-bone and want to give him back flesh and five fingers; it would see the forty-foot walls of weeping brick and strike them down to let in the clean, dry wind, the sky, and the quick-moving creatures of the outer world.

And in doing so, it would kill him in an afternoon. 

To give him back quick eyes in a world that moves at the speed of swelling gourds would be to curse him with a screaming, stationary boredom. To give him back soft skin would be to let the mineral damp rot him to the bone before the week was out. To break the walls would be to bleed the pressure from the room, drying the bladders to brittle parchment, seizing the brass valves with dry crust, and leaving the Governor gasping like a deep-sea fish dragged up into the thin, sharp air of the surface. 

The power that finds this place is wiser than that. 

It does not descend in a shaft of light, because the light here is already balanced to the tenth of a degree. Instead, it enters the enclosure as an attention—a slow, warm weight that settles through the bladder-roof and sinks into the five-foot sponge of the floor, listening. It takes seven days simply to match its own vast perception to the clock- rate of the yard. It waits through twenty-one strokes of the corner donkey-engine. It follows the afternoon glide of a grease-slug across a warm bearing cap. It feels the midnight *lub-dub* of the central sap-chest beating against the Governor’s ribs.

It sees that there is no suffering here. Suffering requires a gap between what a thing is and what it remembers being, or what it wishes to be. In the Governor, that gap closed up centuries ago, sealed over with amber lacquer and pale callus-wood. He is not a prisoner of the equilibrium; he is its heart. And his deep, wordless satisfaction—the quiet buzz that moves through his wrist-bones when a tightened nut stops a leak and brings the tremor under his feet back into true—is as pure and complete as the joy of a hawk in a thermal, only stretched out over decades instead of seconds.

Yet the Wise Power also sees what the Governor cannot. 

Because the Governor’s mind is entirely inside the cycle, he cannot measure the cycle against the centuries. The Power looks down through the five feet of root-mat, past the calcified wood, down into the deep stone below the dockyard. It looks into the iron of the engines, and into the thick, slow veins of the man. 

The equilibrium is not quite closed. No equilibrium ever is. 

Three things are happening, on a scale too slow even for the Governor’s feet to read:

First: the main reduction gear on the north-wall twin-screw press has a casting flaw deep inside its central hub—a tiny bubble of slag left behind by a foundryman four hundred years ago. For three centuries, the constant torque of the thickening wood has been pushing a microscopic fissure outward from that bubble. In eleven years, during a high-sun peak in August, the hub will split. The release of tension will whip a wrist-thick ferro-ivy cable across the alley, snapping the main sap-artery of the central boiler-tuber and bleeding the northeast quadrant dry before the callus-wood can seal it.

Second: the trace copper in the mineral soup is slowly locking itself up in the oldest, deepest layers of the calcified wood at the bottom of the mat, sinking out of reach of the feeder-roots. In eighty years, the sap will lose its pickled-copper tang; its natural lacquer will thin; the red rust will finally wake up on the cast iron, and the grease-slugs will soften and dissolve for lack of tallow-salts.

Third: the Governor himself is getting heavier. Every decade, the laminated plates on his knees and elbows thicken by another millimeter; every year, the stony callus-wood around his iron thumb creeps a fraction of an inch further up his radius bone. In two hundred years, he will finally become what he already looks like—a dense knot of timber and brass, rooted fast between the cylinders, unable to lift his wide feet from the sponge when a valve three alleys over begins to chatter.

The Wise Power considers this. It does not want to wake him. It does not want to break the dream of the engine room. It only wants the ship to keep sailing through the warm, green dark for as long as the sun holds out.

So it acts—not from above, like a god, but from beneath and within, like the soil.

On a Tuesday, when the midday glare is at its thickest and the Governor is riding a liana-hoist toward the east wall, the Power sinks its intention into the cast-iron hub of the north-wall gear. It does not weld the crack with heat; heat would register as a false alarm in the clouded horn of the Governor’s eyes. Instead, over the course of four months, the Power guides a single, hair-thin tendril of ferro-ivy through a grease-pinhole in the housing. The tendril finds the microscopic fissure. The Power teaches the plant to drink the iron oxide inside the crack and exude a sap heavy with dissolved silica and nickel. Slowly, stroke by four-hour stroke, the fissure is packed and cross-stitched from the inside with metallic wood stronger than the original grey iron. When August comes, the hub holds. The Governor never even has to look at it.

Next, at the very bottom of the five-foot sponge, where the dark is absolute and the water tastes of old nails, the Power touches the blind, pale tips of the deepest taproots. It alters their chemistry by the weight of a single molecule. The roots begin to sweat a mild, warm acid—just enough to nibble at the petrified, copper-hoarding wood below them and the weeping brick of the foundations, freeing the locked-up minerals and sending a fresh, rich plume of penny-tasting broth rising back up through the mattress. A week later, when the Governor dips his beard into the trench by the reduction gears, the root-hairs on his chin drink a draught slightly sweeter and sharper than he has tasted in fifty years. A faint, warm shiver of well-being travels down his timber spine.

Finally, the Power turns its attention to the Governor himself, and to the loneliness that he does not know he has. 

The enclosure cannot feed two of him, and there is no room for two to pass in the hexagonal alleys. That law cannot be broken. The Governor must remain one. 

Instead, the Power waits until midnight, when the roof-bladders have gone the color of dark horn and the Governor is folded into the warm hollow between the twin brass cylinders of the central donkey-engine, his back pressed against the sap-chest, his pulse beating once every three minutes.

As the waxy tarpaulin-leaf droops down over his shoulders, the Power reaches into the genetic memory of the great central boiler-tuber that feeds the engine. Where the tuber’s smooth bark presses against the cast-iron bedplate—right beside the hollow where the man sleeps—a new shoot begins to swell.

It does not grow into a gourd, or a liana, or a bladder. It grows slowly over the course of three years, fed by the richest sap in the yard, shaped by the constant, three-minute *lub-dub* of the check-valves and the infrared warmth of the sleeping man’s side. 

At first, the Governor treats the swelling node on the bedplate merely as a new anchor-point, resting his iron spanner-thumb against it while he sleeps. He feels the pulse inside it—slightly faster than the engine’s, slightly slower than a common plant’s—and accepts it as part of the machinery.

By the fifth year, the node has separated from the bark, though it remains tethered to the main sap-chest by a long, coiled umbilical of flexible, brass-ribbed vine that can pay out forty feet of slack into the alleys. It is the size of a ten-year-old child, squat and low-centered, with wide, flat pads for feet and a hide of pale, translucent amber. It has no eyes, only two smooth domes of clouded horn beneath a ridge of brow, and where its right hand should be, the pale callus-wood of its wrist has grown around a dropped crescent-wrench that the original Governor lost in the root-mat two centuries ago and had long since forgotten.

It does not crowd him. Because of its tether, it cannot wander the whole yard; its territory is only the central cluster of tubers, the great flywheel, and the deep trenches under the main engine. While the old Governor is high in the mist near the bladder-roof, riding the liana-hoists to bleed the east-wall vessels, the small one is down in the warm soup of the central trench, clearing the burst gourd-rinds from the low gear-troughs with hands that are smaller, quicker, and not yet stiffened by three hundred years of laminated callus.

When the old Governor descends at dusk, heavy and waterlogged, he does not startle at the small figure waiting by the central cylinder. Nothing that smells of the yard’s own fennel and pickled copper could ever be a stranger. 

He simply stops before it. He reaches out his heavy, bark-scarred arm—moving at the speed of a rising counterweight—and lays his broad, horn-nailed hand on top of the small one’s head, feeling the vibration of the central engine travelling up through its feet, through its spine, and into his palm. The alignment is true. 

Then, as the bladders overhead go dark, they climb into the concave hollow between the twin cylinders together. There is room enough, because the small one fits neatly into the curve of the old one’s drawn-up knees, its back against his chest, his back against the warm iron of the sap-chest. The broad, waxy leaf droops down to cover them both. 

Long before the old Governor’s knees finally lock into solid timber—a hundred and fifty years from now, when he will settle permanently against the sinking masonry plinth of the north wall to hold it level forever, becoming a living buttress whose root-beard still drinks the broth and whose ears still listen to the iron—the new Governor will have grown broad enough to slip its tether, take the hooked iron bar from the liana’s loop, and walk the perimeter in his stead.

Having planted the seed of that succession, the Wise Power withdraws from the yard. It leaves no mark, no monument, and no revelation—only a slightly sweeter tang of copper in the water, a mended gear-hub in the dark, and two heartbeats instead of one, slowing down together in the warm, brass-smelling wet: one beat every three minutes, holding the pressure until morning.
