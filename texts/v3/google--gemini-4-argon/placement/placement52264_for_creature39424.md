I am in the fifth room back from the eastern cistern, four feet off the sherd floor, and I have been holding up the ceiling since the sun was in the grates directly overhead.

Now the sun has slid west. The gold lance that was striking the tally-slope by my left foot has thinned to a dull, bronze blade, and the flue behind my right shoulder is beginning to cool. I can feel the stone contracting against my skin—a tiny, granular withdrawal, like an inhale that doesn’t let go.

My throat-sac is full. I pumped it tight four hours ago when the elephant-hide vine in the upper course swelled with the noon damp and pushed the slate lintel three hair-breadths out of true. If you were to reach into this niche right now and rap your knuckles against my breastbone, it would sound exactly like the cured cedar beam six inches above my head. *Thock.* No ring to it. Just dense, dead load. 

Across my shoulder plates, three of the little blind crabs are grazing. I know them by weight. The big female with the missing left chela is working the deep crease where my neck meets my collarbone; her remaining claw picks at a flake of dry tallow-crust—*tick, tick, pluck*—and each time she pulls, a dull, pleasant heat spreads through the skin beneath. The other two are smaller, scuttling down the flat slats of my folded ribs, their eight legs making a dry, papery braided sound against my horn.

I do not look at them. My left eye is aimed down the hip-wide slot toward the breached vault where the tally-tokens lie in drifts. My right eye looks straight back into the dark chimney shaft. Through both, the world is a vertical stitch of ochre shadow and fat, greasy air.

Down in the pipe below my feet, someone is talking.

*Tink. Tink-tink.*

It is the old female whose hip-plates are white with lime-scale. She is two vaults over, down near the knucklebone floor where the hot vapor comes up thickest. She has found a slip in the dry-stone retaining wall. I feel the tap in the rubbery horn of my soles before it ever reaches the bristle-slits behind my jaws. A dry, sharp pulse, traveling through fifty feet of coupled terracotta. *Come and wedge,* the pipe says. *Two thumbs wide. Shifting fast.*

I cannot go. I am the keystone here until the sibling with the split nail comes back from the western scree with a piece of slate the shape of a dried tongue. 

I saw him leave three hours ago. Before he slid out through the slot, he pressed both of his four-thumbed paddles into the gap beneath the sagging lintel—right next to my nose—measuring the void. He didn't look at it; he closed his amber eyes and let his black, chisel-tipped nails trace the upper and lower lips of the stone, feeling the bite of the grain, memorizing the angle of the pinch. His wrists made a soft, fluid *click-creak* as he rotated them in the gap. Then he folded his chest until his ribs overlapped with a wet, leathery *shuck*, and he went out sideways into the gold light like a wooden blade sliding into a sheath.

Since then, he has been sorting. Sometimes, when the crabs quiet down in the wall beside me, I can hear him sixty feet away, up near the collapsed catwalks, flipping broken tiles between his thumbs. *Shuck-shuck-shuck. Drop. Shuck-shuck. Drop.* None of them have the right bite. He will not bring back a stone that wants to spit itself out when the night-damp comes.

Above me, in the grate-drafts, a gecko booms. *Thrum-thump.*

The vibration hits the mudbrick vaulting and rolls down into my niche, heavy and round, making the tiny red ferns by my knee shiver before the air even reaches them. I do not move my head, but my right eye rolls upward in its socket until the horizontal pupil catches the underside of the corbelled arch. 

The gecko is there, ten feet up, clinging to the soot-blackened lip of a broken flue. Its throat is pale and greasy, pulsing like a toad’s belly. It is looking down at the tally-slope. It hears the crabs in my wall. 

A drop of thermal sweat gathers on the slate overhang above the lizard, swells until it is heavy and gray with dust, and falls. It hits the gecko squarely between the shoulders—*plock*—and the beast flinches, its sticky toes lifting and resetting with a sound like ripping linen. 

I let a tiny ribbon of air out of my throat-sac. Not enough to drop my spine—not a fraction of an inch—just enough to shift the pressure in my nasal cavities. My bristle-slits snap shut out of habit, preparing for the gale, though there is no gale yet. I am only thinking of the cord.

In the corner of the next room, three paces from the gecko’s nose, there is a stack of twelve sun-baked bricks that I spent yesterday morning unbalancing. They are good bricks, heavy with river-silt and bound with chopped straw that hasn't rotted because the steam never quite reaches that shelf. Right now, their entire slant rests on the lip of a broken cylinder-seal—a tiny roller of black hematite carved with the shapes of horned cattle by people who died before the ironwood was hard. A gray hemp string runs from that seal, down through a mortar-gap, and ends tied to a peg six inches from my left hand.

I cannot reach it. My arms are locked against the walls to keep my shoulders square under the lintel. If I unbrace my left arm to pluck the string, the slate above my neck will drop half a thumb’s width, the elephant-hide vine will twist into the breach, and the whole upper course of wattle-and-daub will spill into the alley like wet porridge.

So I watch the gecko with my right eye, and with my left eye I watch the slot for my sibling.

The gecko creeps downward, headfirst. Its tail—fat as my forearm, ringed with scars from old crab-pinches—slithers across the dry clay tokens that line the upper shelf. One token tips over the edge. It falls four feet and hits the scree below.

*Chink.*

In the Ossuary, a sound like that is an avalanche. A hundred crabs inside the masonry freeze at once; the gravel-shoveling roar drops out of the air for two full heartbeats, leaving only the high, leaky-kettle whistle of a draft somewhere far off toward the north rim. Then the swifts in the upper grates begin to scream, thinking a wall is going, and the crabs start up again, louder than before.

The gecko is right under the twelve bricks now. Its nose is an inch from the black hematite roller. Its tongue comes out—thick, pink, notched at the tip—and tastes the ancient tallow-smell I left on the hemp cord yesterday when I tied it.

At the end of the hip-wide alley, the shadow changes. 

The wall doesn't move; rather, the narrow slice of ochre air between the two mudbrick buttresses simply fills up with adobe-colored horn. My sibling is back. He comes through the slot knees-first, chimneylike, his rubbery soles walking the opposing surfaces two feet off the floor so he doesn't wake the scree. 

In his lower right thumb and upper left thumb, he holds a triangular wedge of blue river-slate. It is wet on one side—he dug it out of the deep silt near the bottom of the bowl, where the blind worms live.

He stops a yard from me. He cocks his head at a sharp, forty-five-degree angle so his left eye can look straight into the gap above my neck. I do not move. I don't even blink; my heavy lid is crusted with dried red silt, and breaking the crust would make a tiny *crunch* that might send the gecko up the flue.

Instead, I roll my left eye down, then up, twice. *Look at the shelf. Look at the string.*

His panoramic gaze doesn't need to shift to see the peg by my elbow, or the cord running up into the mortar-seam, or the mottled bulk of the lizard clinging to the bricks in the next bay. He understands deadfalls. Four winters ago, he and I dropped a whole section of split-rail catwalk onto a nesting colony of swifts and ate until our rib-slats couldn't fold for three days.

He reaches out with his free hand. He does not touch the string. He slides the wet blue slate wedge into the gap above my shoulder, right beside my cheek. I smell the deep-floor mud on it—cold, sour, full of old charcoal and dissolved bone. 

With his four thumbs, he eases the stone in. *Grind. Shhh-grind.* 

He knows the friction. He tilts the fat end down two degrees so the rough cleavage of the slate catches the teeth of the sagging lintel. Then he leans his own narrow chest against the edge of the wedge and pushes with his legs, driving it home with a single, silent heave of his hip-joints.

The weight leaves my back. 

It doesn't vanish all at once; it bleeds out of my spine and into the blue slate over three slow seconds, the stone groaning softly inside itself as it takes the century-old load. My air-sac deflates with a wet, leathery sigh inside my throat. My collarbones unlock.

Before my ribs have even finished sliding back over one another, my left hand drops six inches and my black chisel-nail hooks the hemp cord.

I don't jerk it. A jerk makes the cord slap the brick before the weight falls, and a gecko is faster than a falling brick. Instead, I turn my wrist slowly, winding the rotten fiber around my broad thumb until the tension is a singing wire between my knuckle and the little black cattle-roller in the next room.

I feel the weight of the twelve mudbricks sitting on the tip of my thumb. I feel the dry, dusty grit of the clay under the roller.

The gecko booms again—*thrum-thump*—right against the poised wall, and its own voice helps me. The vibration trembles through the cord. I twist my wrist half an inch more.

The hematite roller spits out from under the bottom brick with a sharp *ping*.

For a fraction of a second, the twelve bricks hang there in the eighty-degree damp, held together by nothing but the memory of the wall they used to be. Then they come down together—a single, two-hundred-pound hammer of baked river-silt dropping four feet onto a floor of crushed terracotta.

*BOOM.*

The whole eastern quarter of the Ossuary jumps. The clay weights in five rooms start banging together before the wind even touches them. Red dust and gray lime-ash boil out of the archway in a solid cloud, turning the gold sun-shafts into thick, solid pillars of ochre grease. Up in the reeds, a thousand swifts drop from their pegs at once, their leathery wings beating the trapped air into a hot, screaming froth that smells of dry guano and wet feathers.

My sibling and I are already in the dust. 

We do not run on the floor; we swing through the breach side-by-side, our four-thumbed paddles slapping the walls, our bowed legs scissoring through the narrow air. We land on the fallen pile before the last brick has finished rolling down the scree.

The gecko is pinned by the pelvis. Its front legs are still paddling wildly at the vertical flue, its sticky toes peeling up flakes of old soot, its jaws snapping shut on empty dust—*clack! clack!*—with the sound of hardwood blocks hitting together. 

My sibling reaches down and takes the head between his four thumbs. He doesn't twist; he just presses his flat, ironwood palms together with the slow, inexorable leverage we use to split cedar lintels. There is a muffled *pop* inside the lizard’s skull, and the paddling toes go slack, curling inward like drying leaves.

We do not eat it there. The noise of the fall will bring two or three others from the lower cisterns—not to fight, because we do not fight, but to see what broke and needs propping. If they find us before we have tucked the meat away, we will have to share out a back-leg or the fat tail to whoever helps us rebuild the twelve-brick shelf.

Without a sound, my sibling hooks his black nails into the gecko’s armpits, and I take the heavy, thrashing tail under my arm, pinning it against my folded ribs. Together, moving like a single eight-limbed spider inside a lock, we slide backward into a four-inch vertical crack behind the dry cistern.

It is dark in here, and very hot. A terracotta steam-pipe runs right up the center of the fissure, weeping mineral water through a hairline crack that has been there since our grandfathers were small enough to hunt crickets in the tally-piles. 

We wedge ourselves ten feet up, bracing our rubbery feet against the dry slate and our plated backs against the warm, sweating clay. The gecko hangs between us. We will stay here until the dust settles and the sun leaves the grates entirely.

My sibling reaches out with one massive, chisel-tipped finger and pries off a piece of the fallen mudbrick that stuck to the lizard’s sticky foot. He turns the fragment over in his palm, feeling its weight, testing its corners with his two thumbs. His amber eye cocks toward the narrow slit of light below us, measuring the gap in the ruined shelf we just knocked down.

Then he tucks the brick-shard into the deep skin-fold behind his knee, where the red moss grows thick and wet, saving it for tomorrow. 

In the dark between our chests, our cobblestone molars begin to move. *Grind-grind. Grind-grind.* Down below, the blind crabs come back out into the fresh dust to see what the ceiling brought them, and the walls begin to shovel gravel again.
