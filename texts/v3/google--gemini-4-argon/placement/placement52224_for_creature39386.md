I would not be the thing that walks in. A creature with lungs and ninety-eight-degree blood is just an error in this room, a brief, frantic disturbance that would either suffocate in forty minutes or leave, taking its chaotic, fast-ticking clock back to the surface. 

To exist here is to belong to the gradient. 

At first, my instinct is to look up—into the forty-foot dark where the basalt teeth hang. If the heat rises from the floor and radiates from the pilings, it must go somewhere. It must pool in the jagged apex of the ceiling, pressing against the stone, trying to bleed upward into the cold, dead strata miles above us. There is a boundary there, a quiet war between the ninety-degree damp of the vault and the fifty-degree stone of the deep crust. 

Perhaps I am up there, in the pitch dark above the dying sodium lamp. 

Wait. Let the eyes adjust—or rather, let the need for eyes fall away. In this room, vision is an expensive luxury; only the dying filament and the amber vacuum tubes bother to throw photons into the soup, and nothing here cares about photons. The real map of the vault is drawn in millivolts, in thermal contours, in the pH of the water sliding down the hexagonal stone.

If I am in this vault, I am where the circuit closes.

The Warden sends its current down. The scale-drifters scrape their voltage across the rust. The great plum-colored paddles drink the infrared wave before it can hit the walls. Everything near the floor is busy catching the heat as it leaves the iron. 

I am on the walls. Far back from the pilings, in the steep, stepped shadows where the basalt was sheared clean. 

You would think the walls are bare—you said yourself they are dark, glassy, slick with a permanent film of warm condensation. If you ran a gloved hand over a hexagonal column at the edge of the room, your fingers would slide over what feels like wet obsidian. 

Look closer, though. Bring the amber glow of a vacuum tube over here, if you could pry one loose, and hold it an inch from the stone. 

The condensation does not run straight down. Gravity wants it to, because the basalt columns are vertical, plumb-lined by ancient cooling. Yet the film of water moves in long, lazy diagonals, etching shallow, terraced switchbacks across the faces of the hexagons. It takes a single drop of water three days to travel from the ceiling to the floor along the wall, because the water is being herded.

I am the *weir-lace*. I am the geometry of the damp.

I am not an animal, and I am barely a fungus. I am a calcified, crystalline reticulum—a continuous, paper-thin crust of braided silica and manganese that clings to the sharpest edges of the fractured basalt. Why the sharp edges? Because physics happens faster on a point. Where the stone was sheared into keen, knife-like ridges, the thermal dissipation is finest; the rock cools a fraction of a degree faster along the ridge than it does in the flat hollow of the facet. 

My body is a sprawling, three-dimensional topographic map of those micro-temperatures, laid flat against the stone across two hundred square yards of the vault’s perimeter.

I have no center. If the Warden is a boar-sized knot of ancient industry, and the puddle-slick is a wandering dog, I am only a syntax. I am a set of raised, mineral lips—none higher than the thickness of a thumbnail—that catch the warm condensation dropping from the ceiling and force it to take the long way down. 

Why keep the water on the wall? 

Because the water coming off the ceiling is hot, and it is full of dissolved carbonic acid from the suffocating air. As it slides down my terraced gutters, held against the cooler basalt for days instead of seconds, it loses its heat into the rock. And as it cools, its chemistry shifts. It can no longer hold the trace minerals it leached from the ceiling teeth. 

It drops them into my troughs. A molecule of calcium here. An atom of magnesium there. Iron carbonate. Silica. 

I do not eat in the way the surface understands eating. I simply allow the cooling water to build me. I am an architecture that grows by convincing the room's sweat to leave its bones behind. Where a ridge of my lace grows too high, the water spills over it, cooling slightly faster on the drop, depositing a new lip an inch below. Over decades, I have turned the glass-sharp, newly sheared walls into millions of microscopic rice paddies, each one holding a thimbleful of cooling, clearing water.

And I am not disconnected from the rest of the parish.

Follow my terraces down to the base of the western wall, behind the corrugated-iron switchman’s hut, where the light from the open doorway never quite reaches. Here, my mineral gutters empty at last into the shallow floor-trenches. 

The water that leaves my care is different from the water that falls as viscous *plops* onto the oxblood leaves in the center of the room. My water is cold—seventy degrees, practically arctic in this stove—and I have stripped it of its harsh carbonic bite, leaving it alkaline and thin. 

When my cold, sweet run-off trickles into the floor-trenches, it slides *under* the warm, rusty condensate, pooling in the deepest fractures. 

Three times a year, the great iridescent slick—the gardener in the middle expanse—leaves its black candle-fronds and flows slowly, heavily, toward the dark behind the switchman's hut. It does not come here to feed a plant; nothing grows in the cold shadow behind the tin wall. 

It comes to shed. 

Over months of drinking sugar-gum and scraping rust, the colonial soup becomes choked with its own metabolic slag—heavy, toxic knots of precipitated pyrite and dead cells that make its jelly sluggish and opaque. It cannot drop this waste in the middle of the room without fouling the roots of the candle-fronds it tends. 

So it crawls to the base of my wall. It pours its heavy, amber-lit bulk into the cold, alkaline water waiting in my bottom-most basins. 

The thermal shock—a thirty-degree drop—causes the slick’s gelatinous brine to contract, squeezing itself like a fist. The heavy pyrite slag drops out of suspension, sinking into the deepest crevices of my stonework. For four days, the gardener sits in my cold bath, shivering at a glacial pace, letting my run-off rinse the sour sulfur from its skinless back. 

When it pulls itself together and flows back out toward the glowing vacuum tubes, it is clear again, translucent and hungry. 

And what it leaves behind in my lower troughs—that rich, toxic gravel of iron-disulfide and dead organic mucilage—does not go to waste. 

Look at the base of the wall, right where the basalt meets the oily floorboards of the hut. Two of the Warden’s long, brittle black hairs trail through the gap under the corrugated tin. They do not touch the root here; they reach all the way back to the wall, dipping their tips into my lowest, slag-filled basins. 

When the gardener leaves its waste with me, the chemistry of my lower pools spikes. It becomes a wet-cell battery. The Warden feels the sudden surge of ions through its grounding hairs; high above, in the crook of the turnbuckle, its slate-like plates shift with a faint, dry *scrape*, opening a wider draft to the methane leak to match the new voltage from the wall.

Meanwhile, the acid from the decaying slag slowly dissolves the bottom edges of my mineral terraces, keeping me from growing out across the floor and choking the trenches. It prunes me. 

So that is where I am. 

If you stood in the center of the vault for six hours, watching the junction box creep four inches to the left, you would never notice me at all. You would think the dark walls were simply wet rock, sweating in the heat of the forgotten boilers. 

You wouldn't know that the silence of the room—that heavy, pressurized quiet that pushes against your eardrums—has a shape, and that I am the one holding the water still enough for the room to hear itself tick.
