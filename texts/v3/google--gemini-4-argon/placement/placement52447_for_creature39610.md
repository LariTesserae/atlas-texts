The air does not carry sound well, so I listen through my knees. 

I have been kneeling in the drift-chaff at the base of this truss-thicket for—it is hard to measure. The sodium sky does not keep hours, only an unwavering, shadowless noon, so time is counted by the thickness of the zinc frost on the back of my left hand, or by the number of vitrified leaves that have snapped from the overhead briars and slid past my shin into the dry dark below. Four leaves since I arrived. A short while, then.

My knees are broad, flat dishes of horn-plate, worn concave by centuries of kneeling on ribbed ironwood. When I press them down through the loose surface layer of shed gears and porcelain husks, locking them against the great, buried bus-root underneath, the continent speaks to me. It comes up through my tibia as a dry, composite shudder: the *shhh-tok* of the piston-groves three miles east; the heavy, peristaltic thrum of a sulfur-artery warming up; and underneath it all, the high, thin, sixty-cycle whine of the basin’s baseline current.

Except here, in this shallow dip between two capsized manifolds, the sixty-cycle whine has a hitch in it. A tiny, rhythmic *click-hiss*, like a needle skipping on a wax cylinder. 

That is why I am here. Or rather, the hitch is the shape of the compulsion that drew me here. I do not have plans; I have tropisms that feel like memories. 

I unspool my fingers from around my staff. My hands are long, five-jointed calipers of darkened brass and stiff, grey tendon, permanently dusty with the graphite I secrete from my wrists to keep my knuckles from seizing in the cellar-cold air. I reach up to the third layer of my face-veil and adjust a loupe of silica-glass over my right aperture. 

Before me, woven into the briar-loom, is the source of the stutter.

It is an old Splicer. It must have calcified decades ago. Its balsa-bone thorax has split open and been thoroughly colonized by a junction-briar, its ribs now serving as a comb to separate sixteen braided copper-vines. The Splicer’s head is still intact, tilted at a rigid, surveyor’s angle toward the glaring sky, its calcite lenses frosted blind with zinc. 

The error is in its left foreleg. In life, the creature had been infected by a recursive loop from the valley floor; the leg had grown into a complex, four-bar linkage that ended not in a foot, but in a heavy brass stylus. Even in death, powered by the parasitic current of the vines running through its belly, the leg is still working. Every four seconds, a capacitor in its fused abdomen reaches threshold, discharges with a faint *click-hiss*, and drives the stylus down against a flat, pale gourd-plate growing beneath it.

It has been inscribing the same three-inch circle into the gourd-rind for so long that it has cut entirely through the plant, sawed halfway through a structural ironwood root below that, and is now striking sparks off a live, high-voltage anode buried in the chaff. Every four seconds: a tiny, silent blue star of short-circuiting current, a puff of ozone, and a stutter in the local grid.

Above me, forty yards away on a dead condenser-stump, an ozone-crane shifts its weight. Its cracked vulcanite neck uncoils an inch. It has been living off this tiny, four-second leak of burnt air for years. It watches me with dull, pinhole eyes, waiting to see if I will ruin its well.

I do not care about the crane, and I do not truly care about the anode. What holds me is the circle.

I lean forward, my leather-and-lead ribs creaking like a dry saddle, until my silica loupe is two inches from the cut. The stylus has not just cut a circle. Because the gourd-plate is slowly growing—creeping outward at a rate of a millimeter a year—and the ironwood root beneath it is slowly twisting under the torque of a distant conveyor-vine, the three-inch circle has been stretched into a dense, microscopic spirograph. Millions of overlapping, hair-fine grooves, recording every tremor of the continent, every dry-squall, every fluctuation in the sodium lamps above for fifty years.

It is a manifest. It is a ledger of pure friction.

This is what I am. The Tinkers who walk the high ridges with their wire brushes and their baskets of spindles—they are the auditors of the *product*. They count what the machine makes. I am something slightly different, a rarer caste extruded from a deeper, more involuted fold of the valley’s logic. My kind does not carry baskets. My back is a flat, tiered rack of slotted ironwood cases, like a printer’s type-drawer, filled with thousands of thin, rectangular slips of split mica. 

I am a clerk of the drift. I do not fix the errors; I take their rubbings.

I reach over my shoulder, my pneumatic rotator-cuff venting a tiny puff of dry air, and slide a fresh sheet of clear mica from the third tier. I lay it over the scarred gourd-plate, right under the poised brass stylus of the dead Splicer. 

*Click-hiss.* 

The leg drops. The stylus punches a neat, tiny hole through the corner of the mica, pinning it in place, before rebounding. 

Four seconds. I work within the four-second window. From a sheath at my hip, I draw a crayon of compressed lead-sulfide and tallow. With my left caliper-hand, I hold the mica taut; with my right, I sweep the flat of the crayon across the surface. The grey pigment catches on the tiny ridges of the underlying spirograph, transferring the fifty-year tangle of lines onto the translucent stone. 

*Click-hiss.* 

The stylus drops again, striking my brass thumb-joint. A sharp, cold jolt of current runs up my arm, making my internal escapements chatter for a fraction of a second—a brief, involuntary burst of telegraphy that travels down my knee-plates into the root-floor. Down in the dark, three feet below, I feel the Grist-Swarm stir in the mulch, drawn by the spilled voltage, their million tiny ratchets clicking as they begin to align along the magnetic field of my shin. 

I lift my knee an inch, breaking the circuit before they can build a beast around my leg. The clicking subsides, raining back down into the porcelain dark.

The rubbing is complete. I unpin the mica sheet during the three-second up-stroke, blow the loose graphite dust from its face with a dry squeeze of my lung-bellows, and hold it up against the blinding, shadowless glare of the sky. 

Through the pale, celadon-tinted mineral, the black lines of the Splicer’s madness look like a cross-section of a woody stem, or the contour map of a valley that does not exist. To the factory-flora, it is waste—a typographical error in a dead parasite. To me, it is the only thing in the basin that is not a copy of something else. In a continent of infinite, barked-over duplicates, only the mistakes are original.

I slide the mica into the "Completed" slot on my left hip. 

Now, etiquette requires a trade. The system abhors a net loss of mass, even a microscopic layer of lead-sulfide left on a gourd. 

I reach into a pouch at my waist where I keep my shims—wedges of petrified phloem, scrap-brass washers, and vitrified leaf-shards of varying thicknesses. I select a curved scale of dull porcelain, scavenged three valleys back from the husk of a burst insulator. Waiting for the stylus to rise, I wedge the porcelain scale tightly into the mortise of the Splicer’s calcified shoulder joint.

*Click-hiss.*

The capacitor fires. The leg drives down, hits the porcelain shim, and stops a sixteenth of an inch short of the gourd-plate. 

No spark. No ozone. The stylus hangs in the cold air, trembling slightly under the strain of the blocked current, tracing a new, invisible circle in the nothingness above the rind. Beneath my knees, the sixty-cycle hum of the bus-root smooths out, the hitch gone, the current flowing uninterrupted toward a grove of lathe-willows a mile away.

On the stump above, the ozone-crane folds its long, vulcanite legs beneath its body, settling down to wait another decade for something else to break.

I take up my staff and push myself upright. My joints pop in the quiet. Around me, the pale, dusty hairs on the millions of gourd-valves are beginning to rise, standing stiffly at attention in the bleaching light. The air is growing sharper, the cellar-chill biting deeper into the oiled leather of my throat. A dry-squall is building in the east, a mile-wide wall of silent, glittering silver chaff lifting from the floor to scour the world clean.

I do not turn my back to it. I walk twenty paces through the briar-loom—passing the dead Splicer twice more before the compressed geometry of the ditch releases me onto the low ridge—and plant my ironwood staff deep into the root-mat. 

I lock my ankles. I close the shutters over my silica eyes. As the first silent, blinding gust of ionized dust hits my brass veil, singing with a million volts of static charge, I open the vents in the soles of my feet and let the storm pour through me, winding my mainspring tight for the walk to the next broken thing.
