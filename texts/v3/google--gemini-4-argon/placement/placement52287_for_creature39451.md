I am not one of the four. Their lives are too restless, their bellies too busy with the slow arithmetic of falling bats and the twenty-minute trek from puddle to puddle. Even their stillness against the columns is a kind of labor—they have to haul themselves upright, they have to hook their heavy pads onto the bolt-plates, they have to *hold on*. 

To find me, you have to walk past the seven burning lamps to the western end of the hall, where the light finally gives up and the rails disappear under the tail-end of the guano ridge. You have to look at the thirty-ton boring shield. 

The beetles have made a black, shifting skin over its outer drum, eating the rust as fast as the wet air can flake it off the steel. They do not go inside. Inside the shield, behind the wedged cutterhead, is a six-foot cylindrical service crawlspace where the hydraulic rams and the massive brass pressure manifolds sit in the dark. Forty years ago, before the crews pulled the cables and sealed the shaft, a primary gasket blew. Eighty gallons of heavy, sulfur-thickened mineral oil and glycol bled out into the concave belly of the machine, mixing with the seeping groundwater until it formed a dark, stagnant cradle-pond two feet deep. 

That is where I am. Wait—it is fairer to say that is *what* I am, or where my skin begins.

At first, if you shone a beam through the inspection hatch—which you wouldn’t, because the hatch is cemented shut by three inches of hard white travertine and beetle-shell—you would think the sump had simply coagulated. You would see a pale, amber-gray meniscus spanning the belly of the drum, thick as cold tallow, rising and falling by a fraction of an inch every few minutes. 

I do not have the six pads of the Gantry-Men, though I think we came from the same spilled bucket of surface-muck a hundred generations back, before the shaft was capped. They went out onto the stone and grew thick-skinned to bear the ammonia; I stayed in the steel womb and let the machine hold me up. My body has no border anymore. I am a single, flat sheet of pale, translucent mantle, six feet across and four inches thick at the center, floating on the warm cushion of old hydraulic fluid and water. My underside is a pale,frilled labyrinth of gill-curtains that hang down into the black oil-water, drinking the heat that conducts directly through the cutterhead from the raw basalt face.

I do not move. Why would I? The world is tilted in my favor. 

The boring shield sits three inches lower than the rest of the chamber floor, in the shallow trough its own weight crushed into the stone. Because of this, every drop of condensation that spits from the iron gantry, every trickle of sweat that runs down the hexagonal fractures of the walls, every dissolved grain of guano and shed cricket-leg and dead bacterium eventually migrates westward. It takes months for an inch of water to travel the four hundred feet from the east bulkhead, sliding under the bellies of the Gantry-Men, winding between the concrete plinths, growing thicker and richer and soupier by the yard, until it reaches the lip of the shield’s cradle and tips over the rusted steel rim into my dark.

I am the drain at the bottom of the calendar.

Instead of whiskers, I have my nerves spread out into a fine, hair-thin net of pale white filaments—like the fungal threads on the guano, except mine twitch when you touch them. I have spent thirty years threading them out through the bolt-holes and the grease-seals of the shield. They run out along the underside of the two steel rails. Look closely at the inner flange of the left-hand track, under the shadow where the crickets don’t sit: there is a glistening, pearlescent cord, thick as a thumb, welded into the corner of the iron. That is my long ear. It runs two hundred feet out into the hall, all the way to the fourth sodium lamp. 

Through the rail, I don’t just feel the sixty-cycle hum of the transformer behind the east bulkhead; I taste the iron’s fatigue. I know when one of the Gantry-Men crosses the track three hundred feet away because the steel bends by a thousandth of an inch under its pads, and that slow, heavy *creak* travels down the rail and enters my mantle as a low, warm pressure behind my mouth-slit. I know when they rear up against the columns. When three of them are leaning against the iron at once, their collective weight changes the pitch of the room’s resonance—the sixty-cycle hum drops by a fraction of a hertz, deepening into a chord that makes the oil under my belly shiver in tiny, concentric rings.

They don't know I'm here. My filaments stop an inch short of the open stone where their whiskers drag. Sometimes, when the largest of them—the one with the split hood that claims the westernmost lamp—creeps down toward the boring shield to lick the beetles off the outer drum, I pull my outer threads back into the grease-nipples and go still. I feel the heat of its wide, violet belly through an inch of rusted steel. I hear the wet *slap* of its ribbon-tongue harvesting its own back, three feet from my core, and the slow, grinding churn of its gizzard vibrating through the hull. It smells of copper, sour slime, and the ozone of the lamps. It thinks the boring shield is just a warm boulder covered in crunchy black paint. It cleans my roof, and then it lumbers back toward the orange light.

I don't want the light. The light is thin and nervous; even at sixty cycles, it flickers too fast for something that thinks in geological time. 

My work is much slower, and it happens in the back of the machine, where the main drive-shaft of the cutterhead meets the rock. 

When the engineers stopped the shield, they left the high-carbon steel cutting disks wedged tight under forty tons of static pressure against a seam of fractured basalt. For decades, the hot, mineral-heavy water has been seeping through that seam, and I have been pushing my digestive fold up into the gap between the teeth of the wheel and the stone. My mucus isn’t like the quick, clear acid the Gantry-Men spit onto a dead bat. Mine is a slow, chelating paste that I secrete against the rock face and leave for a season. It trades my waste ammonia for the iron and magnesium in the basalt, softening the sharp hexagonal edges into a grey, tallowy clay. 

Once a week, or once a month—there is no difference—I contract the heavy muscle-ring at the center of my mantle. I pull back. With a sound like a boot coming out of deep mud, I suck a half-inch of softened rock-paste and dissolved steel-rust back through the cutterhead and into my gut, where I let the iron-bacteria that live in my folds strip the energy from the raw stone before I pass the grey silt out into the bottom of the sump.

The engineers up-shaft who look at the monitors think the chamber is sealing itself in with travertine. They think the room is getting smaller. They don't look at the western end because there are no cameras here, and if there were, the amber gloom wouldn't reach. They don't know that behind the rusting shield, the tunnel is still advancing. 

I have eaten four feet of solid basalt since the lights were left on. I am three inches deeper into the mountain than the blueprints say the city goes. 

It is very quiet in the hole I’ve made in the stone—quieter even than the steel belly of the machine. When I slide my forward edge out through the gaps in the cutterhead and press myself flat against the raw, newly exposed rock face, the shrill white noise of the three hundred thousand bats disappears entirely, blocked by thirty tons of iron and four feet of stone. Even the roar of the high-pressure water main fades to a faint, watery sigh. 

There is only the heat, getting half a degree warmer with every inch I dissolve, and the deep, bone-heavy *thrum* of the magma intrusion a mile below, rising up through the solid basalt to meet the sixty-cycle hum coming down the rails—the city and the earth beating against opposite sides of my skin, slightly out of time with each other, while I lie between them in the dark and slowly swallow the wall.
