If you walk the valley with a human body, your knees will ruin themselves within an hour. 

You will step down toward what looks like a flat apron of black silt six inches below your boot, and your heel will strike solid stone three inches *above* the plane your eyes promised you, jarring your spine up to the base of your skull. Or you will reach out to steady yourself against a waist-high hummock of bog-myrtle and find your arm plunged to the shoulder in forty-degree water that shouldn't begin for another ten yards. Your inner ear will revolt. The sideways pewter light and the twin-pointed shadows will strip away your sense of verticality until you are reduced to crawling on all fours through the grease-veined marigolds, vomiting thin, cold bile into the square plates of moss while the boulders ring your teeth like tuning forks.

To exist here without being shaken apart by the valley's grammar, you cannot have a single, rigid frame. You cannot insist on being in only one place at a time, and you certainly cannot insist on being a *person*.

If I am here, I am down in the wet dark between the plates.

Look at the borders where the square patches of copper-colored moss slide against one another. As they collide, forcing up those little ridges of dying green that turn immediately to black slime, there is a narrow, shifting trough of exposed peat and oil-heavy water. Down in that trough, riding the slick, anaerobic mud just above the humming bedrock, is a ribbon of something that looks like wet, smoky quartz or a spilled rope of frog-spawn, three fingers wide and twelve feet long, though you will never see more than six inches of it at once.

The rest of me is tucked into the micro-faults—the tiny, stepped ledger-lines where the valley’s geological sentence stutters and repeats a syllable.

I am a *Tally-Leach*, though like the Draggers, there is no tongue left in the basin to wrap around the syllables. I do not have eyes; eyes are for things that trust light to travel in straight lines. Instead, my entire dorsal surface is a long, continuous tympanum of tough, translucent gel that reads the shear-stress of the mud and the electrical bleed of the flora. 

My world is not visual. It is a dense, overlapping map of tensions, pitches, and sour tastes.

Right now, three yards to my left—which is also four inches above my tail-tip, because the shelf I am threaded through is folded like a carpenter’s rule—a boulder the size of a haywagon is building toward a phase-correction. I feel it long before the Draggers do. The silicate lattice inside the rock is tightening, drawing a thin, sharp whine out of the surrounding peat as the local gravity well steepens by a fraction of a percent. The water in the braided channel above me grows heavier, its viscosity thickening from chilled oil to warm tar. 

I do not brace against it. I *thin* myself. I pour my middle sections backward through a thumb-wide seam in the gray shale, drawing my gelatinous bulk out of the blast radius and pooling it in a quiet, foreshortened pocket beneath a clump of skunk-cabbage fifty feet upslope. 

*Bong.*

The sound does not travel through me; it briefly *becomes* me. For a third of a second, my skin tessellates in sympathy with the mere-water, breaking into thousands of tiny, hexagonal dimples that click softly against the mud. A warm, chalky rain of shed silicate slurry filters down through the sedge roots, coating my mucus-layer in fresh mineral grit. 

This grit is what I am here for. It is my bone and my bread.

The boulders shed more than just stone when they correct themselves; they shed *old measurements*. The intelligence that rewrote the valley’s matter left its arithmetic in the rocks, and when a rock blurs and adjusts its density, that arithmetic sloughs off in the silicate dust as a high-energy, metastatic static. If it builds up in the silt, the valley floor begins to seize. The square moss-plates lock together instead of sliding; the water forgets how to flow uphill and pools into stagnant, crushing lenses that would drown the marsh.

So I eat the grit. I glide out from beneath the skunk-cabbage, flowing through the dead-zones where the Draggers have boiled the rushes gray and limp. Those newly dead patches are my highways. Because the Dragger’s feeding-skirt has shorted out the computational static in the roots, the mud beneath a grazed patch is quiet—blissfully, numbingly blank. Moving through it feels like slipping into cool linen after hours of being rubbed with dry wool.

I slide my anterior end—which is only the "front" because it is the part currently tasting the way forward—over the lip of a deep, round post-hole left by a Dragger’s stuttering leg. The hole is brimming with clear, heavy water and a thick sediment of fresh silicate paste. A few Seam-Mites, their mica-shells glinting dully in the pewter light, are already scrambling around the rim, waiting for the residual spatial crease at the bottom of the print to pop shut and spit out their newly hatched young.

I do not care about the mites. I sink my length into the post-hole, pressing my ventral surface against the cold, humming paste at the bottom. 

As I absorb the silicate slurry, my body does what it was grown to do: it *sums* the dust. Inside my translucent jelly, the gray mineral grains do not dissolve. They align. Pulled by my weak internal current, they arrange themselves into long, delicate filaments of metallic silver-gray that run the length of my body like a spine made of mercury and iron filings. 

If you were to peel back the braiding rushes and look down into the post-hole, you would see me glowing—not with the sickly, greenish grease-light of the marigold veins, but with a crisp, cold, blue-white cherenkov flicker as two incompatible sets of physical laws meet inside my gut and cancel each other out. 

It feels like swallowing peppermint and snow. It feels like a cramped muscle finally letting go.

I stay in the footprint until the water above me begins to shiver—the warning tremor of a suspended rain-lattice six inches overhead losing its surface tension. Before the column of water can drop like a wet sack and flatten the sedges, I have already poured myself sideways through a hairline fracture in the peat, emerging ten yards away beneath the ropy, black holdfasts of a Joiner colony.

This is where I go when I am heavy with silver.

The Joiner’s roots smell fiercely of vinegar, ozone, and the high, thin air of the cliff-rim two miles away. Its low vents are whistling a flat, minor third today, which means the mist at the far end of the valley is pressing down hard against the shale. The black, horn-like mucilage the Joiner uses to stitch the spatial tears is brittle on its own; left to the forty-degree damp, it would crack under the torque of the folding cliffs within a season.

I coil my twelve-foot length around the base of the Joiner’s thickest root, right at the lip of the three-inch gap in the air where the colony emerges from the cliff-face two miles off. Looking down into that gap is dizzying even for a thing without eyes—I can feel the sheer, empty drop of the waterfall fifty yards to the left of the cliff-top pulling at my skin, trying to suck my fluid bulk out into the open sky high above the mire. 

I anchor my tail around a tough, woody stem of bog-myrtle, press my sides against the Joiner’s weeping black horn, and I *discharge*.

The metallic filaments of summed silicate inside me liquefy and sweat out through my skin in a sudden, milky cloud. The Joiner shudders—a long, muscular ripple that starts here in the marsh mud and finishes two miles away in the high mist, shaking a shower of loose shale into a plunge-pool I will never reach. The milky silicate bonds instantly with the Joiner’s wet mucilage, turning the brittle black horn into something like flexible, gunmetal steel. The frayed edges of the spatial seam pull tight, cinched together by a fresh weld of stone-math and fungus. 

My body goes completely clear again, empty and light as rainwater.

For a long while afterward, I do not move. I lie coiled in the warm, vinegar-scented pocket beneath the Joiner's whistling vent, listening to the valley reset itself around the newly tightened seam. A hundred yards out, the gaunt, powder-gray heron strikes at the mud, its neck omitting the cold air between desire and fulfillment. Above me, a chalk-white eel slips from one hanging curtain of rain to the next, its belly brushing the top of the sedges. 

I have no memory of what the valley was before it lost its place, and no curiosity about what lies beyond the slumped gray lip of the rim. I only know that the rock is about to ring again, three shelves down and half a mile through the glass, and that when the dust settles, there will be work to do in the dark between the moss.
