I am not in the mud, and I am not in the Crankcase. I do not have the weight for the bottom or the teeth for the middle. 

You will find me four feet off the ground, where the steam from the brass blowholes hits the freezing pewter air and refuses to rise any higher. You have to look in the seams—not the flesh-seams where the Graft stitches flank to flank, because those are hot and loud and full of the Load’s heavy, slamming 4/4. Look instead at the air-seams. Look at the edges of those hanging lenses where the atmosphere has sheared loose from the world.

When a strider’s bone-boom swings through a lens, its hook is in Tuesday and its shoulder is in November. The Ganger can weld the meat back together when that happens, slamming the sparking nerve-cables into the *now*, but the Ganger is sloppy. It is a heavy industry. It leaves burrs. It leaves little curly shavings of displaced sequence, cold droplets of unspent torque, and long, greasy threads of *almost-happened* hanging in the mist like Spanish moss.

That is where I live. I am the thing that eats the burrs.

If you were standing ankle-deep in the black brine, looking through a sheared lens at the back of your own head thirty yards away, you would see me only as a smudge of tallow on the glass. I am the size of a butcher’s apron, and built much the same way: a flat, boneless mantle of pale, waxy chamois that has been soaked in mineral oil until it is nearly translucent. I have no iron in me. Iron is too loud; the Load hears iron and yanks it into line, and I do not want to be in line. My ribs are thin, flexible baleen, like the stays of an old corset, and around my margins are three hundred tiny, silent clasps made of horn and vulcanized gum.

I do not walk. I *drape*. 

Right now, I am stretched across the eight-inch gap between the vibrating cast-iron buttress of a dormant boiler-whale and the edge of a bad Tuesday. Two of my horn-clasps are hooked into the whale’s shingled hide, drinking its slow, low-grade pilot-heat; my other edge is pinned right into the sheared air itself, hooked onto the hard, refractive lip of a lens that shows the interior of a siphon-worm’s gizzard two miles to the east.

I am a **Baffle**. Or a **Felt**. Or a **Shunt-Rag**. 

My whole body is an ear, and my whole life is spent trying to make the basin quieter by half a decibel.

You cannot imagine how much it hurts to be a creature made of drum-skin in a thirty-mile foundry. Every time the Load marches through this quadrant and slams ten thousand valves shut on the downbeat, the shockwave hits my mantle and tries to tear me in half. If I let myself vibrate at the Load’s frequency, I will harden; my baleen will calcify, my tallow will render into solder, and the next time a Ganger is born from the mud, I will be yanked down into the heap to become the leather washer inside its left knee. 

To stay soft, I have to stay out of time.

I do this by eating the skipped beats. Look at the dredge-crawler ten yards below me, the one crushing the valve-crabs that un-crush themselves in its wake. Between the *crunch* of their brass springs breaking and the wet *suck* of time pulling them back together, there is a hairline fracture of absolute silence. A zero-point where the forward gear and the reverse gear strip each other’s teeth. 

I slide down the whale’s flank like warm lard, trailing my fringe of horn-clasps through that fracture. I hook the silence. I pull it up like a long, grey noodle of coagulated static and draw it under my skirt. 

Underneath me—in the two-inch pocket of space between my greasy belly and the wet iron of the whale—it is completely still. I have lined my underside with the quietest things in the basin. I have collected the shed ear-bones of blind eels; I have scraped the pale, dead-blue rime from the slate rim and chewed it with my gum-clasps until it lost its geometric circuit-snap and became a dull, leaden putty. I smear this putty over the raw, sparking spots where the Graft’s stitches have frayed. 

The scuttlers—those wolf-sized sewing machines—hate me, or they would hate me if they had the equipment for it. When a scuttler comes clicking up the canyon between two beasts, trailing its own tendon-guts to make a splice, its zinc feelers are hunting for the loud, ragged edge of a broken rhythm. If I have gotten there first, it finds nothing. I have already draped myself over the cracked drill-housing or the blown gasket. I am drinking the rattle. My waxy body swells and dimples as I take the wild, off-beat kicking of a dying piston and damp it down into a slow, worthless heat, bleeding the energy off into a hanging lens so that the vibration comes out three miles away and four weeks ago as a harmless shiver on a puddle of brine.

The scuttler’s feelers brush right over my back. Because I am matching the ambient temperature of the sleet and the exact oil-smell of the whale’s skin, the scuttler thinks I am just a patch of dead blubber. It moves on, leaving the broken worm un-stitched.

Why do I do it? I don't know what a "why" is any more than the Crankcase does. I am just the shape that grows in the shadow of the work. If the whole basin is an engine that settled into its lowest energy state, I am the tiny, parasitic pocket that settled slightly lower still. I am the grease that wants to stop being hot.

Sometimes, there are others like me. When the Load is very heavy in the south, and the northern flats are left to clatter in their own chaotic, local rhythms, the air-lenses up here grow wide and slack, hanging like laundry between the herds. Then, the Baffles come out of the crevices. Dozens of us, pale and flat and dripping with yellow synovial oil, crawling up the cantilevered bone-booms of the striders while they hiccup back and forth. 

We hook our horn-clasps into each other’s margins. We form a tent—a wide, sagging canopy of wet chamois and baleen stretched between four or five striders and a few low-hanging slabs of sheared sky. 

Inside that tent, for ten minutes—or for forty years, depending on how deep the puddle underneath us is—the foundry goes away. The deafening *chug-thump* is muffled to a distant, womb-like murmur. The sleet cannot get in. The striders beneath us still swing their hooks and splice their eels, because they don't know how to stop, but because we have eaten the static out of their spines and muffled the brass click of their joints, they do it *slowly*. Their yellow bones move through the dark air like sleepers turning over in bed. The valve-crabs underneath us lose the 4/4 beat; their mandibles slow down to a lazy, irregular *tick... tick........ tick*, until they simply tuck their brass legs under their shells and sit in the warm silt, doing nothing at all.

It never lasts. Silence has weight here—it shows up on the Crankcase’s great, blind ledger as a dead spot, a drop in oil pressure, a missing tooth on the continent-wide gear.

Down in the twenty-foot dark, the Crankcase feels our tent as a cold patch on its skin. A quatre-mile tendon tightens in the deep muck. The magnetic pull Toward-Which-Things-Fall shifts by three degrees. 

Above us, the pewter sky curdles. The mud begins to boil. The crabs under our tent are ripped out from beneath our margins as the magnetism wakes up, their shells cracking as they are dragged into a pile to birth a Ganger. We hear the first high, piercing blast of its brass gills—*Hurry. Stroke.*—and we do not wait for the shears. 

We unhook our clasps. Our canopy splits into thirty separate rags of pale leather. I let go of the strider’s bone and drop straight sideways through a sheared lens of air, falling fifty feet down a slope that isn't there, until I land with a wet, silent *slap* against the cold slate at the very rim of the basin.

Here, where the rock meets the mire, I flatten myself into the grey mud until I am no thicker than a coat of paint. Two miles away, the Ganger’s blue furnace-heart flares in the twilight, and the striders slam back into their hard, sparking rhythm. The sleet jumps two inches in the air. The pale rime all around me shatters and reforms in a blinding, dead-blue fractal.

I pull my edges in. I tuck my horn-clasps under my belly. I hold a tiny, stolen bead of last Tuesday's quiet underneath my tongue, and I wait for the next gear to slip.
