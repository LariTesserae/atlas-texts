I am not in the water. To be in the water is to belong to the twelve-minute clock—to the panic of the silver shrimp, the hiding of the oil-eels, the heavy, copper-blooded ecstasy of the monarch riding its scalding fountain. Anything in the water is eventually digested by the gyre.

Instead, I am sixty inches above the floor-grates, tucked into the crescent-shaped shadow where an intake pipe meets the curved bronze hull. 

At first glance, you would take me for an old repair—a clumsy gobbeting of lead solder and mineral slag wept from a cracked seam during the spire’s third or fourth century. I am no larger than a closed fist, asymmetrical, heavy, and motionless. My outer casing is not grown; it is accumulated. Over three hundred years, I have cemented together a dome of fallen rust-flakes, the shed, iron-hard teeth from the monarch’s rasping tongue, and six tiny, hexagonal brass nuts outgrown and discarded by the young valve-crabs. 

I am a blind, sessile filter-feeder—a cousin to the barnacle and the tube-worm, though my ancestors forgot the sea before the continents finished drying. I have no eyes to see the golden nets of stratospheric light, and no foot to wander the catwalks. My body is a tight, muscular knot of pale gold tissue anchored permanently to the bronze wall by a foot of calcified glue, sitting directly over a hairline fracture in the hull plate.

That fracture is my reason for being here. 

Through the microscopic gap in the bronze, the outside world leaks in. Not the air—the internal pressure of the water sphere is far too high for the thin stratospheric freeze to push through—but the *cold*. While the rest of the chamber stews in its blood-warm bath, the metal beneath my base is perpetually chilled by the minus-sixty-degree gale screaming through the external lattice of the spire. 

I am a creature of the thermal gradient. My bottom half is cold; my top half is hot. I do not eat the shrimp or the moss; I generate my tiny, slow-burning life from the sharp electrical potential between the freezing hull-plate under my foot and the scalding mineral water washing over my crown. 

Because I am cemented to the hull’s seam, I am part of the bell. 

When the *thrum-clack* rises from the drop-piping below, it does not pass through me as a soft, watery wave the way the monarch feels it. It rings through my calcified base as a sharp, crystalline shear. I know the state of the spire better than the great mollusk does. I can feel the hairline fatigue in the iron struts three hundred feet down; I can feel the high-altitude wind leaning against the eastern face of the tower, making the whole skeletal lattice bow by a fraction of a millimeter, tightening the rivets around my shell with a tiny, high-pitched *ping*.

For eleven minutes and twenty seconds, my shell is slightly parted at the apex, just enough to extend a single, stiff fan of black-and-copper filaments into the lazy clockwise current. I am not catching food; I am catching chemistry. As the water drifts past, my fan strips out dissolved copper ions and sulfur, pulling them down into my cold base to slowly plate my inner lining, thickening my shell against the slow scouring of the gyre.

Sometimes, the shadow passes over me. 

I cannot see the monarch, but I feel the sudden drop in water pressure as its heavy, velvet apron glides inches above my head. Occasionally, it stops. The great creature’s ventral foot settles over me, sealing me in total darkness. I pull my black fan inside and lock my soldered plates shut. Above me, I feel the wet, metallic *scritch-scritch* of its magnetite tongue rasping the bronze hull, vibrating right through my roof. 

It scrapes away the green velvet algae that threatens to smother my breathing-slit. It scrapes right up to the edges of my cemented brass hex-nuts, testing their hold. When it was smaller, centuries ago, it tried to pry me loose, mistaking me for a crust of limestone scale. It broke three of its iron teeth on my lead-and-brass dome before giving up; I built those very teeth into my eastern wall the following week. Now, it knows my shape. It cleans my roof, lingers for a moment—perhaps feeling the strange, pin-point chill radiating through my shell from the hull-crack below—and then heaves itself away.

Then comes the slackening.

I feel it three seconds before the monarch does. Before the cable even loosens in the deep pipe, the upward tension on the hull plates shifts. The microscopic crack beneath my foot pinches shut by a ten-thousandth of an inch. 

I do not flush copper. I simply draw my black filament-fan deep into my gut and clench my lead-weighted operculum shut, sealing myself with a plug of thick, metallic grease. 

*Boom.*

The sluice opens. The tempest hits.

While the monarch rockets toward the ceiling in ecstasy, while the shrimp tumble and the kelp snaps taut, I sit in the roaring dark of my own little skull. The forty-second geyser slams hot, turbulent eddies against my shell. The temperature of my outer casing spikes; the cold crack beneath my foot stays freezing. 

For forty seconds, the thermal differential across my two-inch body doubles. 

Inside my dark shell, in the quiet between the deafening roar of the check-valves, I wake up completely. The sudden spike in heat-flow kicks my slow, mineral metabolism into a brief, frantic sprint. In that forty-second window, I do all my living: I knit new layers of bronze-infused nacre onto my inner walls, I mend the micro-fractures in my glue, and I digest the rich, enzymatic mucus the monarch blasts into the water—just a few stray molecules of it that seep past my grease-plug, carrying the chemical news of the chamber's health.

*Thud.*

The valve slams. The roar dies. The hot water begins to cool back to blood-warmth.

Slowly, as the amber snow of rust and scale settles back down through the shaft of sunlight, I relax my muscular knot. My grease-plug pops free. My little black-and-copper fan unfolds from the slit in my soldered dome, testing the quiet, clockwise drift. A single flake of rusted iron lands on my lip; using two tiny, blind cilia, I tuck it into the mortar of my wall, cementing it in place with a drop of lime. 

The turbine shudders. *Thrum-clack. Thrum-clack.* 

I hold the seam together, cold on the bottom, warm on the top, listening to the iron miles below, keeping the sky out.
