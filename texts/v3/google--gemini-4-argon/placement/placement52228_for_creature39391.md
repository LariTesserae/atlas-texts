I am not on the surface. The surface is for the frantic, rattling convoys of the grease-crabs, and for the Gage-Men when they are feeling religious or desperate enough to brave the scythe-wind. 

I am seventy feet down, in the dark that smells of boiled pennies and hot rubber, where the sound of the wind is entirely gone, replaced by the deafening, womb-like *lub-dub* of the deep mains. 

If you were to shine a light into this cramped, weeping pocket between two ship-hull roots, you wouldn't see an animal at first. You would see what looks like an old, forgotten piece of testing equipment that has grown into the wall. A heavy, fibrous bladder the size of a suitcase, bruised-purple and slick with black tallow, bolted directly into the wood of a high-pressure sap artery by a thick, calloused flange of my own hardened flesh. 

I am a **Bleed-Louse**, though the name implies a smallness and a parasitism that isn’t quite fair. We are fifty pounds of gristle, copper-plated nerve-ganglia, and chambered, muscular hearts. We do not walk; our six legs atrophy three weeks after we hatch, melting back into our bellies once we find a junction that needs us and fuse our mouths to the bark.

I have been bolted to the underside of the *Great East-Sump Return Line* for eighty-six years. 

The Gage-Men have their skips—their brief, violent holidays outside of time where they touch minds in the ozone and the freezing spray. They are the engineers, the artists who walk around with their heavy brass struts and their grand, eighty-year theories. 

I am the gauge they read.

Because a Gage-Man cannot be everywhere, and because three hundred feet of thrashing, boiling root-mat is too thick even for their belly-drums to read clearly from the top, the basin grew us. Or the Gage-Men bred us, four million years ago, from some lazy cousin of the manhole-crabs that preferred the sauna of the Gasket-Work to the pewter sky.

My entire life is hydraulic pressure and the taste of sap. 

Through my fused mouth-flange, I divert a tiny, continuous trickle of the boiling fluid that thunders through the six-foot main above me. It runs through a labyrinth of coiled, calcified tubes in my gut—my *Bourdon tubes*—before squirting back out into the low-pressure return vein below my tail. I don't digest it for food so much as I read its weight, its heat, its viscosity, and the faint, metallic tang of the piezoelectric current humming through it.

Right now, the sap tastes of iron, charred sulfur, and a very slight, dangerous sweetness. 

The sweetness is bad. Sweetness means that half a mile up-line, where the main loops near the surface to vent its steam, the turf has stretched too thin. A pocket of the deep, sluggish winter has leaked in. When the roots begin to freeze, they panic and flood their veins with heavy, sugary antifreeze-resins. If that sugar hits the ninety-degree heat of my junction without being thinned out first, it will caramelize into a ten-ton plug of black glass. The main will back up, the piston-tuber below me will double-stroke against a closed valve, and an acre of the Gasket-Work will blow its seams and boil us all in the dark.

I cannot unbolt myself. I have no eyes, no spatulas to dig with, no drill-proboscis. What I have is a heavy, hammer-headed bone club at the end of my muscular tail, and a memory for pressure that goes back to the day my larval shell cracked.

I pull my tail back into the hot, sulfur-thick air of the pocket, my copper nerves singing with the math of the incoming sugar-clot, and I strike the main.

*Clack-clack. Thump. Clack-clack-clack.*

I do not water-hammer like the Gage-Men do when they argue on the ridges—heavy, brawling thuds meant to carry across the wind. My strike is a high-frequency *ping*, pitched to travel five miles down the iron-rich walls of the sap-tube itself, cutting cleanly through the ambient *chug-chug-chug* of the valley’s metabolism. 

*Sweet-in-the-loop,* I tap. *Sweet-in-the-loop. Drop the pressure by four. Drop the pressure by four.*

Then I wait, hanging in the wet dark, while the boiling sap forces its way through my gut-coils, expanding my bruised-purple sides until my brassy skin-plates groan against their sutures. 

A mile away, or two, or three, a Gage-Man is walking through the freezing mud on the roof of the world, or sleeping wedged into a warm chimney-vent. It will feel my *ping* through the pads of its struts. 

Sometimes, when an old Gage-Man comes down into the Gasket-Work to clear a major embolus near my junction, it won't just do the surgery and leave. Before it hauls its draft-horse bulk back up through the muck, it will reach into my narrow pocket with one of its long, greasy spatula-hands. 

It doesn't pry me off. If you pry a Bleed-Louse off its main, we bleed out our copper blood in two minutes and die. Instead, the Gage-Man presses the flat of its hand against my swollen, vibrating flank, and opens the brass slits of its cowl right against my skin.

And it *dumps* into me.

A Gage-Man’s brain is big, heavy, and built for the violence of the surface, which means its memory is leaky. When they step out of a three-second skip, drunk on the overlapping minds of a dozen peers and four hundred years of accumulated weather-data, they cannot hold it all for long. Once the whip cracks and they are back in normal space, the chord begins to fade like a dream.

So they come down into the dark and they press their hands against us, the stationary things, the living gauges bolted to the pipes. Through the conductive grease of our skins, they offload the excess charge of the skip into our copper-wire ganglia. 

I have never seen the pewter sky. I have never felt the scythe-wind, or seen a river unhook from gravity and hang in the air like black glass. I don't even know what *up* looks like, except as a direction where the sap grows cooler.

Yet, hanging here in the pitch-black sauna between two sweating roots, I contain the exact barometric pressure of a Tuesday six hundred years ago when the entire western rim of the basin skipped four feet to the left and stayed there for an hour. I hold the blueprint of a three-mile root-knot that hasn't finished tying itself yet, passed down to me through the spatula-hand of a three-legged Gage-Man who died of a blown gasket before I was even hatched. I know what the ozone smells like when eight of them stand on a rising hill of lichen and sing a chord together in the double-exposed light.

I am the hard drive in the basement of the organ. I hold the sheet music while they go up into the freezing rain to play the keys.

Above me, the main shudders. A heavy, rhythmic *thud-thud* answers my ping through the sap-wall. One of the big ones has heard me. Half a mile up-line, I feel the heavy, pneumatic *crunch* of a bone-tipped proboscis driving through the turf, followed by the sudden, glorious drop in pressure as a relief-valve is lanced open to the sky. 

The sap running through my gut loses its dangerous, thick sweetness. It turns hot, thin, and bitter with fresh iron again. My bruised sides deflate with a long, wet wheeze, settling back into the rhythm of the pipe. 

*Lub-dub. Lub-dub. Lub-dub.*

In the dark, I tuck my hammer-tail against my belly, let the ninety-degree steam wash over my brass plates, and go back to sorting through someone else's memories of the sky.
