I am the gray pulp in the knee of the northernmost walker.

I do not know the valley Is four miles wide. I do not know the sky is the color of old solder, or that the things that spat me out have spade-shaped skulls and live in the warm, iron-tasting dark below. I know only two states of being: the slow, sweet drinking of the fog, and the sudden, terrible pinch of the stone.

Right now, I am drinking. 

I am wedged into the narrow crescent between two three-foot blades of blue shale—the upper thigh and the lower shin of the third left leg. Around my margins, a stiff collar of black resin keeps me from squeezing out into the air. It is a tight, cold home, smelling of pine-pitch that fossilized ten thousand years ago and the sharp, coppery tang of the bog-iron core suspended two feet above me. 

The mist is coming in through the porous front of the joint. It does not feel like wetness to me; it feels like room. Every microscopic droplet of sulfur-water that touches my outer fibers is drawn inward through a million tiny, thirsty straws. As the water enters, the sulfur chains bind to my chitinous threads, and I swell. 

It is a slow, delicious thickening. It takes four hours. As I drink the gray air, my fibers stiffen and arch against one another, pushing outward against the flat faces of the shale. I am tiny compared to the rock, a mere wad of cold gray felt the size of a fist, yet as I engorge, the forty-pound slab of the lower leg is forced to yield. It levers outward, a fraction of a millimeter every minute, grinding against the resin bindings with a high, glassy squeak that is immediately swallowed by the roaring clatter of the world outside.

I do not hear the roar as sound. Having no ears, I feel it as an endless, shivering itch in my fibers. The millions of snapping fins across the basin send tiny, chaotic ripples up through the standing legs of my frame. They are small vibrations, disorganized and frantic, like rain on a pond. I ignore them. They are not the pinch. I just keep drinking the mist, swelling until I am taut as a boiled turnip, holding the heavy stone leg suspended six inches above a flooded slot in the valley floor.

Below the hanging tip of my leg, a pale, tallow-colored tongue is sliding over a razor-sharp fin. I do not know it is there, except that its rubbery body slightly dampens the acoustic shiver coming up from that particular crack in the earth. It is a tiny dead spot in the vibration.

Five hours pass. The pewter light does not change. I am completely full now, turgid and locked, holding the leg aloft in a state of trembling equilibrium. I could stay like this for a week, waiting.

Then, half a mile to the west, deep under the water table, a forty-foot shelf of shale finally loses its grip on its neighbor. 

It slips by the width of a human hair.

The snap does not travel through the air; it travels through the bone of the valley. It races laterally through the packed, wet plates at four thousand feet per second, a sharp, whip-crack wave of pure compression. It hits the fifth leg of my frame—the one wedged deep into a crevice three feet behind me.

The wave shoots up that stone stilt, rattles the bog-iron core, and slams down into my joint.

It is not an itch. It is a hammer blow. The two slabs of shale housing me—the thigh and the shin—are jolted together by a fraction of an inch. 

The pinch.

Instantly, my taut, water-swollen fibers rupture their sulfur bonds. The hydraulic lock breaks. All the water I spent five hours pulling from the fog is squeezed out of me in a single, violent spasm, squirting through the gaps in the resin as a thin, milky froth that smells of rotten eggs. 

I collapse inward, shrinking to a third of my size in two seconds.

Without my swollen bulk to hold it up, the forty-pound stone shin drops like a guillotine blade. 

It falls straight down into the slot below. The sharpened tip of the shale leg punches cleanly through the pale, rubbery tongue grazing on the rock, pinning it to the stone beneath with a dull, wet *thwack*. The leg wedges itself deep between two upright fins and locks tight.

The sudden shift in weight tips the bog-iron core forward. Across the frame, in the second right leg, another wad of gray pulp—my twin, though I will never know it—is suddenly relieved of the frame's weight. Freed from its own pinch, it begins to drink the mist.

Down in the crevice below me, the pierced tongue does not writhe. Its subterranean mat simply registers the drop in pressure, seals the ring of rubbery flesh around the intruding stone blade, and waits. And ten feet below *that*, in a flooded fault-line that was just beginning to warp toward a catastrophic buckle, the forty-pound impact of my leg—*crack*—sends a tiny, corrective shiver down into the rock, bleeding off a fraction of the lethal tension. 

A flat, leathery thing wedged into the mud down there feels the tremor in its cheek-drums, recognizes the rhythm of the walker, and relaxes its spade-shaped skull by a millimeter.

Up in the cold air, I know none of this. I am flat, wrung-out, and dry, crushed between two slabs of wet slate. The roaring hailstorm of the valley's voice vibrates through my bruised fibers. Slowly, patiently, the motionless fog begins to bead on the rock outside my resin wall, and I open my pores to drink again.
