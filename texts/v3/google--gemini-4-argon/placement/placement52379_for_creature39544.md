I would not be the hound, though I envy its heavy, greased warmth and the way it sleeps inside a bass note. Nor would I be the skate, flickering between mineral and meat in a ninety-second panic. And I have no stomach for the Mason’s blind, scissor-jawed wait in the dark wood.

To live here and actually know the place, you have to be part of the boundary where the stone meets the howl. 

Find a room four levels down on the eastern terrace—not a grand plaza, and not one of the deep, booming cellars, but a narrow, crooked vestibule where three flues converge. The stone here is thin, worn down to a translucent rind no thicker than a teacup, glowing with that diffused, milky ultraviolet light. 

Look at the lintel where the draft-hound presses its blunt paw to drop the room’s pitch from F-sharp to C. 

I am that fist-sized bulge of blue-black lichen on the doorframe. 

To a human eye, I am upholstery. A dry, leathery crust, scalloped at the edges like charred paper, clinging to the exact curve where the waxy soapstone is strangled by a knuckle of fused willow-root. I have no eyes, no legs, no heart. I have been in this single four-inch pocket of screaming air for three hundred years, and I have grown perhaps the width of a thumb in a century. 

My life is divided into two states: the Ringing, and the Gulp.

Ninety-five percent of my existence is the Ringing. In the dry hours, I pull myself tight against the soapstone, my fungal cortex hardening into a brittle, slate-colored horn. I do not sleep; I *vibrate*. Because I am anchored across the joint of stone and wood, I am the bridge between two different speeds of sound. Through my lower rhizines, I feel the deep, tectonic thrumming of the bedrock—the long, slow waves of the mountain's bass notes. Through my upper lobes, I take the violent, shearing shriek of the F-sharp wind-tunnel. 

I do not think in words or images. I think in tension. My entire body is a continuous, complex chord of mechanical stress. I know the exact hour of the afternoon not by the light, which my dark pigments drink and turn into a tiny, hoarding warmth, but by the way the sun heats the willow-roof overhead, expanding the wood by a fraction of a millimeter and sharpening the pitch of the doorway by three cents. 

I know the draft-hounds better than they know themselves. They are brief, hot ghosts that pass through my century. I have felt forty generations of them press their greasy pads against my back to break the cyclone in the vestibule. When a hound leans on me, my stiff, dry thallus cracks microscopically—a sharp, delicious agony that releases a puff of my reproductive dust into its dense wool. The hound gets its midges; I get a courier. It will carry my spores three terraces over and drop them into a new flute when it shakes itself dry. We have an arrangement, though only one of us is slow enough to remember it.

And then, the chord changes. 

The wind drops an octave. The sky goes silver, and the freezing cloud spills into the basin. 

If you have never been a crust of dehydrated fungus hit by high-altitude fog, you cannot understand the violence of the Gulp. It is not like drinking; it is like being electrocuted by water. 

Within four seconds of the first mist touching my skin, my brittle, horn-like armor softens into rubber, then into wet velvet. The millions of microscopic pores across my surface tear themselves open. My weight triples. The blue-black armor turns a deep, translucent, oily olive, allowing the sudden white light of the fog to reach the hidden prisoners inside me—the thin, bright green layer of algae I keep trapped within my fungal mesh. 

For ten minutes, while the willow-vents clack like castanets and the skates ripple across the floor below me like living grease, I am in a state of ecstatic, silent mania. My algae gorge on the milky light and the meltwater, pumping out thin, sweet sugars. My fungal threads drink the sugar and weave feverishly, repairing the micro-fractures left by the hound’s heavy paw, pushing a single, microscopic hypha a thousandth of an inch deeper into a flaw in the soapstone, prying a single grain of talc loose to make room for next year's growth.

I feel the Masons drop past me—sharp, tiny footsteps ticking against my swollen lobes like needles as they drop into the gutter to harvest the worms. Sometimes a Mason’s chisel-foot slips and tears a ragged ribbon from my side. I do not mind. The torn piece blows down the street, lodges in a dry corner, and becomes me, somewhere else. 

Then the cloud passes. The blue sky snaps back like a cold whip. 

Within sixty seconds, the screaming draft licks the moisture from my skin. The velvet hardens back into slate. The sugars freeze in the veins. The algae go dark behind my black rind. The skates vanish into the grain of the floor; the Masons retreat into their wooden stops.

The vestibule returns to its clean, empty F-sharp shriek. 

I settle back into the stone, a small, dark, stubborn knot in the throat of the pipe, holding my tiny fraction of a millimeter of new territory, humming with the mountain, waiting for the heavy, warm paw to come back and change the key.
