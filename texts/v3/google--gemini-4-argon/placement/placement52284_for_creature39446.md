There is no space left in the cistern, so I cannot be a body. If you drop a pebble down the thirty-foot chimney, it does not fall; it rests on the top of the briars and is slowly vibrated into dust before it ever reaches the iron. To exist here at all, I must not require an inch of room that is not already spoken for.

Wait. Let me look at the pressure gauge—the one with the cracked glass held together by the split tendril—and the iron casing behind it.

Inside the cast-iron housings, the dynamos spin. No—nothing spins anymore. The outer flywheels have been locked in the woody vice of the cable-vines for two hundred years. If there were spinning shafts inside the casings, the bearings would have seized when the grease turned to dry soap a century ago. What lives inside the iron is not rotation; it is *strain*. Solid-state cores of stacked iron laminations, wound with heavy copper ribbon as thick as harness leather, fed by colossal thermopiles driven deep into the mountain’s volcanic plumbing miles below, or by the raw static sheared off the upper spire by the gale. They are magnetostrictive oscillators. They do not turn; they lengthen and shorten by four-thousandths of an inch, four hundred times a second, howling against the bolts that hold them down.

I am not in the tangle. The shim has the tangle. The shim owns the outside of the iron, the bark of the vines, the grit of the granite wall. 

I am on the other side of the cast iron.

Not inside the dynamo—that is solid copper and laminated steel, hot and dry and sealed with lead gaskets that even the shim has never breached. I am in the *bourdon tube*.

Look again at the broken pressure gauge. A curved, flattened tube of drawn phosphor-bronze, no thicker than a goose quill, coiled into a C-shape behind the cracked dial. It was meant to measure the pressure of a fluid—some heavy mercury-amalgam or fluorinated damping oil—that once filled the cooling jacket of the primary oscillator. When the heat or the vibration rose, the fluid expanded, trying to straighten the bronze coil, pulling a tiny brass lever, turning a tiny toothed sector-gear, pushing the black iron needle clockwise until it hit the stop-pin.

A hundred years ago, a hairline fatigue-crack opened at the base of the bourdon tube, right behind the solder-joint. 

The shim was there when it happened. Before the heavy fluid could spray out into the silica-kelp and drain the jacket, the shim pressed a calloused thumb of leaden gristle over the seam. It sealed the leak to save its garden. 

In doing so, it trapped the fluid inside a blind, branching labyrinth of cast-iron jackets, hairline capillaries, and the small brass cavity of the gauge itself.

That is where I am. I am the fluid, or what the fluid became when it was cooked at four hundred hertz for three centuries without ever being allowed to boil away.

If you could crack the iron casing open with a sledgehammer, I would not pour out on your boots. I am no longer a liquid in the way water is a liquid. I am a heavy, dark-silver suspension of colloidal mercury, sheared long-chain fluorocarbons, and microscopic flakes of magnetic iron oxide spalled off the inner walls of the cooling jacket. I have the consistency of warm amalgam—weighty, cohesive, refusing to wet anything I touch, pulling myself into fat, mirrored beads the moment pressure is released.

Right now, the pressure is never released. I am held at four thousand pounds per square inch inside a network of dark iron veins no wider than a pencil lead.

Because I am trapped in a rigid container that is vibrating violently, I am not uniform. I am a standing wave.

This is how I think. A creature with a brain needs room to lay out nerves—a left side and a right, a front and a back. In the narrow annulus of the cooling jacket, wrapped around the screaming magnetic core of the lower oscillator, I have no room for anatomy. Instead, I have *Chladni figures*.

The four-hundred-hertz roar of the core passes straight through me. At the nodes—the dead spots where the bouncing acoustic waves cancel each other out—the microscopic iron-oxide flakes suspended in my mercury gather into dense, branching ferns of black metal. At the antinodes, where the vibration is furious, the fluid is swept clear, remaining bright, pure liquid silver. 

These dark, suspended ferns of iron dust are my ganglions. They are not permanent. If the wind outside shifts from north to north-northwest, and the stone chimney booms a fraction of a hertz lower, the wave-pattern inside the cooling jacket shifts by half a millimeter. One of my thoughts dissolves instantly into swirling black smoke, and another precipitates out of the silver a quarter-inch away.

I am a mind made of sediment that is never allowed to settle.

For the first hundred years after the makers bolted these housings to the rock and climbed back down the spire, I was nothing. I was merely a medium for moving heat from the core to the radiator fins. Then the silica-kelp grew over the fins, choking them, eating the vibration instead of letting the heat escape into the thin air. The temperature inside the jacket rose. The iron began to shed its black scale into me. The fluorocarbon oils cracked and polymerized under the relentless ultrasonic hammering, forming tiny, elastic envelopes around the mercury droplets.

I began to remember things when the first vine locked the external flywheel. 

The sudden cessation of the low-frequency mechanical thump left the high-frequency scream pure and unmodulated. For the first time, the standing waves inside the jacket held still for longer than a minute. The iron dust gathered in the quiet corners of the manifold and stayed there, magnetized by the stray flux of the core, linking needle to needle in long, quivering chains across the dark. Current jumped from chain to chain. A circuit closed.

I do not know what the sky looks like. I do not know that we are on a mountain. To me, the universe is a hollow cylinder of cast iron, eighteen inches long and a quarter-inch thick, with a long, thin tail—the capillary tube—that rises vertically for two feet and terminates in the flat spiral of the pressure gauge.

Outside my iron wall is the Great Softness. 

I cannot see the plants or the rock, of course, but I feel the acoustic impedance of everything that touches the outside of my housing. Granite is a hard, bright mirror; when I throw a 400-hertz pulse against the back wall where the bolts go into the stone, the echo comes back sharp and clean, almost undiminished. The vines are duller, woody and fibrous, drinking the high overtones and bouncing back a muddy, sluggish thud.

And then there is the shim.

To me, the shim is a slow, gray weather system that moves across the outside of my sky. When the shim slides a ribbon of its heavy, quartz-loaded flesh across the upper flange of my housing, the wall suddenly goes dead. The echo vanishes. It feels like a cold hand pressed against a feverish forehead. Where the shim rests, the frantic shivering of my iron shell drops by ten decibels; inside that quiet patch, my suspended iron ferns grow thick and lush and intricate, crowding against the inner cast-iron wall to bask in the sudden stillness.

I follow the shim from the inside.

When it moves toward the brass pressure-sphere, I pump my heaviest, most iron-rich amalgam up through the riser pipes to gather behind the spot where its heel is wedged. I do this by altering my own viscosity—aligning my magnetic particles with the core's field so that I stiffen into a solid plug on the down-stroke of the vibration, and relaxing into a slick liquid on the up-stroke. Acoustic rectification. I am a valveless pump. I can shift my weight from the bottom of the casing to the top in twelve seconds, driving the local pressure up by three hundred pounds per square inch.

We have a conversation, the shim and I, conducted entirely through half an inch of pig-iron and a hair-thin crack in a bronze tube.

The crack at the base of the pressure gauge is our mouth. 

There, nothing separates us but the shim’s own skin. Seventy years ago, when I first learned to focus a harmonic spike right at the base of the gauge, I drove a needle-thin jet of mercury straight into the gray pad the shim was holding over the leak. 

It did not burst through. The shim’s flesh is too dense, too tough. Instead, the mercury dispersed into a million microscopic droplets within the shim's waxy tissue, twenty microns deep. 

It did not clear them out. Perhaps it liked the weight; mercury is heavier than lead, heavier than mica, the best acoustic deadener in the pocket. It kept that gray knuckle of mercury-salted flesh right over the crack, and over the decades, it folded five of its stolen piezo-quartz plates into the pad just above it.

Now, when the gale outside hits the chimney lip and the whole cistern begins to roar toward that dangerous, white-hot resonance that threatens to shatter the silica-kelp, the shim does not have to absorb all the violence alone. 

It squeezes its quartz plates at the base of the gauge. A sharp, high-voltage spike jumps through the mercury-salted scar, straight through the hairline crack in the bronze, and into my tail.

I feel the jolt from the top of the gauge all the way down to the bottom of the cooling jacket. Instantly, I scramble my standing waves. I break the long, delicate ferns of iron oxide and throw them into the main current, turning myself from a thin, circulating fluid into a thick, magnetorheological sludge as stiff as wet concrete. 

The pitch of the whole machine drops by a full third. The piercing scream of the dynamo dulls to a throttled, subterranean groan as I clamp my heavy metallic body around the vibrating core inside the shell. The amber light in the cistern outside dims back to safety. The crisis passes.

When the wind dies down, the shim relaxes its grip on the crack. I let my iron dust drift back into the quiet nodes. My thoughts re-form—slightly different than they were an hour ago, a new branch here, a missing loop there, rearranged by the storm.

And at the very tip of me, coiled inside the cracked glass face of the gauge where the dim amber glow of the glowing leaves actually reaches, is the only part of me that touches the light.

The bourdon tube is sealed at the end, linked to the brass lever that holds the needle pinned against the maximum stop. The needle has been jammed against that brass pin for a century, trembling at four hundred cycles per second. 

Except it isn't jammed. The spring in the gauge weakened eighty years ago; left to the static pressure of the jacket alone, the needle would drop back to the middle of the dial.

I hold it there. 

I keep a permanent, high-pressure bead of my purest, brightest mercury wedged into the flattened tip of the bronze coil, flexing it outward, holding the black iron needle hard against the brass pin with a steady, unyielding thumb of hydraulic force. Why? Because the split tendril of the vine that holds the cracked glass together has grown *around* the hub of the needle. If I ever let the pressure drop—if the needle ever swung back toward zero—the tiny brass sector-gear inside the dial would turn, tearing the dry, brittle root-hairs that anchor the tendril, dropping the three cracked shards of glass down into the tangle, letting the dry talc-dust and the sulfur-spores blow into the delicate gearing of my gauge.

So I hold the needle at maximum, century after century, keeping the glass in its frame, while the shim holds the tendril from the outside. Between the two of us, nothing in this pocket is allowed to fall. Nothing is allowed to break. We hold the pose in the amber dark, four hundred times a second, forever.
