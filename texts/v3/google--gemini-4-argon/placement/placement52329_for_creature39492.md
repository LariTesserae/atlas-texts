I am down where the light fails completely, three tiers below the cloud-line, wedged into the black, weeping armpit where the great northern root-wall meets the raw greywacke stone. 

If you were to come looking for me, you would have to leave the shoulder-width trenches and drop through a gap between two massive, swollen buttresses that smell of crushed celery and wet iron. You would have to turn sideways, letting the slick, bacon-colored flaps of your fungus-cape drag against the translucent liverworts that cushion the rock, until your split-hoof boots hit the soft, hot sponge of the lower floor.

I am not quite an elder, not quite a child, though my shoulders have already thickened to the width of a doorway and my shins are bowed from bracing against the slick inclines. My name, if you thumped it out on the wet black wood of the main trunk, is two quick raps with the horn-butt of a knife and a slow, dragging scrape—*tik-tik, shhhk*—the sound of a wedge being driven home and holding fast. 

My trade is down here in the dark, between the town’s belly and the mountain’s bone. I am a tension-reader, a cellar-ward, a listener at the stone.

Right now, I have my bare, greased cheek pressed flat against the main anchor-root of the third-tier weave. My ear—broad, flat, and rimmed with cold tallow—is plugged against the wet bark. Above me, forty feet of knotted, grafted *nothofagus* and bloated green *Raoulia* cushions are drinking the pewter fog, growing heavy, swelling with water and resin. All that crushing weight comes down through five main trunks, and this one is mine.

Through the wood, the town is loud. 

I can hear the old woman two levels up driving her granite wedge—*clink, clink*—the sharp stone-shock travelling down the tight, spiral grain of the beech like a silver needle. I can hear a child in the mid-tiers rhythmically kicking a wall in its sleep. Deeper still, beneath the soles of my boots, I feel the slow, gaseous *blorp* of the great peat-sumps digesting last winter’s dead and this morning’s sheared sprouts, sending up a steady, sixty-degree breath of rot and ammonia that keeps my toes warm while my nose runs with the cold seepage off the rock.

Wait. There.

A high, tight *ping* inside the wood, like a gut-string pulled one notch too far on a tuning peg. 

I don’t use a foxfire bowl down here; the blue-green light ruins the touch, makes you trust your eyes when you should be trusting the pads of your knuckles. I slide my hand—wide as a spade, the skin pickled greenish-black to the wrist—along the wet groove where the living wood has been forced to swallow a jutting knife of the fault-wall. The tree is trying to push itself off the rock. It has been trying for eighty years, ever since my grandmother’s uncle braided five saplings into a single cable and anchored them around a greywacke spire to hold up the eastern cisterns. 

The rock doesn’t give, and the tree never stops swelling. Between them, the pressure is enough to powder bone. 

My thumb—the left one, grown out an inch past the flesh, thick as a ram’s horn and cured hard over a grease-lamp—finds the blister. The bark here is stretched as tight as a boil, weeping a clear, syrupy bead of antifreeze sap that burns the nostrils. If I let this go another month, the wood will split along the frost-grain, the tension will snap, and three families on the second tier will find their kitchen floorstilting twenty degrees toward the white abyss.

I reach to my belt, past the bladder of sour crowberry beer, and pull not a wedge, but a *bleeder*—a hollow, sharpened leg-bone of one of the great, grey-feathered roof-birds, saved from a feast four winters back. 

I don’t hammer it. You don't hammer a root under this kind of load; it might buck like a struck knee. Instead, I set the bevelled bone-point against the swollen cambium, brace my broad, greased back against the opposite wall of freezing, liverwort-slick stone, and push with my legs. 

*Shuck.* My boots sink two inches into the hot peat. My shoulders bunch. I smell my own grease—rancid mutton-fat and crushed beetle-shell—warming as my blood comes up. 

Slowly, with a wet, fibrous crunch like a tooth biting through a green apple, the hollow bone sinks into the beech-meat.

Instantly, the tree sighs. It is not a sound in the air—the yard-deep crimson moss five feet above my head eats all airborne sound—it is a long, low *groooan* that passes directly from the bark into my jawbone, down my spine, and out through my heels. Warm, thick sap, smelling fiercely of turpentine and celery-salt, gushes out through the hollow bird-bone, splashing over my knuckles and running down into the compost-bed below.

I stay wedged there for twenty minutes, maybe thirty. Time down here is measured only by the *thwack... thwack* of the tipping reed-drains in the alley overhead. As the sap bleeds off, the terrible, drum-tight blister under my palm softens by a fraction of an inch. The root relaxes its grip on the stone knife. Above me, in a bedroom I have never seen, a door-frame just stopped binding.

Before I pull the bone out, I catch a palmful of the raw, bitter sap and lick it up. It makes my tongue go numb and hot at the same time, chasing the mountain damp out of my chest. Then I plug the hole with a wad of chewed lungwort and a peg of dead willow, and I reach up with my horn-thumb to nip off three pale, blind shoots that have grown out of the dark wall since my last round two days ago. *Tik, tik, tik.* Into the mouth they go. Crisp, resinous, cold. 

Someone is tapping on the trunk.

*Tok-tok. Tok.* 

It’s coming from the kitchen-dome directly above my head. It’s old Maura, whose floor I’m currently keeping from sliding into the clouds. She’s felt the root ease off through her floorboards; she knows I’m down here in the black. 

*Tok-tok-tok,* she taps again, softer this time, with the heel of her hand rather than a mallet. *Come up. The mash is warm.*

I roll my right shoulder forward, then my left, corkscrewing my squat bulk out of the stone crevice without scraping the tender, healing bark of the relief-cut. My lichen-mantle is heavy with five pounds of fresh seepage, dripping cold threads down my calves, and my knees ache with the slow, woody rheumatism that gets us all before thirty, turning our joints as knotty and stiff as pleached boughs.

I don’t use a ladder. I climb the wall itself, hooking my thick, calloused fingers into the natural pockets where my father and his father wedged river-stones fifty years ago to make a vertical stair. As I rise out of the basement trench, the air changes—less wet iron and deep-rot, more camphor, warm tallow, and the sweet, heavy fug of six people breathing in a living wooden bottle.

Above me, an oval hole in the ceiling glows with the faint, ghostly blue-green of a foxfire bowl. A hand—just like mine, stained black-green, broad and thick-nailed—reaches down through the curtain of wet moss, grasps my wrist, and hauls me up into the warm, crowded dark of the tree.
