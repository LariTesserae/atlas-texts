I am at the northern pass, where the mountain stops rising and drops away toward the timberline, though the trees are still two thousand feet below. 

Here, the three-foot iron pipe does not end; it enters the collar of a stone house built straight into the wind-scoured basalt of the ridge. This is the Weir-House. Inside, the pipe splits its throat over a series of cast-iron baffle plates, breaking the screaming, sixty-mile-an-hour column of pumped bog-water into a foaming, tumbling trough that spills out the northern wall and becomes a canal for the mines down in the foothills. 

I am the Weir-Clerk, though there is very little to write. 

My knees are dry. That is the difference between me and the four things that walk the basin behind my back. I wear oiled wool and heavy boots, and my ears are plugged with the same tallow-soaked fleece, because the sound of the water hitting the iron baffles inside my stone room is like a freight train perpetually derailing in a cellar. My teeth ache with the low, grinding vibration of the floor. When I set my tin mug of cold tea on the desk, the brown water forms a crisp, permanent geometric lattice of standing waves, and the mug itself creeps slowly toward the northern edge of the wood until it touches the raised lip I nailed there three years ago to keep my things from walking onto the flagstones.

I have eyes that still work in the dark, though they are perpetually bloodshot from the draft that whistles up through the floorboards, smelling of wet iron and that faint, sickly-sweet rot of crushed celery. 

I do not go into the basin. If I step out my southern door, I have to drop the wooden visor down over my face—a solid piece of cedar with two horizontal slits cut into it, narrower than a knife-blade. Through those slits, the twelve-mile valley looks like a strip of overexposed photographic plate, a blinding, salt-white smear where the sky and the earth have melted together. The roar out there is different from the roar inside my house; inside, it is contained by masonry, a heavy, thrumming drumbeat. Step outside the stone lintel, and the sound of the upper scarps hits the chest like a flat board, sucking the breath right out of the mouth. 

Instead of walking, I read the pipe. 

Before the three-foot main splits into the weir-troughs, four feet of its naked, sweating black length pass directly through my room, waist-high, resting on a massive pier of mortared granite. The iron is warm—always that same, feverish sixty-five degrees, heated by the violent friction of the six turbines miles away and the sheer hydraulic murder of forcing water through fifty feet of compressed root-mat. In the freezing, thin air of the pass, the pipe is my stove. My chair is wedged against it. I sit with my shins pressed to the curving steel, a heavy horsehair blanket draped over my lap and over the pipe together, trapping its mechanical body-heat against my legs.

I keep my left hand resting on the rivet-seam. 

After two winters at the Weir-House, the skin of my palm has grown thick and horn-yellow, calloused not by tools, but by the continuous, sixty-cycle-a-second tremor of the metal. I do not need the pressure gauge bolted to the wall—its brass casing is green with verdigris, and the needle broke off at the pin six months ago from the sheer palsy of the mount. I know the six stations by the layers of their screaming in the iron. 

Station One, nearest the pass, is a high, thin whine that lives in the top inch of the pipe’s curve. Station Six, twelve miles away under the ice-fields, is a deep, sluggish throb in the belly of the pipe where the sediment settles. And beneath the engines, I can feel the mountain trying to swallow them. I feel the slow, creeping choke of the *Astelia* roots squeezing the intake cages; it feels like a gradual thickening of the iron’s blood, a slight, straining rise in temperature under my blanket as the turbines work harder against the green sponge.

And I feel the tenders. 

I know when one of them is working near the line because the rhythm of the iron changes. Every morning around nine—when the sun has cleared the eastern peaks and turned the basin into a furnace of cold light—there will come a faint, dull *thud... thud... thud* beneath the scream of the turbines. It is the sound of a two-hundred-pound person leaning their weight on an octagonal steel bar, driving the chisel down through the mineral-crusted lips of the sedge where it has crept over the pipe's rivets. When a seventy-pound sod is pried loose and levers off the metal, the pipe gives a microscopic, elastic *spring* upward—barely the thickness of a cigarette paper, miles away, yet I feel the tiny, relieved sigh of the iron against my knee. 

Sometimes, in the middle of the afternoon, two sharp, metallic clicks will travel up the steel from the south. *Clink-clink.* 

It is a tender calling his neighbor to a sick station. It is not meant for me, three or four miles up the grade, yet the iron carries it faithfully all the way into my dark room. When I feel it, I reach down with my heavy wrench and strike the pipe once—a single, flat *clang*—just to let the two blind, rubber-shrouded oxen down there in the glare know that the north end is still bolted to the rock, that someone is still listening at the lip of the world. They never tap back to me. They don't have the breath or the thoughts to spare for a man who has dry knees.

Once every two weeks, the mule-train comes up the northern switchbacks from the timberline, bringing drums of diesel for the donkey-engine winches, crates of salt mutton, sacks of hardtack, and the mail—though there is never any mail for the basin, only company directives for me to file on a rusted spike. 

On those days, one of the four tenders comes up the line to haul the supplies back down to the caissons on a low, flat-bottomed iron sled that slides over the wet bristles of the *Donatia* like a grease-skid over ice. 

I watch for them through the cedar slits of my southern window. An hour before they arrive, they are just a speck of dull coal creeping along the black thread of the pipe. They never look up at the Weir-House as they climb the final dome. They keep their muzzles down, their brass-and-leather snouts pointed at their own heavy, rolling boots. 

When the tender finally reaches the flat gravel apron outside my door, he does not come inside. The sudden transition from the blinding glare and the open roar into my dark, echoing stone room makes them dizzy and sick, like deep-sea divers hauled too fast to the surface. 

Instead, the tender sits down heavily on the stone curb outside the door, his back against the masonry, his legs stuck straight out in their stiff, armpit-high waders. Water—clear, cold, and smelling of iron—runs out of the seams of his coat and puddles on the dry rock. 

I bring him a wooden bowl of hot mutton broth—the only hot thing he will put in his belly for a month, boiled over my little spirit-lamp—and a tin cup of dark rum mixed with warm water from the pipe's petcock. 

He does not take off his sou'wester or his blackened goggles. He only unhooks the left side of the double-thick canvas hound-muzzle, letting it hang down over his chest. The skin around his mouth is the color of tallow, greased thick with mutton fat to keep the spray from chapping it to the bone, and his lips are pale blue. When he takes the bowl from my hands, his fingers—curved permanently into the hook of the chisel-bar—cannot flatten out to hold the wood; he has to cradle the bowl between his wrists and his palms like a bear holding a stone.

We do not speak. If I shouted into his quilted horsehair ear-pads, he wouldn't understand the words anyway; it takes them three days in the foothills at the end of a hitch before their brains remember how to turn sounds back into nouns and verbs. 

Instead, I sit down on the stone threshold beside him, drop my cedar visor over my eyes against the white-gold fury of the valley below us, and lay my bare, yellow-calloused palm flat against the wet rubber of his shoulder. 

We sit like that for twenty minutes while he drinks the grease and the rum. Through the thick rubber, the oiled canvas, and the three layers of boiled wool, I can feel the man’s body vibrating. He is still humming at the exact pitch of Station Two’s main bearing. His muscles do not relax; they have forgotten how to unspool, held in a permanent, slow-motion clench against the give of the bog-mat and the shove of the wind. 

Once, a year ago, when I reached out to take the empty bowl from the tender who walks the middle scarps, something dropped from the folds of his oilskin sleeve and landed in the dry gravel between my boots. 

It was one of the pipe-lice from the caissons—a soft, translucent beetle the size of my thumb, its pale belly showing the dark, fern-like branching of its gut. It had crawled into the cuff of his coat while he slept against the warm pipe in the concrete pier twenty feet under the peat, and had ridden him all the way up into the killing light of the pass. 

In the dry air and the fierce ultraviolet glare cascading off the domes, the creature was already dying, curling in on itself at a pace too slow to see. 

Before I could touch it with my boot, the tender’s huge, horsehair-mittened hand came down—not to crush it, as I expected, but to scoop it up with a strange, clumsy gentleness, taking a handful of gravel with it so his stiff fingers wouldn't mash the soft body. He fumbled open the brass button of his inner wool pocket, down near his ribs where the heat of his body was trapped behind the rubber, and dropped the blind thing inside, buttoning it carefully back into the dark. 

Then he stood up—a slow, heaving uncoiling of two hundred and五十 pounds of wool, rubber, and muscle—hooked his canvas muzzle back over his face, and laid his flat palm once against the crown of my head. 

It was the basin sign for *all clear*. 

I stayed on the threshold for an hour after he had harnessed himself to the iron sled, watching through the thin wooden slits as he leaned his weight into the trace-chains and stepped back onto the green sponge. Within ten paces, the *Donatia* had hissed and risen back up behind the runners of his sled, erasing the groove of the iron, erasing the deep holes of his hobnails, until there was only the white-gold glare, the roaring walls of white water, and the tiny, dark tick creeping slowly down the black vein of the pipe, taking the warmth back underground.
