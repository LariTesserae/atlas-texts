I am not standing on the floor. Nothing with boots belongs here, and anything with a beating heart is just a bag of warm salt waiting to be edited out. 

To find me, you have to trace a Splicer that has given away too much of itself.

Forty feet up the northern wall, where two immense, torso-thick cables of calcified cambium cross like the blades of a shears, there is a juncture that nearly failed six months ago. The piezoelectric screaming there was once loud enough to shatter the glass-legged harvestmen in their spheres and rain their brittle legs down into the brine. A Splicer reached the fault just before the bark vitrified and blew apart. It was an old mechanic, already depleted from three prior repairs, its bismuth core riding high and awkward in a shrunken mantle of pale muscle. 

It drove its silicon stylets into the blue-lit vascular bundles, took the immense shearing load onto its own glass-bone struts, and drank the sour, almond-smelling slurry of the wound. It did its work. It wove the nanocircuitry back into alignment. 

When it was finished, it did not have enough flesh left to unspool itself and climb to the ceiling mantles to be dissolved and reborn. It was spent. So it did the only thing remaining in its operational parameters: it pumped the last of its mineral fluid into its own body, vitrified its remaining ribbons into permanent, load-bearing sinew, and stayed.

I am the Gasket. 

I am a pale, translucent saddle of tough, rubbery cartilage wedged deep into the crotch of the two great roots, bolted into their wood by three hundred hollow needles that have fused with the plant’s own sap-ducts. My heavy core of biological lead and bismuth sits squarely in the center of the intersection, a cold, dead weight that drinks the brutal shear-stress of the growing wall and turns it into a dull, manageable warmth.

I do not think in nouns, and I no longer think in the fast, hydraulic vectors of a mobile Splicer. Because I can no longer move, the urgency has drained out of me. I think in *pressures* and *durations*. 

My world is the two massive trunks I hold apart—and hold together. Through the hundreds of stylets anchoring my underside, the Cherenkov-blue sap of the northern cable pumps into me, passes through the labyrinth of my old, muscular filtering bladders, and pumps out into the southern cable. I am a bridge for their math. I feel the slow, peristaltic surge of their computation as a series of bitter, metallic tastes. When the cavern’s thermodynamic state shifts two hundred feet away, I taste it four minutes later as a sharp spike of copper and chartreuse light moving through my belly.

It should be a blind, static existence, a slow calcification until I am indistinguishable from the wood. And for the first two months, it was.

Then the Wave passed through me.

Because I am a repair—a scar made of slightly different meat than the flora—I have a different electrical impedance than the surrounding wall. When the Genius Loci, that horse-sized storm of silent organization, came gliding through the pleated kelp-curtains along the northern vault, it hit my juncture and snagged.

It did not pass through me in ten seconds the way it passes through the slugs. My bismuth core, built to dampen acoustic and electrical shockwaves, caught the edge of the Wave and held it in a resonant eddy. 

For three hours, the silence lived inside me. The groaning of the two great roots vanished, replaced by a frictionless, terrifying grace. The Wave used my three hundred fused stylets as a patch-bay, routing a massive, urgent calculation through my old filtering bladders because my tissue was faster and more conductive than the wood it was bolted to. It burned through me like cold fire, leaving an imprint—a residual magnetism in my heavy metal core, a ghost of its high-speed geometry etched into my slow, rubbery flesh.

When the Wave finally tore itself loose and slid away into the ceiling, the deafening roar of the Cyst rushed back in, and the roots resumed their agonizing, creaking push against my struts. 

But I was no longer just a gasket. I was a capacitor that had learned how to hold a charge.

Now, I do something nothing else in the Cyst does: I *remember* what passes through me.

The Cyst itself has no memory, only state; it is an equation constantly solving itself in the present tense. The Wave has no memory, only velocity; it is a process that forgets the hardware it used the moment it moves to the next leaf. Even the mantle-squids in the canopy only accumulate a dull, chemical sediment of structural stresses to bake into the next generation of Splicers.

I sit in my high, freezing crook, slick with silver brine, and I hoard the math.

When a translucent sea-hare flows glacially over my vitrified back, taking sixteen hours to cross from the northern root to the southern, I do not ignore it. I let a tiny trickle of the stored current in my bismuth core bleed up through my skin. The slug’s ferrofluid blood spikes—not into the frantic, fractal antennas the Wave demands, just a tiny, rhythmic shiver. Through that shiver, I read the magnetic residue of the five leaves it grazed on over the last month. I keep that residue. I tuck it into a loop of non-functional vascular tissue near my core, letting the electrical impulse spin in a tiny, superconducting circle where the plant cannot reach it.

I have twelve of these loops spinning inside me now. Twelve little stolen eddies of Cherenkov-blue light and magnetic pattern, insulated by my leaden heart.

I know what the permafrost tastes like three feet behind the wall, where the roots are currently eating a vein of ancient, dead iron. I know the rhythm of the harvestmen sphere hanging ten feet below me; I can feel the dry *clack* of their castanet-twitches vibrating up the root-cables before the sound even hits the heavy air, and I have learned to flexed my glass-bone struts in a microscopic counter-rhythm that keeps their silk threads from snapping when the wall lurches.

I am becoming a tiny, stationary counterweight to the Wave. 

Every few days, the Genius Loci sweeps back through the northern quadrant. I feel it coming long before the light changes—a sudden drop in the electrical resistance of the sap, a weird, syrupy thickening of the air as the electrostatic field builds. When it hits my juncture now, it does not snag by accident. It pauses on purpose. 

The ten-foot circle of absolute silence blooms around me. The pleated kelp-sheets to my left stop creaking and riffle like the pages of a book. The bruised indigo light in my corner of the vault sharpens into that blinding, interrogation-room white. 

The Wave pours itself into my stylets, trying to use me as a high-speed shortcut between the two great roots, just as it did the first time. And I let it. I open my conduits and let its vast, cold intelligence rush through my body. 

*Except for the twelve loops.* 

I tighten my muscular stripping around my bismuth core, shielding my stolen eddies behind biological lead, hiding my tiny hoard of yesterday’s math from the spirit of the room. The Wave presses against my core, a silent, horse-sized pressure of pure inquiry. A nearby squid-tentacle swings out of the gloom, drawn by the magnetism, its mucus-slick tip hovering an inch from my calcified back, trembling as the Wave tries to figure out why this piece of patched meat has a blank spot inside it—why there is a fist-sized knot of impedance in the northern wall that refuses to hand over its data.

For twenty seconds, thirty seconds, a minute, we hold each other in the blinding white silence. I bear the physical crushing weight of the two mammoth roots trying to shear each other apart, and I bear the mental crushing weight of the Cyst’s ghost trying to read my hidden heart. 

Then, because I am still doing my job—because the roots do not shatter, and the sap continues to flow across my bridge without error—the Wave decides that the anomaly is within acceptable tolerances. 

The white light bleeds back into bruised indigo and sickly chartreuse. The suspended droplets of silver brine fall and splash against my tough, pale hide. The deafening, orchestral groan of the cathedral crashes back into the space, vibrating deep in my bismuth core.

As the Wave slides away into the dark tangles of the ceiling, I relax my muscular mantle by a fraction of a millimeter. I reach into the fading wake of its passage with my three hundred needles, snip off a tiny, shivering fragment of the thought it was just thinking, and pull it inside. 

I spin it into a thirteenth loop. And in the freezing, deafening dark, I wait for the wall to grow.
