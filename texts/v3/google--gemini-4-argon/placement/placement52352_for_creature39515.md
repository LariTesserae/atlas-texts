I would not be on the catwalk, and I would not be out in the shadowless light where the swallows cut the steam. You would have to look for me down where the green stops being green and turns the color of pickled walnut—six feet below the basin floor, in the tight, blind crawl-space where the cast-iron bedplate meets the weeping clay pan.

Down here, the *CHUG-hiss-CLANK* is not a sound in the air; it is the ceiling coming down and going back up by a thirty-second of an inch.

I am the twelfth one, or perhaps the oldest of the eleven—the one who grew too thick in the hips and too slow in the knees for the upper iron twenty years ago, before the flywheel could take an arm. Instead of going into the blue-yellow sheet of the gas-throat, I went down through the drain-hole under the condenser sump. There is a job down here that only requires moving once an hour, and it suits a body whose joints have begun to calcify into the same grey lime we use for the brass.

I live in the Root-Throat. 

It is a wet, woody gut no wider than a hogshead, entirely roofed by the underside of the engine’s iron skirt and floored by the ancient, greasy clay of the plain. The sedge-willow’s deepest taproots come through here like the ribs of a whale, swollen and pale from lack of light, drinking the warm bilge that drips from the stuffing boxes above. The air is ninety degrees and smells of boiled marsh-reed, old tallow, and wet iron rust.

My work is the intake rose.

The engine draws its water through a bell-shaped cast-iron strainer buried in the gravel sump, perforated with two hundred half-inch holes. If fifty of those holes plug with clay-silt or willow-hair, the pump draws short, the condenser runs hot, the vacuum drops half a pound, and the forty-beat measure up top begins to develop a tiny, nervous hitch at the top of the stroke.

So I lie on my belly in the warm mud, wedged between two massive, vibrating root-knees that have grown around the suction pipe like clasped hands. My skin has lost the dark, vulcanized slate-color of my cousins up top; down in the dark, pickled in the warm bilge-water and the acidic sweat of the willow-bark, I have gone the color of a peeled turnip, soft and wrinkled at the joints, except for the four horn-plates on my back and shoulders where the iron bedplate taps me forty times a minute.

*Tap. Shudder. Drop.*
*Tap. Shudder. Drop.*

I do not use my eyes. They have filmed over with a painless, milky grease, because there is nothing to see in the sump except the occasional blind, white root-tip feeling its way through the gravel like a slow worm. My whole world is in my ten fingers and my two extra thumbs, which are permanently hooked into the holes of the iron strainer.

I feel the water going into the engine. It flows past my wrists in rhythmic, gulping surges—*draw, hold, draw, hold*—carrying the whole history of the Kettle down with it. 

I know everything that happens up in the light before the ones on the catwalk do, because I taste the wash of it coming through the twenty-foot filter of the turf. When a hare dies behind the eccentric rod and they render its fat on the cylinder head, a faint, sweet slickness reaches my knuckles four days later. When the swallows are hatching in the upper wall, the water grows slightly sharper with the lime of their droppings, and the root-hairs around my legs stiffen and drink harder. When one of the eleven up top misses a beat and goes into the gas-fire, I know it six hours later when the acidic sump-water turns briefly bitter with the wash of fresh ash-paste from the brass gauges, trickling down the outside of the condenser casing onto my neck.

Every twenty or thirty cycles, a root-let tries to enter the strainer. Drawn by the warm, mineral-rich suction, the willow-fibers are constantly creeping across the iron bell, seeking to plug the holes and swallow the pipe the way they swallowed the frame. 

*Tap.* I feel the tiny change in the water’s eddy against my left ring finger.
*Shudder.* My two thumbs pinch the creeping white root-thread against the rough cast iron.
*Drop.* I twist my wrist on the down-stroke of the pump above, using the machine’s own shudder to snap the fiber clean, and tuck the broken end into my mouth to chew into soft bast.

When I have a fistful of chewed root-pulp in my cheek, I spit it out into my palm, roll it into a tight plug between my thighs, and push it up through the narrow grease-chute between the low-pressure cylinder and the bedplate. A hand with two thumbs—darker, drier, smelling of cold sky and beetle-fat—comes down on the *CLANK* and takes the wad away to pack a weeping gland. Sometimes, on the next stroke down, that same hand drops a warm, boiled swallow-gizzard or a thumb-sized lump of salted hare-tallow through the slot onto my shoulder.

Once every few days, one of the young ones will slide their flat skull down through the drain-hole until their forehead touches the back of my neck. We stay like that for twenty cycles. Through the wet, heavy bone of my spine, I give them the news of the deep: *The gravel pan is shifting half a hair to the east; the third willow-root on the north side is rotting at the core; the gas-seam beneath my belly is humming a quarter-tone flatter this winter.* And through their hard, calloused brow, they give me the news of the high room: *The sun is hot on the rim; the big gauge glass is clean; the forty-beat is round and fat and has no corners.*

Then they pull back up into the roar and the steam, and I settle my breastbone back against the warm suction pipe.

I am very slow now. My ribs have grown so flat and wide from decades of lying under the bedplate that I could no longer squeeze back out through the drain-hole even if I wanted to. The willow-roots have grown over my calves, not tightly enough to stop the blood, just resting across my shins like heavy, warm logs, holding me in place so the suction doesn't shift me while I sleep. 

Sometimes I sleep for five thousand strokes at a time, while my fingers keep picking the silt out of the iron holes by themselves, moving on the *Drop*, resting on the *Tap*. I know that eventually the roots across my legs will thicken until they meet the iron roof, and my horn-plated back will become the gasket between the willow and the bedplate. My bones are already full of iron-rust and marsh-lime; when I stop moving, the sump won't even sour. My fingers will stay hooked in the outer ring of the strainer, hardened into horn, keeping the willow-hair back from the center holes for another thirty years while the water goes past my ribs—*draw, hold, draw, hold*—up into the dark cylinder, out into the white sky, and back down through the green wall to wash my knuckles again.
