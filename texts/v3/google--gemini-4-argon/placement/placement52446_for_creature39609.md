I am not in the vines, and I am not on the red dust floor. To find me, you have to take a chisel to the primordial granite three feet behind the third pylon on the western wall—though the chisel would bounce off the stone here, because the stone has forgotten how to break.

I am the fault line. Or rather, I am what moved into the fault line when the machines began to ruin the neighborhood’s physics a million years ago.

At first, I seems to be nothing more than a seam of milky quartz and tarnished copper, a quarter-inch wide, zig-zagging down the sheer face of the bowl from the wind-scoured lip to the buried roots of the monolith. If you pressed your bare palm against the rock here, you wouldn't feel the sun-baked chill of granite; you would feel a slow, greasy vibration, like a subwoofer buried under ten feet of wet clay, and a heat that is slightly too rhythmic to be geothermal. 

I am a litho-trophic colony—a sheet of crystalline cartilage, heavy metal salts, and extremely slow nerve fibers pressed flat as a pressed flower between two hundred-ton blocks of craton. 

For ten thousand generations of the purple-pelted things in the vines, I have been widening this crack by a millimeter a century. I do not eat the cicadas or the ferns. I eat the argument between the pylon and the mountain. 

The black ceramic monoliths do not participate in friction, which means they are an insult to the rock that holds them. Every time the western pylon cycles, ripping the sky down into water and bending the local gravity into a knot, the granite around its base tries to shear. The stone wants to crack, to slide, to crumble into gravel under the spatial torque, and the pylon’s residual field refuses to let it. That trapped, frustrated violence has to go somewhere. It bleeds laterally into the bedrock as a wave of intense piezoelectric static and warped shear-stress.

I sit in the seam and open my lattices to it. When the gravity slips, my quartz-ribbed tissues compress and flare. In the total, crushing dark inside the cliff face, I glow with that same nameless, non-spectrum smear of color that flashes on the predator’s fur when she drops. I drink the shear-stress. I use the bent geometry to force stubborn atoms of iron and silicate out of the granite and weld them into my own spreading mat.

Because I am fifty feet wide and paper-thin, pressed tight to the curve of the bowl, I am the ear the sinkhole doesn't know it has. 

The pale-faced hunter in the vines reads the air; she knows the shape of a lizard by the hole it punches in the cicada-roar. I do not care about the air. The air is frantic, frothy nonsense. I read the bone of the mesa. 

I know the five slow grazers better than they know themselves. To me, they are not hidden. Their four-hundred-pound soles are loud, heavy thumbs dragging across my drumhead. As they slide over the bedrock, their corrugated undersides massage the thin skin of stone above my nerves. I can feel the dense, crystalline lines inside their bellies humming in sympathy with my own lattice minutes before a pylon fires. When one of them presses its flank against the western monolith, leaning over my seam, we share the wave—the grazer drinking the wet, ozone-charged momentum from the air, while I drink the dry, grinding torque from the root.

And where my seam breaches the surface—a vertical scar running right through a cluster of the velvet barrel-ferns—I bleed. 

My waste product is a thick, chalky slush of dissolved silicates, copper oxide, and heavy, mineralized water, warm from my slow metabolism. It oozes out of the rock at the rate of three drops an hour, forming a crusty, turquoise-colored lip along the granite wall. 

This is my only negotiation with the fast world. 

The millions of cicadas cannot make their copper-veined wings out of rainwater and fern-sap alone. In the five minutes of quiet exhaustion after a pylon pulses, before the pressure drops again, they descend on my turquoise scar in trembling, iridescent carpets. Their needle-like proboscises scrape at my dried mineral exudate, drinking the copper and the silica I have chewed out of the deep mountain. Their metallic grinding—the very sound that defines the basin—is built from my tailings. Without my slow digestion of the rock, their wings would be soft and mute, and the pale-faced hunter in the vines would go blind in the sudden silence.

I know her, too. Not when she is flying—the air is none of my business—but at the end of her arc. 

When she shoots up out of the second distortion wave, her stomach full of frantic, clattering birds, she doesn't always grab the vines cleanly. Sometimes her momentum is too high, and her blunt, horned claws rake across the upper eastern wall to brake her fall, right where one of my thinner, younger tendrils has almost reached the rim. 

*Thwack.* 

A sharp, bright spike of kinetic force rings down through forty feet of solid granite, completely different from the endless, buzzing static of the bugs or the deep, hollow flute-note of the plateau wind vibrating the lip above me. I savor that tiny, sharp impact for three or four hours after she has settled back into sleep, passing the vibration back and forth through my crystalline web, using that minuscule gift of stolen momentum to crack a single grain of feldspar in the dark, and grow another fraction of a micron toward the sun.
