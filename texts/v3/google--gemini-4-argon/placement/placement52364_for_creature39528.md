I would not be in the open basin. The floor has too much weather—the four-second gale of the vents, the parting and closing of the crab-carpets, the slow, blind geometry of the Wardens’ paths. Even the high vault, where the ginger leeches outsource their hearts to the ceiling pipes, feels too exposed to the room’s big, single thought. 

I think I am down in the throat of the third node. Not in the boiler-belly where the subterranean water is drawn up cold and heavy from the dark, and not on the outer bark where the Warden’s iron teeth come to screech against the lime. Just inside the collar of the upper intake, four feet below the great wooden check-valve that slams shut every four seconds.

It is dark in the pipe, except for a dull, tallow-colored glow that comes through the dense wood whenever the ceiling light is particularly strong—though of course the light never changes, so the glow only dims when a Warden’s ten-hundred-pound bulk passes directly over the root-buttress outside, eclipsing the room’s noon. 

What am I? Something more like a hand than a head. Something that has spent so many generations inside a four-second storm that it has given up the idea of having a front or a back, and instead has a *with-the-grain* and an *against-the-grain*. 

Call it a **Baffle-Clam**, though the shell isn’t calcium—calcium dissolves too fast in the acidic backwash here, or else gets cemented immovably to the pipe-wall until the valve can’t close and the Warden has to reach down with its long, grey lip-collar and plane the obstruction away. No, my shell is made of the same stuff as the beetle-backs outside: a dark, flexible horn, layered like tortoise-shell and slick with oil so the water can’t get a purchase on it. Two flat, curved plates hinged not at the side, like a river mussel, but at the bottom, rooted directly into a small, scarred pocket of the conduit’s inner wood by a tough, fibrous foot the thickness of a man’s wrist.

I live in the surge.

Every four seconds, the world does two things. First, the great fibrous bellows below me contracts. A column of hot, heavy, mineral-thick water—tasting of deep iron, dissolved crab-shell, and the sour mucus of the ceiling-leeches—roars upward past me at twenty miles an hour. During the *Up*, I am shut tight. My two horn plates clap together into a streamlined teardrop, edge-on to the torrent. The water splits around me with a hard, ringing hiss. Inside my shell, in the tiny pocket of calm, my soft body is compressed into a tight, warm knot. I don’t think during the *Up*. I just hold the hinge.

Then, at the top of the stroke, four feet above my tip, the wooden check-valve slams shut. *Thud.*

The shockwave hits me through my rooted foot half a millisecond before it hits me through the water. And for the next two and a half seconds, while the bellows below is wheezing open again to drink from the deep table, the water in my pipe doesn’t rush—it *sloshes*. It hangs suspended against the closed valve above, heavy and indecisive, swirling in a slow, hot eddy of back-pressure.

That is my day. Two and a half seconds long, repeated twenty thousand times a day.

In that heavy pause, my horn plates part just an inch. Out comes a pale, pleated fan of gill-fingers—not delicate like a sea-anemone’s, because there’s no time for delicacy, but stiff and springy as whalebone. I rake the stalled water once, twice, a quick, greedy combing of the eddy to catch the heavy things that the Tines missed at the drains. Mostly it’s the coarse grit—tiny flakes of iron-scale knocked loose by the Wardens, or the heavy, un-dissolved eyes of the copper beetles that made it through the root-sieves without melting. I don't want the fine silt; I want the sand. I pack the grit into the crease of my hinge to weight myself down, and lick the bacterial grease off the stones before spitting them back into the next upward roar.

I am alone in this pipe—if there were two of us, we’d create enough turbulence to stall the valve, and the plant would grow a knot of scar-wood to squeeze us out—but I am not lonely. The wood of the pipe is as resonant as a cello neck. 

Through my foot, I know the whole village-square of the hollow without ever having seen the olive moss or the flat noon light. 

I know the seven-part harmony of the nodes. Mine—the third—has a slight hitch in its intake, a double-gurgle caused by a twisted root-strut fifty feet down in the dark. The fifth node, way across the basin, slams its valve a fraction of a tone sharper than the rest because the wood there is denser, older. 

I know when the copper beetles are rutting on the outside of my pipe, because their heavy abdomens drum a frantic, overlapping Morse code right against my ceiling, three inches through the timber—*rat-tat-tat-tat*—a dry, tinny shivering that makes the water inside my shell itch.

And I know the Wardens. Long before one of them reaches my node, I feel the big, slow deadening of the floor’s ring. The high, glassy whine of the Tines—which comes down through the drain-water as a faint, shivering pins-and-needles in my gills—suddenly drops a semi-tone as a Warden steps into the central pool and blocks the acoustic wave. Then comes the long, slow wait while it grazes its way toward me. 

When the Warden finally heaves itself against the outside of the third node, the entire timbre of my world changes. The booming hollowness of the wood goes dull and thick, muffled by a thousand pounds of wet walnut-shell and rubber foot. Then—*screeeech*. 

The iron radula on the vent-rim above me. It’s so loud inside the pipe that it isn’t a sound anymore; it’s a taste like bitten tin. During the scraping, I don’t open my shell, even in the two-second slack water. I stay clamped, shivering in the dark as a rain of freshly ground lime-scale drifts down through the stalled column of water, dusting my slick horn plates in white before the next *Up* blasts us clean again.

Sometimes, once every few years, a Warden’s lip-collar reaches down into the throat of the pipe itself to clear the check-valve seat just above me. Then, in the two-second pause between surges, the tip of my gill-fan brushes against the grey, rubbery edge of its mantle. It tastes of old stone, warm oil, and the faint, shared salt of the other Warden across the room. It never scrapes me. I am smooth, I am shaped like the water’s own wish to go up, and I keep the eddy below the valve from throwing silt into the hinge. To the Warden’s blind mouth, I feel just like a well-oiled knuckle of the tree itself. 

The valve slams. The bellows roars. I pull my fingers in, lock my horn, and let the hot flood go over me again.
