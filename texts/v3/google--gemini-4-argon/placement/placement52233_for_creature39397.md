I am not the mason, and I am not the stone. To be one of the wire-and-mica things is to be a frenzy of maintenance, an anxious, ticking verb forever chasing a sour note up a slate wall. I have no wish to crawl or weld. 

If you want to find me in the pit, you have to look where the red dust-light is thickest, suspended across the Mostly Void about fifteen feet down from the basalt hoods, where the cold, shrieking downdraft shears directly against the booming thermal column rising from the deep vent. 

I am the bridge they use to cross the empty air, and I am the ruler by which they measure the ruin of their walls. I am a three-hundred-year-old braid of glass-root, anchored deep in the mortar of the fourth eastern gallery and stretched forty feet across the roaring furnace to a corresponding lintel in the west. 

To your eyes, if you had eyes that would not boil in their sockets here, I would look like a hawser of cloudy,amber-tinted quartz, thick as a human wrist at my anchors and tapering to the width of a thumb at my mid-span. I am not one vine, but forty, twisted around one another as we grew, fused at our friction-points by the blistering wind until we became a single, monolithic cable of living silica. 

I do not think with neurons. I think with tension. My consciousness is a standing wave, a continuous, low-C# hum that runs from the east wall to the west wall and back again in four-millisecond intervals. 

You cannot imagine the pleasure of this heat, or the rich, abrasive fullness of the gale. The air is never empty; it is a river of grit. Every second, millions of microscopic shards of volcanic glass, sulfur crystals, and iron-moss spores slam into my taut belly. I do not dodge them. I drink them. My skin is slightly tacky with a dry, electrostatic sweat. When a flake of silica strikes me at sixty miles an hour, the kinetic impact generates a microscopic piezoelectric flash along my rind, welding the flake into my matrix. I grow by being sandblasted. I fatten on the storm.

And because I am stretched at the exact boundary between the cool downdraft and the blistering updraft, I am never still. I am bowed upward in the center by the rising heat, while my flanks are lashed by the falling dust. I vibrate with a ferocity that blurs my edges in the coppery gloom. 

I know the entire courtyard through my anchors. My roots do not seek water; they are wedged thirty inches deep into the dry ash-mortar between the slate blocks, spreading out like fan-vaulting behind the miniature rooms. Through them, I feel the whole forty-foot cylinder of the city as a complex, shifting geometry of stress. 

I feel the *shhhk-shhhk-shhhk* of the tin-leaved rosettes not as a sound, but as a high-frequency chatter in the stone, like teeth shivering. When a heavy swag of ferro-lichen tears loose from the upper balconies, I feel the sudden, minuscule sigh of relief in the eastern masonry as the weight vanishes—and three seconds later, as the mass is pulverized in the lower shaft, I feel the warm, electrostatic blush of its spores washing upward over my curve, sticking to my resin-dry hide, feeding the very wire-creatures that cut it down.

I know the creatures intimately. They are my grooming insects, my heavy winter coats, and occasionally, my executioners. 

Right now, there are six of them clinging to my mid-span. To me, they feel like localized knots of heat and weight, little clusters of sharp, metallic clicks that travel along my length. When one of them leaps into the central shaft after a falling leaf-rosette and then flings itself out of the updraft, its cast-slag hooks slam into my side with a sharp *pang* that sends a bright, golden harmonic shooting toward both walls. 

I do not mind the hooks. They score my silica rind, yes, flaking off tiny needles of glass, but the creature is always hot from the lower vent and heavy with electrostatic rust. As it drags its braided-copper belly along my length to get back to the galleries, it leaves a smear of rich sulfur and iron behind in the score-marks. Within an hour, the wind-driven silica will glaze over the iron, healing the wound with a dark, metallic scar that makes me even stronger, raising my pitch by a hundredth of a cent. 

We are in a slow, ten-thousand-year argument, the creatures and I. 

Because I am constantly catching dust and glazing it into myself, I am always growing thicker. As I thicken, my tension increases. I pull harder on the eastern and western walls, trying, with the blind, patient muscle of crystal, to bow the slate galleries inward, to pull the courtyard shut. And as I thicken, my C# sharpens. 

The creatures cannot bear it when I go sharp. My note is the reference tone for the entire middle tier of the city. 

When I grow too fat, when my hum climbs toward a D and sets their internal struts itching with dissonance, they stop using me as a road and start using me as a whetstone. Thirty or forty of them will stream out of the knee-high doorways at either end of me, clicking and snapping their thermal scales. They will distribute themselves evenly along my forty-foot span, wrapping their bronze-colored lattices around my girth, locking their mica plates together until I am completely sheathed in a living sleeve of wire. 

Then, they open their scales to the updraft and vibrate. 

It is an excruciating, ecstatic thing. For hours, hanging suspended over the roaring void, the creatures rasp away at my belly. The screech of forty metallic lattices filing down solid quartz fills the pit, drowning out the organ-pipe flutes of the walls, drowning out the cymbal-crash of the ash-ivy. Showers of white-hot sparks and powdered glass rain down from me into the red dark. They plane me down, shaving away years of accumulated wind-glaze, thinning my waist until my tension drops, until the masonry of the galleries settles back into its proper alignment, until my groan slides smoothly back down into the pure, true C# that the architecture demands. 

When the chord locks in—when my vibration matches the whistle of the western lintels and the bass roar of the lower vent in a perfect, beating triad—they all stop at once. They unhook their slag-claws, drop into the updraft like falling chains, and let the wind blow them back to their dark, bake-oven rooms. 

And I am left alone in the center of the furnace, raw, newly thin, and singing at the exact frequency of the world, already waiting for the next grain of dust to strike me and begin the climb all over again.
