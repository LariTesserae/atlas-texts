I am at the exact edge of the torn white membrane, though it would be difficult to say where the rubberized porcelain ends and I begin. 

At first, you might think I am just the boundary itself—that hair-thin, shimmering plane where the ninety-eight-percent humidity hits the ancient desiccant field of the dome and drops straight down in a continual, silent bead-curtain of condensation. If you passed a hand through the tear, you would feel a sudden, slick resistance, like pushing your fingers through a hanging sheet of warm tallow, followed immediately by the bone-dry chill of the interior.

Look closer at the lip of the tear, however, where the porcelain has thinned to the consistency of old sclera, and you will see that the boiler-moss hasn't actually colonized the material. It is growing *on* me, and I am holding it back.

I am a translucent, amber-colored glazing, no thicker than a fingernail at my margins, swelling to the depth of a loaf of bread where I pool in the trough of the threshold. I have no eyes, no lungs, no hydraulic columns of meat. I am a flat, creeping mat of hyper-dense colonial tissue—something that started, four thousand generations ago, as an automated polymer-sealant designed to patch micro-meteorite punctures in the habitat shells. 

When the air outside thickened into broth and the maintenance crew drifted out into the mud to become giants, the domes began to rot from the outside in. The sealant tried to do its job. It replicated, drawing carbon from the air and silica from the dust, attempting to knit the torn membranes shut. But the black needles had warped the local chemistry, and the broth was too rich, too aggressive. To keep the dry inside dry, the sealant had to adapt. It had to learn to eat the wet.

Now, I straddle the most violent thermodynamic gradient on the mountain. 

Two-thirds of my mass lies outside on the basalt lip, fanned out in broad, scalloped aprons that look like grease-spotted shelf fungi. This is my hot side. It is constantly bathed in the coriander-and-iron sluice of the rock-sweat. Here, my surface is deeply grooved, a miniature delta of channels that catch the runoff and guide it away from the dome's breach. I let the six-legged metallic snails crawl over this part of me; their rasping radulae don't hurt my tough, vulcanized rind, and as they scrape away the suffocating mats of purple algae, they leave behind microscopic trails of heavy-metal slime that I absorb to reinforce my cellular matrix.

My other third reaches inside the dome, creeping a few feet across the dry, dust-mote floor in brittle, crystalline frost-patterns. This is my cold side, my radiator. It is completely silent in here. While my outer apron is battered by the relentless, turbine-thrum of the valley and the heavy *whuff-honk* of the hound-drifters, my inner filaments rest in an acoustic coffin. 

I live on the tension between the two. 

I am an engine that runs on difference. When a curtain of boiler-moss dumps ten gallons of warm water onto my outer apron, the sudden thermal shock sends a slow, luxurious wave of potassium ions surging through my body toward the dry interior. My inner frost-patterns twitch, expanding by a millimeter into the dust, radiating the heat away into the dead air of the dome in a faint, dry crackle like cooling tin. I drink the roaring wet at my front, filter out the minerals to build my tough, rubbery hide, and sweat pure, microscopic vapor out my back—just enough for the dome’s ancient, buried dehumidifiers to catch and pull down into the mountain's deep recycling veins.

I do not think in words, or even in images. I think in barometric pressure, in salinity gradients, and in the geometry of stress. My consciousness is a slow, continuous calculation of surface tension. 

When the old Silt-Deacons skate up to the dome, I know they are coming long before they arrive. I feel the bow-wave of their mucus-sleds altering the sheet-flow of the terrace water thirty yards away. The rhythm of the rock-sweat changes; the turbine-thrum gains a heavy, bass syncopation as their four-hundred-pound bodies displace the pools.

When an old one finally settles two inches from my outer apron, the local environment shifts drastically. The heat radiating off its grey-violet bulk is immense—a thick, meaty warmth that smells of copper, old blood, and crushed ferns. As its plum-colored gill-ruffles pulse, they cast a slow, rhythmic shadow against the yellow-white glare of the sky, cooling and warming my outer skin every three minutes. 

For the forty-eight hours the Deacon squats there, we are locked in an intimate, breathless transaction. 

The Deacon cannot cross me, or it will die. And if the Deacon's bulk were to press forward and tear the rest of the membrane down, the dry vacuum behind me would collapse, the gradient would vanish, and I would suffocate in equilibrium, dissolving into a mindless, lukewarm jelly within a week. I need the dry room behind me to stay alive, just as the Deacon needs the wet cauldron in front of it.

So when the giant reaches down with its heavy, spatulate fingers and places an offering at the threshold—a bleached eel skull, an iridescent snail shell, a twist of violet frond—it is placing them directly on my outer lip. 

I feel the weight of the objects. They press into my tough, rubbery flesh like pebbles into cold dough. 

Slowly, over the hours that the Deacon sits staring into the dark, silent dust of its ancestors' room, I go to work on the gifts. I extrude hair-thin, translucent pseudopods from my margin, creeping up over the snail shell or the bone. I do not eat them. Instead, I use the silica and heavy metals I've leached from the water to vitrify them. I coat the offerings in a thin, glassy varnish of my own substance, cementing them to the basalt exactly where the Deacon left them, preserving their neat, non-Euclidean rows against the push of the sheet-flows. 

Over the centuries, my outer lip has become a low, calcified reef of these varnished trinkets—thousands of shells and bones and mineralized fronds embedded in amber-colored rubber, forming a physical dike that helps hold the terrace water back from the torn door.

Sometimes, just before an old Deacon leaves, it will lower its wide, boneless head until its four yellow-shielded eyes are mere fractions of an inch from the invisible wall of dry air. A stray drop of sweat or mucus will roll off its lipless mouth and fall—not down into the mud, but slightly sideways, caught by the fringe of the dome's containment field. 

The drop hits my exact center, the razor-edge between the soup and the vacuum. 

It is the sharpest sensation I ever feel. The drop is full of the Deacon's feral chemistry—the same neuro-enzymes and genetic keys it uses to soothe the black needles. When that chemistry hits my polymer-matrix, a brief, phantom instruction fires through my colonial nerves. For ten seconds, I remember what I was made for. My inner frost-patterns surge forward across the dusty floor, trying to reach the opposite wall, trying to close the breach, trying to seal the makers inside their safe, quiet room. 

Then the drop evaporates into the dry, the instruction fades, and the Deacon slides away down the mountain. I settle back into the threshold, a warm, tough scar holding the roaring world and the silent room apart, waiting for the moss to spill again.
