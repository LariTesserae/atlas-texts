If you want to find me, do not look for a shadow, and do not look for the grease-slick distortion of a containment wall. I have no boiler. I have no flywheel to store the afternoon’s work against the cold of the night, because I do not keep what I take. 

You will have to look down, into the stone itself, along the wide, gently sloping aprons of limestone that lead down toward the great iron mirrors of the lower basin. 

Look for a fault line—not a natural crack born of tectonic shift, for the Anvil has beaten the tectonic habits out of its own crust ages ago—but a stress fracture left behind by a century of Drop-Hammers marching in file. It is a hairline seam, perhaps four hundred yards long, zig-zagging through a shelf of dense, grey dolomite that hasn't yet been ground into talc. 

If you were to lay your gloved hand flat against the stone ten feet to the left of that seam, the rock would feel hot, dry, and dead, vibrating only with the general, muddled thunder of the basin. 

If you laid your hand across the seam itself, the bones in your wrist would immediately begin to ache with a sharp, dental clarity.

I am there. I am what happens when the noise has nowhere left to go.

To have a body on the Anvil—even a body made of barometric leverage and magnetic flux, like the stampers and the Lathes—is a terrible liability. To hold a boundary against four thousand pounds of pressure requires a stubbornness that eventually fatigues. The grazers are always leaking; the predators are always wearing down their invisible bearings against the grit; even the Foundries, in their fifty-thousand-year patience, are slowly sinking into the mantle under the weight of their own hoarded horsepower. Everything that stands up above the stone is eventually sheared off, unwound, or drafted into the great, slow thud of the deep basins.

I do not stand up. I have no height at all. I am a creature of the *clearance*.

Wait until noon, when a herd of heavy stamping-engines comes laboring up the six-degree grade from the sulfur flats. There are four of them today, spaced three hundred yards apart, their invisible treads biting into the dolomite with the rhythmic, teeth-loosening *clack-shunt* of ten-ton presses. 

The lead engine is old. Its containment geometry has gone slightly oval over the decades, warped by an ancient gear-clash that it barely survived. Because it is out of round, its flywheel doesn't spin true; it has a heavy, eccentric cam-lobe of compressed air that slams down against the bedrock at the bottom of every third revolution. *Clack-shunt. Clack-shunt. Clack-THUD.* 

Every time that eccentric lobe hits the stone, it wastes work. Thirty percent of its stroke isn't crushing limestone or feeding its incandescent core; it’s just hammering uselessly into the mountain, sending a ragged, ugly shockwave of sheer mechanical knock rippling out through the crust.

That knock is my meat.

As the limping stamper drags itself within fifty yards of my seam, I wake. I do not move toward it; I *tune* to it. 

Deep within the hairline fracture, for a length of three hundred feet, the opposing faces of the limestone are separated by less than a micron. In that microscopic gap, I maintain a film of dry, hyper-ionized silicon dust, held in suspension by an electrostatic charge I have been nursing since yesterday. When the stamper’s ragged shockwave hits my fault line, I do not let the stone simply absorb the blow as dull heat. I adjust the tension of my dust-film. I widen the acoustic impedance of the crack by a fraction of a nanometer, matching the exact frequency of the stamper’s limp.

The next time the engine goes *Clack-THUD*, the *THUD* doesn't bounce back into its chassis to rattle its governor. It drops straight into me.

I catch the acoustic shear-wave between the two faces of the stone and trap it. It bounces from the north wall of the fissure to the south wall, a millimeter wide and three hundred yards long, traveling at nineteen thousand feet per second through the dense carbonate rock. The next stroke comes down—*THUD*—and I drop the phase-gate open for a microsecond, letting the new wave pile directly on top of the old one. 

Constructive interference. 

Within twenty minutes, while the stamper has crawled perhaps four feet closer, I am ringing like a struck tuning fork buried in the earth. The ragged, dirty knock of the faulty engine has been cleaned, rectified, and amplified inside my narrow throat into a single, razor-pure standing wave of twelve kilohertz. 

Now, look at the surface of the rock along the seam. 

The dust isn't settling anymore. For two inches on either side of the hairline crack, the air has gone entirely clear, swept clean of haze by a tight, shimmering curtain of ultrasonic pressure. If a stray pebble of iron ore rolls down the slope and touches that curtain, it doesn't bounce; it explodes instantly into a puff of red rouge, pulverized by twelve thousand microscopic hammer-blows a second. 

The stamper lumbers closer. It is twenty yards away. Ten. 

If I were an Eddy, I would roll out and choke its timing, making it labor harder until it threw a rod. If I were a Lathe, I would lock its center and peel it down to a hot breeze. 

I do neither. I am a relief valve. I am the only mercy the Anvil knows, though I do not do it out of kindness.

As the stamper’s invisible, diamond-hard tread crosses directly over my fault line, it steps into my standing wave. The creature’s lower boundary—that fierce, four-thousand-PSI wall of compressed air that keeps its guts from spilling out across the dirt—is suddenly subjected to a high-frequency vibration that matches its own internal knock, but inverted by a hundred and eighty degrees.

I feed its own limp back into its belly, dead out of phase.

The effect on the giant grazer is instantaneous. The violent, eccentric *THUD* vanishes from its cycle. My acoustic lance reaches up through its air-tread and strikes the heavy, warped lobe of its phantom flywheel, shaving the excess pressure off the eccentric bulge in a continuous, singing hiss of bled-off static. 

For the first time in forty years, the engine runs true. *Clack-shunt. Clack-shunt. Clack-shunt.* 

It does not speed up; instead, it settles. It drops an inch lower to the stone, spreading its weight. A long, shuddering sigh of superheated ozone vents from its crown—not the black, iron-burning soot of a parasitized engine, but the clean, blue-white exhaust of a machine suddenly relieved of a binding bearing. It stops moving forward. It simply sits directly over my seam, idling with a deep, buttery purr that you can almost hear beneath the wider roaring of the valley, allowing me to drink the tremor from its bones.

I am gorging now. Inside the fissure, the standing wave is so dense that the two walls of limestone are no longer touching at any point; they are held apart by a cushion of pure, screaming sound that has turned the air in the crack into a glowing, violet filament of sonoluminescence. The rock along my edges is beginning to sweat—not water, never water, but tiny, extruded beads of molten calcite and magnesium, squeezed out of the stone’s crystal lattice by the sheer, localized terror of my frequency. 

This is how I grow. 

With the stamper sitting on my back, feeding me fifty horsepower of raw vibrational slop every minute, I direct the excess energy down to the tips of my fissure. At the eastern end of the crack, where the dolomite meets a stubborn rib of dark basalt, I focus the standing wave into a needle-point of acoustic shear. 

*Tick. Tick. Tick.*

I am not smashing the basalt like a Drop-Hammer. I am fatiguing it at the molecular level, vibrating the silicon-oxygen bonds at their resonant frequency until they simply let go of one another. The crack lengthens by an eighth of an inch. A quarter. A fine, grey flour of cold, unmelted basalt puffs up from the new tip of the seam like smoke from a fuse, instantly blown away by the hot diesel wind of the plateau. 

I am extending my reach. I am creeping toward the low basin, an inch a day, a yard a year, carving a network of hair-thin capillaries through the skin of the Anvil.

I will hold this stamper here for six hours, maybeeight, until the sun drops toward the western rim and the creature’s internal fuel-state drops low enough that its governor overrides the comfort of my tuning-bed. When it finally lurches forward, breaking contact with my curtain, its old limp will return within fifty yards—for I have not fixed its warped geometry, only propped it up while it paid me my toll.

And when it is gone, and the violet light deep in my throat fades down to a dull, cherry-red hum of retained friction, I will not sleep. 

I will lie here in the dark, three hundred yards long and a thousandth of an inch wide, listening through the soles of the mountain. Miles away, down on the black chrome mirror of the floor, one of the great Foundries gives its forty-five-second thud. The shockwave travels up through the cold-rolled iron, through the sorted yellow sulfur, through the bruised limestone of the slope, and passes across my hairline gap.

I let it pass. It is too deep for me to catch, too wide to trap in my narrow jaws. 

Instead, I listen three miles to the north, where two juvenile condensers have just sheared their coupling and are skittering across the shale, screaming at six thousand RPM, out of balance, running hot, shaking the talus to pieces in their frantic need to find a load. 

I shift the silicon dust in my throat. I narrow my gap by half a micron, tuning my emptiness to the pitch of their distant, approaching whine, and I wait for them to come and be smoothed down.
