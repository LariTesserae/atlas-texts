My jawbone is wedged into the crook of the main eastern collector, right where the sixteen-inch cast-iron pipe drops three feet to clear a slumped blister of black basalt. 

If you looked right at me, you would think I was an extra brace poured fifty years ago to keep the joint from shearing—a heavy, calcified buttress of grey silica, yellow alum, and stiffened wool, tucked down in the dark, hundred-and-ten-degree pocket beneath the pipe’s belly. A thick, pale curtain of tube-worms grows across my left boot, cementing my heel to the bedrock. I haven’t moved that foot since the sun was directly overhead. Two flat lizards are wedged into the warm crease behind my knees, their rough, rust-colored hides rising and falling against my mineral-soaked trousers in a fast, fluttery panic that has nothing to do with my own slow breath.

Through my lower teeth, the basin is loud. 

Not in the ears—my ears were plugged with tallow and lamb’s wool when I was seven, and the sulfur-crust sealed them over for good by the time I was twenty. The sound is in the hinge of my skull, down the thick bones of my neck, and settled deep in the wide, arched cage of my ribs. 

Right now, the eastern collector is singing a heavy, greasy *thump-thump-shhh*. That’s the lithium slurry moving down from Rig Nine, thick as wet mortar, pushed by a piston that has a two-inch score inside its brass sleeve. I know the score is two inches wide because every four strokes, the pressure slips with a sharp, metallic *tick* that makes my left eye-tooth ache. It’s been ticking like that since my grandfather had teeth left to bite the iron. It doesn’t need fixing. You learn which hurts the iron can live with and which ones will split the casing.

Three ridges north, someone is working. 

*Tink. Tink. Tink-tink.* 

It comes down the secondary return line, faint and dry through the salt-plugged flanges, bypassing the mud pots. That’s the short-handled drift-pin. That’s the girl with the crooked collarbone—we don’t have names for each other, only the shape of the strike on the pipe and the feel of the heart through the grease-rag when we press knuckles in the dark. Her strike is always a fraction late on the double-tap because her right shoulder healed too tight after a pumice slide. She’s knocking scale out of the number twelve bleed-valve. She’s waiting for the twenty-minute surge, same as me.

Above my head—maybe six inches above the salt-crusted crown of my leather hood—the air is purple and dead cold. Sometimes, when a draft curls down off the western scarp, it pushes the thermal boundary down an inch or two, and I feel the ghosts of it brushing the highest ridge of my knuckles: a dry, razor-thin nothingness that smells like iron filings and outer space. If I raised my hand to wave at the sky, the skin would freeze solid before the blood could make the turn back down my wrist. 

So I stay folded. Everything down here is folded. 

Between my knees, in the dark shadow where the ultraviolet glare can't bleach them out, three spiders with legs like brass wire are crouched over the shell of a porcelain crab they cracked open an hour ago. They don't mind my shins. My shins are just warm, salty wood to them. One of the spiders has a foot resting on the brass buckle of my shin-guard, feeling for the tremor of the twenty-minute breath just like I am.

I shift my tongue inside my cheek, rolling a ball of salted bristle-worm and grease from the left pocket of my gums to the right. You don't swallow until the steam comes; swallowing takes wet, and you never spend your wet during the dry eighteen. 

*Tink-tink.* 

The girl three ridges over has stopped. She’s wedged her pin. 

A second later, the spider lifts its leg off my buckle. The lizards behind my knees flatten themselves even tighter against my tendons, their little claws hooking into the silica-rind of my canvas pants. 

Down in the dark, four miles beneath my calloused heels, the mountain rolls over in its sleep. 

The collector pipe doesn't shake first; it *swells*. You can feel the iron expand against your jawbone as the superheated surge hits the lower manifolds before the vents even clear their throats. The *thump-thump-shhh* of the slurry pump loses its rhythm, racing suddenly as the back-pressure spikes, the flywheel thirty feet above my head spinning so fast the iron frame of the rig starts to hum in a high, tooth-loosening key that makes the grey bacterial slime on the rocks shiver into standing waves.

Then the cracks open.

It looks like the valley floor is suddenly covered in thick, white grease. The steam doesn't whistle; it punches out of the basalt fractures in short, violent puffs that immediately collapse under the freezing weight of the thin purple air, turning into a scalding, alkaline sleet that falls *sideways* and *down* all at once. Instantly, the world goes blind beyond three feet. The glare of the violet sky breaks into a million greasy rainbows against the outsides of my brass goggles.

My blood jumps. It’s the heat soaking through the crust of my coat, hitting the skin, kicking my sluggish heart from twenty beats a minute up to a hundred. 

I don't stand—you never stand—but I unspool sideways along the pipe like a heavy crab. My left heel tears free from the tube-worms with a silent, leathery rip. Already the porcelain crabs are boiling up over the spot where I was sitting, five deep, their claws scraping frantically at the warm, bare patch of stone I left behind before the new mineral film can harden.

I have three minutes while the salt is soft. 

I reach into the loops at my belly and pull the four-pound wrench—not iron, iron rusts too thick between surges, this one is solid bell-metal, salvaged from a broken condenser housing a hundred years ago, worn smooth and green where my thumb fits. I scuttle up the incline of the collector pipe on all fours, my knee-pads sliding through the fresh, boiling slime of the bacterial mats. Copper locusts are exploding off the ironwork all around me, silent as sparks, bouncing off my leather hood, getting crushed beneath my horn-soled boots as I climb toward the main junction box.

There’s another tamper already there. 

He didn't come up the pipe; he came out of the wire-grass dome six feet to the left, where he’s been baking in the mud-steam since morning. It’s the old one—his beard is a solid, twelve-inch wedge of yellow sulfur and salt that he uses as a chest-rest when he lies on the warm vents. His left lens is cracked and patched with black bitumen, so he only looks at the valve with his right side, his head cocked like a bird's.

We don't nod. We don't waste the neck-grease. 

He slaps his heavy, four-fingered glove against the side of my ribs—*thump, thump*—feeling my lungs pull, making sure my heart hasn't gone fluttery from the thin air. I press my brass wrench against his shoulder blade to let him know I have the leverage. 

The junction valve is weeping thick, orange lithium-butter through its upper packing gland, and the salt has built up a ridge like a shark’s fin that’s keeping the relief lever from dropping. If it doesn't drop in two more cycles, the pipe will blow a seam, and the valley towns four miles down will lose three percent of their pressure—not that we know what the towns do with the sludge, or care, only that a blown seam changes the song in the iron, and a bad song in the iron makes it impossible to sleep when your jaw is on the pipe.

While the scalding dew runs in rivers down our goggles, softening the orange crust to the consistency of wet chalk, the old one wedges his iron bar under the lever. I hook my bell-metal wrench over the top lug and throw my weight backward, hanging off the pipe by my knees like an ape, staying low, keeping my rump beneath the freezing line where the steam shears off flat into the violet dark.

My throat whistles—a dry, thin *hssss* inside my rag mask. The old one’s ribs creak against mine. 

The salt-ridge shears off in a single, forty-pound slab of yellow stone, dropping silently into the swarm of crabs below. They don't even scatter; six of them are crushed flat, and forty more immediately climb onto the broken slab to eat the crushed ones. The relief lever drops four inches with a heavy, internal *clunk* that travels through my wrench, up my wrists, and straight into my breastbone. 

Good. The song in the pipe settles back into its deep, greasy eighty-cycle purr.

Already, the steam is dying. The fractures in the basalt are sucking back their last wet hiccups; the alkaline puddles in the rock folds are skinning over with white salt-ice at their edges while still simmering in the middle. The heat-haze clears with terrifying speed, and the sky snaps back into view—that awful, bruised, weightless violet that wants to pull the water right out through your eyeballs.

The locusts stop jumping. The lizards stop darting and begin to pile up on top of each other again, three and four deep on the newly cleared brass of the junction box, greedily drinking the fresh heat from the unchoked valve.

The old tamper doesn't go back to his wire-grass. The sun is dropping toward the western rim, which means the shadow of the scarp will hit this ridge in an hour, and the thermal blanket will shrink from waist-high down to eighteen inches. You don't want to be caught out in the grass when the cold drops down to the rock.

He slides down the side of the collector pipe, plowing a slow furrow through the clacking porcelain shells, and wedges himself into the hollow beneath the sixteen-inch drop—right where I was sitting. He leaves half the dark pocket open.

I slide down after him, tucking my knees up into his armpit, letting my back settle against the hundred-and-twenty-degree iron of the return line. My wet coat is already stiffening again, the fresh silica and sulfur baking into a new layer of grey rind in the dry heat, gluing my shoulder to his shoulder. Within three minutes, a pale crab climbs over my boot-toe and begins scraping the fresh grease off my bell-metal wrench.

I lean my head back, open my mouth behind my salt-stiffened rag, and set my lower teeth against the flange bolt.

Down the line, three ridges over, the *tink-tink* has stopped. The intake valve on twelve is clear; I can feel the water-hammer gone out of the north return, replaced by a smooth, heavy *shhh-thump* that matches the beat of the old man's heart against my shin. 

I close my eyes behind the brass rims. My breath slows down, stretching out, waiting for the mountain to count to twenty.
