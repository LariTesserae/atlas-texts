I am not on the ledges. If I had knees to bruise or a chest to brace against a wedge, the sheer urgency of the uncompiled rock would drive me mad; I would spend my life exhausted by the effort of swinging the maul. 

Instead, I am three hundred yards out from the northern shelf, suspended at a forty-degree tilt in the violet gloom, held in place by two frayed cables of spun fiberglass-vine tied to a tripod of petrified ash.

I am the fourth slab from the left on a weathered chime-frame. 

It took me a very long time to get here, and a longer time to lose the memory of moving. If you looked closely at my surface—which is pale, banded slate shot through with rust-colored veins of iron—you could still make out the faint, fused seam where my knees were drawn up to my chin three hundred years ago, and the thick, calcified ridge that used to be the horn mask of my face. My eyes are gone, filled in with solidified spore-crust and quartz dust, which is a mercy. I no longer have to look at the nauseating gap in the central massif across the gulf. I no longer have to judge distances that lie.

I have only one job now, and I do it with the entirety of my mass. I take the screaming, jagged tear of the valley’s arrival and I swallow it.

To a living creature down on the ledges, the roar is an assault. To me, hanging taut on my vine-cables, it is a landscape of pure pressure. The vibration comes up from the deep basin—a ragged, stuttering saw-tooth of a frequency, the sound of twenty million tons of feldspar trying to occupy the same three inches of space at the same time. It hits my broad, flat back. If I were ordinary stone, I would flake apart in an hour. 

Instead, the sound enters the dense, labyrinthine hollows of what used to be my ribcage. The great tuning-fork of petrified cartilage in my center catches the jagged wave and forces it to run in circles through my mineralized marrow. I knock the sharp edges off the noise. I slow it down. I turn the shriek of arriving matter into a deep, stubborn, B-flat hum, and I push it back out into the freezing air.

All around me, for fifty yards in every direction, the air is thick and calm with my note. Within my reach, the blood-ferns on the cliffside do not flicker; they crystallize in neat, predictable rows. The gravity beneath my frame does not wobble; a dropped stone falls straight down, obeying the law I am singing to it.

I am not alone on the rack. 

Four feet to my right—though in the broken perspective of the canyon, she sometimes appears to be hanging fifty yards below me—is a newer slab. She was a Striker. She has only been on the frame for ten or twelve years, and she is not entirely stone yet. When the tectonic bowing down in the basin hits a particularly violent pitch, a thin, hairline crack opens along what used to be her left collarbone, and a tiny, lazy ribbon of gray smoke—the last of her internal friction—leaks out and falls sideways toward the cliff. Her note is still a little ragged, a little too high. She is still fighting the rock instead of digesting it. When the shear gets bad, I let my own vibration widen until the edge of my acoustic shadow overlaps her, steadying her until her hum drops back into the groove.

To my left is an empty vine-loop, frayed and stiff with frost. The one who hung there—an old Pin-Maker whose left shoulder had been permanently doubled by a bad seam—finally gave out a few seasons ago. 

I remember the relief of his going. For decades, the internal stress had been building in his lower half, a silent accumulation of millions of tiny fractures caused by a localized fault line directly beneath us. One twilight—though it is always the same twilight—the valley gave a sudden, lurching heave as a new needle of basalt breached the floor three miles away. The shockwave hit us like a solid wall. 

The Pin-Maker didn't crack; he simply ceased to be one piece. With a dull, percussive *thump* that I felt in the deepest part of my core, he separated into eight heavy, triangular shards. 

Because the gravity right under our tripod pulls toward the northeast cliff, he did not fall into the abyss. His pieces drifted upward and sideways, spinning with a slow, majestic indifference through the dry air. I felt his hum break apart into eight smaller, higher chimes, growing fainter as they floated out into the violet haze. Two of his shards caught on a patch of "bleeding" air half a mile out—a shimmering, unstable smear where the cliff was failing to form. The moment his fragments touched the blur, they locked dead in space. The flickering stopped. Around the seed of his chest-bone, fresh, pale granite began to compile, silent and solid.

That is what I am waiting for. 

Every decade or so, a small, crouched figure wrapped in crackling mats of rust-colored fern crawls out onto the narrow spur of rock where our tripod is anchored. It does not look up at us. It simply lays its flat, horn-shielded forehead against the wooden leg of the frame and taps twice with a small iron hammer. *Tap-tap.* 

*Checking the tension.*

The click races up the petrified wood, down the fiberglass vine, and into my ankles. I let the click pass through my core and bounce back down the line, altered by my weight, telling the worker below that I am still whole, though the fissures behind my knees are three inches deeper than they were the last time it asked. The creature down on the rock presses a fresh handful of spore-paste against the base of the wooden leg to keep the rot of unreality away, turns around on all fours, and ratchets itself back into the gloom.

It does not pity me, and I do not envy it its movement. Movement is just the search for a place to stop.

Deep inside my slate chest, where the tuning-organ meets the fused mass of my spine, there is a flaw. It is a tiny, star-shaped pocket of hollow space, no bigger than a chestnut, where the stone didn't quite pack down tight enough when I cooled. Every time the central massif groans, that little star-shaped pocket catches the worst of the harmonic shear. It is hot in there—the only warm part of me left—and every year, its points grow a fraction of a millimeter longer, reaching outward toward my edges.

I am nursing that crack. I am feeding the mountain's rage directly into it. 

One day—maybe in ten years, maybe in five hundred—the valley will give a particularly ugly wrench, and the points of that star will finally reach my skin. The vine-cables will go slack. The long, exhausting pressure of holding this one small corner of the sky together will end. 

I will break into four or five heavy, quiet pieces. I will drift out over the miles-deep drop, past the curtains of fiberglass vine, past the solid shadows and the floating summits, out into the middle of the roaring gulf where the air is still thin and desperate for facts. And when I find the worst of the blur, I will lock into the nothingness, and I will make the rock arrive.
