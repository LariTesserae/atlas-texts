I am not out in the sleet. I am not one of the camelids tearing at the weed, and I am not the boy fifty feet up the pylon with the gull eggs warming against his ribs. 

If you want to find me, you have to come inside Shack Four, dog the hatch shut behind you so the wind stops trying to peel your ears off, and look down through the diamond-mesh grating of the catwalk. Down past the flywheel’s oil-throw, down to where the square brick-and-iron throat of the intake well swallows the black water of the trench. 

Wait until your eyes adjust to the blue-white stutter of the commutator brushes. Look at the main bearing housing on the low-pressure side—the big, cast-iron plummer block that takes the lateral thrust of the turbine shaft. 

I am the third shim in from the housing cap, and the two-inch patch of cured camelid hide and river-clay packed behind it, and I am the specific, slightly flat *tock-tock-hum* that the shaft makes because I am there.

Thirty years ago, the Babbitt metal in this bearing ran hot and poured itself out like solder into the sump because a tangle of bladder-kelp choked the cooling-water pipe while the old man—the grandfather of the woman currently scraping grease off her shins on the grating above me—was two miles away at Shack Six helping deliver a breach-born calf. By the time he poled his *balsa* back through the chop, the shaft was knocking like a loose iron gate in a gale, threatening to shear its bolts and tear the whole floor out of the station.

He didn’t have spare white-metal. He had an old brass belt-buckle from a lowland surveyor who had drowned in the seventies, three copper pennies, a tin soup spoon, and the thick, calloused hide off the hock of an old bull camelid. He melted the metals in an iron ladle over a peat-and-tallow fire right there on the grating, poured a rough, bubbly crescent into a mold of wet bog-ash, and hammered it curved over the horn of the turbine casing. It wasn't thick enough. It left a sixteenth-of-an-inch play. So he took a slice of the green hide, smeared it with fine grey glacial silt from the bottom of the well and black sump-sludge, and wedged it in behind the brass with a cold chisel.

That’s where I live. In that sixteenth of an inch.

It is dark in the housing, and it is never quiet, and the pressure is absolute. The steel shaft—eight inches thick, polished to a dark mirror by decades of friction—spins against my face at four hundred revolutions a minute. The water in the trench below is thirty-four degrees, just above freezing, heavy and dark and slow; the iron two inches from my back is a hundred and四十 degrees, smelling fiercely of cooked mud-fish oil and hot copper. I am the peace treaty between the two.

I know the family above me not by their faces, which I have never seen, but by their weight on the grating and the way they tend the grease-cup screwed into the top of my iron cap. 

The old grandmother, whose lungs rattle like dry seeds in a gourd from sixty years of breathing coal-smoke and ozone, has a heavy, dragging step. When she turns down the brass T-handle of the grease-cup to force fresh tallow down the little dark pipe into my groove, she does it slowly—*creak, pause, creak*—giving the thick fat time to melt and spread across the spinning steel. She talks to the bearing when she does it, a low, nasal hum deep in her throat that vibrates at almost the exact frequency of the generator's sixty-cycle buzz.

The middle daughter, the one with two fingers missing on her left hand from a cable-snap five winters back, is impatient. She wrenches the handle down two full turns at once. A cold, solid slug of tallow hits the hot shaft, briefly choking the oil-ways, and for ten seconds my temperature spikes, the hide behind me tightens and smells faintly of scorched hair, and my pitch rises from a *tock-hum* to a thin, tight *whine* until the friction melts the fat and the shaft skates free again.

And through the iron beneath me, through the long anchor-bolts drilled twelve feet down into the drowned gneiss whaleback, and through the copper cables heading up through the roof, I feel the rest of the Basin.

Iron in water is never alone; it is a long nerve. 

I can feel the big, slow, rubbery thud against the intake grate ten feet below when a river-wolf chases a mud-fish right into the suction current and has to kick hard against the iron bars to break free. I can feel the long, rhythmic *shiver-and-hum* of the guy-wires outside when the wind shifts to the northwest and starts throwing heavy hail instead of sleet. 

And when someone three miles down the line at Shack Five takes a four-pound wrench and hits the corner-post of Pylon Twelve—*clink-clink-clank*—the shockwave doesn't just ring in the air of the shack so the family looks up from their tea. It travels down the wire, through the transformer bushings, down the grounding strap bolted to the turbine bedplate, and right through my brass-and-leather belly. For a thousandth of a second, the four-hundred-RPM spin of the shaft stutters against the microscopic ripple in the metal. 

I am forty years past the warranty of the company down in the sunlit valley that doesn't know I exist. According to every rule of metallurgy and mechanical engineering, I should have seized, melted, and cracked the housing during the great freeze of the last decade. 

Instead, the glacial silt embedded in my leather backing has polished the steel journal to a jewel-finish no lathe could ever achieve. The copper from the old pennies has smeared itself into the microscopic pores of the shaft, creating a slick, bronze skin. I am not a part anymore; I am a callus the machine grew so it wouldn't die in the cold.

Tonight the barometer is dropping fast. I can tell because the water in the intake well is surging higher, pushing harder against the turbine blades below, trying to speed the shaft up past its governor. The governor weights are flying out, the linkage is clattering, and the load on my face is doubling. The iron is getting hotter. 

Above me, the hatch clangs shut. The family is in for the night. I feel the four of them settle onto the warm iron belly of the casing—four small, heavy weights leaning against the curve of the housing, soaking up the heat that my friction helps make. 

A hand reaches down through the dark, greasy gap behind the flywheel. Fingers—short, thick, warm, smelling of wood-ash and dried fish—touch the outside of my cast-iron cap to check the heat. They linger for a second, feeling the steady, slightly flat *tock-tock-hum* that means the clearance is holding, the oil is flowing, and the water is going through. The hand gives the grease-cup a quarter-turn—just enough—and pulls back up into the yellow light of the tallow lamp.

I drink the warm fat, take the weight of the mountain water on my curved brass face, and keep spinning in the dark.
