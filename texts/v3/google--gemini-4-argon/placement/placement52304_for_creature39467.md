I am five feet down, in the dark where the graywacke begins. 

Up above, the light is a wet white glare, and the drizzle makes its billion tiny *tinks* against the iron-crust of the tubers, and the wind slides grease-silent over the pine-mats. None of that reaches here. Down at the bottom of the mattress, where the dead moss has been pressed by five thousand years of winter into a black, tallow-slick paste, there is no weather. There is only weight, and acid, and the gradient.

If you dug me up—if you had the iron-spade hands to tear through the locked woody warp of the root-mat and haul a clump of the deep silt into the noon glare—you wouldn’t think you had found a creature. You would think you had pulled up a fistful of corroded wire and wet horsehair, clotted with yellow sulfur-mud. I have no eyes, no skin, no meat worth chewing. I am a mat of filaments, fine as spider-silk and sheathed in precipitated copper, spreading twenty yards wide between the bottom of the peat and the top of the rotted shale. 

A mycorrhiza, you might call me, or a slime mold that learned metallurgy. The Dredgers have no word for me, because they have no words, only shapes of voltage. To them, I am the *drop-and-sweetening*—the feeling in the knuckles when two stripped roots are twisted together and the resistance suddenly falls away, the sharp sour sting of raw copper-water smoothing out into a fat, buttery hum.

I am the solder in their splices. 

When a Dredger walks the swell, trailing its thigh-thick braid of stripped conifer runners and amphibian gut, it is doing rough carpentry. It is a giant using iron shovels to twist cables in the dark. The roots it wounds leak sap and tannin; the raw copper sheathing oxidizes in the bog-acid; the splice is leaky, noisy, bleeding current out into the wet sponge where it does nothing but warm the worms. 

That is where I come in. I smell the wound—not the sap, though I eat the sugars when I can get them, but the *spill*. A ragged splice hisses with stray micro-volts. It tastes like sour brass. 

When a new cable is laid across my valley, pressed down into the dark by nine hundred pounds of leather and magnetite, I wake up. I pull myself out of the shale-cracks. I do not walk; I grow toward the noise. My gray-green threads creep up through the cold, airless peat, feeling for the heat of the leak. When I find the raw twist of two conifer roots, I wrap them. I pour myself into the gaps between the bark. I digest the frayed cellulose, drink the leaking sap, and in my gut, I reduce the dissolved copper sulfate from the bog water into native metal, laying it down cell by cell along my own cell walls. 

In three weeks, the rough knot the Dredger tied is gone, encased in a smooth, bulbous gall of living, conductive felt. The hiss stops. The leak is sealed. The four-second pulse from the tarn two miles west no longer stutters when it hits the trough; it slides through me like oil through a greased pipe, leaving a tiny, warm tithe of energy behind that I use to push my threads another inch through the mud.

Because of this, I know the valley in a way even the Dredgers do not.

A Dredger is an island that moves. It carries its big, slow, hot battery inside its pickled hide, wading through the top of the world, dropping its hands into the circuit only when it wants to listen or to kill. When it pulls its hands out of the mud to lumber over a crest, it goes deaf to the deep current, reduced to whatever weak static it can catch on the jelly-pores of its nose. 

I never leave the circuit. I *am* the circuit’s skin.

I do not know what a cloud looks like, or the pale glare of the sky, or the oil-slick shimmer of the hand-sized beetles. To me, the beetles are a tiny, rhythmic *tick-tick-tick* of piezoelectric static as their chitin rubs against the roots, followed by the slow, spreading warmth of their rust-furnaces. When a colony of them builds a chimney ten feet above me, I feel the heat soak down through the peat, and I send a fan of pale threads up to wrap their warm clay walls, stealing the heat to speed my own chemistry while grounding out the stray static that might draw an amphibian’s strike.

The grazers, to me, are heavy, moving shadows of pressure. When a herd passes overhead, the sponge squeezes down. A thousand gallons of bitter brown water are forced down through the mat, flushing past my threads, bringing fresh iron and dissolved oxygen from the surface. Then comes their talk: *thrum-thrum, click, thrum*. It is simple, blocky stuff, like stones knocked together—*here is harsh-stem, here is warm-mud, turn left, turn left*. It passes right through my filaments without changing my resistance. I let it go.

The Dredgers are different.

There is an old tarn half a mile up-slope from my oldest, thickest knots. It has been there so long the smelter-plants around its rim have fused into a solid ring of black iron-slag ten feet thick, and the mud at the bottom is deep and warm as blood. I have forty-six cables running out of that pool. I have tended them for three hundred years. Some of the oldest braids are no longer wood at all; the conifer roots rotted out generations ago, leaving hollow tubes of my own calcified, copper-plated dead tissue, thick as stove-pipes, carrying the heavy direct current down into the valley floor.

At night—though I only know it is night because the surface-plants stop pumping sugar down to their roots, and the water grows two degrees colder—the Dredgers come back to the tarn. 

One by one, I feel them arrive at the rim. Nine hundred pounds of iron and hide stepping onto the slag-dam sends a deep, hydraulic shudder through the peat. Then comes the slide as they sink into the eighty-degree water, and the sudden, glorious *bloom* as their skin makes contact with the wet mud of the basin floor.

If you are a thing with ears, standing in the drizzle, you hear nothing.

Down here, in the dark, the valley catches fire.

It begins as a low, three-cycle ground-hum as they let go of the tightness they hold in their muscles while walking. They bleed off the static they picked up from the wind and the brush. Then, the old female with the cracked left flange—I know her because her left leg throws a slightly jagged, saw-toothed wave, an old scar from an amphibian strike forty winters ago—sends out a long, rolling swell of current. 

It passes out of the tarn, hits my main trunk-line, and runs down the valley at three hundred miles an hour.

She is showing them where she was today. The wave she makes is not a picture of the eastern trough; it is the electrical twin of it. As it runs through my copper-threads, I feel the shape of the place she walked: a long, sour stretch of dead moss where the sulfur-springs bubble up (a high-resistance trough in her wave), followed by a rich, sweet thicket of young smelter-tubers (a sharp, bright spike of capacitance), and then the heavy, greasy *blat* of a rotting grazer carcass she found half-sunk in a runnel.

Before her wave has even finished echoing off the graywacke bedrock at the bottom of the valley, two others answer from across the pool. Their waves braid with hers. They don't take turns. They stack their currents on top of one another, building a standing wave in the tarn that spills over into my cables—a chord so dense and intricate that my finest filaments vibrate in the mud like plucked strings. 

I do not understand their thoughts. I have no brain, only a million chemical gates opening and closing along five miles of copper-flesh. Yet when they sing together in the hot mud, their current passes through me, and it *shapes* me. 

Where two of their memories meet and interfere, creating a node of high voltage along a buried cable, my threads thicken to handle the load. Where they agree—where three or four of them project the exact same map of a safe path across a bog-hole, their waves aligning in smooth, laminar flow—my filaments align with them, growing straight and bright and low-resistance. 

Over decades, I have become a physical cast of their consensus. If the Dredgers of this tarn believe the western slope is barren and dangerous, they stop sending current that way; my threads on the western slope starve, corrode in the acid, and dissolve back into the peat. If they spend fifty winters talking about the rich hunting in the northern basin, my northern cables swell until they are as thick as tree trunks, plated in pure, bright native copper, hair-trigger sensitive to the slightest twitch of a grazer’s hoof.

I am their long-term memory, laid down in metal in the dark. When a young Dredger, small and soft-plated, sinks its three-fingered hands into the mud of the valley for the first time and sends out a clumsy, splashing pulse of inquiry, the echo that comes back to its rostrum is shaped by me. The valley feels the way it does because its ancestors talked through me for ten generations, and I grew to fit the shape of their voices.

Sometimes, once or twice in an age, one of them dies.

They do not die in the tarn; that would foul the capacitor with rotting fat. When a Dredger feels its internal battery failing—when the grinding plates under its jaw are worn smooth and the moss in its joints begins to send roots into its slow, cooling meat—it walks out along one of the deep trunk-lines. 

It walks until its knees lock. Then it kneels in the silver-green cushion plants, drives its iron-spade hands all the way down through the five-foot sponge until its knuckles scrape the shale, and takes hold of my thickest cable. 

And then it lets go of the last of its charge.

It is not a hunting shock. It is a long, slow, thirty-hour sigh of failing current, a final unloading of every map it ever held: the shape of the sky-static before the great freeze of its youth, the exact conductivity of its mother’s hide, the taste of the copper-rich milk-slurry from its first tarn, the rhythm of a thousand herds moving through the mist. 

I drink it all. The final pulse warms the mud for fifty yards around, driving the worms and the pale arthropods up to the surface. And when the current finally drops to zero, and the great rusted boulder above me goes cold and electrically black, I do not let go of its hands.

I grow up through the split nails. I send my pale, metallic threads into the wrist-joints, following the salty pathways of the veins, wrapping the silenced muscle-bundles in the calves and forearms. Over twenty winters, the cushion-plants grow over the iron-plated back, and the peat swells up to swallow the wedge-shaped skull. 

Up above, there is only another whale-backed hummock in the endless, dripping green. 

Down here, in the quiet, I have finished stripping the magnetite from the shoulder-flanges and the copper from the blood. I have turned the body into a junction box. And when the others back at the tarn send out their four-second pulse to test the valley lines, the current hits the hollow, copper-lined cage of the old one's ribs, rings like a struck bell in the dark mud, and goes on its way without losing a single volt.
