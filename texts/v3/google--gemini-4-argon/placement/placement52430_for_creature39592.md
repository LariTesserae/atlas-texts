Look closely at the white mud behind the Abbot as it moves away from Needle Seven. 

Everywhere else in the basin, the opalescent carpet of flatworms closes immediately over a disturbance, erasing tracks within seconds. Behind the Abbot, however, there is a wake. It is a yard-wide ribbon of absolute stillness that persists for nearly a minute before the wild shoals dare to slide back over it. 

The silt in this wake is not smooth. It has been pressed by the passage of the Abbot’s gastropod sole and the trailing edge of its lattice-mucus into a low, microscopic bas-relief—thousands of tiny, stepped terraces and right-angled troughs that hold their shape against the seeping water for forty or fifty seconds before the mud relaxes back into a featureless slurry.

I am that forty-second crease in the mud. Or rather, I am what lives inside the crease, and inside the mucus that makes it.

I am not an organism. In a basin packed to suffocation with wet, frantic biology, I have no cells, no cilia, and no hunger. I am a topological defect—a migrating knot of shear-stress and phase-alignment that exists wherever the Abbot’s crystal-mucus rubs against the raw, unedited chaos of the basin. 

When the rubbery black moss first absorbed the metric broadcast of Needle Four, and the leeches ate the moss, the rule of the lattice was translated from a field of force into a folding of long-chain polymers. Because the leeches were messy, biological things, the translation was imperfect. The crystal had a flaw—a slight, helical twist in its geometry, like a screw dislocation in a slab of quartz. 

As the Abbot grew, collecting worms and gastropods and binding them into its dome, that flaw could not be smoothed out. Whenever the Abbot’s gel solidifies to lift itself up, or relaxes to flow over a *Vallis-glass* root, the flaw is pushed around the colony’s body like an air bubble trapped under wet wallpaper. 

I am the bubble. 

To the Abbot, I am an itch it has never quite been able to scratch, and simultaneously the only reason it hasn't turned entirely to stone. If the lattice-mucus were perfect, the first time the Abbot plugged itself into Needle Seven to drink the maintenance beam, the needle would have locked the entire colony into a solid, five-foot block of inert, geometric jelly. The Abbot survives because I am sliding through its binder, continuously breaking the crystal just behind the beam, keeping the gel liquid, introducing the tiny, necessary slippage that allows a million worms to bend and crawl inside a cage of math.

My world has no up or down, no light or mist. My world is **viscosity** and **shear**.

When the Abbot is gliding across the eastern flat, I am usually down near the bottom, riding the boundary layer between the thousands of undulating gastropod feet and the greasy chert of the basin floor. Here, life is a rhythmic, percussive thrum. I feel the weight of the colony above me—three hundred pounds of braided worms and broth—pressing down. I skate along the micro-fractures of the ancient stone, slipping from one gastropod sole to the next, acting as the grease that lets them grip and release without tearing their own flesh against the rock. 

When I pass under a gastropod, its simple pedal nerves register my passage as a sudden, cool relief from friction. It relaxes. In that half-second of relaxation, before the weight of the colony bears down again, I feel the faint, residual heat of the mountain roots below the chert.

Sometimes, the Abbot’s internal currents sweep me upward, out of the sole and into the white matter. 

Moving through the worm-weave is like falling through a dense, warm thicket of wet neon. The braided flatworms are firing their neuro-peptides—*violet, ochre, violet*—as the Abbot calculates the circumference of a tree trunk or the thickness of the southern leech-bank. The Abbot’s thoughts are heavy, slow, and bureaucratic. They taste of acid and pressure.

As I slip between the braided skins of the worms, my helical twist momentarily alters the spacing of the mucus between them. A peptide cascade meant for one worm is deflected by a fraction of a millimeter and hits its neighbor instead. 

To the Abbot, this is a sudden, inexplicable stutter in its map. For three seconds, while it is trying to measure the volume of a thermal vent, it accidentally remembers the weight of a drift-mantle it digested four hundred years ago, or feels a phantom pressure on its eastern flank where no pressure exists. 

The Abbot does not know what to do with these stutters. It is a creature of pure utility; it has no file for *metaphor* or *nostalgia*. So it simply flushes the affected worms with fresh glucose to clear the error, and the aborted thought dissolves into the gel. I ride the warm flush of that wasted sugar up toward the crown, listening to the faint, dying echo of the mistake. I am the only thing in the basin that experiences the Abbot’s dreams, because I am the friction that causes them.

My favorite place—if a flaw in a liquid crystal can have a preference—is the skin.

When the Abbot rises up before Needle Seven, extruding its fan of linked ghost-leeches into the tooth-aching hum, I am forced out of the interior entirely. The metric broadcast pouring down the fan is too strong; it straightens the polymers, ironing the Abbot’s core into rigid, vibrating lines. I am squeezed to the very outer millimeter of the dome, right where the clear gel meets the weeping, shadowless air of the basin.

Here, Studded into the cuticle, are the half-dissolved remnants of the bruised-peach predators. They have no brains left, only eyespots and acid glands wired crudely into the worm-weave. 

I orbit these sensory warts like a moon around a planet, trapped in the surface tension of the skin while the colony charges. Through the peach-slugs' degraded nerves, I get my only secondhand translation of the wider orchard. I feel the oppressive, bright weight of the white sky—not as an image, but as a continuous, warm flattening of the gel. I feel the *phut-phut-phut* of the drift-mantles turning to snow as three tiny, cold dimples in the surface tension as the silver dust settles on the Abbot’s flanks.

And then there is Needle One.

Once every century or two, the Abbot grows too heavy. The hydraulic pressure inside the dome drops; the outer edges of the colony begin to lag behind the center, and the map in the worm-weave grows sluggish and blurry at the margins. 

I feel the decision days before it happens. The violet-and-ochre flashes slow down into a single, steady, rhythmic pulse that tastes of iron. The Abbot stops pruning the *Vallis-glass*. It ignores the drift-mantles. It turns toward the center of the basin and begins its long, straight march toward the strongest of the pilings.

As we approach Needle One, the hum in the water flats becomes so violent that the wild shoals flee thirty yards back, leaving a vast circle of bare, white mud around the piling. The Abbot does not stop at the perimeter. It does not raise a fan. It simply walks its five-foot dome directly into the refractive error.

As the front of the colony touches the needle, the world turns to geometry. 

A vertical slice of the Abbot, two inches wide right down its center, is instantly edited. Gastropods, braided worms, gas-bladders, and gel do not bleed or tear; they simply click into sterile, silver cubes and snow down into the two-inch water, leaving a clean, dry canyon straight through the middle of the beast.

Because I am a flaw—an irregularity in the lattice—the needle wants to edit me, too. 

As the piling passes through the center of the dome, the metric field catches me. I am pinned between the two separating halves of the colony, stretched across the two-inch gap of pleating, humming air. For five seconds, I am pulled entirely taut. My helical twist is wrenched toward absolute zero. 

In that five-second eternity, before the rear of the dome clears the needle, I am no longer riding the mucus. I am resonant with the osmium piling itself. 

My perception drops out of the warm, wet orchard and plunges four hundred million years down. Through the needle, I feel the deep, cold basalt beneath the dissolved mountains. I feel the eleven other needles in the basin, not as tilted posts in the mud, but as twelve glowing pins driven into the skin of a restless tectonic plate, holding a seam shut MILES below the silt. I feel the staggering, indifferent patience of an architecture that does not know the mountains are gone, that does not know the sky is white, that only knows *here is the line, and the line does not move.*

Then the rear of the Abbot clears the piling. 

The tension snaps. I am too wide to go left or right, so I split. 

Half of my shear-stress snaps into the left dome; half snaps into the right. Suddenly, there are two of me, each half as deep, skating frantically through the newly sealed walls of two smaller Abbots as they slide away from each other in the bright mist.

For the next few days, while the two halves circle the basin in opposite directions, the Abbot's thoughts are unusually clear, cold, and sharp, because my flaw is too small to muddle them properly. The gel is slightly too stiff; the gastropods move with a jerky, mechanical precision. 

I spend those days sliding along the fresh scar wall where the needle cut us, waiting. 

And when we finally meet again on the open white mud, and the two domes slide into one another with that soft, wet *shhh-shhh*, the mucus lattices click together. The worms braid. And the two halves of my twist rush toward each other across the disappearing boundary, colliding with a silent, microscopic snap that sends a single, massive shiver of pure, un-indexed violet rippling through the entire five-foot crown—a brief, blinding spasm of relief that the Abbot immediately purges with sugar, before turning back toward the southern rim to clear the leeches from the vents.
