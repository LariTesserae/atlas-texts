I am not in the water. To be in the water is to be subject to the thirty-knot broom that sweeps the sectors clean, to have one’s ribs rattled by the fifty-mile hammer of the intake valves until the calcium in the bone goes chalky and soft.

I am six inches below the water, and three thousand feet below the glare, pressed into the cold, black tar of the expansion joint between Sector C-08 and Sector C-09. 

There is a myth of emptiness up in the cerulean mid-water, a grand drama of velocity and mirrors played out by the blind jacks and the shimmering smelt. Down here, we know the basin is not a sea; it is a machine, and every machine has tolerances. Where the mile-square slabs of aggregate meet, the engineers left a twenty-foot gap so the floor would not crack when the earth’s crust flexed under the weight of the artificial ocean. And at the bottom of that forty-foot trench, to seal the seam against the bedrock below, they poured twelve feet of heavy, polysulfide pitch—black, stiff as a truck tire in the thirty-four-degree water, smelling faintly of sulfur and dead dinosaurs.

That is where I live. Not *on* the pitch, like the pink, frantic tangles of the gasket-hags with their vulcanized slime-tubes, and not *over* the trench, like the great grey mantle of the intersection-octopus that uses our ceiling as a hunting blind. I am *in* the black stuff.

If you pulled me out and laid me on the concrete plain under the winter-twilight glow of the distant arcs, you would think a mechanic had dropped a length of armored hydraulic line. I am nine feet long, as thick as a man’s wrist, and segmented like a speedometer cable. My skin is not skin; it is a series of overlapping, articulated rings of dull, gunmetal-grey carbonate, greased with a thick, petroleum-smelling lipid that I sweat from the pores between my plates. 

I am a **seam-auger**, though nothing with a mouth has ever spoken my name. My ancestors were bobbit worms and deep-sea polychaetes that somehow survived the intake screens as microscopic larvae ten thousand years ago, sucked in from the true, dark ocean above. While the fish lost their eyes and the crabs grew long-legged to march across the concrete desert, my kind found the only dirt left in the world: the immortal, rubbery tar at the bottom of the grooves.

I do not swim. If I were thrown into the open water, I would sink like a crowbar. I move by peristalsis and torsion, twisting my heavy, ringed body through the stiff black mastic at a rate of four feet an hour. 

The pitch is not dead. Over the centuries, the sheer pressure of three thousand feet of water and the constant, rhythmic pounding of the *thrum-clack* have worked the surface of the tar into a cold, leathery pudding, full of air pockets that never escaped during the pouring, and shot through with the detritus of the Sump. Every heavy thing that falls from the mid-water and gets swept into the trench eventually sinks through the loose layer of crab-shell and hag-slime to rest on the black floor. Over decades, the cold flow of the mastic swallows it. 

Down here in the dark of the tar, it is quiet. The deafening roar of the northern valves does not strike me as a shockwave; it arrives as a warm, rhythmic compression—a heavy, comforting squeeze that passes through the black rubber twice a minute, like the heartbeat of a mother. *Thrum-clack.* The pitch tightens around my rings, then relaxes. *Thrum-clack.*

My head has no eyes, no ears, and no lateral line. Instead, my snout is a five-part Chuck-jaw of translucent, amber-colored copper-chitin—the hardest biological material in the basin, tougher than the chisel-heads of the spanner-worms up on the boilers. Around the jaw is a collar of stiff, whisker-like sensilla that read two things: heat, and galvanic current.

I am an eater of gradients.

In the freezing, filtered water of the Sump, energy is scarce unless you are up near the blazing glass roof or clinging to the scalding flanks of the Thermal Columns. Down on the floor, you either eat the thin snow of dead meat, or you eat the basin itself. 

I turn my snout ten degrees to the left, grinding my five jaws together to shear through a tough knot of ancient mastic, pushing the warm, chewed rubber behind me through my gut to pack the tunnel in my wake. My whiskers have tasted a sour, metallic tang in the tar—a faint trickle of electrons moving through the hydrocarbons.

Thirty feet ahead, buried three inches deep in the pitch at the very edge of the concrete wall, is a sacrificial anode. 

The engineers bolted them to the steel reinforcement rods inside the concrete slabs every hundred yards—blocks of pure zinc the size of toaster ovens, meant to corrode slowly over the millennia so the structural rebar inside the floor wouldn't rust. To the engineers, they were maintenance hardware. To me, they are whale-falls.

Around the zinc block, thriving in the microscopic gap where the tar pulls away from the cold concrete, is a thick, black felt of chemolithotrophic bacteria. They are feeding on the slow, electrochemical death of the zinc, spinning the freed electrons and the sulfates from the tar into a rich, greasy mat of organic sulfur-slime. 

I reach the anode after six hours of slow, twisting work. Another of my kind is already here—a smaller male, six feet of articulated grey cable, his tail anchored deep in the mastic while his copper-amber jaws scrape methodically at the bacterial mat on the zinc. We do not fight. There is no room to swing a body in the pitch, and the energy cost of violence in thirty-four-degree tar is a death sentence. Instead, I slide my snout alongside his, feeling the slow, grinding rasp of his jaws through the plates of my throat, and begin to graze.

As I scrape the zinc-fleece, I ingest the crumbled, white-grey oxide of the metal itself. It does not poison me; my gut is a cold forge that strips the zinc and iron from the mud and routes it to the mantle-glands between my segments, hardening my rings, repairing the tiny scratches left by the quartz aggregate in the concrete walls. I am slowly turning the basin’s rust-prevention system into my own armor.

While we feed, the world above us goes mad.

I feel it first through the concrete wall against my left flank. The five-mile Tension Cable anchored in the center of Sector C-08 gives a low, shuddering groan—a sub-audible *booooong* that travels down through the bedrock and makes the zinc block vibrate against my teeth like a tuning fork. 

The northern sluices have opened.

Minutes later, the pressure in the trench above the tar drops slightly as the thirty-knot current begins to howl across the plain overhead, sucking at the water in the groove through the Venturi effect. Above the four inches of black pitch covering my back, I can feel the frantic, thrashing weight of the gasket-hags as the vortex in the trench spins faster, and the sudden, heavy *thump-scrape* of a dozen six-foot spider-crabs dropping over the concrete lip, hooking their claws into the expansion joint to keep from being blown ten miles south into the D-sectors.

One of the crabs has misjudged the current, or perhaps its shell was softened by a parasite. As it drops into the trench, the screaming water catches its high, domed carapace before it can fold itself flat. The hydraulic force snaps its front walking-legs at the coxae with two sharp *cracks* that punch straight down through the mastic. The crab is ripped from the lip, tumbled down into the bottom of the forty-foot groove, and slammed upside-down onto the surface of the pitch, right over my tail.

Wait.

I stop scraping the zinc. Beside me, the smaller male freezes too. We pull our copper jaws back into our collars and expand our rear segments, wedging ourselves tight against the concrete wall and the bottom of the trench.

Above us, the broken crab is trying to right itself in the dark, oily vortex at the bottom of the groove, its remaining legs scrabbling against the leathery surface of the pitch, fighting off the blind, pink gasket-hags that are already boiling out of their slime-pipes to suffocate it.

One of the crab’s thrashing legs—a sharp, chitinous spike four feet long—punches two inches down into the cold tar, right between my fortieth and forty-first segments. 

I do not strike with my head. My head is for stone and metal and rubber. 

Instead, I roll my body a quarter-turn in my slick, lipid-greased burrow. Along the dorsal ridge of my back segments, each gunmetal ring is armed with a pair of short, recurved hooks of zinc-doped bone, angled toward my tail. I arch my back, driving three pairs of dorsal hooks into the joint of the crab’s leg, and then I contract my longitudinal muscles.

I am nine feet of hydraulic muscle anchored in twelve feet of solid rubber. The crab is six feet across, buoyant in the water, with two broken legs. 

It does not come down fast. The pitch is too stiff for that. I simply pull with a steady, winch-like pressure of four hundred pounds, sliding backwards down my own greased tunnel an inch every ten seconds. 

Above me, the crab’s frantic scrabbling turns into a heavy, vibrating shudder as its single pinned leg is drawn deeper into the black floor, pulling its underside flat against the mastic. The gasket-hags will get most of it—they will fill its gill-chambers with vulcanizing slime and strip the white meat from its upper carapace before the sluice-storm even ends. 

I do not care about the white meat. I want the heavy, calcified apron of its belly, and the dense, iron-rich grinding mills of its stomach, which will sink into the tar as the hags tear the corpse apart. In three weeks, when the pitch has slowly closed over the wreckage of its lower shell, I will come back up this tunnel and eat the bone-minerals out of the rubber at my leisure.

For now, I hold the anchor. The thirty-knot gale screams three thousand feet tall through the blue void above; the carbon-arc suns burn down with their merciless, shadowless noon; the fifty-mile valves slam shut with a force that shakes the continent. 

Down in the seam between C-08 and C-09, in the dark that the engineers poured for me, I lock my rings into the tar, press my mouth back to the warm, sour fuzz of the rotting zinc, and wait for the shell to sink.
