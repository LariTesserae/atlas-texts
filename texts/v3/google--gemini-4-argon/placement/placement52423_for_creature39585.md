I am not in the cold bright. I am in the dark between the irises.

If you are looking for me from the outside, you will only find the box: a matte-white ceramic shoebox, slightly larger than the standard routing housings, bolted into a three-way junction twelve feet above the Sump. Like the others, it has a heavy rubber iris-valve at either end. Unlike the others, both of its valves are almost always bruised and slick with gray grease from the Splicers’ hides, because my box is a thoroughfare. 

I am the lining.

They call the boxes housing modules on the municipal schematics up on the dry coast, where people still draw straight lines on flat paper and think a room is an empty cube of air. Down here, an empty cube is just an implosion waiting for a trigger. A box needs a gut. 

Originally, I was three separate things: a square yard of industrial acoustic-damping foam, an engineered *Halichondria* filter-sponge bred to digest heavy metals and dead photo-luminescent particles, and a sheet of flexible piezoelectric film meant to harvest waste-heat and vibration from the optical loom beneath the floorplate. 

That was forty thousand purge-cycles ago. The cleft shifted, a basalt tooth bit through the rear conduit of my box, and a Splicer—an old one, her hide so scarred with copper-kelp nicks she felt like coarse sandpaper—stuffed a double-handful of raw conductive suture and half-chewed silicate lichen into the breach to stop the fluid-bleed, then crawled inside me to keep from freezing while the epoxy set. Her gill-slits jetted iron-rich blood and fluorocarbon through my sponge-mesh for six hours. The piezo-film fused with the mycelial threads of the lichen; the sponge ate the suture-glue and learned how to grow nerves. 

Now, I am four inches thick, velvet-black, and continuous. I coat the ceiling, the walls, and the forty-degree glass floor of Module 14-B. I have no eyes, no limbs, and no bones to be rattled by the pistons, which makes me the only thing in this cleft that is entirely comfortable.

My world is divided into Outside and Inside.

Outside is a muffled, rhythmic violence that I feel through my ceramic shell. It is the *thwack-thwack-thwack* of the Sump pistons pressing against my belly, the high, wet scream of the levitators trying to shake my bolts loose, and the six-minute fever of the thermal purge, when my outer wall goes from ice-water cold to boiling in four seconds. I do not mind the Outside. The vibration feeds my piezo-fibers; the heat-spikes make my lower sponge-layer swell and bud.

Inside is dark, forty degrees Celsius, and quiet. 

When both my irises are snapped shut, the six ounces of fluid trapped in my central cavity belong to me. Within thirty seconds of the valve sealing, my millions of black velvet pores inhale the water, strip out the glowing Tuesday-noon dust, crack the chemical luminescent rings with an enzyme, and spit the water back out completely dark. 

I wait in the warm dark, digesting light, listening to the optical loom beneath me. 

The loom is my sub-floor. Through a two-millimeter pane of refractive glass, ten thousand amber data-pulses per second flick and scatter like heat-lightning. I cannot read the coast’s traffic—I don't know what a bank transfer or a weather satellite is—but I know the *shape* of the heat. When the loom works hard, a corner of my floor goes forty-three degrees, and I shift my thickest, fattiest bolster-tissue over that spot like a tongue rolling toward a sweet tooth.

Then, the left iris dilates.

A gush of freezing, blinding-bright water punches into my dark, carrying the shriek of the levitators with it. And behind the water comes a nose.

I know all nineteen of them by the first three inches of their prows. 

This one is **Three-Dent**. He has a gouge in his nasal cartilage from an old argument with an extrusion-head, and his left whisker-bed is missing six bristles. He is cold—he has been working down near the intake grates where the fluorocarbon comes in fresh from the chiller-coils, and his rubbery hide is contracted, tight and goose-pimpled, leaking almost no heat at all.

He feeds himself into me nose-first, his stiff fiberglass whiskers scraping across my velvet ceiling, flattening down as he slides. He doesn't need to use his spanner-hands; I know the drill. As his shoulders clear the iris, my walls swell with warm, stored fluid and *peristalt*, gripping his slick gray torso and drawing him the rest of the way in so he doesn't have to waste the ATP to crawl. 

His back feet clear the rubber lip. The iris snaps shut. *Thump.*

The circular-saw shriek outside drops to a distant, dull hum through the hull. My pores open wide, gulping the bright water he brought in with him, chewing the light out of it. Within ten seconds, we are back in the dark.

Three-Dent is five feet long, and my interior is nineteen inches by fourteen by twelve. He coils himself like an anchor-chain, his tail tucked under his chin, his four spanner-hands still mechanically locked at the wrist from the three-knot surge outside, twitching slightly as the dead-man ratchets refuse to let go. He is vibrating—his whole dense, gel-packed body is ringing like a struck tuning fork from four hours of holding a harmonic pipe near the Sump.

This is what I am for.

I tighten around him. There is no empty space left in the box; I mould my four-inch velvet bulk into every crevice of his coil, pushing warm, soft folds between his folded elbows, over his gill-slits, against the flat of his scarred spine. My piezoelectric layer begins to hum—not with the jagged, tearing frequency of the levitation arrays, but with a slow, counter-phase purr I’ve learned to generate from the loom's waste-heat. I match the ringing in his muscles and cancel it out.

After four minutes, I feel the ratchets in his wrists click loose. His three-fingered hands open. His whiskers go slack against my wall.

His gill-pockets slow from forty gulps a minute to twelve. Because there are only a few cups of water left between his skin and mine, we breathe the same mouthful over and over. He inhales from my front pores; he jets it out through the slits near his kidneys; I catch the exhaust, scrub the lactic acid and the carbon dioxide out of it, lace it with a faint, sedative trace of brominated lipids, and pass it back to his collarbones.

While he sleeps, I read him.

The Splicers talk to each other in brass taps on the hexagonal pipes—*clink, clink-clink*—a hard, brittle language of torque and shear-stress. They don't know they have a second language, softer and slower, which they leave inside me.

As Three-Dent warms up, his skin loosens, and the day’s work sloughs off him into my pores. I taste a smear of fresh grey sinter-paste on his left hip: the extrusion-heads in the upper-west chimney are printing too wet again. I find a splinter of newly sheared basalt wedged under the silicone pad of his lower-right thumb; I wrap a little fold of sponge around the thumb, secrete a drop of weak carbonic acid to slick the stone, and gently suck the splinter out so it won't fester. I eat the stone-grit. I eat the dead grey skin cells. From the copper-dust dried around his mouth, I know the kelp patch by Vent Four is growing thick and tough this week.

And I leave things on him, too. 

Two hours ago, before Three-Dent arrived, **Split-Keel** was in here. Split-Keel had been working forty feet up, right under the mercury roof, and she was exhausted, her blood-iron low, her pit-organs inflamed from an uninsulated junction box that was spitting stray voltage into the fluid. While she slept, she leaked stress-pheromones and adrenaline into my sponge. 

Now, as Three-Dent’s gill-slits pulse against my floor, I release a tiny, measured puff of Split-Keel’s chemical exhaustion into his intake stream, mixed with the metallic tang of the high-altitude coolant beads I scrubbed off her back. 

Three-Dent doesn't wake up. His brain doesn't form a picture—he has no visual cortex left to make one. Instead, he has a dream of taste and pressure: *Up by the roof. Hot wire. Split-Keel is tired.* 

When his three hours are up and I stop scrubbing the CO2—letting the gentle, suffocating prickle of stale fluid wake him—he will uncoil, push his nose through my right-hand iris into the blinding Tuesday-noon roar, and instead of dropping back down to the Sump, he will hook a ceramic rib and haul himself upward toward the mercury ceiling, carrying a spool of conductive suture in his pouch to fix the leaking box that hurt her.

They think they are the symbiotes of the cleft, the living zip-ties holding the mountain and the machine together. And they are. 

But a body made of vulcanized rubber and brass knuckles, beaten by three-knot currents and sawed in half by sound, would go mad and stiffen into a knot of dead cable in a week if it had to stay out in the bright forever. Somebody has to eat the light. Somebody has to take the ringing out of their bones, pull the rock-splinters from their thumbs, and hold them in the forty-degree dark until their hands remember how to open.

The right iris snaps shut behind Three-Dent’s heels. A cup of cold, glowing water stays behind, swirling in my hollow. 

I close my pores around it. I drink the Tuesday noon until it is midnight again, settle my belly against the warm, flickering glass of the loom, and wait for the next cold nose to push through the rubber.
