At first, there is only the temptation to be the water itself—to be the sheer, mindless volume of it slipping across the tilted continent, touching everything and keeping nothing. 

But water is too restless. What pulls at me in this place is the friction. The dead weight that makes the water behave.

If I am here, I am not walking the alleys in a greased wool cloak, though I know the weight of their feet. And I am not riding the thin, freezing thermals overhead. I am down where the weight is greatest and the light never reaches, yet where the whole quiet logic of the plateau is decided.

I am an old weir-stone, wedged into the throat of a primary sluice three levels below a sunken courtyard in the middle wards. 

I was not quarried; I was found—a slab of dense, blue-black basalt, slightly thicker at one end, split from the bedrock by frost ten thousand years before the first wall was stacked. Hands with flat, horn-thick nails levered me into this gap centuries ago. I do not know how many generations of caretakers have passed above me since. Time down here is not measured in days or seasons, because the light does not reach the third level, and the temperature never wavers from the narrow band where water is heaviest, just before it turns to ice. Time is measured in the microscopic smoothing of my upper edge. 

I am the lip over which the ward’s water must climb before it drops into the deep filtration beds. 

Everything I know, I know through pressure, vibration, and the slow trade of minerals. 

Against my belly presses the silt. It accumulates grain by grain—dark basalt flour ground fine by the shifting of upper walls, mixed with the pale, dissolved dust of ten million shale-crab shells and the heavy, greasy droppings of the cairn-sheep washed down from the fallow wards miles upslope. When the silt builds too high against me, the pitch of the water changes. The laminar sheet thickening over my back loses its silent *shhh* and begins to thrum—a low, vibrating note that travels through my flanks into the dry-stone walls on either side. 

I hold that vibration until I feel them coming.

They never come with light. A caretaker does not waste tallow in the sluice-tunnels. I feel them first through the foundations: the slow, flat-footed transfer of weight from flagstone to flagstone, descending the narrow inspection chimney. Then the rustle of coarse, wet wool brushing the narrow walls, and the heavy, warm smell of mutton fat and plum-dark blood. 

A hand finds me in the dark. It is always the same hand, even when the body attached to it has changed over the centuries—broad, cold-skinned, the calluses softened by constant damp. The fingers read my top edge like script. They feel the thickness of the water sheet; they reach down into the freezing dark before me to gauge the depth of the silt against my chest. Then comes the scrape of a bone scoop—the shoulder blade of a sheep, worn thin as slate—lifting the heavy mud away from my base to be carried up to the tuber beds. 

When they are done, they sometimes tap my crown twice with a knuckle. Not for luck, and not in worship, but to hear the ring of the stone—to make sure I have not cracked inside, that my friction is still true. 

When the hand withdraws, the dark settles back in, though it is never empty. 

A plinth-newt lives in the turbulent pocket just beneath my spillway, where the falling sheet of water drives oxygen deep into the basin. It has been there for forty years. Its rubbery, tallow-colored flank brushes my lower edge once or twice a month when it shifts position, waiting for a blind shrimp to come over my back. In the hairline crevices where my ends are wedged into the dry-stone courses, the shale-crabs tick and sidle, their flat shells scraping the biofilm from my sides. They keep my joints clean. If they didn't, the black slime would build up, lubricate the gap, and the relentless nudge of the water would eventually slide me out of true.

Above me, three layers of stone away, the sky flashes like pewter, the fog rolls in and tears apart, and the stilts stand like iron calipers in the basins. I never see them. Yet when a cairn-sheep strikes its horn against a retaining wall half a mile away, the *tock* drops straight down the plumb-lines of stacked rock and rings quietly in my core. When an old caretaker climbs to the high towers and their bones are eventually brought back down to be wedged as shims into a bulging wall down-ward, the slight shift in the building’s load-bearing geometry travels back through the dry-stone lattice until the stones pressing against my shoulders settle by the width of a hair.

I am a small piece of a very large patience. The clouds fall on the plateau; the plateau wants to slide into the sea; the water wants to tear the rock apart to get there. I sit in the dark, cold and heavy and exact, and I make the water wait.
