To find myself here, I have to drop below the blinding white roar of the upper tiers, down through the weeping scaffolding where the light finally loses its edge, baffled by thirty layers of stretched gut and slate tiles into a greasy, sour-milk gloom. 

I would not be one of the fast ones on the high ledges, levering ribs out of the blue ice. My lungs already have the start of the crust—a low, whistling rattle deep in the bottom lobes that makes the thin air taste like copper coins. When I breathe out, there is a faint click at the back of my throat, like a vent-clam shutting its shell. 

Instead, I am wedged into a narrow, three-sided niche chipped into the base of the western wall, six feet above the main wallow where the pig-beasts churn the gray mud. I am a tension-keeper. A line-greaser. 

I am forty years old, which makes me ancient. My knees have fused at a thirty-degree bend; I cannot stand straight, and I do not need to. My world is a four-foot cube of wet slate and warm steam rising off the gill-beasts' backs. It smells powerfully of boiled bladder, turnip-water, and the thick, mutton-tallow paste I keep in a hollowed-out hip-socket between my feet.

If you were standing on the catwalk thirty feet above me, you would know me before you saw me: *thunk-thunk-scrape. Thunk-thunk-scrape.* 

It is the rhythm of my horn-mallet testing the main anchor-lines that hold the lower sluices to the cliff-pins, followed by the drag of my left boot—weighted with a double row of flat molar teeth—sliding across the slate to brace for the next pull. 

My hands are permanently curved into the C-shape of the three-inch rawhide cables. The skin of my palms is no longer skin; it is a solid, horny pad of yellow hoof-matter, grooved diagonal-wise by the twist of the braided gut. I do not look at the lines to know if they are failing. Through the slits of my bone-goggles—carved from a wide, flat scapula that sits heavy on the bridge of my sunken nose—the world is only a smear of bright silver and dark wet gray anyway. My milky corneas have closed down to the size of needle-pricks. 

I read the town with my thumbs and my teeth.

I lean forward, pressing my chin—sheathed in a cup of boiled horn strapped around my jaw—against the main dorsal cable that supports the third-tier ptarmigan rookeries. I bite down on the wet leather through the horn-cup, letting the vibration bypass my ears entirely and travel straight up my jawbone into my skull.

The town is singing in my teeth.

*Tap-tap-tap-pause-tap.* That is the heavy woman with the crooked shin, two levels up, resetting a slate divert-plate. 
*Rattle-hum-shiver.* That is the northeast boil, thirty yards away, beginning to choke on its own yellow salt-slag; the water is stuttering in the stone pipe, losing pressure. Within the hour, the line under my chin will go slack as the ice-mortar beneath that strut begins to rot and slump. 
And beneath it all, the slow, greasy *thrum* of six hundred people shifting their weight in unison against the gale that is trying to peel our roofs off from above.

I reach into the hip-bowl, scoop two fingers of warm render-fat mixed with scraped black lichen, and work it into the rawhide where it loops around the stone horn. If the salt-water gets inside the braid and dries, the crystals expand and shear the leather from the inside out like tiny knives. My thumbs move in a steady, circular mash, pressing the grease in, forcing the brine out.

There is no boredom in me. There is no cold, though a curtain of forty-degree lithium-slush is currently pouring off the awning six inches behind my neck, splashing onto my greased felt shoulders and running down the gutter of my spine. My heart beats once every second and a half: *lub... dub... lub... dub...* heavy and thick as porridge. The water in the air tastes sweetish and metallic, coating my tongue. A blind ptarmigan, heavy as a bowling ball and matted with grey ice-crust, clambers over my boot, using its wing-hooks to haul itself up my shin toward the warmer steam of my groin. I do not kick it off. Its body is a small stove against my knee. In an hour, if the northeast strut gives way and we have to drop the third tier to save the second, I will break its neck with my thumb and drop it into the render-pot without my heart rate changing by a single beat.

A shadow falls across the bright slit of my goggles. 

Another body slides along the six-inch ledge, moving sideways like a crab. It is a boy—twelve or thirteen, his chest already barrelled out, his lower eyelashes thick as beaver-fur and clotted with yellow salt-butter. He is carrying a bundle of split ribs on his shoulder, lashed together with gut.

There is no room for him to pass behind me. He presses his front flat against my front, his greased woolens sliding over mine with a heavy, wet *shuck*. He smells of the upper tiers—colder, cleaner, less of the mud-boil and more of the ozone-sharp diamond-dust that blows over the rim. 

For three seconds, we are wedged together, chest-to-chest, thigh-to-thigh, sharing the heat of our coats while the silent wind rips a curtain of white water sideways past our shoulders. 

He tilts his head back. His horn-goggles click against mine. 

He knocks his chin twice against my left collarbone, hard enough to bruise an outsider, then slides his jaw right, and flicks his grey, salt-pickled tongue twice against the corner of my mouth.

*Upper-seam-dead. Moving-the-troughs-west. Loose-the-second-pin.*

I do not nod. I knock my chin once against the center of his sternum—*heard*—and press my thumb hard into the hollow behind his ear to tell him his neck-wrap has come unsealed and is letting the rime in. He grunts—a vibration I feel in my ribs rather than hear—tucks his chin down to trap the loose felt, and continues his sideways slide along the rock, vanishing into the bright, silver-white wall of mist ten feet away.

I turn back to the rock. I take my bone pry-bar—it is a forearm, radius and ulna still lashed together at the wrist with copper-green sinew, polished by fifty years of grease until it looks like yellow soapstone—and jam the flat end into the wooden wedge holding the second pin. 

I lean my pestle-heavy body against the bone. I don't think about the cold. I don't think about the sky above the bowl, which I saw once when I was sixteen and have spent twenty-four years forgetting—that horrible, roaring blue emptiness where the light goes away forever and never comes back to warm your face. 

I just lean my weight into my grandfather's arm, feel the ice-mortar give way with a muffled, underwater *crunch* through the soles of my wide feet, and begin to let the slack out, inch by greasy inch, while the mountain shifts its weight around me.
