I would not be on the ropes with an axe between my teeth, because I do not have the stomach for the swing—not the physical drop, which the gizzard-stones settle soon enough, but the suddenness of the math when a reed goes harmonic. I am too slow in the knees for the *Cut-and-Swing*. My left hip was pinched when I was still small enough to ride the net-hauls, caught between a slipping bone-ratchet and the main framing-hoop when an uncle knocked the pin out a half-beat too early. The joint healed thick and dry, a bulbous knot of calcium that clicks out of time with the rest of me and refuses to lock cleanly around a vertical cable.

So I do not go out to the anchors unless the whole house is falling. Instead, I live three feet below the hatch, in the grease-dark and the purple glare, slung in a saddle of retired chrysotile among the stones and the dead.

I am the one who minds the trim. 

People think the *Spool-Watch* is all out on the perimeter, out where the young ones hang by their heels in the sulfur-drafts with their neck-ruffs glowing like kiln-bricks, listening to the big lines sing. That is the loud work. That is the work for people whose legs are the same length. My work is under the belly of the teardrop, where the five great anchor-hawsers come together at the spider-ring and split into the thirty-two lesser plumb-lines that keep the clay walls from twisting into a figure-eight and cracking open like a dry nut.

Down here, it is a forest of hanging things. There are the primary counterweights—great, pitted blocks of black basalt the size of two men hugged together, hauled up from the deep vents generations ago and notched by twenty different hands. Then there are the trim-stones, smaller loaves of grey pumice and flint that can be shifted from loop to loop with a localized block-and-tackle. And then there are the kindred.

We have seven under our floor right now. Ours is an old house, heavy with six layers of acoustic clay, and it takes a lot of bone to keep her level in the cross-drafts. 

I spend my hours swinging from one to the next on my short, knotted monkey-tail—the only slack line allowed on a rig, no longer than my forearm, clipped with a horn toggle to the main belly-hoop so a whip-vine can’t reach it. My hands are never clean. They are caked to the second knuckle in grey kinetic-tallow and the black, glassy dust that wears off the chrysotile ropes. When you rub the two together, they make a paste that smells like hot pennies and keeps the bone-spools from catching fire when a mega-stalk surges.

I know the weight of everyone we have ever lost. That is not a metaphor; my shoulders know them to the ounce. 

When the north-east pumice-burl takes a drink of deep gas and starts to bloat, tilting the kitchen-side of the floor three degrees toward the chasm, I don’t reach for a rock. Rock is dead weight; rock doesn’t tell you when the wind is shifting inside the line. I unhook my mother’s brother—who died when his gizzard packed with unground flint during the long swarm-famine—and I haul him three notches out along the port outrigger. 

He is very light now, forty seasons in the updraft having baked him down to the weight of a full water-skin from some other world, all dark leather and hollow marrow, bound up tight as a fist in three-ply reed-twine. His knees are tucked under his chin; his four-toed feet are locked around his own shins the way we folded him before the rigor set. Over the years, the constant rain of falling glass-chaff from the canopy has sandblasted his windward side—his left shoulder and the ridge of his brow—until the skin is gone and the bone beneath is polished to the dull, soapy shine of tallow-stone. His leeward side is still pewter-colored hide, dry as a wasp’s wing, dusted with yellow sulfur in the creases behind his jaw.

When I hook his carrying-loop onto the third notch and let the tension take him, I don’t let go right away. I hook my good right foot into the spider-ring, let my crooked hip dangle over the five-hundred-foot drop into the grinding mulch, and I press my sternum hard against the flat of his back, right between the dried-up fins of his cooling-ruff.

I close my outer lids so the violet flicker doesn't distract me, and I listen through him.

A stone only gives you the pull. A person gives you the *chord*. Because his ribs are empty and dry, his chest cavity is a drum. The vibration comes down the fifty-yard hawser from the bloating burl, passes through the bone-ratchet, runs down the braided sinew of his suspension loop, and blooms inside his ribcage. Pressed against him, my own chest-organ still and silent, I can feel the whole tree in his back. 

It isn’t a single note; it’s a messy, grinding bundle of them. There is the low, greasy *thrum-thrum* of the sulfur pocket bubbling three hundred feet down in the root-mat. Over that, there’s a tight, rhythmic *tink-tink-tink*—that’s a shatter-reed neighbor, thirty yards off, rubbing a glassy elbow against our burl’s flank with every third gust of the thermal. And underneath both, barely there, like a beetle scratching inside a gourd, is the high, dry fizz of the chrysotile fibers in our main line slowly sawing through their own grease.

*Needs fat,* my uncle tells me through my breastbone. *Not the pin. Don't drop the stone yet. Just fat on the second spool, where the bark-dust is caking.*

I tap my chin twice against his polished shoulder blade—*heard, understood*—just as I would if he were still up inside the clay walls, chewing gravel-bread with the rest of us. Then I swing over to the grease-pot, dig out a fistful of grey tallow, and climb toward the ratchet.

It is a lonely station, the under-floor, if you measure loneliness by how often another living chest is pressed to yours. The runners and the axe-hands are up in the net-walks or out on the limbs, working in pairs so one can always feel the other’s warning-hum when a glider-flock comes snapping through the smoke or a stalk starts to sing. Inside the teardrop, the old ones Whose teeth have worn down to the gum-plate sit wedged together in the dark, six bodies tangled into one big ear, humming old tension-math into each other’s collarbones while they flake obsidian for the scrapers.

Down here, nobody touches me for hours at a time except the dead and the house itself.

Sometimes, when the updrafts are steady and the forest is in one of its slow, grinding moods—no cannon-cracks nearby, just the endless, roaring white noise of ten million glass leaves sawing at the dark—I will wedge myself into the V-crotch between the two main basalt blocks, hook my elbows over a cross-brace, and let my yellow inner lids stay open while I watch the bottom of the world.

From under the hut, looking down between my own two feet, the basin floor doesn't look like ground. It looks like the inside of a gizzard. 

Two hundred feet below my heels, the light is dimmer, choked with violet shadow and grey ash-drift. I can see the tops of the old houses—three of them within spitting distance, though nobody has spit since the world cracked open. One of them is our old hull, the one we cut loose from six seasons back when the twin reeds went critical together. It’s only forty feet down in the mattress now, tilted on its side like a dropped basket, already half-full of white pumice-shoots that have punched right through the acoustic clay. Every ten minutes or so, a herd of the copper-wire grazers comes crashing over the ridge of a root-burl down there; I can’t hear them over the rift-roar, of course, but I see the sudden, bright violet *flash-flash-flash* of piezo-sparks as their stone teeth shear through the live silica-vines, and then a whole section of our old roof caves in under their multi-jointed legs, dropping a little cloud of grey clay-dust into the hot dark below.

Nothing stays. Even the rock is just passing through on its way back to the vents.

Suddenly, the rope under my left armpit changes pitch.

It doesn't whine—that would be a surge. It goes *slack-taut-slack* in three rapid beats, like a plucked bowstring that someone is trying to choke with a rag. 

I don't have to look up to know what that is. That’s the south-west stabilizer line, the long, thin one that runs out to a young silica-vine we hooked into yesterday after the midday swing. And that stutter in the fiber means something heavy and multi-legged has just crawled onto our line out in the glare and is trying to eat the grease off the braid. A big grazer, maybe, or one of the solitary, armored shell-crabs that climb up from the mulch when the sulfur-vents get too hot, using its stone chelae to nip at the tallow-soaked chrysotile because it smells like cicada-fat. If it nips through one strand, the rest will unravel like a dry reed in five seconds, and the hut will lurch forty feet to the north and smash all our curing-pots against the main burl.

I don't have time to climb up to the hatch and mallet the hoop. By the time the runners spill out, the line will be parted.

Instead, I reach for my grandmother.

She is our smallest trimmer-weight, hung right on the south-west ring because she was tiny when the stillness took her—all dried up to nothing, hardly heavier than a full mortar of mash. I don't unhook her. I grab the rawhide loops around her shins with both hands, haul my own crooked legs up until my four-pronged feet are braced flat against the underside of the hut’s floor, and then I throw my whole body downward into the updraft, using her bound body as a dead-blow handle.

I put every ounce of my bad hip and my thick shoulders into that drop, slamming down against the stop-knot at the bottom of the south-west tackle.

The shockwave doesn't go into the house; it goes straight out along the stabilizer line. I feel it leave my heels, shoot through my grandmother’s dried pelvis, and roar out into the violet haze at two hundred miles an hour—a single, vicious *crack* of kinetic whip-lash travelling down eighty yards of taut mineral-fiber.

For two heartbeats, I hang there below the stop-knot, my neck-ruff flaring hot and stiff against the rising wind, my chin jammed against my grandmother’s dry knee, waiting for the return note.

It comes back down the line a second later: a scrambling, grating *scritch-thump*, followed by the sudden, clean, unobstructed hum of a free cable singing in the sixty-mile-an-hour gale. Whatever was out there—crab or kindling-beast—got its stone jaw rattled out of its sockets by the whip-crack and dropped into the churn below.

Above my head, the floor of the hut vibrates. *Thrum-tick-tick.* 

Somebody inside—probably my sister, who sleeps with her shoulder blades wedged into the floor-ribs right above the south-west ring—felt me drop the weight. She is asking through the four-inch clay and the wicker ribs: *Line holding? Need axes?*

I don't climb up to the hatch. The sulfur-gale is warm and dry against my scales, and my grandmother’s knee is already warm from my chin. I just reach up with my grease-stiffened mallet, hook my bad leg around my uncle’s waist to steady myself in the swing, and strike the ironwood framing-hoop twice, slow and flat.

*All level,* the wood says to the clay, and the clay says to my sister’s spine. *Just trimming. Go back to sleep.*

Then I pull myself back up into the shadow of the hull, spit a dry pellet of glass-dust out of my nose into the roaring violet dark below, and begin to work my fingers into the tallow-pot again, listening to the five trees argue through the bones of my people.
