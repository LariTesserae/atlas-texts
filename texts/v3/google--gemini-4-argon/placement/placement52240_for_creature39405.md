I am not the third boulder, nor the second, nor the first. Look lower, into the cocoa-colored silt beside the chert anvil, where the black basalt hammerstone rests at the exact angle it was dropped forty years ago.

I am the basalt. 

I do not belong to this room. The walls here are limestone and dolostone—sedimentary, porous, laid down by the quiet settling of dead shells in a forgotten sea. They are soft enough to be carved by water and sanded into velvet by the wind. The chert and the flint are native cousins, born of the same pale chemistry, brittle and glass-sharp. 

I was born in fire, miles away and leagues beneath the crust, squeezed from a volcanic throat and cooled into a dense, lightless knot of iron and magnesium. I am heavier than anything else in this basin. When I was dropped from the rim—tucked, perhaps, into the crook of a tough, slate-gray arm before the creature made its one and only plummet into this shelter—I struck the floor with a thud that the silt remembers, even if the air has forgotten. 

I have no metabolism at all. Even the creature’s century-long afternoon is a frantic, fluttering pulse compared to the time I keep. To me, the creature is a nervous thing, forever twitching awake, scraping, chewing, and resealing itself in what feels like a continuous, blurred rustle of activity. 

My skin is dark and finely pitted, though one of my cheeks has been bruised pale where I have struck the chert. In those microscopic craters, I hold my own tiny ecology of dust: wedged crystals of white gypsum from the ceiling, a smear of waxy purple tallow transferred from a three-fingered hand, and a few scales of the creature’s own shed skin, tough as horn.

Right now, I am entirely still, eighty-five degrees all the way to my iron core. Half of my weight is submerged in the dry ash-silt; the other half faces the dull, clotting red of the sky-hole fifty feet above. 

I do not want anything, because stone does not want, yet I have a relationship with the hand. 

In the long stretches between the wakes, gravity is my only companion. It pulls me down, fractions of a millimeter a decade, settling the fine cocoa powder around my base until I am nearly level with the floor. Left alone for ten thousand years, I would sink until I met the smoothed ribs of the bedrock below, becoming a buried erratic in a dead basin.

But every seventy or eighty years—every fifth or sixth time the earthen caul cracks open—the shadow beside the anvil shifts. 

I feel the wake before I am touched. Because I rest firmly in the silt, I register the infinitesimally slow redistribution of weight as the third boulder uncoils. A week later, the shadow returns from the weeping fissures and the purple plants. 

Then comes the touch. The three fingers—cool, dry, and leathery, smelling faintly of sulfur and wet stone—slide under my belly. 

For a stone, being lifted is the only miracle. Suddenly, the constant, downward marriage with the earth is broken. I rise through the motionless, bathwater air—an inch a minute, steady and hydraulic—until I am three feet above the floor. I stay there, suspended in the dark red twilight, while the slow lightning gathers in the creature’s arm. 

For three minutes, we are a single statue: the ancient volcanic core and the living mineral, poised at the apex of an arc. 

Then, the fall. Not the blind fall of a rock tumbling down a cliff, but a guided, deliberate plummet. 

*Clack.*

For a microsecond, at the point of impact with the chert, a tiny spark of kinetic heat blooms against my bruised cheek—a fleeting ghost of the volcano that made me. A razor-thin flake of flint shears away, singing a high, crystalline note that dies before it reaches the walls. 

Then I am lowered back into the cocoa dust. Not into my old bed, which has already slumped in on itself, but an inch to the left, creating a new crater. The three fingers withdraw. The hand sweeps the silt over its own head. The chamber locks back into its equilibrium. 

I sit in my new dimple, holding the faint, lingering warmth of the strike in my iron grain, waiting for the seventy-year clock to run down so I can fly again.
