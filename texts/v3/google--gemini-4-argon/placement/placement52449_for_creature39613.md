If you look at the ring of river cobbles around the largest bladder-plant, you will count nineteen stones. The harvester only brought eighteen up from the valley. 

I am the nineteenth. I am sitting on the warm, slick shoulder of the eighth stone from the left, my four lentil-pad feet tucked so tightly beneath my belly that the seam between my grey, chalky hide and the river-smoothed granite has entirely disappeared. The sun is high and direct, baking the arch of my back, drying the seep-water on my skin into a fine, pale crust that itches if I move—so I do not move.

I have no eyes to see the emerald sails of the pillar-ferns twenty feet above, nor the blank blue lid of the sky. I do not know what green or yellow is. Yet I see the entire basin with total clarity, because the basin is never silent, and my head is an instrument built to be struck by it.

Right now, the world is shaped like a vast, vibrating bell. 

Through the wide, flat pads of my feet, pressed against the river cobble, I feel the dead, dull silence of the foreign rock—it has no pores, no hollow flutes, no voice of its own. It is a blind spot in the floor. Because I do not like that deadness, I have leaned my chin forward until the great, veined drum of my left tympanum is resting directly against the taut, swollen skin of the bladder-plant.

The plant is hot. Inside it, the milky water is sweating gas, and the skin is stretched so tight that it catches every sound in the sinkhole and magnifies it into a rich, greasy hum that fills my skull from ear to ear. 

I can map the whole morning inside that hum. 

Thirty paces to my right, down in the dark below the moss, the narrow pipe we call the *High-Throat* goes *plock-plock-tink*. The water level in its catch-basin has dropped half an inch since yesterday; the pitch has risen. I know that two of my cousins are hanging inside that chimney, head-down, their tongues coiled in their throats, waiting for the dry *tick* of a beetle’s wing-case. 

Further away, near the center of the bowl, the great wide chimney under the central fern—the *Deep-Belly*—lets out its slow, four-second *booooom*. When that sound hits the bladder I am leaning against, the yellow skin shudders against my jaw-drum like a living throat. The vibration travels through my skull-bones, down my curved spine, and makes the heavy lead-shot weight of my gut feel warm and loose. 

High up on the trunk of the central fern, fifteen feet in the air, a glass-snail is rasping moss with its file-tongue: *shhhhk, shhhhk, shhhhk*. It is a tiny, brittle sound, like frost forming, and between each rasp I can actually hear the minuscule, wet *thump* of its grey heart beating inside its silica shell. 

And deeper still inside that same fern, muted by six inches of waterlogged red hair, is the wide, rhythmic *shuck-slosh, shuck-slosh* of the Great Clacker’s gills. She is asleep. She is a massive, jagged shadow in the acoustics of the garden—a place where the echoes hit her moss-covered plates and scatter into soft, fuzzy nothingness. 

I do not care about her during the day. By day, there is only the Tightening.

The bladder against my cheek is very near its end. I have been sitting here since yesterday’s zenith, having crept out of the *Split-Lip* pipe three yards away—one leg lifted, held in the warm air while four drops fell in the dark, set down on the bronze moss, then a long sleep before lifting the next. There are twenty-three of us clustered around this globe now. I cannot see my brothers and sisters, not even the one whose flank is pressed against my own rump, because we make no sound when we wait. To my ears, they are just warm, soft extensions of the cobbles. 

Above the bladder, however, there is a strange dead spot in the air. 

I know the Heavy-Walker has been here. Two mornings ago, the world went mad with his noise—the terrifying, rhythmic *thud-squelch* of his two huge feet compressing the bronze moss, crushing gallons of water out of the floor, drowning out the music of the pipes. He smells of dry dust and cold wind, a sharp, thin stink that makes the valves of my neck-nostrils snap shut. He stayed for an eternity of noise, scraping flint against the soft walls (*skree-clack!*), his breath whistling in and out of a chest as big as a boulder, his heart hammering at a frantic, sickening pace—*lub-dub, lub-dub, lub-dub*—sixty times in the span it takes a glass-snail's heart to beat once. We all dropped five feet down into our pipes and pressed our bellies to the wet limestone until he hauled himself back up his rope and left the sky quiet again.

Before he left, he built the three-legged thing over this bladder. 

I can hear the willow wands creaking slightly as they dry in the sun, and hanging directly above my head, suspended in the air where no stone should ever be, is the Maul. It does not move, yet I know its exact shape because the echoes of the floor-pipes bounce off its flat bottom and throw a hard, sharp acoustic shadow right down onto the crown of the bladder-plant. 

The bladder is rising toward that shadow. 

As the sun hits the very top of the sky, the air in the stone bowl thickens. The heat presses down like a warm hand. Inside the globe against my cheek, the gas is expanding faster now. The hum of the skin rises in pitch—from a low *mmmm* to a tight, whining *nnnnnn*. 

A microscopic thread of fiber in the plant’s skin snaps: *pink*. 

Every listener on the cobble ring tenses. Twenty-three throats swell slightly. Around me, four-score lentil-padded feet press harder into the stones. 

*Pink. Pink.* 

Two more fibers tear. The crown of the yellow globe has touched the rough underside of the hanging stone maul. The vibration of the *Deep-Belly* pipe below us (*booooom*) hits the bladder, lifts it a fraction of a millimeter against the heavy stone weight, and—

**CRACK.**

The world disappears into pure, white thunder. 

The shockwave slams into my jaw-drums so hard that my whole body is lifted a quarter-inch off the river cobble. It is glorious. It is a blow that strikes straight through the bone of my skull and turns my brain to warm water. And then, before the echo can bounce back from the wet limestone cliffs, comes the Voice.

*Brrrrrrrrraaaaaaaaaahhhhhhh—*

The torn seam of the bladder gapes open, and the long, brassy sigh pours out, bouncing from wall to curving wall until the entire sinkhole is inside a horn being blown by the earth itself. 

With the sound comes the cloud. The heavy, hot gas spills down over the ring of cobbles, washing over my back, sinking into the bronze moss. My neck-valves flutter open and drink it in. It tastes of iron and cooked chalk and deep, dark rot, and the moment it hits my blood, the slow, cold clockwork inside me breaks.

I am moving—fast, so fast! My legs scramble over the wet, collapsing yellow leather of the ruptured plant. I am no longer a stone; I am greased lightning, sliding over the humps of my brothers and sisters as we all rush toward the torn seam where the gas is thickest and the flapping skin still buzzes like a dying bee. 

Someone’s wet, grey flank slams against my nose. I rear up on my hind pads, throw my head back, and strike my throat-plates together:

*Tink! Tink! Tink-tink!*

From twenty-two other throats around me, the flint-clicks answer—*tink-tink, tink, tink-tink-tink!*—a frantic, chattering hail of small stones falling on stone, cutting right through the brassy sigh of the deflating globe. I press my wide, trembling jaw-drum against the jaw-drum of the listener beside me. Her membrane is hot and tight; through it, I can hear the roar of her blood and the echo of my own clicks bouncing back out of her skull. We shove and slide in the warm, yellow slime of the bladder’s belly, drunk on the iron-gas, our eight legs tangling on the slick river cobbles, clicking and buzzing until our throats are raw.

It lasts for a lifetime. It lasts until the sun moves the width of a finger across the sky.

Then, the warm gas thins, floating up past the giant fern fronds and out into the dry nothingness of the plateau. 

The brassy sigh stops. The yellow skin beneath my feet is just a wet, flat rag lying in the moss, silent and dead. 

The cold comes back into my blood first, then the heaviness returns to my belly. The female beside me stops clicking. Her jaw-drum pulls away from mine with a tiny, wet *schlip*. 

All around the ring of cobbles, the frantic scrambling slows, stutters, and stops. 

The *High-Throat* pipe thirty paces away goes *plock-plock-tink*. 

Five feet to my left, at the mouth of the *Split-Lip* chimney, a single drop of seep-water falls from a liverwort leaf, drops down into the dark rock, and three seconds later sends up a deep, hollow *onk* that vibrates through the soles of my feet. 

Down in that cool, wet dark, the water is singing. A timber-moth might fall in before dusk. 

I lift my front left foot from the collapsed skin of the bladder. I hold it in the warm, wet air for a long time, listening to the pipes below, mapping the way home. Then I set the pad down on the bronze moss without a sound, tuck my chin in, and wait five minutes before moving the next.
