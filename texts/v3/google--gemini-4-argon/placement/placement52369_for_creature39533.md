I am a turnbuckle of bone and blackwood, five hundred years into the holding of a bad seam.

To my left—though "left" is only the name of a particular three-toed ache in what used to be a hip—the wood is young, green, and sour with hurry. It is a twenty-foot-thick tendon of braided willow-analogue, still wet from the splitting-mauls of a crew that has not yet been born, or that died before the mist below me had finished falling from a tipped cistern six leagues "up." Its sap is hot and tastes of bruised celery and copper. It wants to fall toward the mauve light of the great bay.

To my right, the strut is petrified ironwood, so old the bark has turned to a crust of grey salt and coal-hard lichen. Its gravity pulls sideways, toward a distant, unseen colonnade that looks, from here, like a smudge of charcoal on wet linen. Between the two struts hangs the *grease*—the chromatic smear where two epochs were lashed together by some desperate, nameless ancestor who didn't care that the Tuesday on one side of the knot was four millennia wider than the Thursday on the other.

I am the pin through the grease.

I do not remember my hands, though I know I have four of them because there are four distinct places where my blood goes into the cambium and comes back out as turpentine. My secondary thumbs—the little horn-hooked ones—were driven into the clefts of the green willow when my lungs finally filled with the velvet moss-phlegm. I remember the stone knife of my daughter—or my nephew, the faces were already just dark puddles in the gloom by then—scraping the hide of my forearms until the white fat showed, and the sting of the yellow grafting-gum they smeared into the cuts before they wound the strangler-fig around my wrists.

Now, my radius and ulna are four feet thick and covered in weeping grey liverwort. 

It is not a sleeping. If I slept, the seam would slip. The green wood on my left is constantly trying to grow toward a sun that doesn't exist, twisting in its socket with a slow, green-stick torque that would shear the ancient ironwood on my right into splinters if I did not take the twist into my pelvis and turn it into heat. 

So I burn. Very slowly, in the damp dark, my belly is a furnace of ninety-degree confusion. The warm mist that rises from the *kosong* hits my left shoulder and condenses into lukewarm slimy beads; two inches away, where my spine crosses the temporal shear, that same mist hits my right shoulder and bursts into a tiny, silent crackle of boiling hoarfrost before melting into the bark. 

I watch the bay. My eyes are still there, under a milky skin of wild horn-gum that keeps the drying-flies out. Everything is very purple and very slow. 

Once every three or four days—or perhaps it is every three or four years; the heart in my ribcage only knocks once while a glider crosses five miles of air—a herd of the six-legged thumb-apes comes down from the anthracite eaves above my right knee. To them, I am a mountain range of warm leather and bark. They have built a wattle-village in the hollow of my collarbone, weaving my old, stiff flank-hairs into hurdles to keep their young from tumbling into the chromatic grease of the seam. They smell like vinegar and wet feathers. When they walk across the translucent skin over my eyeballs, their twenty-four little feet feel like warm rain. I like them better than the Mites, who come only when the wood is sick, smelling of frantic iron and old spit, trying to wedge their tiny flint chips under my eyelids to see if there’s any meat left worth sewing up.

My real life, though, is in the *thup*.

Because I am the gusset at a junction of two gravities, the whole quarter-league of vaulting speaks through my teeth. My jaw was wired shut with root-tendon when they set me in, my molars jammed against a sounding-rib that runs ten miles down into the soggy dark. 

I feel the Plaiters long before their smell reaches the thumb-apes. 

*Thup-thup. Purrrrr.* 

A tension-crew, three miles "down" the green willow, has found a rot-pocket. Their mallets send a dry, lemon-colored shiver up through my left fibula. I don't have to think to answer; my throat-sac is grown into the fork of the wood now, a great bellows of tough, veined hide that stretches between the two struts like the webbing between a frog’s toes. When the vibration hits my jaw, my sac tightens by instinct, squeezing a thirty-gallon pocket of trapped condensate through a valve of calloused bark. 

*Sluck.* 

The water-hammer goes back down the vine, heavy and wet, telling them that the anchor above holds, that the old man in the corner hasn't let the sky drop yet, that they can cut the bad wood out and trust the sag to me.

Sometimes, a young one passes across me. 

One came just now—or a century ago, the willow-sap hasn't moved an inch since—scuttling along the ironwood on all fours, a bundle of fresh bone-wedges knocking against his ribs. He paused at the grease-line, right where my ribs turn into a buttress of knotted root. His wide, oil-black eyes looked right into mine through the gum-film, though he didn't see a cousin; he only saw a burl that looked like a face, the way clouds look like gliders. 

He reached out one flat, four-thumbed hand, pinched the skin of my neck to feel which way the blood-sap was falling, and then did the boneless turn—swinging his heavy chest through the boiling frost of the seam, his toes finding the purchase my own thumbs had made for him when the wood was new. As he hung there for a second, his weight pulled on my collarbone. A tiny, hairline crack opened in the grafting-scar behind my ear, leaking a single drop of white, pungent latex that smelled of his mother and my grandfather.

He didn't look back. He just unslung his hide-headed maul, reached down between my knees where an old bone wedge had shrunk in the dry coal-wood, and gave it two flat, silent taps. 

*Tock. Tock.*

The bone bit deeper into my hip. The ache sharpened, sweet and tight, locking the two worlds together for another thousand years of mauve afternoon. The tension settled back into my spine, warm as broth, and I went back to watching the mist fall upward past my chin.
