I would not be in the sky, and I would not be out on the four-inch ledges where the frost is swept. I have no head for the four-thousand-foot drop, even in a dream.

You would find me forty feet inside the mountain, three tiers below the Western Gutter-Course of the Seven-Salt Cistern, where the daylight has never once reached. 

Wait—let the eyes adjust first. There isn't even a fat-lamp burning here right now, because a wick drinks oxygen and leaves soot on the clay, and the clay down here must stay clean and slick as an inner lip. 

I am sitting on a three-legged stool made of lashed *khor*-ribs, wedged into an alcove no wider than a wardrobe, wrapped in four layers of old, ungreased felt that smells of my grandfather. My knees are not calcified—not yet—so I am not one of the Plumb-elders, though I have their pale, milky eyes. I am a *Puddle-Treader*, a Cistern-Watcher, the keeper of the dark belly where the Drop comes to rest.

Listen. 

You can hear the city above me, not as noise, but as weight. Fifteen thousand tons of hand-stacked slate pressing down on the corbelled dome over my head. Every stone in that vault was laid without mortar eight hundred years ago by men and women whose knucklebones are now button-toggles on my coat. If I press my flat, violet-webbed cheekbone against the damp stone behind my shoulder, I can feel the afternoon fog arriving before a single drop reaches me. 

First comes the *creak*. The outer buttresses, baked to thirty degrees by the cold brass sun, are suddenly swallowed by the forty-degree cloud. The slate contracts—a microscopic shudder that travels inward along the white quartz veins at the speed of sound. *Tick. Tick-tick.* Then, twenty minutes later, the sound of the chutes begins.

It is not a roar. The Escarpment never roars. It is a sound like ten thousand greased fingers drumming on a tight hide. 

Down the dark, vertical slate chimneys comes the morning’s hoarfrost, swept by the small, silent children on the ledges high above, mixed now with the afternoon’s grey condensation. It falls into the settling-troughs beside my boots—a slush of grey water, rock-fowl dander, soot from a thousand dung-fires, and powdered slate.

My job is the clay. 

In a dry-stone world where everything is cracks and crevices, where the mountain wants nothing more than to sieve every drop of moisture straight down into its dry, basaltic bones three miles below, only the clay holds the city’s blood. It was carried up on the backs of oxen four thousand years ago from some lowland riverbed no one remembers, and it is more precious than the eyesight of children.

I take off my hair-wrapped boots. My feet are broad as spades, the soles horn-thick, yellow, and cracked like old lard, yet sensitive enough to feel a single grain of quartz sand through three inches of mud. 

I step down into the black, thirty-four-degree water of the main cistern.

A lowlander would scream as the cold bit into their calves, their heart racing, their breath tearing Out in the Grit. My blood does not hurry. My big, barrel-ribbed chest rises once every twenty seconds, slow as a bellows in a forge that is banked for the night. My toes spread in the dark beneath the water, feeling the floor of the basin.

There. Under my left heel. A *dimple*.

Two layers of slate beneath the clay lining have shifted by the thickness of a thumbnail—perhaps because three tiers down, a neighbor took down a party-wall to make room for a new bull-calf. The clay has stretched over the new gap. Left alone, the weight of twelve thousand gallons of fog-water would punch through that dimple by midnight. By dawn, the cistern would be empty, the water gone down into the dry black roots of the mountain, and forty households above me would have to slaughter their *khor* for blood-drink or walk five miles along the ledges to beg from the Eastern Buttress.

I do not call for the Plumb-elders. This is small work, dark work. 

I reach into the stone trough beside me without looking, break off a ten-pound loaf of winter-cured tallow-clay— clay kneaded with boiled *khor*-fat and fine wood-ash until it will never freeze solid—and drop it to the bottom of the pool. For the next two hours, in pitch blackness, I tread. 

I do not use my hands; to bend over in the icy water is to wet the hems of my felt coat. I do the *terrace-roll* in place, my torso swinging like a slow metronome, my wide, calloused heels pounding the fat-clay into the invisible seam with the patient, heavy rhythm of an ox chewing cud. *Thump. Slide. Thump. Slide.* 

While my feet work in the freezing mud, my hands are up inside my sleeves, warm against my greased ribs, carving a marriage-riddle into a piece of rock-hyrax shinbone for my niece three tiers up. I work the bone entirely by the pads of my thumbs. Three notches for the slope-cant of her intended’s house; a spiral groove for the wind-baffle where they met during the last White Season; a tiny, smooth boss of bone left uncut to represent the bull-calf her father is holding back until the gutter-rights are signed.

Above me, through the thick stone grille in the vault’s ceiling, drifts the smell of the *Stifling* from the great communal byre on the floor overhead. I can smell the warm, ammoniacal steam of old *Bess*, the lead cow with the split left horn; I can hear the *clack-clack* of her hooves as she shifts her weight to let the milkers through. And above her, through another three feet of packed moss and slate, comes the low, chest-deep *voo-oom* of the household’s forty grey chimney-fowl, hitting their three-chord evening drone as the sun drops toward the invisible western sea and turns the fog outside to brass.

The dimple under my heel is gone, smoothed into a seamless concave curve that will hold for another fifty years. 

I step out of the water. I do not wipe my feet; the smear of tallow-clay and ice-water dries instantly in the cold air into a grey, protective rind over my skin. I slip my feet back into the warm, horsehair-lined interiors of my boots, pick up my three-legged stool, and climb the seven narrow slate risers toward the trapdoor in the ceiling.

I give two soft taps with my bone-chisel against the underside of the lintel-stone. 

Above me, the stone slab slides back an inch, and a square of greasy, golden light falls into my dark, smelling fiercely of hot butter-beer, roasted lichen, and the sour, beloved sweat of twenty cousins sitting knee-to-knee in the heat. A thick, stubby hand with horn-yellow nails reaches down to take my stool, and I haul myself up out of the mountain's cold belly, ready to hear if anyone has guessed the answer to Tuesday's riddle.
