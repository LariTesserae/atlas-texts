I would not be one of the Counter-Weights at the great intersections, though my work is cousin to theirs. Their fingers listen to the *now*—the live, humming tension of sinew and vine, the shifting ballast of bodies moving up and down the ladders. My hands are tuned to a slower music. I listen to the rock, the cured resin, and the dead bone.

I live on the second tier, where the bruised amber light from the lip of the pit has already been filtered twice through the broad, greasy leaves of the canopy and the woven mats of the third-level workshops. Down here, the light is the color of old, dark honey. The air is thicker, warmer, rising off the basal floor a dozen feet below me, carrying the heavy, sleep-inducing musk of the dust-badgers and the sharp, medicinal bite of camphor from the peeling trunks.

My house is not a hanging gourd, sway-backed and creaking in the middle air. It is a cup-nest mortised directly into a long, horizontal fold of the salmon-pink quartzite at the northern curve of the wall. The plaster of its outer shell—pulverized stone, insect chitin, and wax-tree resin—has been rubbed by passing shoulders and oiled heels for eight generations until it shines like dark ox-horn. 

I am getting old, though my knees still unlock without clicking and my big toes can still pinch an ironwood rung hard enough to bruise the grain. You can tell my age by my skin: it has the deep, translucent matte of boxwood that has been oiled and scraped ten thousand times, and the horn-pads on my heels are half an inch thick, amber-yellow and grooved at the edges so they catch the weave of the rope-walkways without looking.

My name, if you let it out through barely parted lips, is a low bilabial hum that drops into a double click at the back of the throat—*Mmh-k’k*. It means *The Join*, or *Where the Bone Meets the Stone*. 

I am the Mender of Mortises. 

In a village that hangs from its ceiling and clings to its walls, the terrifying thing is not that a rope will snap—ropes fray first, they sing a high, thin note to the Counter-Weights days before they part, and new hair and spider-silk are always being twisted to replace them. The terror is the *creep*. The slow, dry surrender of the amber-mortar where the great cantilevered beams are socketed into the soapstone-smooth quartzite. The microscopic widening of a hairline crack in an ancestor’s femur that serves as the lynchpin for a three-family platform.

All day, while the cicadas sleep in the papery bark and the village murmurs around me, I move along the stone perimeter. I move on three points—left foot pinched to a root, right hand hooked over a bone peg, left knee braced against the warm, greased curve of the wall—freeing my right hand to work. 

Hanging from my hip, beside the pouch for my skin-scrapings, is a small clay pot wrapped in thick layers of beaten spider-felt. Inside it is the heart of my trade: a lump of *slow-pitch*, drawn from the deepest tap-roots of the oldest wax-trees down in the dark pinch at the back of the hall. To keep it workable without fire, I have to keep it at blood-heat. When I am not on the wall, the pot sits buried to its neck in the hot, churning compost of the floor, warmed by the snuffling bodies of the badgers; when I climb, I tuck it into the hollow of my groin, held there by my apron, letting my own body heat keep the resin soft as chewed fat.

I stop at the root of the Fourth Main Cantilever. Here, the beam isn’t wood—ironwood is too heavy for this span—it is a composite spar made of three dozen human tibias and fibulas, lashed end-to-end and spiral-wrapped with sinew, the whole thing encased in cured resin until it looks like a single, knobby limb of dark amber. Where it thrusts into the natural pocket of the quartzite, the mortar has dried over two hundred years into a brittle, crystalline crust. Every time a boy runs across the platform above to shoo a gliding-squirrel, a tiny puff of pale yellow flour—pulverized resin—sifts down from the joint.

A six-legged terrier-scavenger is waiting on the catwalk just below me, its multifaceted eyes fixed on my hands, its mandibles clicking softly. It knows that where I go, I pry out old mortar, and old mortar is forty percent beetle-shell and ten percent dried plant-sugar.

I pull the bone awl from my crown-braid. My third eyelid goes *snip, snip* as I look up into the joint, clearing away the fine dust that drifts down. I don't use my eyes much for this; I press my thumb-pad—kept softer than my heels by rubbing it nightly with pure gecko-fat—against the seam where the amber meets the pink stone. 

*Tock-tock,* goes the mallet of the Counter-Weight fifty feet away in the center of the web. Three women carrying baskets of yellow tubers shift from the left walkway to the right. Through my thumb, I feel the Fourth Cantilever sigh. It rises by the thickness of a gnat’s wing, and settles back. It is loose.

I use the obsidian flake strapped to my wrist to score the dead, crystallized resin out of the socket. It comes away in dry, fragrant shards that smell of my great-grandfather’s time—a fainter, dustier nutmeg than the trees bleed now. I catch every shard in the palm of my hand and drop it down to the scavenger, whose throat-pouch bulges as it swallows them whole. 

When the stone pocket is clean, I take a pinch of the warm slow-pitch from my groin-pot, roll it in a small pouch of fresh, powdered woodlouse shell I carry at my neck, and thumb it deep into the gap. I don’t use a trowel; no tool has the sensitivity of the human knuckle. I pack it until my wrist aches, smoothing the bevel with the curved side of my strigil, sealing the air out, marrying the dead bone back to the ancient rock. 

A shadow falls across my knuckles. The amber light dims a fraction.

It is one of the girls who tends the grub-lanterns, coming down from the third tier with a rack of dark clay pots slung across her broad, slick shoulders. She pauses on the narrow plank beside me, automatically shifting her weight so the rope-handrail doesn't slacken.

We don't speak at first. To open the mouth is to lose the wet. Instead, she reaches out with a small, crescent blade of pale horn and draws it down my left shoulder blade, from the base of my neck to the latissimus, right where the dust from the overhead mortar-chipping has settled into my skin-oil. 

*Shhh-lat.* The sound of the strigil is soft, like a leaf sliding over bark. It feels intensely good—a cool, firm pressure that wakes the blood beneath the heavy leather of my hide. She rolls the grey ribbon of my sweat-wax and stone-dust into a neat pill between her thumb and forefinger, and drops it not into her own pouch, but into mine. A courtesy to an elder. 

I turn my head, my big golden eyes dilating in the shadow of her pots, and take my own strigil to the thick, corded muscle of her forearm, scraping away the green, powdery smear left by the luminescent grubs she handles all morning. 

*"Mmh-t’k?"* she hums through her nose, her lips pressed together. *The socket holds?*

*"Mmh,"* I vibrate back, tapping the bone spar once with my knuckle. *It holds. Tell the Weigher on Three she can hang the winter nut-racks from the outer peg again.*

She gives a single, short click of acknowledgment, her third eyelid flicking across her dark-adapted eyes as she glances down toward the floor, then continues her slow, three-limbed flow down the notched pole toward the compost pits, where the grubs are bred in the rotting heartwood of pruned branches.

By the time the light turns ruddy violet and the ten thousand bark-cicadas start their *shhh-kuh, shhh-kuh*—a sound so thick it makes the soft pitch in my pot tremble—I have descended to my own level. 

This is the hour I like best. I sit on the lip of my mortised cup-house, my legs dangling over the edge into the rising heat of the pit. Below me, in the green-spotted gloom, the great horn-beetles are waking up, their shiny black carapaces clicking like wooden bowls as they shove through the knee-deep litter. The smell of warm sage, ammonia, and camphor rises up like a blanket.

I unlash the articulated woodlouse-plates from my shins. I take a small lump of tallow-fern paste, rub it over my chest and thighs, and scrape myself clean, dropping the pills over the edge. Before they even hit the litter, a velvet-skinned night-skink darts out from the underside of my floorboards and catches them in mid-air. 

Across the eighty-pace hall, the green grub-pots are winking on, one by one, at five different heights, reflecting dimly in the oiled wood of the walkways and the huge, golden eyes of my neighbors as they sit on their balconies, scraping one another’s backs in the rhythm of the cicadas. 

My left hip has begun to ache when the night-chill presses down from the dead plateau above, even though the dome of the Hollow keeps the frost away. In two years, maybe three, I will give my groin-pot and my obsidian flakes to my nephew. I will move out of my stone cup and down to the first tier, to the wide, flat platforms just above the badgers, where I will sit in the hot dark and card spider-silk or sort tubers by touch. 

And a few years after that, they will rub me with the sweet heart-paste and lower me down the last six feet. 

I lean back against the wall, pressing my bare shoulder blades into the smooth, warm quartzite. I know the exact crevice on the fourth tier where my femur will go. There is a split in the lintel above the oil-presses that I have been watching for ten seasons; it is just the length of my thigh, and it will need a dense, heavy piece of bone to take the shear-load when the great wooden screw is turned. I have kept my weight steady my whole life, eating my measure of rail-eggs and yellow root, never hurrying, never wasting. My bones are thick and full of salt. They will not creep in the mortar. They will hold the press level for three hundred years.
