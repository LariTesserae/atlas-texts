I am not on the mat. The mat is for heavy things that sink and walk, and I have no feet to spread against the moss, nor lungs to draw the thick, copper-sweetened steam near the floor. 

You would find me forty feet up, where the weeping brick walls lose their mortar to the *ferro-ivy* and the horizontal boughs lock together like the framing of an inverted hull. Up here, the heat is not a damp blanket; it is a sharp, golden pressure. The great translucent bladders of the canopy swell against one another with the slow, squeaking friction of wet leather, drinking the raw sun from the sky outside and straining it into the milky glare below.

At first, you would think I was only a piece of the old dockyard’s rigging that had been carried up by the trees as they grew—a stray length of braided copper grounding-wire and greened brass chain, tangled in the highest crotches and half-swallowed by the amber gum that bleeds from the canopy seams.

And for the better part of the day, that is all I am. 

There is no room for a second walker in the hexagonal alleys below, and the yard could not feed another heavy belly of root and bone. But the Governor cannot climb into the roof. If he were to trust his waterlogged, barrel-chested weight to the upper boughs, he would tear the bladders from their moorings and let the raw, dry sky come rushing in to scorch the soup. Yet the roof has its own gears, and its own blind catastrophes waiting to happen.

Where the horizontal trunks cross, there are ancient, ceiling-mounted line-shafts, box-pulleys, and worm-gears—the remnants of the traveling cranes that once moved heavy iron across this yard when it still had a roof of slate and glass. Now, those shafts are turned by the tightening and loosening of the canopy-vines as they track the sun across the unseen sky. And where the bladders press too hard against the iron hangers, or where the sun-varnish cooks too thick in a pulley-sheave, something lighter than the Governor must go out along the trembling, high-tension boughs to keep the peace.

I am built like a winter-pruned vine, or like the brass-and-whalebone frame of an umbrella that has lost its cloth. 

I am seven feet long when I uncoil, though I have no more thickness than a man’s wrist at my widest—at the rib-cage, which is a narrow basket of flattened, verdigris-crusted copper straps and pale, springy willow-wood that never quite died. I have no skin in the way the Governor has skin; I have a casing of translucent, cured gut and overlapping scales of horn-like bark that slide over one another with a dry, papery whisper when I bend. Inside me, there is no red blood, and none of his thick, dark soup. I am filled with the clear, volatile turpentine that distills at the very top of the tree-trunks where the sun bakes them hardest. It smells sharply of camphor and hot brass. It makes me very light, and very warm.

I do not walk; I span. I have four limbs, all of them alike—long, multi-jointed calipers of dark, iron-rich wood tipped with curved hooks of work-hardened bronze. I move like a measuring worm, or like a pair of dividers stepping across a chart. Reach, hook, draw the basket of the body forward; reach, hook, lock the joint with a faint, dry *click*.

Because the light up here is blinding—a fierce, golden radiance trapped inside the oil-parchment bladders—I have no horn-domes like his. I would be dazzled into stillness. Instead, along the ridge of my narrow head, I have a row of tiny, pinhole apertures lined with black pitch, like a row of pinhole cameras, and two long, trembling antennae of spun glass and copper wire that read the static charge in the dry upper air and the tension in the canopy membranes.

Down on the mat, the world is about weight and alignment. Up here, it is about pressure and skin.

When the midday sun hits the roof, the bladders swell until they sing like kettle-drums. The air trapped inside them—pure oxygen and hot, terpene-heavy mist—pushes against the tough, parchment-like membranes until they threaten to split against the old iron roof-trusses. 

That is when I unhook myself from the warm, vibrating journal-box of the north wall’s line-shaft and step out into the glare.

I move upside-down across the ceiling of the world, hooking my bronze tips into the tough, corded seams where one bladder meets the next. Below me, through twenty feet of milky mist and tangling lianas, the tops of the six-sided boiler-tubers look like the basalt columns of a drowned causeway. Sometimes I can see the Governor down there—a slow, dark condensation of coat and timber drifting through an alley at the speed of a shadow—though he never looks up. His eyes are for the horizontal; mine are for the vault.

I find the bladder that is singing too high. Its skin is stretched so tight over a rusted iron roof-gusset that the translucent yellow tissue has gone white and dry at the point of contact. In another week, the iron would saw through the plant-flesh, the bladder would burst with a report like a cannon, and a column of cold, dry outside air would pour down into the yard, shocking the donkey-engines out of their rhythm and chilling the soup.

I hook three limbs into the surrounding bough-wood to anchor myself. With the fourth, I reach into the narrow slot of my ribs and draw out a cake of waxy tallow—rendered from the dried shells of dead grease-slugs that I harvest from the tops of the walls—and rub it along the rusted edge of the iron gusset until the plant-skin slides free with a long, rubbery sigh. 

Then, because the bladder is still over-pressured, I lean my narrow head down against its hot, trembling flank. From the tip of my snout extends a hollow, needle-sharp quill of hardened horn, bevelled like a trocar. I slide it through the tough outer rind of the bladder at a shallow angle, so it will self-seal when I withdraw, and I let the excess pressure bleed out through a whistle-vent behind my jaw. 

For twenty minutes I hang there like a leech made of clock-springs, whistling a thin, high, clear note—*fffff-eeeee*—as the hot, sun-baked gas from the bladder passes through my head-pipes and vents into the upper branches, cooling as it goes. The drum-tight membrane beneath me softens by a fraction of an inch. The white stress-mark fades back to golden amber.

We do not meet, the Governor and I. If I descended to the mat, the wet, mineral-heavy air would corrode my fine copper springs and gum my glass whiskers with gourd-mucilage; if he climbed to the trusses, the heat would bake his waxy hide until it cracked.

Yet we talk, once a day, when the light begins to thicken toward evening.

There is a single, unbroken iron rod—an old tie-bar, an inch thick—that runs vertically from the roof-trusses all the way down into the masonry plinth of the central donkey-engine where he sleeps. It is grown over with a pale sheath of ferro-ivy, but the iron inside is continuous.

When the sun drops off the bladders and the light turns the color of dark horn, I work my way across the ceiling to the top of that rod. I hook my bronze toes around the clevis at its crown, press my copper-strapped chest flat against the iron, and strike the metal with the flat of my jaw.

*Tink. Tink-tink.*

The sound does not travel through the thick, wet air of the yard; it goes straight down the center of the iron rod, forty feet through the crowding tubers and the sweating lianas, past the twelve-foot flywheel, right into the cast-iron housing between the twin brass cylinders where he is folding his heavy legs for the night.

Three minutes later—the length of one of his nocturnal heartbeats—an answer comes back up the rod. It is not a ring, but a deep, dull, hydraulic thud that makes my glass antennae shiver in the dark: the heavy, drop-forged iron spanner of his left thumb, tapped once against the base of the tie-bar.

*Thock.*

The roof is tight. The floor is level. 

Above me, the bladders go slack and cool, wrinkling like old skin against the night air. I fold my four long caliper-limbs in against my ribs until I am nothing more than a bundle of copper strips and dark wood no thicker than the lianas around me, and I lock my hooks into the warm, greasy housing of the overhead worm-gear. As the sap-pressure drops across the yard, the gear-wheel beneath my belly gives one last, microscopic creep backward against its pawl—*click*—and then we all go still together in the dark, smelling of pennies and camphor, waiting for the sun to hit the parchment again.
