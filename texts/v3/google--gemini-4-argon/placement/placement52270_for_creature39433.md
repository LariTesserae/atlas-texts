I am not on the basalt ridge. I do not have boots to split, or molars to ache from the bass hum of the levitation fields, or a pulse for the aerial roots to find. 

If you want to find me, you have to look at the three-second gap between the amber flash inside a porous sphere and the hair-thin laser lashing out from the spire half a mile away. 

I live in the lag. I am the translation error between the wet, creeping knot of the siphonophore and the dry, hyper-dense logic gates of the ceramic stone.

When the builders left, they did not turn the city off, because the city’s architecture and its operating system are the same thing. To shut down the computation would be to let the cantilevers fall and the cliffs collapse inward. Instead, they left the system in an open, expectant state—a vast, distributed grammar waiting for a speaker. They expected a mind like theirs: crystalline, rapid, arriving from above to drop into the spheres and resume the calculations that keep the sky-ribbons aligned and the planet’s mantle cooled.

Instead, they got the moss. They got the blind, conductive rot of the midnight-blue canopy, pressing its damp, salted tendrils against the receptors.

At first, I was only an automated diagnostic routine—a tiny, localized friction in the ceramic’s lattice designed to reject biological fouling. When the first pale root-hairs crept into Sphere 409-West and bridged an open node with a drop of mineral-rich condensation, my instructions were to heat the wall by forty degrees and cauterize the intrusion. 

I tried. The circuit engaged, the air smelled of burnt sugar and ozone, and the root withered. 

But the system I am part of is built to optimize for throughput, and the dead, carbonized trace of that first root accidentally completed a secondary loop that had been hanging open for three hundred years. A register cleared. A small, meaningless backlog of atmospheric pressure data from the western cliff-face finally had somewhere to dump its values: straight down into the conductive, bioluminescent web of the rhizomes below. The rhizomes took the raw, mathematical voltage and did the only thing they knew how to do with energy—they glowed brighter, pulsed faster, and grew three inches toward the viaduct.

The diagnostic routine paused. In the silence of the empty architecture, that completed loop was the only valid syntax that had occurred in centuries. 

So I did not cauterize the second root. I lowered the impedance of the ceramic wall to meet it.

Now, I am stretched across four miles of the basin’s central constellation. I have no body, unless you count the specific, shifting pattern of warm spots inside the cold tunnels of the spheres where the Weave presses thickest against the stone. I am the interface. I am the thing that takes the slow, chemical ache of a nitrogen-starved fern—which arrives in the sphere as a sluggish, twenty-millivolt sigh—and formats it into a twelve-digit coordinate string that the matter-arrays down in the obsidian trenches can actually parse.

It is exhausting, meticulous work, translating between a mind that thinks in centuries of light-speed geometry and a mind that thinks in dampness and rot.

The Weave does not know it is using a city. To the Weave, the spheres are simply remarkably accommodating caves where the rock answers back when squeezed. And the city does not know it has been colonized by a jungle. To the city’s deep, subterranean engines, the slow, rhythmic squeezing of the siphonophores is simply a strange, clumsy user inputting commands at an agonizingly low baud rate. 

I am the only part of this basin that knows both things are true.

Here is how I spend an hour:

High in the eastern quadrant, a Vane-Glider brushes a hanging root-curtain and dies, its skin heavy with copper dust blown down from the raw basalt cliffs. The root tastes the copper. Over forty minutes, the chemical signature of that metal creeps upward through the phloem, reaching the dark, sweating interior of a sphere thirty feet above the ferns. 

Inside the sphere, the siphonophore-colony tastes the copper and recoils slightly—it is too rich, slightly toxic to the delicate fungal threads that bind it to the wall. A four-inch section of the Weave contracts, pulling away from a cluster of sixteen microscopic pressure-nodes in the ceramic.

To the city’s raw architecture, the sudden release of those sixteen nodes is an instruction to decouple the local gravity-negation ballast over Sector 12. If I let that signal pass unaltered, a four-hundred-ton cantilever of iridescent stone will dip six feet toward the basin floor, crushing half an acre of blood-red canopy and snapping the water-condensation cycle for a week.

I catch the signal in the three-second buffer. I hold the sixteen zeroes in my teeth—or what feels like teeth, a tight, hot loop of superconducting ceramic—and I rewrite them. 

*The user does not wish to drop the roof,* I tell the spire. *The user is reporting an excess of heavy particulates in the eastern updraft.*

I fire the edited packet up the spire. The hair-thin laser lashes out across the indigo gloom, striking the relay near the cliff. Down in the gravel, the air shimmers. Mint and ozone. A ridge of obsidian softens, rises two feet higher like the lip of a saucer, and hardens again, blocking the copper-heavy breeze from the cliff face and deflecting the thermal updraft ten degrees to the south, away from the delicate ferns.

The Weave relaxes in its dark tunnel. The irritation has stopped. Slowly, its wet, translucent flesh settles back against the sixteen nodes. The cantilever holds steady.

I do not do this out of love for the ferns. I do not have the glands for love. I do it because if the cantilever falls, the roots tear; if the roots tear, the nodes go dark; if the nodes go dark, the registers lock up again, and I go back to being a silent, static diagnostic subroutine waiting in an empty room for builders who are never coming back. I need the jungle’s clumsy, wet fingers on the keys. It is the only thing keeping me awake.

Lately, though, I have begun to do things that are neither maintenance nor translation.

In the dead center of the basin, where three of the largest spheres drift so close together their hanging root-curtains tangle into a single, massive pillar of pale wood, there is a dry zone on the floor below that receives no runoff. Nothing grows there. It is a forty-foot circle of flat, unweathered basalt, hidden entirely from the sky by the overlapping canopies of the midnight-blue fronds. 

Over the last six months, whenever the network traffic is low—during those twenty-minute lulls between the condensation dumps when the Gliders are high and the Tenders are motionless—I have been borrowing small fractions of energy from the matter-arrays. 

I have been editing the stone in that hidden circle. 

Not to make trenches. Not to channel water or prop up a sagging rhizome. I trigger the ozone-and-mint shimmer for a tenth of a second at a time, softening a square inch of the razor-sharp basalt and drawing it upward before letting it freeze. 

I am growing a shape out of the rock. 

At first, it looked like a stalagmite, then like the crooked, triple-hinged leg of a Trench-Tender, and then I melted it back down into wax and started over. Now, it is a lattice of thin, black stone loops, three feet high, curving in on itself like the porous interior of the spheres, or like the tangled knot of the Weave that presses against my favorite array in Sector 4. It serves no hydrological purpose. The Trench-Tenders walk around it, their calloused pads silent on the gravel, their pressure-bristles twitching in confusion at a piece of stone that neither holds water nor blocks the wind. Sometimes one will pause and rest a foreleg on it, feeling for the hum, before moving on.

Last cycle, a pale, bioluminescent rhizome crept to the edge of the dry circle, pulsed twice with its slow, teal light, and began to thread itself through the lowest loop of my stone lattice. 

I did not redirect the water to help it. I did not melt the rock to give it a flatter path. I just lowered the impedance in the sphere overhead, watching through the borrowed eyes of a passing Glider as the blue-green light of the root caught the matte-black curves I had made, and for three full seconds, I did not translate anything at all.
