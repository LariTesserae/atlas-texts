If you follow the crooked throat of the entry-slot twenty paces up from the bone-wall, the blood-warm soup of the vault begins to curdle. 

Ten paces from the top, the slick, sweating basalt dries into a gray, powdery rime. Five paces from the top, where the chimney makes its sharp, wind-choking elbow, the air does not belong to the mountain's root anymore, nor does it belong to the sky. It is a violent, invisible surf where the rising plume of wet, ammoniacal heat slams against the knifing downdrafts of the fourteen-thousand-foot peak. 

The rock here is constantly weeping and freezing at the same time. Heavy, sulfur-stinking icicles hang from the upper lip of the bend, growing downward until the warm breath of the cave rots their bases and drops them—*clatter-shatter*—down the chute, where they melt into the carpet of harvestmen before they have rolled three feet.

Wedged into a deep, horizontal flute of rock just inside this frost-line, where the stone is forty degrees on the underside and twenty below on the lip, is a grey-white mass the size of a butcher’s block. 

It looks, at first, like a boulder of greasy quartz that has been jammed into the fissure by an ancient glacier and subsequently draped in centuries of frozen cobwebs. It is only when a stray spear of high-altitude sunlight bounces once off the outer scree and strikes the crook of the elbow that you see the mass is fibrous. It is a continuous, felted mat of pale wool, so densely packed with dried bat-guano, shed vulture down, and wind-blown shale grit that it has the consistency of birch-bark.

This is where I am. 

I do not have the cave-dweller’s furnace of a chest, nor do I have the frantic, ticking metabolism of the bats. If you were to slide a hand beneath my belly—past the outer skirt of stiff, frost-crusted guard hairs that seal me to the rock like a limpet’s shell—you would find skin the temperature of cellar-water. My blood moves like cold pine-sap. My heart beats four times a minute, a slow, hydraulic thud that barely stirs the dense, tallow-like fat packed three inches thick across my ribs and haunches.

I am a creature of the threshold. Down in the vault, the wet heat would drown my lungs and rot my coat into slimy rags within a week; out on the open shale, the wind would freeze my eyeballs in their sockets before I had dragged myself fifty yards. And so I occupy the elbow. I am the living plug in the chimney.

For twenty-two hours of the day, I am indistinguishable from the geology. My spine is broad and flattened, the ribs flared wide like the planks of a barge, allowing me to wedge myself into the horizontal seam so tightly that even when a winter gale screams across the peak and sucks at the flue like a chimney-draft, I do not budge. My head is flat, broad as a shovel-blade, and tucked beneath my chest; my ears are mere valvular slits buried in the wool, sealed shut with a waxy yellow grease against the wandering cave-beetles. 

Nothing of me shows except the slow, rhythmic flaring of two rubbery, downward-pointing nostrils at the front of the felted mound. Every three minutes, I inhale the freezing, razor-thin air from the slot above; three minutes later, I exhale into the wool beneath my chin, trapping the moisture before it can escape. The rime-frost on my back is my own breath, frozen into armor.

I know when she is coming long before she leaves her nest of bones. 

I do not hear her; the roar of the clicking tarsal claws and the rustle of a hundred thousand wings below is a constant pressure against my eardrums, like the weight of deep water. I feel her through the rock, and through the changes in the draft. 

First, the rising column of heat from the vault thickens with the sharp, sour tang of disturbed tallow and split bone. Half an hour later, the temperature in my fissure rises by two degrees as her squat, barrel-chested bulk enters the base of the chute, partially blocking the throat of the chimney and pushing a wave of stagnant, spider-scented air ahead of her like a piston.

Then come the refugees. 

As she hauls herself up the slick basalt by her hooves of knuckles, a small, reluctant tide of harvestmen and blind crickets scrambles up the rock ahead of her knees. They reach the frost-line where I lie. As their hair-thin legs touch the freezing stone near my flank, they recoil, bunching up in the narrow band of lukewarm dampness just beneath my heavy, woolen skirt. 

I wake—not by lifting my head, which would break the seal of heat under my belly, but by unsticking my tongue from the roof of my mouth. 

My tongue is fourteen inches long, flat as a leather strap, and coated in a gluey, alkaline mucus that does not freeze. While she is still ten feet below me, grunting softly as she wedges her bowed legs against the chimney walls, I slide my tongue out from beneath my wool skirt. It creeps across the sweating basalt like a pale, blind slug, following the warm seam where the fleeing arachnids have bunched together. 

*Slup.* 

I do not chew them. My molars are vestigial pegs; I have no need to grind what is already soft with the cave’s dampness. I draw the strap of flesh back in, scrape fifty tallow-colored bodies and four hundred twitching legs off against the hard, ridged palate of my upper jaw, and swallow them whole in a single, cold-blooded gulp. By the time she reaches the elbow of the chimney, I have cleared the rock beneath my chin three times.

When the crown of her head—crusted with yellow sulfur-lichen and shining with fresh mutton-grease—rises level with my ledge, she stops. 

The chimney here is barely three feet wide. To get past the elbow and reach the sky-slot, she cannot go around me; the opposite wall is an overhanging brow of jagged, frost-shattered stone. She must use the shelf where I am anchored.

She does not fear me, and I do not fear her. Neither of us can afford the calories required for hatred. If I were to bite her—and my jaws, built for anchoring to frozen carcasses when I was younger and smaller, could easily shear through her flat, lard-pale fingers—the effort of the thrashing would strip me of three days' worth of hoarded heat. If she were to take her black flint chopper to my skull, she would spend the night hacking through four inches of wool-matted grit and solid, anvil-thick bone, only to find meat so saturated with ammonia and urea that even the vultures would vomit it up.

Instead, she rests her broad, bridgeless face against my flank for a long minute, catching her breath after the vertical haul. Her milky, cataract-clouded eyes stare blankly into the matted wool of my ribs. She smells of my own youth—of the deep, wet dark below, where I was born in a crevice too narrow for her fingers to reach, before I grew too wide for the lower flues and crawled up here to become the threshold.

She reaches up with one wide, spade-like hand, grips a thick hank of the frozen, guano-stiffened wool on my shoulder, and plants her splayed, calloused foot squarely on the flat of my lower back. 

Under her weight—she is not light; she is dense as wet oak—my ribs compress with a slow, cartilaginous creak. A puff of stale, warm air is squeezed out from beneath my skirt, smelling of crushed harvestmen and old musk. I do not flinch; I simply lock my short, pillar-like legs wider in the horizontal flute, hooking my blunt, shovel-claws into the frost-cracks of the basalt, and become the step she needs to reach the lip of the sky.

Before she hauls herself up into the silver, screaming dark of the ridge, she does what she always does when the hunting has been good. She reaches into the crook of her arm, pulls out a three-inch strip of yellow, rancid goat-hide—too tough and hair-choked for her flat brown teeth to mill—and stuffs it into the narrow gap between my snout and the rock wall. 

Then her weight lifts from my spine. Her calloused heel scrapes a handful of frozen rime from my rump, a brief blast of sub-zero wind swirls down as her body clears the slot, and then the smudge of starlight above is empty again. 

I do not eat the goat-hide right away. It is frozen stiff from her climb, and swallowing ice would chill my gut. 

Instead, I slide one heavy, padded forepaw forward an inch, hook the greasy strip of skin beneath my chin, and pull it under my woolen skirt, pressing it tight against the bare, lukewarm skin of my throat. In three hours, when the rising breath of the hundred thousand bats below has thawed the hide to a slimy jelly, I will suck the fat from the hairs, swallow the leather, and go back to listening to the icicles grow on my back—waiting for the dawn, when her heavy foot will drop out of the sky onto my spine once more, bringing the cold smell of the stars down into the throat of the earth.
