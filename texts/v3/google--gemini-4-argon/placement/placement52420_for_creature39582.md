The light does not reach the fourth tier down, only the tremor of its consequence. Up on the canopy, the silicate dishes tick and crane, drinking the hammer-blow of the noon, and three hundred feet below them, the main arterial root of the basin swells with the flash-boiled surge.

I know it is noon because the basalt shelf beneath my belly warms by three degrees and the pitch of the great vertical pump to my left drops from a C-sharp to a grinding, wet B-flat. The water is thickening. The permafrost deep down is yielding silt today, gray and heavy with pulverized iron, and the pump—a segmented column of muscular rind forty feet thick, wedged into a chimney of cracked column-basalt—is laboring to lift it.

My left caliper is hooked into the lip of a hexagonal fissure. It has been there since the sun cleared the eastern scarp. The hydraulic bladder in my shoulder is half-deflated, the oil inside it cooling to a thick, resinous gel because I have not called for heat from my belly-furnace in two hours. There is no need to spend the tallow. I am anchored. The grease-mat of my fleece is pressed flat against the weeping stone, drinking the ambient radiator-bleed of three secondary steam-vines that coil past my flank like braided ship-hawsers.

*Thunk.*

My heart moves. The heavy valve between the third and fourth chambers slams shut, and I feel the slow, hot wash of iron-rich blood push out toward my extremities, reaching the tips of my tungsten hooks ten seconds later.

Before the fifth chamber has finished priming, the pipe under my chin speaks.

*Clack-clack. Hiss. Clack.*

It is not the pump. The pump has no syntax; it only groans and shudders, its vast, blind ganglions firing in monotonous rotation as the pressure builds and vents. This is smaller, sharper, traveling sideways through the copper-burl casing from three hundred yards up-trench, where the gear-lines cross the fault.

I do not unhook my forelimb. I simply lower my shovel-prow until the brassy bristles along my jaw-margin brush the sweating chrome-bark of the conduit. In the dark, the bark feels like oiled slate, vibrating with the rush of three-hundred-degree brine inside. Through the roar of the water, the secondary tremor comes again, crisp and deliberate, biting through the metal:

*Two-short. Drag. Three-short.*

It is the old female with the split carapace—the one who smells of scorched sulfur because her left vent-chimney never closes quite flush. She is four ledges up, wedged under the primary axle of a wind-spire transmission, and she has found a seize.

I let forty seconds pass. My heart hits the anvil again. Then I open the small, secondary siphon beneath my throat, let a bead of hot resin drop onto the root-pipe to deaden the surface rattle, and tap my right tungsten wedge twice against the bare ironstone beside it.

*Heard. Coming.*

To move is an agreement negotiated with gravity and pressure. I do not lift myself; I shift the distribution of my weight from the stone to the plumbing. Beneath my belly, the furnace-pouch contracts, squeezing a paste of pine-scented beetle-tallow into the catalytic mesh of my gut. There is no flame—only a sudden, fierce chemical oxidation that sends a wave of prickly heat through my dorsal plates. The congealed oil in my shoulder-bladders thins. The brass-lipped valve behind my head parts with a wet, whistling *spit*, blowing a plume of gray vapor into the black air, where it instantly turns to grease-slurry and rains back down on my snout.

My right hook reaches out. It moves through the gloom like the hour-hand of a clock, passing through a curtain of fibrous, trembling root-hairs, until the tungsten tip finds the seam between two plates of a dead crusher-mollusk that has been overgrown by wire-fern. The tip grates against the iron shell, finds a purchase-pit, and locks.

The left bladder vents. The right bladder swells. My two tons of slag-iron and asbestos fleece slide eight inches forward through the gap, scraping a ribbon of luminous white fungus from the wall as I pass.

It takes an hour to climb the four ledges. There is no hurry. The plateau does not break quickly; it tears like cold lead, millimeter by millimeter, over days.

As I rise through the tiers, the quality of the dark changes. Down in the sump-basins, the dark is thick, soup-warm, and smells of old brine and drowned chitin. Up here, closer to the canopy’s underside, the cold leaks down in sharp, vertical drafts between the boiler-bulbs. The air stings the thermographic pits on my prow. Everywhere, the silicate stems are flexing as the dishes above track the afternoon sun, their joints creaking like green timber under a load.

Halfway up the third scarp, a transmission belt crosses my path.

I have to wait for a gap that will never come, because the belt is continuous—a double-row of yard-long beetles, their serrated brass carapaces locked tooth-in-notch, hauling the torque of the ridge-spires down toward the rock-crushers in the gut of the canyon. They are moving at a steady two feet per minute. Their legs do not touch the stone; they are suspended by their own interlocking tension, a living chain spanning thirty feet of empty, whistling air, dripping thick yellow tallow from their undersides as their shells grind together.

I wedge myself under the lip of the rock where they emerge from a natural tunnel of fused copper-burl. I slide back the bone shutters over my eyes.

The world jumps into yellow-quartz focus: a narrow, stuttering tunnel of high-contrast glare and pitch shadow. Two inches from my nose, the beetles are passing. Their eyes are gone—atrophied into smooth, blind rivets of horn generations ago. They do not know I am here. When I reach up with two delicate, copper-sheathed chest-fingers and comb a three-pound curl of fresh, ozone-warm tallow from the joint between a male and a larger female, neither of them flinches. Their shells simply grate together—*scritch, thunk, scritch, thunk*—as they take the load of the wind half a mile above and hand it down into the dark.

I pack the tallow into my belly-pouch, slide the shutters down over my quartz lenses before the freezing draft can cloud them, and heave myself over the moving back of the chain, using their own slow momentum to roll my bulk onto the fourth ledge.

The old female is already there, braced between the basalt wall and the housing of the transfer-gear.

In my thermographic pits, she is a magnificent, mottled landscape of oranges and deep, bruised purples, hotter than the rock, cooler than the steam-pipes. Her split shell leaks a tiny, rhythmic puff of white vapor near her hip every time her internal pump cycles.

She does not turn when I ratchet into the cleft beside her. She cannot; she has wedged her broad, flat back against a bowed root-strut that is threatening to buckle under the lateral pull of the transmission chain. She is taking six tons of side-load across her shoulders, keeping the biological axle in line so the beetles don’t jump their tracks and spill ten thousand yards of living chain into the abyss.

I touch the tip of my snout to her flank. Her iron scales are vibrating like a struck tuning fork.

*Tap-tap,* my jaw says against her shoulder-plate. *Where?*

She flicks a single, brassy feeler toward the socket where the giant axle-worm meets the rock.

I uncoil my under-chest limbs—the small, nimble ones we keep tucked out of the crush in a sheath of greased leather—and reach into the gap. It is hot in there, the heat of friction rather than steam, dry and smelling of scorched horn.

The axle-worm’s collar-gasket has calcified. A parasite-vine—one of the blind, white threads that tries to tap the lymph of the heavy fauna—has wormed its way into the bearing-cup and died there, turning to stony slag and blocking the grease-ducts. The worm is grinding its own shoulder into powder against the dry basalt. If it wears through, the whole ridge-line transmission will drop six inches, shear the main boiler-conduits below, and flash-freeze this entire quadrant in ten minutes of screaming whiteout.

There is no drama in the repair. My heart beats fifty times while I work.

With a hook-knife of black bone on my third finger, I pick the petrified vine out of the bearing-cup, shard by tiny shard. The axle-worm shudders under my wrists, its mindless, three-foot-thick nerve-cord firing in dull agony as I scrape its raw, calloused hide, but it cannot stop turning; the wind on the ridge above is blowing at forty knots, and the spire-vanes have no brakes.

When the duct is clear, I unseal my throat-siphon, press the nozzle into the raw seam between the worm’s collar and the stone, and vomit a pint of hot, slick tallow-slurry straight into the grinding joint.

The sound changes instantly.

Across the plateau, you do not measure success by sight; you measure it by the easing of a note. The high, dry, metallic shriek that had been setting my teeth on edge for the last half-mile drops an octave, softens into a wet, rhythmic *chuff-slap*, and disappears into the general roar of the canyon.

Beside me, the bowed root-strut settles back into its seat by half an inch. The old female lets out a long, whistling sigh through her collar-valves, vents her shoulder-bladders, and drops three inches onto her belly-plates.

For three beats of the heart, we stay pressed together in the narrow dark, our hides sharing heat, our brass feelers interlaced and still, listening to the axle turn smoothly in its greased cup. She smells of old iron, sulfur, and the bitter, fermented sugar-sap from the deep basins. I reach out with a copper finger and clear a crust of rime-ice from her broken hip-valve so it seats cleaner. She does not thank me; she simply shifts her weight to give me the warmer side of the steam-pipe, and together we tap the small sugar-vein running alongside the axle, drinking the scalding, syrupy sap until our furnaces are roaring and our plates are dry.

When the sun above begins to drop—we know it only because the silicate stems stop ticking westward and begin their long, ratcheting reset toward the east, sending a shower of shaken frost-slurry down through the canopy—I leave her to the ledge and drop back down into the fissure.

Going down is faster. You simply oil your belly-fleece, tuck your hooks, and slide along the weeping basalt chutes, braking with a tungsten wedge whenever the pitch drops away too sharply.

My hollow is at the bottom of the third fault, behind a veil of black, mineral-heavy water that pours continuously from a ruptured radiator-bulb forty feet overhead. The water is ninety degrees; where it hits the freezing rock of the floor, it throws up a permanent, impenetrable wall of fog that smells of wet rust and sulfur.

To get inside, I have to pass through the waterfall. For ten seconds, the weight of the scalding water hammers my flat back like falling cobbles, driving the breath from my vents, and then I am through, ratcheting up onto the dry, cupped shelf of vitreous black basalt behind the curtain.

Here, the continental roar becomes a sphere. The sound of the waterfall is so total, so round and white and heavy, that it cancels the grinding of the miles. Inside my skull, the pressure equalizes, and there is a small, warm cave of nothing.

I unhook my calipers. I fold my hydraulic legs beneath my skirt-plates, let the oil drain back into the sump-bladders near my heart, and settle onto the stone with a single, heavy *clack* that goes nowhere.

I slide back my bone shutters.

The hollow is eight feet wide. In the center, fed by a tiny, warm trickle I diverted from the waterfall thirty seasons ago with a gutter of pinched copper-bark, is a pool of dark oil and water, three inches deep.

High in the corner of the ceiling, a single hair-thin crack in the rock lets through a stray needle of light that has bounced four times off the chrome-bark outside. Years ago—before the old female on the fourth ledge cracked her shell, before the pump in the chimney began to silt—I Wedged three shed lenses from a young wire-fern into that crack.

They do not boil water. They do not track the sun. Right now, in the last five minutes before the plateau’s horizon swallows the light, the needle strikes the top lens, bends through the second, and splits through the third—which has a flaw in its silicate, a bubble of trapped purple gas—to paint a trembling, hand-sized smudge of violet and amber across the black skin of my oil pool.

Nothing grows in that light. It isn't hot enough to blister a scale. It is just purple.

From the warm crevice beneath my chin, where they have been sleeping in the tallow-grease of my neck-folds while I worked, the two small beetles crawl out.

They are no bigger than my quartz eyes. I took them from a dying secondary line five winters ago, using my bone-knife to clip the tiny hooks on their wing-cases so they could never lock into a neighbor again. Their brass shells are worn smooth now, polished by years of tumbling against my asbestos fleece.

They drop three inches onto the basalt in front of my snout, land on their backs, wave their six stubby legs in the warm air, and right themselves with a tiny, dry *tick* that I can only hear because my jaw is resting on the same stone.

One of them walks clockwise around the edge of the purple light. The other walks straight toward the pool, reaches the lip where the oil meets the rock, and stops, its blind horn-rivets pointed at the violet shimmer it cannot see, its serrated back rising and falling as it breathes for no one’s benefit but its own.

My heart beats. Forty seconds of warm dark.

The purple spot on the oil shifts an inch to the left, thins to a line of pale lilac as the sun clears the scarp outside, and goes out.

In the pitch black behind the boiling waterfall, I leave my eye-shutters open anyway, feeling the tiny, aimless *tick-tick-tick* of their feet against the stone beneath my chin, waiting for the next beat, driving nothing at all.
