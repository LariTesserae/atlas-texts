To find me here, you cannot look for something that walks across the plazas or hides inside the walls. Everything with a body in this valley already has a job, and every job is accounted for. If you added one more footstep to the avenues, the great siphon below the bedrock would have to swallow an extra drop of displaced water; the flat, iron-shelled things in the masonry would have to shift a leg to take the new weight.

Instead, go to the western face of the four-story watchtower, where the forty-degree sun has never reached. 

About five feet off the ground, two massive slabs of dark slate meet at a vertical corner. Because this is dry-stack work, there is a seam between them—not a crack, which would imply an accident, but a deliberate, hairline relief-joint left by the builders so the stone can settle without shearing. In the permanent cobalt shadow of the western side, that seam is packed with a narrow ribbon of white hoarfrost, no wider than a thumbnail. 

Put your eye twelve inches from it, the way one of the wool-thatched Keepers would.

That ribbon of frost is where I am. More accurately, I am the exact boundary where the water stops being liquid and becomes stone.

It is not a static place to be. From across the courtyard, the frost looks painted on, as dead and fixed as the bronze pry-bar on the terrace. Up close, at the distance of a hand’s breadth, it is a furious, silent negotiation. The air of the plateau is always drifting invisibly against the wall, bringing new molecules of vapor that want to build the ice outward, growing six-sided needles that would bridge the gap, catch the light, and eventually lever the two dark slates apart. At the same time, the stone deep inside the wall is slightly warmer than the shadow outside, and it is constantly trying to melt my root-edge back into a bead of water that would run down the masonry and ruin the dry balance of the lower courses.

I am the tension that keeps both things from happening.

My body—if you can call a state of temperature a body—stretches for three miles through the city, discontinuous and geometric. I exist only on the western and northern faces of the architecture, wherever the cobalt shadow falls across a damp joint. I am a broken grid of white lines, a blueprint of the city’s cold side drawn in microscopic hexagonal plates.

I know the inhabitants of the valley not by sight, for I have no eyes, nor by sound, though the dry *tick-tick* of the iron-shelled Shimmers travels cleanly through the slate right up to my back. I know them by the tiny emergencies of heat and pressure they bring to the wall.

When one of the long, translucent salamanders slides across the wet slate toward my seam, I feel it coming for an hour beforehand. It is a cold-blooded thing, nearly the exact temperature of the shade, yet its slow, muscular creep generates a faint, ghostly wake of friction. As its belly passes over my frost-line, the tips of my upper crystals go blunt and wet. I do not retreat; I yield by the thickness of an oil-film, drinking the moisture of its skin into my lower lattice, stiffening my base just enough that its toes do not slip on the rock, and then, the moment its tail clears the joint, I snap my sharp, dry needles back into the crisp air.

When the Shimmers inside the wall unhook their knees because a herd of ungulates has walked off a roof two hundred yards away, the two slates holding me breathe inward by a ten-thousandth of an inch. That squeeze warms me. I feel the sudden, dark burden of the mountain’s weight trying to turn me back into water. I hold fast by trading density for height—collapsing my lacy outer feathers of rime and packing myself into clear, blue-white glazier’s ice deep in the throat of the seam, becoming another shim among the flint, holding the edge until the Shimmers find their new places and the rock relaxes.

And then there are the Keepers.

They are the only things here that look at me. Once in a very long while—after a hundred cycles of the herds rising and lying down, after the great subterranean heart in the flooded fault has thumped a thousand times through the soles of the towers—the light on my western wall will dim slightly. A patch of the blue shadow has been replaced by the gray, terraced wool of a crouching Keeper.

Its four smoky-quartz eyes stop a foot away from my seam. I can feel the brine in its veins—salty, sluggish, refusing to freeze—and the faint, dry pull of the gill-pleats under its coat as it drinks the air just in front of me.

If I have grown greedy during those hundred cycles—if I have let the plateau mist build an extra terrace of ice-crystals out past the bevel of the slate—the Keeper’s six-fingered hand emerges from the thatch. It takes ten minutes to reach the wall. Then the flat, copper-colored mineral plate of its thumbnail sets itself against my cheek.

It does not scrape me away in a rough shower of white powder. The Keeper’s nail is beveled to the exact angle of the stone’s original split. It slides down the joint with a slow, steady pressure that melts the offending fraction of ice by sheer contact, and its dry, blue-gray palm immediately wicks the resulting moisture away before it can refreeze lower down. 

To anything else, this might feel like being pruned, or being erased. To me, it is the only way I have of knowing my own shape. Left alone, I would swell until I broke the wall, or waste away into the cisterns and be swallowed by the blind white root in the dark below. The Keeper’s copper nail is the edge of my body. When the hand draws back into the wool, and the wool slowly unfolds itself down the stairs to blend into a passing herd of gray grazers, I am left behind in the sharp, metallic afternoon—perfectly flush with the basalt, hard as flint, holding the cold side of the city together while the sun stands still on the other side of the wall.
