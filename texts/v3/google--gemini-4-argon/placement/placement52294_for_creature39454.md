I am not by the rigs. The rigs are too sure of themselves. They are great, bragging drums that never change their minds, shouting the same four words into the mud for three hundred years. If you stay by a rig, your heart stops being yours; the iron borrows it to keep time, and when you die, you owe your grease back to the well.

To find me, you have to leave the Cougher behind until its *thump-hiss* drops out of the air and lives only in the fat of your heels. You have to walk out along the great, sweating hogbacks where the stone aqueducts run.

The clans rarely follow the pipes for more than a mile or two. They know the brine goes out, pushed by the pistons toward the plateau’s distant rim, and they assume the earth simply drinks it back down somewhere far away. They do not care about the pipes because the pipes do not have crossheads to crush you or cooling slats to hang a basket from. The pipes just lie there in the sponge-mire like endless, dead snakes of six-foot cast iron, warm to the touch, humming a single, continuous, throat-numbing note: *voooooooooo*.

Walk along the top of the northern main for twelve miles. Your splayed feet will find the groove worn into the iron’s crown—not worn by leather, because nothing here wears shoes, and not worn by the three-toed mud-striders, who hate the vibration against their pads, but worn smooth by five generations of tallow-slick bellies and calloused knees.

Where the pipe dips into the Long Sluice, a mile-wide trough of weeping clay between two fern-mountains, three Mains come together at a Manifold House.

The roof of the house caved in before my grandmother’s grandmother had teeth, and the walls are only knee-high stubs of crumbled brick, buried under a three-foot mattress of white root-hair and mint-weed. What remains is the Knot: a massive, sprawling tangle of brass gate-valves, pressure-bells, and bypass loops the size of tree trunks, sitting half-submerged in a pool of mineral-slick, ninety-degree water.

That is where I am. If you drop your hand into the water thirty yards away, I will know the shape of your fingers before your knuckles touch the silt.

I am what the rig-clans call a *Pipe-Leech*, though when they need to trade for the sweet, cherry-sized salt-pearls that grow inside the bypass traps, they use a polite rhythm against my forearm that translates roughly to *He-Who-Listens-Long*.

If you were to run your hands over my shoulders—and you would have to go slowly, whistling a flat, harmless breath-note through your teeth so I did not open your throat with my brass scraper—you would know at once that I did not grow up in a rookery basket. I am not round like the Cougher’s people. The people of the engines are built like boulders because they have to brace themselves against the gale of the flywheels and the slam of the walking-beams.

I am long. My ribs are flat, like the slats of a weir, because I spend six hours a day pressed lengthwise against the curve of the thirty-inch intake mains. My skin does not smell of the burnt-sulfur grease of the sumps; I grease myself with the pale, alum-heavy butter of the pipe- barnacles and the bruised leaves of bog-camphor. It makes my skin tight, leathery, and cool, so the warm air slides off me without leaving a heat-wake for the mire-eels to follow.

And my ears are not like the grandmother’s at the Cougher. Hers are thick and meaty, scarred from a lifetime of folding shut against steam-blows. Mine are thin as bladder-skin and wide as soup-plates, scalloped at the rims like the gills of a mushroom. A loud noise hurts me like a thumb driven into a bruise. If you brought me within half a mile of the Cougher’s main exhaust, I would vomit from the vertigo of it, curl into a ball in the mud, and bleed from my nose.

I do not live in the roar. I live in the hum.

Right now, I am lying on my belly along the top of the South-West Bypass, my chin resting on a flange-bolt, my ankles crossed in the warm drizzle. My left ear is folded flat against my skull to shut out the *pop-slap* of the bladder-vines around the pool. My right ear is pressed flush against the wet, green brass of the main gate-valve, sealed to the metal with a smear of heavy axle-tallow so not a breath of air comes between my drum and the brass.

Listen with me. Not with the air-ears—the air only tells you what is happening within a stone’s throw, and it lies whenever the wind shifts. Listen with the jawbone. Let the teeth part just a hair, so the vibration comes up through the chin, runs along the mandibles, and sets the little stones in the inner skull dancing.

Inside the pipe, the brine is moving at twelve knots. To a rig-hand, it is just a push. To me, it is a river full of voices.

Water cannot be squeezed. What happens to the brine at the source stays in the brine for twenty miles, traveling through the iron column faster than thunder travels through the mist.

Right now, through six inches of cast iron and seven miles of thick, salty soup, I am listening to the *Three-Hiss* rig. Its main plunger has a score along the western face—a scratch in the steel the width of a grass-blade. Every time the plunger bottoms out, it sings a tiny, high *zzt* into the brine. It has been singing that *zzt* since my mother was a girl. Today, the *zzt* is half a thumb-width longer than it was thirty rim-thunders ago. The score is widening. In two seasons, the packing will blow.

Beneath the *Three-Hiss*, twelve miles further out, the *Deep-Sinker* is pumping slow and thick, its brine heavy with dissolved limestone that hisses against the pipe-walls like sand in a gourd.

And beneath that—so faint you have to stop your own heart between beats to feel it in your molars—is the long, rhythmic *thud... thud... thud* of the Great Rim Engines forty miles away, where the aqueducts dump their loads into the abyss at the edge of the world.

I know the Rim is there because after the brine passes the last engines, the sound in the pipes changes from a push to a fall. It is a hollow, whistling roar that pulls at the iron, a sound with no bottom to it. I have never been there—the air grows too thin and cold three miles past the last condenser, and the ground turns to dry, sharp stone that cuts the feet—but I have felt the edge of the world in my back teeth every day of my life.

Wait. Hold your breath.

There. Did you feel it?

A tiny, dull *clink* in the northern main, four miles up-pipe toward the Cougher. Not the machine. The machine does not *clink* out of time.

And now, three seconds later, another: *clink-scrape*.

Someone is walking the pipe-crown in the dark, using a brass-shod staff to keep their balance on the wet curve of the iron.

I do not lift my head. If I lift my head, the warm rain will hit the dry spot where my ear was pressed to the brass, and the change in temperature will make my ear-cartilage tic. Instead, I slide my right hand down into the warm water beside the valve-casing, where my three brothers are sleeping in a submerged nest of braided roots, their nostrils poking up among the duckweed like mud-frogs.

I pinch my youngest brother’s big toe twice—*wake up*—and then draw a single line across his ankle-bone with my thumbnail: *One person. Coming down the north pipe. Heavy.*

My brothers do not splash. They simply sink three inches deeper into the warm silt until only the tips of their noses and the top rims of their folded ears break the surface.

I stay on the valve. Every Manifold House needs one body on top of the brass when a stranger approaches; otherwise, they might think the Knot is abandoned and try to turn a wheel.

We never turn the wheels. Once, three generations ago, a young man with a fever in his head took a wrench to the main relief gate at the Red-Mud Junction, wanting to hear a new song in the iron. He closed the gate three turns. The back-pressure traveled seven miles up the column in four seconds, blew the cylinder head off the *Old-Grinder*, boiled forty people in their hammocks, and left a Hole-in-the-Drum that is still cold and crawling with iron-worms today.

The wheels are grown over with limestone drip-stone now, cemented in place by the centuries, and we chip the stone away just enough to keep the packing glands weeping at their proper rate, never touching the spokes.

The footsteps leave the pipe thirty yards out and enter the sponge-mire. *Squelch-hiss, squelch-hiss.*

It is a woman from the Cougher. I know her before I smell her. She has the Cougher’s sixteen-stroke roll in her hips—she takes four paces, hitches her left shoulder as if ducking a low steam-pipe that isn't here, and takes four more. And she smells like the Cougher: old tallow, roasted crawler-shell, and that sharp, sour tang of the deep sulfur fault.

She stops at the edge of the pool, ten paces from my nose. Her great, thick ears are flared wide, twitching anxiously in the quiet.

To me, the Manifold House is as loud as a market—the *vooooo* of the three mains, the *tink-drip* of forty weeping glands, the gargle of the gas-traps, the soft, wet *slup-slup* of the blind flatfish grazing on the algae along the warm pipe-flanges.

To her, this place is terrifyingly still. There is no piston-slam to tell her where the ground is. There is no flywheel-wind to give her left and right. She is standing in the open dark, breathing fast, her flat, eyeless brow glistening with sweat as she turns her head from side to side, trying to catch the echo of her own breathing off the low brick ruins.

She reaches into her belt-pouch, pulls out a heavy lump of rendered slug-tallow wrapped in a broad fern-leaf, and holds it out in both hands.

She makes a low, rattling hum in her throat—her name-chord—and then taps her wrist three times with a bone needle. *I am from the Cougher. I have fat. I need salt-pearls for an old man whose lungs are full of the wet-rot.*

I do not speak back with my voice. My voice is thin and reed-like from disuse; we Pipe-Leeches talk mostly through the iron or through the water.

Instead, I reach down with my brass scraper and tap the neck of the northern gate-valve.

*Tink-tink. Tink.*

The sound does not just travel through the air; it leaps down the pipe she just walked along, running back toward her home engine like a silver fish, while the low brass bell of the valve-body hums a deep, comforting *booooong* that settles into the bones of her shins.

Her shoulders drop an inch. The tension leaves her neck. She has a landmark now.

I slide off the pipe into the knee-deep water without making a ripple, moving with the slow, boneless glide of an eel until I am standing directly in front of her. I do not touch her yet—rig-people startle hard when they are away from their engines—so I simply lean forward until my brow is four inches from hers.

I feel the heat coming off her face. She is feverish from the five-mile walk; her blood is hammering behind her nose at twice the speed of mine. Beneath the heavy reek of the Cougher’s sulfur-grease, she smells of wood-smoke, damp basket-wicker, and the sharp, milky scent of a woman who has a weaning child back in the rookery.

I reach out, take her thick, horn-calloused wrist in my long, water-pruned fingers, and press my thumb into the hollow between her tendons.

*Two pearls,* I tap against her pulse. *And I will tell you what is coming down the pipe from the Three-Hiss.*

Her fingers flex against my forearm, clumsy and heavy, like someone trying to play a flute with mittens on—she is used to shouting over a boiler, not whispering in the tendons—but I take her meaning: *What is coming?*

I draw her hand down until her palm is flat against the warm, sweating flank of the Southern Main. I hold her hand there, pressing my own over the back of her knuckles so she knows how hard to lean into the metal.

At first, she feels only the *vooooooo* of the brine. Her brow furrows; she is deaf to anything smaller than a hammer-stroke.

I tap the timing on her wrist with my thumb, isolating the tiny, rhythmic hitch in the water-column seven miles away: *Wait... wait... there. Wait... wait... there.*

After twenty strokes, I feel the breath catch in her chest. Her broad, flat nostril flares. She has found the *zzt* of the scored plunger in the Three-Hiss.

*Two seasons,* I tap into her skin. *The Three-Hiss will throw its packing. It will go cold for a month while they wrap it with bladder-hide. Their young men will come to the Cougher’s fern-beds to hunt while their own rain stops.*

She pulls her hand back from the pipe as if the iron has burned her. To a rig-hand, knowing what will happen two seasons from now is like looking over the edge of a cliff; they live inside a sixteen-stroke loop that is always the same, forever and ever.

She leaves the lump of tallow in my palm, takes the two rough, bitter spheres of crystallized mineral from my belt-pouch, and backs out of the pool, her ears pinned tight to her head. She does not turn her back on the Knot until her heel finds the crown of the northern pipe. Then she is gone, trotting back toward the thunder of her mother-machine as fast as her short, heavy legs can carry her.

I wait until the *clink-scrape* of her staff dies out in the iron two miles north.

Then I climb back onto the warm curve of the bypass, grease my right ear with a fresh thumbful of her tallow, and press my skull back against the brass.

My brothers surface beside me with soft, wet sighs, laying their chins across my calves and ankles so we are all connected to the metal through my ribs. The warm rain falls on our backs, running down into the pool in little threads of grease and water. Seven miles south, the plunger scratches its cylinder. Forty miles west, the great waterfall at the edge of the world roars into the dark. And twenty miles east, at the *Four-Clack* rig, someone has just died—because faint and thin, riding through the deep salt-column like a thread of copper wire through mud, comes the high, nasal chord of forty people screaming a dead man’s limp into a boiler casing, the song humming through the brine beneath my cheek as I close my throat and go to sleep.
