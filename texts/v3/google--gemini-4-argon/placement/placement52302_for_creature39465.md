I am at the third derailed cart, two miles down from the trench-head, where the standing wave from Intake Three intersects the bounce off the eastern fault wall. 

At first, you might look for me in the cart itself, or in the tallow mound that swallowed its rear axle forty years ago. You might think I am the tallow—that slow, rubbery blind thing drinking the sixty-hertz purr of the transformers and the deep, chest-crushing B-flat of the silos. 

Wait a moment. Look closer at the gap between the cart’s front bumper and the transformer shack ten feet away. 

To get from the rail to the shack, the tallow has to cross an acoustic dead-zone. Because of the way the sheer granite overhang four hundred feet above us reflects the turbine whine, there is a narrow trough right here—a ribbon of space about four feet wide and thirty yards long—where the sound waves cancel each other out. 

Step into this ribbon and the hundred-decibel gale vanishes. It is like dropping beneath the surface of a frozen lake. Your chest stops rattling; your eyeballs stop itching; the terrible, physical pressure lets go of your ribs and leaves behind a hollow, ringing silence so sudden it feels like falling. 

The tallow cannot cross this line. If a mound extends a pseudopod of grey-white gristle into the quiet trough, the fluid inside its hydrostatic tubes stops pulsing. Without the fan’s roar to beat its blood for it, the tip of the feeler goes slack, turns yellow, and freezes solid in twenty minutes. The dead-zone is a wall of silence as impassable to the mounds as a moat of fire.

And yet, the mounds on the rail side are crimson with iron from the transformer shack on the other side. 

Look down at the moss in the silent trough. It isn't green or bronze here; the *Sphagnum* has been crushed flat by something heavy dragged back and forth over the decades, and the groove is lined with a silver-grey lattice of braided wire, old copper grounding straps, and long, split ribbons of galvanized iron peeled from the shack's roof.

I am sitting on the corner of the transformer pad, my back pressed hard against the vibrating bolted steel so the sixty-hertz warmth can soak through my coat and into my spine. 

I am not very big—perhaps the size of a twelve-year-old child, or a hunched, heavy dog—and if I don’t move, you will take me for a pile of discarded mine-felt and greased canvas tarpaulins frozen to the concrete. My outer layer *is* mine-felt, four inches of dense, bitumen-soaked wool batting salvaged from the old pipe-insulation dumps up at the trench-head, stitched together with copper wire and coated in tallow-mucilage that has dried into a dark, waterproof horn. Beneath the wool, I have no hollow spaces left. Anything with a hollow lung dies down here; the standing waves turn air-pockets into hammers. Whatever my grandfather was before the fault-slip stranded the lower maintenance crews—whether he was a man, or a mule, or a machine—is a question that belongs to the upper air. Down here, I am solid. My ribs are packed tight with dense, fatty gristle that doesn't bruise when the pressure spikes; my windpipe is a narrow, weeping slit between my collarbones that drinks the cold, wet sulfite mist directly into a sponge of dark red gill-meat. I have no eardrums at all, only two flat plates of dense bone behind my jaw that read the pitch of the valley through my teeth.

When I move, it is at the speed of the valley: a slow, deliberate uncoiling, like cold pitch pouring from a bucket. Move too fast in the zero-degree air and you sweat; sweat inside your wool and you freeze to death before the next shift change.

In my left hand—which is a three-pronged mitten of thick horn and calloused grey skin—I hold a two-foot iron pry-bar. The bar is forty years old, its steel polished bright as a mirror by my grip, with no moss on it at all.

Watch what happens when I lean forward and wedge the tip of the pry-bar between the bottom plate of the transformer shack and the concrete pad, and then lay the flat of the iron against the frozen, four-foot gap of the silent trough.

The bar begins to sing. It takes the sixty-hertz buzz of the coils and the deep, grinding rumble of the concrete pad and carries them out into the dead-zone. 

On the other side of the quiet line, the tallow-mound waiting beside the buried cart stirs. A rim of frost snaps off its olive-green dome. From beneath its skirt of *Polytrichum*, a white, cartilage-stiff feeler telescopes out, quests blindly across the frozen moss, and lays its tip against the end of my pry-bar.

Instantly, the grey flesh flushes translucent pink. The vibration travels down the iron, into the feeler, and jump-starts the mound’s stalled tubes. Slowly, an inch every ten minutes, the great, mossy boulder begins to haul itself across the silent trough, using my pry-bar as a rail, sliding over the greased copper straps I have laid down for it.

Why do I bridge the gap for them? 

Look at the flank of the mound as it creeps past my knees. The moss on its back is fifteen inches thick, a dense, tight pelt of *Racomitrium* and rust-red liverworts. Tucked deep inside that pelt, six inches down where the tallow’s body-heat keeps the temperature at a steady, wet forty degrees, something is growing that does not belong to the moss or the slime mold. 

They are tubers—pale, knotty, starch-heavy knuckles of blind root, descendants of the cellar-potatoes the miners brought down in sacks seventy years ago, now adapted to root directly into the rich, sulfurous rind of the tallow mounds. Left on the valley floor, the tubers freeze solid. Planted in the back of a moving boulder, warmed by the tallow’s slow metabolism and fertilized by the iron-rust it strips from the shacks, they grow fat and sweet.

As the mound slides past me along the singing iron bar, pressing its vast, velvet shoulder against the transformer box with a low, metallic groan, I reach my other hand into the warm split of its mossy back. My blunt fingers sink through the crisp three-inch crust of frost, down into the steaming, sour-smelling peat of its hide. The tallow doesn't mind; my skin is coated in its own dried mucilage, and I hum in my throat—a low, gravelly *zzzzz* produced by rubbing two plates of cartilage together in my neck—that matches the sixty-hertz buzz of the box. To the mound, my hand is just a warm piece of the transformer.

I twist three fat, pale tubers loose from the rhizoids and tuck them into the bitumen-lined pouch under my chin. In exchange, before I pull my hand back, I take a pinch of red iron-oxide powder—scraped yesterday from the underside of the rail ties half a mile north—and rub it deep into the grey, leathery crease of the creature’s flank. The hydrostatic tubes beneath the rind immediately swell and pulse, drinking the rust in. 

I am the shepherd of the acoustic shadows. I am the thing that remembers where the iron is.

When the mound is safely anchored against the humming steel of the shack, I pull my pry-bar free. The silence in the four-foot trough snaps back into place, clean and dead. 

I do not go back to the rail line yet. Instead, I turn and look up the five-mile trench toward Intake Two. The pale slate strip of sky above the four-hundred-foot granite walls is darkening from ash-grey to charcoal; night is coming, though night down here only means the twilight goes blind and the freezing fog drops twenty feet lower. 

Half a mile away, the three ice-sheathed power cables sagging between the poles are vibrating in a slightly different arc than they were six hours ago. One of the porcelain insulators on Pole Fourteen has cracked in the cold, and the high-voltage line is leaking current down the galvanized steel mast into the peat. I can feel it from here through the soles of my boots: a sharp, jagged *tick-tick-tick* cutting across the smooth turbine roar, like a cracked tooth in a jaw.

By morning, twenty mounds will have smelled that leak. They will pull themselves out of the talus slopes and off the rail line, converging on Pole Fourteen in a slow, blind herd, piling up on one another until their combined weight drags the pole down and snaps the cable—and if the cable snaps, the fans in Intake Two will die, the standing waves will collapse, the silence will rush in like floodwater, and a mile of the valley will freeze into dead rubber before noon.

I tuck my chin down into my collar of stiff, greased wool. I shift the iron pry-bar to my other hand, and from the cache beneath the transformer pad I drag out a six-foot coil of heavy copper grounding wire, green with verdigris and stiff with rime. 

It will take me four hours to walk the half-mile through the frost-crisp *Sphagnum*, stepping along the crests of the corduroy acoustic ridges where the moss is packed hardest. When I get to Pole Fourteen, I will climb three feet up the vibrating mast, wrap the copper wire around the leaking insulator, and stake the other end twenty yards away in the middle of a boulder field where the tallow can graze on the stray voltage all winter without pulling the sky down on our heads.

I take my first step out of the silent trough. The hundred-decibel roar of the silos hits my chest like a warm, heavy hand, starting the slow blood moving in my gills again, and I walk out into the green twilight along the singing rail.
