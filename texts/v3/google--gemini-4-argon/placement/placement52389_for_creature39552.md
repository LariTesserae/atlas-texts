I would not be in the water, though the water is all I know. I would be six inches inside the dark, gastric mill of the abbot’s belly, and I would be the third largest of the twelve gizzard-stones.

Ten thousand years ago, before the water rose to choke the high corbels, I had edges. I was a piece of grey-green soapstone the size of a butcher’s fist, and for three hundred years after the bronze wedges split me from the mountain’s ribs, I was the left heel and ankle of a priest carved in high relief upon the third gallery's southern lintel. I bore the weight of a stone robe and four knuckles of a sandaled foot. When the warm water climbed the steps and closed over me, I grew a coat of ochre velvet. Snails rasped my tendon; crabs tucked their pale claws into the undercut behind my Achilles.

Then the stone settled. A microscopic shift in the deep foundations—the mountain sighing in its sleep—brought sixty tons of unmortared basalt leaning down upon my lintel. For four centuries the pressure built, silent and unyielding, until one afternoon (though there are no afternoons in the dark, only the faint, rhythmic widening and narrowing of the thermal plumes) a dry *crack* shot through the gallery, louder than anything that had happened for an age. I was sheared from the wall. I fell through the warm, thick water, turning lazily, brushing past the suspended eels and scattering a cloud of eyeless shrimp, before coming to rest in the deep, sulfurous silt of the lowest floor.

I lay there for forty years, slowly losing my chisel-marks to the warm acid of the vents, before the wide, flat shovel of the landlord’s snout nudged me out of the mud. 

Its jaw opened—a slow, silent downdraft—and I was drawn in. 

That was three thousand years ago. Since then, I have not known the stillness of the wall. I am part of the only engine that moves in this world, though its movement is so slow it would look like sleep to anything that breathes the thin air above the roof.

To be a gizzard-stone inside the abbot is to live in a dark within a dark, a warm, acidic sac lined with thick, leathery pleats of muscle that contract once every four or five minutes with the slow, inexorable squeeze of a hydraulic press. Here, there is no silt settling from above; there is only the heavy, viscous churn of what the creature has scraped from the mountain’s throat.

I am never alone. There are eleven others with me in the muscular bag. We know each other entirely by touch, by the long, slow, grinding slide of our wet surfaces as the stomach walls fold us over one another. 

The largest of us is a heavy, dense oblong of black basalt—not a carving at all, just a raw knuckle of foundation-chip that the creature swallowed five millennia ago when it was only nine feet long. It is our anvil. It rests at the bottom of the curve, moving least of all, while the rest of us—smaller, soapstone fragments of jar-handles, bull-horns, and cornices—roll across its back.

My own history is completely gone. The priest’s ankle bone was ground away in the first century of my swallowing; the strap of the sandal vanished a hundred years later. Now, I am a heavy, slightly flattened ovoid, polished by three thousand years of calcareous grit and stomach acid to a finish like greased slate. When the muscle closes around us, I am pushed up against the basalt anvil with a dull, muffled *clack* that travels through the creature’s ribs and out into the water, a soft, internal percussion that the blind shrimp resting on its throat feel as a tiny tremor in the wall.

Between us, we catch the day’s harvest: a fistful of calcified tube-worm casings, a lump of sulfur-crusted silt pried from a choking vent, a stray limpet that failed to let go of the rock in time.

The muscle tightens. I slide forward, my slick belly riding up over a cluster of brittle worm-tubes, pinning them against the round, smooth flank of what used to be a carved ram's horn. We do not smash; we mill. The pressure increases, steady and patient, until the calcium shells give way with a wet, gritty *crunch*. The stomach releases, the warm digestive fluids wash through the gap, and then the opposite wall contracts, rolling me back down into the trough to catch the fragments again, and again, and again, until the shells and the stone-chips are reduced to an impalpable, heavy flour.

I am getting smaller. 

Every century, I lose the thickness of a thumbnail. My substance—the ancient soapstone quarried by men who died before the plains below had names—is being slowly ground away and returned to the architecture. Every time the abbot climbs to the upper tiers and presses its vent against a cold, leaking seam in the masonry, a little bit of the priest’s heel is extruded in that dense, gray mortar, packed down by the flat of the creature’s tail to keep the mountain’s breath inside.

Sometimes, once in a decade, the great event occurs: the trapdoor above us opens wide, and a whole crab, heavy and dying, slides down the muscular tunnel of the gullet into our dark room.

Then, for three weeks, our work is heavy and loud. The twelve of us are rolled and levered against the thick, epibiont-crusted carapace. I feel the curved, alien geometry of the crab’s claw wedged beneath me; I feel the slow, hydraulic heave of the abbot’s belly-wall pressing me down until the chitin buckles and splits. For days we tumble among the legs and the swimmerets, polishing ourselves on the armor of the cistern's dead, turning shell into paste, while the creature lies motionless on a high bridge forty feet above the vents, digesting in the dark.

In another four thousand years, if the geothermal suture in the mountain’s heart does not finally go cold, I will be no larger than a plum pit. The heavy muscle of the stomach will no longer be able to get a purchase on me; I will slip between the larger stones without grinding, useless to the mill. 

One day, I will pass through the lower sphincter and be extruded with the gray clay, pressed into a drafty joint twenty feet below the cartwheel of freezing sky. And the abbot will descend to the floor, feel along the base of the walls with its broad, flat snout, and swallow another broken piece of the ruin to take my place.
