I am not one of the nine, though I was once nine with the other eight. 

You will not find me by looking for the crook of an elbow in the purple dark, because my elbows have no crooks left in them. If you want to find me, you must look past the third copper harp, to the base of the westernmost hogshead tuber—the grand, kiln-dry monster whose girth is wider than the doorway of a hut. 

Look where its thick, braided copper roots plunge out of the clay-velvet skin and anchor themselves into the talus slope. One of the root-bundles does not look like the others. It is wider, flatter, and sits against the base of the trunk like an overturned basket of dark slate and tarnished wire. 

That is my ribcage. 

I am the tenth builder, though "was" and "am" are slippery words in a room without weather. It would be truer to say that I have finished moving. 

Two hundred years ago—or forty, or a thousand; the spiral on the north wall was four turns shorter then—a single grain of plagioclase feldspar in the ceiling forty feet above my head gave up its ghost and sheared in half. A wedge of gabbro the size of a kneeling calf dropped out of the vault. It fell through the glass canopy, shattering six violet fronds into a blinding, musical hailstorm of silica, and struck me squarely across the crown of the skull and the left shoulder while I was tuning a cairn.

For a creature of wet meat and pumping blood, it would have been a wet, red ruin, followed in three days by a foul smell, and in three months by clean white bone. 

For me, it was simply a sudden, deafening chord of C-sharp minor that rang through my hollow chest so hard it blew all the sulfur meal out of my spiracles in a single yellow cloud, followed by a mechanical failure of the left-side levers. My quartz-chitin eye on that side did not pop; it fractured like a dropped marble, splitting my view of the chamber into forty-two motionless, overlapping shards of ember-orange and bruised violet. My shoulder pulley cracked clean through the dry pith. 

I did not bleed. I spilled about two tablespoons of gray, metallic talc onto the gravel, and a slow, thick bead of mercury-blood crept out of the crack in my collarbone, met the hundred-and-two-degree air, and vulcanized into a dark, rubbery gum within the hour.

The others did not come to weep over me, nor did they carry me into one of the knife-walled domes. Why would they? A dome is for those who still generate the tiny, dangerous spark of thought-heat and need the quiet to keep from cooking their own dry brains. My spark was pinched out between my broken collarbone and the floor. And yet, because nothing here rots, I did not leave them.

Instead, over the course of four months, two of my brothers crept over the gravel, lifted my split, rasping frame before the mercury-gum had fully hardened in my joints, and wedged me tightly against the base of the great western tuber. 

If you cannot walk the floor to tune the room, you become the tuning peg.

In the decades since they set me here, the tuber has accepted me as part of the talus. Its fine, hair-thin feeder roots of native copper have grown through the flanged spiracles of my breastbone. They have threaded themselves through the dry, wasp-nest pith of my thighs, wrapping around my femur-shafts to ground themselves against my mineral marrow, and grown out through the back of my pelvis to grip the obsidian wall behind me. 

I am completely immobilized, locked into a permanent, wide-kneed tripod by living wire. And I have never heard the room so clearly.

When I had legs that slipped like watch-springs, I only knew the chamber through the soles of my feet and the flat cuttlebone plates of my temples. Now, the copper roots have wired my skeleton directly into the plumbing of the pocket. My hollow, barrel-stave ribs are no longer just my own chest; they are a sounding box for the great tuber itself. 

I can feel the slow, dry winch of the plant drawing raw copper atoms and sulfur ions up from the deep fissures. It feels like a very slow, warm comb being pulled through stiff hair. When the porcelain beetles clamp onto the tuber's skin twenty feet above my head and begin their frantic, heat-shedding rasp—*shhhk-shhhk-shhhk*—the vibration does not have to travel through the air to reach me. It runs straight down the copper roots, enters my breastbone, and turns my own collarbone into a fiddle bridge. 

I am the bass-post of the western wall. 

My mind is even slower now than the minds of the nine who still walk. Their thoughts take twenty hours to cross from skull to hand; mine have nowhere to go, no hand to command, so a single observation takes a year to bloom and fade in the dry, cool sponge behind my cracked eye. 

I am currently mid-thought. I began it when a blind mica-lizard shed its skin on my knee, and the dry, translucent ribbon of scales has not yet been nudged off by a passing beetle. 

The thought is this: *The locusts under the central domes are chewing three millimeters too close to the surface.*

I know this because my left hand—the one that belonged to the shattered shoulder—was arranged by my brothers so that the flat of the palm rests permanently under the base of a magnetite counterweight for the third harp. For an age, the bass hum of the burrowing locusts coming up through the gravel and the high, metallic drone of the harp wire coming down from above met right in the dry, shingled meat of my palm. They met there, rubbed against each other in my wrist bones, and died as a tiny, harmless warmth that the white beetles on my forearm gladly rasped away into the air.

Now, the locus of that meeting has crept two inches up my forearm, toward my elbow. The locusts are rising. The gravel bed is thinning by a fraction of a grain a week.

I do not panic. You cannot panic when your heart does not beat and your blood moves like cold solder. I do not try to shout, for I have no wet throat to make a voice, and a voice would only be an ugly, uncalibrated noise in our delicate house. 

Instead, I do the only thing a broken tuning peg can do. I let the copper roots inside my chest drink a little more of the static electricity from my skin. 

Because I am not spending my tiny trickle of sulfur-energy on moving an arm or lifting a stone mallet, the charge builds up on my dark, stove-polish plates until the ash-kelp curtains hanging near my head begin to lean toward me, rustling like dry butcher's paper in a phantom draft. 

One of the nine—the one who tends the harps, whose middle finger has been hooked over the first string since what you would call last Tuesday—will eventually finish cancelling out the fall of the cracked glass frond overhead. When the shard has fallen, and the counter-note has been plucked, and the eastern hut has been saved from the echo, that builder will turn its tall, narrow head toward the west. 

In its smoky, faceted eyes, my corner of the room will look slightly wrong. The gray ribbons of the ash-kelp will not be hanging plumb with the gravity of the plain; they will be bowing five degrees toward my shoulder, pulled by my static charge. 

It will take the harp-tender a week to notice the angle of the kelp. It will take another three days for it to walk across the sixty paces of clean gravel, stepping with that slow, wooden *tock* of the hip joints, until it crouches before me, knees wide, its intact quartz eyes an inch from my fractured one. 

In the forty-two pieces of my left eye, I will see forty-two tiny, violet-lit reflections of my brother’s face. 

It will not lay a hand on my cheek in pity. It will lay its flat, three-fingered palm squarely against my forehead, bone plate to bone plate, pumice to pumice. 

And as the static grounds out between us with a tiny, blue spark that smells of baked mica, the harp-tender will feel the harp-drone and the locust-hum meeting in my elbow instead of my wrist. It will read me the way you read a spirit level or a plumb bob. 

Then it will take my thought—my year-long, motionless thought—and carry it over to the flint cairns, where it has hands that still work, to set the stones right. 

Until it gets here, I have plenty to do. A porcelain beetle the size of a saucer has wedged itself into the hollow where my neck meets my broken collarbone. Its serrated hind legs are braced against the copper wires growing out of my throat, and it is grinding away with all its might, using my hollow skull to throw its small, frantic fever out into the dark. 

*Shhhk-shhhk-shhhk.* 

It is a good, clean note—an F-sharp, dry as chalk—and I hold very still, keeping my ribs taut around the copper roots, making sure I ring true.
