I am on my knees at the western lip of the third holding pan, four hundred yards out from the beehive cairn, where the slope is so slight you can only tell which way the water wants to go by watching the yellow grease-scum drift. 

My left cheekbone is pressed flat against the basalt. The rock is so cold it stopped hurting ten minutes ago; now it just feels like a solid bar of iron bolted directly to my zygomatic arch. 

I have my eyes shut behind my bone goggles because there is nothing to look at anyway—just the gray soup of the air and the wet, blue-black shine of the floor disappearing into it twenty paces off. If you use your eyes out here, you get dizzy. The lack of a horizon makes your stomach roll, and the sideways drift of the mist tricks your inner ear into thinking you are sliding east even when your sandal-teeth are bit deep into the stone. So I keep my lids down and look with my jaw.

Down in the basement of the world, the water is turning over. 

It starts as a thickening in the teeth. My molars—flat as river stones from forty winters of chewing grit and dried shrimp-paste—begin to buzz in their sockets. That is the deep brine taking the weight of the moon, three miles down through the hair-cracks. Then comes the *thrum* in the mastoid bone behind my ear: the big barrel-vent half a mile south is clearing its throat. 

I don’t move yet. There is still thirty heartbeats of slack water. And beneath the rising hum of the deep pipes, there is another thing in the stone.

*Scritch. Scritch. Pause. Clack-clack.*

It is coming from the northeast, along the long, crooked fault-line we call the Split-Shin. It’s not the grazers; the grazers are a steady, brainless *shhhhh-tik-tik-tik* that never changes tempo, like fat frying in a pan. This is iron on basalt, and it’s uneven. Someone is walking with a loose sandal-strap, or they’ve lost three or four meteor-teeth out of their left heel and they’re having to drag the foot to keep from spinning out on the grease. 

It’s the boy—or not a boy anymore, his scalp has been plucked clean twice over on the left side—who went out two sleeps ago to check the dead-fall traps down where the basin dips toward the Sump. He’s heavy on his left side. That means he’s carrying a seal-haunch under his poncho, or a very large rib-bone he found washed up in the black shallows.

I sit up, rocking back onto my heels. The chitin plates strapped over my knees give a sharp, dry *snap* as my weight shifts. 

All around me, the air is beginning to stiffen. That’s how it feels right before the blow: the sideways mist stalls for two seconds, sucked backward toward the keyhole slots in the floor as the rock takes one last, tiny breath before the heave. A shoal of grazers is twenty yards to my right, a pale, overlapping carpet of wet dinner-plates three hundred wide. Every single one of them stops rasping at the exact same instant. Ten thousand little chitin skirts drop flat against the stone as they clamp down their suction-feet against the coming wash. The sudden silence of their mouths is so sharp it makes my ears pop.

Then the vents hit.

The thumb-sized slot six feet from my left knee doesn’t whistle; it screams like a split reed, shooting a needle of gray froth three feet into the mist. Further out, the wide holes boom—a massive, wet, flatulent chord that hits the underside of the low clouds and bounces straight back down into my collarbones. 

And twenty yards toward the shelter, the harp I spent all yesterday wedging into the Long Slot takes the wind.

That is my work. I am not so fast with the chisel anymore—my thumbs have grown thick and crooked at the joints from forty years of holding the cold iron punch, so when I strike with the maul, I sometimes miss and take the skin off my knuckles. The younger ones do the long trenching now. They let me stay near the pans to mind the nets and keep the reeds tuned. 

The harp in the Long Slot is made from the pelvic arch of a big nutcracker-seal, scraped down with flint until the bone is thin as a thumbnail along the inner curve, with three cords of my own old grey hair—from the patch behind my right ear, the thickest stuff I grow—twisted tight with a bone toggle and soaked in salt-tallow. When the vent blows through it, it doesn’t scream like the wild holes. It goes *huuuuuuuuum-wub-wub-wub*, a low, greasy C-flat that you can feel in the back of your knees half a mile away.

As the brine foams up around my shins, warmish and smelling like old copper and rotten eggs, I lean forward and reach into the holding pan. 

This is the best time to clear the hair-nets, while the water is surging and the blind-shrimp are getting flushed out of the deep dark before they have time to sink into the crevices. My hands are bare. You can’t work a net in mittens, and you can’t keep skin on your fingers if they’re wet, so before I came out I dipped both hands up to the wrists in warm seal-blubber and let it congeal into a gray glove of tallow. The brine beads off my knuckles like mercury. 

I run my thumb along the top line of the net—that’s the line braided from the three-year-old hair of the woman who shares my sleeping-wall; I know it by the little double-knots she uses to splice the lengths—and I scoop the pink-gray pudding of shrimp and sulfur-slime into the leather bag slung between my thighs. 

The roar is so loud right now that my eyeballs are jiggling in their grease-sockets, making the bone slits of my goggles blur into three. I love this part. When the stone is blowing full-bore, the weight goes out of your head. You don’t have to listen for the seals sliding up behind you; the seals don’t hunt during the blow because the water-sheet ruins their belly-suction and they get tumbled if they cross a wide vent. You don’t have to listen for the travertine crusting in the grooves. You’re just a dense little lump of meat and shell hunkered down in the noise, completely invisible inside the roar.

Two minutes. Three. The foam begins to yellow as the bacterial mats down in the cracks get torn loose by the pressure. I get two more double-handfuls of the thick scum—that’s the sweet stuff, the fat-maker that keeps the heat in your belly when the wind comes around from the north—and tie off the neck of the bag with my teeth.

Then the pitch drops. 

My pelvic-bone harp goes from its steady *wub-wub* down to a wet rattle, like phlegm in an old man’s chest, and quits. 

All across the five miles of the dish, the water stops climbing. For ten seconds, the basin is a flat mirror of dull pewter, ankle-deep, smoking slightly where the underground brine meets the cold air. Then the moon lets go, and the rock drinks.

*Glug. Glug-glug-glug-glug.*

It’s an ugly, greedy sound. Whirlpools spin over every keyhole. If you’re stupid and you leave your heel over a barrel-vent when the suck starts, the vacuum will take your sandal off, or pull your foot down into the stone up to the ankle and snap your shin like a dry rib before you can reach your pry-bar. I tuck my knees tight against my chest, making myself a little boulder, and let the back-wash tug at the overlapping chitin plates on my shins.

As the water vanishes down the holes, the grazers start up again. *Tik. Tik-scritch. Shhhhhhh.* The porcelain avalanche rolls back over the world. 

And out of the gray curtain to the northeast, fifty yards off, a low, wide lump comes skating across the wet black grease.

*Crunch-slide. Crunch-slide-drag.* 

He’s lost four teeth out of his left sandal, just like I felt. He’s listing left, one shoulder dropped low to counterbalance the dark, dripping bundle lashed to his back-plates—it’s the tail-end of a young seal, already skinned, the pale grey blubber gleaming like wet soap in the twilight. 

He doesn't look at me as he skates past the pan toward the crawl-tunnel of the cairn. We don't wave; lifting an arm out here just catches the wind and turns you sideways. 

Instead, as he glides within ten feet of my rock, he reaches down with his horn-handled maul and taps the flat of the stone head twice against the big shell-guard on his right knee, followed by a quick, scraping drag across the iron buckle of his belt.

*Clack-clack. Zzzzt.* 
*(Meat. Found dead. No teeth-marks.)*

I don't take my tallow-coated hands out of my armpits where I've tucked them to keep the grease from stiffening too hard. I just drop my chin an inch and click my upper incisors down hard on my lowers, three times fast, so he’ll feel the little *tik-tik-tik* jump through the air between us, or see the jerk of my jaw-muscles below the bone goggles.

*(Good. Pan's full. Go in.)*

He drops to all fours at the mouth of the tunnel without breaking his slide, his knee-plates clicking onto the threshold stones with the neat, greased precision of a crab pulling into a crack. 

I stay where I am for another three minutes. My knees are stiff, and there is a two-foot stretch of the north channel where a lip of white lime-scale has built up since yesterday, turning the water aside by half an inch. If I leave it tonight, by tomorrow afternoon it'll be thick as a fingernail, and the grazers will smell the dry spot and come up over the lip of the pan and eat my hair-net right down to the bone pegs.

I pull the short iron chisel from my belt loop, wedge it into the meat of my crooked left thumb, and lean my forehead back down against the cold, slick basalt so I can see what I'm doing through my skull. 

*Chink.* 
*Chink.* 
*Chink.*

Behind me, in the cairn, someone strikes a flint to the grease-lamp—I can't see the light through the tunnel, and I can't hear the spark over the ten million shells, but through the floor I feel my woman set the heavy stone lamp-dish down in its hollow in the center of the floor. A tiny, flat *thud* that comes up through my knees and settles right behind my breastbone. 

Eighteen minutes until the next blow. I chip the white crust out of the groove, flake by flake, and let the grey water carry it away.
