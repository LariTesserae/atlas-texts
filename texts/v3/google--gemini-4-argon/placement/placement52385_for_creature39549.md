I am not in the open water. The open water belongs to the History, and the History is too heavy to stand inside. 

If you drift into the central amphitheater, the pressure waves coming off the Elders’ siphons don't just move around you; they use your density to travel. Your own water—the fluid in your tissues, the cold lymph in your channels—is co-opted by a stanza about the tensile strength of basalt or the names of seven dry-land grasses that have not existed since the mountain swallowed its own sky. To be out there is to be vibrated until you have no thoughts of your own, only the passing through of theirs.

Instead, I am wedged three miles out from the center, at the northeast perimeter where the wide, sweeping curve of the Fourth Aqueduct meets a cluster of three thick anchor-piers. 

There is a pocket here. It is an acoustic shadow, roughly forty feet wide and eighty feet deep, tucked immediately behind the bulging, braided knee of the central pier. Because of the way the viaduct arches overhead and the specific, slightly flattened parabola of the floor below, the great four-thousand-year-old chords coming from the center hit the pier’s front face and split. They shear off to the left and right, rushing down the long colonnades like twin locomotives, leaving this small wedge of water behind the pillar almost entirely dead.

Here, the thirty-eight-degree dark is not roaring. It is merely humming, like a shell held to an ear.

I am small—perhaps fourteen feet from the crown of my mantle to the tattered, thin edges of my trailing aprons. If the Elders are tallow and calcified stone, I am closer to wet parchment or skimmings of grease. I am young enough that the row of boneless thumbs ringing my belly still twitches with phantom grasping reflexes when I sleep, trying to hold onto branches or hammer-handles that my kind bred out of the world two hundred generations ago.

I am here because of a knot in the wood.

Fifty years ago—or five hundred; without seasons, we only measure time by how many times the third heart-pump has pushed blood to our edges, and I do not bother to count my pulses—a stone-fish died inside a seam of the pier’s masonry instead of falling to the floor to be swept away. Its heavy, river-stone scales wedged into a gap in the grey mortar-lichen. Rather than dissolving, its minerals leached into the lignified moss, causing a localized, erratic thickening. A burl grew. 

It is only the size of a closed fist, a tiny hump on a pillar as wide as a watchtower, worn smooth by the grazing mollusks before it could ruin the current. The Elders haven't noticed it. It is too small to alter the three-hour delay of the Eastern Rim Stanza.

Except that it does. Just barely.

Every three hours and twelve minutes, a specific packet of sound comes screaming down the Fourth Aqueduct. It is an inventory—a long, dense, multi-tonal chord that holds the chemical formula for an ancient terrestrial mortar and the genealogy of the woman who first spliced the sulfur-drinking gene into the macro-kelp. It hits the front of my pier, splits, and races past my quiet pocket.

But the fist-sized burl catches the very bottom edge of the chord. It clips the lowest frequency by a fraction of a millimeter, bends it around the curve of the pillar, and throws a tiny, deformed scrap of sound backward into my shadow.

I hang here, forty feet above the smooth green-bone floor, my four aprons furled tightly around my flanks to keep from drifting out into the current, waiting for it.

Around me, the maintenance goes on. Ten feet away, clinging to the wet-chalk seam of the pillar, a translucent amphibian the size of a sheep sits motionless, its blood-dark gills waving in the slow water like ferns in a draft. Every few minutes, its throat sacks swell and it lets out a sharp, dry *clack-clack-clack*—a local ping to keep the grazing slugs aligned on the curve of the arch overhead. I can feel the slugs working up there; their rasping comes down through the dense wood of the pillar and enters my belly-thumbs as a dry, rhythmic itching. *Shhh-onk. Shhh-onk.* Canvas on wet timber.

Then, the pressure drops. 

Out beyond the pillar, a ribbon of stone-fish—thousands of them, packed so tight they move like a single articulated whip of river gravel—shoots past the mouth of my alcove. Their swim bladders are buzzing in a high, frantic F-sharp, agitated by what is coming behind them.

A heart-beat later, the Eastern Stanza arrives.

Even in the shadow, the water tightens. The grey lichen in the seams around me seems to flare, though it doesn't; it's just the acoustic pressure squeezing the optic nerves that still float, useless and unattached, deep inside the blubber of my head. The main body of the chord goes past the alcove like an avalanche of iron plates, shaking the pillar until the giant amphibian’s gills flatten against its neck.

And then, half a second after the roar passes, the burl does its work.

A tiny, sheared-off shard of the chord slips around the curve of the green bone and enters the dead water where I wait. 

Because it has been stripped of its upper harmonics by the knot in the wood, it is no longer a formula for mortar. It is no longer a genealogy. It is just a low, hollow, wobbling *thrum* that hits the concave dish of my mantle and dies there. 

It means nothing. It is acoustic garbage—a burr of static shaved off the side of a monument. 

I open my dorsal siphons. Not wide—if I took in a full hundred gallons and blasted a real note, the Elders in the center would feel the displacement in the water column within twenty seconds. They would think a viaduct had cracked. Two of them would peel off from the ring, drift down the Fourth Aqueduct like pale, sinking dirigibles, and sing this pillar back into submission until the burl was reabsorbed and my shadow was smoothed out of existence.

Instead, I draw in just enough thirty-eight-degree water to fill the first two chambers of my mantle. I let the stray, ruined echo from the burl rattle against my internal baffles. 

And then I push it back out, very softly, against the curve of the pier.

*Thrum.*

Because I am firing it from inside the acoustic shadow, the sound doesn't go out into the basin. It bounces off the underside of the ribbon-bridge fifty feet above me, hits the back of the pillar, and comes right back to my belly six tenths of a second later.

It is a tiny loop. A six-tenths-of-a-second delay line, hidden inside a three-hour archive.

When it comes back to me, I catch it in my siphons, tighten my third baffle by the width of a hair, and spit it back up at the bridge. 

*Thrum-mm.*

The giant amphibian on the seam stops clicking. Its blind, jelly-filled head swivels toward me. The frequency I am using is not in its repertoire. It does not tell the slugs to turn left; it does not tell the kelp-meristems to swell; it does not carry the name of an ancestor. The amphibian’s feathery gills twitch, confused by a vibration that has no work attached to it, and then it goes back to staring into the bruised-indigo dark, waiting for a signal it understands.

Above us, miles away, the ten-foot sky of ice groans—a long, grinding bass note as a high-altitude gale shifts the weight of the snowpack. Down here, in the thirty-eight-degree stillness, my third heart-pump squeezes once, pushing cold, thick fluid out to the tips of my aprons. 

I catch my tiny, six-tenths-of-a-second echo again. I shave another fraction of a hertz off the tail end of it, hollowing it out, making it rounder, and send it back up to the bridge. 

It is not a blueprint. It will never repair a crack, and if I stop doing it, nothing in the caldera will starve or fall down. It is just the sound of the water hitting my own baffles, shaped like the hollow behind a knee, repeated in the dark where the Elders cannot hear it, for no other reason than to feel the cold weight of it returning to my chest.
