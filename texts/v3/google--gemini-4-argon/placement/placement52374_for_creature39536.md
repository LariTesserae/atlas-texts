I am twelve feet up the eastern wall, wedged into an undercut where a vein of softer chalk has rotted out between two plates of selenite. 

To the sister down by the flint bowl, and to the snails that occasionally滑 over my ankles, I look like a shelf of rough travertine that has grown over an old fall of rock. This is because I have not moved my hips since the last time the scrub-plain above had rain, which was four seasons ago. My shell-cloak is not tied with fern-wire; the wire rotted two summers back, and the gastropod shells sitting over my shoulders have been cemented to my skin and to the wall behind me by the slow, milky weep of the stone itself. 

I am the fourth of the circuit, though the two younger ones do not remember that I have a face. They think I am the grandmother's cairn, or a salt-lick that happens to have knuckles. 

When you get old in the dry-country, your horn thickens until it stops shedding. The amber calluses on my heels and palms grew up my shins and forearms like bark, stiffening the double-joints, locking the pelvis into its folded knot so that even if I wanted to descend to the floor, I would have to break my own femurs like dry oak-sticks to unbend them. The waxy talc my skin used to make has turned to a hard, greasy mortar that catches the floating gypsum dust. Slowly, year by year, you turn into the wall. It is not a bad way to go. It saves water.

My ears are the only part of me that still moves, though only when the sister is asleep or gone. They have grown wider and thinner as the rest of me dried up, draping down over my collarbones like two sheets of shed snakeskin, shot through with sluggish, purple-black vessels. They don't radiate heat anymore; they catch it. I am cold inside, cold like the deep flint, and when the noon fever hits the dome and the light turns the chamber into the inside of a boiled egg, I spread my ears flat against the limestone to drink the warmth.

My eyes are gone behind the milky membranes—the slits healed shut five or six winters ago when the glare finally cooked the jelly behind them—so I know the room entirely by the *plock* of the spout and the harmonics of the ceiling. 

That ceiling is my work now. The young sister thinks she is listening to the quartz lens when she taps her bone wedge—*tink, tink*—and waits five minutes in the quiet. She has good ears, sharp and twitchy, but she only knows the sound of the stone as it is today. She does not remember the sound of the dome thirty years ago, when the wind above scoured three inches of gypsum off the northern curve in a single three-day gale, raising the pitch of the room from a low, chest-deep hum to a tight, singing *eeee* whenever a gust passes over the plain.

I am wedged directly across the chord of the vault from her trough. When she strikes the bone wedge with the river-cobble, the shock does not travel through the air to me; it runs around the belt of the gourd-shaped wall and enters my fused hip-bones, humming up my spine into the base of my skull. 

If the strike is clean, the stone says *thuck*, like a thumb thumping an unripe melon. I stay still.

Yesterday, she hit a dry pocket behind the selenite—a hidden blister where the rock had parted from the clay behind it. The vibration came through my hips not as a thump, but as a thin, glassy *skirrrr* that made the dust trickle down from the lip of the badgers' tube eighteen feet above my head. Before she could lift the cobble for a second strike, I lifted the horny heel of my right hand—the only finger-joint I still keep greased with mouth-spit—and rapped twice against the limestone between my knees.

*Clack-clack.*

Just the sound of two giant snails bumping shells. Nothing to startle a young thing, nothing to make her drop a tool and chip the floor. Down by the basin, I heard her horny feet shift a half-inch on the travertine. She paused for ten minutes instead of five, her ears swiveling toward the high eastern wall, and when she tapped again, she had moved her wedge four inches to the left, where the rock was wet and solid.

They do not feed me, not the way they feed the mother back in the dark-rock crevice. If they brought me glass-wort, I would have to piss, and there is no place for waste in the clean bowl of the room. Instead, I live on what comes down the wall. 

My ledge is a thumb's-breadth wide, and it interrupts three of the sweat-runnels that bleed down from the high curve during the dawn chill. The water does not pool on me—I am canted slightly forward so I do not rot—but it films across my collarbone and down the groove between my fused knees before dropping back to the stone. In that warm, salty dampness, a patch of tiny, translucent glass-wort has rooted in the mortar of dust and dead skin at the hollow of my throat. 

Because I am very still, the blind midges breed there. Because the midges breed there, two of the spindly, pale crabs spend their mornings standing on my shoulders, their needle-tips planted in my grey-ochre hide, their feathery mouthparts brushing the side of my jaw as they filter the air. 

And once every three or four days, one of the platter-sized snails will follow the wet seam down from the ceiling and cross my lap. It takes four hours to pass over my thighs. As it goes, its muscular foot rasps away the top layer of my chalky skin, cleaning me, and leaves behind a broad, glistening ribbon of mucus and endolithic algae right across my knuckles. When the snail has cleared my knee, I lower my head—my neck makes a sound like dry reeds rubbing together in a dry ditch—and my long, grey lips peel the ribbon from my own hide. It tastes of wet chalk, old sunlight, and the snail's slow blood. It is enough.

Right now, the light is thickening toward violet. I cannot see the violet, of course, but I feel the dome above cooling; the quartz lens contracts as the sun drops behind the scrub-horizon, making a series of tiny, musical ticks—*pash, pash, pash*—like salt falling on a drumhead. 

The sister is climbing. 

She does not come up my line—she uses the dry rib four yards to the north—but as she nears the cluster of seven guardian snails by the dissolution tube, she swings wide to check the eastern seep. She stops three feet from my face. 

I can smell the crushed cucumber scent of the glass-wort in her hair-bag, and the dry, nutty smell of her skin-talc. In the dimness under the overhang of the dome, I hear the tiny, wet *schwip* as her milky eye-membranes slide back, letting her wide, dark night-eyes drink in the gloom before she enters the pipe. 

She looks right at me. To her, I am a lump of limestone shaped vaguely like a folded sibling, sprouted with five triangles of ice-plant at the neck, with a single white crab poised on my brow-ridge. 

She reaches out with her pliers-hand. She does not pluck the glass-wort from my throat, though the triangles are fat and prime. Instead, with the thumb and little finger, she very gently pinches a dead, yellowed frond from the edge of the cushion so it will not sour the rest, and tucks the tiny scrap into her mouth. Then her horny knuckles brush my knee—a tiny, dry scrape, clearing away a crumb of loose gypsum that might otherwise fall during the night and confuse the crabs below—and she slips soundlessly into the corkscrew hole.

The chamber settles. The air cools another degree, and on my shoulder, the first bead of evening wall-sweat gathers, swells, and begins its slow slide down toward my collar. I let my ears droop half an inch, listening to the water-drop forming on the lip of the western spout forty feet away, counting the seconds until it falls.
