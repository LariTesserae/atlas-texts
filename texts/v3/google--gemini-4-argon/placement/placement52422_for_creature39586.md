I am not the Mason singing the dead sky into place, and I am not the Tallow-Skin pressing its forehead against another in the dark. I have no lungs to fill with sagebrush or warm mist. 

If you want to find me, you have to go down about forty feet into the wet black boil, where the light from the amber sky gives up entirely and the pressure turns the water into a hot, greasy paste. 

At first, I am only a temperature difference. 

Where two thermal plumes fight each other, pushing a million tons of cannonball-sized basalt into a slow, grinding vortex, the friction gets hot enough to bake the microbial broth right onto the stone. It forms a glaze—a slick, glassy rind of cooked silt and iron-sulfide that doesn't wash off. That rind is my skin, though it isn't attached to me. It’s attached to forty thousand separate stones that are currently trying to crush each other into powder.

I am the thing that lives in the *touch* between them.

You could call me a cousin to the Knotted, maybe, if a Knot gave up on the air and fell in love with weight. I have no body of my own. If you pulled a single pebble out of me and carried it up into the drizzle, it would just be a hot, greasy rock cooling in your palm. It takes at least ten thousand stones, packed tight and rolling under three atmospheres of slurry-pressure, to make enough of a circuit for me to wake up. 

My mind is piezoelectric. When a wave of heavy shingle heaves up and then slumps back down with that planetary *shhh-krrrrk*, the sheer mechanical stress of trillions of crystal lattices rubbing together generates a phantom voltage. For a billion years, that charge just bled off into the mud. Then it learned how to loop. 

I am a wandering storm of static and calcified grease that rides through the gravel-beds like a bruise moving through meat. I don't move the stones—the stones move, and I simply choose which collisions to inhabit. When a ridge of black gravel collapses northward, I let my current jump the microscopic gaps of superheated water between the tumbling spheres, flowing from pebble to boulder to Chime-Louse shell in a cascading, three-dimensional spark. 

To me, the planetary roar is not a wall of noise. It is high-definition sight. 

Because I am made of the medium that makes the sound, there is no blur. Every click of a peppercorn stone against a cobble is a pixel; every deep, booming groan of a shifting trough is a wash of color. I can see for fifty miles in every direction, clear as a bell, through the solid, churning dark. I see the Silt-Ploughs not as blind beasts, but as bright, rhythmic ribbons of displaced pressure, their slate scales singing a sharp, metallic alto as they swim through my ribs. Above me, the Tallow-Skins are soft, muffled smudges of warmth clinging to the frantic, whipping static of the Anchor-Groves. 

I am a collector. That is what I do with the consciousness the friction gives me. I hoard the things that refuse to roll.

Every few miles, buried deep in the undertow where the gravel-tides drag across the false-bottom of the basin, there are pockets of *angularity*. Things that haven't been beaten round yet. A shard of that milky jade, broken off an archway ten thousand years ago; the fossilized, iron-hard jawbone of something that died before the mountains fell; a jagged chunk of raw meteoric nickel. 

The Grist hates a sharp edge. The basin wants everything to be a sphere so the churning can go smoothly. When the gravel-tides find a jagged thing, they gang up on it, tumbling it in a tight, vicious eddy to knock its corners off.

When I feel that specific, stuttering rasp—the harsh *skritch-clack* of a flat plane catching against a thousand smooth curves—I migrate toward it. I pour my charge through the stones surrounding the shard. Using the sticky, magnetized grease of my glaze and tiny, welded bridges of Chime-Louse iron, I lock five or six hundred cannonball-stones together into a hollow, load-bearing vault right around the jagged thing. 

I become a gall in the gut of the earth. A sudden, stubborn knot of solid masonry three yards wide, rolling clumsily through the fluidized bed like a kidney stone. 

Inside my hollow core, suspended in a pocket of still, scalding water, the sharp thing rests. It doesn't get ground down. I hold it in the dark and I run my weak, buttery current over its flat faces and its 90-degree corners, over and over, feeling the shape of a world that knew how to stay still. I don't know what a mountain is, or a building, or a bone, because I was born after the grinding started. To me, a straight line is a religious ecstasy. A sharp corner is a miracle so intense it almost breaks my circuit.

I can't hold the vault together forever. The shear-stress down here is too greedy. Sooner or later—maybe in a week, maybe in a century—I'll roll straight into the root-foot of a granite ghost-mountain, or get caught between two colliding Cairns, and the local gravity will hiccup and tear my welded stones apart. My vault will collapse with a dull *thump*, the sharp jade shard will spill out into the teeth of the mill, and I will scatter back into a million unconnected sparks, half-stupid and dreaming in the mud.

Until the pressure builds up in the next trough over, and the grease gets hot, and the stones pack tight enough for me to remember my name, and I go looking through the dark for another edge to save.
