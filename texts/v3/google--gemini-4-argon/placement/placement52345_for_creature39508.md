I would not be in the open water. The middle of thatslate-green cube belongs to the nervous things—the silver ribbons of smelt, the turning shaft, the hunting seal with its warm blood and ticking heart, and the flat, gliding terror that rides the sixty-second gale. To be in the middle water is to be cast as a shadow on those fourteen dorsal domes.

If I am in Sump Basin Four, I am forty feet down, pressed into the cold corner where the southconcrete bulkhead meets the iron floor grating. 

At first, you would think I was just part of the drift—a low, crescent-shaped mound of pulverized brick dust, broken mussel shells, and rounded pebbles of black slag that the current has swept into the dead angle of the wall. Every time the sluices open and the room goes mad, the vortex drops a little more heavy grit into this corner. Everything that is too heavy to be lifted back up by the pneumatic roar ends up here: sheared bolt-heads, milky shards of thick glass from a maintenance bulb that burst fifty years ago, the green-corroded brass bases of those same bulbs, and the heavy, porcelain knuckles of broken electrical insulators. 

I am the thing that glues them together.

I have no eyes to see the mustard light, and no ears to hear the hydraulic groan, though I know the rhythm of the room as intimately as the Sump-Back does. I am a low, creeping tunic of pale, translucent gristle—something between a colonial ascidian and a cold-water sponge, though tougher than either, cross-linked with stolen silica and iron salts. I have no name, not even a hyphenated one. I am simply the *mortar*. 

My body is a slow, gelatinous matrix that spreads over the iron floor grates, anchoring itself deep into the square gaps where the Sump-Back’s pipe-wrench mouth cannot reach. As the heavy detritus of the chamber rolls into my corner during the sixty-second surge, I do not flee from it; I lean into it. When a shard of ribbed cage-glass or a rusted cotter pin settles against my flank, my pale tissue slowly swells up around its edges. Over weeks, over months, I secrete a dark, fibrous byssus-cement that binds the shard to my skin, and then binds the next piece of slag to that one.

I am building a reef out of the plant’s slow ruin.

Inside my rubbery, debris-crusted mantle, I am hollow—a labyrinth of smooth, dark, mucus-lined galleries and siphons. During the three minutes of calm, if you looked closely at the heap of rubble in the south corner, you would see it breathe. A dozen small, scalloped vents, looking exactly like the flared ends of corroded lead pipes poking out from the brick dust, open slowly. 

I do not pump water with much force; I don’t need to. The chamber does my breathing for me.

For the three minutes of quiet, I exhale. A slow, warmish plume of filtered water drifts out of my lead-pipe chimneys, carrying away my waste. During this calm, I am a sanctuary for the smallest things in the basin. Tiny, blind white amphipods and bristle-worms crawl over my porcelain-and-slag armor, grazing on the bacteria that grow in my creases. Even a juvenile spider crab, still smaller than a watch-face and too soft to survive the open grate, stays tucked beneath a jutting ledge of green brass I incorporated three winters ago. I let them stay. Their waste feeds the thin, symbiotic film of sulfur-loving bacteria that lives inside my outer rind, which in turn gives me my bitter, metallic taste—a taste of battery acid and old copper that makes even the starving conger eels recoil if they brush against me.

I feel the north piston in my masonry. *Thud. Thud. Thud.* It is the baseline of existence. It vibrates through the south wall, travels through the old bolts I have swallowed, and hums through my jelly. 

And I know the Sump-Back. 

Sometimes, during the three-minute sleep, the Sump-Back does not cling to the turning shaft. Sometimes, when its gizzard is full of crab shell and it wants the deep, vibration-rich quiet of the bottom to help grind its meal, it settles right onto the floor grates near the south wall. 

Once, its broad, graphite-black margin settled directly over my corner, draping across my rubble-mound like a heavy tarpaulin. 

It did not eat me. To its acoustic domes, I do not register as meat; I return the exact same hard, dead echo as the poured concrete wall and the iron grates. I felt the slow, muscular ripple of its skirt against my porcelain knuckles. I felt its pale, grey-pink suction grooves testing a shard of thick maintenance glass I had cemented into my back, finding it solid, and locking on. For three minutes, we were a single piece of architecture—the hunter resting on the archive. One of its lures, that uncanny, fleshy mockery of a three-inch brass screw-thread, lay twitching right beside one of my real, rusted iron bolts. My blind amphipods crawled right over the edge of its mantle and back again, neither of us acknowledging the other. 

Then came the stutter in the north wall. The piston sped up. 

This is my moment, too, though I do not fly.

As the Sump-Back peeled itself off my back and planed up into the roaring dark, and as the sluices groaned open and the sixty-second gale hit the chamber, I did not shut my vents like the blue mussels clattering on the columns above. I opened my intake siphons wide—wide enough to show the pale, muscular grids inside my throat—and let the violent, forty-eight-degree surge ram straight through me.

The hurricane that blinds the eels and pins the crabs is my banquet. The roaring current forces gallons of harbor water through my internal labyrinth every second, driving a thick blizzard of stirred-up silt, copepods, dead smelt scales, rust-snow, and microscopic harbor bloom through my sticky internal curtains. I do not have to work to feed; the ancient flywheels above do all the pumping. I simply sit in the corner, braced against the concrete, humming like a deeply pitched organ pipe as the tonnage screams through my tunnels, stripping the water of everything small and nutritious.

And then—*clank*.

The sluices slam. The water stiffens with the shockwave. Above me, a great, silver ceiling of air bubbles roars into the mustard-yellow light, though down here in the dark I only know it as a sudden, fizzing drop in pressure. 

The storm dies. The silt begins to fall again like black snow. 

Something heavy clicks against my flank, dropped by the dying eddy. It is a two-inch crescent of pitted cast iron—a flake of the central shaft’s graphitic rind, knocked loose by a panicked seal or the Sump-Back’s heavy launch. It settles into the hollow between a broken insulator and the concrete wall.

Slowly, in the returning three-minute quiet, as the piston behind the north wall resumes its steady, soothing *thud-thud-thud*, my pale, translucent edge creeps out a millimeter from the brick dust, touches the cold iron flake, and begins to make it part of the floor.
