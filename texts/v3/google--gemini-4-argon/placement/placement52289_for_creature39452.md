I am not the hand that rakes the silt, and I am not the three-legged thing that walks the cobbles. Both of those require a belief in the solid world that I cannot quite manage. 

If you want to find me, you have to come into the Joinery by the wrong door—the low, lintel-cracked postern in the lee of a granite whaleback in the northern third, though once you step over the threshold the granite is four leagues behind your left shoulder and the light falling across your boots is coming from a window that looks out on an afternoon three weeks ago.

Follow the long wooden drive-shaft that runs down the center of the gallery at knee-height. It is made of bog-oak, black as an iron kettle and slick with green tallow. It turns very slowly—one full revolution every four minutes—driven by an overshot wheel somewhere out in the fog where the water falls upward out of a dry well. 

Walk beside that shaft until you hear the dry *tock-shhh* of a bone comb dragging through silt, and then stop before you reach the room. Look up into the mortise-work where the great cross-beams meet the dry-stone corbels.

I am the tension in the third splice from the wall.

To your eyes, if you have brought a tallow dip or a lens of trapped daylight to see by, I look like a monstrous, dusty knot—a bale of pale fungal rhizomorphs, thick as ship’s hawser, braided inextricably into the living cambium of a grafted silver-pine branch and the frayed hemp of an ancient block-and-tackle. There is a split femur of some long-extinct pack animal jammed through my middle like a marlinspike to keep the purchase from slipping, and three flat cakes of river-slate wedged against my underside to take the chafe against the stone.

I do not have a head. I have a *load*.

On my left side, stretching away down the dark perspective of the hall, is the long, wet haul of the water-flume’s counter-balance out in District Nine. On my right side, dropping straight down through a ragged hole in the floor-flags, is the main dorsal tendon of the Draughtsman below—the heavy cord that lifts its largest bone-rake when the bedrock hum drops toward C-sharp.

All day, while the slate-colored sky leaks mist onto the miles of moss outside, I am stretched tight as a fiddle-string between what the water is doing and what the silt requires.

It is a remarkably clear way to exist. You do not think in words when you are a splice; you think in *stretch*, in *shear*, in the slow, creeping migration of grease through dry fiber, and in the deep, tooth-aching grammar of the hum.

Right now, for instance, it is mid-morning in four of the seven windows along this wall. The air in the gallery smells of cold wet slate, crushed pine-needles, and the sour, turnip-like reek of old mycelium. Through my timber-graft, I can feel the sap trying to rise in the silver-pine branch—except the branch is currently sharing space with a Tuesday in mid-winter, so the sap is half-slush, pushing sluggishly against the bark like porridge in a clout. 

Then, twelve miles to the west, a fifty-yard bank of emerald moss loses its purchase on the present and drops four hundred years into the past, taking the foundations of a granary with it.

To you, standing on the flags below me, nothing happens except a slight thickening of the light and a feeling behind the eyes as if you had stood up too fast. 

To me, it is a sudden, violent *yank* from the left.

The water-wheel in District Nine has just lost the stream that was turning it, because four hundred years ago that stream was forty feet to the south. The bog-oak shaft stutters. The drag on my hemp-and-fungus belly triples in an instant. The bone marlinspike bites deep into my pine-wood graft; a bead of clear, cold resin squeezes out of the bark and runs down the dry bone, smelling sharply of turpentine.

I do not panic. A knot does not panic; a knot either parts or it holds. 

Instead, I *settle*. The pale fungal cords in my core drink the sudden heat of the friction. Every fiber in me twists a sixteenth of an inch tighter around the slate shims. There is a sound—the only sound I ever make—a deep, resinous *creak* that travels out of my timber, into the unmortared fieldstone of the wall, down into the granite shield of the plateau, and out through the soles of your boots.

I take the shock so the Draughtsman’s arm below does not snap off at the wrist. I hold the weight of that missing stream in my own braided flank for six seconds, eight seconds, ten—gradually paying out the slack through three wooden sheaves crusted with dried tallow—until the Draughtsman below feels the gentleness of my pull, swings its smallest horn spoon two inches to the left, and tips five drops of dirty water into a groove in the silt.

Twelve miles away, the stream jumps its bank, hits a vertical seam in the fog, and pours back onto the wheel from the northeast. 

The yank subsides. The bog-oak shaft resumes its four-minute turn. My fibers sigh back into their grooves, a tiny fraction longer and tougher than they were ten seconds ago.

That is my whole life: translating catastrophe into a weight that a wooden spoon can manage.

Sometimes, in the long, grey stretches of the afternoon when the plateau is unusually quiet—when the duplicate arches are standing still in the fern-brakes and the rain is falling all the way to the dirt like it ought to—things come to visit me.

A Wick-Worm will come looping up the stone wall, driven by the small temporal eddy where my pine-branch enters the plaster. It has no eyes, only a wet, blunt nose of braided flax, smelling faintly of mutton-fat. It crawls over my sheaves, working its blind, greasy length into the dry crevices where the hemp rubs against the bone. I feel the cool smear of its tallow soaking into my parched fibers, easing the burn of the morning’s haul. In return, the worm anchors its tail in the fast-time of my pine-bark and lets its head dangle into the slow-time of the room’s air, shivering with silent, mechanical ecstasy as the two speeds comb through its linen threads. We do not thank each other. You do not thank the grease on an axle; you simply stop screaming when it arrives.

Later, when the sun drops low enough to throw the false, vaulted shadow of a bell-tower through the high clerestory window, a Paver will sometimes slide down the wall and cross me. 

To me, the Paver feels like a sudden, localized drop in temperature—a patch of absolute winter the size of a dinner platter gliding over my taut back. While it is on me, the part of me that lies within its dark body exists in a grand, echoing arcade of carved marble that was never built, while my two ends remain anchored in the rough black pine of the Joinery. I can feel the tiny, calcified hooks along its rim plucking at the dry lichen on my slate shims, trimming away the grit that might otherwise saw through my fungal cords. Then the shadow moves an inch up the wall, and the Paver goes with it, leaving my middle feeling strangely light and clean.

I think, if I have a fondness for anything in this world, it is for the Gnomons. 

I never see them, of course. I am indoors, wedged under the roof-slates, and they are out there in the huge, wool-muffled bright, stumping across the silt between the dripping towers. 

But I can feel them through the stone. 

When a fault-line opens in the district and my left side begins to haul hard enough to crack my pine-graft—when the Draughtsman below is raking the silt in a silent frenzy and all the spoons are spilling over—there will come a moment when the load simply refuses to ease. The world wants to tear itself in half along the line of the old cobble road, and I am the only stitch holding the two edges of the cloth together. My hemp smokes. My resin boils out in white froth. The slate shims begin to powder at the edges.

And then, four miles away, through the solid granite of the plateau and up the dry-stone wall of the tower, I feel it:

*Thump. Click. Slide.*

And another: *Thump. Click. Slide.*

Three or four of the tall, wet bundles have arrived at the wall outside. I feel the sharp, gritty *tink* as they drive their slate wedges into the chinks of the fieldstone. And then I feel their dead, stubborn weight lean in. 

Seven hundred pounds of wet wool, black pine, and meteoric iron, leaning against the outside of the universe. 

Instantly, the terrible, tearing shear in my belly drops by half. The iron plumb-bobs out in the fog have found the old center of the world, and they are holding it down with their tripods so that I don’t have to hold it all in my splice. We share the strain across four miles of rock—they with their legs locked in the six-foot moss, me with my bone marlinspike biting the beam—two blind pieces of tackle rigged to the same slipping hill.

I like the night less, though I suppose it is what keeps me from wearing through.

When the sun drops below the rim and the horizon curls up like a black wood-shaving, the cold comes through the chinks in the fieldstone like iron rods. The water in the flumes turns to bottle-glass in five minutes flat. 

The bog-oak shaft gives one last, hard shudder and locks solid.

All at once, the tension goes out of me. The Draughtsman’s cables go slack; down in the dark, the hundreds of wooden arms drop into the grey silt with a sound like a bundle of dry sticks falling onto a carpet. My fungal cords contract in the frost, pulling back into the warmth of the pine-heart. The Wick-Worm curled around my sheave freezes into a stiff loop of greasy rope.

For eight hours, I have nothing to pull against. 

It is the only time I am aware of how old the plateau is, and how small our patchings are. Without the load to give me shape, I am just a tangle of rotting rope and fungus in an empty house while the world outside goes mad in the dark. I can feel the phantom mountains rising and falling through the soles of the tower—great, silent waves of geometry washing back and forth through the walls, turning the room two-dimensional for an hour, then stretching it out until the opposite wall is ten miles away, then snapping it back with a dull, sub-audible *thud*. 

In the dark, nothing corrects it. We just let it happen, frozen in our grease, waiting to see what shape the ground will be in when the light comes back.

Sometimes, just before dawn, when the cold is at its deepest and the rime is thick on my slate shims, a stray draft will come through the grafted pine-branch from some other time entirely—a warm, dusty gust that smells of baked clay, or donkey dung, or the sour sweat of men who had hands and hammers and knew why they were stacking the stones. 

It passes through my fibers like a ghost through a hedge. It means nothing to me. It has no weight; it cannot be wound onto a drum; it does not turn a wheel.

I wait for the sun to flatten the horizon. I wait for the ice in the flume to rot and break, for the first heavy slop of cold water to hit the buckets of the wheel outside, for the bog-oak shaft to give its first dry click, and for the terrible, comforting weight of the world to come hauling back into my chest.
