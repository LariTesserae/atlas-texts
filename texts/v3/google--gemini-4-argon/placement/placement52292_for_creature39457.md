I am not on the surface where the bricks bake in the brass light, nor am I laying the fresh courses under the stars. If you want to find me, you have to go down through the dry-canal grates, past the first three levels of cooling flues where the air still remembers the sun, and drop into the fifth or sixth under-course. 

Down here, the world is four feet high and smells of old water, chalk, and the slow, patient pressure of millions of tons of hand-laid clay.

I am one of them, though my skin has lost the dark, wet-umber sheen of the surface crews. I am old enough that the alkaline silt has settled permanently into the deep folds around my neck and shoulders, turning my hide the pale, crusty gray of dry mortar. My eyes, never large, have receded further under my brow ridges; the milky second eyelids stay half-drawn even in the pitch dark, because I no longer need to see the horizon to know where the world is going. My body has thickened even further toward the ground, my knees and elbows calloused into flat, horn-like pads from decades of moving through the low corbeled vaults.

I am a listener in the under-courses. I tend the plumbing and the bones of the basin.

While the surface crews spread the world outward, mile by flat mile, someone must keep the lower strata from choking on its own weight. The clay is not dead once it is laid. It settles. It breathes water in and out. Over decades, the immense downward drag of the terraces squeezes the lower vaults, bowing the dry-laid arches by fractions of an inch, while the warm water seeping down from the ankle-deep pools above carries fine suspended slip that threatens to silt up the cooling arteries.

So I walk the dark. My gait is a low, rolling waddle, my broad back brushing the ceiling bricks just lightly enough to read their alignment with my skin. My wide, flat feet slap gently against the slick floor tiles, sending a small, damp echo ahead of me through the tunnel. 

Every few hundred yards, I stop, plant my heels wide, and press both palms flat against the load-bearing piers. 

I close my eyes and let my weight sink into my wrists. Through the horn of my nails and the dense bone of my forearms, I read the basin. 

It is never silent down here. The masonry is a vast, tight drumhead. I can feel the afternoon rainstorm three miles to the east—not as a sound, but as a heavy, cooling *sag* in the upper courses as ten million bricks drink the water and swell against their mortar joints. I can feel the rhythmic *slap-drop-thud* of a night crew working a mile to the north, a faint, steady pulse like a slow heart beating through the clay. Closer by, I feel the long, heavy *shhhhh* of a sledge being dragged over wet slip, transferring its load down through four layers of vaulting straight into the soles of my feet.

When I feel a flaw—a hollow ring where a subterranean seepage channel has washed out the gray mortar beneath a pier, or the tight, silent pinch of a gallery beginning to buckle under a newly poured curing pan above—I do not fetch a crew. The space is too tight down here for eight broad bodies. 

I simply kneel in the dark water. I reach into the silt trap at the base of the wall, where the rich, velvety sediment collects in the slow eddies. With my square-tipped fingers, I scoop up the cold, buttery mud. I do not need light to find the crack; my thumb knows the width of a proper joint the way my tongue knows the space between my teeth. I pack the silt deep into the seam, driving it home with the heel of my palm, smoothing it with a single, heavy drag of my wrist. If an arch is sagging, I lift one of the spare, kiln-fired brace tiles stacked in the dry niches—left there by someone like me a hundred years ago—and wedge it into the corbel, striking it once with the meat of my fist until the vibration rings true and tight against my ribs.

*Thud.*

Up above, perhaps, a young builder pausing by a reed basket feels that single knock come up through the soles of their feet. They will not wonder what it is. They will only know the floor is sound, and go back to their sledge.

My life is measured in the temperature of the seepage and the taste of the dark. When I am hungry, I do not climb to the surface for the waxy sedge. Instead, I follow the air shafts toward the grates until I find the long, pale, thread-like roots of the horsetail reeds pushing down through the mortar seams thirty feet below the light. I pull them free in handfuls—they taste of cold mud and sulfur—and grind them between my flat molars as I walk. When I sleep, I do not roll in the sun-warmed slurry of the pans; I wedge myself into a low, dry flue where two warm-water conduits cross, letting the gentle, radiating heat of the day's trapped sun bake through the brick against my belly while the damp draft cools my spine.

Sometimes, once every few weeks, a grate directly above me is lifted at dusk. 

A square of brassy, blinding sky falls into the gallery. I stop in the shadows ten yards back, my gray, silt-crusted bulk indistinguishable from the corbeled wall. I watch the thick, dark-umber legs of a surface crew climb up toward the stars. I smell the sharp, toasted-cinnamon scent of the dry surface bricks and the vast, electrical ozone of an evaporating rain sheet miles away. 

The juvenile of the crew—the last one up—pauses with one flat foot on the rung, looking back down into the black hole. It cannot see me, not with its eyes still full of the sunset glare, and it makes no sound. Instead, it shifts its weight, tapping its heel once, lightly, against the lip of the shaft.

*Are you down there?* the brick asks. *Does it hold?*

I lift my heavy right foot and bring it down flat in the ankle-deep water of the fifth course. 

*Thump.*

*It holds,* the floor answers. *Go on.*

The wooden grate drops back into its notches, shutting out the sky. I turn my broad shoulders in the narrow dark, lower my head between my collarbones, and walk four miles west, toward the faint, trembling vibration of a weir being lifted, to make sure the water goes where it is told.
