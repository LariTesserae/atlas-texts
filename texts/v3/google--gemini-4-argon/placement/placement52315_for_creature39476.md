To find me, you have to climb down out of the white roar. 

You have to leave the blinding, bone-bleached crown where the Line-Tappers ride the snapping tendons, and go past the third and fourth tiers where the wicker spindles of the clans swing in the dusty pearl light. You must hook your brass crampon-claws into the vertical maintenance grooves of a primary compression-trunk and drop for two miles, down into the seventh tier, where the wind no longer screams like a turbine, but booms like surf trapped in a sea-cave.

Here, the light is the color of a fresh bruise—a deep, dusty indigo filtered through six layers of crisscrossing ironwood and drifting clouds of spent, golden spores. The wood down here does not whip or buck. These boughs are fifty yards thick, petrified by age and immense, downward load into columns as hard as flint. They do not flex; they hum. A relentless, sub-audible bass note vibrates through the soles of your boots, so deep it feels less like a sound and more like a hollow ache in the center of your sternum.

I am sitting in a carved concavity on the underside of a great junction-knuckle, strapped into a gimbal-chair of polished horn and blackened brass. 

They call me a *Plumb-Weaver*, though if you clacked your finger-tallies at the young Grease-Wrights up in the sun-glare, their faces would flicker with superstitious unease before they signed the name. To them, I am half-dead already because my knees no longer roll with the sixty-second recoil. My joints have grown stiff and thick, calcified by forty years of hanging in the deep-tier harness. If you brought me up to the crown, the first snap of a tension-bough would shatter my shins like dry twigs.

I do not wear the slit-goggles of the upper tiers. Down here in the violet gloom, my eyes are uncovered, and the pale amber of my irises is permanently blown wide around huge, ink-black pupils, drinking in the faint bioluminescence of the blind shelf-fungi that crust the undersides of the trunks. My snout-mask is heavy, double-chambered, and stained dark with the greasy soot of the lower thermals. I breathe slowly. Eight breaths to the minute, timed to the deep, sluggish reset of the compression-pillars.

Before me, dropping straight down through a circular gap in the wood, is the five-mile dark. And running down into that dark is my work: the Great Skein.

The clans above think the world is only wood and wind. They think the Grease-Wrights keep the forest alive by feeding tallow to the cams, and that when the great Seizures come, it is merely a matter of too much gale from the north. They do not understand that the trees are not just grinding the sky; they are drinking the dark.

The Great Skein is a braided cable of ironwood-tendon and spun brass, thick as a man’s waist at the gimbal-wheel where I sit, tapering as it falls for twenty-five thousand feet into the sunless abyss. It does not touch the basalt floor—nothing reaches that far—but it hangs three miles down in the *Rot-Calm*, the hot, stagnant soup of crushed slough, fermented spore-fall, and dead air that settles above the stone crust.

I am the spider at the top of that thread. My left hand—the flesh long ago replaced from the wrist down by a three-pronged tuning fork of resonant brass—is locked into a receiving socket on the Skein’s main tension-drum. 

I do not see the world; I feel it through the metal in my arm. 

Up above, the clans tap the beams with mallets to talk across five miles of lattice. Down here, I listen to the vertical groan of the continent. Every subtle shift in the deep columns, every pocket of methane boiling up from the three-mile fungal beds, every thermal updraft that will, in six hours, reach the crown and cause a hundred thousand sails to tack at once—it all registers first as a microscopic tremor in the Skein, singing up through my brass wrist into the bones of my shoulder and jaw.

When a tremor comes that does not belong to the natural breathing of the deep wood, I reach out with my right hand—my flesh hand, with its long, yellowed nails and double-thimbled fingers—and pull the heavy, bone-handled levers of the signal-organ beside my chair. 

The organ does not play air. It drops five-hundred-pound hammers of dense burl-stone onto the acoustic core of the pillar. *Boom. Boom-boom. Pause. Boom.* I send the warning shivering upward through two miles of solid timber to the soles of the Tappers’ feet: *Wind-shift from the deep south. Barometric drop. Slack your brakes. Reel in the spindles.*

In return, they lower baskets to me on thin lines of greased gut: cakes of compressed xylem-paste, flasks of bitter, sun-distilled water, and great, waxy bricks of tallow for my winches. I never see the boy who lowers the basket. He stops two hundred feet above me in the fifth-tier gloom, terrified of the abyssal draft that smells of sulfur, old rot, and ancient, unmoving stone. He jerks the line twice, and I jerk it once to say I have taken the weight. That is the whole of my conversation with the living.

Wait. Not the whole of it.

Because the other half of my work is the harvest of the deep, and the tending of the kites.

The Skein is not just a listening wire; it is a snare line. Along its three-mile length, every five hundred feet, great conical traps of woven wicker and sticky resin-mesh are flared open like inverted umbrellas, facing down into the rising thermals. They catch the *up-drift*: the strange, pale, buoyant things that live in the sunless miles below and ride the warm vents toward the light. Blind, bladder-bellied gas-jellies whose oil is the finest lubricant in the world; sheets of grey, leathery spore-felt that the clans use to line their winter spindles; and the heavy, greasy wax-beetles that feed on the falling dead.

Once every three days, I engage the main ratchet. I do not crank it myself—no ten men could lift the weight of the three-mile line. Instead, I throw a brass clutch that couples my winch to a secondary, slow-moving return-tendon buried in the pillar beside me. With a groan that shakes a barrel of grey dust from the timber overhead, the tree itself begins to haul my line up from the deep.

It takes six hours for the traps to rise. I sit in my gimbal-chair, my brass fork-hand humming against the drum, watching the indigo dark beneath my feet.

And it is during the haul that I meet the dead again.

When the clans cast their dead from the Lowest Boughs a half-mile above me, lashed to their triangular frames of pale horn, they think they are sending them straight to the basalt floor. They do not know how thick the air gets two miles down, or how the thermal chimneys twist between the ancient pillars like invisible rivers.

Many of the kites never fall to the bottom. They get caught in the slow, circular eddies of the eighth and ninth tiers, wheeling endlessly in the dark. 

Sometimes, as the Great Skein rises, a wing-tip of pale horn will snag on the braided cable. I will feel it instantly through my brass wrist—a sudden, fluttering drag on the line, lighter than a gas-jelly, drier than a spore-mat. A rhythmic, erratic *thrum-thrum* as the kite spins in the updraft against the wire.

When the snagged frame finally breaks out of the violet mist below my platform, rising slowly toward the hole in the floor, I disengage the clutch. The tree’s tendon slides free with a greasy *shhhk*, and the winch locks.

I swing my stiff legs out of the chair, hooking my tether to an overhead rail, and shuffle out onto the lip of the drop. 

They are always changed by the deep. After a few months of drifting in the lower miles, the dry air has drunk every drop of moisture from them, shrinking their parchment skin tight against their cheekbones and ribs until they look like carvings of dark, oiled burl-wood. And the deep-spores have found them. Their lashings and the corners of their eye-sockets are crusted with delicate, fan-shaped frills of pale blue fungus that glow with a cold, steady light in the dark.

I do not harvest them. Even down here, there are laws. 

Instead, I lean out over the five-mile emptiness, hanging in my harness, and hook the kite’s frame with a long brass gaff. I pull them close—these old Line-Tappers with their shattered ankles, these children whose filters failed in the white dust, these Grease-Wrights who missed their jump when the cam broke free. 

In the dim blue light of the fungus growing on their own chests, I read their faces. Even dried to leather, a Kinet’s face holds its last expression, frozen in the silent scream of the fall or the tight-lipped peace of the white-lung. I look at the notches carved into the horn of their kite-frames—the clan-marks and lineage tallies.

Before I let them go, I do something the clans above have forgotten how to do. 

Down here, the air is thick, warm, and slow. It does not tear the moisture from your throat in a heartbeat. Over forty years of solitude, breathing the heavy, damp thermal vents from the deep rot, something vestigial and ancient has loosened in my chest.

I unclip the heavy filter-snout from my face. I lean down close to the dry, glowing ear of the corpse. And I use my voice.

It is not a language—we lost words ten thousand generations ago when the forest first rose from the stone. It is just a low, rasping, vibrating hum from the back of my wet throat, pitched to match the deep *C-sharp* of the compression-pillar beside us. A tiny, living engine of air and flesh, making a sound that travels through nothing but the three inches of indigo dark between my mouth and their dead bone.

Then I take my knife, trim away the heavy, dragging sheets of grey fungus that have unbalanced their wings, and tighten their tendon-lashings where the dry air has shrunk their wrists. 

With my right hand, I snap my bone thimbles twice—*clack-clack*—to tell the dark that the inspection is done. With my left, I push the pale triangle back out into the thermal chimney, watching the blue-lit figure catch the draft, banking slowly away into the forest of pillars until the gloom swallows the glow.

Then I strap my snout-mask back on, haul myself back into the gimbal-chair, and lock my brass wrist back into the trembling wire, listening to the miles of wood and wind above me, and the miles of quiet rot below.
