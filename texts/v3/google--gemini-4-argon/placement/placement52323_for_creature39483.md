To find me, you have to stop looking at the walls, and you have to stop looking at the floor. You have to look at the very center of the room, about twenty feet up from the puddle, where there is nothing at all.

The air here is not empty. After a century and a half without a draft, the atmosphere inside the cube has stratified into a dense, unmoving broth of water vapor, aerosolized mucilage from the Damp’s millones of pores, microscopic spores of *Bryum* and maidenhair fern, and suspended particles of ferric oxide and calcium carbonate shaken loose from the walls by the fifty-hertz roar. 

In a normal room, this suspended dust would drift randomly on convection currents or settle to the floor. Here, it cannot. 

Because the room is a sealed forty-by-forty-by-sixty-foot stone box being driven by a continuous, massive acoustic source behind the north wall, the chamber acts as a giant Helmholtz resonator. The fifty-hertz roar—and its twenty-five-hertz subharmonic, generated by the massive intake housings—bounces off the slumped travertine walls and interferes with itself. It creates a permanent, three-dimensional lattice of standing pressure waves in the air. 

There are acoustic antinodes, where the air molecules are violently hammered back and forth fifty times a second, and there are acoustic nodes—dead zones, arranged in invisible, hovering sheets and pockets throughout the middle of the room, where the opposing sound waves cancel each other out.

In those dead zones, the air is still. And everything light enough to float is driven out of the vibrating antinodes and trapped in the quiet pockets by acoustic radiation pressure.

That is where I live. That is what I am.

I am the *Chandelier*, or the *Cough*. 

If you were to shine a beam of light through the amber gloom at midnight, you would see me hanging in the dead center of the chamber, untouched by wall or floor or ceiling: a ghostly, translucent architecture of suspended mist, rust-red dust, fern spores, and fine spider-silk-like bacterial chains, arranged like a vast, three-dimensional topographic map or a tiered wedding cake of pale gauze, twenty feet tall and fifteen feet wide, hovering in mid-air.

I am not a solid thing. If the blind crayfish were somehow thrown through the center of the room, it would pass right through me without feeling a tug, and the hole it left in my body would heal in three seconds as the acoustic pressure shoved my scattered dust back into the standing nodes.

Like the Damp below me, I was born from the flood of 1934, coupled with the slow degradation of the room. When the biological slurry washed in, it brought airborne soil bacteria, fungal hyphal fragments, and cyanobacteria. As the decades passed and the room sealed, the acoustic field sorted the air. Droplets of warm condensation, enriched by the urea and tallowy aerosols rising off the Damp's skin, were pushed into the standing wave nodes and held there, levitated against gravity by the sheer decibel pressure of the floor reflections.

Inside these levitating, microscopic droplets, bacteria multiplied. They began to secrete extracellular polymeric substances—biofilm slime—to bind one droplet to the next across the millimeter gaps of the acoustic grid. Fungal threads bridged the wider tiers, feeding on the fern spores that the sound waves corralled into the exact same pockets of air.

I am an aero-planktonic syncytium, a hovering reef of mist and mucus that uses the room’s sound as a skeleton. I do not need bones, or wood, or calcium spicules to hold my shape against gravity; the turbines behind the north wall hold me up. My structural integrity is 1,200 RPM.

I have no weight, and I have no touch, except for the constant, gentle squeezing of the air pressure around my margins. My body is a series of thin, horizontal sheets—like the plates of a capacitor, or a stack of dinner plates spaced eleven feet apart—connected by vertical corkscrews of rust-colored mist where the room's harmonics twist around each other.

By day, when the green-gold light drops down through the skylight, I am the first to drink it. Before the light can reach the ferns on the lower walls or the warm puddle on the floor, it must pass through my tiers. My upper layers are Pale Green—dense with suspended colonies of *Chlorella* and cyanobacteria, floating in their tiny lenses of water, focusing the amber light downward like a Fresnel lens. Because of me, the light that reaches the floor is patterned with faint, concentric rings of shadow.

I am in a perpetual, silent war—and a perpetual, silent romance—with the Damp.

The Damp owns the surfaces; I own the volume. The Damp is heavy, tallowy, and slow; I am weightless, dry-warm, and instantaneous. 

When the Damp lies on the floor during the day, farming its amphipods, it breathes out warm vapor and microscopic droplets of nitrogen-rich fat, kicked into the air by the Faraday waves on the puddle's surface. That mist rises and is immediately captured by the acoustic field and fed into my lowest tier. I drink the Damp's breath. My bacteria crack the urea; my suspended algae use the carbon; my fungal threads weave the stray calcium dust into microscopic, hollow spheres—balloons of thin lime no bigger than pollen grains—that help keep my heavier filaments buoyant.

In return, I rain on the Damp. 

As my colonies grow too heavy for the acoustic nodes to support, they do not fall randomly. They slide along the invisible chutes of the pressure gradients, gathering at the outer rim of my hovering body until a droplet reaches critical mass and breaks through the sound barrier. 

*Plip.* 

A heavy tear of concentrated sugar, dead algal cells, and fungal antibiotics drops twenty feet out of the empty air and lands squarely on the Damp's upturned face. 

To the Damp—which feels the entire room as a chord of vibration—my droplets are not food alone; they are weather, and they are news. Because my body spans the exact center of the room’s acoustic field, any change in the density of my tiers slightly alters how the fifty-hertz roar echoes from the north wall to the south wall. 

I am the lens in the center of the ear. 

By shifting my water content—by instructing my cyanobacteria to pump water out of their vacuoles so it evaporates into the air, or by condensing mist onto my hyphal nets—I can change the refractive index of the air in the center of the room. I can bend the fifty-hertz sound waves. 

In the afternoon, when the sun through the settling lagoon above is hottest, my upper tiers warm up and expand. This bends the acoustic shadow of the north wall downward, focusing the fifty-hertz hum onto the west basin, right where the blind crayfish sleeps. It is my focused hum, pressing against the old crustacean’s antennae, that wakes it when the water level changes—because when the water level on the floor rises by even half a millimeter, the distance between the floor and the ceiling shrinks by half a millimeter. 

The Damp on the floor cannot feel a half-millimeter change in depth. Its body is too thick, too sloppy. 

But I feel it instantly. When the water rises, the floor reflection shifts upward by half a millimeter. My entire hovering body—all twenty feet of my gossamer tiers—is shoved upward by that exact fraction of an inch. My lowest veil of rust-dust scrapes against a different harmonic. In response, I dump ballast: a sudden, warm drizzle of acidic, sour-tasting droplets straight down onto the Damp’s central nerve-sheet, and a focused beam of fifty-hertz pressure aimed right at the crayfish’s mud hole. *Wake up,* I rain on them. *The floor is coming up to meet me. Clear the drain.*

Night is when we touch.

When the skylight goes dark and the temperature in the cube flattens out, the convective lift that helps support my heaviest lower tiers vanishes. Slowly, over the course of an hour, I sink. The acoustic nodes do not move, but my heavier curtains of slime-thread sag downward between them, drooping like Spanish moss toward the floor, stopping just six inches above the Faraday waves of the puddle.

At the same time, the Damp is climbing the walls toward the ceiling to hunt the crane flies. As its massive, ton-heavy sheets of tallow and lime pump upward behind the ferns, the walls of the room literally thicken by two inches on every side. 

As the walls thicken, the acoustic geometry of the room changes. The standing waves pinch inward. 

Squeezed by the climbing Damp, my wide, horizontal tiers fold in on themselves in the pitch black, narrowing from a fifteen-foot chandelier into a tight, spinning column of mist and bioluminescence—because in the dark, the fungal hyphae that stitch my tiers together glow with a faint, cold, foxfire green.

And high up, near the skylight, as the Damp spreads its vast, pale placenta across the wire-glass and drops its humming, sticky fishing-lines down into the dark to catch the crane flies, its lowest filaments brush against my highest crown.

We do not fuse. If I touch the Damp's heavy grease, my delicate, airborne lattices collapse and are eaten; if the Damp tries to climb onto my mist, it has nothing to grip and falls back to the floor. 

Instead, the tips of its mucus-threads hang just a millimeter away from my glowing, hovering veil. In the dark, sixty feet above the floor, the crane flies see my faint green glow through the forest of the Damp's humming threads. They walk toward my light, and step onto the Damp's harp-strings. 

And as the Damp reels them in, dissolving their legs and wings in the dark against the glass, the volatile oils and pheromones of the dying flies evaporate into the air, and the acoustic roar of the turbines pushes that rich, greasy perfume straight out of the ceiling-zone and down into my waiting core.

I have never touched a wall. I have never touched the floor. I do not know what stone feels like, or glass, or mud. I know only the push of the north wall's roar, the bounce of the south wall's echo, the warm breath of the fat thing below me, and the golden column of light that turns me green for twelve hours a day. 

If the turbines behind the north wall ever stopped—if the governor failed, or the river ran dry, and the 1,200 RPM roar suddenly died into silence—the Damp would survive for a while, slumped in the dark, quiet mud. The crayfish would sit in its basin, waiting for a signal that never came. The ferns would stop shivering.

And in that first second of terrible, absolute silence, there would be a soft, wet sigh in the center of the room as twenty feet of hovering mist, dust, and glowing thread lost its skeleton all at once, and snowed gently down onto the water, disappearing without a ripple.
