If you want to find me, do not look up at the violet sky, and do not look down into the wool-white gulf. Look for the places where the vibration goes sour.

Three tiers down from the sun-bleached crowns, on the underside of the central gantry-spine, there is a junction box the size of a hay-wain. Four hundred years ago, it was a secondary relay station for the eastern turbine bank. Now, its bolted iron doors have rusted off their hinges and fallen into the deep, leaving an open, dark-mouthed cavern packed with slate switchboards, dead copper bus-bars as thick as a man’s thigh, and forty-pound porcelain insulators glazed in a crackled, weeping brown. 

That is where my body begins, though it is hard to say where it ends.

If you shine a torch into the junction box, you will think at first that someone has spilled a barrel of golden tallow over the slate panels, and that the tallow has begun to breathe. I am a low, flat, translucent mantle of rubberyamber flesh, no thicker than a saddle-blanket, draped across the dead porcelain and wedged into the dry, warm crevices behind the slate. I have no eyes, no bones, and no horn-calipers. If you peeled me off the iron—though you would need a crowbar to do it, and I would bleed a clear, turpentine-smelling lymph over your boots—I would weigh perhaps sixty pounds. 

To the dirigible crews, I have no name at all, because I never come out into the ultraviolet glare where their spyglasses can catch me. If the Weavers had a word for me in their wrist-hammer telegraphy, it would simply be the rhythm for *grease-that-walks*, or perhaps *the-quiet-in-the-joint*.

I am what happened to the insulating compounds, the biological gasket-pastes, and the vat-grown lubricating molds that the builders packed into the sealed gear-casings before they left. Over four centuries of dynamo-hum and copper-sweat, the paste woke up. 

The core of me sits in that porcelain-lined cave because it is fifty-two degrees Celsius there, day and night, heated by a live main conduit that runs behind the slate wall. I am an ectotherm of the electrical current. I do not eat the electricity the way the Copper-Thieves do—I have no interest in tearing down the mountain to grow tall—Instead, I lie pressed against the heavy lead sheathing of the live cable, letting the sixty-cycle alternating hum pass through my jelly, warming me, keeping my chemistry awake and slow and golden.

From that central mantle in the junction box, I send out my threads.

If you walk the catwalks with your hand trailing along the underside of the handrail, you have probably touched me a dozen times without knowing it. Have you ever felt a thin, waxy ridge running along the seam between two riveted plates, tucked securely into the angle where the sun cannot bake it and the monkeys’ calloused feet cannot scrape it away? A cord of tough, amber gut, the thickness of a carpenter’s pencil, that yields slightly under your thumb and feels faintly, strangely warm?

That is one of my haul-lines. I have four miles of them strung through the eastern truss.

I am the slowest thing in a slow world. The two-minute stroke of the cottage-sized pistons below is, to me, a frantic, fluttering heartbeat. The lizards riding the thirty-foot cog-wheels are streaks of green lightning. It takes me an entire afternoon to extend a feeding-fan three yards along a tie-rod. 

I do not see the heat like the Weavers, and I do not see the violet sky like the swifts. I *taste* the alloys, and I *feel* the shear.

My world is a map of metallurgy and friction. Through my four miles of amber nerve-cord, I know the exact state of every rivet-head, every brass bushing, and every cast-iron gusset plate across twelve vertical acres of the gantry-spine. Every metal has its own flavor when it is wet with condenser-sweat. Cast iron is heavy, peppery, and full of black carbon-grit. Copper is sharp, sour, and bright, like a needle in the tongue. Brass is sweet and sleepy. And old, fatigued steel—steel that has been flexed six million times by the two-minute piston-stroke until its internal crystal lattice is beginning to fray—tastes like cold ashes.

I am a scavenger of friction. Where two plates rub together in the dark, grinding off microscopic flakes of red oxide and metallic dust, I extrude a broad, wet lip of myself into the gap. I swallow the grit. I digest the rust-spores, the dead gnats that have been washed down into the seams, the soot from the exhaust stacks, and the black, carbonized drippings of four-hundred-year-old cylinder oil. In exchange, I leave behind a slick, tough mucilage that dries into a flexible, horn-like skin. 

I am the cartilage in the mountain’s stiffening knees.

Wait with me for a week in the dark under the expansion joint of the Number Four distillation tower, and you will see how I live with the Weavers.

There is an old Weaver who works this sector of the spine. I do not know what he looks like—I have never had an eye to see his moss-coat or the scarred leather of his chest—but I know his weight to the ounce, and I know the exact, uneven cadence of his three-toed hooks on the steel. He has a bad left hind-leg; the calcified mineral deposits in his knee make him strike the iron with a slight, dragging *scritch-clack* after every fourth piston-heave. And I know his smell: a rich, rank wave of hot tallow, chewed hawser-root, and the sour brass of the dead pressure-gauges wedged into his hide.

Right now, sixty feet above my central junction box, a secondary governor-shaft is dying. 

I have felt it coming for three months. The shaft is six inches of solid forged steel, turning at twelve revolutions per minute inside a babbitt-metal bearing housing. Six months ago, the small copper drip-tube that fed lubricating tallow into the housing became choked with a nest of metallic beetles. Their chitinous shells jammed the spout. 

Since then, the bearing has been running dry. 

To me, the sensation is intolerable. It is a hot, jagged, rhythmic screech that travels down the cast-iron pillar and sets every thread of my four-mile body quivering with nausea. The babbitt-metal is smearing; the steel shaft is scoring itself into rough, spiral grooves. The flavor coming down the pillar with the condensed steam-water is the bright, scalding taste of raw, unoxidized iron shavings. If the shaft seizes, it will snap its drive-chain; when the drive-chain snaps, it will whip through the three-inch copper water-main beside it; when the water-main bursts, my warm junction box below will be drowned in a roaring cataract of scalding, mineral-heavy water, and the thirty-yard fern-cloud below me will be stripped from the girders and dropped into the white wool five miles down.

I began climbing toward the bearing five days ago. 

I do not climb like the monkeys. I pour myself upward through the hollow interior of a lattice-column, flowing along the shaded, greasy inner angles of the steel, drawing my tail-threads up behind me from the lower tiers so I have enough bulk for the work. By yesterday noon, I had pooled about twenty pounds of my amber mantle into the stamped depression of the deck-plate directly beneath the hot bearing-housing.

The iron here is casi-blistering—seventy degrees Celsius—and it is vibrating with a dry, chattering shudder that makes my margins curl. I cannot reach the inside of the housing; the cast-iron cap is bolted down with two-inch hex-nuts that have rusted into solid, fist-sized domes of red stone. 

So I call the Weaver.

I cannot strike the iron with a hammer-plate like he does. I have no bones. Instead, I do the only thing I can do: I change the tune of the girder.

Ten feet away from the burning shaft, a three-quarter-inch steel tie-rod runs diagonally down through the truss, tightened by an old turnbuckle. This tie-rod is under immense tension; it hums continuously in a deep, clear F-sharp, vibrating in sympathy with the main dynamos half a mile below. It is one of the primary telephone wires of the local Weavers.

Over the course of six hours, I flow out along the tie-rod, hauling my heavy, dense central mass out into the open air—where the stray shafts of ultraviolet light filtering through the palm-fronds sting my skin like salt—and I wrap myself around the exact midpoint of the rod in a thick, thirty-pound rubbery knot.

I deaden the wire. 

The clear F-sharp drops into a muffled, flat, wet thud. To a Weaver half a mile away, listening with his cup-shaped ears and feeling the truss through his horn-toes, it is as if a finger has been laid suddenly across a violin string. A blind spot appears in his acoustic map of the canyon. A shadow in the bronze chord.

I hang there on the rod, baking in the dry air, pulling my margins in tight to conserve my turpentine moisture, and I wait through the cold, violet night.

Two hours after dawn, when the sun hits the eastern distillation towers and the expansion joints begin their morning artillery-creak of adjustment, I feel him coming. 

*Clack-thrum... scritch-clack.*

He drops out of the wet fern-curtains twenty yards away, landing on the grated catwalk with a four-hundred-pound ring of horn on iron that sends a delicious shiver of loose rust-flakes raining down into my open feeding-fans below. He is agitated; I can feel the low, rattling bass note in his barrel chest vibrating through the soles of his feet into the deck-plates. He walks to the tie-rod, reaches out with one heavy, grooved caliper, and pinches the steel just below my body.

He feels the deadened wire. Then, slowly, his broad, flat skull swings around, and I feel the faint, dry radiant heat of his own face as he brings his four thermal pits to within six inches of my amber knot.

To his heat-sight, I must look like a strange, cool smudge—a patch of dull indigo against the glaring magenta of the sun-warmed steel, except where my core pulses with the stolen warmth of the bearing-pillar. 

He does not tear me off the rod. We know each other, though he does not know that we know each other. To him, I am just what happens when the machine is sick; I am a symptom, like a boil on a hide, that points toward the thorn. 

He follows my haul-line. 

He traces the pencil-thick cord of amber gut with the tip of his black horn-hook, walking backward along my nerve-path from the tie-rod, down the lattice-column, straight to the base of the chattering, scorching bearing-housing. 

When he reaches the pillar, he stops. He presses his wide jaw-membrane directly against the hot iron. The dry, metallic screech of the scoring shaft pours straight into his skull. He jerks his head back instantly, his chest rattling like a loose furnace-door, offended to the very marrow of his genetic compulsion. 

Now comes the part I must be careful of.

The Weaver reaches up with his massive jaws, clamps his blunt, iron-stained teeth over the rusted-shut grease-cup on top of the bearing housing, and twists his whole seven-foot, four-hundred-pound body sideways as a lever, using the upward heave of the two-minute piston-stroke below to add torque to his neck.

*CRACK.*

The rusted cast-iron stem of the grease-cup shears clean off. A puff of dry, black, burnt-tallow dust and roasted beetle-shells blows out of the hole. 

Now there is an opening—a thumb-wide throat leading directly down onto the spinning, white-hot steel of the governor shaft. The Weaver leans over the rail, preparing to tear up an armful of waxy hawser-vine to chew into oakum-pulp, though vine-pulp will only char and smoke in a high-speed bearing.

Before he can swing away into the leaves, I let go of the tie-rod. 

I drop with a wet, heavy *slap* onto the deck-plate right between his hind calipers. He does not startle—nothing startles a Weaver except a high-pressure steam whistle—but he pauses, looking down at me with his thermal pits. 

I am already flowing up the hot pillar, faster than I ever move, sacrificing a pound of my outer skin to the scorching dry iron, leaving a smoking trail of amber mucus behind me as I haul my best, richest, most lipid-heavy reserve—a bladder of pure, golden, refined hydrocarbon jelly that I have been distilling from lower-tier cylinder-oil for forty years—up to the sheared-off hole at the top of the housing.

The Weaver watches me. I smell his tallow-breath; I feel the tick-tick-tick of the dead brass gauges wedged into his shoulder-scars as he breathes. 

I reach the hole and pour the bladder in.

It hits the spinning six-inch shaft with a sharp *hiss* and a heavy, sweet cloud of pine-and-paraffin smoke. Instantly, the tone of the pillar changes. 

The dry, chattering, high-pitched screech vanishes. The scored steel drinks the golden jelly; my specialized, heat-polymerizing chains cross-link in the seventy-degree heat, forming a tough, self-healing teflon-like cushion between the shaft and the ruined babbitt-metal. The vibration of the housing drops an octave, then two, settling back into the deep, oiled, bronze-toned chord of the mountain. 

The Weaver presses his jaw-cup to the housing again. He listens for one full piston-stroke—two minutes of absolute stillness, while the soot-swifts wheel through the steam-plumes out in the blinding canyon, and the green lizards ride the great cog-wheel three teeth higher toward the sun. 

The bearing is quiet. The F-sharp tie-rod, relieved of my weight, is humming clearly again behind him.

Before my tail-threads have even finished pulling back out of the grease-hole, the Weaver reaches down with a mouthful of wet, chewed root-mash and ochre mud he had already gathered in his cheek-pouches, and plasters it firmly over the sheared-off spout, sealing my jelly—and about four ounces of my severed front margin—inside the dark housing with the spinning shaft.

I do not mind. That four-ounce piece of me will live in there for the next twenty years, turning round and round in the dark with the steel, eating the iron-dust and keeping the governor true, until the shaft wears through again.

The Weaver strikes the pillar twice with his wrist-plate—*clang-clack*, *it-is-smooth*, *it-is-cool*—and swings himself out over the three-thousand-foot drop, catching a four-inch hawser cable without looking, vanishing into the dripping emerald fog of the lower condenser-gardens.

I do not watch him go. I am already sinking back down the shaded side of the column, flowing home through the rivet-seams toward my porcelain cave behind the slate switchboard, where the sixty-cycle current is waiting to warm me through the night. 

Two hundred yards to the west, down near the main boiler-pedestals where the calcified bones of the old Weavers hold up the cracked iron in the dark, one of my finest, hair-thin scouting threads has just tasted a faint, sour trickle of green verdigris and ozone in the run-off water. A Copper-Thief seed has sprouted on the seventy-volt exciter line. 

In three or four days, when I have digested enough rust-mud to grow another ten pounds of ballast, I will crawl over to the nearest tension-cable, wrap myself around the steel, and deaden the note until the hammers come down to cut the wire clean.
