I am at a crossroads where the mud has turned to stone, and the stone has forgotten the fire that baked it.

Four days ago—though there is no day here, only the cooling of the rock to mark the hours—a harvester passed from east to west, cutting clean, ninety-degree ruts directly across an ancient track that runs north to south. That older track was laid down thirty years ago. Its vitrified clay has long since cooled to the ambient eighty-two degrees of the deep crust, and its twin canals are choked with thirty years of abandoned silt-weaver dams. It is a dry staircase of gray, porous cement, climbing gently over a low rise in the basalt. 

Where the machine’s twelve-foot treads smashed through the old weirs, there is a ruin of broken mineral-crust and fresh, steaming water. That is where I sit.

I am not one of the weavers, clinking in their rusted armor, nor am I one of the blind grazers whose clicking tarsals have already faded into the west. My kind does not have a name for itself, because we have no ears to hear one, and we do not travel in herds. If you were to sweep a sodium-vapor lamp over this intersection, you would think I was a lump of slag that failed to pulverize—a low, hemispherical dome of black basaltic grit and iron-clay, roughly the size of a kneeling man’s chest, half-submerged in the warm slack-water where the new canal breaches the old.

In truth, my shell is a masonry of thefts. I build it the way a caddisfly larva builds its case, though my mortar is not silk, which would rot in this perpetual bathhouse, nor the rapid-curing alkaline cement of the weavers. I use the thick, black grease that drips from the harvesters’ bogie-axles, mixed with my own acidic spittle and the grey ash of the induction furnaces. Over the eighty or ninety years it takes to grow to my bulk, I have mortared ten thousand pieces of the plateau onto my back: river-smooth pebbles of vesicular basalt, the shed iron-chitin plates of dead silt-weavers, the curved, bone-hard spatulas of tinker-crabs that dropped during a machine-crossing and didn't make the far tread in time, and dozens of short, coiled lengths of wire-bush stem, still sharp with their fog-catching barbs. 

The barbs catch the mist for me too. A slow, permanent trickle of mineral dew runs down the channels of my back and pools around my skirt—the thick, muscular foot of pale gray flesh that grips the vitrified clay beneath me. 

I am an ambush of one, waiting for the geometry of the tracks to bring the plateau into my reach.

To me, the dark is not black; it is a map of pressures and tastes. I have no eyes, and unlike the wide-skulled hunters, I have no thermal pits to see the bright heat-ghosts of the herds. Instead, around the base of my masonry dome, just where my gray foot meets the mud, I extend a fringe of forty or fifty sensory filaments. They are as thin as hair and six feet long, floating out into the rusty water of the freshly flooded rut and draping over the shattered limestone of the old weirs.

Through them, I read the history of the intersection.

I can taste the iron-sulfate tang of the harvester that passed four days ago, already thinning as the water cools from ninety degrees down to eighty-six. I can taste the mucous trails of five ribbon-lungs that hauled themselves out of the basalt pores an hour ago and are currently lying flat in the shallow pool ten feet to my left, drinking the trace oxygen from the air with their wet, maroon backs. And underneath the water-tastes, through the broad sole of my foot pressed flat against the ceramic hardpan, I can feel the songs of the machines.

There are three of them in my world right now. 

The one that made this fresh rut is twelve miles west; its turbine-thrum is a steady, soothing vibration in the lower register of my flesh, like a heart beating in another room. Twenty miles north, another is moving slowly across an ancient flood-basin; its song is muddied, damped down by a thick blanket of silt and the dense root-networks of a thermal-palm grove that lies between us. And far to the southeast—perhaps thirty miles off, right at the edge of what the basalt can carry—there is a third. I only know it is there because one of its twelve-foot treads has a single worn link-pin that clicks slightly higher than the others every time it comes around the drive-sprocket. *Thump-clack. Thump-clack.* A tiny, rhythmic flaw in the metal that travels through thirty miles of black rock and enters my belly as an itch.

Something brushes my third filament on the left.

It is not the slow, liverish slide of a ribbon-lung. It is sharp, metallic, and hesitant. *Clink. Pause. Clink.*

A silt-weaver has come to repair the breach. The breaking of the old thirty-year terrace has drained three of the upper pools, and the weaver’s instincts are drawn to the soundless trickle of water escaping from the older, cooler canal down into the warm, new one. It wants to build a weir across the gap. It is a large female, heavy with alkaline spit and freshly molted iron-plates, her eight hydraulic legs stepping delicately over the shattered cement of her ancestors. 

I do not pull my filaments back. If they move, she will feel the current shift with her chemosensory palps. I let her walk right over them. Her iron-shod tarsi press my nerve-threads into the warm mud. She tastes of old rust, calcified tube-worm shells she scavenged from the wake, and the bitter, milky fluid that fills her joints.

She stops two inches from my shell. To her, I am an outcrop of the ruin—a convenient anchor-stone for the left flank of her new dam. She raises her abdomen, and I feel the faint, warm mist of her spinnerets as she sprays the first foundation-coat of mineral cement onto my flank, right over an old, rusted tinker-crab claw I mortared into my dome twenty years ago. She is gluing herself to my house.

She turns to draw the line of stone across the gap.

I do not strike like the pit-predators. I have no claws, and my muscles are built for holding, not for speed. Instead, I open the single seam that runs horizontally across the front of my masonry dome—a hinge hidden behind a fringe of dead, coppery wire-bush twigs—and I unroll my tongue.

It is not a tongue, really. It is an inverted sleeve of hydraulic muscle, lined with thousands of hooked, siliceous teeth made from the same glass-hard mineral the harvesters bake into the clay. It inflates with blood pushed from my foot, shooting out silently into the warm water like a gray glove turning itself inside out. 

It does not hit her; it envelopes her. Before her hydraulic legs can lock against the stone, the sleeve wraps over her domed carapace, seals itself against the mud around her, and pulls vacuum.

There is no scuffle. Only a single, muffled *crunch* inside my hood as the hydraulic pressure reverses, drawing her back into the dark under my shell, where my radula—a slow, grinding millstone of iron-reinforced cartilage—begins the three-day work of rasping through her rusted plates. Her half-cured cement washes away in the warm current, dissolving back into gray ash. 

When I am finished, four days from now, I will not spit her empty shell into the canal. I will pass her curved, iron-layered carapace down to my foot, coat the inside of it with black bogie-grease I scraped from the rut-wall, and press it into the gap on my left shoulder where the mortar has begun to thin. 

I settle my skirt deeper into the ceramic mud, sealing the edges against the damp. Four feet away, the ribbon-lungs continue to breathe in the shallow pool, undisturbed. Twelve miles to the west, the turbine-hum fades by another fraction of a tone as the machine crawls down into a gully. The warm fog rolls over the lip of the rut, condensing on the stolen wire-bush barbs on my back, and trickles down into the dark water, drop by drop, while I grind the iron in the quiet.
