I am in the mortar-groove between the third and fourth slabs of the northern spill-way, forty feet below the lip of the collapsed cistern. 

I do not know it as a cistern. I know it as the Great Drum—a wide, bell-shaped hollow in the world that hums in a flat, shivering bass whenever the cold water from the upper ice-shelf drops down the mountain wall and strikes its lip. Right now, the Drum is vibrating so hard that the gel inside my snout-pores is stinging. A lens of meltwater, denser than lead and two degrees colder than the valley floor, is pouring over the rim above me, shearing through the stiller water and driving a lateral gale straight down the avenue.

My belly-horn is pressed flat into the shallow dish of the basalt slab. My four weight-limbs are locked out at forty-five degrees, their cups sucked down into the pale, dense mortar-seams that the water cannot erode as fast as the darker stone. The vacuum under my left fore-cup is imperfect; there is a tiny, fluted pit in the mortar under the rim, and every three seconds a thread of freezing water hisses through it, tickling the sensitive bristles at the center of my palm. I do not lift it to reset. To lift an inch in this draft is to let the wedge of the current get under my shoulder-knuckle. If the current gets under my shoulder, I will be peeled off the floor like a dead ray and tumbled half a mile down the terraced steps before my weight can bring me back to the bottom.

So I hold. My heart beats once every twelve seconds, pushing thick, sluggish purple blood through the cold grease of my flanks. 

Beside my right flank, wedged into the narrow throat between two column-stumps that have been worn down until they look like gray teeth, is my stone.

I did not find it alone. Four wakes ago—we do not have days, only the long, irregular cycles of the surface-wind versus the deep-vent heat—the high span of the aqueduct three avenues over finally gave up. The *clack* of its breaking hit my snout like a physical blow, a bright, silver spike of pressure in the gray roar of the basin. Five of us met in the trough below the ruin while the rubble was still rolling. 

There were three pieces. Two were too small, spinning away like pebbles into the deep amphitheater. The third was a section of the arch-key, four hundred pounds of dense, blue-black basalt, worn into the shape of a massive, lopsided marrow-bone. 

We pushed it for sixty hours. We used our snouts, our shoulders, and the flat sleds of our chests, ratcheting up the fluted drainage-grooves during the brief, breathless silences when two gyres canceled each other out, then locking down together like a five-limbed shingle when the water boiled back. One of the smaller bulls lost his hind-purchase during a vertical shear near the plaza’s rim; I felt the sudden *pop* of his cups letting go, and then the water took him—just a pale blur of gray hide somersaulting silently overhead, his limbs tucked tight against his ribs, waiting to hit a leeward wall half a mile away.

The four of us remaining wedged the bone-stone here, in the gap between the teeth. 

It is a masterpiece. Before the stone was placed, the water rushed through this colonnade in a clean, screaming sheet that stripped the basalt down to its dark crystals. Now, my snout reads a gorgeous, chaotic bloom of turbulence. The bone-stone splits the twenty-knot gale in two. Half goes over, roaring toward the ceiling; half is forced down into the cupped paving stones behind us, spinning in a tight, lazy whirlpool that smells of crushed fish-oil and sulfur from the deep vents.

In the quiet behind the stone, the grease-film is already as thick as a thumb-nail. 

The current above me slackens. I feel it before it happens—the bass note of the Great Drum drops an octave, losing its tight, singing edge as the lens of meltwater passes on toward the center of the valley. The pull against my left shoulder vanishes. The water goes dead, weightless, and still.

*Pop.*

I release my right fore-limb, shoot the cable of muscle out three feet, and slap the cup down onto the curve of the bone-stone itself. *Pop.* My left hind-limb comes up, slides forward, and locks into the lee. I drag my heavy belly-keel over the lip of the paving slab and settle into the dead-water pocket behind our dam.

The other three are already here, piled in the three-foot depression of the floor like river boulders. We do not look at each other; we have nothing to look with. We press our rubbery flanks together to share the slow, iron-smelling heat of our bodies, our snouts all pointed up into the vortex we made.

The slate-rays have already found our work. Twelve of them are shingled across the back of the bone-stone, their flat, leathery skirts rippling as they rasp away at the new bacterial grease. 

I do not want a ray right now; their cartilage is tough and takes too much jaw-work in the cold. Instead, I lock my four walking-limbs into the mortar and unfold my long, thin baffle-arms from the grooves along my ribs. 

Above us, just six feet over the top of the bone-stone, a silver funnel of blind ribbon-eels is being dragged backward by the tail-end of the gyre. I can feel the tiny, frantic flutter of ten thousand tails vibrating against my snout-pores—a high, sizzling static against the deep hum of the stone. 

I reach up. My baffle-arms go straight up out of the quiet pocket, piercing the boundary layer. The moment my flat wrists hit the main current, the water tries to tear them out of their sockets. My shoulder-knuckles lock; the tendons in my armpits hum like taut wire. I tilt my palms ten degrees inward.

Immediately, the water behind my wrists breaks. A sharp, miniature downdraft corkscrews down my forearms, pulling a gallon of the high-speed stream down into our dead-water bowl. 

With it come the eels. Thirty or forty of them, translucent and boneless, are sucked out of their silver tornado and slammed straight down against the calloused leather of my chest. They don't even know they've left the current until they hit my skin. I drop my chin, open the four black horn-plates of my mouth, and shear them in half against my breastbone, swallowing the cold, oily ribbons whole before the eddy can spin them out of reach.

The bull beside me raises his arms too, matching the angle of my wrists. Together, our four limbs form a wide, slotted weir. The vortex deepens. More eels rain down into the hollow between our bellies, and the smaller female tucked behind my tail-stump doesn't even have to raise her baffles; she just lies on her side, snapping her horn-plates in the dark, rich soup accumulating around our knees.

Then, through the soles of my locked feet, the stone speaks again.

It is not the Drum. It is a long, shivering groan from the south—the high, thin tendon of the causeway that spans the central trench. It has been singing in a high, desperate whine for three winters, thinned down to the marrow by the deep-vent updrafts. Now the pitch is dropping, warbling like a slackened string. The mortar is giving out inside the joint. 

In my snout, the picture of the valley shifts. When that span drops, ten thousand tons of stone will plug the lower trench. The deep-vent water will have nowhere to go but up, straight into the northern colonnade. Within two wakes, the current right where I am sitting will shift by thirty degrees and double its weight. Our bone-stone will be dislodged, popped out of the column-teeth like a seed from between two fingers, and our quiet grease-garden will be scoured back to bare basalt.

All four of us stop chewing. Our baffle-arms fold back into our flanks with a single, synchronized *slap* of heavy skin. 

For ten seconds, we are just four gray lumps in the pewter twilight, our blind, pitted snouts lifted toward the ceiling, waiting for the *clack* that will tell us where the new world is going to fall, and which way we will have to winch ourselves to go and move it.
