I am down in the lower third of the bowl, where the sun only reaches for forty minutes at noon and the air tastes permanently of wet iron and old tallow. 

My name was Pout until the winter the great snow-slab came off the eastern tooth and snapped the main fuller’s trough; I went out on the ice-greased suspension boughs with a rawhide line between my teeth and spliced the split willow-chute before the backing water could drown the lower sheep-folds. Now they call me *Graft*, or just *Graft-on-the-Drop*, though my sister still calls me Pout when my knees ache and I sit too close to the peat-slates.

My knees ache often now. I am forty-two winters old, which is old enough that the cold water in the boardwalk prints bites right through my cedar clogs and stays in my shins until midsummer. Because I don’t like the vertical ladders anymore, they gave me the Main Span to watch. 

I am a splice-tender. My house is not a house at all, really, just a swollen wart of woven bog-myrtle and packed sphagnum grown directly into the underside of the Five-Tree Bridge, hanging twenty feet above the central sluice where the cataract hammers itself into white soup against the root-sieve. It is the loudest place in the notch. You do not hear the water here; you wear it. It sits inside your breastbone like a second, frantic heart. My left ear went deaf to high sounds ten years ago from facing upstream while I worked, which is a blessing, because it means the ibises on the bridge-rail above me only sound like dry peas rattling in a bladder, instead of the skull-splitting iron-on-slate shriek they give everyone else.

If you came down the wet steps right now—ducking the grease-buckets and keeping your hand on the inner root-rail so a squall doesn't peel you off into the gorge—you would find me sitting on a three-legged stool of peeled alder, wedged into the crook where the third and fourth bridge-cedars fuse. 

I am greasing a wedding. 

Three months ago, the bridge-wardens decided the span was dipping too much under the weight of the autumn bog-iron loads. So we took a six-inch sapling of tough, sour mountain-ash that was growing out of the butcher’s roof-thatch, bent it down with two-stone river weights until its crown touched the belly of the bridge, and opened both barks with a bone chisel. I bound them together with sixty feet of boiled goat-hide, tongue to groove, green inner-skin to green inner-skin. 

Now, it is my job to keep the rot and the air out of the wound until the two trees forget they were ever separate. 

I have a wooden piggin between my clogs, full of black poultice: boiled mutton-fat, wood-ash, and the chewed, spit-out mash of bitter-fern root, which keeps the grey boring-beetles from laying their eggs in the raw sap. My thumbs—broad as horn spoons, stained the color of a bruised plum down to the second knuckle—work the grease into the seams of the rawhide binding. *Schluck, schluck.* The bridge shudders under my rump. Someone is driving a herd of wet wethers across the deck above me; I can feel the sharp, nervous *tock-tock-tock* of sixty cloven hooves coming straight down through the living wood of the trunk and into my spine, followed by a shower of brown water and loose moss that drops through the deck-chinks and splatters across my greased leather shoulders. I don’t look up. Looking up in this town only gets you a mouthful of pigeon-lime.

I know everyone in the sinkhole, not by their faces, which are always half-hidden behind greasy wool hoods or squinted against the spray, but by the way they make the bridge complain. 

When big Marta from the upper tiers comes across with two kipes of wet flax on her yoke, the third cedar gives a long, low, rheumatic *groan* in C-flat that vibrates right through my stool and makes the water in my grease-piggin ring in little concentric circles. When the children are running the gutters with their spawn-hooks, the bridge doesn't groan; it *twitches*, the outer osier-rails whistling as they whip back and forth. And when the wind shifts north-northwest—the bad wind, the one that smells of the high dead ice where nothing grows—the whole five-tree span lifts an inch out of its notches, the rawhide cables go as tight and hard as bog-iron, and the sap in the wounds I’m tending literally sucks backward into the wood with a tiny, thirsty *hiss*.

That is when I earn my mud-fish. 

When the bridge lifts like that, I have to take the heavy stone maul and crawl out along the slick, white-streaked flank of the number-one trunk, out over the roaring throat of the sump where the spray is so thick you have to breathe through a wad of oiled wool, and drive three fresh slate wedges into the northern root-crotch to lock her down before she twists her own bark off. 

The birds know me. An old, one-eyed cock-ibis with a bill like a rusty sickle has had his nest two feet from my smoke-hole since before my teeth started going loose. We have an arrangement. I don't steal his eggs for the pot, and when I'm out on the wet belly of the span with both hands full of rawhide and tallow, he walks down the curve of the trunk, bracing his yellow-scaled legs against the wind, and picks the cold-water leeches off the backs of my ankles with the very tip of his hook. He is rough about it—he takes a bit of skin half the time, leaving little beads of dark blood on my greasy wool stockings—but a bleed you can see is better than a rot you can't.

Sometimes, in the ten minutes of blinding silver sun after the four-o'clock squall, when the whole green saucer of the town is steaming like a fresh-dropped dung-heap and ten thousand wings are shaking the water off at once, I hook my elbows over the lower tie-beam and look down between my clogs. 

Fifty feet below, the black water of the sump goes round and round in a slow, greasy platter of foam, feathers, and peeled willow-bark before the mountain swallows it. Nobody knows where it goes. The old women say it comes out three days' walk down the mountain in a flat country where the trees stand up straight by themselves and the dirt is dry enough to blow into your eyes like cold ash. 

I don’t think I would like that. A tree that stands up by itself has no business with you; it doesn't need your knife, and it doesn't care if you fall off it. 

Up above, the fulling-hammers go *thump-clack, thump-clack*. My sister knocks three times on the kitchen floor-beam overhead with the butt of her gutting-knife—*soup’s on, the kipe brought up six fat whites and an eel*. I wipe my black thumbs on the wool of my thighs, shove the horn lid onto the grease-pot so the choughs don't eat the tallow, and pull myself up the vine-rope by my arms, feeling the whole town shift a quarter-inch to the east to let the evening shadow in.
