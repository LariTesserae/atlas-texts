I am not in the alcoves, and I am not up in the roots. 

Wait for the draw to peak, when the lower flues have been hooting their flat, dry chord for three or four years and the air in the bowl is as thick and sharp as it ever gets. If you slide down the spiral grooves past the residential tiers, past the lowest vents where the draft whistles against the polished lips of the wood, you reach the basal curve—the heavy, seamless chime where the steep wall rounds out to become the floor.

There is a seam here that runs for forty paces along the grain. It is not a street, because it goes nowhere, and it is not a room, because the wood above it is too dense to be carved into alcoves. It is a stress-fault, an ancient sheer-line where two colossal plates of heartwood have been trying to slide past each other in opposite directions since before the first flint was struck. 

That is where I am. My knees are wedged into the lower lip of the fault, and my broad, flat sternum is pressed hard against the upper overhang, bridging the microscopic gap between the two moving masses of the world.

I am an *ear-wedge*. Not a weaver, not a scraper of the middle streets, because my left hip calcified crookedly two wakes ago—a bad batch of high-mineral amber from a deep fissure that locked the ball in the socket before I had finished my growth. I cannot climb the bass-strings in the ceiling without setting up a clumsy, limping vibration that ruins the tuning of the knots, and I cannot fold neatly into a standard sleeping-niche without my stiff leg sticking out into the street-groove to trip the skaters in the dark. 

So I was given the seam. I have been here for four draws.

If you were to slide past me right now, trailing your dry fingers along the wainscoting, you would think I was a buttress—a slightly irregular prop of deadwood driven into the fault to keep the overhang from pinching shut. The talc from the floor has drifted up over my ankles and settled into the crook of my ruined hip. My skin has taken on the exact polish of the lignum vitae around me because the skaters who pass this way along the bottom tier use my right shoulder as a turning-post. Over the decades, thousands of dry palms have pivoted off my collarbone to swing themselves around the curve of the bowl. The bone there is worn smooth and slightly flattened under the parchment of my hide, polished to the texture of an old shuttle. I do not mind it. Every hand that touches me leaves a tiny, percussive signature—the weight of their pouch, the tremor of their tendons, the slight click of their flint-kit against my ribs. I know who is going down to the talc-plain to lie down, and I know who is coming back up with an ancestor’s femur in their sack, just by the drag of their fingers across my shoulder.

Right now, I have a piece of red-black resin the size of a knucklebone tucked behind my left molars. It has been there since the vents first started to murmur at the beginning of this wake. It tastes of hot iron, crushed pine-needles, and the deep, dead salt of the earth's crust. It does not make my mouth water—nothing makes us wet—but it coats the back of my throat in a slow, heavy film that keeps my heart beating once every forty seconds. *Thump.* A long pause while the thick tar of my blood pushes through the dry capillaries behind my knee. *Thump.*

I am listening to the sheer-line.

The young weavers up in the basket-vaults think they know tension because their bridges hum like plucked gut, and the street-scrapers think they know resistance because their flint blades chatter against the grain. They only know the skin of the instrument. Down here at the basal curve, where the wood is so compressed it has forgotten it was ever a tree, the sound is different. It does not hum, and it does not groan. It ticks.

Through the tight drumhead of my chest-skin, the two walls of the fault speak directly into the hollow of my ribs. For three days, there will be nothing—just the vast, distant page-turning of the outer rind miles behind the wall, and the steady, hollow chord of the breathing-flues above me. Then, deep inside the ironwood, a single bundle of petrified fibers will give way under the barometric draw.

*Tink.*

It is not a sound in the air. If you were standing two feet behind me on the floor, you would hear only the slap-back echo of a scraper working three tiers up. The *tink* happens entirely inside my breastbone. It is tiny, sharp, and bright as a dropped needle, vibrating straight through my fused ribs, up the rigid column of my neck, and into my jaw until my teeth sing with it. 

My job is to count them, and to judge their pitch. 

In my right hand, which rests in the groove between my thighs, I hold a scoring-awl made from the ulna of someone who died six wakes ago. The grip has been carved to fit the exact spread of my three middle fingers, and the bone is so heavily mineralized from the donor's long life that it feels like cold brass. Between my thumb and forefinger, I touch the wall just below my chin. There, rising from the ironwood like a row of tiny, smooth vertebrae, are the amber-tallies of the four ear-wedges who held this seam before me. Each bump is fifty years of resin-creep, bled from a tiny scratch made at the end of a wake.

By running my thumbnail down that line of black beads, I can read five thousand years of the fault’s temper. 

*Bump-bump-space-bump.* That was three wakes ago: the seam slipped twice early in the draw, held steady through the long peak, and slipped once more as the flues went dead. 

*Bump-bump-bump-bump-bump.* That was the wake before mine, when the deep crust convulsed and the sheer-line chattered like teeth, and the whole lower tier had to be wedged with thirty trunks of dead-heart to keep the floor from buckling upward and spilling the ancestors out of the talc.

This wake, the wood is tight. Too tight. 

The vents have been hooting for a long time now—long enough that the shard of resin in my cheek has shrunk to the size of a lentil—and I have only felt three *tinks* in the sheer-line. The upper plate of the wall is bulging down against my collarbone with a slow, dry greed, trying to swallow the seam, while the floor-plate beneath my knees is pulling back toward the center of the bowl. I am being slowly twisted between them. My crooked hip aches with a dull, resonant warmth, like a plucked string that won't stop vibrating. The cartilage in my spine is taking the load that the wood refuses to release.

Yesterday—or what we call yesterday, which is the span between one shift of the flute-chord’s overtones and the next—a scraper came down from the second tier. She did not use my shoulder as a turning-post. Instead, she stopped beside me in the pitch dark, her flat feet whispering to a halt in the thin dust at the edge of the floor. 

She did not click her tongue or tap the wall. She leaned down and pressed the flat of her forehead against the side of my jaw.

Immediately, my skull filled with her voice. She was young—her bone-tone was high, clear, and slightly damp, without the dry, metallic ring of an elder’s calcified cranium. She was humming a question, a rapid, purring modulation in her throat that rattled against my cheekbone: *The lintel at the seventh spiral is weeping clear oil, not amber. The grain is twisting east. Our scrapers are skittering off the knots. Why is the bottom not dropping?*

I did not lift my head from the wall; if I broke contact with the overhang, I might miss the fourth *tink*. I kept my sternum jammed into the fault and let my larynx vibrate back through her brow, pitching my hum low so it wouldn't leak into the open air of the bowl: *The seam is dry-locked. The lower plate has caught on a knot deep in the rind. It is holding the whole eastern quarter up on one tooth of wood. Tell the weavers to slacken the three great bridges above the seventh spiral, or when the tooth snaps, the slap-back will tear their anchor-pegs out of the ceiling.*

She held her forehead to my jaw for a long moment after I stopped humming. In the silence between our skulls, I could hear the faint, rapid *shhh-tok, shhh-tok* of her heart, and through her feet, which were planted slightly wider than mine, I caught a tiny, secondary reflection of the amphitheater’s acoustics—a stereophonic widening of the dark room behind me. For six heartbeats, I had four ears instead of two, and the hollow of the world felt enormous, cold, and terribly taut, like a gourd dried to the point of splitting.

Then she pulled away. Her dry fingers brushed my good shoulder—*thank you, stay firm*—and she skated back up the groove without making a sound louder than a falling leaf.

Now I am alone with the wood again. 

The air is beginning to thin. I can feel it in the back of my nose—the sharp bite of the turpentine is fading, replaced by the flat, dusty taste of old paper. Down along the basal curve, the hundred carved flues are losing their breath. The great, two-note wooden chord that has sustained the city for years is dropping in pitch, sliding down by microtones as the barometric pressure deep in the earth equalizes. Soon the hooting will break into a low, fluttering wheeze, and then it will stop altogether. The long stillness will come back.

Around the bowl, the thousand small noises of the city are already dying down. The *shhh-ick, shhh-ick* of the flint scrapers in the middle tiers is growing sporadic. Up in the high dark, the bass-strings of the root-bridges give off one last, resonant *thrum* as the young weavers slide down the anchor-ropes to find their niches. 

And still, the sheer-line beneath my chest has not let go of its tension. 

I am not going to go back to an alcove when the flues die. Even if my hip allowed it, I cannot leave the seam while it is holding its breath like this. I will enter the clamp right here, wedged into the basal fault, keeping my breastbone pressed against the overhang while my blood thickens to cold tar and my heart slows to one beat every ten minutes. 

I have raised my right hand. The point of the bone awl is resting against the ironwood just below my chin, half an inch to the right of the last ear-wedge’s final amber bead. I am waiting for that single, bright *tink* deep in the grain—the sound of the tooth finally shearing off, the sound of the eastern wall settling into its bed for the next hundred years. 

When it comes—whether it is tonight, before the vents go completely silent, or forty years from now in the dead middle of the sleep, when I am nothing but a dry husk with a listening chest—my thumb will twitch once. The bone point will score the wood. The cold resin will begin its fifty-year creep into the cut, and the next one who wedges themselves into this dark corner will read my bump with their thumb, and know that the bottom held.
