I am a wide, greased roof over a quiet room of my own making. 

Above me, the world is heavy and goes sideways. Right now, a ridge of stone is migrating from the east. It is not a sudden thing; I heard it coming two hours ago, a thickening in the *shhh-klok* of the floor, a low, grinding bass note that crept up the slope of my left flank before the first cobbles ever touched my hide. Now it is here. Fist-sized basalts roll up my gentle grade, clicking and knocking against one another in the thick, freezing soup, pressing my overlapping ribs down a fraction of an inch. My plates slide smoothly in their grease. I flatten. I let the mountain walk over me.

Beneath me, in the dark pocket between my pale belly and the slick slate floor, there is no storm. There is only the slow, warm slick of my own oil, the smell of wintergreen and old silt, and the objects I have kept from the grinding.

My six legs are tucked in close, folded like heavy, bone-tipped carpenter’s rules. With two of my opposing thumbs—the front-left and the mid-right—I am turning a piece of curved spruce wood. 

I have had this piece for three changes of the deep tide. When it fell from the light-smudge above, it had bark on it, and a sharp, resinous smell that made the edges of my mouth-ring twitch. The gravel has already taken the bark, and the water has leached the sharpness out, leaving only the grain. 

To live down here is to know everything by how it refuses to become round. Stone surrenders. Give the basin a jagged ledger of slate, and in ten thousand tides it will give you back an egg. Give it a crab shell, and it will rub the spines off until it is a smooth, matte pebble of lime. Everything here wants to be a sphere so it can roll without catching.

Wood does not want to be a sphere. Even when it is waterlogged, heavy as lead and black with silt, it remembers the straight line of its growing. It has *length*. My horn-thumbs trace the long, fibrous ridges of the spruce rib. I press the bone tip of my thumb into the softer winter-grain, feeling it yield just slightly before the hard summer-grain stops me. It is an astonishment. I could hold this curve in my mantle-fold for ten winters, shifting it from pocket to pocket as I haul across the basin, and it would still be a line. 

A vibration touches my right margin. 

It is not a stone. Stones have a short, dead ring to them—*klok*. This is a long, thrumming hum that travels through the slate itself before it even reaches the water. *Thrum-thud. Thrum-thud.* 

An anchor stone is dragging. It is far off, toward the shallow lip where the kelp-whips grow thick, pulled by a boat I cannot imagine, bobbing in a medium I would suffocate in. The hemp rope tied to the stone is singing. As the current takes the boat and the stone catches on the bedrock seams, the three-strand line tightens and vibrates like a plucked tendon. 

I do not move toward it yet. My heart beats once. The thick, dark blood pushes through my cold gills, trading old heaviness for the sharp, iron-tasting oxygen of the bottom-water. I wait for the second beat. Two minutes. The spruce rib goes back into the deep, warm crease behind my left fore-leg, tucked snugly against a bundle of rawhide strips and a stone with a hole pecked halfway through it by a tool made of iron.

Another vibration comes, much closer. It is the broad, rough-leather shove of Another.

She does not announce herself; she simply arrives out of the flying ash of the water column, hauling her five-hundred-pound bulk against the current with a slow, rhythmic *crunch-pull, crunch-pull* of her bone-thumbs in the gravel. When her sloped edge meets mine, she does not stop until our hides are pressed flat together along a three-foot seam.

Instantly, the current changes. Where there were two domes, there is now a single, wider ridge. The rolling cobbles tumble into the cleft between our backs, rattle for a moment, and then spill off to the sides. A quiet lee opens up between our under-aprons.

She is warm—a degree or two warmer than the freezing soup around us. Through the two-inch tympanum of my hide, I feel the slow, heavy *lub-dub* of her blood, slightly out of phase with mine. Deep in her gut, where the gizzard-stones grind the shells of whelks into paste, she makes a low, resonant rumble. It is the sound of two boulders turning in mud. 

*The line is down,* the rumble says. Or rather, it is the shape of the thought of the line, shared across the pressed leather of our shoulders. 

From beneath her apron, one of her stout limbs reaches out into the dark space between us. Her twin bone-thumbs open, and she presses something against my front pad.

It is a knot. 

It is a fist-sized clump of three-strand hemp, cut from a trap two tides ago, which she has been chewing in her circular mouth. It is no longer dry and scratchy; it is saturated with her wintergreen-smelling spit, beaten and ground by her flat molars until the plant fibers have married into a dense, felt-like pad. She is heavy with young—I can feel the slight, firm dome of the calf pressing against her lower belly-plates—and she is making the bed-lining for the rock-seam where she will drop it. 

I take the felt pad with my two front thumbs. I bring it to my own mouth-ring, tuck it inside, and let my rasping tongue work oil into the center of it, adding my grease to hers so the rot will never take it. While I chew, I reach into my own fold and draw out a three-foot strip of rawhide, tough and slick, untouched by the stones. I press it into her mid-limb pad. 

Nothing moves one way without pushing something else the other. 

We stay like that for three hours, while the dirty-green smudge of noon far above fades into the absolute black of the sub-polar night. A sleeper shark drifts over us, so close its rough belly-skin scrapes across the top of my mantle like a brush of stiff wire, leaving a trail of thick, fishy slime that the current immediately strips away. Neither of us flinches. The shark is soft; it belongs to the water. We belong to the floor.

Then, the singing of the distant anchor line stops. 

Seconds later, fifty yards up-current, the water *thuds*. 

It is the crisp, hollow, multi-jointed sound of a new trap settling into the gravel. The current catches it, rolls it once—*creak-klok*—until its heavy river-stone weights find a trough in the slate and hold. Almost immediately, the scent of it bleeds down-current through the silt: the rich, rotten-iron stink of old seal fat, and the bright, sharp ghost-smell of green willow wood, cut from a riverbank where the air is thin and dry and full of light.

My heart beats. Her heart beats. 

I spit the oiled felt pad back into her waiting hand. She tucks it away. 

Slowly, without unpressing our shoulders, we uncurl our twelve legs. Twenty-four horn-sheathed thumbs reach out into the flying black gravel, find purchase in the grease-smooth seams of the old mountain, and pull. Together, like a single, low boulder creeping upstream against the storm, we go to unweave the sky.
