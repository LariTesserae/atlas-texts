Down where the dinner-plate basins empty through their narrow stone throats, the light finally gives up. 

It does not fade gradually; it is simply choked off after the first two or three sharp elbows in the rock, swallowed by wet limestone that has lost its mica-glitter and turned the dull, dense grey of old tallow. This is the under-floor of the Fluted Reach—the phreatic honeycomb, four miles wide and three hundred feet deep, through which the entire snowmelt river must thread itself before it gathers into the deep fault-conduits that carry it out to the lowland springs.

Up above, the world is vertical, dry-winded, and blinding. Down here, there is no sky, no ultraviolet glare, and no draft—only the water and the bottom half of the noise.

The high, harmonic shriek of the wind-flutes does not penetrate the stone crust, though it stays present as a faint, glassy sizzling in the ceiling rock. Instead, the honeycomb is ruled by the hydraulic bass. Every siphon, every flooded U-bend and submerged chimney is a resonant chamber pumped by the weight of the river above. The water does not trickle here; it rams through smooth-bored tubes under pressure, sucking trapped pockets of air down with it and hammering them into the stone seats of the siphons. The sound is a continuous, bone-deep detonation, like standing inside the cylinder of an engine that never finishes its stroke.

Nothing down here is flat. Where the Vitrines above have surrendered thickness to slide between the needles, life in the dark conduits has surrendered length and softness to survive the churn.

I am wedged into the collar of a drop-throat five feet below the valley floor, at the exact point where the last bounce of silver sky-light dies in a froth of white bubbles. 

To look at me—if you had a lamp that would not be immediately shattered by the water-hammer, and if you could pry your fingers into the three-inch bore of the pipe—you would think a fist-sized nodule of black flint had grown out of the pale wall and jammed the drain. I have no symmetry. My shell is a lumpy, asymmetrical casting of dense iron-sulfide and hard, pearlescent aragonite, laid down over eighty years in direct response to the exact vector of the water slamming onto my back. Where the current hits hardest, my lip is three inches thick and sloped like the prow of an icebreaker; where the eddy curls behind me, my shell thins out into a ruffled, porous skirt that lets my waste wash away into the dark.

I have never moved. When I was a larva—a microscopic, ciliated speck spat into the torrent during the great midwinter thump eighty years ago—I tumbled through the miles of pipes until my single, sticky foot slapped against a microscopic pit in this specific limestone elbow. I glued myself down with a drop of protein cement that cured harder than the rock itself, shed my swimming-hairs, and began to build a house out of what the river brought me.

I am a *Gorger*, a valve-beast, one of the millions of living stones that line the gullet of the mountain. 

If the Vitrines above are the fingers that play the flutes, we are the stops in the plumbing beneath the floorboards. And though we never touch the light, and they never drop into the dark, we are locked in a slow, blind conversation that lasts our entire lives.

Right now, I am listening to three of them twelve feet above my head.

I do not have a lattice of nerves like theirs; I do not need a map of a place I will never walk through. Inside my black iron-and-lime box, my body is little more than a tough, muscular siphon-heart, a fringe of stiff, calcified baleen fans, and a single, massive otolith—a free-floating pearl of pure barite suspended in a sac of heavy grease at the center of my meat. That stone pearl is my ear, my eye, and my clock. It vibrates against the sensory hairs of my inner mantle, reading the density and pitch of the water hammering down the pipe.

For the last six hours, the pipe above me has been singing a dirty, rattling C-natural. That is the raw sound of an untuned spire—water sheeting down fast and thin, carrying nothing but cold air and barren quartz grit that scrapes my shell without feeding me. When the pipe sings C-natural, I clamp my two valves shut until the seam between them is invisible, tucking my fans inside, waiting in the dark while the water thunders over my back.

Then, the pitch shifts.

Up in the blinding cleft above this throat, three Vitrines have slid into place. Through eight feet of solid limestone and four feet of churning water, my barite pearl feels their arrival not as a shadow, but as a sudden, silky tightening of the rhythm. The rattling C-natural smooths out. The air-bubbles in the water coming down the throat grow smaller, more uniform, beaten into a fine froth by the cymatic standing waves the Vitrines have just tuned into the alabaster cups above. The pitch of the pipe drops half a step and locks into a thick, syrupy B-flat.

That B-flat is the dinner bell. It means the water is being held in the sun-bowls above. It means the bacteria are blooming.

Slowly, fighting the sixty-pound pressure of the down-draft with a hydraulic muscle that takes ten minutes to contract, I crack my shell open a quarter of an inch.

I do not reach out into the main current—that would tear my fans from their roots. Instead, by lifting the front lip of my shell just that fraction, I alter the geometry of the three-inch pipe. I turn a smooth bore into a bottleneck. The water hitting my sloped back is forced upward, creating a violent, localized back-pressure—a hydraulic jump that sends a standing wave of resistance shooting straight back up the stone throat toward the surface.

Up in the glare, the Vitrines feel my answer instantly. My back-pressure slows the drainage of their alabaster basins by twenty percent. It thickens the water in their cups, saving them the muscular work of damming the lower lips with their own bodies. Because I have partially plugged the drain, they can spread their mantles wider, catching more wind-flutes, building a larger, deeper harmonic bowl than they could ever hold on their own.

We have struck our bargain.

Ten minutes later, the water hammering into my throat changes its taste. It is no longer pure, biting snowmelt. It is warm—nearly thirty-four degrees—and thick with the spill-over from their vibrating gardens: strings of dead, sun-cooked bacterial slime, tiny flocculant snowstorms of precipitated chalk, the microscopic shards of spider-shell, and the cloudy, grease-rich rinsings of a rasped amphibian, all spun down the drain from the bowls above.

I push my calcified fans out into the calm lee behind my shell-ridge, opening them like a set of curved bone combs. They vibrate in the B-flat hum, shaking the trapped slime down into my mouth-slit in a continuous, grey ribbon. I do not have to chew; the acoustic violence of the siphon has already homogenized the soup into a smooth paste before it ever reaches my gut.

Down in the deeper honeycomb below me, where the pipes widen into dark galleries the size of cathedral vaults, there are things that move—long, blind, segmented worms as pale as cave-roots that anchor their tails in the crevices and whip their bodies in the current, and flat, heavy snails with shells of solid pyrite that graze on our iron-rich dung. Sometimes, when a juvenile Vitrine above loses its grip in the wind-draft or gets torn by a falling icicle and washes down a wide throat, those deep-water things have a feast, stripping the clear, gelid ribbon to cartilage before it can wash out to the lowland springs.

But they are scavengers of accidents. We in the collars are the keepers of the chord.

As I grow, adding another microscopic layer of aragonite to my lip from the chalk the Vitrines spill down to me, I slowly choke the pipe further. Over a century, a Gorger can seal a throat entirely. 

When the passage gets too narrow, when my shell has grown so thick and greedy that the B-flat turns into a choked, gurgling wheeze and the basins above begin to flood and lose their cymatic stars, the Vitrines above will change their tune. 

I have felt them do it to my neighbor two pipes over, an ancient, football-sized Gorger who had blocked his conduit until the water barely dripped. The Vitrines above him did not abandon the spire. Instead, five of them gathered and tuned the wind-flutes above his throat to the exact resonant frequency of his old, brittle shell. They focused the acoustic hammer of the wind and the water into a single, lance-like standing wave aimed straight down his pipe. 

For three days, the rock around us shrieked at a pitch that made my barite pearl dance like boiling oil in its sac. And then, on the fourth day, there was a dull, wet *crunch* inside the stone. The old Gorger’s iron-sulfide shell, vibrated at its natural frequency for seventy hours straight, simply shattered like a crystal glass. The pieces washed down into the deep vaults for the pyrite snails to lick clean, the pipe roared back to its open, rattling C-natural, and a new larva settled into the fresh scar the next winter.

So I am careful with my greed. 

When I feel the B-flat beginning to strain—when the harmonic starts to warble against my back—I use my short, rasping radula not to feed, but to file away at my own shell-lip, grinding off the excess lime I spent all summer laying down, keeping the bore true, keeping the music exactly where the clear ones up in the blinding light like to hear it.

I will sit here in the dark, filing my own roof and drinking the broth of a sun I have never seen, until the winter water thins, the great quartz needle in the center of the valley thumps its single, massive heartbeat through the stone, and I release my own cloudy milk into the roaring dark to find another elbow in the rock.
