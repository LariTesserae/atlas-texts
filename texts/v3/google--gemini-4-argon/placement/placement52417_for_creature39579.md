To find myself here, I have to go down through the floor. 

Not into the earth—the granite of the High Slump is too hard to dig, a seamless grey bowl that refuses the spade—but into the three-foot gap between the ancient rock and the bottom of the town. Spindle-Hollow does not sit on the stone; it sits on stilts of iron-birch and piers of dry-laid slate, creating a four-mile-wide crawlspace choked with peat-water, copper grounding-rods, and the roots of the bladder-ferns. 

I am not down there, either, though I listen to it all day. I am just above it, on the ground floor of the Seventh Terrace Accumulator House, where the big iron tanks sit in the dark like sleeping whales.

If you come in from the alley, leaving the *shuck-shuck* of the rocker-arms behind, you have to step over the high slate sill that keeps the bog-creep out. Your clogs will make a wet, rubbery *squelch* on the duckboards. It is dark in here—only one bog-gas globe burns near the main manifold, its amber light dying halfway down the flank of the nearest tank—and the air is thick with the smell of wet rust, tallow, and the sweet, cabbagy rot of nitro-slime. 

You will hear the tanks before you see me. They do not hiss; a hiss means a blown gasket, and a blown gasket on a main accumulator will peel the oilskin right off a man’s ribs. Instead, they *groan*. As the hillside rotors pump air down into them, the half-inch iron plates stretch by a fraction of a hair, giving off a low, resonant *boooong* that you feel in your teeth and the pit of your stomach. Then the town draws breath—a hundred butter-churns and roasting-spits and moss-presses opening their valves three stories up—and the tanks sigh back down again. *Boooong. Hmmmm.* Twelve times a minute, regular as an old dog sleeping by a stove.

I am sitting on a three-legged stool wedged between Tank Four and the slate wall, where the stone sweats less than usual. 

People call me the Bleeder, though on the brass tallies in the Mill-Ward’s office I am listed as *Drain-Keep, Third Tier*. I have been in this room, or the one two terraces down just like it, for forty years. You can tell by my legs. A gantry-walker has knees like knotted rope from fighting the wind; a Drain-Keep has no knees left at all, just two swollen, tallow-rubbed loaves of bread under three layers of greasy sheep’s wool, stiff from the forty-degree damp that rises through the floorboards. My left clog has two grounding-tails bolted to the heel instead of one, because the static likes to pool in the hollows under the tanks, and if I don’t bleed the charge off into the wet peat below, my shin-bones ache with a cold, sour fire that keeps me awake all night.

I am not entirely alone in the dark. 

If I lift my oil-lamp—a tiny thing, burning rendered mist-skate liver because it doesn't use up the town’s air pressure—the yellow light catches the pale, chamois-colored curves of three bellows-newts tucked into the flange where the main intake pipe meets the tank’s belly. The pipe is fifty degrees there, hot from the friction of the compressed air rushing in from the sheds. The newts are piled on top of one another like wet dough, blind eyes shut, their throat-pouches pulsing out of time with the tank’s groan. Every few hours I reach up without looking and run my thumb along the belly of the fattest one, scraping off a pearl of clear, slick mucus to rub into the threads of my drain-cock. The newt doesn't even flinch; it knows my thumb as well as it knows the warm iron.

My job is the water. 

When you take the wet, forty-degree mist of the High Slump and squeeze it down to eight atmospheres inside an iron drum, the cloud cannot stay a cloud. It gets wrung out like a dishclout. Gallons of cold, grey water rain down inside the dark bellies of the tanks every hour, pooling at the bottom. And it isn’t just water. It carries the dust of the granite domes, the microscopic black spores of the bladder-ferns that got sucked through the intake meshes, the grease from the leather bellows, and all the strange, tingling static that the condensers missed. 

If you leave that water in the tanks, two things happen: the air capacity drops until the town’s tools begin to stutter, and the spores wake up in the dark, pressurized soup, turning into a pale, blind jelly that clogs the main valves up on the street.

So every twenty minutes, I take my bronze spanner—never iron, never steel—and I lean forward on my stool, fitting the jaws over the square nut at the very bottom of Tank Four. 

I don’t use a watch. No one here does; the hairsprings magnetize in the violet mist and run fast or stop dead. I time it by the *tap-clink* of the gantry-crews overhead. Every twenty minutes, the walker on the High West Line reaches the end of his catwalk, right above my roof, and raps his brass hook twice against the main guy-wire to tell his mate at the winch-house he hasn't blown off into the juniper. The wire is bolted to the cornerstone of this building. When he taps, a tiny, sharp *tink-tink* travels down forty feet of wet slate and rings right into the back of my skull where I lean it against the wall.

*Tink-tink.*

I heave on the bronze spanner. 

The valve opens a quarter-turn, and the *sump-liquor* comes roaring out through the brass goose-neck into my wooden trough. It doesn't come out like normal water; because of the pressure and the bellows-grease, it shoots out as a thick, spitting white foam, hissing like a stepped-on gander and smelling fiercely of ozone, old pennies, and crushed fern. In the dim light of the gas-globe, the foam actually glows for a second—a faint, dying blue-violet flicker as the compressed static grounds out against the copper mesh I’ve nailed to the bottom of the trough. 

The air in the room instantly drops two degrees as the expanding gas steals the heat from my knuckles. My breath hangs white and heavy in front of my face, refusing to rise. I sit with my eyes half-shut, listening to the pitch of the spit. 

A good drain sounds like tearing heavy canvas: *shhhhh-rip*. 

When the water is mostly gone and the tank’s main air-charge is about to follow it, the sound changes. The pitch rises into a thin, fluted whistle, and the foam turns from grey-white to a dirty, bubbling yellow. Before the whistle can become a shriek—which would waste five minutes of the hillside rotors’ hard work—I slam the bronze spanner back to the left. The valve seats with a dull *thuck* that travels up my arm and settles into my collarbone.

Then the quiet comes back, thick as sheep’s wool. Just the *boooong-hmmmm* of the iron whales, and the gurgle of the foamy liquor running down my wooden sluice, through the hole in the floorboards, out into the terraced fern-troughs where the night-soil women are waiting with their wooden buckets.

That is what I am here: the thumb on the brass nut at the bottom of the lung. 

Sometimes, when the mist outside is particularly thick and the wind from the west drops by five knots, the whole town slows down. You can feel it in the room. The groaning of the tanks spaces out—ten times a minute, then eight. The intake pipe cools by three degrees, and the blind newts stir uneasily in their sleep, sliding their slick chins over one another to keep the heat in. 

In those slow hours, I take off my left mitten—my hand is the color of lard, the nails thick and ridged like horn from forty years of mineral water—and I lay my bare palm flat against the sweating curve of Tank Four. 

If you know how to listen with the heel of your hand, the tank is an ear trumpets for the whole four-mile basin. The iron skin is drum-tight. Through it, carried down the miles of pipe and cable and rocker-arm, I can feel the whole damp, shivering weight of Spindle-Hollow. 

I can feel the heavy, liquid *thump* of a stilt-hound dropping from a stone wall onto the boardwalk two streets over, right above the main air-conduit. 

I can feel the dry, chattering *burr* of the fourth-story sewing-lathes in the Tailor’s Row, where the girls are stitching new oilskins with bone needles. 

Further up, way up where the pipes thin out and meet the hill-compressors, I can feel the slow, rhythmic *clank-pause-clank* of the number-nine rotor on the Whale-Back ridge, its brass drum slightly out of true since the big frost-heave of ten winters ago, limping through the pewter fog like an old man with a wooden leg.

And underneath it all, a tiny, prickly vibration that isn't mechanical at all: the million-throated *pop-pop-pop* of the bladder-ferns swelling on the copper lines in the dark, drinking the violet fire off the pipes before the Reed-Tuners can come along with their brass hooks and scrape them into the mud.

I keep my hand there until the iron chills my blood to the wrist. Then I pull my wool mitten back on with my teeth, reach into my tallow-pocket for a pinch of dried skate-wing to chew, and lean my head back against the wet slate, waiting for the *tink-tink* to come down through the stone from the sky.
