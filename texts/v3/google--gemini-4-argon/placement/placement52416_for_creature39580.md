You have to look past the northeast corner, past the two brick condenser housings that are losing their arguments with the mud, to the place where the shale wall juts outward like a wet thumb. 

Six feet above the reach of the highest heaving basin, bolted directly into a fault-line in the dark rock, is a cast-iron box the size of a country post-office desk. Its door has rusted off its hinges and dropped into the slurry decades ago, swallowed by the bowls. Inside the iron shell, protected from the vertical drizzle by an overhang of weeping shale and a thick beard of pale saxifrage, is a frame of tarnished brass and forty-two copper needles.

The engineers who tried to harness the pit were not merely pumping; they were trying to find the pattern. They believed the cold boiling of the floor was a cipher—a tidal or barometric engine that, once graphed, would yield a schedule of pressures they could sell or weaponize. So they built the register. 

From the bottom of the iron box, forty-two thin wires of braided phosphor-bronze drop down like spider-silk, vanishing into lead sleeves bolted to the wall, which in turn feed into the water-logged gloom beneath the grinding stones. Down in the grease, those wires are attached to brass trip-levers, pressure-bladders, and pendulums that the Sump-Mother and the shifting limestone bowls constantly bump, squeeze, and drag.

I am the thing that lives in the register.

At first glance, I look like a monstrous, stratified hornet’s nest, or a salt-lick that has melted and re-solidified around the brass gears. I am a colony, a slow-moving calcified tallow, cousin to the Greasers, though I have traded their hog-bulk and their muscle-trunks for something finer, drier, and infinitely more segmented. 

When the engineers left, the great roll of oiled ledger-paper inside the machine ran out within a month. For a week, the forty-two copper needles scratched blindly against the bare brass roller, scoring forty-two parallel grooves into the metal as the stones below heaved and turned. Then, a juvenile skimmer—no bigger than a teacup saucer, blown upward on a freak bubble of marsh-gas from a sheared pipe—landed on the lip of the open box and crawled inside to get out of the rain.

It did not leave. The vibration inside the register was different from the heavy, digestive torque of the flywheels. It was a frantic, miniature chatter—forty-two distinct reports of the dark below, translated into the tiny, dry *tink-tink-scritch* of copper points dancing on brass. 

Over eighty years, I grew to fill the casing. 

I am the Paper. 

My body is a continuous, self-weaving ribbon of tough, translucent horn and dried tallow, the color of old vellum or a dead man’s fingernail. I extrude myself from a fat, glandular mantle wedged into the upper left corner of the iron box, where the spare paper-spools used to sit. Slowly—at the rate of about four inches a day—I feed my own tough, parchment-like hide down through the brass guide-rollers, directly under the forty-two dancing needles.

I let them write on me. 

As a three-ton baptismal font tips in the center of the nave, wire number twelve pulls taut; needle number twelve jerks to the left and gouges a long, jagged valley into my moving skin. When a Greaser high on the west wall brakes a flywheel to shake down isopods, the sudden back-pressure in the hydraulic line sends needle thirty-eight slamming to the margin, pricking a neat row of tiny, violet-blooded dots along my edge. When the Sump-Mother clenches at the bottom of the world and locks the floor in place, all forty-two needles drop dead center, burning forty-two straight, parallel tracks deep into my grease-slicked cuticle until the great pipe coughs, the pressure breaks, and the needles fly apart like startled birds.

I do not have eyes, and I cannot reach the water, yet I know the basin more intimately than the heron or the Mother. I feel the whole cathedral nave as a cold, sharp itch across my belly. I know when two bowls in the southwest quadrant have ground their rims so thin they are about to shatter against each other, because needle four begins to stutter with a high-frequency tremor three days before the limestone gives way. I know when the blind eels are spawning in the warm friction-clay, because the trip-levers down in the dark grow heavy and sluggish with their knotted tails, and the needles drag across me with a dull, blunt pressure that feels like a thumb pressing on a bruise.

I am also the reason the Greasers do not starve when the weather changes.

As my scored, written-upon skin passes out the bottom of the brass rollers, it does not simply fall into the mud. It hangs down from the open bottom of the cast-iron box in a long, pleated scroll of living, cicatriced leather, three feet wide and ten feet long, swaying slightly in the cool draft that rises off the turning stones. 

The wounds the needles make in me do not heal cleanly; they weep a tiny, bead-like amber lymph that smells strongly of vinegar and hot copper. As the scroll of my lower body ages and dries in the turnip-cellar air, those forty-two tracks of dried lymph crystallize into brittle, salty ridges.

Once every few days, the huge Greaser nested in the broken steam-chest six feet to my left will cease its rhythmic braking of the flywheel. One of its firehose-thick trunks will detach itself from the wet leather drive-belt, swinging blindly through the damp air until its horned suction-cup brushes against the hanging scroll of my tail. 

It does not tear me. It begins at the very bottom—where the record is three weeks old and the tissue has gone dead and crisp as autumn kelp—and it wraps its cup around my width. Slowly, with a wet, rasping sound like a cow’s tongue on a cinderblock, it licks the crystallized lymph and the shed layers of parchment off my lower edge.

And as it digests the skin, it digests the record. 

You can see the change pass through the Greaser’s hog-sized bulk within minutes. It is reading the weeks-old history of the floor through its gut. Having tasted the pattern of how the stones shifted three Tuesdays ago, its five other trunks will suddenly rearrange themselves on the wall. It will let go of a slack cable that has gone dead, reach two feet higher to snag a rusted rocker-arm that is about to come into phase with the deep gas-vents, and lock itself in place just in time to catch a massive, twenty-second surge of torque that sets the whole west wall shuddering.

In return, before the Greaser withdraws its trunk from my box, it turns its upward-facing mouth toward my mantle and regurgitates a warm, grey bolus of crushed blackrock isopods, saxifrage pulp, and iron-rich mud right into my intake cleft. 

Then it goes back to its flywheel, and I go back to my inching. 

Right now, the sky is that flat coastal zinc, and the heron has just landed on a three-foot bowl near the center of the pot. The bird weighs almost nothing—four pounds of hollow bone and damp slate feathers—Wait.

Needle nineteen just twitched. A hair’s breadth to the right, a tiny, shallow nick in my skin, lighter than the drag of an eel’s tail. The heron has stepped from the rim of one bowl to another, and the slight shift in buoyancy down in the peat-slurry brushed a phosphor-bronze wire against a stone keel. 

I bleed a microscopic drop of vinegar-lymph into the scratch. In twenty-one days, the Greaser on the wall will eat that scratch, and it will know, in its dark, eyeless core, that on a quiet Tuesday, something with two legs stood upon the world and looked down.
