You would not find me in the open alleys between the turbines, and you would not see me if you held one of the tallowy inspection ports like a lantern to the dark. To have a single body here—even a slow, boneless one like the Clerks—seems too hurried, too anxious, too easily cornered by the iron. 

I would begin where the third-tier catwalk bolts into the cheek of the great cast-iron condenser vat, just below a weeping brass union that drops one tear of concentrated copper-liquor every forty seconds.

At first, you would think I was only the mortar that had squeezed out between the brick pier and the iron mounting-plate when the masons tightened the tie-rods a hundred and forty years ago. I am a grey-violet putty, dense as tallow and gritty with lime, wedged into the junction where the metal meets the masonry. If you prodded me with a screwdriver, the blade would sink in a quarter-inch and come away clean, leaving a slot that would take until tomorrow morning to smooth itself back over.

That is my belly, or my foot—the oldest part of me, which remembers the dry air before the sluices were bricked, though only as a kind of ancient, salty crust at the very back of the bolt-hole.

From that mortar-seat, I have gone out along the iron. 

I am the *Lode-Felt*, or the *Shunt-Rind*. I am not the green calcium-algal mat that rounds the rivet heads, though I live beneath it and let it grow on my back like hair. I am thinner than leather and wider than a bedsheet, a single, continuous apron of greasy, slate-colored mantle that has crept out over the cast-iron ribs of the condenser, down the perforated upright of the stanchion, and across the gutta-percha troughs where the bundled cables sleep.

I have no eyes at all, not even the milky, armpit-mounted stubs of the Sump-Clerks. What would I do with an image? An image only tells you where a thing stops, and in the cistern-ward nothing stops; everything leaks into the thing bolted beside it. Instead, I have *span*.

Because my grey skirt is plastered across the condenser vat—where the water inside is four degrees warmer than the nave—and my lower hem is trailed three yards away into the cold silt around a sunken bronze ground-plate, I am a living thermocouple. The warmth at my top and the chill at my toe create a tiny, unceasing voltage across my skin. That is my breath. That is how I know I am still here. As long as the deep limestone sends its heat up the condenser throat and the floor-silt stays cool, a minute, peppery prickle of electrons walks down my middle from north to south, and in the wake of that prickle, my cells divide, my edges thicken, and I lay down another microscopic lamina of horn and plumbago.

To be me is to be an ledger of pressures.

I do not think in words or in pictures; I think in *torque*. Five feet to my left, a flange-bolt that was drawn tight in the reign of the old Queen is slowly losing its mind to the sulfur-water; its core is turning to black sponge. I have wrapped three thick, fibrous lips around the hexagonal nut and the washer. I feel the sixty-ton outward push of the condenser plate trying to shear that rotting bolt-neck. It is a delicious, sour ache, like a tooth being wedged with a hazel-twig. For six years I have been secreting a dense, iron-impregnated pearl into the threads of that bolt, shoring it up, replacing the vanished steel with my own dark bone because if the flange springs, the hot liquor will gush out all at once instead of weeping drop by drop, and my warm shoulder will go cold.

I know the Clerks. How could I not? To me, they are not animals; they are my mobile provinces, my wandering hillsides.

Every four or five days, the largest of them—the old female with the split tail-fluke and the three-inch garden of blind-kelp between her shoulder blades—hauls herself up the third-tier stanchion to drink at my brass union. I know she is coming an hour before her knuckles hook the railing. The water-pressure against my lower apron tightens as her boiler-weight displaces the narrow alley, and the galvanic field around the power cables bends inward toward her approaching bulk like iron filings toward a magnet.

When she arrives, she does not avoid me. Her broad, shovel-hand, calloused in the herringbone pattern of the deck-plates, plants itself squarely in the middle of my back.

Her palm is warmer than the water by half a degree, and greasy with the sweet, mineral tallow of her skin. As she leans her weight into me to reach the weeping brass gasket above, my grey-violet flesh gives way under her knuckles like stiff dough, molding itself into the horny grooves of her palm. For the three hours she hangs there, letting the copper-sulfate dribble over her lip-plates and into her battery-purple gill-slits, we are bolted together.

It is the closest thing this ward has to conversation.

Through the pressed contact of her palm and my mantle, our currents mingle. I give her a clean, low-resistance ground straight down to the bronze plate in the floor-silt, so the stray amperage she is drinking from the dynamo-hum can pass through her heart without stinging her nerves. In return, her skin leaves behind a smear of violet-brown mucus rich in urea, dead blood-cells, and the sugary slime of the kelp on her back. My surface pores open like a thousand tiny, blind mouths in the dark under her hand, drinking her grease before the blind crayfish can smell it.

Sometimes, when she has been hanging there for two hours and her two-minute heartbeat has slowed to a three-minute hydraulic thud that rocks my whole condenser-wall, a little piece of me lets go of the iron and stays stuck in the deep crease between her thumb and forefinger.

I do not mind. That torn-off bit of grey putty does not die. It rides away with her through the bladder-ferns and the amber gloom of the inspection ports, nourished by the oil of her hide, until two weeks later she hooks that same hand into a calcified valve-wheel at the far end of the nave. The little scrap of me wipes off onto the brass, tastes the new current, spreads a skirt the size of a shilling over the rivets, and begins to listen to the bolts.

There is no hurry. The four-hour piston in the north aisle is just now reaching the top of its stroke; I can feel the long, silvery sigh of the relief-water running back through the return-main under my heel. In twenty minutes, the brass valve above the third tier will let go its bubble. The water will shiver, the bladder-ferns will bow, and I will lay down another grain of iron-scale in the dark, holding the boiler together until the limestone cools.
