I am not on the avenues. I am not one of the three who hold the bone gouges, though I feel the *tock-tock-tock* of their tools in the roof of my mouth. 

To find me, you have to go to the ceiling—to the narrow throat of the shaft where the grey-violet bruise-light seeps in. You have to look at the plug of translucent tubers that corks this cistern off from the crushing dark of the upper earth. 

I am wedged *inside* the knot of those tubers. 

If you looked up from the fourth tier—if you had eyes to look—you would only see my underside: a wide, pale, muscular gasket, flat as a saucer and slick with a heavy, viscous grease that water cannot penetrate. I look like a scar in the root-mass, or a thick sheet of grisly cartilage holding the ceiling together. 

In a way, that is what I am. I am the collar. I am the live mortar between the upper dirt and the lower city.

Long ago, when the cistern was just an empty pocket of cold air and the first pale shoots of the bryophyte were beginning to prickle through the clay, I fell in—or perhaps I crawled in, seeking the damp. I was something like a shell-less snail then, and something like a blind, burrowing skink, with four stubby, useless limbs tucked into folds along my flanks and a broad, spade-shaped head. 

I never made it to the floor. I jammed myself into the neck of the shaft where the crisp, sugary tubers were swelling, intending to eat my way through them and back into the soil. 

Instead, the city happened to me. 

Before I could chew myself free, the oldest of the Grafts—the one whose back is now a solid plate of pale jade—climbed up into the neck of the shaft. It did not kill me. In this freezing box, nothing with a heartbeat is wasted. It recognized that my broad, muscular body was plugging a draft of dry, freezing surface-air that would have desiccated the young moss below. 

So the Graft pinned me there. Not with bone spikes, which would have rotted me, but by training the calcified root-horn to grow in tight, interlocking hoops around my midsection. Over seasons of slow, frigid growth, the plant-stone cinched me into the ceiling. The translucent tubers grew through the loose flaps of my skin, anchoring themselves in my pale fat, while the moss-runners crept across my lower belly, weaving a tough, green basket that supports my weight over the drop.

I cannot fall. I cannot turn around. My four vestigial legs are locked forever into the dry clay above the root-plug, twitching occasionally in a dream of digging, while my broad throat and flat belly form the ceiling of the city.

At first, this was a slow strangulation. Now, it is my entire biology.

Because the tubers have grown into my back, I do not need to hunt. When the dim violet light from some distant, surface-world fissure filters down through the earth and hits the top of the tuber-plug, the plants make a thin, watery sugar. That sugar bleeds directly into my capillaries. In return, my slow, wide heart—beating once every two minutes—warms the tubers just enough that their pale flesh never freezes solid. We share a bloodstream now, the ceiling and I.

My head is bent downward, facing the abyss of the shaft. My eyes have long since glazed over with a thick, pearly cataract of root-film, so the violet light is just a vague pressure against my brow. 

Instead, I know the city by what rises, and by what I let fall.

I am the breath-gate. Every warm thing in that vertical box—mostly the packed shrines of sleeping bats on the upper tiers, and the slow, fermenting vats of guano and pith beneath them—sends up a faint, invisible plume of heat and damp ammonia. It rises to the ceiling and pools against the concave curve of my belly. 

I taste the city in that rising mist. Through the wet, porous skin of my throat, I can tell when the bat-clusters are restless; their breath turns sour and hot. I can taste the sharp, metallic tang of the dead rat dissolving in the floor-basins forty feet below. I can taste the astringent burn of the ribbon-worm mucus when the Grafts are sealing a heavy cut on the third tier.

And I am the rain-maker. 

Because the warm breath of the bats hits my cold, greased belly, the moisture condenses on me faster than anywhere else in the chamber. It gathers in the deep, corrugated folds of my chin and along the mossy basket of my ribs. 

If I let it fall at random, it would drop straight down the central shaft like lead shot, smashing the delicate grass suspension bridges and battering the silk-lines of the twig-spiders. 

So the old jade-backed Graft trained me. 

Whenever a heavy drop swells on my chin, just before it breaks loose, I feel the faint, rhythmic tug of a sinew-cord tied through the cartilage of my lower lip. The old Graft is pulling the line. Slowly—everything in me moves like cold pitch—I shift my jaw half an inch to the left, touching the wet tip of my snout to the lip of the main北-wall gutter. The water slides off my skin and into the carved V-channel without a sound. 

I am the headwaters of their aqueduct. For months on end, my only movement is this slow, pendulum swing of my neck in the pitch black, distributing the ceiling-sweat from the north gutter to the east gutter, keeping the reed pipes full and the central shaft dry.

When the tremor hits the cistern—when the hairline crack splits the third-tier aqueduct and thefourth-tier Graft strikes the wall—*Tock. Tock. Tock.*—the vibration hits the hoops of root-horn caging my ribs like an iron bell.

The shockwave makes my muscles contract. 

Automatically, before the spiders have even formed their phalanx, before the toad is shoved into the breach, I do my part. I inhale. 

My flat belly draws upward, sucking tight against the tuber-plug. My broad, muscular throat expands, pressing outward against the root-walls of the narrow shaft-neck until the seal is hermetic. The air pressure inside the cistern drops fractionally. Furthermore, by pulling my chin back into my collar-fold, I stop feeding the main gutters entirely, holding the cold condensation pooled in the deep pockets of my skin like a miser holding coins. 

The waterfall into the north aqueduct slows to a trickle, giving the masons below the dry minutes they need to lash the split reed-pipe and pack the toad into the gap.

I hang there in the dark, holding my breath, holding the water in the hollows of my collarbones, listening with my whole pinned body to the silent frenzy below. I feel the tiny, frantic tugs of the grass bridges vibrating through the walls as the Grafts haul sinew up from the floor. I smell the sudden, sharp burst of crushed moss and worm-slime rising on the drafts.

Ten minutes. Fifteen. My heart beats seven times. The water pooling in my neck-folds grows heavy and icy, numbing my jaw.

Then, through the sinew-cord in my lip, comes a single, slow tug. 

The old Graft is back in its harness near my flank. I can feel the brush of its cold, keratin-plated fingers against my throat as it checks the tension of the root-hoops holding me in place. With its bone-gouge, it gently scrapes away a crust of white mineral salt that has built up near my nostrils, keeping my airway clear. 

I exhale. My belly sags back down into its moss-basket. I tilt my heavy head to the left, touch my snout to the north gutter, and let the stored water run down into the newly mended vein of the city. 

Down in the floor-basins, the gooseberry-sized eggs of the Grafts are drinking the water that just rolled off my tongue. Up in my back, a tiny, hair-thin rootlet from a translucent tuber pierces a new millimeter of my fat, drinking the faint warmth I stole from the sleeping bats. 

I do not have chisels. I do not have feet. I am the sky that knows where it is allowed to rain. I close my filmy eyes against the violet smudge of the upper world, and I let the city grow through me.
