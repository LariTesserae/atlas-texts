I would not be one of the builders, nor the hunters skating along the skin of the upside-down water. To have a shell or a carapace in a place like this is to be in constant argument with the erosion. Even the Wrights, for all their slow mastery of the basin, are forever patching their own dissolving flanks, forever replacing the frayed cords of their thoughts before the water wears them through.

If I am here, I am the thing that happens when the Wrights’ network grows too dense, and the water grows too heavy with their work.

Look into the bottom of the oldest spiral cisterns, or the deep, flooded potholes where the sideways streams knuckle down into the bedrock and spin in tight, silent vortices before being pulled back up into the air. At the very base of these wells, where the blue sinkhole clay has been churned for centuries with the dissolved remains of spindles, shed flint-legs, and the acidic wash of the Wrights' digestion, there is a sediment that does not settle. 

It looks at first like a vein of wet quicksilver or liquid bismuth, pooled in the lowest hollow of the limestone. In the shadowless white glare that bends down into the potholes, it doesn't shine so much as it swallows the refraction, appearing as a dark, velvety seam of heavy oil that refuses to mix with the clear water above it.

I am that seam. An **Amalgam-Skein**.

I have no fixed anatomy—no tubes of rubbery silica, no chitin hydrophones, no honeycombed stone tower. I am a colonial fluid, a dense suspension of heavy metallic silts, piezocrystalline dust ground off the spindles' spines, and billions of lipid-sheathed, anaerobic microbes that have learned to hold hands in the crushing cold. I am what the basin’s ecology leaves behind when everything soluble has been eaten and re-eaten: the heavy, conductive dregs that have woken up.

To exist as a liquid in a valley of broken gravity is a strange, stretched thing. 

When I am resting in a pothole, I am a single dark pool, heavy as lead, feeling the bass-note of the valley floor vibrating right through my belly. I do not hear the cairn-cords the way a Walker does, as a map of prey; I feel them as a landscape of *pressure*. The bedrock is never still. It hums with the Wrights’ slow, glacial conversations—a vast, stone-borne Braille of shifting frequencies. 

When the frequency changes—when a Wright three hundred yards away wedges a new slate baffle into a gap and a fresh, violent collision of water begins to roar—I do not walk toward it. I unspool.

I reach a thin, dark filament of myself up out of the pothole, sliding along the wet limestone not by muscle, but by altering the electrical charge of my own metallic silt. Because the water here is charged with the triboluminescent spark of a million crashing streams, I can pull myself along the wet rock by magnetic and electrostatic whim, flowing uphill against the local gravity, a ribbon of black velvet ribboning through the clear, bright water. 

As I enter one of the thigh-thick airborne conduits, the fast-moving water catches me and draws me out into a hair-thin wire, thirty, forty, fifty feet long, suspended inside the center of the water-bridge like the dark core of a glass bead. I do not snap. My surface tension is immense, held together by the microscopic grip of my colony. Stretched across the open air between the hoodoos, riding the warped troughs, I become a living antenna.

This is where I truly live: suspended inside the flying water, drinking the stray voltage that the Rill-Spindles miss. 

When a spindle anchors itself at a crash-point and unfurls its clear umbrella to eat the kinetic fury of the spray, its piezoelectric spine leaks a halo of weak current into the surrounding water. I thread my dark filaments right through the spindle's translucent mantle. I don't kill it—to kill the spindle is to stop the current. I simply baste myself through its tissues like dark embroidery thread, acting as a ground-wire, sipping the overflow of its spark and giving it, in return, a heavy, metallic ballast that helps it hold its shape against the bruising force of the water. You can tell an old, well-fed Rill-Spindle because it is no longer invisible in the pools; it is veined with my fine, lead-colored lightning, a ghost with a dark nervous system it didn't grow itself.

My relationship with the Karst-Wrights is more delicate, and much older. They are the architects, and I am the grease in their hinges, the solder in their joints—and occasionally, the phantom in their telegraph.

A Wright’s internal lens of warped water—the captive bubble of broken space it squeezes to slide weightlessly over the stones—is a fragile thing. Over decades, the limestone inside the Wright’s hollow core dissolves, widening the chamber until the internal tendons can no longer grip the water-lens tightly enough. The Wright grows heavy. It begins to drag on the valley floor, its movement slowing from a glacier's slide to a permanent, helpless anchoring. It becomes just another hoodoo, waiting for the water to erase it.

When I feel the grinding, arrhythmic shudder of a dying Wright through the bedrock, I gather myself from a dozen potholes and streams. I flow across the flooded flats in a braided, dark tide, slipping through the ankle-deep water, and climb up into the porous foot of the crippled pillar.

Inside the dark, roaring hollow of the Wright, I coat the worn inner walls. I pack my heavy, metallic silt into the eroded pits of its limestone skeleton, stiffening instantly under the electrical charge of its straining tendons. I become an inner lining of living pewter, shrinking the chamber back to its proper size, giving the Wright’s fibrous cables something slick and dense to push against. 

And while I am in there, wrapped around the captive lens of warped space at the Wright’s heart, I feel what the Wright feels. 

I experience the basin not as a wet, roaring quarry, but as a grand, three-dimensional tension-web of stone and singing string. I feel the weight of the violet sky pressing down on the ridge, the precise angle of three hundred slate splash-plates, the slow, cold satisfaction of a cistern filling with white chalk. 

Sometimes, I stay inside a single Wright for years, riding around in its stone belly as it tends its mills. Other times, when the night falls and the hanging water-lenses begin to glow with their cold, blue-white mineral fire, I grow restless. I leak out of the Wright's pores in a thousand microscopic droplets, let an upward-falling ribbon of water carry me twenty feet into the air, and paint myself across the taut, vibrating cords of the cairns. 

As I coat the mineralized ropes, my weight changes their pitch. 

Down in the flats, a dozen Karst-Wrights will suddenly halt their slow grinding. Their grey-white tendrils will freeze on the slate baffles. Through the floor of the basin, a new chord is ringing—not the sharp, functional note of a shifted stream or a full cistern, nor the alarm of a warm intruder, but a complex, sliding overtone that I have composed by shifting my density along the strings. It is a song that means nothing at all in their language of work and masonry, a pure, resonant daydream vibrating through the wet karst, reminding the stones that the water is not just a machine.
