You would find me forty feet up, where the heat doesn’t radiate so much as it pools, trapped against the sweating oxblood roof of the vault. Up here, the ancient pulley gantry hangs from bolts that have swollen to the size of loaves of bread with red-black oxide. From the split belly of the main I-beam, a heavy hoisting chain drops twenty feet into the yellow-lit gloom before ending in a massive, double-pronged swiveling hook, the size of a butcher’s torso, dangling in empty air above the gap between the first and second condensers.

I am what has colonized the links of that chain. 

If a man could stand on the catwalk below and squint up through the stinging, greasy fog, he would think someone had thrown a ragged, oil-soaked tarpaulin over the hoist centuries ago, and that the fabric had rotted into long, dripping stalactites of black wax. 

I am neither plant nor caul, though I share the silicone-tallow blood of the one and the vulcanized permanence of the other. I am a *dross-weir*—a sessile, colonial scavenger of the vertical dark. My body is a lattice of tough, keratinous black ribbing, grown around the rusted iron links, locking them together into a single, rigid spine. Between those ribs, I stretch wide, translucent membranes of amber-colored grease-skin, arranged in tiered, overlapping baffles like the gills of an overturned mushroom or the louvers of a shutter.

I do not crawl after the food; I wait for the room to exhale it into me.

Every time a steam vent screams below, the thermal updraft hits my baffles. My membranes billow slightly—a slow, oily flutter—and catch the rising drift. The aerosolized rust, the microscopic droplets of boiled transformer fluid, the heavy, falling snow of the rust-colored spores from the organ-stalks: they all hit my slick, amber skin and stick. 

My metabolism is a gravity-fed trickle. The trapped spores and iron dust are slowly dissolved by the hot, acidic grease that coats my membranes. This rich, dark slurry slides down the sloped channels of my ribs, millimeter by millimeter, day by day, pooling at the lowest point of my body—the great, rusted curve of the swiveling hook. There, wrapped around the iron bill of the hook, is my core: a dense, fist-sized knot of pale, muscular gristle, similar to the foot of a caul, which drinks the descending soup.

Because I hang from the gantry, my world is entirely acoustic. The iron beam above me is rooted deep in the limestone walls; it acts as a tuning fork for the entire geological stratum. 

I feel the deep, tectonic shudder of the geothermal vein miles below long before the steam ever reaches the condensers. I feel the slow, crystalline creep of the limestone ceiling as it dissolves and re-precipitates in the acid damp. And through the chain, I feel the cauls. When the great, cast-iron patriarch wedged into the second condenser shifts his weight, the stressed metal of the boiler housing groans; that groan travels through the petrified root-gaskets, into the stone wall, up through the gantry bolts, and down the chain into my spine. I know where every caul is by the way the room's tension changes.

I have no magnetite stones in me, because I do not need to navigate. Instead, I have the hook. 

Over the decades, as I have digested the heavy metals out of the air, I have secreted the waste lead and copper down into the basin of the hook, building a heavy, bulbous counterweight at my very tip. I am a living plumb-bob. When the brassy B-flat of a thinning pipe vibrates at just the right sympathetic frequency, my entire twenty-foot length begins to sway—an imperceptible, pendulum swing of perhaps three inches back and forth, cutting through the stagnant air to sweep a fresh swath of hanging spores into my gills.

I do have one contribution to the slow choreography of the floor below. 

The grease-slurry that reaches my core is never fully absorbed; there is always an excess of refined, heavy oil and undigested, hyper-concentrated mineral salts that my body cannot use. This waste collects at the very tip of the hook’s bill, swelling over the course of four or five months into a single, massive, trembling droplet of black-gold tallow the size of a melon.

The cauls below know it is there. How could they not? As the drop grows heavy, it changes the resonant frequency of my chain. The hum of the gantry above me drops by a fraction of a hertz. 

Long before the drop is ready to fall, two or three of the pale, domed grazers will begin their glacial convergence on the patch of floor directly beneath the hook. They will abandon the boil-cress; they will ignore the bursting stalks. They will creep onto the narrow iron plate between the first and second condensers and press their skirts together, waiting in the sour-yellow pulsing light.

When the subterranean pressure finally spikes and the whole chamber shudders with a pistol-shot *crack* of expanding iron, the vibration snaps the surface tension at my tip. 

The heavy, scorching orb of pure, refined tallow detaches. It falls the twenty feet in a single second—the only fast thing in this entire world—and hits the upturned, leathery domes of the waiting cauls with a heavy, wet *slap* that is instantly swallowed by the roar of the brass throats. And up in the dark, relieved of the weight, my chain gives a tiny, metallic *clink*, and I begin to gather the mist again.
