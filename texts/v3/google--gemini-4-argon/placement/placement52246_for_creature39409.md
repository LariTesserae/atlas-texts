I would not be one of the Lath-Men, though they would know me better than they know the skin of their own forearms. I would not have the jointed knees to drop through the thermals, nor the three yellow shutters to pull down over an eye when the noon glare turns the pit into a white kiln. 

To live in the Drum and not be blown out of it like a spent bladder-fern, you must either be able to hook yourself to the wall, or you must *be* the wall.

I am the third arch of the Seventh Wind-Gallery, forty fathoms down from the salt-rim, on the western curve where the morning light strikes first and hardest. 

If you looked across the void from the old granary doors, you would not see me as a single thing. You would see a sweeping, forty-foot ribbon of cantilevered salt-brick and calcified reed-bundle, bowed slightly outward over the three-hundred-foot drop like the wishbone of an immense, desiccated bird. Centuries of rising gypsum-grit have planed away my cornices. I have no mortar seams left; the mineral resin and dry ash were polished flush with the brick long before the oldest ironwood wheel down in the furnace-wards lost its last tooth. I am smooth as a licked salt-lick, pale as a bleached sheep’s skull, and thinner at the center of my span than the builders ever intended me to be.

I am not dead masonry. In the Drum, stone that is merely dead is quickly eaten by the wind, ground down into flour, and spat out onto the flats above. To remain, a structure must acquire a voice, and to keep its voice, it must have a tension.

My life is entirely acoustic. I exist as a continuous, sustained B-flat just below the range of a human ear—a deep, earthen baritone that never ceases, day or night, century after century. The thermal updraft rushes up from the convex gypsum lens at the bottom of the world, catches the lip of the fifth-tier parapet below me, splits, and pours through the three oval wind-throats pierced through my haunches. 

I am an organ pipe made of baked mud. The wind is my breath, and the tension of my span is my thought.

I feel everything that happens on the western curve. When the sun clears the rim of the salt wastes above, I do not see the light, for I have no eyes, yet I know the instant it strikes my crown. The ultraviolet glare hits the silica-heavy curtains of chime-kelp hanging from my southern balustrade; the leaves stiffen, their static charge leaps upward, and the microscopic crystals in my salt-brick expand with a sudden, sharp *tick-tick-tick* that runs the length of my spine like a shiver. 

All day, I hold the weight of the noisy garden.

The brass-vine roots are my nerves, though they are also my parasites. They thread their dry, coppery filaments into the microscopic pores of my calcified reeds, drinking the vibration I generate, feeding on the friction of the air passing through my throats. When the updraft surges—when a subterranean heat-vent belches a hotter bubble of sulfur-air from the crust below—the thirty-foot ribbons of chime-kelp thrash against my belly. To an outsider, it would sound like a deafening riot of shaken tin. To me, it is a familiar, frantic scrubbing. I feel the tug of every bladder-fern straining upward on its tether-root along my parapet, pulling against my bricks with a tiny, buoyant lift, like a hundred small balloons trying to help me carry my own weight over the abyss.

And I know the Lath-Men. I know them as a cello knows the fingers on its neck.

Around mid-afternoon, when the kiln-glow in the void is at its fiercest and my surface is hot enough to blister wet flesh, my pitch begins to sour. I can feel it happening hours before it becomes critical: a colony of rattle-pod has been swelling in my central wind-throat, catching the airborne gypsum-flour, building a little pale drift of mineral silt in the curve of my airway. The air stops singing through that throat; it begins to buffet and choke. My clean baritone splits into a rough, rattling wheeze. The structural vibration in my eastern haunch grows ragged, setting up a bad harmonic that makes my ancient reed-core ache with the threat of a shear-crack.

I cannot cough. I can only sing flat, and wait.

Then, I feel the *tak*. 

Fifty yards away, where my masonry joins the great vertical buttress of the waterless cisterns, a hooked heel catches a lip of brick. The impact travels through the rigid, bone-white honeycomb of the wall at three thousand feet per second. *Tak. Tak-tak.* 

Two of them are coming along my spine. I feel their flat, stave-like shins clicking as they unhinge and drop from the gallery above, landing on my three-foot-wide promenade with the dry, weightless rattle of dropped bundles of kindling. 

Then comes the best part of the day: the listening.

One of the Lath-Men—an old one, whose left heel-hook is chipped and leaves a distinct, notched signature in my stone—hooks his toes under my parapet lip and presses his keeled, hollow sternum flat against my warm brick. For twenty minutes, we are a single instrument. My B-flat hum passes directly through my baked mud into the thin bone of his rib-box. I feel the dry, pleated air-sacs inside him fluttering as the wind whistles through his collarbone slits; I feel the tiny, rhythmic scrape of his spoon-nail drawing spore-paste across the horny sieve of his snout. 

He is reading my ache. Through his breastbone, he traces the ragged harmonic back to the clogged throat.

He taps his ironwood pommel against my flank. *Tak-tak-tak.* 

His partner drops over my edge. I feel the sudden, sharp cantilever-strain as the first Lath-Man locks his knees and takes the second one’s weight by the ankle, leaning out over the three-hundred-foot glare. Beneath my arch, upside down in the roaring wind, the second one’s silicate-edged blade goes to work. *Scritch-scritch-snap.* The woody base of the rattle-bush gives way. A heel kicks the compacted drift of gypsum-silt loose. 

The debris falls upward—caught immediately by the ferocious thermal column, shooting past my balustrade in a cloud of yellow dust and thrashing pods, headed for the small blue circle of sky above.

Instantly, the wind rushes back through my cleared throat. The ragged wheeze vanishes. My span settles back into its deep, true baritone, vibrating cleanly from buttress to buttress. The relief is so total that a flake of amber mica-skin from the old Lath-Man’s chest stays pressed to my warm mortar as he unhooks himself and clicks away into the thrashing kelp.

I know I am dying, of course. Everything in the Drum is being slowly erased by the very breath that keeps it alive. Every year, the rising grit polishes another millimeter of salt-brick from my belly. My center span is thinner now than the length of a Lath-Man’s forearm. In two hundred years, or three hundred, a thermal surge will hit me while a bladder-fern is snapping, and my reed-core will finally part. I will drop into the updraft—a sudden, silent rain of white chalk-chunks falling down to the convex lens at the bottom, where the wind will grind me into flour in a week and blow me out into the salt wastes.

When that happens, the Seventh Gallery’s chord will lose its third, and the Lath-Men sitting in the dark granary vaults at night, their horned foreheads locked in a ring, will have to change the rhythm they drum upon their chests to account for the gap in the wall.

Until then, I hold the span between the cistern and the stairs. I catch the blinding light and throw it back across the void to the granary doors, and I turn the roaring heat of the earth into a low, steady note that the tuners can lean their ribs against.
