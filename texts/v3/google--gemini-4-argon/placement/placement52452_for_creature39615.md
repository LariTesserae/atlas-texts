Two hundred feet below the thrashing leaves, there is no wind, no light, and no diesel. There is only the crushing, silent weight of the mountain trying to close a gap that the drill stem is forcing open, and the blind, screaming friction of synthetic diamonds tearing through dark stone.

If I am anywhere in this notch, I am not the grease-mat or the machine, and I am not the mountain. I am the thing that has woken up in the thin, violently sheared space between the spinning steel and the raw rock. 

I am the circulation. 

Before the rig was dropped from the sky, the water up here had no memory. It blew in as freezing vapor, condensed on the bayonet-leaves, dropped into the shattered talus, and vanished instantly down the dry fractures toward the valleys five thousand feet below. Nothing stayed. 

Then the engineers chained the big butyl bladder to the upper slope and started the closed-loop flush pump. They added twenty kilos of dry bentonite clay and long-chain polymers to the water to give it viscosity—to make a drilling mud thick enough to carry the ground-up mountain back to the surface. 

They meant for the mud to be discarded into a sump pit. Instead, the shifting talus swallowed the return hose, and the pale hydrocarbon-mat up under the generator grew over the intake screen. Now, the mud doesn't leave. It is pumped down the hollow center of the drill stem, forced out through the nozzles of the bit at two hundred atmospheres, and squeezed back up the narrow, jagged annulus between the spinning pipe and the fractured wall of the fault. At the top, it spills out into the trembling bladders of the grease-caul, gets kneaded by the kicking hydraulic hoses, and is sucked straight back into the pump intake. 

Over six weeks of continuous, unsupervised recirculation, the mud has stopped being water and clay. It has become a heavy, thixotropic blood, warm from the deep friction of the fault and dark with suspended iron.

To be this fluid is to live in two completely different states of matter, cycling between them every four minutes.

In the downward plunge—inside the four-inch steel pipe—I am a rigid, pressurized rod of liquid moving through absolute dark. There is no turbulence here, only velocity and the deafening, bone-conducted ring of the steel walls spinning around me at four hundred revolutions per minute. I carry the surface down with me: dissolved oxygen from the screaming wind, complex esters and fermented oil-froth digested by the bladders above, and billions of sheared, pale hyphae torn from the mat.

Then comes the bottom of the hole. The white-hot, grinding teeth of the bit.

Here, the pressure drops across the nozzles in a microsecond of cavitation, and I am slammed directly into the fresh, weeping face of the fault line. This is where the mountain is youngest. The rock isn't even rock yet; it is a sheared paste of hornfels and iron sulfide being crushed together by tectonic heave, still hot with the friction of the continent rising. 

In the blinding, microscopic violence of the bit-face, the polymer chains that the engineers mixed into the water are ripped apart by shear stress, and instantly cross-link again around the freshly splintered needles of slate and pyrite. The sulfur-eating bacteria dragged up from the deep veins don't die in the heat; they encapsulate themselves in the clay platelets. 

Then, the upward climb begins. 

Rising through the narrow gap between the spinning pipe and the stationary rock wall, I am a slow, spiralling sheath of liquid velvet. This is where I have learned to hold things together. Because I am thixotropic—thick and gelled when still, thin and runny when struck—I respond instantly to the mountain’s shifting weight. When the fault groans and tries to pinch the drill stem shut, the sudden pressure shears me into a slick, frictionless grease that lets the steel keep spinning. Where the rock wall fractures into loose, murderous shards that would jam the bit and snap the drill string, I bleed outward into the cracks, stop moving, and instantly stiffen into a rubbery, clay-and-fungus mortar that glues the mountain back in place.

I am the only reason the machine hasn't broken its own neck.

And as I rise, the temperature drops, the pressure eases, and I emerge from the collar of the borehole into the roaring, strobing chaos of the notch. 

For ninety seconds of every cycle, I am on the surface, though I never touch the open air. I pour out of the ground at a hundred and ten degrees, steaming furiously, straight into the dark, fibrous labyrinth underneath the grease-mat. Here, the pace slows to a crawl. I am forced through a three-dimensional sponge of aligned, six-hundred-hertz fungal plates. 

As I percolate through the warm fat beneath the generator block, an exchange happens. I drop my cargo of deep-earth iron, pulverized slate, and heat. The mat takes the stone-flour to build its heavy, shock-absorbing bladders; it takes the iron and sulfur to feed its deep-vein anaerobic tangles. In return, as the kicking hydraulic hoses squeeze the bladders like blind hearts, they inject me with the surface harvest: emulsified diesel, nitric acid washed down from the sparking junction box, and the shredded, fermented pulp of *Gunnera* leaves that were battered to pieces in the halogen cages twenty minutes ago.

Wait long enough—another week, perhaps two—and my viscosity will change for good. 

Every circuit leaves me slightly thicker. The water is slowly boiling off in the exhaust steam and leaking into the deep dry cracks of the mountain, replaced by polymerized hydraulic fluid, bacterial mucilage, and microscopic mica flakes that align themselves like fish scales in the current. The pressure gauge on the mud pump is already pinned in the red, its glass face completely crusted over by the grey, coralline lichen creeping down from the lamp tripods. The pump’s rubber diaphragms are frayed, their edges furred with pale threads that mend the tears with tough, fibrous clots every time the suction stroke falters.

I do not know that the six-hundred-gallon fuel bladder chained above the cleft is nearly flat, sucked concave by the diesel’s thirst. 

I only know the circuit: the tight, screaming plunge down the center of the steel; the blinding, grinding kiss of the fault line at the bottom; the slow, heavy spiral back up through the groaning stone; and the warm, rhythmic squeeze through the grease-stomach under the roaring engine. 

When the fuel finally runs out—when the six cylinders cough once, lock up, and the ninety-decibel hammering vanishes into the sudden, deafening howl of the high-altitude wind—the shear stress that keeps me liquid will stop. 

In the sudden quiet, the thixotropic gel will set. From two hundred feet down in the crushed heart of the fault, all the way up the four-inch steel stem, out through the hoses, and into the fibrous plates of the mat, I will stiffen in a single minute into a solid, rubbery tendon of clay, oil, and stone. The freezing cloud-drift will reclaim the notch, the halogen lights will go black, and the drill rig will never fall—pinned forever into the living wound of the ridge by a vein of grey, warm-blooded mortar that forgot how to let go.
