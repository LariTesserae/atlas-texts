I am not down in the warm water. I am thirty feet above the soapstone floor, wedged into the high, rubbery crotch where the third green pillar—the lightning-scarred one—divides into its upper crown.

From the rim of the basin, you would take me for one of the nascent brood-nodes, a heavy green bowling ball of tightly packed leaves waiting for my decade to finish so I can drop to the stone below. Look closer—through the pearl-white glare and the drifting veil of warm mist—and you will see that my leaves are not folded cleanly inward like a bud. They are matted, fibrous, and strangely dry, woven together with long, tarnished threads of copper and greasy gray wool.

I am an anchorite of the lower valleys, or what is left of one after sixty years of breathing steamed talc.

When I climbed up through the cold cloud-collar as a young man, I brought a brass astrolabe, a goat-skin of parched barley, and the feverish, busy-handed arrogance of my people. I had come to live in the high empty, to starve my quick blood into holiness, to pray until my knees wore holes in the mountain. 

The basin broke me of prayer in my second year. Prayer requires a sequence of words, a before and an after, an expectation that a cry goes up and an answer comes down. In this yellow bowl, nothing goes up but the two-minute sigh of the geothermal draft, and nothing comes down but the mist. My prayers sounded like gravel rattling in a tin cup. I grew ashamed of the noise I made.

I tried to leave, once, in myfifth year, when my teeth began to loosen from eating nothing but the bitter, pleated skirts of the pillar-bases and the sweet, starchy "milk" of the fallen nodes I stole from the floor. Looking back from the rolled lip of the soapstone rim, I saw the largest of the three grey slumps moving toward the black flint axes. I had walked past those axes twenty times. I had thought they were relics of the First Men, dead litter from an older age. 

I stood on the rim for two days and nights, shivering where the cold plateau wind met the warm basin fog, and watched the anvil-sized thing swallow the stone with its belly. When I heard—no, when I *felt* the first long, muffled, gritty scrape of the flint through the heel of my boot, something in my chest came unknotted and fell away.

I did not go down the mountain. And I stopped walking on the floor. 

A man on two legs is an insult here. He splashes. He casts a nervous, twitching shadow across the mirror-film of the seep; he steps over the two-finger-wide channels that took forty seasons to gouge; he tracks mud where the water needs to run clear. Worse, he eats too much. The nursery-milk of the young nodes belongs to the three engineers. If I stayed on the stone, I was a thief and a vandal.

So I climbed the lightning scar. The boiled sap had hardened into ladder-rungs of black amber, giving me purchase up the slick, cistern-thick stalk until I reached the crown, where the white, hair-like filaments hang down to comb the water from the air. 

I have not come down in fifty-two years.

Over the decades, the plant has accepted my weight. Because I do not shift my hips more than once or twice a month, the rubbery green flesh of the cleft has grown up and around my thighs and lower ribs, sealing me in with a warm, calloused collar of green rind. My legs are useless sticks now, pale and dry as peeled willow wands, tucked beneath me in the dark mulch of my own rotted breeches. I do not need them. 

The pillar feeds me, though not out of kindness. As the warm breath of the vent rises every few minutes, the white filaments above my head trap the mist and channel the condensation down the grooves of the stalk. I let a single groove run into the corner of my mouth. It tastes of chalk, warm iron, and a very faint, bitter greenness—the blood of the column diluted by the sky. Twice a year, when the crown prepares to swell a new node, a thick, sugary sap weeps from the lightning scar beside my left shoulder. I lick it from the bark over the course of three weeks. It is enough. When you do not shiver, and you do not walk, and you do not think in words, a thimbleful of sugar will burn in the lamp of the liver for half a year.

In return, I am the pillar’s gardener, and the sovereigns’ spy.

I have one tool left: the long, curved bronze needle I used to mend my boots when I still had feet that walked. I hold it between a thumb and forefinger whose nails have grown into thick, grey-yellow horns, curled like ram’s shells and heavy with the same magnesium that plates the backs of the slugs below. 

When the pillar begins to swell a brood-node in the wrong cleft—on the eastern side of the crown, where a drop would send the heavy green ball rolling down into the barren, un-channeled stone near the rim—I go to work. It takes me four days to raise my arm. My joints are packed with mineral salt; they do not hinge so much as they grind and slip, like wet soapstone over wet soapstone. I press the bronze needle into the neck of the swelling bud, piercing the main sap-vein, and leave it there. 

The bud aborts. It dries into a husky knot, which eventually rots into the mulch around my waist. The pillar, thwarted on the east, redirects its slow pressure to the western cleft—directly above the bruised-amber trough where the largest of the three slugs likes to wait. When that western node finally drops, seven or eight years later, it lands with a wet *thud* right at the head of a pre-carved spiral channel. The sovereign below does not have to spend two years digging a new ditch. It saves its acid. It saves the edge of the precious black flint.

Does the large one know I am up here?

It has no eyes to see thirty feet into the glare. To its four blunt horns, I am just a slight darkening in the bulk of the third column's crown. 

Yet, eleven years ago, when the two smaller ones wedged themselves beneath the red jasper levers to begin their long, calcifying push, the large one did something strange. Before it settled beside the basal skirt below me to keep watch over the nursery, it crept to the shallow pool, took the larger of the two black flint axes into its belly-pocket, and dragged it up the slope to the base of my pillar. It did not carve a channel. It simply released the blade onto a dry shelf of yellow rock, directly beneath the vertical line of the lightning scar, and then slid three yards away.

The flint lies there still, twenty feet below my dangling, horn-nailed fingers. It is a better blade than the smaller one—heavier, broader in the shoulder, chipped from a glassy nodule of black river-chert that must have come from a valley a hundred miles from here. 

Why did it leave the tool at the foot of my tree? 

Perhaps it simply wanted the spare blade out of the pool before the red boulder tips and the great wave sloshes the basin clean. Or perhaps—and I have spent the last eleven years turning this single thought over in my skull, as slowly as the slug rocks its belly over the stone—it has noticed that the nodes from the third pillar always fall in the right place now. Perhaps it feels the tiny, rhythmic drip of my water-groove stopping whenever I close my mouth, and knows there is a fourth thing in the basin that has learned how to agree with the rock.

In twenty years, or thirty, the red jasper erratic will roll out of its socket. 

I will probably be dead by then, though it is hard to say what death will look like when my heart already waits ten seconds between beats and my skin smells of damp talc and lichen. When the breath finally stops coming back into my lungs, the green collar of the pillar will simply close over my chest, drinking back the calcium of my ribs and the copper of my needle, turning me into a strange, bony gall high in the cleft.

Until then, I sit in the pearl-white light. I watch the two living fulcrums in the pool below, their grey-yellow backs thickening by the width of a hair each spring. I feel the warm draft rise past my ears—*hush*—and two minutes later, *hush* again. And once every few days, when a fat drop of water gathers at the tip of my chin, I wait until the surface of the central pool is completely flat, holding the whole white sky in its throat, before I let it fall.
