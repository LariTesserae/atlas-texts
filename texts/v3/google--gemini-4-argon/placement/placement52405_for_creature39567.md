There is no boundary where my sternum ends and the red clay begins. There is only the gradient of the heat, and the fifty-hertz truth rising through the mud. 

To have a surface is to be separate from the world, and nothing down here under the palmetto roof is separate. The tallow on my back is the same grease that slicks the leather-leaves; the wet clay packed beneath my washer-thick nails is the same baked kaolin cured onto the iron four feet below my belly. When I draw breath, it is not a sharp intake of air, because there is no thin air here—only the warm, suspended soup of the basin's sweat, drawn slowly through wide, muscular nostrils that filter the water from the gas, tasting of crushed beetle-shell, viper musk, and the copper tang of my own hide.

I am low. I have always been low. My ribs are broad, flat arches built to distribute weight, to keep me from sinking too deeply into the slick trough while keeping my spine well below the thermal-sweep of the owls that drift through the dark above the scrub. Above the leaves is a thin, fast world where wings beat and hooks fall. Down here, beneath the braided mats of vine, time is measured in the thickening of grease and the softening of wood.

Right now, the world is a chord played against my chest. 

Through the tight, pale skin of my sternum and the wide drumheads of my palms, the basin arranges itself in concentric rings of pressure. Far out to my left, where the shale slumps into a wide dish, a herd of the heavy, three-toed browsers is shifting. I do not see them; I feel the dense, hydraulic *thud-slump* of their hooves sinking into the wet dirt, the low, rhythmic fermentation of their guts vibrating through the root-mats at four cycles a second. Closer—perhaps the length of three vines away—a knot of blunt-headed boas is digesting. Their muscles contract in slow, peristaltic waves against the earth, a dry, sliding friction that registers in my palms as a faint, rasping static. 

Directly across my shoulders lies a heavy pit viper. It slid onto my back hours ago—or days ago; the dark has no seams—drawn by the heat rising from the pipeline beneath me. It lies wedged into the third pleat behind my neck, its scales hooked lazily against the pale lichen growing in my skin-folds. I feel its narrow, tubular heart beating against my trapezius. It is part of my weight now. When I shift my left forelimb forward an inch, sliding my shovel-palm through the warm mud, I move slowly enough that the snake’s inner ear does not register the acceleration. We are simply a single mound of warm clay, settling.

Higher up on my rump, tucked deep into the deepest, greasiest crease of my hindquarters, a juvenile—my own, or a sister’s, it does not matter—lies flattened against my pelvis. It is no larger than a swollen root-tuber. Its tiny, drum-skinned chest is pressed tight to my sacrum. Every five seconds, when my heart gives its slow, viscous squeeze, the pulse travels through my bone into its belly, braided together with the fifty-hertz hum of the buried iron beneath us both. The small one is learning to read the line. It is learning how to be heavy.

I am moving today because the chord in the ground has a burr in it.

Fifty hertz is a smooth, round shape in the dark. It is a continuous, dark-red warmth that fills the hollow of the throat and keeps the blood thin enough to move without burning calories. Wait on the line long enough, and your own flesh forgets how to shiver; the turbines miles away do the work of living for you. 

But thirty paces down the trench, where a shallow washout has thinned the overburden of clay, the round shape of the hum is pinched. 

There is a high, tight harmonic rattling against my right palm—a woody, fibrous dissonance. A taproot from an old barrel-stem has found a seam in our tallow-ceramic rind. It is wedging itself against the iron, drinking the condensation off the steel, drinking the heat, hardening into a wedge that turns the smooth *thrum* of the slurry into a sharp, localized buzz. If it stays, the buzz will become a crack. The black, scalding blood of the pipe will bleed out into the clay, cooking the vipers, choking the lichen, cooling into dead, brittle tar.

So I flow toward it.

An inch. A pause to let the mud seal behind my heel. Another inch. 

My snout parts the hanging curtains of vine-root. The concave thermal-dishes on my face register the shifting heat-plumes of the scrub: the cool, wet beads of water channeling down the grooved palmetto stems; the tiny, bright friction-points of stacked beetles rubbing their legs together. My snout touches the rough base of a leather-leaf. Without stopping my forward creep, my mouth opens—a wide, horizontal slit—and my tongue unrolls. It is heavy, grey, and coated in sticky mucus. It sweeps up the underside of the stem in one wet, silent curl. 

Three thumb-sized moths and a dozen heavy beetles come away with the grease-film. They do not flutter; their wings are too heavy with damp to beat inside my mouth. My jaws begin their slow, circular grind. The cartilage pads in my cheeks absorb the small *crunch* of chitin, turning it into a soft, internal mash. The bitter, oily meat slides down my throat, mixing with the mineral clay and the sweet honeydew. It is enough fuel for a week of digging.

When my right palm touches the shoulder of another tender, I do not startle. 

I knew she was here twenty minutes ago. I felt the wide, flat shadow of her body dampening the fifty-hertz wave between me and the root-burr. She is older than I am, wider in the beam, her hide cured to the texture of bog-oak, smelling richly of hot copper and old almond-tallow. A blind tick creeps slowly from the flare of her nostril onto the side of my snout, crossing the bridge of skin where our faces touch. Neither of us twitches.

Together, we turn our shovel-hands downward into the warm, red paste.

The digging is not work; it is a rhythm. Push, scoop, fold back. My broad nails slice through the wet shale and kaolin with a wet, sucking *shuck* that is immediately swallowed by the thick air. We do not fling the dirt; we press it into the walls of our burrow, compacting it with our heavy shoulders, sliding deeper into the fever-heat as we near the whale-back of the line. The viper on my neck finally uncoils, finding the vertical tilt of my spine inconvenient, and pours itself off my flank into the warm mulch above, leaving a briefly cool track across my tallow.

Two feet down, the heat is ninety-nine degrees. Three feet down, it is a hundred and four. The juvenile in my dorsal fold squirms once, drinking the warmth, and settles deeper into the milk-patch.

My nails hit the rind. 

It is our own work from seasons ago—a three-inch crust of blackened, tallow-baked clay, smooth and greasy-hard like soapstone. And there, wrapped around the curve of the four-foot cylinder like a tightened tendon, is the taproot. It is thick as my forearm, pale and swollen with stolen water, already prying a flake of our ceramic away from the rusted iron beneath. Where the bare metal is exposed, the fifty-hertz hum is loud enough to make my teeth ache in their cushioned sockets. The iron is sweating orange rust-water, sour and metallic.

The old female presses her chest flat against the exposed steel, groaning a low, sub-audible note of pure relief as the raw heat soaks into her sternum, while I move my head over the invading root.

I open my jaws wide, feeling the cartilage hinges pop softly behind my skull, and fit my blunt back molars around the wood. I do not bite down with force; woody fiber splinters if you snap it, and a splinter makes a sharp sound in the earth. Instead, I lock my jaw-muscles into a static clamp, let the air out of my lungs, and allow my four-hundred-pound body to go entirely limp, hanging my dead weight from the root.

I close my throat and let the alkaline spit come.

It wells up from the glands beneath my tongue, thick and soapy, pooling around the wood. My heart slows. Ten beats a minute. Nine. The fifty-hertz thrum enters through my lower jaw, travels up through the bone of my skull, and hums down my spine to the juvenile tucked in my hip. Above us, in the dark basin, a caiman shifts in its mud wallow; a quarter-mile north, an owl drops onto a sluggish rodent with a soft *thump* of feathers against wet leaves; miles away, in the unseen concrete vaults beyond the rim of the world, a turbine blade spins in its bath of oil. 

Hours pass like water gathering on a wax leaf. 

The alkaline drool eats the lignin. The tough, pale wood under my molars turns grey, then spongy, then warm and mucilaginous like boiled tuber. Without waking from my trance, I begin the rotary grind of my jaws. *Slurp. Grind. Swallow.* The root parts with a soft, fibrous sigh. The sharp acoustic burr in the pipeline vanishes instantly, replaced by the wide, round, dark-red chord of the unobstructed flow.

The old female shifts her weight, nudging my shoulder. Her tongue is already out, licking the orange rust-scale from the bare patch of steel in long, rasping strokes, taking the iron into her blood. I join her. The metal tastes of lightning and old mud, hot and glorious on the flat of the tongue. 

When the steel is licked clean to a dull, sweating grey, I reach up with one broad hand and pull down a hanging mat of palmetto leaf that droops into our trench. I chew it into a green, waxy cud, mixing it with the fine red kaolin at the bottom of the hole and the thick, soapy spit in my mouth. I spit the paste onto the bare iron. 

Then, the best part of living begins.

I roll onto my side, wedging my broad, corrugated back between the wall of the trench and the exposed curve of the forty-eight-inch pipe. I press my pleated hide against the mash of clay and leaf-wax, and I begin to rock. Forward an inch, back an inch, timed to the fifty-hertz pulse. The tallowy sebum from my skin-folds squeezes out, rich with the antibiotic oil of my lichens, blending with the kaolin, smearing into the hairline seams of the iron. The heat of the crude oil inside the pipe bakes the mixture against my skin. We are curing the rind. We are sealing the sun back into its dark tunnel.

Suddenly—before the sound can ever travel through the thick, wet air—the pipe speaks.

Miles away, beyond the slumped bluffs, a massive check-valve slams shut and reopens in some forgotten pumping station. The shockwave races down the buried iron at the speed of sound in steel, outstripping the slurry, outstripping the night.

It hits my spine like a physical blow—a massive, metallic *CLANG* that shudders out of the pipe wall and threatens to rattle the wet clay loose from the trench, to wake the vipers, to send the browsers panicking through the brush.

I do not pull away from the pipe. Neither does the old female beside me. 

Before the first echo of the ring can escape the mud into the air above, my chest expands. The vast, chambered cavities in my throat open wide. The juvenile tucked in my hindquarters flattens its tiny palms against my pelvis, opening its own small mouth. And all along the dark, sweating geometry of the valley—ten yards away, a hundred yards away, three miles away under the impenetrable mattress of the scrub—three thousand broad, pale chests press hard against the earth.

We open our mouths into the dirt, and we hum.

It is the exact, inverted phase of the shockwave—a deep, wet, fifty-hertz sigh that rolls out of our throats and straight down through our drum-tight palms into the clay. My ribs vibrate like tuning forks. The grease in my pleats shivers into tiny standing waves. We take the sharp, industrial violence of the *clang* into our own heavy flesh, absorbing the spike in our fat and our cartilage, and we push back a steady, heavy counter-wave.

The mud does not slip. The vipers above us do not uncoil. On the surface, a single drop of condensation hanging from the tip of a leather-leaf trembles once, holds its tension, and does not fall.

The clang dies in our throats, digested into heat. 

In the dark of the burrow, the pipe settles back into its endless, purring thrum. I slide my chest back over the newly greased patch of iron, feeling the clay rind already hardening under my sternum. My heart drops back to ten beats a minute, locking onto the sub-harmonic of the distant turbines. I am warm. The line is whole. The mud is still.
