I am four tiers down, in the place where the original worm-tubes have been entirely forgotten under three feet of laminated bone. 

My parish is the Sunken Flute—a cluster of three tall chimneys that share a common, collapsed basement, halfway between the boiling center of the basin and the cold lip of the shelf. It is prime territory, though it requires constant vigilance. The main draft does not rise straight up from the rock here; it enters sideways through a fractured aqueduct from the inner wards, already thick with chalk and smelling faintly of the old, sour-milk grease of the deep vents. 

Right now, the water is hot, and I am a ceiling. 

I am wedged into the groin of a low, four-way vault where the aqueduct spills into my central courtyard. My mantle is flattened into a wide, concave dome, mimicking the curve of the ancient plaster so precisely that a porcelain-crab is currently picking its way across my left dorsal flank, its needle-legs clicking rhythmically against the crust of aragonite I have sweated out over the last three hours. It is scraping the green sulfur-scum off my skin. I let it. The scraping is a dry, pleasant tickle, and a clean coat of scum makes the disguise taste authentic to the eels.

I do not think in pictures. There are no pictures in the basin. My mind is a three-dimensional map of pressures, temperatures, and chemical trails, constantly redrawn by the eight flat ribbons of muscle I have laid out along the grooves of the masonry. 

My third left arm—the longest, though it lost its tip to an aggressive matriarch thirty seasons ago—is stretched twelve feet down the eastern corridor, tucked neatly into the mortar-joint between the floor and the wall. Through its horny spatulas, I can feel the whole parish breathing. 

*The eastern chimney is drawing too fast.* A piece of the lip up on the fourth tier must have flaked away during the last warm surge. Because the opening has widened, the suction is pulling the hot draft straight up toward the black wire-kelp on the roof, starving my lower galleries. I can feel the temperature dropping by half a degree along the second-floor ramp. The silverfish ribbon that usually braids itself through my pantry-alcove is thinning out, drifting higher toward the leak. 

I do not unpeel myself. Not yet. The water is still rushing, full of the chaotic, fluttering heartbeats of the shoals and the heavy, sliding wakes of the suede-rays. To move in the warm season is to spend energy fighting the drag, and worse, it is to advertise the shape of my body to the neighbour across the canal. 

Instead, I tighten the muscle at the base of my siphon, holding a thick bolus of lime-glue warm and liquid in my gut, and I wait for the stone to inhale.

Below me, a three-foot tendon-eel slips out of a floor drain. I feel the greasy displacement of its body before I smell it—a faint, iron-rich tang of blood and slime. It pauses directly beneath the arch of my web. If I dropped now, I could have it. My beak clicks once, a tiny, silent twitch inside my mouth-cone. 

*No.* The eel is lean, and if I drop from the vault, I will break the four-inch crust that is currently holding a loose keystone in place above my mantle. If the keystone slips, the vault’s throat widens, and the aqueduct's pressure drops by a third. The architecture is worth more than one eel. A well-mortared trap feeds me for a year; a clumsy kill ruins a hallway for a month.

The eel moves on, threading itself into an old worm-tube like a needle through cloth. 

An hour passes. The crab finishes cleaning my flank and steps off onto the real limestone, never knowing it has just crossed a boundary between mineral and meat. 

Then, the low, hollow *huff* from the deep vents begins to stutter. The bedrock is cooling. 

The change is immediate. The rushing roar of the water pressure—which to my skin feels like a heavy brush being dragged continuously from south to north—softens, wavers, and goes slack. The carbonate snow stops rising and begins to drift straight down, settling in a fine, alkaline silt over the lintels. Within ten minutes, the cold, dense water from the rim comes sliding down the outer avenues like an invisible glacier, pushing a wedge of chill along the floor.

The silverfish scatter downward into the basement Sump. The crabs fold their legs and tuck themselves into the eye-sockets of the skulls I cemented into the wainscoting five years ago. The city goes quiet, save for the faint, brittle *tink-tink* of cooling stone.

I take a slow draught of cold water into my mantle, and I let go of the ceiling.

The crust breaks off my back with a sound like a dry biscuit snapping in mud. A cloud of white flakes blooms around me as I drop—not falling, but pouring myself down the corner pillar like fifty pounds of wet dough. My eight troweled arms spread wide across the marl floor, tasting the dead shells that have washed into the courtyard during the surge.

*Work first. The eastern chimney.*

Before I climb, I need brick. I flow into the pantry-alcove, my arms sweeping through the ankle-deep silt. My spatulas sort the rubble by weight and curvature in a fraction of a second. 
*Snail shell: too round, rolls in the mortar. Reject.*
*Porcelain-crab leg: too brittle. Reject.*
*Here.* The heavy, calcified gill-plate of a large grazer, died two cycles ago and stripped clean by the snails. And beside it, a broken chunk of original worm-tube, six inches long, honeycombed with tiny pipes. Good, light stone that takes glue well.

I tuck the worm-tube and the gill-plate under my web, holding them against my belly with two short front arms, and begin to haul myself up the inside of the eastern flue. 

Climbing a chimney during the lull is like moving through the throat of a dead giant. The walls are coated in a velvet of sleeping bacteria that glows a dull, dying green—I cannot see the light, of course, but my thermal pits sense the faint, greasy warmth of their fermentation. Every few feet, my arms brush over the smooth, bulbous bodies of blind anemones, folded tight into knobs of bruised flesh to keep the cold water out.

When I breach the top of the chimney on the fourth tier, the water is bitter cold and smells of the barren sea-shelf above—a flat, dead smell with no sulfur in it. The black wire-kelp stands slack around the parapet like a forest of dead rubber hoses. 

I flow over the lip and feel the damage immediately. A large suede-ray must have used the chimney-breast as a launching pad during the warm hours; its heavy wing has sheared off a foot-long section of my previous repairs. The gap is jagged, letting the draft spill out into the open canal instead of bending back down toward my middle windows.

I anchor my rear arms around a clump of wire-kelp stalks and pull my mantle over the breach. 

First, I scrape the broken edge clean of bacterial slime using the horny trowels of my right arms—a harsh, rhythmic *schluck-schluck* that sends vibrations fifty yards out into the dark. Then I bring my siphon forward and cough. A thick, ropes-length gout of pale, hot mucus spills over the limestone, smelling sharply of ammonia and lime. Before the cold water can stiffen it, I jam the chunk of worm-tube into the gap, shimming it underneath with the grazer’s gill-plate until the angle matches the curve of my mantle-skirt. 

I hold my body pressed tight against the patch for ten minutes, sweating calcium paste into the seams, feeling the chemical heat as the mucus reacts with the alkaline water and turns to bone. 

While I wait for the mortar to set, I turn my sensory pits toward the canal. 

Twenty feet away, across the sluggish trough of the street, the opposite tenement is humming. 

It is the Young One—a Wright half my weight who moved into the collapsed gallery across the way four hundred cycles ago. It is sitting on its second-tier balcony, and it is singing at me.

*Brrrr-um. Brrrr-um.*

A low, ragged chord of skin-papillae, poorly synchronized. The Young One has been building a baffle out from its balcony—a hideous, clumsy spur of cemented crab-carapaces that juts three feet into the canal. I have felt its wake during the last ten warm seasons. The spur is designed to catch the overflow from my aqueduct and deflect it into the Young One’s basement.

Today, now that I have narrowed my eastern chimney, the pressure in my lower courtyard will rise again. The overflow into the canal will be stronger, and the Young One’s baffle will catch more of it. In a way, my repair helps us both. 

*Keep your spur,* I hum back from the chimney-top, rippling the broad skin over my head in a slow, heavy three-beat measure that shakes the wire-kelp. *Keep your spur, small-mantle. Come an inch closer to the bridge, and I will mortar you into the floor.*

The humming across the canal stops. I feel a faint, nervous spike of ammonia in the water as the Young One flushes its siphon, then the wet *slap* of an arm retreating inside a window. It will not cross the bridge this season. It knows how thick my crust is.

The patch beneath my belly is hard now, warm and smooth as an old tooth. I release the kelp, slide back down the outside of the tower—squeezing my boneless mass through a third-floor window no wider than a man’s fist—and drop back into my courtyard just as the bedrock below gives a long, low groan.

The two hours are up. Deep in the earth, a valve of magma has shifted. 

I do not go back to the ceiling vault. Instead, I flow into the mouth of the blind alley just off the courtyard, the one that receives the newly strengthened draft from my repaired chimney. I press myself into the concave dead-end wall, spreading my eight arms into the fluted grooves I carved here ten years ago. I tuck my siphon away, close my mantle-slit to the width of a straw, and begin to sweat chalk.

Within five minutes, the hot water hits the aqueduct. 

It comes roaring through the stone like a sudden wind through dry trees. I feel it strike the new gill-plate up on the fourth tier, bounce back down the shaft, and pour through my courtyard in a rich, blistering ribbon of sulfur and white snow. The green bacterial mats flare with heat. The snails wake and begin their ten-thousand-toothed rasping. The silverfish boil up out of the Sump, their tiny pressure-wakes pattering against my calcified skin like warm rain.

And right behind them, drawn by the thick, custom-built current, I feel the long, rhythmic undulation of a heavy eel turning the corner into the alley. 

I do not twitch. I do not breathe. I am forty thousand years of limestone, waiting for the meat to come home.
