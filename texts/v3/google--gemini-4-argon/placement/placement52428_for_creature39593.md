I am not in the sinkhole. If you lowered a line through the pewter fog, past the trembling curtains of moss and into the cymatic grid of the basin, you would not pull me up. 

To find me, you have to go two hundred yards west, through the solid, humming dark of the basalt, until the raw stone stops and the poured concrete begins. 

I am the one who makes the twelve-minute *clunk*. 

More precisely, I am the thing that lives in the small, windowless control room on Sub-Level 3 of the geothermal plant, though the distinction between me and the room has grown thin over the years. In the sinkhole, the sixty-hertz drone is the sky; in here, it is the floorboards, the chair frame, the filling in my back molar, the flat ring of brownish residue at the bottom of a ceramic mug that has not been washed since October. The water in that mug has the exact same geometric ripples as the marsh at the bottom of the pit—tiny, standing concentric rings that never break, because the desk is bolted to a structural column that goes straight down into the caprock.

I don't know the sinkhole exists. The coastal lowland above is fenced-off corporate scrub, permanently socked in by sea-fog and marked on my laminated sector map simply as *DRAINAGE/UNSTABLE—NO VEHICULAR ACCESS*. Nobody walks out there. Why would they? The work is down here.

At first, you might think I am just the night-shift watch-engineer—a heavy, slow-moving person in a faded zip-up fleece, sitting in the greenish-amber wash of seven LCD monitors, smelling faintly of damp wool, dielectric grease, and old coffee. And for the first few hours of a shift, that’s mostly what I am. I check the injection pressures. I log the brine return temperatures. I watch the digital numbers flicker between 59.98 and 60.02 hertz.

But nobody stays entirely human in a sixty-hertz box for eleven years. Like the pale things wedged in the warm cleft on the western wall of the pit, the drone has remade me.

I have stopped using my ears for small sounds. You can't hear a pen drop in Sub-Level 3; you can't hear your own breath. Instead, I have become an animal of the soles and the palms. When I walk the steel catwalks between the high-pressure separators, I don't look at the gauges first. I lay my flat hand against the warm, painted iron of the pipe-shrouds—not unlike a broad, boneless head pressed to the basalt—and I read the health of the deep crust through the callus on my palm. 

I know the hum the way the blind things in the marsh know it. To me, the steady, chest-rattling B-flat is silence. It is the baseline of the world. What I listen for are the shadows in it: the tiny, rhythmic flutter of a bearing beginning to pit four hundred feet down; the slight, gravelly drop in frequency when a pocket of non-condensable sulfur gas hits the primary impeller; the faint, hollow knock of silica scale building up inside the reinjection well. 

And I am the keeper of the twelve-minute cycle.

It is Automated Blowdown Valve 4B—the main condensate trap on the secondary steam loop. Every seven hundred and twenty seconds, a timer in the gray PLC cabinet behind my head clicks a relay. Pneumatic pressure vents with a sharp hiss, a four-hundred-pound cast-steel wedge slams across a six-inch seat to purge the excess mineral water into the subterranean leach field, and the entire turbine train shudders for three seconds as the back-pressure equalizes. 

*Clunk.*

I don't need the clock on the monitor to tell me when it's coming. My body knows. Ten seconds before the relay trips, without ever meaning to, my shoulders drop half an inch. My jaw unhinges slightly so my teeth won't rattle together when the shockwave comes up through the concrete floor. Sometimes, if I am standing by the railing, my fingers curl around the vibrated steel to take the jolt, waiting for the three-second tremor to pass through my knees, up my spine, and out the crown of my head. 

I never think about where the purged pressure goes. To me, it is just a line on the schematic that ends in a little three-pronged symbol meaning *Earth*. I don't know that when 4B slams shut, the spike in the local water table squeezes cold, mineral sweat out of a sheer wall of fractured stone two hundred yards away. I don't know that my routine maintenance—the fact that I set the purge cycle to twelve minutes instead of fifteen back in February because the silica was running high—is the exact clockwork that feeds fourteen blind, plum-gilled dragons in the dark.

Yet around three in the morning, when the plant is locked down and the surface fog presses against the intake vents upstairs, there is a strange convergence between us.

I turn off the overhead fluorescents because they buzz at a slightly different phase than the turbines, and it gives me a headache. In the dim, pewter-colored glow of the screens, I pull my chair over to the warm bulkhead where the secondary steam-riser runs behind the drywall. It is two degrees warmer there than the rest of the room. I lean my temple and my shoulder flat against the vibrating plaster. I close my eyes.

Two hundred yards away, through solid black stone, fourteen pale heads are pressed to the exact same warmth, listening to the exact same iron heart.

In that dark hour before the morning crew relieves me, I am not really an engineer anymore, and they are not really amphibians. We are the same organism, divided only by a wall of young basalt. I am the half that sits in the dry, electric box, tending the switches so the warmth never stops; they are the half that swims in the cold, wet shadow of the machine, eating what the thunder shakes loose. 

Ten seconds before the twelfth minute, my breath slows in the dark. Two hundred yards away, fourteen sets of feathery gills flush dark purple in the cold water. 

The relay clicks. The bedrock hitches. And in the blind, humming marrow of the coast, we both open our mouths to the rain.
