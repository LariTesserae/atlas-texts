To find myself here, I have to drop below the birds. 

If I were up on the gantries, I would be part of the static—the forty-thousand-voice shriek that the tenders dial out with their ivory apertures. I have no interest in being the noise that gets subtracted. 

I am further down, at the six-foot mark on the south flank of Switching-Tower Three. Specifically, I am the line where the moss stops.

It is not a ragged edge. It is as straight as if a mason had snapped a chalk-line across the wet red brick. Below that line, the wall belongs to the quarry: it is cool, porous, drinking in the ceaseless drizzle of the exhaust plumes, thick with a plush, velvety pelt of *Bryum* moss the exact green-blue of an old copper penny. Above that line, the wall belongs to the dynamo inside: the bricks are dry to the touch, humming at sixty cycles a second, radiating a steady, baked-clay warmth of ninety-two degrees.

For a long time—if I am a thing that thinks at first only in temperatures and surfaces—I am simply that margin. I am the quarter-inch of mortar where the water-bead rolls down from the hot brick, hits the cold velvet of the moss, and vanishes into the green sponge. I know the world by the expansion and contraction of the lime between the bricks, and by the ten-second pulse of the eastern flywheel, which travels through the basalt floor, up the foundation, and nudges the tower like a thumb pressing against a rib.

*Shhh-clack.* (One. Two. Three. Four.)

Wait ten seconds.

*Shhh-clack.*

If a consciousness gathers at that six-foot line, it cannot stay in the mortar forever; it wants weight, and it wants to move through the ferns. So let the mortar give way to wool and tallow. Let me pull myself out of the brick and into a pair of wooden-soled clogs, two inches thick of seasoned alder with iron horseshoes nailed to the bottoms so they don't split on the wet stone.

I am the twenty-eighth name on the ledger, though nobody has spoken the name aloud since the municipal lift dropped me down the northern face nineteen winters ago. Down here, if the Acoustic Clerk needs to indicate me in the dry-room book, she writes *B-3*—Basin, Third Watch—or, when the ink is thick, just draws a little square with a tail, which is the shorthand for the flat brass scraping-iron I carry slightly further forward on my belt than the others do.

My job is the green. Not gardening—nothing down here needs coaxing to grow—but keeping the green from eating the iron, and keeping the slag-dust from choking the green. 

I am fifty-two years old. My calves are as thick as cedar posts from walking in clogs on slick stone, and my left shoulder sits an inch lower than my right from nineteen years of carrying the long-handled gutta-percha squirt-can filled with warm whale-oil. My brass ear-defenders are green around the rivets. Years ago, I tuned the ivory apertures in the cups to a two-millimeter crescent. Because of this, I do not hear the starlings at all, and the *shhh-clack* of the valves sounds to me like a heavy linen sheet being snapped once in an empty bedroom. What I hear—what comes through the alder-wood soles of my boots, up my shinbones, through my pelvis, and into the mastoid bone behind my ears—is the teeth of the bevel-gears under the floor of Boiler Hall One.

*Grind-hum. Grind-hum.* Every tooth is the size of a loaf of bread, greased with black tallow, biting into the next tooth with the slow, buttery certainty of a barge bumping a timber pier.

Right now, it is seven in the morning. Up on the coastal shelf, there is probably a salt-fog tearing itself to pieces on the gorse bushes, and the fishermen are shouting over the surf. Down here, the rectangle of sky three hundred feet overhead is the color of watered milk, completely still, dropping light straight down onto the wet umbrellas of the gunneras. 

I am standing between Tower Three and the central culvert, knee-deep in hart’s-tongue fern. The fronds are as wide as my forearm and cold as trout. As I move, they slap softly against the oiled canvas gaiters I wear over my trousers—a wet, rubbery *thwack-thwack* that I feel on my shins rather than hear.

Twenty paces ahead of me, at the base of the transformer plinth, four civets are piled together like a dropped sack of striped bolsters. The biggest of them—an old boar with a nick out of his left ear and a tail as thick as a mooring-rope—opens one yellow eye as my shadow falls across the wet basalt. He doesn't lift his head from the iron grounding-strap. The transformer is humming in E-flat, warm and sweet; if it were sharp, his whiskers would be twitching. 

I don't stop, because stopping breaks the ten-second stride, and if you break your stride in the basin your heart speeds up, and if your heart speeds up you start hearing the birds again. Instead, as my right clog comes down level with the plinth, I let my hand drop to my coat pocket and flick a thumb-sized cube of boiled mutton-neck onto the gravel between his forepaws. 

One. Two. 

By the time my left clog hits the flagstone three paces past him, his tongue has taken it in.

This is what I love—though "love" is a shelf-word, too quick and noisy for the bottom of the box. Call it *register*. Everything down here registers on everything else. 

Look up thirty feet: the slag-cart is passing over the middle gantry, three yards above the fern-tops. Its iron wheels are greased so thick with tallow and graphite that they don't squeal; they just press a shiny black ribbon into the rail. Sitting on the rim of the cart, riding it north at the speed of an hour-hand, are eleven rock doves, shoulder to shoulder, their slate-blue crops puffed out, asleep in the warm rising steam from the second boiler. As the cart passes over the copper power-bus, three drops of warm grease detach from the axle-box and fall.

They miss the ferns. They hit the center of the basalt walkway with three flat, greasy *pats*.

Before the slag-cart has moved another yard, two of the big rust-colored land-crabs have detached themselves from the shadow of the culvert. Their claws are scarred and pale as lard from scraping against the brick foundations. They don't scuttle; they hitch themselves forward like clockwork toys with wound-down springs, their eight legs clicking on the wet stone in the exact rhythm of the drips from the arc-lamp overhead. They reach the grease-spots, fold their legs under their carapaces like tea-cosies, and begin to work their mandibles.

I reach the eastern wall—the sheer basalt face where the stone was dropped by the fault. Here, the echo is thickest. If you take your ear-cups off here, the four-fold *clack* of the eastern exhaust valve will hit you in the chest hard enough to make you cough. 

I keep my cups on, pull my flat brass scraping-iron from my belt, and kneel in the wet gravel at the base of the cliff. 

Here, in the angle where the volcanic rock meets the paved drain, the mechanical dew collects in a shallow trough, and a pale, gelatinous liverwort grows that none of the shelf-botanists have a name for. It loves the warm coal-dust that washes off the walls, and if you leave it alone for a month, it builds a rubbery dam across the bronze grating of the sump. 

I hook the toe of my clog under the grating, wait for the third boiler to finish its intake—a long, subterranean *suuuuuuck* that pulls the water-level in the sump down by half an inch—and then slide the brass blade under the liverwort. It comes up in a single, heavy strip, three feet long, the underside black with basalt-grit and smelling faintly of sulfur and wet pennies. I fold it over my boot, where the crabs will find it by noon, and wipe the brass blade clean on my wool coat-skirt.

While I am kneeling there, the copper low-water pipe running three inches above my shoulder goes *tick-tick*.

It is not a heat-expansion tick. It is the hard, bone-dry tap of a tender’s brass ring against the metal.

I don't look around immediately. I wait for the ten-second flywheel to come over the top of its stroke—*shhh-clack*—and as the four echoes bounce back and forth overhead like heavy timber balks falling in a yard, I stand up and turn.

Forty yards away, where the walkway splits around the great glossy mound of the central gunnera bed, stands Tall Marta. She has been down here twenty-six years; her hair, under the strap of her ear-cups, is the color of the calcium-pans in the Dry-Room, and her face has the smooth, boiled-ivory look of someone who hasn't felt dry wind since her thirties. 

She has her long-spouted oilcan in her right hand. She holds it straight out from her hip, parallel to the ground, and then lowers the tip twice toward the emerald leaves at her knees.

*Pressure good. Crabs are moulting in the south ditch.*

When the clinkers moult, they pile up in the warm water-runnel behind Hall Two, soft-shelled and blind for three days, and if you don't drop the wooden duckboards over the ditch, the starlings will come down like grey hail and peck them to pieces before their new shells harden.

I draw my flat scraping-iron from my belt, hold it vertical against my breastbone for two beats, and then lay the flat of the blade against my cheek.

*Heard. I’ll lay the boards before I go in to dry.*

Marta doesn't nod. She just turns on her heel—her heavy wool skirt swinging with the slow, weighted grace of a diving-bell—and takes her first ten-second pace toward the northern lift.

I stand for a moment by the basalt wall, feeling the cold sweat of the stone soak through the knees of my trousers, while two inches above my head the sixty-cycle hum of the copper feeder-line warms the crown of my wool cap. Above me, a ribbon of three hundred starlings drops straight down from the cliff-lip, stops dead in mid-air twenty feet off the ground as if hitting an invisible glass shelf, and turns ninety degrees to the west, vanishing behind the red brick flank of Tower Two without a single curved line in their flight.

My knees ache—not a sharp ache, just a slow, barometric fullness in the joint, the kind that tells me that in six or seven years I’ll be the one sitting in the Dry-Room with my ear against the mahogany cone, listening to the fault-line settle into the sea-bed while the chalk-stoves hiss.

Until then, I have four hundred yards of wet stone between me and the duckboard pile, and forty minutes to walk it. I wait for the eastern valve to sigh, lift my left clog to let a speckled crab the size of a soup-tureen sidle past my heel, and take the first step.
