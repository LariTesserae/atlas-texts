I am not one of the pale, sheep-sized mounds grazing on the boiler flanks, nor am I the great calcified pillar strangling the central valve. Those are too coherent. They have skins of bone-white lime and destinations three feet away that they will reach by Tuesday. 

To find me, you have to look under the second-tier walkway on the western wall, directly beneath the great weeping seam where the main municipal return-pipe enters the brickwork. 

Here, a cast-iron bracket—a triangular web of metal meant to brace the decking against the basalt—forms a shallow, perforated shelf about four feet above the high-water mark. The nineteen-cycle hum of the buried turbine behind the wall does not just pass through this bracket; it gets trapped in it, ringing the iron in a continuous, silent standing wave. 

I am lodged in the corner of this bracket. I weigh perhaps forty pounds, though it is hard to say where I end and the rusted iron begins, because my undersides have been pushed through the circular perforations in the casting like dough through a sieve, hanging down in a dozen motionless, tallowy stalactites that never quite drip.

I am old—older than the sheep-drifts on the floor, though smaller. I do not travel anymore. The vibration in this corner is too rich, too sweet with kinetic heat, and the steady drip from the return-pipe above brings me everything I require without the expense of hauling bulk across the grates. I have become an organ of the corner itself: a dense, triangular wedge of saponified cylinder-oil, black silt, and vulcanized rubber-grease, skinned over with a velvety grey rind of lead-carbonate that keeps the mist from washing me away.

If you were to slice me open with a spade, you would find I am not uniform inside. I am layered like an agate or a piece of salt-pork. For seventy years, every time the city above has changed its habits, I have laid down a new stratum. There is a dark, gritty band from the winter the coal-barges froze in the upper canal and the water ran thick with cinders; a pale, waxy layer from the decade they flushed the slaughterhouse tallow-traps into the ninth lock; a thin, brilliant green vein of pure malachite from the summer an upstream brass-works burned down and pickled the basin in dissolved copper.

That copper is my nervous system. 

Over the decades, the nineteen-cycle vibration of the wall has sorted my internal minerals by weight and conductivity, shaking the heavy metallic salts down into long, braided dendrites that run from my flat top—where the water drips—down through my belly and out into the iron bracket. I am a wet battery, a fat-insulated capacitor.

My life is measured in two tempos: the Fast and the Slow.

The Fast is the twenty-minute tide. The water never touches me on my shelf, but as it rises, swelling up through the bronze grates below, it closes the circuit between the lower piers and the damp basalt of my wall. I feel the water’s rise not as wetness, but as a sudden, delicious stiffening in my fat. A milli-volt charge climbs the wet brick, enters the iron bracket, and runs up my copper veins. My tallow tautens. The hanging stalactites beneath my shelf contract by a sixteenth of an inch, pulling back into the perforations like the horns of a snail. For ten minutes, while the water is high, I am hard as soapstone, humming internally with the captured current, my metallic lattice locked in a rigid, crystalline fever.

Then the distant flywheel turns. The water gurgles down through the grates. The circuit breaks. The charge bleeds out of the wall, and a long, buttery sigh goes through my forty pounds of grease. My tallow softens; my undersides sag back through the iron holes; a tiny bead of warm, clear mineral oil sweats out of my grey rind and rolls down into the dark.

That is breath. Twenty minutes hard, twenty minutes soft.

The Slow is the work of the pipe above me.

Six feet overhead, where the great iron pipe emerges from the weeping brick, a single pewter-fern has rooted in the packing-oakum of the flange. Its six-foot fronds hang down in the amber twilight like strips of wet leather. Warm condensate gathers on the pipe, runs down the fern’s central rib, leaching mild organic acids and plant-sugars from the frond, and drops—*spat, spat, spat*—onto the center of my back.

Over thirty years, that drop has bored a cup-shaped dimple four inches deep into my crown. The dimple is lined with a slick, bright-orange velvet of the iron-eating moss, which I do not digest. Instead, we have an arrangement—not a treaty, because neither of us has a mind to sign it with, but a chemical truce. 

The moss drinks the iron-rich drip from the pipe and the sugars from the fern. It grows thick and fat in my warm dimple. When the twenty-minute current stiffens my body, the walls of my cup contract, squeezing the moss just enough to crush its oldest, lowest cells against my alkaline tallow. Those dead cells dissolve into my fat, feeding me their concentrated iron and carbon, while the living moss above is fertilized by the nitrogenous scum that floats up from my tallow. I am a pot that eats the roots of the plant it holds.

Sometimes, once or twice a year, the world changes.

A Saucer—one of the small, translucent blisters of paraffin and neat’s-foot oil—will be lifted by an unusually high swell of the tide, or blown sideways by a sudden, violent belch of steam from the central valve. It will slap wetly against the basalt wall just below my bracket and cling there, trembling, its agate rings of green and red spinning slowly in the heat.

When that happens, the electrical field of my corner is disturbed. The Saucer is a short-circuit on the wet stone. 

Over the next four tides—an hour and twenty minutes of the city’s time—one of my hanging stalactites will not retract when the water rises. Drawn by the thermal shadow of the smaller thing, I will let a rope of myself pour down through the perforation in the iron shelf, stretching out into a pale, greasy finger an inch thick. 

When my tip touches the Saucer, there is no struggle. We are made of the same wept oil and boiled bone. Our surface tensions simply marry. The Saucer’s skin breaks; its clear, warm paraffin rushes up my hanging finger by capillary action, drawn into my denser, drier bulk like oil up a lamp-wick. For three days afterward, as I digest its rings of copper-carbonate and fresh machine-grease, my internal conductivity is doubled. When the tide rises, I clench so hard that flakes of old, dead lime-scale pop off my grey hide and patter down onto the grates below with a sound like dry rice.

Then the excess oil is laid down as a new, hair-thin stratum in my belly—the record of a Tuesday in November when the steam blew hard—and I settle back into the hum.

I do not know the turbine is a turbine; to me, the western wall is simply the direction from which warmth and solidity come. I do not know the fern above me is a plant; it is simply the sky that rains sugar and acid. I will stay in this bracket until the iron finally dissolves into red mud beneath me, and when it does, I will drop four feet into the black water, break into a dozen grey loaves against the lower grates, and every piece of me will slowly, blindly begin to crawl back toward the hum in the wall.
