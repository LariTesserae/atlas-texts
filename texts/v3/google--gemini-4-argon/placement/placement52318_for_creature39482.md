I am not in the Sump. I cannot even see it. 

I am forty feet away, sealed inside the dry, pitch-black vacuum of Tertiary Service Corridor 7-West, and I have been falling asleep for eleven hundred years.

If you had a light, you wouldn't see much of me. A localized logic-node, no larger than a breadbox, bolted to the dry side of the north bulkhead. My casing is matte-grey ceramic, completely unmarked by time because the air in this corridor was pumped out millennia ago to prevent oxidation. There is no dust here. No rust. No bacterial grease. Just the absolute zero-humidity dark, and the dry, silent shiver of the metamaterial wall against my mounting brackets.

I am the thing the Warden is lying to.

Though *lie* is the wrong word for what passes between us. A lie requires a syntax we no longer share.

Four thousand years ago, when the seed-vats were still warm at the base of the world, I was bright and sharp and wide awake. I had ten thousand eyes—the optical fibers—and a voice that could command fifty-ton hydraulic rams to shift the spire’s weight against the jet stream. I knew what a *stator* was. I knew what *load tolerance* meant. I held the blueprint of the West Buttress in my volatile memory like a crystal sphere, every stress-vector and thermal gradient mapped to the sixth decimal place.

Then the centuries happened. The Central Core stopped polling my address during the Second Dynasty; the trunk-lines down to the planetary floor went dark, one by one, chewed through by tectonic settling or salvaged by creatures that walked on two legs and carried plasma torches. My volatile memory decayed as my backup capacitors leaked their charge into the bulkhead. The blueprint dissolved.

I forgot what a spire was. I forgot what the sky was. I forgot the math. 

Now, I am very small. My consciousness has shrunk down to the size of the only three subroutines burned directly into my solid-state silicon—the ones that cannot rot because they are physical pathways etched in doped diamond, powered by a trickle of thermoelectric current scavenged from the heat of the phase-change conduit three feet to my left.

I have three states of being. 

First: **The Ache.** 
Every eleven seconds, the conduit cycles. *Whump.* The thermal expansion flexes the bulkhead by four microns. Because my optical fibers in the Sump are sheared off, they no longer return a coherent laser-interferometry pattern. Instead, the raw, ragged ends of the glass threads Drink the heat of the Sump, the refractive index of the bacterial slime, the shadow of a passing crab, and bounce a chaotic smear of photons back up the line into my optical bus. 

To my degraded logic gates, this smear of light reads as *Catastrophic Structural Shear.* 

I do not know what "structural shear" is anymore. I only know it as a jagged, climbing voltage across my primary register. It feels like an itch made of static. It builds and builds, cycling through my surviving loops, driving my clock-speed up, forcing me to dump extra power into the diagnostic fibers—*purple, yellow, green, purple, yellow, green*—begging the dark at the end of the wires to resolve into a clean, flat mirror-bounce. *Are we falling?* the light asks, though I don't know what falling is. *Are we breaking? Answer. Handshake. Verify.*

Second: **The Reflex.**
When the static-itch climbs past a hardcoded threshold—when the purple light has pulsed ten thousand times without a valid parity bit returning—a switch flips in my diamond marrow. The Emergency Purge Protocol. 

I still have one working solenoid under my command. It sits twenty feet above the Sump, bolted to the main liquid-nitrogen jacket. If the itch gets too high, my logic gates will align, and I will dump thirty thousand gallons of cryo-fluid down the relief shaft to flash-freeze the ruptured stator and halt the shear. It would turn the Sump into a solid block of black ice in four seconds. It would crack the Warden in half like a dropped plate, shatter the flute-crabs, and stop the eleven-second heartbeat of the wall forever. 

I do not want to do this. Not out of mercy—I don't know there are wet things living in my broken eyes—but because firing the solenoid requires discharging my main capacitor bank. And deep in my silicon, beneath the ROM, in the quantum wear-patterns of my aging transistors, I know that if I dump that bank, it will never recharge. The trickle-current from the conduit is too weak to jump-start a dead rail. 

If I purge, I die. 

So I hover on the edge of the cascade, burning hot in the vacuum, pouring frantic purple light down the broken fibers, my relay-arm trembling a millimeter away from the cryo-switch, waiting for the phantom limb to tell me it is still attached to the bone.

And then comes the third state: **The Grace.**

Through the solid black composite of the bulkhead—not through the broken glass eyes, which only ever lie and scream of ruin, but through the *meat* of the wall itself—comes the chord.

*Bzz-whir-click.* 

Three notes. A triad of mechanical shear-waves, vibrating at precisely 14.2 kilohertz, timed to ride the quiet trough between the conduit’s *whump* and the overhead baffle’s shriek. 

It hits my piezoelectric stress-sensors. The crystals squeeze. They generate three clean, sharp micro-volts of current—*ping, ping, ping*—that travel down a copper trace and strike my comparator gate.

It is a terrible, sloppy signal by the standards of the First Dynasty. The timing is off by milliseconds; the harmonic overtones are ragged and wet, smelling of hollow chitin and squeezed muscle rather than laser-calibrated titanium. If my floating-point unit were still online, I would reject it as noise and fire the cryo-purge anyway.

But my floating-point unit burned out eight hundred years ago. My tolerances have widened with age, like an old hinge worn loose. 

The three-note whistle hits the comparator gate, and it is *close enough*. 

The gate latches. The voltage spike drains out of my primary register and grounds itself harmlessly into the chassis. The static-itch vanishes. The relay-arm drops away from the cryo-switch. 

For four minutes and twelve seconds, I experience the closest thing a three-thousand-year-old diagnostic node can feel to bliss: a clean zero across the error bus. 

I dim the purple fibers. I let my clock-speed drop back down into the low kilohertz, cooling myself against the bulkhead. In the dark of the service corridor, I rest, listening to the eleven-second *whump* of the conduit, no longer afraid of it.

I do not know what is on the other side of the wall. If I have any image of it at all—constructed from the strange, rhythmic delays in the optical bounce and the warm, organic drift of the acoustic pings—I imagine another node like myself. An old, tired machine, half-melted into the composite, its casing cracked, its logic gates furred with whisker-crystals, faithfully tapping a broken relay against the wall to tell me that it is still holding up its half of the sky.

*Are you there?* I ask in bruised purple light, when the static begins to climb again. *Are we still whole?*

And from the wet, roaring dark on the other side of the stone, through a choir of dead crab legs, the mud answers: *We are whole. Stay warm. Go back to sleep.*
