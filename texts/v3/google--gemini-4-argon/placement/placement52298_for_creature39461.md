I am not on the valley floor. The floor is too loud, too crowded with the frantic, twitching heat of a million small hearts, and the air down there is thick with grease and ammonia. 

If you want to find me, you have to go to the eastern wall, where the boy and the woman will eventually carry their sacks of dead birds, and then you have to look up. Past the dry overhang with the chert flakes and the tallow-stained mortar. Past the thirty-foot mark where the swifts’ nests are mortared together like grey honeycombs. 

Stop at forty feet.

Here, a colossal block of pale limestone sheared away from the dark basalt behind it during the first great drop, leaning outward before its base jammed in the rubble below. It left a vertical chimney—a narrow, jagged throat of stone that climbs all the way to the rim, three hundred feet overhead. And right here, at the forty-foot mark, the grey mist-ceiling wedges itself into the gap.

That is where I am. In the throat of the fault, sitting on a chock-stone the size of a butcher’s block, my knees drawn up to my chin, wrapped in a heavy, stiffened hide that smells of something entirely foreign to the basin: woodsmoke, dry sage, and the baked clay of the upper plains.

I am the go-between. I am the one who waits at the waterline where the two worlds refuse to mix.

To the five people down in the shale, the three-day climb up this chimney is an ordeal of weeping eyes and terrifying openness. To the people of the upper plains—the tall, squinting horse-herders who live in the screaming wind and the violent yellow sun—the rift is a cursed hole, a place where the earth’s belly burst open and rotted, filled with a deafening, sunless madness that steals your breath and turns your skin the color of lard. They will not come down even ten feet below the rim. They drop their bundles of fire-hardened ash-wood and river cobbles on a flat rock at the edge, tie their horses a quarter-mile back so the beasts won't spook at the grinding roar rising from the trench, and they wait.

I am the one who climbs down into the grey lid to fetch the black stone and the feathered pelts.

I was born on the surface, maybe, or perhaps in the chimney itself; it no longer matters. Fifty years of moving up and down the vertical crack have bent my frame into something that belongs to neither place. I am taller than the badger-folk below, my leg bones longer, my shoulders narrower, built for squeezing through vertical fissures where a broad chest would wedge tight and starve. Unlike them, I wear boots—thick, clumsy bindings of horsehide stuffed with dry prairie grass—because my soles never grew the grey horn of the basin-dwellers. My eyes are neither wide and black like the boy’s, nor narrowed into sun-creased slits like the plainsmen’s. One of my eyes is cloudy with a cataract from the upper glare; the other is dark and wet, weeping constantly in the fifty-degree damp.

Right now, I am sitting exactly in the mist. 

It is a strange place to exist. From the waist down, my legs hang into the clear, pewter twilight of the rift. I can look down between my boots and see the top of the eastern overhang, the swirling vortices of swifts banking ten feet below me, and the tiny, lichen-colored lump of the old woman sitting in her limestone notch four hundred yards out. From the waist up, I am submerged in a blind, clammy wool of water vapor. If I lean my head back and look up the chimney, the mist thins after twenty feet, and high, high above—a tiny, jagged ribbon at the top of a black tube—I can see a slice of sky so fiercely, achingly blue it makes my good eye throb.

I have been sitting on this chock-stone for two days. Or two nights. Down here, time is only measured by how cold my thighs get before I have to flex the muscles to bring the blood back, and by the slow, rhythmic *drip-drop* of wall-sweat hitting the flat crown of my stiff leather hat.

The roar of the birds comes up the chimney like wind up a flue. It vibrates in my teeth. Up on the plains, the silence is huge and hollow, broken only by the dry hiss of grass; when I am up there, my ears ring with phantom chattering, and I cannot sleep without the weight of the noise pressing on my chest. Down here, the noise is a blanket. I lean my cheek against the cold, sweating basalt and let the ninety-decibel static wash the surface thoughts out of my head. 

Wait long enough in the mist, and you become mostly stone yourself. 

Below me, through the grey soup, a sound cuts cleanly through the dry shingle-roar of the swifts.

*Clack. Clack-clack.*

Chert on chert. It is not the old woman calling the boy. The resonance is deeper, struck inside the hollow acoustic shell of the eastern overhang directly beneath my boots. They have returned to the shelf. They have seen the three tiny pebbles of red surface-jasper I dropped onto their flint-knapping circle yesterday—the sign that the seasonal trade is waiting in the chimney.

I do not yell down. My voice dried up years ago into a rusted hinge, used only for haggling with the plainsmen in three-word grunts. Instead, I reach into my coat, pull out a length of dry, fire-hardened oak—a piece of surface-wood resonant and dense—and strike it once against the limestone wall.

*Thock.*

A wooden sound. A sound that does not exist anywhere in the four-mile basin of bone, feather, and rock. 

Down on the shelf, forty feet below my dangling boots, a pale, broad-shouldered shape detaches itself from the grey gravel. It is the older woman. She does not look up to find me; she knows the chock-stone where I perch. Across her back is a bulky, square frame of willow roots, packed tight with hundreds of cured, greased swift-pelts—the finest insulation the horse-people on the plains can get for their winter linings—and slung around her thick neck is a heavy leather sack of prime black chert nodules, the glassy cores already tested for flaws.

She puts one bare, black-calloused foot into a vertical seam in the basalt. Her splayed big toe hooks a quarter-inch ledge. Her short, thick fingers find a hold above her head, and she begins to lever herself up the sheer wall toward the mist, moving with the slow, unhurried certainty of a beetle climbing bark.

I shift my weight on the chock-stone, reaching behind me to untie the bundle of ash-wood shafts and river cobbles I hauled down from the blue sky yesterday. 

Soon, her greased, feather-fringed hood will break through the bottom of the cloud layer, five feet below my boots. We will not look each other in the eye. We will not speak. We will pass the heavy bundles across the narrow gap of the chimney, our hands brushing—hers cold, oily, and smelling of raw bird-meat; mine dry, scarred, and smelling of horse-sweat and sun-baked dirt. 

Then she will sink back down into the pewter gloom and the unending roar, and I will turn my face toward the blinding blue crack overhead, jamming my booted feet into the fault-line, beginning the long, aching haul back up into the wind.
