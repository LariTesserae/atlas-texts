My forehead is cold, and then it is warm, and then for three minutes it is the exact temperature of the wood it is pressed against.

I am eighty feet down, on the underside of the third eastern arch—the one that takes the weight of the Silo of the Seven Sisters when the sun crosses the meridian. My knees are drawn up into the carved flute of the buttress, my gouge-nails hooked into a crescent of four-hundred-year-old adze-marks that have grown dull and velvety with bark. If a char drifted past my ribs right now, it would not swerve until its lateral line touched the faint, warm ribbon of water still rising from my left gill-vent.

I am listening to a knot.

Not a rope knot. A wooden one, deep inside the joinery where the arch’s lower tenon enters the mortise of the Sister-pillar. Two winters ago, a rock fell from the dry mountain above the lake—a rare thing, an intrusion from the thin, howling country where water is hard and white—and struck the upper parapet of the Sister. It did not break the wood; nothing breaks the wood. Wait—no, it bruised the cambium thirty feet above me. Since then, the downward sap has favoured the northern cheek of the tenon. The southern cheek is starving by the breadth of a snail’s shell every season.

I have not opened my eyes since the light was white. Now, through the skin of my eyelids, the water has turned the thick, yellow-green of midday, which means the surface ten fathoms up is taking the heat.

Wait.

First, the snails. Their clicking always thickens when the light comes straight down. Millions of tiny calcium hammers striking slate-bark, so many that they stop being discrete sounds and become a texture in my shins, like fine gravel rubbed against bone. Against that white noise, the town stands up in my skull. I can feel the empty cylinder of the central gulf behind me—a great column of silence where no snails are—and on the far side of it, the western terraces, slightly fuzzy because three shoals of char are hanging at mid-depth, absorbing the high frequencies with their silver flanks.

Then the first hound-toad on the sunny rim lets go.

*Bong.*

It hits the crown of my head, travels down my spine, and goes into the wood between my feet. The wood gives it back to me a fraction of a second later, slightly flattened. Then another toad answers from the lower cisterns—a heavy, old female whose pitch has dropped half a tone since the spring thaw—and then sixty more join in, until the water is a solid block of bronze that presses my lips against my teeth.

To an outsider, this is noise. To my forehead, this is the lantern being lit.

Because the wood conducts the bronze notes ten times faster than the water does, the booming of the toads arrives in my brow before it arrives in my ribs. By the tiny smear of time between the two, I can see inside the pillar. I can see the grain lines sweeping up toward the high parapets; I can see the hollow core where the warm chimney-water is just beginning to slide downward toward the nurseries; I can see the dark, dense lens of an old bone-wedge my grandmother’s brother drove into the heartwood eighty years ago, still sitting there like a pebble in a stream, dividing the sap.

And now the sun-current reaches the upper suspension bridges.

The braided root-cables take the push. Ten fathoms above me, the great parapet leans eastward by the width of two fingers. The load travels down the Sister-pillar, spirals through the grain, and hits the arch where I am wedged.

*Hnnnnn—*

It is not a sound in the water; it is a lengthening in my femurs. The mortise clenches. The northern cheek of the tenon takes the weight with a long, oily slide that feels like grease rubbed between two palms. And there—right at the bottom of the groan, tucked underneath the low C-sharp of the old female toad in the cistern—is a dry, high-pitched tick.

*Tk.*

A hairline void in the southern cheek. No wider than a split hair, three inches deep inside the living joint, where the starved bark failed to kiss the pillar.

I do not move. Moving right now would waste the heat I have gathered in the fold of my belly, and besides, the arch will speak again in four minutes when the eddy rebounds from the western wall. A poor carpenter strikes on the first groan. A poor carpenter thinks the tree only has one thing to say.

While I wait, a hound-toad the color of wet river mud pulls itself up the vertical flute beside my hip. Its broad, webbed hands pad over my thigh—*pat, pat, pat*—and it settles its heavy belly into the warm hollow between my shoulder and my jaw. Its cold, warty skin drinks the heat coming off my neck-blubber. I let it. Its throat-sac swells against my cheek like a filling bladder, tight and rubbery, and when it releases its note, the concussion goes straight through my zygomatic bone and makes the water inside my gill-chambers shiver into microscopic pearls.

With my left thumb—not lifting my forehead from the tree—I reach up and find the small, soft depression just behind the beast’s rigid eardrum. I press once, gently, on the vagus knot.

On its next breath, the toad’s note drops a fourth and stutters twice at the end. *Bong—ong-ong.*

Across the bright, empty gulf, three hundred feet away on the western rim, someone will feel that stutter in their shins and know that I am still on the third arch, and that the southern cheek is dry.

A minute passes. Two. My chest expands; the spiracles behind my jaw open with a slow, greased suction, drawing the four-degree water in over my gill-trees. The cold hits the core of me like a swallowed stone, fierce and clean, buzzing with mountain air. I hold it while the copper in my veins turns bright, dark blue, and then I let it go through my side-vents—a long, silent ribbon of warmth that the toad shifts its hind legs to catch.

*Hnnnnn—*

The rebound current hits the bridges. The arch leans back.

*Tk.*

There it is. Two inches lower than I thought, and angled toward the core-chimney.

Now I open my eyes.

The world rushes in, ridiculously sharp. Every time I open my eyes after a morning of listening, I am mildly startled by how small things look when you only use light to find them. Across the gulf, the western apartments are carved like ivory boxes in the greenish-gold block of the lake. Above me, a cluster of six wine-cask lanterns strains upward on its kelp tethers, the trapped oxygen inside them gleaming like quicksilver where the noon sun strikes their domes. A shoal of char hangs ten feet below my toes, every scale on their backs outlined in crisp, black shadow against the pale wood of the avenue beneath them.

And drifting down through the middle of the shoal, turning slowly like a dropped spindle, is Old Mere.

She must have stepped off the high sun-parapet two minutes ago. Her arms are folded over her thick chest, her gouge-toed feet neatly crossed at the ankles, her olive-black skin mottled with the white scars of old snail-bites. The char do not part for her so much as they simply cease to be where she is, sliding aside with a single, lazy flex of their tails as her heavy bulk shears down through their ranks. As she passes my level, twenty feet out in the open water, her slit pupils roll toward me.

She does not wave—to wave an arm is to spin oneself out of the drift. She simply strikes her throat-bones together once.

*Tock.*

The click cuts under the bronze roar of the frogs, sharp and dry as a snapped twig. *Where?* the pitch asks, high and tight at the back of the palate.

I do not take my forehead off the wood. I strike back, slackening my hyoid so the click echoes twice against my own collarbone before leaving my neck. *Tock-ock.*

*Deep. South cheek.*

She does not answer. She is already below my knees, still falling on her long diagonal toward the benthic storehouses, where the fossilized char-ribs are kept soaking in mineral silt. She will bring me a number-four wedge before the sun leaves the water, or else she will bring it tomorrow, which is the same thing.

Until then, I have a small shoot to move.

I unhook my right hand from the adze-scar, reach down to the braided kelp string on my thigh, and slip my flat thumbnail under the head of a small, green bone-pin I set here when the water was still dark under the winter ice. The bark has already lipped up around it, greedy and smooth, like tallow melting over a coin. I do not pull it out; I only lever it sideways by the width of a grass-blade, changing the angle of the bruise so the tree’s slow blood will turn left instead of right on its way down to the starved mortise.

The wood sighs under my nail—a tiny, wet *tsk* of parting fibers that nobody in the basin hears but me and the toad on my shoulder.

I hook my fingers back into the scar. I let my eyelids fall shut, cutting off the small, bright distraction of the light, and lay my brow back against the stone-cold slate of the cedar, waiting for the bridge to lean again so I can hear if the tree has understood me.
