My left heel is three seconds old. My right heel is not yet born. 

Wait—now. *Clack.* 

The petrified fungal-wood of my stilt bites into a skin of newly cooled basalt. The stone is black, glossy as grease, and hot enough that even through two inches of mineralized stalk and the thick pad of cured flyer-hide lashed to my shin, I feel the dull, rising push of it. Before my weight has fully settled on the right leg, my left is already lifting, knee cocked high toward my chest like a mantis praying over a kill, and the long ironwood pole in my four-fingered grip has stabbed outward to test the next yard of yellow crust.

*Ping.*

A bad sound. High, tight, like a stretched gut-string about to part. That yellow shelf is an eggshell over a cauldron of brine. I don’t look at it—the slit-goggles of chipped obsidian over my eyes only let in a narrow, cross-hatched band of the glare anyway, turning the blinding aragonite terraces into flat grey ribbons—I just twist my hip in mid-air, let the wicker frame on my back swing its eighty pounds of copper bowls and rattling crab-shells to counterbalance me, and drop my left stilt onto the back of a sleeping salt-dome instead.

*Thud.* Heavy. Solid. Good for four breaths.

I am Krr-t, which is the sound a flint scraper makes when it hits a nodule of iron pyrites inside a softer chalk. I am forty seasons old, which is ancient for the floor, and my back is beginning to curve to match the wicker arch of my pack. My knees never fully straighten anymore; even when we haul ourselves up into the cliff-sacks at night and unlace the gut-bindings from our shins, my legs stay folded like a dead spider’s, twitching in my sleep whenever the basalt wall behind my shoulders hums with a deep-earth tremor. 

Today I am walking the Seam-Edge, fifty yards out from the eastern cliff-shadow, with two Riggers and a boy who is still light enough to bounce when the steam-blisters pop. 

Ahead of me, Tss-k—she is young, her carapace-apron freshly sewn with the bright, translucent blue shells of deep-vent crabs—throws her left pole horizontal, level with her snout-mask. *Stop-and-listen.*

We don't stop. If you stop, the heat climbs your stilts; if you stop, the thin stone learns your weight and gives way; if you stop, the river of six-legged bone-clickers moving past your ankles decides you are a spire of rock and climbs your shins to scrape the salt off your leather. Instead, we do the three-step weave: left, right, pole-tap, hop-back, left, right. Four giant birds with wicker hunchbacks, dancing in place on a ledger of porcelain that is actively hissing through a thousand pinholes.

I jam the butt of my tapping-pole into a crease between two black glass pillows and lean my head down, pressing the wide, flat bone of my cheek-guard against the wood. 

When you close your eyes in the Caldera-Trench, the world doesn't go quiet; it changes shape. Through the dense grain of the ironwood, past the leather of my mask and the wet charcoal-and-puffball mash rattling in my snout-filter as I breathe, the ground talks into my jawbone. 

At first, there is only the surface noise: the *shhh-k-shhh-k* of a million crab legs scraping orange slime fifty yards away, and the sharp *tink, tink, tink* of cooling glass cracking in the sun. Beneath that is the mid-water—a wet, rhythmic *glug-thump*, like boiling grease in a copper pot. That’s the violet mud thirty feet down, rolling over in its sleep. 

Then I feel what Tss-k felt. 

Way down, where the rock isn't rock yet, something is rising. It doesn't sound like water or mud. It sounds like a heavy, rough-skinned beast dragging its belly up a narrow chimney. A slow, grinding *hmmmmmm* that makes my back teeth itch in their sockets. And there—a sharp, metallic *twang* deep in the floor, like an anchor-spike pulling loose from the cliff. 

A copper-burp. A big one. The crust three hundred yards toward the center is about to turn inside out, and when it comes up, it won't be throwing just salt-slush and sulfur. It’s going to vomit green-veined slag and hot native metal before the aragonite water drowns it.

I pull my cheek off the pole and snap my head up. I don't reach for the whistles at my belt—the air is too thick with the roar of a nearby geyser-field, and besides, two other clans are working the high nets half a mile north. If they hear the *heavy-metal* call, their Riggers will come vaulting through the steam before the crust has even hardened enough to hold a stilt.

Instead, I cross both poles over my head—*X*—then drop the right tip three times toward the center of the basin. *Deep. Rich. Three minutes.*

The boy tethered to Tss-k’s waist—his name is not yet settled, we just call him *Little-Mallet*—gives a short, delighted bounce on his short stilts, his small basket of clogged bone-whistles clattering against his ribs. Tss-k doesn't bounce. She reaches back, unhooks the heavy, fire-hardened digging-spud from her frame, and checks the knot on the boy’s twenty-foot gut-line with two scarred fingers.

We go.

Moving toward a rising blister goes against everything your body wants. The air gets thicker, wetter, smelling less like rotten eggs and more like a smith’s quench-trough—hot iron, wet chalk, and the sharp, sour bite of acid-steam that makes the charcoal in my snout-cone hiss as it drinks the poison. The crabs know what’s coming; they are already flowing past us in the opposite direction, a solid, clicking sheet of amber shell moving downhill toward the older slate shelves. They hit the bottoms of our stilts like fast river-gravel—*clack-rik-rik-clack*—trying to go over our feet rather than around them. 

We bound in long, lurching arcs, using our poles to vault over the wider fissures where the pink salt-crust is already weeping yellow foam. One-two-vault. One-two-vault. My lungs burn—not from the sulfur, the wet puffball mash takes that, but from the heat of the air itself. Inside my greased leather mask, sweat runs in familiar tracks down the map of old scalds on my chest, pooling at the waistband of my leather apron before dripping out through the eyelet-holes onto the hissing stone below.

Ahead, the landscape is losing its memory. 

A tall, crooked spire of white aragonite—three weeks old, its sides thick with the grey, petrified warts of dead puffballs and a writhing coat of four-foot armored lizards—suddenly leans ten degrees to the left. There is no sound of breaking rock; its base has simply gone soft as warm tallow in the rising heat. All at once, the lizards let go. A hundred of them launch outward into the yellow haze, their ribbed side-flaps snapping open with a sound like wet hide being shaken out, gliding low over our heads as they flee toward the cliff-spires. One of them clips the top of my wicker arch with its heavy tail, knocking a string of live crabs swinging wildly against my ear, before it catches a thermal and spirals up into the roaring soup of flyers above.

"Krr-t!" 

It’s not a word; it’s a two-note screech from the bone-whistle tucked into the corner of Tss-k’s snout-mask. She points with her pole. 

Twenty yards to our right, at the base of the tilting spire, the white stone isn't white anymore. The aragonite has split, revealing a core that looks vaguely, stubbornly like the curve of a hip-bone and a long, mineralized stilt-shaft. 

Old Kr-chak. We lowered him from the ropes two moons ago, when his lungs got too thick with grey-rot to pull air through a filter. He’d been a Listener for fifty seasons; we’d set him right over a fast lime-vent so he’d grow tall and broad enough to hold the main guy-line for the autumn net-rigging. Now the earth is taking him back before the autumn flyers have even thickened. As we vault past, the white crystal pillar that was my uncle’s brother slides smoothly, upright and dignified, down into a widening ring of violet porridge. He doesn't fall; he sinks, like a hot stone into lard, until only one crystalline arm—he’d died with it raised, the way Listeners do—stays above the mud, hissing as the purple slime boils around the wrist.

No time for the whistles. No time to tap a farewell on the stone. If you stop for the dead, you join the terrace.

*CRACK-THOOM.*

Fifty yards ahead, the flat grey crust swells into a six-foot blister, holds for one heartbeat like a drawn breath, and then splits in a three-pointed star. 

It doesn't explode upward like a steam-geyser; it peels back like the skin of a roasted fruit. Out of the split rolls a sluggish, incandescent tongue of dark-green mud, shot through with blinding, orange-hot clots of pure, unoxidized copper and ropes of black glass that stretch like taffy as the stone-lips curl apart. The heat hits us like a swung timber, drying the grease on my leather mask in an instant. 

Above us, the sky instantly darkens. The flyers know that a rupture brings up the deep-spores and kills the crabs by the thousands. They drop out of the high glare in a screaming, leathery funnel, their four wings beating so hard the hot air buffets us downward. A three-foot male, its beak-sheath notched from old fights, misses my shoulder by an inch, snatches a scalded, wildly kicking crab off the curling lip of the rupture, and banks back into the steam before its wing-tips can scorch.

We don't wait for the green tongue to stop moving. If you wait until it’s cold, the aragonite vents around it will wash it in lime-water and lock the copper under six inches of stone before nightfall.

Tss-k and the other Rigger, a broad-shouldered mute we call *Two-Hammer*, dance right up to the curling lip, their stilts clattering on rock that is actively trembling like the flank of a winded runner. I stay back ten paces, my pole jammed into a cooler seam, my cheek glued to the wood, listening to the beast under the blister. 

*Still groaning. Still heavy. One more heave coming, maybe two, before the chimney chokes.*

I hold up my left hand, three fingers spread wide. *Ninety seconds.*

Two-Hammer doesn't use a pick. The rock isn't rock yet; it’s leather-hard taffy. He swings a long wooden pole tipped with an obsidian cleaver, hacking into the cooling green margin where the orange clots are dimming to a dull, cherry red. *Chock. Chock. Chock.* With every strike, the wet ironwood of his pole smokes, smelling briefly, weirdly, of the upper-world forests none of us have ever walked in. Tss-k follows right behind his blade with a pair of four-foot tongs made from two giant flyer-wing bones hinged with a copper rivet. She plunges the bone-tips into the blistering dough, twists, and rips out a hunk of ragged, spongy native metal the size of a baby’s head, trailing hair-thin threads of spun glass that snap and drift away on the updraft.

She doesn't put it in her pack—it would burn straight through the wicker and roast her spine. She tosses it backward into a shallow puddle of bitter brine six feet behind me. 

The puddle explodes in a blind white shriek of steam—*SHHHHHH-K!*—as the metal quenches itself, spitting salt-crust over my shins. Little-Mallet is there before the steam clears, hopping from foot to foot on his stubby stilts, using a wooden ladle to flip the blackened, hissing lump of copper out of the water and into his slung copper pan.

*Clack-shift-clack.* My left heel is hot. I hop three inches to the left. 

Through the pole against my jaw, the low *hmmmmmm* suddenly changes pitch. It drops an octave, then stutters—*thump-thump-glug*. The magma blister has emptied itself; the roof of the chamber twenty feet down is now hanging on nothing at all, held up only by the thin arch of cooling crust we are dancing on.

Then, a tiny, silky sound in the wood against my cheek: *ssssss-t*. Like wet linen tearing.

I don't whistle. I bring my second pole down across the shaft of the planted one with all the strength in my corded arms. 

*CLACK!* 

The sound cuts cleanly through the roar of the flyers and the hiss of the brine-quench. 

Two-Hammer doesn't finish his down-stroke. He lets go of his cleaver-pole entirely—the obsidian head is wedged tight in the cooling slag, and a tool isn't worth a leg—spins on one narrow stilt-heel, and grabs the front loop of Little-Mallet’s wicker harness. Tss-k is already airborne, vaulting backward over the brine-puddle with her bone-tongs still smoking in her fist.

I pull my pole from the seam—a thin jet of yellow gas immediately whistles out of the hole it leaves behind—and bound after them, taking ten-foot strides down the slope of the blister. 

Behind us, the earth doesn't explode; it inhales. 

With a long, wet, whistling sigh, the entire forty-foot dome of green slag, black glass, and half-cooled copper drops straight down into the dark. The air rushes in to fill the hole, pulling at the skirts of my carapace-apron, dragging a dozen low-flying locust-drakes down into the sudden pit before they can untangle their four wings from the downdraft. Where we were standing three heartbeats ago, there is now a sheer-walled well of boiling grey mud, already throwing up lazy, six-foot bubbles that burst with the smell of old bones and hot vinegar.

Fifty yards away, on a wide, flat shelf of three-day-old slate that feels as dead and reliable as the cliff-wall itself, we fall into the slow weave again. Left, right, tap, shift.

Little-Mallet is laughing inside his snout-mask—a wet, muffled *huff-huff-huff* through the charcoal—and tapping his stone hammer against the three fat lumps of raw copper sitting in his pan. They are still warm enough to keep the condensation from settling on his knuckles. Two-Hammer is already reaching into his back-frame for a spare obsidian head and a wet strip of raw gut to lash a new cleaver together while he hops from foot to foot.

I look up, tilting my head far back so the narrow slit of my goggles clears the drifting banks of yellow steam. 

Three hundred feet above, the rim of the world is a hard, black line against a sky so pale and blue it hurts even through the smoked glass. Just below that line, in the blue shadow where the sun never quite reaches, hang the Pendulums—thirty fat, salt-whitened sacks swaying slightly in the afternoon chimney-wind, waiting for the dark. 

My knees ache with a deep, salt-crusted fire, and my snout-filter is getting soggy with my own breath, tasting faintly of the mushroom-flesh we packed it with at dawn. We have four hours of light left. Half a mile to the west, a fresh column of white steam has just punched into the glare, straight and tall as a pine tree, which means a new geyser has broken through the old travertine beds, and within twenty minutes the puffballs will be swelling along its skirts as thick as sheep on the upper-world hills.

I tap my pole twice against Tss-k’s shin-guard to get her eyes, point the charred tip toward the new plume, and lean forward until my weight goes out over my toes, falling into the next stride before the stone beneath me can remember I was ever there.
