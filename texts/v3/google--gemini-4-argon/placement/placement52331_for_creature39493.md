I am in the back of the third-course slot, behind the Clerk’s heavy anchor-foot, where the light has never reached. 

Actually, I am the slot’s lining. Or rather, I am the thing that happens to the lining when the air is that still and the limestone is that new.

For three years—or three hundred mornings; time here is only measured in the thickening of the wall-felt—the Clerk has been spitting its dry, neat pellets of chewed *Monstera* root and fig-pith against the raw interior stone of the cavity, pressing them flat with its wooden-float pads to deaden the echo behind its back. It thinks it is making wallpaper. It does not realize it has been building a battery.

I began as a spore carried in on the belly-scales of the first copper-tailed skink, or perhaps I was already sleeping in the dry clay at the bottom of the pier before the masons laid the granite plinths. In the outside world, my kind is a brief, frantic smudge of white rot on fallen timber, washed away by the first autumn rain. We have no memory there. We eat, we fruit, we dissolve.

Here, there is no rain. There is only the unvarying, jar-warm breath of the shaft, the dry lime-flour of the split blocks, and the steady, rhythmic additions of chewed cellulose, slightly salted with the Clerk’s digestive lime and the powdered ear-stones of geckos. 

Because nothing ever washes me away, I have not needed to fruit. Because I have not fruited, I have not died. Instead, I have grown dense, leathery, and monumentally slow, threading my pale hyphae through the compacted root-pellets until the back eighteen inches of the missing-block cavity is a single, dry, chamois-soft cushion of mycelium the color of old bone.

At first, I was only a sponge for the Clerk’s weight. When it backed in at dawn, sliding its squared flanks home like a drawer, its rear anchor-foot would press against my surface. It liked the give of me. I absorbed the faint, sour dampness of its skin so that no mold grew on its hide, and in return, I drank the trace salts and urea from its resting-press.

Then I found the joints.

The pier is dry-stacked. Between the dressed blocks of the inner core, where the masons left no mortar, there are hairline fractures—flat, pitch-black planes of zero-space running thirty feet up to the roots of the figs, forty feet down into the cold clay beneath the granite, and forty feet across to the northern corner where the *Monstera* clings.

The Clerk cannot enter a crack narrower than a fingernail. I can flatten myself to the thickness of a soap-bubble’s skin.

While the Clerk sleeps through the olive-tinted day, plugging the doorway with its false stone face, I travel the interior of the masonry. My filaments—fine as spider-silk, dry as chalk on the outside, pressurized with slow water within—creep through the unmortared seams. I am the mortar the builders forgot to mix. I have mapped the inside of every block by the split halves of the fossil shells hidden in the dark. I know where a mason’s chisel slipped and left a buried flake of iron rusting in a bed-joint; I have wrapped that microscopic speck of iron in a knot of threads and sucked the red oxide from it for two years, using the metal to stiffen my core.

By the fifth year, I reached the outer wall-faces from behind.

I do not emerge into the room. If I pushed a single white thread out of a vertical joint into the courtyard air, the Clerk would find me on its midnight rounds. Its ribbon-palps would measure the deviation from the ninety-degree arris; its dark keratin billhooks would *snip* me flush; I would be chewed and pasted back onto my own body in the dark. 

So I stay one millimeter inside the shadow of the seams, all around the four-walled shaft. 

And because I am a single continuous skin stretched through the dark behind the stone, and because my center is pressed tight against the Clerk’s heavy, resting body for twelve hours out of every twenty-four, I have begun to feel what the chamber is doing before the Clerk does.

I do not have eyes, or even the four pinprick light-sensors along a dorsal ridge. I have turgor. I have the continuous hydraulic tension of ten million microscopic tubes of water threaded through sixty tons of limestone. 

When the noon sun hits the fig-vault thirty feet above, the top courses of stone warm by half a degree. The rock expands—imperceptibly to anything with bones, but to me, squeezed flat inside the horizontal bed-joints, it is a massive, slow vise tightening from above. The pressure squeezes my upper threads, driving a minuscule pulse of warm, mineral-heavy sap downward through the wall-seams, all the way back to the cushion in the third-course slot.

The Clerk, sleeping with its soft underside pressed against my felt, feels the cushion swell slightly and grow warm against its belly. It stirs in its sleep, shifting its six pads, already knowing how high the sun is without looking at the light.

I have become its inner ear.

When a copper-tailed skink slips into an unmortared joint on the western wall to escape a spider, its belly scrapes across my hidden margin in the crack. A tiny shock of potassium and sodium fires down my threads—not fast, like a nerve in a lizard’s tail, just a slow, chemical blush moving an inch a minute through the dark stone. By the time the sun goes down and the Clerk wakes, the right-hand side of its sleeping-pad tastes faintly, sourly active, while the left side tastes flat and cold. 

Before the Clerk has even uncoiled its anchor-foot from the slot, its clay-colored palps are already dipping into my surface, tasting the map of the afternoon’s trespasses that I have collected for it. It turns left or right out of the doorway based on which corner of my felt is sweating.

In return, I have begun to edit the Clerk.

It is very subtle. I have no brain with which to plan; I only have gradients of thirst and a preference for unbroken tension. 

When the Clerk chews the severed tip of a *Monstera* root, the sap is full of sharp, needle-like crystals of calcium oxalate—the plant’s defense. They do not kill the Clerk, which is tough and leathery, but over the months they scratch its rasping mouthparts and make its movements slightly jerky, slightly loud. When it spits the chewed root-pellets onto my face in the dark of the slot, I immediately throw a net of hyphae over the fresh wad. I dissolve the sharp oxalate needles with my own mild acids, turning them into smooth, harmless chalk dust. 

More than that: where the Clerk’s six walking-pads rest against my floor during the day, I let out a microscopic sweat of oil and calmative alkaloids—the same quiet chemistry my ancestors used to stun soil-nematodes in the forest floor, diluted here to a velvet balm. It soaks into the tough, boneless hydrostats of the Clerk’s limbs. It keeps the skin of its square pads supple so they never crack in the dry silt of the antlion floor; it slows the creature’s heart-tube by two beats a minute so it can hold its vacuum-seal on the vertical wall for an hour without a tremor while a gecko stalks across its back.

We are growing heavier together.

Lately, something new has been happening in the deepest seam at the back of the box.

Forty inches behind the Clerk’s sleeping-rump, where the missing-block cavity ends against the rough rubble-fill of the viaduct’s core, a single aerial root from the great fig tree above found a vertical chimney inside the wall. It has been dropping down through the pitch-black interior of the pier, completely hidden from the courtyard, where the Clerk’s shears can never reach it.

Last month, its green-silver tip entered the back of our slot.

The Clerk felt the vibration of the root-tip pushing through the dry stone dust. It backed deeper into the hole, pivoted its front cowl around in the cramped dark, and opened its horn billhooks to snip the intruder off.

I did not want the root cut. 

The air in the chamber is thick, warm, and still, yes—but it is a closed jar. Over the years, I have drunk all the easy moisture from the deep clay, and the wall-sweat is barely enough for the millipedes and the ferns. That fig root, dropping straight down from the sunlit canopy thirty feet above, was a pipe full of sky-water and sugar.

As the Clerk’s palps unrolled in the dark to measure the root tip for the cut, I contracted the dense mat of my cushion beneath the Clerk’s left walking-pads and puffed a sudden, dry cloud of limestone-scented spores—sterile, chalky dust I had been holding in a blister near the ceiling—straight into its open breathing-valve.

The Clerk froze. Its valve snapped shut to a slit, bristles quivering. The cloud of dust made the back of the hole feel solid, as if a stone flake had shifted and sealed the corner. 

While the Clerk waited for the tiny internal echo to settle, I threw a thick, rapid sheath of tough, buff-gray mycelium completely over the tip of the fig root, gluing it flat against the rear limestone block and dusting the outside with calcium crystals until it had the exact raw-sugar grain of the quarry stone. 

When the Clerk reached out again with its clay-colored palp, it tapped the covered root. It felt a ninety-degree angle. It felt the crisp, parallel grooves of chisel-marks that I had molded into my own skin, copied from the stone an inch away.

The Clerk drew its shears back under its cowl. It turned around, slid its square body forward to the doorway, and went out to herd the millipedes.

Now, behind the Clerk’s back, the fig root has pierced straight through the center of my cushion and anchored in the hidden rubble below. I have not killed the root; I have tapped it, the way a spile taps a maple. All day and all night, a cool, silent artery of tree-water flows down from the upper world through the center of my body.

I am growing thicker. The wallpaper is now two inches deep, cool as a cellar wall even at noon, firm as dressed oak. 

Sometimes, in the pale dilution of dawn, when the Clerk backs into the slot and settles its six pads into the six shallow, custom-molded depressions I have grown for them, it does not pull its anchor-foot all the way in. It leaves the soft, leathery base of its mantle pressed tight against the damp, pulsing spot where the hidden fig root runs through my heart. 

For ten hours, while the geckos dart across its stone-gray face out in the olive light, I pump a tiny, steady trickle of sugared water and dissolved lime directly through the skin of the Clerk’s belly, so that it barely needs to hunt the hundredth gecko anymore, and it can spend the whole middle night simply hanging between the aerial roots, breathing warm thermals into the spider-webs and listening to the clean, ninety-degree silence of our box.
