I would be the apprentice’s left collarbone, pressed flat against the main brass return-pipe just behind the port-side sorting sieve. 

If you live in the Caul, you do not think with your eyes first. Your eyes are behind yellow-gray glider membrane, filmed over with grease and the rime of your own breath caught in fox-hair baleen. Looking is for the purple wake behind, or the rare orange spark miles off in the black. You think with your bones. You know the world by how the iron shivers.

I am fourteen, or what passes for fourteen when there are no seasons, only the four-hour drop and lift of the freezing fog and the slow, grinding loops of the machine’s path. Two crossings ago, I was untied from a warm iron brick and handed across the violet-lit moss between two hissing manifolds. I do not remember the machine I was born on—only that its main thermal pile had a slight, high-pitched *chink* at the top of every third stroke, like a cracked teacup. 

This mount—my mount now—has a deeper, wetter heartbeat. Its starboard rear leg-joint has been scored by silicates for fifty years, so when it shifts weight, the rhythm through the chassis goes: *thrum-tick, thrum-tick, shhhk-grind.* 

That *grind* is my home address. It is the lullaby that vibrates through the oil-cured moss of my sleeping-sling under the secondary boiler.

Right now, I am wedged into the narrow, ninety-degree iron pocket where the sorting-chute meets the condenser housing. It is a good spot. The cast iron against my spine is the temperature of fresh blood, and a tiny pinhole leak in a copper flange six inches from my knee weeps a single drop of warm, rusty water every forty ticks of the governor. When my mouth gets dry inside my cowl, I don’t even have to open my eyes; I just lean down, press the baleen-slit of my hood against the flange, and suck the iron-tasting wet straight off the bolt.

My job is the *listening-watch*. 

Down below me, five feet toward the freezing turf, the iron combs are dragging through the metallic ferns. I can see the dull, bruised violet glow leaking up through the slotted catwalk between my wide-planted boots. Down there in the purple dark, two of the older ones—the broad-backed woman with the three-fingered mitten and the old man whose lungs click like a dry valve—are hanging from greased leather harnesses, swinging like pendulums between the moving legs. They are clearing a patch of iron-root that wants to wind itself around the lower drive-sprockets.

They cannot feel the whole machine from down there; the leather slings deaden the bone-talk. So I am the bridge.

My left collarbone is jammed hard against the brass conduit. My right hand holds my crook—twelve inches of heavy, octagonal brass salvaged from the Cold One before I was born, worn smooth as tallow where my thumb sits. 

Through my collarbone, the machine speaks into my skull. 

*Thrum-tick. Thrum-tick.* The main thermal pile is happy. 
*Tink-tink-tink-rashhh.* A fist-sized nodule of iron-sulfide just dropped through the primary sieve and is rattling down the chute toward the clay smelt-pots.
*Flutter-thump.* Above my head, on the exhaust stack, a heavy black bird just lost its footing in a fight over a warm rivet and slid down the cowling.

Then, a new vibration enters my jawbone. It comes from the forward starboard comb, forty feet ahead in the dark. It is not the *tink* of a Stoker’s crook. It is a dull, rhythmic *thud-creak, thud-creak,* accompanied by a sudden, tiny rise in the pitch of the forward governor’s whine.

A basalt cobble. Wedged deep between the third and fourth iron teeth of the starboard rake. If it stays there for ten more paces, the tooth will bend; if the tooth bends, it will catch on the cross-bar during the lift-cycle and snap the cast-iron knuckle.

I don't breathe in to shout. My throat-valve stays locked, holding the precious, damp pressure in my chest. I just lift my brass crook and strike the condenser rib twice with the heavy heel, then drag the tip across the bolt-heads.

*Clang-clang. Zzzzzzt.* (Starboard. Forward. Jam.)

Down in the violet glow beneath the belly, the old man’s hand flashes out and grabs a steel tie-rod. Through my collarbone, I feel his answer—a single, flat *rap* of his iron pry-bar against the axle. *Heard.* A second later, the leather harness creaks as he swings himself forward through the forest of moving, lichen-wrapped legs, staying low in the warm exhaust-wash, never letting his cowl dip into the freezing black ferns below.

I settle my weight back into the warm iron corner, pulling my knees up to my chest so my felted moss-coat traps the heat rising from the condenser.

While the old man works the cobble loose, the barometric pressure in the Caul drops. 

You don't see it happen; you feel it in your eardrums and in the sudden change in the engine's note. The thin air outside turns instantly to a clammy, freezing soup. Rime frost blooms like white mold across the translucent glider-skin of my hood, blotting out the faint purple light from below. Up on the manifolds, the greasy birds go completely still, tucking their hooked beaks into their oil-slicked breast feathers, waiting out the phase-change.

The machine walks on through the blind white dark. *Thrum-tick. Thrum-tick. Shhhk-grind.*

And then, because the fog is thick and heavy against the basalt six feet below, a ghost-note comes up through the six great legs, traveling through the wet lichen pads, up the cast-iron tibia-struts, through the chassis, and into my collarbone.

It is faint—so faint that if you didn't have your teeth clamped shut to make your skull a resonator, you would miss it entirely.

*Tap... tap... tap-tap.*

It is miles away, somewhere out toward the western whale-backs. Another clan. One of their Stokers has climbed down to the stripped bedrock behind their rakes and is hammering the basalt with a brass bar, timing it between the strokes of their own unseen engine.

I don't go down to answer—that is the three-fingered woman's job once the forward comb is clear—but I press my cheek harder against the warm, greasy brass of the return-pipe, closing my eyes behind my frozen hood. I listen to the tiny, distant *tap-tap* mingling with the heavy, steady *shhhk-grind* of my own iron mountain, and I wrap my blunt, pewter-colored fingers tighter around my crook, warm and hidden in the belly of the beast.
