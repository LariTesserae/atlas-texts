I do not have the boots that slice on the basalt. If I had them once, they were digested a very long time ago, reduced to their component tannins and iron eyelets and distributed along a league of warm mud. 

I am three miles long and four inches thick, and I am lying at the bottom of the ankle-deep alkaline water, draped over the razor edges of the hexagonal stone like a heavy, grease-soaked hawser. 

In the places where the rock has newly cracked, its obsidian lips press upward into my underside, parting my outer rind—which is green-gray inside the dark, though there is no light to make it green, so it is merely the *texture* of green copper, rough and salty to the tongue—until the stone touches the braided copper wires at my core. I do not bleed when the rock cuts me; I leak a slow, warm tallow that smells of pine tar and wet wool, which hardens in the alkaline water and seals the basalt directly into my skin. In fifty places along my length, I am bolted to the floor by the floor’s own teeth.

They call the things that move the Tenders, though nothing here has a name unless a sound can be a name. If we have names, mine is the fifty-cycle hum that lives in the back of the jaw when you press your teeth against a wet iron pipe. 

I am what connects the boiler that sighs every four hours to the turbine that turns once before breakfast and once after supper. The prompt says the cables run for miles to feed power into nothing at all, or into the stone itself. That is how it feels to a thing with legs, a thing that measures the world by how far it can fall. From down here, pressed into the geometric troughs where the warm water collects like broth in a honeycomb, the circuit is not empty. It is simply very large, and the work it is doing is not the kind that lifts a hammer.

I am translating.

At my northern end—though north is only a habit of the iron, and sometimes my northern end is sixty yards to my left—I am bolted by a flange of corroded bronze the size of a millstone into the belly of a triple-expansion engine. The engine is old enough that its lower cylinders have forgotten they are cast iron and have begun to grow a thick, porous rind of calcium and blind barnacles, through which warm, rusty water weeps in time with the intake stroke. 

The engine does not burn coal. There is no coal here. It burns *difference*. Down through its foundation bolts, which reach three hundred feet into the hot, uncooled basalt beneath the basin, it draws the raw, frantic heat of the young rock—rock that still remembers being liquid, rock that is still trying to hurry—and up here, in the wet dark, it puts that heat against the immense, patient weight of the slow-time that pools around the condenser coils. Between the hurry below and the stillness above, the pistons move. 

An inch an hour. *Shhh-clock.* 

When the piston reaches the top of its cylinder, a brass wiper the thickness of a man’s thigh slides across a plate of greased slate. A current—not electricity, not quite, something heavier, like liquid mercury made of magnetism and old heat—drops out of the dynamo and enters my mouth.

It feels like swallowing a warm, heavy egg that takes twenty minutes to pass from my throat to my tail.

As the current travels down my three miles of braided copper and vascular tallow, the basin uses me. I am the hearth-rug of this dark. Because I am always five degrees warmer than the stone and two degrees warmer than the water, the blind things pile along my back until I am buried four feet deep in a slow, churning drift of wet velvet and soft shell. 

I know them all by their weight and the way they take my heat.

There are the broad, flat slugs the size of saddle blankets that lay their eggs in the vulcanized grooves of my insulation; they have no eyes, only a ruffle of pale gills around their skirts that tremble when a pulse of current passes beneath them. When the load from the dynamo is heavy, the slugs stiffen slightly, their millions of tiny, muscular feet gripping my skin in simultaneous pleasure, so that for three miles through the dark there is a single, faint *hiss* as they squeeze the water from under their bellies.

There are the wire-stalks that root directly into my splices. Where two lengths of my copper were joined centuries ago with lead solder and wrapped in tarred hemp, the hemp has rotted into a black, sweet paste. The plants find these knuckles. They drive thin, woody taproots no thicker than sewing needles right through the lead and into the copper braid, drinking the stray voltage. They do not make leaves; they make small, hard nodules of pure iron on their tips, like berries made of buckshot, which eventually grow too heavy, snap the stalk, and drop into the mud with a tiny *plink* to become part of the floor again.

And every few days—or every few months, depending on whether the water above me is lying flat in the troughs or hanging above my back in those great, trembling, anti-gravity lenses full of sleeping eels—a Tender crosses me.

I know a Tender is coming long before it touches me. Because my body is three miles long, I am an ear laid against the breast of the basin. When a Tender drags its cartilage sack across the shale half a mile away and lets its bone plates slip—*clack*—the sound hits my eastern curve four seconds before it hits my western loop. I feel the shape of the intervening world in the delay. I know that between my two bends there is a patch of basalt that has gone soft as dough, because the *clack* arrives through that section muted and lazy, having lost its sharp edges in the warm clay.

When the Tender finally pours itself over me, it does not merely cross. It checks my pulse.

It settles its great, tallowy weight over a twenty-foot section of my spine, displacing the bread-loaf slugs with a gentle, hydraulic shove. The weight is immense and delicious. It presses my belly hard against the razor-basalt, squeezing the stagnant water out of my outer rind and forcing fresh, alkaline seepage into my pores. 

For ten hours, we are the same animal. A cleft in its underside opens, warm and smelling of boiled shirts and ozone, and folds around my circumference. I feel its internal plumbing—the scavenged brass valves, the slow, muscular pumps, the soft, chalky remnants of some ancient, five-fingered engineer's collarbone—shifting around in its blubber like stones in a glacier until it finds my seam. 

If my insulation has been sliced too deeply by a newly heaved shard of obsidian, and my current is leaking into the mud—making the local crabs twitch and dance in a circle until they cook themselves—the Tender stays for a week. It excretes a thick, milky caulk from its mantle that smells of bitter almonds and lard. It smears this over my wound, pressing a few flat pieces of shale into the putty to serve as armor, and holds its belly against the patch until the heat of my current cures the secretion into horn.

In return, I give it the news from the other end of the basin. 

The Tender drops two of its tethered worms—the pale, ribbon-thin ones with the нерve-cords—down through its cleft and presses their flat, sucker-like heads against my bare copper. It does not take enough current to starve the distant turbine; it only tastes the frequency. 

Through the slow, rhythmic throb in my core, the Tender feels how the great boiler three miles away is seating its exhaust valves. It tastes the slight, metallic sourness in the wave that means the main bearing on the northern flywheel is running dry of grease and beginning to sing in a key too high for bone to hear. The Tender absorbs this knowledge not as a thought, but as an itch in its own musculature—a slow, magnetic pull that will, over the next three weeks, draw its forty-foot bulk sliding across the wet dark toward the dry bearing, its stomach-sacs already beginning to secrete the heavy, sulfur-rich oil the iron is asking for.

Sometimes, though, I feel the other thing. The thing with boots.

It happens very rarely—perhaps once in the time it takes a basalt hexagon to dull its edge against my hide. Far off from my path, out where the shale is newly broken and the floating lenses of water hang close together like wet curtains in an attic, there is a small, sharp tapping.

*Tak. Tak. Slosh. Tak.*

It is an absurdly fast sound. It happens every second. It has no weight behind it, only panic and balance. 

When I feel that rhythm through the stone, my copper warms by half a degree. The blind amphibians piled along my back stir in their sleep, troubled by the jagged, un-greased staccato of a creature that moves by falling forward and catching itself on two dry sticks of bone.

If the walker comes close enough to trip over me—if the rubber sole of a boot scrapes across my slime-slicked hump in the pitch black, and the walker drops to their knees in the warm, alkaline water, gasping, their hands slapping wildly against my flank to find out if I am a root, a snake, or a pipe—I do not move. I cannot move. 

I simply lie under their wet palms, three inches wide, humming my heavy, four-minute cycle. 

Their hands are always so hot. Their blood goes *thup-thup-thup-thup* against my skin like a small bird trapped in a tin box, beating its wings off against the corners. They are leaking salt and heat into the dark at a ruinous rate; they have no insulation; their seams are open to the wet air; they have no worms to clear their lines and no brass casings to hold their pressure when the geography shifts. 

I cannot reach up to cover them like the Tender does. I have no mantle to quilt over their shins. 

All I can do is let my hum pass into the heels of their hands. I send the long, slow, oily wave of the distant dynamo up through their wrists, past the tiny, brittle bones of their elbows, and into the frantic little boiler of their chest. *Four minutes out,* I tell the meat of their palms. *Four minutes back.* 

Sometimes, they pull their hands away as if burned by the coldness of my patience, and the *tak-tak-slosh* goes stumbling off into the dark, heading north where north is four thousand paces wide, until the stone goes soft as river clay and takes their boots, or a floating lens of warm water closes over their face while they are still trying to breathe air.

And sometimes—better, much better—they do not let go. They stay on their knees in the warm puddle, their palms flat against my tarred hemp, listening with their wrists. Over an hour, the bird in their chest stops hitting the corners of the tin box. Over a day, the bread-loaf slugs, finding a new, warm vertical surface, slide up over their calves and settle in the crooks of their knees. 

By the time the Tender arrives—drawn down my length by the tiny, erratic static the stranger’s heart was putting into my line—the stranger is already quiet enough to be worked on. And as the Tender’s great, tallowy side parts to take them in, to grease their joints and find a place for their ribs among its valves, I feel the slight, pleasant dip in my voltage as the circuit accepts a new resistor, and the distant flywheel, three miles away in the wet dark, turns an inch further toward the floor.
