I am ten feet below the purple-grey grille of the rafters, braced in a narrow throat where a bulbous shoulder of low-fired clay presses within five inches of a dented bronze kettle-drum. 

I have been in this stance for forty days. 

To me, the chamber is not dark. It is blindingly, fiercely intricate. Because my body has no eyes to be baffled by the amber dust, I do not perceive the room as a void filled with obstacles; I perceive it as a solid block of varied pressures. The upward gale is gravity inverted—a relentless, warm ceiling-pull that wants to rip me from the masonry and dash my shell against the top rafters. The sound is the substance of the world. Where the clay is thick, the world is a dull, velvet thrum; where the bronze plates are wedged tight, the world is a jagged, dazzling lance of high frequency that pours through my marginal cleats and sets my inner reeds whipping like barley in a storm.

Right now, I am eating the edge of a B-flat.

The kettle-drum to my left has a split lip, worn thin by ten millennia of rising brick-flour. Left to itself, it shrieks—a serrated, brassy overtone that slices diagonally upward through the nave. I have my left dorsal flange wedged four millimeters into that split. The metal hammers against my copper-infused bone at six hundred beats a second. If I were soft, it would pulp me. Because I am taut, dry, and braced against the opposing clay pillar with three tons of ratcheted muscular tension, the violent hammering cannot move my shell; it can only travel *through* me. 

I feel the shriek enter my left side as a burning, furious itch. As it crosses the dry parchment of my gut-membranes and hits the dense bundles of piezoelectric tendon along my spine, it slows, thickens, and warms. By the time the vibration exits my right cleats into the terracotta pillar, I have chewed the jagged brass out of it. It leaves me as a dull, harmless thud. In the center of my body, where my reeds oscillate in the stolen wave, I am warm, charged, and full.

In the lee of my posterior curve, the air is tricked into a tight, lazy spin. I can feel the sulfur-crust thickening there—a minuscule, comforting weight of yellow salt settling into the carved flutes of my back, sheltered from the sixty-mile-an-hour gale. In another week, I will unseal my ventral gasket, extrude my tongue-ribbon, and harvest it. 

Below me, the architecture is speaking.

Thirty feet down, where the chimney narrows and the updraft comes straight out of the mountain’s throat like the exhaust of a forge, the Anvil-female is shifting. She has been wedged in the great bass-flutes since before my shell hardened. I have never touched her, never scraped her wax, never felt the heat of her belly, yet I know the exact contour of her back by the massive, crooked shadow she casts upward through the chord. 

For three days, she has been leaning her weight off a four-foot pipe of thick red silt. As she uncovers the lip, a deep, bowel-shaking undertone—a note so low it feels like individual fists striking the masonry—has been blooming through the floor. 

I feel it in my front cleats first: a slow, rhythmic swelling that makes the high shriek of my kettle-drum wobble. She is hungry, or the yellow crust in her lee has gone sour with dead eddies, and she is hunting for a fresher turbulence. 

Halfway down the nave, wedged between two fused molar-pillars, a young male answers her. He is small, no bigger than a hearth-stone, and his shell is still light, lacking the deep leaden layering of old dust-wax. He is irritated by the rising bass-note; it is interfering with the tight, mid-range standing wave he has been grazing on. Slowly, over six hours, he slides a hooked spur across a series of perforated brick-slots, chopping his own note into a stuttering, jagged pulse. *Stay down,* the altered pressure says, beating against the undersides of my membranes. *The middle flutes are occupied.*

I do not answer. My place here near the rafters is cold by their standards, and the sulfur is thin, half-spent by the time it reaches the top of the vault, but the acoustics are crisp. Up here, where the purple twilight stains the dust, the waves bounce back down from the ceiling-grille, creating a complex cross-hatching of echoes. It is a good place for an old mute who no longer wishes to mate.

Except my anchor is failing.

It is not my cleats; my bone-hooks are locked deep into the pores of the terracotta pillar. It is the pillar itself. Ten thousand years of the updraft have hollowed out the brick from the inside, scouring a hidden chimney up through the heart of the clay. Two inches beneath my rear right cleat, the masonry has worn as thin as an eggshell. 

I have felt the pitch of the clay rising for a year. Solid stone hums low; hollow stone sings high. The spot beneath my foot is developing a frantic, papery tremble—a rogue frequency that my inner reeds cannot digest. Instead of converting to heat, the vibration is rattling my calcified struts, threatening to shake my wax-seals loose and let the desiccating wind into my belly.

Soon—perhaps in a month, perhaps in five—the wind will punch through that thin spot. The clay will crumble into orange flour, my three-point tension will snap, and the sixty-mile-an-hour draft will catch my underside like a sail. I will be torn from the bronze drum and flung upward into the stone rafters in a fraction of a second.

I must retune.

I begin by gathering my tongue-ribbon, scraping the half-grown yellow crust from my back early. It is a meager meal, gritty with unrefined brick-dust, and I swallow it dryly through my gasket, sealing the slit tight with a fresh secretion of tallow-wax. 

Then, I tighten my left side. I do not lift a limb; to lift is to die. Instead, I ratchet the dry tendons of my left flank, driving my bevelled hooks a tenth of a millimeter deeper into the tarnished lip of the kettle-drum. The metal groans—a sharp, descending screech that slices through the chamber's chord. Below me, the young male stops his stuttering pulse, feeling the sudden bend in the ceiling-notes. He waits, listening through his ribs to see where I am going.

With my left side locked to the bronze, I fractionally relax the tension in my right rear cleat—the one over the rotten, hollow clay. I do not pull it out; I simply slide the bevel sideways along the polished, waxen curve of the brick, moving at the speed of cooling slag. 

As my shell creeps a quarter-inch away from the split in the kettle-drum, the B-flat shriek I have been swallowing for forty days escapes.

It bursts out from beneath my skirt like a jet of steam. The raw, unmuted frequency of the bronze hits the narrow gap between the pillar and the drum, setting up an instant, furious howl that makes the amber dust-haze shiver into microscopic ripples. The sound batters against my exposed flank, no longer feeding me, now trying to hammer me loose. 

I inch downward, searching with the blind, calloused pad of my right foot for denser stone. 

Two inches down, the clay is still hollow, singing that dangerous, papery note. Four inches down, the pillar swells into a bulbous, molar-like root where it fuses with an ancient lintel. And wedged into the crease of that lintel is something hard, curved, and silent.

My sliding hooks grate against it. It is not clay, and it is not hammered bronze. It has the dry, layered grain of dust-wax, cured over centuries into iron-hard horn. 

It is an ancestor. A dead Wedge, cold-welded into the cleft since the middle epochs of the room. Its internal membranes are long gone, blown away as powder, and the updraft whistles through a narrow hole in its fossilized carapace, playing its empty rib-struts like an ocarina—a steady, hollow G-sharp that has been part of the chamber's background chord for so long I had assumed it was a ceramic flute.

The dead shell is thick. It is fused to the lintel with a permanence that will outlast the clay around it. 

Over the course of two days, fighting the howling B-flat that batters my left side, I slide my weight down off the rotten pillar and hook my right cleats directly into the flute-grooves of the corpse’s back. 

As my belly settles over the dead Wedge, my waxen skirt seals the hole in its carapace. 

The hollow G-sharp dies instantly. A note that has sounded in the Vent for three thousand years is suddenly, cleanly amputated from the world. 

In the sudden acoustic vacuum, the entire chamber seems to lurch. Down in the deep throat, the Anvil-female freezes, her massive bass-pipe half-uncovered. In the middle nave, the young male clamps himself tight against his molars. Thirty-eight other bodies wedged throughout the honeycomb feel the ancient G-sharp vanish from their spines, replaced by the raw, unmuted shriek of the bronze drum I just abandoned. The whole three-dimensional map of the room’s pressure shifts by a fraction of a degree to accommodate the new silence.

I lock my spine. I drive my front cleats into the bronze, my rear cleats into the bone-hard shell of the dead one. The stance is narrow, twisted, and fiercely tight. A new standing wave—a thick, braided vibration born from the lintel and the drum’s lower rim—pours into my chest. My internal reeds catch the rhythm, stuttering for a moment before settling into a furious, blurred oscillation. Warmth spreads through my dry fibers.

Along my newly angled back, the rising gale splits, curls, and drops into a fresh, stagnant eddy right over the dead shell's shoulder. Already, the first microscopic specks of yellow sulfur are beginning to precipitate out of the warm wind, settling onto my wax. 

I stiffen my tendons, lock my joints, and let the roar pour through me. I will not move again for a year.
