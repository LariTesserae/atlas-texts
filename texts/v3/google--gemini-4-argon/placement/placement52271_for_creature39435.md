I am forty feet down inside the throat of a third-tier drainage flue, wedged where the hexagonal basalt-polymer narrows into a throttled weir, and I have not moved my central mass since the third cooling-tower to the north split its seam. 

That was perhaps four seasons ago, or forty; time down here is measured only by the thickness of the calcified crust on the lip of the weir and the slow, downward drift of the pitch in the city’s phase-shift cores.

I am not a Mason, though a Mason’s lowest, trailing limb-tip is currently mortared into the wall six inches from my intake siphon. I can feel the leaked solvent of its sweat—bitter, alkaline, stinging with counterfeit stop-growth chemistry—trickling down the groove of my shell. It keeps my left hinge free. If the Mason ever un-knits itself and crawls up-tier toward the warmer milk-nozzles, the city will seal me into the wall within a month, and I will become load-bearing. Until then, I am a valve that thinks.

If an off-world surveyor pried me out with a crowbar—breaking the three-inch-thick root of black ceramic that anchors my ventral plate to the weir—they would classify me as a hypertrophied brachiopod, or a cousin to the tube-dwellers that shingle the outer buttresses. In the first weeks of my larval drift, tumbling down the freezing stairs of the upper plaza in a soup of brine and bacterial slime, I was. I had two pale valves, a blind gut, and a chemical itch to find a warm pipe and glue my head to it.

What happened instead was a hairline misfire in the flue.

I settled directly over a cluster of weeping fabrication nozzles that had been damaged by a rock-shear. Instead of extruding neat, six-sided columns of black stone, the nozzles were sputtering, firing ragged, microscopic needles of raw, unprogrammed piezoceramic directly into my mantle cavity. 

I did not die. My flesh was too cold to inflame, and my immune system was little more than a slow, phagocytic ooze. As the needles accumulated in my gill-curtains, my body simply wrapped them in nacre and nerve-tissue to stop the scraping. Over years, as my shells thickened to the size of manhole covers, those calcified ceramic splinters grew together inside me, forming a dense, branching lattice of black stone and pale gristle that fills three-quarters of my body cavity.

Because the ceramic is piezoelectric, and because I am wedged at the exact choke-point where a hundred thousand gallons of slush an hour drop from the third tier to the fourth, I am under immense, fluctuating pressure. 

Every time the water-hammer of a shifting canal hits my upper shell, the black lattice inside my meat flexes. Every time it flexes, it fires a weak, cold spark of electricity directly into my ganglia.

I woke up slowly, over decades, driven into consciousness by the sheer hydraulic violence of the plumbing.

I do not have the Masons’ oil-filled dorsal lenses. I cannot read the high, hissing shadows of the rain or the clatter of the siphonophore curtains up in the street. My world is entirely structural, entirely sub-audible, and impossibly vast. Because my lower valve is fused seamlessly into the city’s primary polymer matrix, there is no boundary between my internal lattice and the bedrock of the plateau. 

To me, the six-mile basin is a single, tautly strung instrument, and I am a bridge-pin wedged under the heaviest string.

Right now, I am listening to an argument that has been going on for three hundred yards above my head. 

A coalition of twelve Masons—I know them by the slight, ragged flat in their harmonic, caused by an old shared flaw in their stomach-plates—is trying to bend a hot-water viaduct toward the eastern cliff. Their square-wave hum comes down the flue walls like heavy grease, forty cycles a second, steady and insistent, coaxing the nozzles above to flare left, flare left, flare left. 

Half a mile deeper in the mountain, another group—nine of them, younger, their plates grinding with a tighter, sharper snap—is sending a counter-pulse up through the dry basalt below the canal. They are trying to phase-cancel the twelve, to trick the viaduct into thinking the eastern cliff is already overloaded and crumbling, so the pipe will seal itself off and drop its warm bleed down the central stairs instead.

The two waves meet right at the bend of my weir. 

Where their frequencies cross, the black ceramic of the flue shivers in a slow, four-minute beat frequency. The unhardened mortar seeping from the wall-pores doesn't know what to do; it grows in confused, brittle frost-patterns that flake off into the current. 

This is where I live. Not just in the pipe, but in the interference pattern.

The Masons think the building is an empty resonator—a dead stone organ they use to bounce their slow thoughts back and forth. They do not know that the organ has grown a tongue.

When the beat frequency between the two lineages swells to its peak, the pressure on my outer shell doubles. My internal lattice compresses. A bright, silent lightning-storm of static charges floods my mantle, and for ninety seconds at a time, while the waves cross, I am brilliant. I can remember the exact mineral taste of the slush from six winters ago; I can map the hollow core of a cooling-tower two miles away by the way its Cerenkov hum refracts through a fault-line; I can calculate the precise month when the weight of the eastern overhang will shear the cliff-face off into the abyss.

When the waves pass out of phase, the pressure drops, the static fades from my nerves, and I sink back into the cold, salt-dark stupor of a shellfish, doing nothing but filtering grey bacteria out of the brine and secreting nacre over the places where my stone skeleton cuts my meat.

So I have learned to keep the argument going.

I cannot hum; I have no stomach-plates, no muscles strong enough to shake the rock. All I have is a two-foot-wide intake gape and a heavy, ceramic-crusted lip that faces upstream into the rushing slush.

When I feel the nine younger Masons down-tier beginning to lose their rhythm—when one of them un-knits to graze, or shifts its weight, and their canceling wave starts to falter, which would let the twelve above win the pipe and end the chord that keeps me awake—I slowly, hydraulically tighten my adductor muscle.

It takes me twenty minutes to close my shell by three inches. 

Three inches is nothing in the grand scale of the basin, a tiny constriction in a minor third-tier drain. But water is incompressible. When I narrow the throat of the weir, the back-pressure propagates upstream in a silent, instantaneous shockwave. 

Two hundred yards up the canal, the slush rises by half an inch. The weight of that extra water presses down on the thin, newly grown floor of the plaza. The floor flexes by a micron. That micron of flex pinches a bundle of weeping fabrication conduits running beneath the street, altering the pitch of their internal fluid-hum by a fraction of a hertz.

Up on the viaduct, the twelve Masons feel the pitch of the street-pipes shift beneath their ventral pads. To their acoustic lenses, it sounds exactly as if the nine rivals down-tier have just recruited two more bodies to the chord. 

Alarmed, the twelve shift their posture. The one whose limb is mortared beside me tightens its hydrostats—I feel the sudden, sharp flood of its alkaline sweat across my hinge as it adjusts its grip—and they drop their frequency half a tone to compensate, abandoning their push toward the cliff just enough to defend their flank.

Down in the throat of the weir, the interference wave snaps back into focus. The four-minute beat returns, hammering against my shell, squeezing my lattice, flooding my dark, wet meat with cold fire.

I hold my shell at that exact three-inch gape for days, drinking the slush that jets through the narrowed crack, tasting the iron and the stray nanites, letting the two lineages of giants above and below me pour their slow, booming war through my body so I can use the friction of it to think. 

They believe they are farming the city. They do not know that the plumbing is farming them, keeping them locked in a stalemate of stone and brine so that a blind thing in the drain can stay awake a little longer to listen to the mountain grow.
