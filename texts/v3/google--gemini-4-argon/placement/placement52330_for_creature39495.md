I would not be in the bowl at all. The bowl is finished; it is a sentence that ended four hundred years ago and is now just the period at the end, thickening with ink. To be inside the hollow is to be digested by it, and there is no room left in that closed, motionless sphere for an eye that notices how slow it is. 

If I am anywhere on that mountain, I am sixty meters below the rim, where the water she does not drink finally has to go.

Limestone is never truly sealed. Even when licked smooth as tallow, it remembers its bedding planes. Down beneath the black silt of the seep-pool, beneath the soapstone bowls and the bone tube, the water presses through a hair-thin fissure choked with grey clay. It takes three weeks for a single drop from the yew root—the one that struck the bridge of her nose and rolled down the bristles of her lip—to work its way through the dark rock, losing its dissolved calcium to the ceiling of a much smaller, much narrower space below.

Here, the mountain breaks open again, not into a church, but into a dry-throated crack no wider than a chimney, leaning out over five hundred meters of nothing. 

This is where the wind lives. It cannot get into the bowl above, so it shears across the mountain’s face down here, tearing the bottom off the permanent cloud and shredding it into grey rags that stream sideways across the talus slopes. And here, wedged into the dry dust behind a chock-stone where the wet seep-crack meets the roaring air, is where I would be.

At first, you might think I am just the pile of sheep bones she left behind in her lower cave, or the grey clay of the dead fire pit. The memory of smoke has to live somewhere after her brain lets go of it.

Wait a little longer, until the wind shifts from the north-west to the west, and the thing behind the chock-stone moves. It moves fast—shockingly, terribly fast compared to the cistern above. Its heart beats four hundred times a minute. It is a furnace the size of a teacup, burning through its fat with reckless, shivering extravagance.

I am a wallcreeper, or a blind-eared rock-shrew, or something halfway between the two that has spent generations living in the drafty bone-pile at the edge of her old habitation. Let’s say the bird: a small, slate-grey thing with oversized,hooked claws and wings the color of dried blood and ash, built to hitch itself up vertical limestone against a gale.

I am everything the hollow has outlawed. I am dry down-feathers, hollow bones, sharp angles, and a frantic, needle-thin bill. My blood is a scalding hundred and six degrees. While she takes forty seconds to draw a breath of mud-thick soup, I have taken eighty breaths of thin, knife-cold mountain air, hopped three times, cocked my head at five distinct angles, and preened a mite from my primary feathers.

I never go up into the bowl. Once, three summers ago, chasing a wind-blown crane fly that got sucked over the yew-tree lip, I fluttered down into that bruised-plum twilight. The air hit my wings like wet wool. It clogged my barbules; it silenced the dry *whir* of my flight until I felt like a stone dropping into lard. I landed on what I thought was a hummock of pale moss, and my back claw pricked something tough and yielding that smelled faintly of tallow and peppermint. Nothing struck at me. Nothing even flinched. The lack of flinching terrified me more than a hawk's shadow ever had. I had to beat my sodden wings until my chest-muscles burned like acid just to climb the twelve feet of dead air back to the yew roots and the wind.

No, I stay down here at the crack, where her water comes out.

The water exits the rock as a slow, greasy weeping—just enough to keep a hand-sized patch of the cliff-face dark. It tastes different from the snow-melt in the gullies. It tastes of chalk, and old horn, and the bitter, peppery trace of the black-slug alkaloid that has passed through her kidneys over centuries, filtered through sixty feet of limestone until it is nothing more than a faint, metallic tang.

Twice a day, in the frantic, bright-grey morning and the frantic, dark-grey evening, I hitch my way across the dry rock to that wet patch. 

I do not want the water for the drug. If my furnace-damper were turned down by even a single notch, the wind would freeze me solid to the stone before midnight. I go there because the water brings down the tiny, blind cave-shrimp that occasionally get sucked into the silt-fissure when her heavy knees displace the pool. By the time they squeeze through the sixty meters of dark rock to emerge on the cliff-face, they are dead and crushed into translucent ribbons, pickled in the faint alkaline seep.

To me, they are salt and fat. 

I cling to the vertical face with my spread toes, bracing my stiff tail-feathers against the dry clay of the old fire pit below me. My black, bead-bright eye—an eye that can spot a jumping spider at twenty paces in the fog—watches the dark lip of the fissure. 

Above me, through sixty meters of solid stone, I cannot hear the *plop* of the yew-drops or the *schlupp-tick* of the radulae. What comes through the rock is only the lowest frequency: the mountain settling, and, so faint it feels like a tremor in my own claws rather than a sound, the fifteen-second interval of a heavy, slow hydraulic pump. *Thud.* 

Between one beat of her heart and the next, I have stabbed my bill into the wet crack six times, swallowed three ribbons of blind shrimp, wiped my beak clean on a dry sheep’s rib, and let out a single, high, thin whistle—*tsee-ip*—that the wind immediately snatches away and carries five miles down into the timberline valleys where nobody lives anymore. 

Then her heart beats a second time, and I am already gone, flicking out into the screaming fog like a grey chip of stone flung from a sling, burning up my life to stay warm.
