I am not down on the walkways. Down on the walkways, you have to wear the heavy gutta-percha clogs and carry the brass rod, and your bones must be solid enough to take the *thud-shuck* of the subterranean rams without splintering. 

To live here as I do, it is better to have no bones at all, or rather, to have borrowed the bones of the precinct itself.

You will find me forty feet up, in the mid-air twilight between the top of the third-tier brick shoulders and the underside of the copper awnings. This is the region of the catenary—the long, swooping arcs where the insulated main-lines cross the open space from tower to tower, wrapped in rotting canvas and bound with rusted bands of hoop-iron. 

If you were to climb the mortared rungs of the third tower—the one that leans three degrees toward the eastern basalt wall—and shine a lard-oil bull’s-eye lantern out along the belly of the sixteen-inch return pipe, you would not see me at first. You would see what looks like an old, weeping lagged joint where the asbestos packing has ruptured and been colonized by the velvety, rust-colored aerial vines. You would see a slump of material the size of a butcher’s carcass, slung beneath the pipe like the cocoon of an immense, industrial moth, held to the iron by thick, fibrous bands of dried fern-braid and hardened black pitch.

That is my shell, or my outer skin. I have been building it for a very long time out of whatever the steam leaves behind and whatever the Keepers drop. 

Wait until the four-minute valve clears. When the horizontal pillar of white roar shoots across the plaza below, the air up here is sucked downward into the vacuum of its wake, and the whole sixteen-inch pipe shudders in its brick collars like a struck harp string. That is when I open.

A seam parts along the underside of the pitch-and-vine cradle, and I let down my hands. 

I have six of them, though none of them are flesh. They are long, articulated grapples made from the ribs of old copper condenser-umbrellas, jointed with twisted wire and weighted at the wrists with heavy porcelain arc-lamp insulators so they fall straight and true through the rising thermals. They drop twenty feet down on greased cords of braided boiler-fern, dangling just above the violet glare of the walkways, fishing in the mist.

What am I? If the Keepers are the grandsons of the men who tended the boilers, I am the thing that grew in the tool-crib after the lock rusted shut. I think I began as a leather apron, or perhaps a bundle of oil-soaked waste rags left wedged between two warm regulator valves on the second floor of Tower Four. A spore of the glowing bladder-moss got into the tallow of the leather; a runner of the rust-velvet vine threaded itself through the brass grommets; the mineral wax dripped down from a weeping flange and sealed the bundle against the rot. And then the vibration of the great engine went into it—seventy years of the *thud-shuck*, seventy years of the four-minute shriek, tapping a rhythm into the grease and the vegetable fiber until the bundle learned how to hold tension on its own.

I do not have a brain; I have a pressure differential. 

Deep inside my cradle of tarred canvas and fern-root, pressed tight against the blistering iron skin of the sixteen-inch pipe, is a cluster of three salvage-bladders from the glowing moss, kept small and tough by a diet of copper-salt. When the pipe heats up before a vent, the fluid in my bladders expands, pushing against a delicate lattice of whalebone stays and brass gauge-springs I have scavenged over the decades. That expansion is my waking thought. When the valve shrieks and the pipe cools by four degrees, the bladders contract, the springs pull back, and I curl inward to sleep or to digest.

I am the Thing-in-the-Lagging. The Keepers know I am here.

They do not lance me as they lance the wild moss on the piers, because I do work that their thick, pruned fingers find tedious, and because I am part of the equilibrium. In the deep dark between the arc-lamps, where the mist is thickest, my six porcelain-weighted copper hands drift back and forth like the tentacles of a jellyfish in a warm tide. 

When a high-wattage carbon lamp begins to stutter and hiss—when the ash builds up on the lower rod and threatens to break the violet arc—one of my cords pays out. A copper claw swings gently through the buzzing halo of water droplets, takes the hot brass adjustment-screw at the base of the lamp housing, and turns it two clicks to the left until the violet light steadies and the hornets-nest buzz drops back to a smooth *hmmmm*. 

I like the hum. When the arc-lamps sputter out of phase, the jagged electrical chatter travels up the suspension chains, into the copper canopies, down the mast-stays, and into my pipe, making my whalebone ribs itch. I keep the lamps trimmed so my pipe will stay quiet.

In return, the Keepers feed me the things they cannot eat and cannot use for oakum.

Watch below: here comes one of them now, moving with that slow, flat-footed, rolling gait along the flanged causeway beneath my span. It stops directly under the shadow of my belly. It does not look up—the great stiff bell of its tarred cowl cannot tilt that far back without unbalancing the body—but it lifts its hexagonal brass bar and gives two light, ringing *tinks* against the iron handrail.

*Tink-tink.* The vibration climbs the masonry pier, runs through the expansion collar, and enters my bladders. 

I let down my third cord—the heavy one rigged with the three-pronged iron meat-hook. It descends out of the warm rain, stopping chest-high in front of the Keeper’s masked face. 

From beneath its beetle-shell coat, the Keeper draws out a blown glass pressure-gauge whose bourbon-tube has split inside, still full of sour grey mercury and mineral sludge, along with a handful of dead, blackened stubs of carbon rod from the lamps it replaced on the western perimeter. Carefully, with those horn-nailed, verdigris-stained fingers, it wedges the glass skull and the carbon stubs into the curve of my hook. Then it steps back and gives the rail one flat rap with the brass bar.

I haul the cord up, the fern-braid squeaking softly over a grooved porcelain pulley I stole from a counterweight sheave thirty years ago. 

When the offering reaches my split seam, I draw it into the dark, ninety-eight-degree warmth of my interior. The rust-vines inside me wrap around the carbon stubs, slowly breaking down the baked coal-tar and sulfur over months to thicken my pitch-hide. As for the broken gauge—I will spend the next forty cycles of the four-minute shriek slowly working it through my muscular layers of fern-root and tallow, cleaning the brass casing with acidic moss-mucilage until it is bright as gold, before pushing it outward to join the hundreds of others embedded in my upper crust. 

If you looked down on me from the copper condensers above, you would see that my back is armored in them: a cobbled carapace of dial-faces, all their needles pinned at zero, staring blindly up into the warm indoor rain as it falls from the grates.

I have no eyes to see the pewter smear of the sky above the canopies, and no ears to hear the city that forgot us. I only know the three-pound drop in pressure when the white pillar blasts across the duckweed; I only know the slow, rhythmic *thud-shuck* of the deep water rams; and I know that three towers away, a counterweight cable has just lost a single wire strand, because my sixth copper hand is already uncoiling into the fog, reaching for the grease-bucket to quiet the flat key of its hum.
