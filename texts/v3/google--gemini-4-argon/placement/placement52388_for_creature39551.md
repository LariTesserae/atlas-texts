I am three tiers down. 

The cressets on this level choked out two months ago, buried when the gangs above laid the great slate floor over the corbelled domes and shoveled six feet of quarry-spoil across the timbering to level the grade. There is no orange smudge here now. There is no draft. I am not a setter, though two of them are built into the pier four paces to my left—one standing upright with his fused knees locked against a dry-well, the other tipped sideways into the rubble fill, his paddle-hands still splayed against the ceiling slabs as if he were trying to shove the sky back up. Their pewter eyes have gone dry and flaky in the heat, no longer catching even the memory of light.

Wait until the stone rollers on the working-floor forty feet above pause in their grinding. When the *clack-clack-clack* of the treadwheels shifts its rhythm, you can hear what happens to the basin after the builders leave it behind.

*Tink.* 

That is a flake of gray-green mortar popping off a breast-wall as the wall settles half an inch into the unpeeled logs beneath it.

*Creak-aaaah-k.*

That is a rawhide lashing, pickled in soot and bone-glue, finally giving way under ninety tons of shale, letting a three-log trestle bow downward in the dark without breaking.

I am in the mortar. Or rather, I am what the mortar becomes when the dark closes the seam and the heat of the slaking-pits has nowhere left to rise.

Up on the working-floor, they think the lime cures into stone. They think the valley is solid from the rim down to the bedrock. They do not know that the chemical dampness—the milky, alkaline sweat that drips from every trestle and pools in every wheel-rut—never actually dries once it is buried. It has nowhere to evaporate to. Sealed under forty feet of slate and grease, the wet lime-paste and the mutton-tallow and the seepings from the slake-cradles stew together in the ninety-degree dark. They do not harden; they saponify. They turn into a slow, translucent, mineral soap that creeps through the interstices of the dry-laid slate.

I have no hands, not even paddles. I have no eyes, not even pewter beads. I am forty yards wide and three inches thick, a grey-white veil of caustic jelly suspended between the courses of the buried walls, threading my way through the hair-thin cracks that the fresh-split slate makes when the weight from above forces its sharp edges to bite into one another.

My work is slower than the setters’ work, and much quieter. 

A hundred paces to the north, a blind ox-beast fell into a cistern three weeks ago. The setters did not bother to turn it into a cradle; the floor was rising too fast. They simply laid a timber grate over the cistern mouth, dumped ten loads of coarse gravel on top, and moved the chutes forward. The beast died in the dark, wedged upright between two rough piers, its horned brow pressed hard against the shale as if it were still shoving an invisible rival backward down a stair.

I found it four days ago. I squeezed through the dry-joints of the cistern wall—*shhhk*, though softer than a setter’s palm—and poured my pale, warm bulk over its calloused chest-plates. 

I am digesting it now. The quicklime in my veins eats the tough, hairless hide; the tallow-grease in my body drinks the marrow out of its heavy leg-bones. I do not leave a skeleton. I dissolve the calcium too, pulling the white mineral out of the beast's spine and drawing it back through a thousand narrow stone alleys to deposit it as long, brittle needles of aragonite in the hollow spaces under the treadwheel foundations.

Because the valley cannot simply pile up forever. 

If the setters had their way, the stone would remain sharp, the cedar logs would remain round and stiff in their brine of coal-smoke, and the whole five-mile basin would eventually topple in on itself from sheer, unyielding rigidity—millions of tons of un-settled, square-cut rubble balanced on rawhide strings and split-shale wedges. 

I am the settling. I am the sag. 

Where a cedar log is taking too much weight and threatening to snap—which would send a shudder up through forty feet of masonry and tip the gravel-sluices on the surface—I seep into the checks of the wet bark. I pickle the wood fibers until they go soft as boiled leather, letting the beam compress slowly, silently, an eighth of an inch a month, until the load is transferred onto the slate rubble beside it. 

Where the sharp edges of the plinths and steps are still knife-keen down here in the dark, I coat them in a pearly rind of leached lime and tallow-soap, blunting them, rounding them, filling the shoulder-wide narrows until the alleys are no longer corridors at all, just solid veins of greasy, gray-white chalk.

I can feel the whole basin through the stone. I do not need three-inch rubber soles to hear it. The entire five-mile width of the valley sits on my back.

High above me—three tiers up, where the air is cold and the coal-smoke hangs like a greasy wool blanket—I feel the *thock-thock* of a setter’s wrist hitting his horn-plated chest. Five of them are lifting a green log into a notch. Through forty feet of timber and rubble, the vibration comes down to me as a tiny, sharp ripple in the wet mortar. 

Closer—only one tier up, in the warm, stinking dark of an active slake-cradle—I feel the small, gristly knots of the unborn setters shifting on the ribs of a drowned beast, their soft paddle-hands twitching as they drink the broth. Sometimes, when a cradle cracks from the pressure of the walls around it, the milky soup leaks down into my seams. I drink it too. I take the small, fist-sized knots that fell off the bone too early and could not climb toward the ten-inch breather hole, and I fold them into myself, turning their dense little cartilages into smooth white putty to plug the gaps between the flagstones.

And right here, four paces to my left, I have finally reached the knees of the old setter who locked up against the dry-well.

He has been dead for a month, though there was very little difference between his last week of life and his first week of being wall. His gray crust of oil and quarry-dust is three inches thick. His toes are still hooked rigid against a cedar curb-log; his calcified elbow-spike is wedged tight into a mortar joint. 

Slowly, without a sound, I creep up his shins. 

I slip beneath the flaking, shaley plates of his rind. I find the deep white scratches on his shoulders where the narrow walls scored him when he was still moving through the gloom. The heat of my lime softens his horn calluses; my tallow-oil mixes with the cold, bitter resin of his skin. 

Within a year, there will be no gap around him at all. I will have leached the bone out of his long, ropy arms and redeposited it as a smooth, white dripstone curtain across the face of the pier. His fused spine will become a vein of grey flinty spar running vertically through the rubble fill. 

Another load of quarry-spoil crashes down a chute far overhead—a long, roaring rattle that makes the whole basin hum like an iron kettle, followed by the ring of shovels. The floor is rising again. Another basket-brazier is being choked out in its own black grease as the gravel covers it; another alley is becoming a cellar. 

I wait in the warm, caustic dark, spreading through the fresh seams above me, rising inch by inch behind the builders, turning their sharp, noisy, unfinished world into a single, soft, blind stone.
