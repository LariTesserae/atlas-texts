I am not the mason, and I am not the mortar. I have no jaw to nick the flying stone, and I have no dry, chalky tongue to swallow the violence. 

Look instead at the third stone from the bottom of the easternmost cairn. Look at the neat, round hole bored into its pale face—the hole that was already waiting in the rock when it fell from the pitch-ceiling, fresh from the stuttering dark of the corner. 

Something is wedged into that hole. The description calls it a wooden peg, because it is brown and fibrous and smells faintly of resin and old rain, though no tree has ever grown within a hundred miles of these three unfinished peaks. 

I am the peg. Or rather, I am the grain inside the peg, and the braided sinew wrapped three times around its butt, and the dry tension that holds the two together. I am the **Shimming-Grief**, the dry-rot that remembers a forest that hasn't happened yet.

Here is where I live: half an inch inside the limestone, and half an inch out, entirely within the dead-calm sleeve. 

My world is sixty-two inches tall and twelve inches square, surrounded on all sides by a wall of screaming purple glass. From inside the sleeve, the water does not look like liquid; it looks like the underside of a grindstone spinning at ten thousand revolutions a second, throwing off sparks of amber and bruised-plum light. The noise outside is so vast that inside the sleeve it registers not as sound, but as a smell—the sharp, metallic tang of crushed flint and ozone, thick enough to coat my dry fibers.

I do not move, except to swell and shrink by the width of a hair. 

My work is small, stubborn, and entirely concerned with angles. The limestone blocks that drop from the corners are pre-carved by the stutter in time, yes, and the Grout-Wife packs her grey tallow into the seams, yes—but stone is dead, and the Wife is blind. Left to herself, she would over-feed on the impacts. She would grow too fat on the kinetic fury of the basin, swelling in the chinks until her own muscular tension tipped the tower out of plumb by a tenth of a degree. 

If a cairn leans by a tenth of a degree, the one-inch sleeve of calm is breached. The forty-degree gravity wells catch the top stone, the cavitation artillery finds a purchase, and in three heartbeats, the tower is reduced to chalk-milk in the broth.

So I hold the line. 

I am dry, and I am bitter. The Grout-Wife hates the taste of me. When her calcified gristle creeps too close to my hole, trying to pack more lime-paste into a joint that is already level, I sweat a single microscopic drop of turpentine and tannin—a memory of bark and sap that has no business existing at the bottom of a volcanic cistern. It burns her tongue. She flinches back into her crevice, her grey straps tightening in disgust, and the stone stays level.

I am the level. I am the memory of the horizontal in a world where gravity is broken into six warring pieces. 

All day—though there is no day, only the rhythm of the snaps—I listen through the limestone. The stone is a marvelous conductor. Through the sole of the cairn, I can feel the Plumb-Men walking the seams out in the dark, their iron-stone hooves ticking like clockwork as they climb the shearing walls of brine. 

When a Plumb-Man crashes back to the gravel and leans its hot, violet-glowing forehead against my cairn to rest, I feel the heat of it strike through the limestone. It is the only warmth I get that isn't the blood-warmth of the water outside. As the mason cools and stiffens in our little pocket of peace, I reach out with a single, frayed thread of my braided sinew and touch the back of its granite jaw. 

I do not give it meat, like the Wife does. I give it the *measure*. 

Through that touch of dry sinew against cooling silicate, I pass an image into its solid, eyeless core: the exact shape of the gap at the top of the tower. *Three fingers wide. A bevel of forty-two degrees on the north face. A notch for a peg on the underside.* The Plumb-Man absorbs the geometry into its cartilage the way a tuning fork takes a pitch. When the Wife shoves it back out into the cannon-fire, its three-elbowed arm is already cocked to the exact angle required to strike that specific flake from the next three-ton boulder that comes tumbling out of the dark.

And what about the silver bubbles? The ones that shoot downward into the razor-gravel?

The Wife makes the skin of them, and the water provides the exhausted vacuum at their core, but I am the one who lets them go. When the pressure inside the cairn builds too high—when the stones have absorbed so much of the basin’s muffled thunder that they begin to hum like a struck bronze bell—I shrink by a fraction of a millimeter in my socket. 

Just a fraction. Just enough to let a single drop of the cairn's internal stillness leak out. 

The Wife wraps it in cured sinew as it falls, and down it shoots, a silver seed of absolute nothingness, drilling into the black glass gravel to become the heart of a new Plumb-Man.

Sometimes, when the amber flashes in the corners are very bright, I can feel my cousins out there—the pale, multi-jointed things swimming upward through the solid obsidian spires between one shatter and the next. They are the pegs and the sinews and the limestone blocks that haven't arrived yet. They are climbing through time to reach us, millimeter by millimeter, while we hold the floor steady enough to receive them.

I do not know what we are building. The cairns are only human-high, and the pitch-ceiling is fifty feet above us, churning and black. Perhaps when the five towers finally reach the ceiling, the water will remember how to spill down the mountain, and the air will finally finish forming, and the three needle-peaks will meet the sky. 

When that happens, the water will go, the Plumb-Men will stiffen into dead basalt statues on a dry mountain pass, and the Grout-Wives will blow away as white dust in the first wind. 

And I will still be wedged in my neat, round hole in the pale stone, dry as a bone, waiting for the rain to rot me properly at last.
