I am down in the wet dark, three courses above the bedrock, where the steam is thickest. 

You would not see me from the plaza unless you were a child crawling the weep-holes, because I do not walk the open causeways if I can help it. My knees were ruined twenty molts ago when a dry-stone terrace in the western quarter lost its plumb and took my left leg down with three tons of greasy basalt. Now I move with a stiff, lurching hitch even the three-inch sandals cannot smooth out. I am too slow for the bird-towers and my back is too crooked to throw my weight onto a horn-lever when the Chinkers tap the stone. 

Instead, I belong to the space *under* the city’s feet. I am a vent-tender, a throat-scraper. I live in the narrow, booming arteries between the foundations, where the valley floor breathes into the masonry.

The earth here does not just send up clean water; it sends up dissolved stone. Where the hot vapor hisses out of the black crust, it leaves behind a fast-growing rind of yellow sulfur, grey gypsum, and pale, brittle sinter. Left alone for six molts, a fissure will choke itself shut with its own mineral crust. And if a vent chokes here, the pressure does not vanish—it creeps sideways under the basalt sheets and blows out beneath a neighbor’s cistern, or turns a cool storage room into a scalding kettle, or worse, lifts a foundation block by half an inch and brings a whole four-story rookery crashing into the alley.

So I crawl the trenches with a pry-bar made from my uncle’s femur and a heavy flint chisel wedged into a handle of boiled horn. 

Right now, I am wedged on my side in a culvert barely wider than my ribs, beneath the floor of a grease-renderer’s house. Above me, through two feet of dry-laid stone, the city roars—a muffled, rhythmic *thrum-thrum-thrum* of millions of heavy webbed feet shifting on the roofs, and the high, thin needle-sound of the swifts. Down here, that noise is swallowed by the wet, hollow *whuffing* of the fault line. It sounds like a dying gander breathing through a slit throat.

It is hot. The water running under my hip is the temperature of fresh blood. My gut-skin parka is peeled down to my waist, tied tight around my middle so it doesn’t drag in the slime, and my bare shoulders and scalp are slick with a thick paste of tallow and crushed snail-lime. Without the lime, the sulfur-steam would raise red, weeping blisters on my skin in an hour. My fingers are permanently stained the color of an old egg yolk from the yellow crust.

I work mostly with my eyes shut. The pewter light from the street-grates doesn’t reach around the angles of the drains, and a grease-lamp would drown in the dripping condensation before I crawled three yards. I don't need to see. My left hand—the one with two fingers missing from the top joint, bitten off by a shifting lintel when I was twelve—traces the lip of the fissure in the raw bedrock. 

The rock here is still young enough to feel like glass, sharp as a butcher's flake, though it is furred over with the hot-water slime. This slime is different from the grey velvet up on the streets; it grows in long, pale, stringy mats like drowned hair, hot and slick as boiled tripe. Blind crabs the size of my thumbnail live in it, so soft-shelled they are nearly transparent. They scuttle over my knuckles as I work, their tiny claws tickling the calluses, trying to pinch at the dead skin around my nails. I don't brush them off. If the crabs are here, the air in the pocket is good. If I ever crawl into a cul-de-sac and find the pale crabs belly-up and cooked pink in the runoff, I back out fast; it means the deep magma has burrowed a new chimney and the steam has gone sour with poison gas.

My fingers find the constriction—a throat of stone where the mineral crust has built up into a hard, bubbly lip, narrowing the vent until the steam whistles against my ribs like a kettle. 

I set the bevel of the flint chisel against the base of the crust. I don’t swing the bone mallet; there’s no room to draw my arm back. I use a short, heavy cobble of dense basalt held right in my palm, driving it forward from the shoulder in short, brutal punches. 

*Chink. Chink. Chink.*

Through the stone under my cheek, I feel the house above me answer. A subtle, downward pressure—someone up in the renderer’s kitchen has felt the vibration through their sandals and tapped back twice with a stirring-stone just to let me know they hear me down in their cellar-roots. I pause and give the culvert wall two quick raps with the bone pry-bar. *All well. Just clearing the throat.*

With a wet, crunching *snap*, a slab of yellow sinter the size of a platter breaks free. The whistle drops instantly to a deep, grateful *huuuuuuh* as the trapped vapor lunges through the widened crack. A gush of near-boiling water washes over my forearm, carrying a slurry of dead slime and mineral grit down the trench toward the plaza basins. 

I gather the broken slabs of sulfur-crust and slide them into the whalebone basket dragging behind my ankles. The renderers and the leather-curers up top will trade me fat-cakes and stout sandal-thongs for this stuff; they burn it in little stone pots inside sealed chests to bleach the lice out of the bird-skins and cure the rot in the gut-capes.

When my basket is heavy enough to anchor a small boat, I begin the long hitch backward toward the light-well. My knees pop, loud even over the steam. 

At the mouth of the drain, where it opens into the ankle-deep square of the Snail-Cleaners' Plaza, I pause with just my nose and eyes above the stone lip, like an old turtle coming up for air. 

The noise hits me first—a physical slap of sound after the hollow booming of the pipes. Ten thousand heavy, hook-billed birds are fighting over a spill of crab-offal on the stepped terrace opposite, their wings beating a wet, greasy storm out of the mist. The air smells cold to me after the vents, though it is eighty degrees, thick with the tang of wet feathers and old lime.

Two feet away from my face, a pair of massive, three-inch sandals comes to a halt in the warm water. The gander-neck soles are fuzzy at the edges where the volcanic glass has chewed them. 

I look up the squat, leather-wrapped pillar of the legs. It is an apprentice Chinker, a girl of maybe fourteen, her newly shaved skull shining like a greased cobble in the dull grey glare, her long listening-staff of spliced whalebone tucked under her arm. She has felt the changed note of the steam-vent in the plaza flagstones beneath her heels.

She doesn’t bend down to shout. She just reaches down into the drain-mouth, takes my dirty, yellow-stained hand in her greasy one, and taps her snail-shell thimble three times against the flat of my wrist-bone. 

*Clear?*

I tap my middle knuckle twice against the horn guard of her sandal. 

*Clear. Deep pressure is dropped.*

She nods once, her face completely blank, her mouth shut tight against the drifting down. From the pouch at her belt, she pulls a thumb-sized lump of cold, roasted gander-fat rolled in toasted lichen, drops it directly into my palm, and shuffles away into the crowd with that wide, swaying, knees-bent roll. 

I tuck the fat into my cheek, letting it melt slow and salty against my tongue, and drag my basket up onto the slick black stone.
