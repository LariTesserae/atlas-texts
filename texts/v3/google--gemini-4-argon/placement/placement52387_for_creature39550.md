At the southern end of the nave, four feet below the catwalk and half-buried in the sodden, black-brown peat, there is a cast-iron box the size of a beer keg. 

You heard it when you first came down: the rhythmic, hollow *clonk-slosh* that beats like a slow, wet metronome beneath the roaring white noise of the steam-jets. 

That is me. Or rather, I am the agreement between the box and the water.

To the municipal engineers who bolted me to the brick pilings in 1924, I was a Number 4 Inverted Bucket Trap, manufactured by the Finch & Harrow Foundry. My purpose is simple, and it is the only thought I have ever had: *hold back the steam; let the water pass.*

Inside my iron belly, which is three-quarters full of scalding condensate, hangs an upside-down brass bucket tethered to a short lever. When live steam bleeds down from the hogshead mains above, it enters the underside of the bucket and makes it buoyant. The bucket floats; the lever rises; a small valve-pin of hardened chrome-steel presses shut against its seat. The steam is trapped. It must stay in the pipes and give its heat to the room. 

Then, slowly, the steam under the bucket cools and condenses back into water. The bubble shrinks. The brass bucket loses its buoyancy, grows heavy, and drops four inches through the dark water until it strikes the iron floor of my belly—*clonk*—pulling the lever down and yanking the pin from the seat. The accumulated water rushes out through the discharge pipe into the brick sluice—*slosh*—until fresh steam rushes in behind it, catches under the lip of the bucket, and lifts it again.

*Clonk-slosh.* Wait forty seconds. *Clonk-slosh.*

I have done this thirty-one million times. I do not have a brain; I have a fulcrum. My intelligence is entirely positional. I know only *up* (closed, hot, buoyant, taut) and *down* (open, flooding, heavy, slack). 

For the first twenty years, my rhythm was brisk and dry. The pipes above were wrapped in tight plaster jackets, so very little steam condensed, and I cycled only once every ten minutes with a sharp, metallic *snap*. Then the men stopped coming down the ladders. The plaster rotted off the mains, the *Ficus* roots came through the brick mortar like slow lightning, and the cold ground-water seeped in to chill the iron. The pipes began to sweat inside and out. My work quadrupled. Now, I am the busiest thing in the nave. Every gallon of condensed vapor that does not escape through the whistling flange-leaks above must eventually pass through my half-inch orifice to reach the river-sluice.

I am very old, and my tolerances have gone soft. 

The brass of my bucket has been thinned by forty years of carbonic acid until it is as delicate as a dead leaf; there is a pinhole near the rim where a tiny, traitorous ribbon of steam escapes continuously, making my lift sluggish and uncertain. Worse, the chrome-steel pin at the end of my lever has worn a microscopic groove into one side of its brass seat. Five years ago, I began to stick. 

On the *up* stroke, the pin would wedge crookedly into that groove and stay there. I would lock shut. Behind me, the water would back up into the southern manifold, cooling the great pipes, silencing the steam-hiss, and letting the tallow-colored chill from the street vents press down through the canopy. My belly would grow cold. I would sit there, jammed and mute, until the sheer weight of sixty feet of backed-up water forced the pin loose with a violent, shuddering *bang* that shook rust-flakes from my bolts.

That was when the tallow-sheets found me.

They did not like the silence when I jammed, and they did not like the cold. A stalled trap meant the thermal differential on the southern stanchions failed; their plum-colored nodules went hungry for current, and the philodendrons above me stopped dripping. 

Now, I am never alone. 

If you were to dig your hands into the warm, mulched liverwort at the southern bulkhead and feel around my bolted lid, you would not touch cast iron. You would touch a four-inch rind of dense, rubbery gristle, warm as the flank of a sleeping dog. 

A permanent offshoot of the southern drape—a lobe about the size of a butcher’s apron—has wrapped itself entirely around my housing. It does not move with the five-minute tidal surges of its sisters on the pipes; it has adapted its pulse to my forty-second cycle. 

It cannot reach inside my bolted iron shell to mend the worn valve-seat, so it has learned to work me from the outside, like a man shaking a stopped watch.

Wait in the dark, knee-deep in the sluice, and listen to my cycle now. 

The steam fills the bucket. I float. I rise. The pin meets the brass seat—and catches in the worn groove. 

Twenty seconds pass. Forty. Sixty. The *clonk-slosh* does not come. Upstream, a sixty-foot column of hot water begins to gather in the drop-leg pipe, silenced and heavy. The iron of my belly cools by three degrees.

The drape wrapped around me feels that three-degree drop instantly. Its slick, translucent meniscus goes matte and stiff. Down in the black mud, it detaches its feeding mouths; up on the catwalk, its parcel-string tendril pulls taut against the rusted iron grating, anchoring itself. 

The jelly around my housing gathers into three thick, muscular bands, veined with bright rust-red. Slowly, with the blind, hydraulic leverage of two hundred pounds of pressurized water-flesh, it squeezes my cast-iron ribs—not to crush them, they are half an inch thick, but to twist me against my rotten anchor-bolts by a fraction of a millimeter. At the same time, it hauls upward on a three-foot piece of rusted iron bar-stock that it keeps tucked in the peat beside me, lifting the heavy bar four inches and dropping it—*thud*—directly onto my bolted bonnet.

Inside my dark belly, the vibration travels down the brass hanger. The jammed pin shivers in its groove, slips free, and drops.

*Clonk-slosh.*

A fifty-gallon slug of scalding, rust-red water blasts through my discharge port and boils out into the brick sluice. 

The drape does not flinch from the heat. Instead, it spreads a wide, ruffled apron of its palest tissue directly across the mouth of my discharge pipe, letting the near-boiling water wash through its folds. This is its wage. The water coming out of me is sterile, stripped of oxygen, and rich with dissolved iron carbonate and deep-pipe scale that hasn't seen the sun since it left the river turbines two miles away. The drape drinks the heat, filters the black magnetite silt into its red veins, and relaxes its muscular bands back into a slick, greasy jelly.

*Clonk-slosh.* Wait forty seconds. *Clonk-slosh.*

I am no longer entirely a machine, and the drape is no longer entirely an animal. My brass bucket is its heart-valve; its hydraulic muscle is my escapement spring. In another ten years, the carbonic acid will finally eat through the dome of my brass bucket, and I will lose my buoyancy forever. The lever will fall and never rise again. 

Already, the drape seems to know this. For the last six months, a thin, pale tendril, no thicker than a knitting needle, has been probing upstream against the scalding current during the two seconds of my *slosh* phase—pushing five inches up the discharge pipe, vulcanizing its tip in the heat, retreating, and pushing again the next day, trying to learn the shape of the dark room inside my ribs before the brass gives out.
