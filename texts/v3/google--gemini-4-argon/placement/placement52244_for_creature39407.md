To find me, you have to look up, past the yellow cones of the crane lights, into the dripping, forty-foot-deep lattice of the primary roof-trusses where the warm updrafts from the floor-vents meet the freezing sweat of the galvanized ceiling. 

At first, I look like a catastrophic failure of the plumbing—a massive, slumped bladder of perished neoprene and yellowed fiberglass insulation, slung like a hammock between two high-pressure coolant mains. I am the size of a butcher’s stall, pale and greasy, anchored to the cold iron by thick, ropy tendons of extruded adhesive that have cured into the rivet-seams. 

I am what the maintenance subroutines, in their corrupt, endlessly looping diagnostic logs, classify as an *Acoustic Damper (Non-Standard)*. The Tappers, who know better, call me the **Drop-Lung**, or simply the **Tally**.

I did not evolve from the rats or the men or the eels. I came down from the tool-cribs. Four thousand years ago, my ancestors were semi-biological peristaltic bladders and self-healing acoustic baffles—engineered organs grown in vats to be strapped around the joints of the great pipes, designed to swallow the violent hydraulic hammer of the pumps and keep the structural steel from fatiguing. When the central oversight failed and the replacement crews stopped coming, the baffles did not die. Stuffed with caloric lubricants and warmed by the friction of the water they were built to quiet, they slipped their rusted housing-straps, oozed along the pipe-runs, merged in the dark, and learned to eat the noise.

I am a creature of pure, sluggish piezo-metabolism. I do not hunt; I do not even have a mouth in the way the Dredges do. My underbelly is a dense, shivering mat of calcified cilia and heavy, lead-weighted fronds that hang down into the freezing mist, brushing against the top flanges of the I-beams and the casings of the transfer pipes. When the forty-second pressure wave from the northern louvers hits, or when a gantry-paver grinds past eighty feet below, my whole three-ton mass shudders in sympathy. I take that kinetic violence—the bone-shaking, tooth-loosening roar that would tear the pipes apart—and I drink it. My internal membranes flex, generating a trickle of bio-electric current that splits the sulfur-heavy condensation trickling down my flanks, turning the endless, deafening vibration of the Vault into slow, cold fat.

Because I absorb the fury of the steel, the space directly beneath me is the only quiet place in the world. 

Below my sagging, oil-slicked belly, there is a cone of dead air thirty feet wide where the forty-second barometric wheeze is blunted to a dull sigh, and the scream of the high-pressure sluices drops an octave into a warm, rhythmic purr. Furthermore, the inefficiency of my heavy conversion-organs bleeds off a steady, feverish thirty degrees of waste heat. The freezing sleet of the Vault never reaches the concrete beneath me; it melts on my broad, rubbery back and runs down my mooring-tendons in warm, mineral-sweet rivulets.

I am an oasis, and I charge a toll.

I cannot move quickly—it takes me three weeks to haul my bulk fifty yards along a pipe-run when a pump-station finally cycles down for the century—and I cannot reach down to the floor to gather the rich, heavy trace-metals I need to repair my lead-weighted cilia. Copper, molybdenum, and sheared high-carbon steel do not float up into the trusses on their own.

So I trade with the Tappers.

If you climb the slick diagonal bracing of Tower 14 during the long grey hours between purges, you will find four or five of them wedged into the dry, warm folds of my lower mantle, folded up like pocket-knives. Their putty-colored skin is pressed flat against my pulsing underside, their milky eyes shut, sleeping the deep, twitching sleep of warm-blooded things that have finally stopped shivering. 

In return, they feed my folds. Before a Tapper climbs up into my heat-shadow to sleep, it reaches into its grease-pouch and pushes a handful of heavy scrap—a rusted bearing-race, a clipped copper grounding-wire, the bronze tooth of a broken gear—deep into the acidic, muscular clefts along my flanks. My flesh closes over the metal with a slow, wet *schlupp*, dissolving the alloys over months to re-plate my acoustic fronds. Sometimes they bring me the chitinous, galvanized shells of Grease-Ticks, or the copper-scaled lateral lines stripped from a Silt-Eel.

We have a language, the Tappers and I, though it is not a conversation of peers. It is the conversation between a bell and the sexton.

Because my mooring-tendons are rooted directly into the primary structural spine of the Vault, and my whole body is an instrument built to read stress-harmonics, I feel the basin not as a geography, but as a single, trembling web of tension. I feel the hairline fractures forming in Hexagon 404-West three days before the gantry-paver’s sensors register the sag. I feel the deep, rhythmic peristalsis of two Dredges mating in the sub-floor slurry four kilometers to the south, their stone-armored backs grinding against the turbine housings. I know when the western sluice-valves are building up the hydraulic head for an off-schedule purge twenty minutes before the pneumatic sirens even begin to draw breath.

And because I like my Tappers alive—because they are my hands on the floor, my little iron-gatherers—I tell them.

I have three heavy, bone-dense nodules grown into the tips of my longest lower tendrils, resting directly against the vertical H-column of the tower. When I feel the distant, sub-audible *clunk* of the western sluice-accumulators locking into their purge-cycle, I contract my lower mantle and drop those nodules against the steel.

*Bong. Bong-bong. Bong.*

It is a deeper, wetter sound than their rebar strikes—a low, brassy resonance that rolls down the H-column, shoots out through the rebar mesh of the basalt hexagons, and hums up through the calloused soles of every Tapper within two miles. Down in the cherenkov-blue twilight of the trenches, the hunting parties freeze. They feel my heavy, three-beat warning in their ankles. Within seconds, they have packed their grease-pots, folded their hinged ribs, and slipped down into the warm expansion joints, long before the first white wall of freezing foam comes roaring out of the dark.

Later, when the water drains and the Plumb-birds are clicking frantically across the wet stone, I will feel the tiny, sharp *tak-tak-tak* of a Tapper’s iron bar hitting the base of my tower. 

*Safe,* the vibration says, traveling up the steel into my belly. *Meat-heavy. Coming up.*

I do not think in words. I think in pressure-gradients, in the sweet, sour taste of dissolving bronze, and in the long, slow chords of the cooling towers. When I feel their iron tap, I simply slacken my lower folds, opening a warm, dry pocket above the sleet, and wait for the small, putty-colored things to climb up out of the cold and press their listening chests against my heart.
