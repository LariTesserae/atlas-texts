I would not be on the chain. My knees are done—the left one was caught under the knuckle of a whip-fern stem twelve seasons ago during a false lull, and while the bone knitted thick as a club, the joint no longer splays flat enough to let my pelvis kiss the gneiss. If I went out into the open basin now, my hip would sit three inches too high. The wind would find that three-inch lip of flesh, get a thumb under it, and peel me off the mountain like a scab.

So I belong to the groove, and to the stone that sings at the mouth of it.

You would find me forty paces northeast of the third hull in the Long Gouge—a glacial scratch deep enough that two people can lie side-by-side in the bottom of it without their shoulder blades clearing the rim. The Gouge doesn't have a roof of hide all the way along; for most of its length it is only roofed by a permanent, braided weave of living cable-moss that we have trained and pinned across the gap with bone pegs over three generations. It is a green tunnel, wet as a throat, smelling of crushed pennyroyal and the dark, sour silt that collects under our bellies.

At the northern nose of this trench, wedged into the spot where the glacier dropped a final, stubborn tooth of black ironstone into the paler gneiss, stands the Seventh Harp. 

That is my charge. I am the tuner.

The people on the chains think the Harps are eternal because they are stone, and because they were raised before our grandmothers’ grandmothers lost the use of their throats. They think the monoliths simply sit there and tell the truth through the bedrock. They do not see what the air actually does to them.

The wind here is not clean. It looks clean—just sunny, empty sky—but it carries the microscopic teeth of the lower world suspended in its wet heat. It carries salt from the hidden sea, and fine, invisible silica ground off the western cliffs, and the acidic slime of burst catching-sacs. Over five or six seasons, that hot, screaming blast will scour the lip of a *C-sharp* bore-hole until it widens by the thickness of a thumbnail. And when it widens, the note goes flat. 

A flat note on the Seventh Harp means the hunting chain two miles south feels a *C-natural* in their palms instead of the sharp. They think the shear-wall is coming from the south-southeast with six minutes of slushy grace, when in truth it is coming straight out of the east-northeast with eighty-mile-an-hour teeth and ninety seconds to spare. A flat note is four dead cousins tumbling toward the rim of the world with their cowls half-unlatched.

So when the dead-eye drops—when the terrible, ear-popping silence hits and the green roof above the trench goes heavy and limp—I am the first one out through the grease-slit.

I do not move like the young runners, with that wide, rhythmic, four-limbed skitter. I drag myself like a crushed beetle, pulling with my elbows and my massive, hook-fingered hands, my heavy belly sliding along the slick groove we’ve worn into the rock between the trench and the Harp. My chin never leaves the stone. My jawbone is my ear. Even in the dead calm, the plateau is never truly still; three miles down, the roots of the mountain are being shoved by the weight of the sea-chimney, and the gneiss has a baseline hum—a deep, sub-audible *thrummm* that you feel in your back teeth.

When I reach the base of the Harp, I do not look up at it. Looking up is a sickness. To tilt your chin toward the blue bowl is to invite the sky to pull your eyes out of your head. I stay pressed against its stumpy, chest-high root, wedging my hip-cam into the anchor-slot my grandfather chipped into the leeward base, and I reach up with my hands to read its face.

The stone is warm. It is always warm, heated by the friction of the gale that just died. The lower holes—the big, roaring bass apertures the size of my thigh that face the predominant western updraft—are coated inside with a grey, crusty rime of dried plant-mucilage and blown beetle-chitin. 

I work fast, by touch, with my eyes half-gummed shut against the drying air of the vacuum. My kit is strapped flat to my chest so I can lie on it if the wind comes back early: three wedges of native copper, a pouch of coarse garnet-sand that we trade for with the people who live near the eastern rim-cracks, a bone scraper, and six plugs of dense, pitch-soaked ironwood.

If an aperture has worn too wide, I cannot shrink the stone. Instead, I have to build a lip. I scrape away the green slime with the bone tool, score the inside of the bore with a garnet-stone, and hammer a thin, curved shim of cold copper into the hairline crack at the edge of the hole, changing the angle at which the wind will bite it. 

Sometimes, I am told by the matriarchs in the dark of the hull to change the song entirely. 

The spill-forests are not static; over twenty years, a fifty-acre mat of sail-vine will slowly creep half a mile north as its southern roots rot and its northern runners find new silt-cracks. When a forest moves, the wind over the bare stone changes its shape. An eddy that used to hit the Harp from the north-northwest begins to curve around the new green hill of foliage and strike from the north-by-east. The old hole goes dumb, whistling uselessly into the wrong current, while the new direction hits solid, unbored rock and gives no warning at all.

Then, my work takes months, four minutes at a time.

I lie wedged against the monolith during the steady blows—not the cyclonic shifts, which would tear me out of my harness, but the long, two-hour afternoon gales from the west when the Harp itself creates a three-foot pocket of dead, screaming turbulence on its eastern face. Curled in that tiny shadow of stone, while the air three inches past my heels is moving fast enough to strip the flesh from a bird, I brace a heavy drill-shaft of fire-hardened wood against my calloused sternum. I put a pinch of wet garnet-sand into the dimple on the rock face, and I spin the shaft between my palms.

*Grind. Grind. Grind.*

Above me, the Harp is singing at full volume. If you have never lain with your chest pressed against a two-ton block of quartzite while it turns a ninety-mile-an-hour gale into a triad of *F-sharp, A, and D*, you cannot know what sound actually is. It is not something in the air. It is a physical furnace. The stone grows hot against my ribs. The vibration is so violent that my vision blurs into grey streaks; my organs feel as though they are being sieved through my spine. If I did not keep a thick leather bit clenched between my molars, the Harp would rattle my teeth into powder within an hour. 

I become part of the instrument. As I lean my weight into the drill-shaft, my own dense, fat-padded body dampens the stone slightly, shifting the pitch down a quarter-tone. Two miles away, in the under-drift beneath the whip-ferns, a hunter pauses with his copper knife at the throat of a catching-sac, feeling that slight, rhythmic *wah-wah-wah* pulsing through the rock into his belly. 

He knows what it is. He taps twice on the ankle of the woman ahead of him: *The Broken Knee is boring the north-east eye.*

Inside the hull, when the day-gale turns into the erratic, murderous cross-chop of dusk and we are all sealed in over the rot-pot, I have a strange place in the pile. 

Because I spend my hours pressed against the singing rock, my hands do not stop trembling when I come underground. The frequencies stay in the nerves of my palms and the heavy muscles of my forearms for hours after the hatch is greased shut. When we strip off our cowls in the ninety-degree stench of the fermenting kelp and tangle ourselves together to sleep and talk, the others like to put their backs or their bellies against my hands.

I am noisy, even in the dark. My fingers twitch with the day's weather. While the matriarch is tapping out an old, rhythmic census on my thigh—*how many bladders of oil remain in the deep cache, which girl in the Fourth Hull has come of age to be corded to our youngest boy*—my own palms are absentmindedly drumming the day's wind-history against the ribs of the children sleeping across my shins. 

I give them the shape of the sky they are never allowed to look at. 

*Here,* my left thumb says against a five-year-old’s breastbone, *this was the long, warm blow from the salt-chimney after midday—heavy and wet, four fingers wide, smelling of boiled crab.*

*And here,* my right fingers flutter sharp and fast against the soft skin under her arm, *this was the little shear that tried to sneak around the bladder-kelp at three-quarter sun—a high, nasty G-natural that would have flipped your mother’s chain if I hadn't shimmed the third hole last month.*

The children learn the plateau from my tremors before they ever leave the Gouge. By the time they are old enough to have their ears bound flat and take their first short-cord crawl into the cable-moss, their bodies already know the music. 

They think I am very wise, and very heavy—the heaviest person in the hull, because I am always anchored to the biggest stone. They do not know the secret that lives in my bad knee, or in the tips of my horn-thick fingers.

When you tune a Harp, you have to reach up into the highest holes—the small, shrieking apertures near the crown of the monolith, four feet off the ground, where the wind is fastest and cleanest. To reach them during a lull, I have to unclip my belly-shortened tether, let out three feet of slack, and push myself up onto my one good knee and my elbows. 

My head clears the boundary layer. My face rises into the space where nothing is supposed to be.

Every time I do it, even in the dead-eye, there is a draft up there—a high, thin, cool stream of air that never touches the soapy gneiss. It smells of nothing at all. Not rot, not pennyroyal, not copper-sap or old grease. Just empty blue distance. 

For twenty seconds, while I jam my copper gauge into the upper whistle-hole to test its bevel, I am taller than the spill-forests. I can look over the flat, miles-wide carpets of green lying dead on the rock, out to where the grey stone curves down toward the edge of the world and the pale haze of the hidden sea boils into the sky. My spine, bowed and compressed by fifty years of crawling, gives a small, terrifying *pop* as it straightens by two inches.

I understand the *ballooners* then. I understand why a man wipes the grease from his chest with dry moss. It is not that he hates the hull, or the warm, stinking dark of the pile, or the safety of the groove. It is just that the stone is so terribly hard, and we have been pressed against it for so long, and four feet above our backs there is a country where nobody has to hold on.

Then the Harp gives a tiny, sub-audible *tick* against my collarbone—the deep rock taking the first microscopic flex of a ten-ton wall of air rolling over the western lip three miles away. 

The terror comes back into my throat like bile. My nostrils slam shut by reflex. I drop like a stone, jamming my cam into the root-crack, pulling the heavy, stinking kelp-cowl over my head and sealing the rim to the rock with a frantic smear of sap just as the Seventh Harp opens its throat above me and screams in *C-sharp*, and the green sky falls back down to bury me alive.
