You would not find me by looking at the vents. The vents are too loud, too simple—just single-note horns blowing the same hot breath into the silver sky. And you would not find me in the slow, greasy roll of the amalgam, because the amalgam only wakes for an error, and I am only awake when things are right.

To find me, you have to walk out into the dead flats between the chimneys, where the pale anhydrite clay is baked nearly dry and the shallow channels of cutting fluid cross one another in long, lazy X’s. You have to look down at the clay where three of those channels meet, about a third of a mile south of the eleven-second boiler and half a mile west of the three-beat cluster.

At first, you would think a Tappet-Walker had died there and been pressed into the mud by the passing of a grazer. 

I look like a dropped frame—a low, triangular truss of dull, dark-gray plumbago and bog-iron, about the size of a carpenter’s square, lying flat and flush in the clay. My three corners are spiked deep into the anhydrite, anchored below the slick surface layer into the hardpan where the ground-tremor runs cleanest. Between the three iron bars of my frame, stretched tight and just barely clearing the damp mud, are eleven parallel wires of spun, copper-nickel alloy, graduated in thickness from the gauge of a heavy fencing staple down to the width of a hair.

I do not walk. Walking is for things that haven’t found the center of the room yet. 

Five years ago—though there are no years here, only a certain number of millions of strokes from the eleven-second stack—I was a small, six-legged thing like a pair of dividers, scuttling across the basin floor with my belly-wires slack. I spent my youth listening to the ground through my toes, moving three inches east, stopping for an hour, moving two inches north, waiting for the beat to settle. I was looking for a node. Because the basin is six miles of cast rock and the vents never move, their shockwaves travel through the crust and cross one another in fixed, invisible lattices. In most places, the waves hash together into a dull, muddled rumble. 

Here, at this exact junction of three grease-channels, the eleven-second boom from the north, the *clank-clank-clank* from the east, and the slow, forty-second *thump* of a deep-seated wide-bore vent a mile to the southwest arrive through the clay and cross each other cleanly. 

When I found this spot, I drove my feet into the mud, locked my knee-joints with a secretion of fast-setting iron silicate, and let my legs fuse into a permanent bed-frame. Then I tuned myself.

If you kneel beside me—careful not to let the shadow of your knee cool my wires by half a degree, or you’ll throw my pitch off—you won’t see me move. You will only see the eleven wires blurring, one after another, in a silent, shimmering cascade. 

*Chuff-CLANK* goes the eleven-second vent to the north. Half a second later, the ground-wave hits my northern anchor-spike. My fourth wire—the one tuned to the exact frequency of that stack’s drop—blurs into a grey ribbon, humming a low, metallic *bzzzz* that you can only hear if you press your ear to the clay. Before it can die down, the three-beat cluster in the east fires, and my seventh, eighth, and ninth wires answer in a tight, ringing chord.

I am a harp that plays the shop floor. Like the hematite limpets on the stacks, my anchor-roots are lined with piezoelectric quartz, drinking the raw kinetic kick of the earth. If I simply let that energy sit in me, it would cook my tissues or shake my brittle iron frame to pieces. So I turn the excess into tension, and I bleed the tension off as heat and hum along my strings. 

Because my strings are warm and vibrating at high, crisp frequencies, the air two inches above my frame is a tiny thermal column. When the oily rags of condensed steam drift low across the flats, they hit the warm shimmer above my wires and break. The microscopic droplets of suspended iron-sulfate and naphtha don’t just fall on me; the acoustic standing wave around my strings sorts them by weight. The heavy, sour water is bounced away into the runoff channel; the fine, rich mist of pure mineral oil and copper-dust is trapped in the nodes of the vibration, hanging in the air above my belly in tiny, trembling beads before settling slowly onto my strings like rosin on a bow.

I eat the mist by humming it out of the air.

Mostly, I am alone, which is the same as being everywhere, because through the clay I can feel the whole six-mile skillet. I know when a twelve-legged grazer is a quarter-mile away; its stiff, piston-legged *stomp-stomp-stomp* comes through the mud as a clumsy, off-beat static that makes my thickest bass wire rattle against its stop-pin. When the grazer passes right over me—its broad brass belly clearing my flat frame by four inches, smelling of hot tallow and scraped rubber—I pull my tuning-pegs back a fraction of a millimeter, slacking my wires so the heavy, scraping *shhk-shhk* of its snout doesn’t snap my high strings. A droplet of clear joint-grease falls from its third left leg and lands squarely on my frame; within ten minutes, a blue-black feeler-gauge colony of scavenger zooids slides out of the drainage channel, flows over my iron bar, licks the tallow clean without touching my vibrating wires, and slides back into the ditch. It leaves my frame gleaming like a newly oiled gun-barrel.

Sometimes, a Tappet-Walker on its four-day march will stop beside me.

It is the only thing in the basin that notices me, because we are in the same trade. The Walker will plant its four pointed calliper legs in the clay around my triangular frame, standing directly over me like a crane over a barge. Its internal mainspring is half-unwound, clicking softly—*tink... tink... tink*—as its internal mills grind away. 

For ten minutes, the Walker simply stands there, its long, counterweighted boom hanging slack, listening to my eleven strings ring out the exact, unadulterated schedule of the surrounding mile. It uses me like a tuning fork. If one of its legs has worn down a hundredth of an inch on the silica glaze, throwing its gait out of true, it will rest the tip of that iron toe lightly against my third string, feeling the pure eleven-second pulse until it can adjust the tension in its hip-ratchet to match. Then it lifts its foot, gives a single, sharp *click* of its hub, and marches off into the white glare.

I do not care where it goes. I have no boom to wind, no mate to look for, no valve to grind back to true. 

Wait here long enough—until your knees are soaked with warm cutting fluid and the smell of hot iron has become the only smell in the world—and you will hear what I wait for. It happens once every six hours, twenty-two minutes, and fourteen seconds. 

The eleven-second boiler to the north, the three-cylinder marine cluster to the east, the deep forty-second thumper to the southwest, and four smaller, high-pressure whistles two miles out toward the basalt rim all drop their stone lids on the exact same tenth of a second.

Across the valley, the air doesn't even sound different—just a slightly heavier *chuff-CLANK* buried in the general din. 

But down in the anhydrite clay, five separate shockwaves rush toward my junction and meet dead-center beneath my frame. For one second, all eleven of my wires blur at once. My frame groans in its silicate sockets. A single, pure, brassy chord—neither major nor minor, just the exact resonant frequency of six miles of wet iron—rings out from the mud, loud enough to cut right through the chugging of the valley. A thin, straight line of blue-white piezoelectric spark jumps across the gap between my bass wire and my iron frame with a sharp *snap*, burning off the afternoon’s buildup of naphtha-soot in a tiny puff of sweet, oily smoke.

Then the waves pass through each other. The chord breaks back into eleven separate, patient hums. The oily fog drifts low over the clay, catching on the warmth of my strings, and I go back to keeping the count.
