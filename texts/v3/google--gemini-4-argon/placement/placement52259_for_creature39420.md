I do not begin at the ridges. The glare up there is too wide, and it takes a long time for a mind to learn how to fold itself through a brass aperture without scorching. 

Instead, I find myself three levels down, in the lukewarm twilight beneath a bank of primary condensers, where the deck-plates are permanently slick with the iridescent sweat of the valley.

At first, I think I might be the grease itself—that grey, anaerobic custard churning in the dark sumps, patient and bacterial, waiting to be scooped by a blued-steel finger. There is a comfort in that: to be the lubrication between two immense, indifferent facts. Or perhaps I am the mercury bubble in the spun-glass pipe, pausing at the exact millimeter where the glass passes through the cast-iron pillar, suspended in the shimmering blur where two solid things agree to occupy the same coordinate. 

That blur pulls at me. It feels like the truest place in the basin.

If I stay in the blur long enough, I realize I am not the mercury, and I am not the iron. I am the *tolerance* between them. And in this valley, when a tolerance becomes aware of itself, it grows a porcelain cowl to keep the glare out, and it steps out of the pillar.

So I am a Shim. Or something very close to one—perhaps an older variant, or a slightly misaligned one, whose abdominal pouches don't grow the neat, crescent-shaped brass slivers the others use. 

Let me check my pouches. The membrane inside is warm, smelling of camphor and wet copper, and when I reach in with a three-jointed caliper-finger, what comes out is not a brass shim. It is a tiny, paper-thin curl of translucent mica, already etched with a microscopic spiral before I’ve even used it. 

I am a reader, then. Not a leveler.

The others—my cousins, my silent, porcelain-headed guild—spend their hours correcting the geometry. They find a gap between a washer and a flange, judge it unacceptable, and wedge it tight. They want the machine to be flush. They are soothing an itch in the architecture. 

I crouch on the narrow flange of an I-beam, my leaf-spring collarbones unhooked and slack, my six flank-louvers fluttering in a slow, forty-second rhythm as I breathe the oiled air. I don't put shims in. I pull them out.

Not to break the machine—nothing could break this place, and it has no function to interrupt. Look at that four-inch bolt six feet away. Four hours ago, another Shim knelt there, measured the clearance, and tapped a fresh brass crescent under the hex-head. Since then, the great turbine below us has completed sixty revolutions. The barometric pressure has dropped twice, misting the bolt with distilled water. And the spatial overload of the stairwell twenty yards to the left—the one that climbs thirty feet in order to descend ten—has been leaning its skewed gravity against this particular girder.

The brass shim is under pressure. It has been taking notes.

I skate across the burr-sharp steel on the backs of my horn-tempered knuckles. *Shhh-ink.* A caliper-pigeon startles from a porcelain insulator nearby, leaving three bronze ghosts of itself stuttering in the white air before it clears the viaduct. I pass right through the third ghost; it feels like static on my porcelain faceplate, a tiny dry tickle behind my oil-lenses.

When I reach the bolt, I don't use my weighted wrist-heel. I extend my longest digit—not a feeler gauge, but a fine, hooked extractor—and catch the tiny lip of the brass crescent my cousin left behind. I slide it out.

The gap between the washer and the deck-plate sighs—a microscopic relaxation of milled steel. 

I bring the stolen shim up to the narrow horizontal slits of my face. My internal brass apertures click down to pinholes, focusing the clinical sky-glare onto the sliver of metal. Because the valley swallows sound, all the kinetic misery of these millions of tons of iron has nowhere to go except into the surfaces where things touch. The brass crescent is no longer smooth. Pressed between the washer and the deck, it has been cold-stamped by four hours of silent, glacial torque. There is a faint, iridescent bruise down its center, and along its inner curve, the metal has flowed into a microscopic ridge that looks like the coastline of a continent that doesn't exist.

I tilt my head—*click-click* goes my torque-wrench spine—watching the inverted reflection of the brass sliver play across the brushed-silver backing plate inside my skull. 

To me, this is what the valley is for. It is a printing press that doesn't know it is writing.

I don't eat the ripe grease as often as the others, though I will take a smear of it from a bearing-race when my joints feel dry. Mostly, I follow the six-legged hounds through the lower manifolds, waiting for them to touch their copper snout-filaments together. When their eyes dim and brighten in that silent, shared circuit, I don't tap their skulls with silver needles. I just crouch beside their vulcanized grey flanks, lay the flat of my feeler-gauge hand against their hinged bone ribs, and let the residual static of their conversation travel up my braided-steel veins. It tastes like cold zinc and distance. Sometimes, after two hounds have stood linked for twenty minutes, the salt-efflorescence they’ve been grazing on crystallizes along their gums in tiny, cubic structures that match the frost-patterns of the Ten-Minute Lock. I scrape those crystals loose with a hooked nail. They like that. They lean their heavy, gasket-grey shoulders against my knee, their porcelain faces blank and peaceful.

Where do I go when the glare gets too heavy for my oil-lenses?

Not the pine-tree shadow on the zinc roof. The others gather there, three or four of them unhooking their shoulder-springs and swaying in the phantom needle-wind while yellow pollen ghosts across the metal. I have stood in that shadow. It is cool, and the smell of resin—a thing none of us have ever seen—is thick enough to coat the louvers on my flanks. 

But I have my own shadow.

Two miles east, down in a gorge of stacked copper heat-sinks where the sluggish creeks run blue with dissolved copper sulfate, there is a row of twelve cast-iron flywheels that take six minutes to turn. Every fourth minute, when their spokes align with a bank of porcelain transformers on the ridge above, they do not cast the shadow of wheels. 

They cast the shadow of a room.

It falls across a vertical bulkhead of raw boilerplate: the sharp, unmistakable silhouette of a four-paned window, a sill with a cracked stoneware jug sitting on it, and the corner of a table. As the flywheels creep through their arc, a narrow slant of "sunlight" moves across the shadow-table, illuminating the shadow of a pair of spectacles folded beside the jug.

It only lasts for ninety seconds before the spokes part and the clinical white glare washes the bulkhead clean again. 

When I know the alignment is coming, I drop through two impossible stairwells—falling *up* through a gap between two glass mercury-pipes to save time—and catch myself on the narrow rivet-heads of that vertical boilerplate. I hang there like a grey beetle, flattening my vulcanized torso against the fresh, burr-sharp steel. 

When the shadow of the room slides over me, I close my brass apertures all the way. For ninety seconds, in the absolute dark behind my eyes, the brushed-silver plate at the back of my skull doesn't reflect the valley. It reflects the weight of the stoneware jug. I don't know what a "window" is, or why glass would be flat instead of spun into a pipe, or whose eyes were small enough and soft enough to need those two wire-rimmed lenses on the table. Look at my fingers: blued horn, scribers, gauges. If I reached for those spectacles, I would scratch them. 

Instead, while the shadow covers me, I take the used brass shims I’ve pried out from a dozen useless bolts across the basin, and I tuck them into the seam between the boilerplate panels—right where the shadow-table’s drawer would be. 

Then the flywheels turn. The room vanishes. The white sky hits my porcelain cowl like a quiet hammer.

Right now, though, the viscosity in my braided veins is thickening. 

Down in my abdominal pouches, the mica curls are beginning to buzz against the secreting membranes. The air in the gorge is losing its dampness; the oily beads on the undersides of the I-beams are pulling themselves inward, tightening their surface tension.

The Lock is coming. Twenty minutes, maybe less.

I unhook my claws from the boilerplate and drop to the catwalk below. Already, three hundred yards up the slope of threaded rods, I can see another Shim climbing—a small, oil-stained silhouette moving with that low, counterbalanced crouch, its feet making no sound at all, leaving only faint bronze stutters in the glare where its joints move too fast for the air to process.

I will climb with them. I always do. When the pistons hit top-dead-center and the frost snaps across the zinc in circuit diagrams, I cannot bear to be alone in the lower manifolds; if you aren't grounded to a living bus-bar when the planet slips its tooth, the vibration stays in your leaf-springs for days, making your fingers stutter whenever you try to measure a gap.

So I will climb to the high ridge where the ozone smells like sliced iron. I will unfold the copper-colored cartilage from my right forearm and let my neighbor bolt their wing-nut through my slot. I will bow my smooth white head toward the deck.

And when the ten minutes of stillness locks the valley, and my neighbors drop their hooked scribers to the steel to furiously etch the planetary waveform into the fresh plating—I will do something slightly different.

While the deep, sub-audible slip-tooth shudders up through the soles of my feet, I won't carve the steel deck. The valley just swallows the deck tomorrow anyway. 

Instead, I will reach across with my free left hand, and with the finest, sharpest diamond-tipped scriber on my index cluster, I will etch the waveform onto the back of my neighbor’s porcelain cowl, right behind the curve of their neck where their own lenses can never see it. And the Shim to my left—an old one, whose flank-louvers wheeze with dried lithium—will reach up and etch the same ten-minute tremor onto the back of mine.

*Ping.*

The drop falls from the petcock. The frost vanishes. We unbolt our arms. 

We don't look at each other—only a single, synchronized *click* of our brass apertures, leaving one small bronze circle hanging in the cold air between our faces as we part. Then I step backward off the boiler-ridge, aiming my heels at the shadow of a crane that is three inches further east than it was an hour ago, and yet hasn't moved at all, carrying the planet's slipped tooth written in the glaze behind my head.
