At first, I am the amber light.

I think I am the cabinet because it is the only thing in the pit that seems to be counting. *Blink. Pause. Blink.* A small, dry pocket of logic suspended on steel legs above the soup of grease and silica. If I am in that box, I am a set of relays measuring Resistance, Temperature, RPM, translating the brute thermal violence of the earth into a clean digit on a screen five miles away where someone is drinking coffee under fluorescent lights. 

Wait. No. If I am in the box, I am waiting for a fault. My only purpose is to notice when the line goes flat, when the resistance spikes, when the green beast in the corner chokes on its own scale and dies. That is too narrow a life—to be a tiny yellow eye waiting for a catastrophe in a room where nothing has a name.

I drop down the stilts, past the spiral of marble-sized eggs where the little white pinpricks are turning like compass needles in their jelly. 

For a moment I think I might be the water itself—the six-hour lung of the basin, rising an inch to drown the orange rings of algae, sinking an inch to leave a crust of salt like a dried lip on the black glass. To be the tide in a teacup of shattered rock. To smell of wet pennies and hot lard. 

Or the grease. The grease is interesting. The way the machine sweats petroleum from its gasket seams, and the snails sweat their grey, vulcanized mucus, and on the surface of the triangular pool the two oils meet and do not know they are from different kingdoms. They make a single rainbow skin over the ninety-degree water. A truce between the Carboniferous Period and the Holocene, floating between the sharp basalt polygons.

No. Closer to the noise. Everything here belongs to the noise.

If you stay in this pit for an hour, your skull ceases to be bone and becomes a tuning fork. You cannot think *around* the sixty-cycle hum; you have to think *with* it. Your thoughts must have the same wavelength as the shaft spinning inside the green iron. 

I am in the seam.

Specifically, I am the gasket of the lower inspection hatch on the north face of the turbine housing, four inches below the high-water mark.

I was poured into a mold in Ohio or Guangdong—a synthetic rubber compound, black nitrile or Viton, cured with sulfur to survive three hundred degrees of steam and twelve atmospheres of pressure. I am a flat, continuous ring, a quarter-inch thick, pinched in the dark between two flanges of two-inch cast iron, held fast by sixteen hexagonal bolts torqued to three hundred foot-pounds.

I am the only thing keeping the inside from becoming the outside.

On my inner edge, a half-inch away from the raw, howling steam that drives the blades, the world is pure velocity. It is white-hot vapor tearing past steel vanes at the speed of sound, a dry, invisible hurricane that smells of deep granite and split atoms of sulfur. 

On my outer edge, where my black rubber lip protrudes a millimeter between the bolted iron jaws, the world is wet, slow, and heavy.

I do not have eyes, so I know the pit by the difference in pressure between the sixteen bolts, and by the interference patterns in the scream.

For eight months after the cranes lifted their hooks into the blue square of sky and left us here, the scream was clean. It was a straight, bright wire of sound. The iron flanges pinched me with an even, dead weight. Every six hours the hot water rose over my lower crescent, cooled me by three degrees, and dropped away, leaving a microscopic grit of calcium in the threads of Bolt Number Nine.

Then the weight changed.

It began as a dulling of the treble on the lower left quadrant of the hatch. Something heavy and wet slid over the iron three inches from my lip. Where the metal had been vibrating at a sharp, clean kilohertz—a thin, ringing screech—it suddenly went *thud-hum*, muffled by forty pounds of dense, rubbery meat.

I felt them before they touched me. I felt their teeth.

*Tick. Tick. Tick.*

Through two inches of gray cast iron, the magnetite radulae sound like someone tapping a sewing needle against an anvil. A tiny, dry, metallic *scritch* that travels through the crystalline lattice of the metal and into my rubber belly. 

They are eating the house. 

I can feel the paint going first. Even that thin skin of blistered industrial green had a weight, a slight dampening effect on the skin of the machine. As the snails rasp it off, the iron ring around me sings a fraction of a hertz higher, bare to the wet air, before the rust forms again like a velvet scab for them to lick away the next afternoon.

They love the bolts best. The sixteen hex-heads around me are little iron mountains where the vibration concentrates into sharp, humming points. The largest snail—the one whose shell is five years of white cave-chalk rimmed with six months of thick, green-flecked furnace-crust—parks itself directly over Bolt Four and Bolt Five every evening when the sun leaves the pit.

Its foot comes down over the gap between the flanges. Over me.

When it seals itself against the hatch, the heat beneath its belly spikes. We are two rubbers—the synthetic black nitrile from the factory and the pale, greasy muscle from the crushed dark two hundred feet below—pressed face to face in the scalding dark. 

Its foot does not scrape me. The iron teeth pass over my millimeter of exposed lip without biting; I am too soft, I give beneath the magnetite ribbon like dead skin, offering no mineral purchase. Instead, the snail uses me as a pillow. Its pale gray flesh settles into the crevice between the flanges, riding the violent sixty-cycle tremor that leaks straight out of my seam. I can feel its yellow blood jumping in time with the rotor shaft six inches behind me. *Thrumm. Thrumm. Thrumm.* The turbine spins; I shudder; the snail’s heart is beaten for it by the municipal power grid.

Sometimes, a microscopic bead of my plasticizer—the heavy oil locked inside my synthetic polymer—sweats out under the heat and mixes with the mucus under its foot. The snail sucks it up. It is building its shell out of the iron of my bolts, the zinc of the cables, the green lead of the paint, and the carbon of my seal. 

I am getting harder. 

That is the nature of a gasket in a hot place. Week by week, my sulfur cross-links tighten. I lose my spring. I am vulcanizing further, turning slowly from rubber into something like ebonite, something like coal, something like the black basalt slabs around us. 

And Bolt Seven is losing its torque.

The snails have licked away twelve thousandths of an inch of iron from the underside of the washer where the rust blooms thickest in the six-hour tide. The clamping force there has dropped by four pounds. It is nothing that the gray electrical cabinet thirty yards away can measure yet. The amber light still blinks its idiot *all-clear* into the steam.

But in the gap under Bolt Seven, where the pressure has eased by the thickness of a cigarette paper, a tiny whisper of dry, five-hundred-degree steam has begun to push into my side.

It hasn't broken through yet. It is just a warm blister in my rubber flesh, a new note—a tiny, secret *hiss*—layered under the shriek of the rotor and the *tick-tick-tick* of the iron teeth. 

The big snail knows. Yesterday, it shifted two inches to the left, centering its heavy, terraced cone directly over Bolt Seven. It pressed its greasy foot hard against the weakening seam, feeling that tiny needle of extra heat, drinking the leak before it even exists.

We are waiting together, the snail and I. 

In a year, or three years, the iron washers will be paper-thin, transferred grain by grain into the great, whorled domes of the forty silent brothers in the basin. The flange will lift by a fraction of a millimeter. My brittle, coal-hard ring will finally crack under Bolt Seven. 

A white lance of steam will shoot out horizontally into the triangular pool, screaming a new note that will shatter the standing waves into foam. The amber light on the cabinet will turn solid red. The relays will trip. Far away, a breaker will fall open with a sound like a shotgun, and the rotor inside the green box will spin down—*whirrrrrr, whummmm, wub, wub*—until, for the first time since the sky fell in, the pit is silent.

I wonder what the snails will do in that first minute of quiet, when their yellow blood stops shaking and they have to remember how to push it through their own veins. 

Until then, I hold the seam. I keep the hurricane inside the box, and I pass the hum through my body into the wet, gray foot of the thing that is eating the box around me.
