Do not look for a face under a felt cap, or a pair of knees flexing to read the deck plates. Look further down, into the blind, frost-thickened gap between a rising three-foot hex of blue-black stone and the cast-iron skirt of a district condenser.

That is where the cold is heaviest. It settles there like brine in the bilge of a boat, thick with the smell of wet slate and the bitter, green tang of bruised ironwood. 

I am the thing that lives in the two-inch clearance. 

If you had a long-handled brass scraper and a lantern, and you pried back the rubbery curtains of winter-fern that choke that seam, you would not see a beast at first. You would see what looks like a forty-foot run of leaked black tallow that has frozen halfway down the rock and begun to grow a skin of oxidized copper dust. 

I have no bones. In a valley where the earth shears upward with the dry *tick* of a breaking glass and ten-ton counterweights seat themselves without warning, a spine is merely a lever waiting to be snapped. A skull is a nut to be cracked between a rising shelf and a slow bricklayer. Instead, I am a flat, muscular ribbon of dense, grease-slicked cartilage and grey mantle-flesh, four yards long and rarely thicker than the sole of a citizen’s boot. My cross-section is whatever the seam requires it to be. When the basalt calves and narrows my corridor to the width of a thumb-thimble, I simply flatten, spreading sideways up the cold stone like ink pressed between two panes of glass, my edges curling into the mortar joints of the neighboring tenement while the rock grinds past my belly.

I do not see the ordinary, indifferent blue of the afternoon sky. I have no eyes for it. Eyes are for the gap-flashing birds and the lapel-flipping citizens who live in the line-of-sight. My world is read entirely through the lateral grooves that run the length of my flanks—deep, pleated channels of raw nerve and heavy gel that drink the low-frequency grammar of the basin.

To me, the city is not quiet. It is a blinding, three-dimensional roar of pressures. 

Through my belly against the emerging basalt, I feel the deep, slow, tectonic push from three miles down—a vast, rhythmic tightening in the basement of the world, like a fist clenching in cold water. Through my back, pressed simultaneously against the sweating cast-iron of a boiler housing, I feel the sixty-cycle hum of the district induction coils, the thick, oily *shunt-shunt* of the tree-trunk pistons adjusting a roof two blocks north, and the sharp, bright staccato of a citizen’s iron thimble tapping a drainpipe eighty yards away: *tink, tink-tink, scrape*. 

I know what that tap means as well as her partner does. It means a crane-gantry has taken a load and is about to swing its counterweight into the trough at the end of the alley. 

Four seconds before the iron block arrives, I have already drawn my tail out of the moss-cushion where it seats, flowing upward along the vertical corner of a brick dormitory like a draft of heavy smoke pulled up a flue. 

We have an arrangement, the citizens and I, though we never exchange a flash of yellow silk. They are the tending caste of the high friction; I am the keeper of the blind tolerances where their spoon-billed iron bars cannot reach.

The automated systems have no improvisation, and the humans, for all their boneless drops and belt-hooks, are thick, round things. They cannot enter the four-inch manifold sleeves where the frost-lichen sends its toughest, woodiest taproots to drink the hydraulic sweat. Left alone, those roots swell like wedges, freezing solid in the sub-zero run-off until they lock a stabilizing collar against its guide-rail. 

That is my pasture. I flow into the collar-sleeve, wrapping myself around the chilling pipe like a broad leather belt. My underside is lined with forty thousand microscopic, overlapping teeth of black chitin, arranged like the grain of a rough bastard-file. As I undulate through the tight dark, I rasp the frozen lichen-wood and the encroaching green ice clean off the brass sleeves. I digest the leached copper salts and the turpentine-bitter starch; I lick the leaking, mineral-sweet hydraulic fluid straight from the weeping gaskets before it can freeze into a piston-jamming rime. And what I leave behind me, secreted from the pores along my back to keep my hide from freezing to the raw basalt, is a thin, glassy film of biological mucilage that smells faintly of mutton fat and wet iron—a lubricant that stays slick at forty below zero, finer and longer-lasting than any bird-tallow the crews smear from their pots.

Sometimes, in the narrow vertical chimneys where three basalt columns meet the corner of an old tenement, a citizen and I will work the same seam. 

I will feel the heavy, padded thud of his rubber boot bracing against a hex three feet above my anterior end. I will feel the sharp, ringing bite of his iron bar flicking a wedged brick out of the track. When the stone shudders upward an inch and pinches his boot-heel before he can shift his weight, he does not panic, and he does not pry at the rock. He taps his thumb-thimble twice against the basalt right beside my flank: *tink-tink*.

I know the cadence. I bunch my muscular ribbon below his heel, thickening myself from half an inch to four inches in a single, hydraulic spasm of my own hydrostatic chambers, and I secrete a heavy gout of cold grease. His rubber sole slips free on my back with a muffled *schlump*; he catches the passing stirrup of an ascending brick-cradle, and is gone into the white shafts of sunlight above, leaving behind a chunk of fried bird-tallow wedged into a mortar crack as payment.

Wait long enough in the deep, damp gloom at the base of the sixty-year spires, and you will find my other use.

I am very old—older than the tallest column currently standing before the hammers take it. Because I have no fixed shape to break, the basin does not kill me; I simply divide when I grow too long for a single ward's manifolds, leaving a rear third of myself behind in a warm sump to grow a new sensory head while the rest of me slides north into new rock. 

Because I spend my decades pressed flat between the rising basalt and the stationary iron, my back is a physical memory of the valley. The soft, tough cartilage of my upper hide takes an impression under the immense, slow pressure of the seams, hardening like vulcanized rubber where it is pinched. 

If you were to catch me crossing a sunlit grate for the three seconds it takes to drop from a dying condenser into a fresh culvert, you would see that my dark, greasy back is covered in raised, pale scars—a sixty-year relief map of every chisel-mark I have ever slid across to keep a column from binding against a tenement wall. 

Along my neck are the backwards, inside-out impressions of the ten-year-old boy’s fresh name, still sharp and smelling of bruised slate. Mid-way down my flank are the reversed love-notes and warning-glyphs of his parents, flattened and widened by twenty years of my growth. And down near my tail, worn almost smooth by the friction of a hundred thousand iron walkways being lifted and bolted overhead, are the tallies of the dead who have already gone into the accumulator sumps and come back out through the copper pipes as frost—the salt of their bones sweating out through the cold brass, dripping onto the dark ferns, and trickling down into the narrow seam to wet my skin as the stone pushes us all another inch toward the pale, indifferent sky.
