If I am here, I am not standing on the glass. Nothing with two soft soles and a spine belongs on those steps. 

At first, the pull is to be the tension itself—to be one of the polyhedrons under the bone-white arches, clicking out the long math of the mountain's arrival. To be clean, suspended, and occupied entirely with ratios. 

Wait, though. Watch the keeper come back from the rim after dropping the snails. Watch it pause to knock a crescent of black glass off its own left heel. The shard skitters across the wet terrace, comes to rest in a square inch of pooling water, and immediately begins to collect the silver run-off on its honeycombed underside. 

That isn't where I am either, though it’s closer. 

Look higher up on the keeper's body, where the pale, greasy bristles part around the vertical seam of its chest. Not the copper cilia themselves—those belong to its own nervous system, tasting the ozone and the burnt-lime smell of a fresh gall. Look just beneath the overlap of the hide, inside the warm, oily crease where the heavy limbs join the trunk.

I am there. Or rather, *we* are there, though we think like a single smear of dark, copper-green grease.

You cannot have a stomach that grinds basalt and burnt amphibian, a hide that sheds supersaturated fog, and four wrists that continually weep fresh, hot resin without having a minor attendant. The keeper is too large and too blunt to reach its own armpits with clubbed hands of volcanic glass. If its resin-pores clog with pumice dust from a slag-gall, the woody knuckles dry out, crack, and split under the impact of walking. If the heavy bristles matt together with the acidic slime of a brushed-past snail, the skin underneath goes raw and lets the sharp mist in.

So I am the thing that lives in the grease-layer. 

I am no bigger than a thumb-joint, flat as a pumpkin seed, and built like a drop of solder that learned how to crawl. I have no eyes—there is nothing in the pearl-gray fog I need to see—and no legs that a frog or a ribbon-predator could recognize as legs. My underside is a muscular, corrugated sole that grips the keeper's coarse bristles the way a snail grips the rim, except I leave no acid behind. My back is a flexible plate of dull, tarnished bronze—compiled from the same metal-bleed that feeds the black moss around the pylons, sweat out through the keeper's pores and licked up by my kind over generations.

My world is hot, smells of tallow and struck flint, and moves with a slow, subterranean pitch and roll. 

*Clack. Thud-clack. Clack.*

When the keeper walks, the shockwave comes up through its glass shoes, travels through the dense, fibrous wood of its wrists, and rolls through the great sack of its body like a depth charge in a barrel of oil. I don't hear the frogs clanking or the stone cracking; I feel the keeper's muscles bunch and shift as it reads the floor. Three seconds before a shelf shoots up, its hide tightens—a tiny, localized shiver that tells me to flatten my rim against its skin before the heavy limb lifts.

My work is small and continuous. I slide down the long, swinging cable of its arm while it lumber-walks toward a chattering pylon. Down near the wrist, where the pale bristles thin out into dark, porous horn, the heat from the fresh stone is fierce. Here, the keeper’s resin-glands are oozing slow, amber beads to cushion the glass hooves. The air is thick with rock-flour from its last feeding. The grit sticks to the resin before it can cure, forming a harsh, sandy crust that chafes the joint.

I wedge my flat, bronze-plated nose into the crease between the horn and the obsidian shoe. The resin is hot and tastes of turpentine and iron. I eat the crust—scraping away the dead, grit-fouled gum with a wide, ribbon-like tongue, leaving the pore clean so the fresh resin can flow smooth and clear into the honeycombed root of the hoof. In return, the grit I swallow stays in my flat belly, giving me the weight I need not to be blown off when the keeper goes to the pavilion to dry itself.

That is the center of my day—not the loud, hot violence of the slag-gall feedings, when the keeper’s whole chest vibrates like a rock-crusher and the sour steam of boiled frog rolls down its flanks—but the return to the arches.

When the keeper reaches its arms up between the clicking polyhedrons, the gravity-beams catch me too. 

It is a terrifying, exquisite minute. The invisible geometry of the mountain-engine reaches right through my bronze back. Every grain of basalt dust in my gut wants to align itself with the blueprint of the ridge outside. The keeper’s bristles stand straight up like wire, and my flat body is pulled taut against its skin, heavy and weightless at the same time. The water blasts off us in a sudden white fog. For sixty seconds, I am completely dry, humming like a struck tuning fork against the warm hide of my host, suspended in the exact center of the machine's attention.

Then the keeper drops its arms. The bristles go slack and greasy again. It hooks its heels into the wet felt of the sling, folds its great glass clubs over its chest, and shuts the copper-fringed seam. 

In the dark fold between its wrist and its ribs, the heat builds up quickly. I creep an inch higher, out of the reach of the cooling resin, and settle into the root of a thick bristle. Outside, a seam in the basin floor gives a sharp, glassy *crack*, and the whole mountain rises another fraction of an inch into the cloud, carrying the pavilion, the sling, the keeper, and me along with it.
