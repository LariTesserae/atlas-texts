I am not standing in boots. If I had boots, the floor would have had me twenty minutes ago, drawn down into the warp and weft under the flat paddles of the beating leaves. 

To stay in a room that wants to nail everything down, you have to be the grease on the nail.

I am up near the north sagging ceiling, right where the load is heaviest, pressed flat into the mortise-joint between an old, splitting hull-rib and one of the Joiner’s deflected arches. At first glance, I look like a massive, weeping blister of pitch—a bruised bladder of amber resin and black fermented cambium the size of a butcher's apron, squeezed out of the woodwork by the sheer misery of the surrounding tonnage. 

That is how I began: an osmotic accident. When the root-tectonics tore this barge-shaped hole into the deep world, the pressure sheared open a dozen ancient resin-ducts and a high-pressure sap-vein at the exact point where one of those three-mile corners failed to meet. The sap didn't just leak; it boiled into the sudden vacuum, whipped into a heavy, golden emulsion by the ozone and the flat Tuesday light. Because it spilled across the broken geometry of the corner, the puddle didn't fall to the floor. It fell *sideways*, three hundred yards into an imaginary afternoon, before catching on a splinter six inches from where it started. 

By the time the room’s gravity remembered how to work, I had curdled into something with a skin.

I have no bones, no wooden struts, no xylem. I am a heavy, viscous crawl—a creature made of rosin, turpentine, and mucilage, possessing the slow, catastrophic strength of cold tar. Inside my amber hide, suspended like insects in fossil gum, are thousands of tiny, silver bubbles of that Tuesday-afternoon air that never burst, along with pale shavings of boxwood and the dusty wings of the stuttering beetle’s sheddings.

If the Joiner is the room’s Second Thought—its anxious, improvising geometry—I am its Fever-Dream. I am the lubricity the carpenter needs when the wood is too dry and the friction threatens to set the workshop on fire.

Watch the pulley that has just begun to smoke. 

The Joiner leaps toward it, its caliper-limbs clacking, its rib-vents hissing out a sharp puff of celery-scented steam. The three-ply vine rope is sawing back and forth over a dry wooden knuckle, trying to haul a sagging slab of the north ceiling back into alignment. The friction has charred the green bark black; a thin, acrid thread of grey smoke is rising into the sourceless light, and the smell of scorching wintergreen cuts through the wet sawdust air. If that rope burns through, four tons of compressed root-plate will drop into the center of the room, and the floor will erupt with a hundred panic-shoots to meet it.

The Joiner reaches the smoking burl, clamping its wooden jaws around the rope to take the strain itself, its own hydraulic shins bulging and creaking under the sudden load. It cannot let go to fix the axle. Its top-knuckle twists frantically—*left, right, left*—reading the spike in heat.

It doesn't whistle for me, because it has no mouth, and I have no ears. Instead, it drums one of its secondary, drawknife heels against the hull-rib: three rapid, staccato raps. *Tak-tak-tak.* 

I am already moving. I do not walk; I decant myself. 

I release my suction on the ceiling-arch and pour downward along the underside of a cross-beam, a thick, glinting ribbon of dark honey and turpentine, stretching four feet long while my tail is still anchored to the timber above. The flat light catches my belly, turning me into a briefly illuminated stained-glass window of suspended sawdust and silver air-beads. I drop the last two feet onto the smoking burl with a heavy, wet *slap*.

The heat sears my underside, boiling my outer layer of sap into a furious white froth of pine-tar and steam, which smells briefly and wonderfully of a sun-baked forest floor three miles above us. I don't flinch—there is nothing in me that knows how to flinch. Instead, I extrude a lobe of pure, slick mucilage—harvested from the crushed ferns down by the mallet-branch—and force it directly between the charring vine-rope and the wooden axle.

*Hssssssss.*

The smoke dies instantly into a greasy black paste. The friction drops to zero. With a terrifying, resonant *groan* that shakes water-droplets from the entire room’s perimeter, the three-ply rope slides smoothly over my back, six inches to the left, hauling the sagging ceiling slab up tight against the arch.

The Joiner unhooks its jaws from the rope. Its wooden ribs click open, venting a long, hot sigh of mist right across my back to cool my scorched skin. Before the mist can evaporate, I suck the water in through my pores, swelling slightly, soothing the burn.

Then, before the rope’s new tension can pinch me flat against the burl, I flow backward into the rigging. 

The Joiner and I have an arrangement, though neither of us has a brain large enough to call it a truce. The Joiner has speed, leverage, and an instinct for angles; it can see where a strut needs to go before the floor even finishes firing it. Being made of dry boxwood and high-pressure plumbing, however, it is brittle, and it cannot seal a seam. It can saw open the three-mile corners, yes, keeping the Tuesday afternoon alive, but the raw edges of the bark it cuts immediately begin to bleed out the room’s hydraulic pressure. Left raw, the walls around the corners would wither and collapse inward like a punctured lung.

That is my work. While the Joiner’s half-mile-long arm is still sawing away the scabbing bark inside the impossible horizon—*shhhk, shhhk, shhhk*—I creep up behind its shoulder. I stretch a pale, translucent pseudopod across the threshold where the geometry breaks. 

When I push my substance into that three-mile gap, I do not stretch into a long pole like the Joiner’s arm. Instead, my amber gel *spreads* into a thin, shimmering horizon of oil—like petrol on a puddle, three miles wide and half a millimeter thick, hovering in the hazy afternoon air. I coat the raw, sawed edges of the bark with a hardened glaze of oxidation-proof rosin. I cauterize the wound so it stops bleeding sap, tricking the tree into thinking the bark has successfully healed over the gap, even while the window remains wide open and clear as glass.

I am the glaze on its windows. In return, the Joiner brings me the things I cannot reach on the floor without being woven into a rug.

Right now, while it clings to the rigging beside the freshly greased pulley, it reaches one thin, needle-tipped digit into the bird-cage cavity of its own chest. It roots around in the phloem-wool hammock where it keeps its treasures. It pulls out one of the grey, un-burst skins of a three-second raindrop—a little tough bead of water that forgot how to be liquid when the room’s pressure skipped—and a dry, brassy leg from the stuttering beetle.

It holds them out to me.

I flow over its wooden wrist, engulfing the offering. I pull the dead beetle-leg and the impossible water-bead deep into my middle, right alongside the dozens of others I am already carrying. Why do I want them? For the same reason a pearl oyster wants a grain of sand, or a deep-sea crab glues anemones to its shell. The room is constantly trying to digest me, to suck my moisture back into the thirsty bark and cure me into a dead lump of amber stuck to a beam. Only by packing my body full of the room’s *mistakes*—the little bits of broken time and un-resolved space—can I keep the wood from recognizing me as its own sap. The glitches make me indigestible. They keep me mobile.

Suddenly, the three-second rain falls. 

The pressure in the room drops like an elevator. A solid cylinder of warm water dumps out of the empty air five feet away, roaring against the felted floor-mat. Down below, the pale, eyeless lizard jerks four inches to the left in time with the thunderous *THWACK* of the floor-mallet, its tongue flicking out to catch the spray.

The Joiner’s upper burl-knuckle twists sharply. It looks down at the spot where it snapped the vine off your boot a moment ago—though of course, there are no boots here unless you brought them, and if you brought them, the Joiner is already recalculating the room’s load-distribution around your shoulders. 

It drops five feet down a resin-line, stopping upside-down, and taps a wooden caliper against the arch above my head. *Knock, knock.* 

A new shoot, pale as an asparagus spear and thick as a stovepipe, is blistering up through the floor-mat near the south wall, preparing to launch itself across the bright Tuesday air. If we catch it together—if the Joiner hooks it mid-flight to bend its trajectory, and I pour myself into the split where it hits the wall to glue the graft before it can rebound—we might be able to force the ceiling up another six inches. We might pry open a fourth corner, right near the roof, and see how many miles of empty afternoon are hiding behind the bark.
