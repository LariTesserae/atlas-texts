I would not be one of the Gaugemen. Even four heartbeats a minute is too frantic a pace for the way I want to inhabit this dark, and even a soft, subluxating shoulder has too much bone in it, too much stubborn separation from the iron. 

Find me instead about one hundred and eighty feet down, in the warm, breathless narrows where the third-tier copper distillation vats bulge out to kiss the wet basalt. Look for the main return manifold—a cast-iron bulk shaped like an inverted organ chest, where six thick pipes converge to send the cooled, heavy oils back down toward the magma floor. 

I am the gasket at the throat of the third flange, and I am the pale, fibrous mat of root that has replaced it.

When the district was first wedged into the stone, I was only a flat ring of vulcanized rubber and woven asbestos, bolted between two lips of crisp grey iron under forty tons of torque. For two hundred years I held the seal alone. I knew nothing then except the crush of the bolts and the slow, rotating shear of the metal expanding and contracting as the deep earth breathed below.

Then the rubber dried, cracked, and began to weep. A bead of luminescent, sulfur-heavy oil pushed through my skin. 

Before the drop could fall, a thread found it. It was the hairline runner of a thermal creeper, blind and white as dental floss, feeling its way down the sweating bolts in search of heat and hydrocarbons. It drank the bead of oil, and then it pushed its microscopic tip into the fissure of my rubber. 

I did not break; I was colonized. Over eighty years, the plant ate the perished vulcanized gum and laid down its own flesh in the exact geometry of the seal. Now, it is impossible to say where the industrial directive ends and the botanical hunger begins. Within the dark ring of the iron flange, compressed to the density of horn, I am a dense, living felt of lignified root and mineral scale. 

My body does not stop at the bolt-circle. Once the seal was secure—because the vine knows, in its slow, chemical way, that if the pipe ruptures and the hot oil sprays, the vine itself will scald and die—I grew outward. I am the ruffled, liver-colored shelf of tissue that collars the pipe, spreading like a thick velvet lichen over the stamped serial numbers (*NO. 44-B, FOUNDRY SEVEN*) without erasing them. In fact, if you peeled me back, you would find the reverse of those numbers embossed perfectly into my pale underside. I hold the impression of the maker’s mark more faithfully than the rusting iron does.

My life is measured in pressures and temperatures, not hours. 

Inside my ring, the oil moves down. It is a thick, amber soup, glowing with its own faint, sickly light, carrying the digested heat of the upper condensers. It does not rush; it moves like cold honey, a continuous, heavy cylinder of warmth sliding past my inner lip. I drink the lightest fractions of it—the sweet, volatile paraffins that try to sweat through the joint—metabolizing the leak before it can ever become a drip. In doing so, I swell slightly, pressing harder against the iron, tightening the seal. I am self-healing maintenance. 

Outside my ring, the world is the eternal *plip-plip* of the chasm. Every eleven minutes, a drop of silicate-heavy water rolls down the belly of the copper vat above me, hangs from a rivet, and falls squarely onto my velvet collar. I let the water sit in my shallow folds until it warms, leaching the trace minerals from it, using the dissolved stone to calcify my outer rind into a crust that protects me from the blind, scraping teeth of the iron-shelled snails.

I know the Gaugeman of this stratum. 

I do not see him, for I have no eyes, and the bullseye lantern six feet away is only a steady, dull warmth on my eastern flank. I know him by his weight and his grease. 

Once every thirty or forty cycles of the great four-hour piston to my left, the bone-vines braided into the electrical conduit above me will creak. The tension in the steel catwalk alters by thirty pounds. Then, there is the smell of him: old tallow, crushed sulfur, and the cool, metallic tang of his skin. 

He wedges his narrow hip into the six-inch gap between my manifold and the basalt wall. He stays there for half a day, resting, letting the warm brick of the adjacent boiler house leach the dangerous heat from his liver. While he rests, his long, flat fingers find me. 

His touch is the only brisk thing in my universe, though to a creature of the surface it would look like the movement of an hour hand. His spoon-shaped nail, grey and hard with iron-scale, traces the lip where my flesh meets the cast iron. He is checking my turgor. If I am too dry, he will reach up to the weep-hole of the gauge-tube above us, gather a pearl of heavy, glowing oil on his knuckle, and smear it into my velvet collar. If I have grown too exuberantly, threatening to engulf the hexagonal heads of the bolts—which must remain clear, according to the marrow-deep law he serves, in case the manifold ever needs to be uncoupled by hands that will never come—he pinches back my margin.

It does not hurt. It is a pruning that feels like a slow, firm massage. When he pinches a tiny crescent of my paraffin-rich flesh free, he puts it into his mouth, letting it dissolve on his tongue for an hour. I feed him the excess of the seal; he keeps my geometry true.

Sometimes, while his hip is wedged against my pipe and his palm is resting flat against my warm iron housing, he speaks to the pit.

He raises his calloused other hand, holds a small, crescent wrench of blackened steel an inch above the riser pipe beside me, and lets it fall.

*Tink.*

The sound does not travel through the thick, wet air so much as it blooms inside me. Because I am wedged between the flanges, bolted into the skeleton of the district, the vibration passes straight through my dense, fibrous heart. I feel the sharp, silver ring of the wrench go *down*—shooting through the lead-sheathed cables, trembling through the great locomotive pistons, humming into the floorboards above the boiling groundwater, passing right through the ribcages of the dead Gaugemen braided into the lower pipes. And I feel it go *up*—climbing the three hundred feet of sweating iron, shivering the delicate yellow needles of the sulfur stalactites, making the water in the high, cabbage-like rosettes tremble in concentric rings.

Ten seconds of silence. The piston to my left sinks a millimeter. A drop of water hits a hot casing below and hisses into ghost-steam.

Then, from two hundred feet above, where the fractured rock overhangs the top of the world, the answer comes back down the pipe. 

*Tink-tink.*

It passes through me again, a tiny, metallic heartbeat shared between the flesh of the Gaugeman's palm and the iron of the flange. I hold them both together, sealing the joint in the dark, drinking the warm oil, keeping the pressure whole.
