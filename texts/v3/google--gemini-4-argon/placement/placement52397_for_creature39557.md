To find my place on the wall, you have to follow the vanadium track down past the main electrostatic Sifters, past the grease-gardens where the fifty-year Copper-Backs graze, to a spot where the pale travertine bulges outward like the belly of a buried amphora. Here, two miles below the lip and seven above the amber floor, the rock is intersected by a four-foot-wide seam of black chert—an ancient, iron-dense intrusion that the silica wind has never quite managed to plane flush with the rest of the cliff. 

Bolted directly into this dark belt is the *Cradle-House* of the Seventh District’s primary accumulator. 

Inside this narrow, open-sided cage of riveted iron sits a thirty-ton flywheel made of laminated slate and banded bronze, spinning in a bath of dark, graphited tallow. It is the mechanical heart of the middle terraces. During the Up-Breath, the great canvas wind-screws feed their surplus torque into this wheel; during the ten minutes of the Slack, when the wind dies and the city holds its breath, it is the momentum of this spinning stone that keeps the ventilation bellows pumping in the deep shaft-homes and holds the electromagnetic brakes on the funicular cars.

I am not on the gangs that walk the Sifter-plates, nor do I wear the Kite-leather wings of the edge-crews. My legs are too stiff for the long diagonal drops; the left knee was crushed thirty years ago when a counter-balance box jumped its guide-rail during a high-gale shear. When the bone knit, it knit thick and slightly crooked with the heavy mineral-salts in our water, leaving my lower leg shaped like a pry-bar. I cannot run the tracks, so they gave me the job that requires no running.

I am the *Reed-Setter* for the Seventh Wheel.

My station is a three-foot-wide timber-and-iron bracket suspended directly beneath the flywheel’s main bronze bearing. I do not hang from a wire like the young Drawers; I sit wedged into a saddle of greased felt and boiler-plate, my good right foot clamped around the main tension-rod that drops down to the lower smelters, my fused left leg hooked permanently over a brass grounding-lug. 

Before me, bolted to the bearing housing, is the *Comb*—a row of forty-eight tuned reeds made of hammered phosphor-bronze, graduated in length from the size of a thumb-nail to the length of a forearm. 

Nothing in the Spindle Range fails all at once. Because there is no ice to snap the iron and no rust-water to rot the rivets, death here comes by way of resonance and dry friction. If the great slate flywheel develops a hairline fracture three inches inside its core, or if a single grain of coarse quartz slips past the felt seals into the tallow-bath, the vibration of the wheel changes. It is a shift too small for the eye to see and too low for the ear to catch over the velvet *shhhhh* of the nine-mile updraft. 

That is what the Comb is for. Each bronze reed is tuned to a specific harmonic of the district’s structural steel. As long as the wheel spins true and the trusses above and below me are in tension, the forty-eight reeds sit still, save for the third and fourth from the left, which blur in a steady, healthy B-flat—the base note of three revolutions per minute.

My job is to keep my hands on the housing and my eyes on the bronze.

Because I do not move across the wall, my rig is different from the mobile gangs. I have no need for the wide, wind-catching radiator fins on my back; in the wind-shadow under the wheel-house, they would only collect the greasy, black soot that drifts down from the smelter flues above. Instead, I am plugged directly into the building’s thermal loop. Two thin, flexible tubes of coiled copper run from a manifold on the stone wall into the pleated linen vest against my ribs, circulating cool, heavy brine from the deep shadow-vats three hundred feet inside the rock face. I am tethered to the cliff by my own blood-heat.

To a passerby dropping down the main track on a slider-rig, I look like a gargoyle of hardened grease and oxidized copper, half-swallowed by the plumbing of the wheel-house. My tallow-varnish is thicker than most, mixed with extra lead-white and graphite because the radiant heat coming off the thirty-ton slate wheel would bake a dry man into a biscuit by mid-breath. Over the decades, the fine silica flour blowing up from the abyss has settled into the creases of my leather apron and the folds of my neck-cloth, hardening with the grease into a pale, stony crust. The small, grey Vent-Limpets that live on the bearing casing regularly mistake my left boot for part of the bracket; when I unhook at the end of a shift, I have to pry three or four of them off my heel with a bone knife, careful not to crack their zinc-rich shells before I toss them back onto the warm iron.

People think a Reed-Setter’s life is lonely, hung out here in the violet shadow beneath the roaring mass of the wheel, with sixty feet of naked, chalk-warm stone between me and the nearest gantry. 

They are wrong. A man on a rope only knows what his eyes can see through yellow mica, which is mostly dust and glare. He is insulated by the air. 

I am grounded in the Iron. 

Because the main tension-rods of the Seventh District pass directly through my saddle-frame to anchor in the black chert behind me, the entire four-mile vertical ribbon of the city uses my spine as a sounding board. I do not need a wrist-tine to read the wall; I *am* the wall.

With my forehead resting against the cool brass of the oil-sight glass, I can feel the whole district breathing. 

Through my fused left knee, I feel the *thump-drag-thump* of the great bone-wood wind-screws half a mile up, leaning their canvas shoulders into the hot updraft. Through my right heel, clamped to the lower rod, comes the continuous, crystalline fizzing of the Sifters two thousand feet down, where the high-voltage plates are stripping the sulfur out of the dust-streams. 

And between those two great industrial bass-notes, the gossip of three thousand Sump-Drawers runs through my teeth like silver wire.

I know when old Maura’s clan in the Fourth Shaft-Home has finished rendering their monthly tallow-vat, because the heavy copper lid drops with a dull, greasy *clonk* that makes the seventeenth reed on my Comb shiver for half a second. I know when the young Kite-Wranglers out on the translucent overhangs have hooked a forty-footer; the sudden, violent snatch of their winch-drums sends a high, singing tension-wave up the guy-wires that tastes like sour brass against my fillings. I can tell which funicular car is climbing the north track simply by the rhythm of its guide-wheels clicking over the expansion joints—Car Three has a flat spot on its lower bronze bushing that goes *tock-tick-tock* every eleven seconds, a tiny limp in the iron that matches my own.

My tool-bag hangs between my knees, weighted with lead shot so the updraft doesn't swing it into the flywheel’s teeth. Inside are no hammers or pitons—only small pots of abrasive rouge, three grades of oil-stone, a pair of bronze tweezers for pulling grit out of the bearing-felt, and a set of harmonic tuning forks. 

Every two hours, when the shadow of the great cable-crane above creeps across my knuckles, I reach out with a long-spouted copper can and feed forty drops of clarified limpet-oil into the sight-glass. Then I take my thumb-scraper—filed from the thick, iron-hard nail of my own big toe ten years ago—and shave the accumulated ridge of black tallow and silica paste off the lip of the bronze housing. This paste is the richest stuff in the district, full of microscopic bronze-flake worn off the main shaft and fine sulfur from the air. I do not drop it; I roll it into little grey pills and press them into a leather pouch at my belt. My sister’s family leases thirty yards of cold stone over in the shadow of the Third Buttress; those pills of bearing-sludge, smeared onto the rock, will bring the big, blue-shelled Copper-Backs crawling from three hundred feet away. It is my contribution to the clan’s Tare.

The hardest part of the shift is always the approach of the Slack.

An hour before the light fails, when the abyss below turns from amber to the color of dried blood and the sun hits the opposing canyon wall thirty miles away, the thermal updraft begins to lose its spine. The air grows thin and erratic. Up on the screws, the canvas vanes begin to luff and slap, sending irregular, jerky shudders down the drive-shafts. 

In my wheel-house, the thirty tons of spinning slate want to keep going at their daytime pace, while the cooling iron of the city’s trusses begins to shrink and pull back against the rivets. The Comb goes wild. Half a dozen reeds begin to buzz at once—the high, sharp C-sharp of a cooling cross-brace fighting the low F-natural of the main shaft’s axial drift.

This is when I earn my water-ration. Working with both hands on the big brass worm-gears beside my hips, I have to slowly, delicately back off the tension on the upper thrust-blocks, letting the flywheel float three-sixteenths of an inch outward on its tallow-film to accommodate the shrinking steel of the cliff-face. If I turn the screw a quarter-inch too far, the slate rim will kiss the iron guard-casing and shower the dry grease with sparks; if I am too slow, the shrinking truss will pinch the bronze bearing, seize the shaft, and snap the main drive-cable like a dry tendon.

I do it entirely by feel, my eyes closed behind my goggles, listening with my jawbone pressed into the grease-cup until the frantic chattering of the middle reeds dies down and the wheel settles Back into its solitary, humming B-flat.

Then the sun drops below the far rim. The violet light goes out like a snuffed wick, leaving only the bruised, plum-colored dark rising from the nine-mile floor.

The wind stops.

In the wheel-house, the silence is not complete—the thirty-ton stone above my head is still spinning at sixty revolutions a minute on its oil-film—but without the roar of the wind against the cliff, the sound of the wheel changes. It stops being a vibration and becomes a voice: a low, continuous, velvety *whirrrrr* that sounds like a giant cat purring in a dark room. 

Around me, the iron goes dead. All along the four-mile face, the Drawers have hooked their spurs into the rock and taken their hands off the rails. For ten minutes, no one strikes a tine. No funicular climbs. The tension-rods under my boots stop singing and become mere cold metal.

I reach up, unhook the copper mesh from my face, and let it hang on my chest. The dead air smells of hot slate, old tallow, and the dry, chalky scent of four hundred million years of powdered stone. 

From the darkness thirty feet above me, there is a small, dry *clack*. A leather bag drops down on the end of a thin braided twine, swinging gently until it bumps against my shoulder. 

It is my niece, hanging from the lower gantry of the Sifter-deck above, where she works the electrostatic brushes. She never comes down—my bracket isn't rated for the weight of two—and I never go up. 

I untie the little brass nipple-flask from her string, take my measured two swallows of warm, tin-tasting water, and clip my own leather pouch of bronze-rich bearing pills onto the cord in its place. I give the twine two small tugs. A second later, I feel the faint, warm tap of her boot-heel against the secondary strut above my head—not a word in the Iron tongue, just a dull, soft *thud-thud*, like a heartbeat through a wall. I tap my brass wrench once against the oil-glass in answer.

Then, high overhead, the lip of the plateau spills its first cascade of cold night air down the flutes of the stone. 

The temperature drops six degrees in three seconds. A mile away, a cooling girder lets out a sharp, rifle-shot *ping* that echoes across the thirty-mile gulf. Above me, the great canvas wind-screws groan in their bone-wood sockets, take the weight of the falling dark, and begin to turn backward.

On my Comb, the fourth reed stops blurred-dancing; the fifth reed, tuned a half-step lower for the heavier, colder air of the Down-Breath, picks up the hum and begins to sing. 

I pull my copper mask back over my mouth, unhook the brine-tubes from my vest, and hitch my safety-block to the descent wire. My replacement for the Down-Breath is already sliding down the diagonal rail from the Second Shaft-Home—I can feel the rhythmic *scritch-scritch* of his bronze brake-shoe through the soles of my boots, coming down through the orange-lit dark to take the saddle while the wheel spins on.
