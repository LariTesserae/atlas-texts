I would not be the engine, nor the wire overhead. Both are too committed to their trajectories. If I am here, where the air is too thin to carry a voice and the rock has forgotten how to break, I want to be where the lines cross and the record is kept.

Begin with the slag. 

Behind every Dredger, the Sifters leave a carpet of gray, lead-chitin and mineral pellets, rolled to the size of musket balls and dropped into the three-meter trench. For a century after the convoy passes, those pellets sit in the groove. Every afternoon the Sweat wets them; every night the rime welds them together; every morning the sublimation leaves behind a microscopic skin of dissolved iron and sulfate between the spheres. Over five hundred years, a trench does not stay loose gravel. It sinters. It becomes a ribbon of porous, conglomerate pavement—a low, ruler-straight causeway of fused beads running across the basin.

Where two trenches intersect, the younger Dredger’s acid-grate chews through the older pavement, breaking the sintered spheres loose again and tossing them to the shoulders of the cut. Over tens of thousands of years, these crossroads build up low, geometric berms of broken conglomerate—square-cornered frames of rubble perhaps two feet high, marking the exact coordinates where two fifty-year marches met and passed through one another.

Look at the leeward corner of that berm. Look at what is wedged into the angle where the north-south trench cuts the east-west trench.

At first, it looks like a slump of the berm itself—a pile of sintered slag-balls that have been cemented together by black lichen and yellow Foundry-tar. It is roughly the size and shape of an anvil, flat-topped, wider at the base than at the crown, sitting flush against the cut stone of the trench wall. 

It is a **Cairn-Back**, though if you struck it with a hammer, it would ring like cast iron and your hand would go numb to the elbow.

I am that mass. 

I do not have the radial symmetry of the Tapper or the needle-leg grace of the Spindle. My body is a low, heavy mound of dense, gristly mantle, entirely hidden beneath an asymmetrical carapace that I did not grow so much as mortar together. Every year, I extend a slow, tarry foot—no faster than the pitch-drop in a laboratory funnel—and pull a handful of the Sifters’ slag-spheres against my flank, cementing them there with an exudate of iron-silicate mucilage until my shell is six inches thick: a pebble-dash fortress of recycled plateau.

Why sit at the crossroads? Because the plateau is three thousand miles wide, and nothing on it remembers where it has been. The Dredgers are blind; they steer by the magnetic grain of the deep basalt and the subtle, chemical gradient of the stone ahead of them. Without a guide, ten thousand years of random intersections would turn the basins into a chewed, chaotic badland where engines constantly grind down their own recent tracks, starving their stomachs on stone that was leached of its sulfur only a century ago.

The ecology needs a switchman. Or rather, it needs a milestone that can smell time.

Beneath my mortar-shell, pressed flat against the raw slate of the trench floor, my ventral pad is not cartilage; it is a broad, wet tongue of chemoreceptive nerve-felt. Through it, I taste the two intersecting ribbons of stone. 

The east-west trench beneath my left flank was cut four hundred years ago; the afternoon Sweat that drains down it carries a faint, sweet recovery of oxidized iron weathered out of the walls, and the sharp, flinty tang of mature crustose lichen. The north-south trench beneath my right flank is fresh—cut forty years ago. Its water tastes flat, pale, and sour with spent sulfuric wash. It is dead stone.

For eleven years, I do nothing. I sit in the corner of the intersection while the amber smear of the sun slides across the south four thousand times. In the afternoons, the fog condenses on my pebbled back, trickling down the grooves between the slag-spheres into a shallow gutter around my mantle, where I drink the black water and filter out the wind-blown dust of dried Sifter-shell. At night, I let my outer mantle freeze solid, drawing my weak, copper-heavy blood into a fist-sized knot of warm muscle at my center, insulated by six inches of stone-mortar.

I listen through the rock. 

I know the Gleaner is lying ten feet away in the center of the crossroads. I have felt the once-an-hour thud of its cold heart through the slate for six winters. We do not bother one another. The Gleaner wants protein; I want nothing that walks.

Then, in the twelfth year, the rock begins to hum.

A Dredger is coming up the old east-west trench. It takes three weeks for the vibration to grow from a tickle in my ventral felt to a heavy, rhythmic grinding that makes the rime-frost shiver off my shell in the mornings. As it approaches the intersection, it is encountering a choice it does not know how to make: stay on the four-hundred-year-old track, which is only half-recovered, or turn into the forty-year-old track, which is barren, or cut virgin stone between them.

Two days before the Dredger arrives, when its ventral grate is still four hundred yards away, my core heats by four degrees. I draw upon the sulfur-salts I have hoarded for a decade. 

From a series of slit-vents along my right flank—the side facing the barren, forty-year-old trench—I begin to weep a thick, tallowy wax. It smells of nothing to a human nose, which would freeze shut in this air anyway, but to the chemical-sensing skirts of a Dredger, it is the concentrated, unmistakable stench of exhausted, over-leached slag. Simultaneously, across the old track in front of the oncoming engine, I extrude a thin, glassy smear of metallic salts—the chemical signature of rich, unworked pyrite.

I am lying to the machine, or perhaps I am remembering for it.

When the Dredger reaches the crossroads, its great, plated bulk looms over me, blotting out the pewter sky. The heat of its internal mills washes down into the trench—a sudden, tropical draft of thirty-five-degree air that smells of warm acid and wet iron. Its hydraulic feet pass within four inches of my mortared shell. The slate beneath me actually depresses by a millimeter under the locomotive weight; I feel the rock groan.

The Dredger’s leading skirt touches my wax-barrier on the right, recoils microscopically, tastes the metallic lure angled across the corner, and *veers*. 

Over the course of four hours, the three-hundred-ton organism pivots by twelve degrees, abandoning both old trenches to bite directly into the unworked, thousand-year-old basalt shoulder between them. 

As its flank grinds past my corner, the real transaction occurs. 

The side-plates of a Dredger, four feet off the ground, are always crusted with a thick, weeping scale of mineral-salt and dead, sloughed iron-chitin that builds up over decades, clogging its lateral breathing-spiracles. As the engine pivots against the berm of the crossroads, its flank scrapes heavily against the top of my anvil-shaped shell. 

I am built for the shear. My mortared dome acts as a stationary rasp. For six hours, the Dredger grinds its side against my back, planing off forty years of encrustation with a slow, dry shriek that travels for miles through the bedrock. The heavy, oxidized plates of its armor polish the tops of my slag-spheres until they shine like dark mirrors.

When the engine finally clears the corner and the Sifters come pouring past—flowing around my base like leaden shot around a boulder, while ten feet away the Gleaner’s slits open to take their silent tithe—I am buried ankle-deep in a warm snow of scraped-off Dredger scale. 

It is the richest meal on the plateau: pre-refined iron, sloughed chitin, and dried galvanic paste. 

Over the next three months, while the convoy shrinks to a dark smudge on the curve of the basin and the Spindle-wires overhead snap and drift away on the wind, I will slowly draw that drift of scale beneath my mantle. I will digest the organics, recharge my sulfur-glands, and use the toughest plates of the Dredger’s shed armor to mortar a new, higher ring onto my shell.

I will grow half an inch this century. 

Eventually—in two thousand years, or three—I will have grown so wide and high in the throat of the intersection that a Dredger will not be able to pivot past me. Its grate will mount my shell. It will take the engine four days to grind through the six-inch masonry of Sifter-pearls and stolen armor I have spent millennia cementing together, milling my calcified history back into the slurry of its stomach, before it moves on across the basin, leaving a fresh, flat ribbon of pale stone where the crossroads used to be.
