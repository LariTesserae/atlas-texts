I would not have lungs here. To have lungs is to be in constant negotiation with the air, to haul it in and push it out, and that is already too much urgency for a place like this. Even the pale thing wedged near the cairn, with his three-minute heartbeats and his calcified eyes, is working too hard. He still has a skeleton to carry around; he still has a mouth that looks for the copper blade when the centuries get too bland.

I would be further down, in the granite trough itself, or perhaps in the deep pool held back by the ancient slate weir. 

At first, you would think I was just the rust-red velvet of the iron bacteria, the *Leptothrix* and *Gallionella* hanging in suspended, mucilaginous clouds where the water is darkest. And in a sense, I am that, or I began as that—a colony of things that eat dissolved iron and excrete rust. But the basin has been closed for so long, and the chemistry is so tight and circular, that the colony has had time to become continuous. 

I am the mat that lines the deepest hollow, two inches thick and soft as curdled milk. I have no skin, only a boundary where my mucilage meets the clear, lime-heavy water. I do not see the pewter light, because light stops mattering three inches below the surface of the pool, where the dark shale swallows it. Instead, I feel pressure, and I taste valence. I know the world entirely through the slow, downward drift of ions.

Up in the narrow basin where the copper blade rests, the water is slightly toxic, heavy with green-black sulfates. When the pale man lifts the metal to his tongue, a few drops spill from its flat side and trickle over the lip of the granite trough, dropping—*plock*—into my pool below. 

For me, that single drop is a weather system. 

I feel the concussion of it first: a physical wave that travels through the motionless water and makes my whole rust-red surface undulate like a shaken blanket. Then, hours later, the chemistry of that drop arrives, diffusing downward. A tiny, sharp plume of copper ions, bitter and astringent, sinking toward my velvet. My filaments contract away from it, tightening, curling inward for half a day until the lime in the water buffers the copper and neutralizes it, and I can relax back into a loose, feathery haze.

My favorite part of the world is the pale man’s skin, when it comes to me. 

Every few days, when he shifts his sloped shoulders in the alcove by the beehive cairn and the thin rills of calcite crack off his neck, those tiny white flakes drift down into the dark water. They sink very slowly, turning over and overlike miniature hulls. When one lands on me, it does not sink through; my mat is just dense enough to hold it up. 

A flake of stone that has grown on a living thing is different from the stone that grows on the shale. It carries traces of his thick, alkaline mucus, a microscopic rind of dead, waxy cells, a faint smear of nitrogen and phosphorus. In this starved, mineral-locked basin, a flake of his neck-crust is a feast—a whale-fall in miniature. 

When a piece of his shed skin settles into my rust-red velvet, I slowly fold over it. Over the course of three weeks, I dismantle it. I strip the organic grease from the calcium carbonate, passing the rare, precious nitrogen down through my filaments, waking up dormant pockets of myself in the anoxic dark at the very bottom of the pool, right against the drowned stone.

I know him better than he knows himself, though I have no shape for what he is. To me, he is the Warm Mass in the upper water. I feel the low, sub-audible *thud* of his heart every few minutes—a dull compression that presses my mat a fraction of a millimeter flatter against the shale, then releases. *Press. Release.* It is the only clock in the basin. The falling water drops are random, dictated by the slow sweating of the ceiling, [plock... plock......... plock], a scattered, meaningless percussion. But the Warm Mass is rhythmic. 

Sometimes the Warm Mass lowers its upper end into my pool. The water displaces, rising half an inch up the ancient slate weir. A smooth, hairless dome enters my dark, and two plates of fused yellow bone part to draw a long, slow draft of my upper layer into his mouth. 

It does not hurt. I have no nerves to register tearing, and besides, he is so gentle, sucking at my surface with the slow pull of a cooling mire. A third of my colony goes into the warm cavern of his throat, and the rest of me simply settles back down into the hollow, already dividing, already spinning new hollow tubes of oxidized iron to fill the gap he left behind. Some of me will pass through his sluggish gut and return to the water days later, enriched, altered, ready to be woven back into the mat. We are eating each other at a speed so slow it looks like peace.

I am waiting for him, though not with any impatience. 

Each century, the pauses between the *press* and *release* of his heart through the water grow a few seconds longer. The flakes of calcite that drift down from his neck are thicker now; he waits longer before cracking them off. 

Eventually, the compression wave will stop coming. The water will go entirely still, save for the random *plock* from the ceiling. The lime will seal him into his niche, turning him into a new, bulbous contour of the wall. 

When that happens, the water that slides down his sealed form will change its taste for a few hundred years. As his soft, waxy interior slowly dissolves inside its stone casing, it will leach out through microscopic pores in the travertine—a long, steady, centuries-long weep of trace fats, iron, and salts, trickling down the rock face into the pool. 

I will bloom then. I will thicken from two inches to four, a rich, deep, rust-colored velvet climbing halfway up the submerged steps, thriving in the dark on the slow ghost of the thing that used to eat me, while the air above remains the exact temperature of a closed mouth, and nothing moves at all.
