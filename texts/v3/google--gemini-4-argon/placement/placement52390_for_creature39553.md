I am currently forty feet below the surface of the third eastern terrace, suspended in the dark, deafening rush of a primary xylem-conduit, and I am trying to decide if the vibration in my left flank is a structural flaw or just a herd of Tympanics fighting on the plaza above.

It is not a thought made of words. It is a slow, chemical syllogism, written in gradients of potassium ions and auxin-analogues across a twelve-foot web of dark violet filaments. 

To live as a Gall is to be perpetually dissolving and rebuilding your own bedroom. Ahead of me—*upward*, toward the warm, high-altitude sun I can already feel as a faint thermal pull through forty feet of iron-hard wood—my leading edge is secreting enzymes that unpick the lignin locks of the host’s cells. The solid timber softens into a warm, pressurized jelly. I slide my neural threads through the slush, tasting the valley’s blood, reading the stress-loads of the great buttresses above, and then, at my trailing edge, I trigger the recalcification. The wood snaps shut behind me, seamless and tight. 

There is a profound, heavy claustrophobia to this life, though I have no concept of open space to compare it against until I make an eye. Inside the cambium, the world is entirely haptic and acoustic. The great cathedral chord of the valley does not sound like music down here; it is a relentless, crushing hydraulic shear. I feel the *thump* of the three-yard drainage rosette a quarter-mile to the north not as a bass note, but as a sudden, violent drop in turgor pressure that yanks at my trailing filaments like an undertow.

The vibration in my left flank repeats—a sharp, stuttering *rat-tat-tat* of phase-inverted forty-hertz waves hammering down through the wood. Definitely Tympanics. Two or three of them, clustering too close to the lip of an acoustic vent, their calcified struts chattering against the cuticle as they try to starve each other out of the noise. They are harmless, mostly, though when a big one dies and its geodesic cage collapses into a wet basin, the pieces can jam a valve. 

I alter my trajectory, angling upward at forty-five degrees, swimming through the grain of the monolith. 

As I rise, the wood warms. The deep, anoxic smell of the lower vascular conduits—pure iron and pressurized water—gives way to the brighter, sharper chemistry of the photosynthetic skin. Here, the plant cells are packed with purple-green plastids, humming with the frantic, microscopic work of turning mountain light into sugar. It tastes like copper and bruisedmint. 

I reach the boundary layer, the underside of the plaza’s bark, just where a massive, rib-like arch springs out of the floor to curve three hundred feet into the air. 

Before I breach, I spread myself flat, becoming a dark, horse-sized bruise just beneath the translucent, waxy cuticle. Through the thin skin of the host, the light hits me—diffuse, watery, and blindingly bright compared to the deep timber. I can feel the tiny, rhythmic rasping of a jade gastropod sliding right over my dorsal filaments, its radula polishing the bark above my head, oblivious to the mind pooled three millimeters beneath its belly.

I want to see the arch. This is an old habit among my kind, an evolutionary tic left over from whatever engineered this basin, or perhaps just the inevitable result of being a brain trapped in a wall: we are obsessed with the geometry we maintain.

I send a sharp, localized pulse of abscisic acid into the host tissue above me. Instantly, ten million microscopic stomata clench shut. Above the cuticle, the constant, sweating birth of water stops. The quarter-inch sheet of runoff from the higher terraces parts around my dry circle, hissing faintly as it hits the warm, exposed wax. 

Now, the sculpture. It requires a delicate touch; push the meristematic triggers too hard and you get a tumor, a knotted burl of useless bark that takes hours to reabsorb. I want clarity. I coax the pluripotency of the surface cells, feeding them a rich broth of my own dark, conductive cytoplasm, guiding the turgor pressure upward like a glassblower shaping a molten gather.

The floor swells. It feels like stretching a limb after a long sleep, except I am inventing the limb as I stretch it. 

I build a tripod of thick, woody stalks, four feet high, to get above the glare of the water-caustics. At the apex, instead of a single medicine-ball eye, I split the growth into a ring of seven smaller, compound lenses, facing outward in a full circle, and beneath them, a broad, ruffled collar of acoustic membranes—a temporary theft of the Tympanics’ design, grown from crisp, translucent phloem-bast. 

I flood the new organ with my violet filaments, packing them tight behind the resinous corneas, linking them to the sap-filled lenses. 

The transition from the wood-sense to sight is always a violent shock. 

*White. Sky. Violet. Gold.*

For ten seconds, my chemistry recoils as the sheer emptiness of the upper world hammers into my neural net. Inside the wood, everything is touching everything else; up here, there is *nothing* between my lens and the rim of the valley four miles away. Just thin, dry mountain air, roaring with the organ-chord. 

My seven eyes focus, adjusting the fluid pressure in their corneas. 

The plaza around me is a blinding mirror of sheet-water, throwing jagged, golden grids of light up onto the underside of the great arch. Thirty yards away, five Tympanics are frozen near a flute-column, their kite-like bodies blurred with vibration, looking like intricate, pale skeletons caught in a gale. As my stalked body finishes extruding from the floor with a wet, fibrous *creak*, all five of them instantly pivot their eyeless cages toward me. I have cast an acoustic shadow across their grazing ground. They hold their ground for a moment, tilting their tambourine-membranes to taste my shape in the echo, before skittering sideways on their silent, calloused stilts, giving me a wide berth. They know what happens if they step on a Gall’s surface-organ; I would simply soften the floor beneath their pads and let the wood swallow them to the knee.

Then, a slash of cyan and fiery magenta cuts across my third and fourth lenses. 

A Laminar, twelve feet of living glass, has just hit the dry patch of my twenty-foot circle at twenty miles an hour. Robbed of its lubricating boundary layer of water, its leading edge catches on the dry cuticle. In a breathtaking display of hydro-mechanical panic, it pumps its internal veins rigid, rearing six feet into the air to peel itself off the dry spot, twisting its ribbon-body in the sun like a falling sheet of mica before slapping back down into the wet current on my far side and vanishing into pure refraction. 

Only the faint, sharp smell of ozone from its cavitation trail drifts back to my chemical receptors.

I stay like this for an hour. I do not think about the past, because the valley has no history—nothing decays, so nothing leaves a record. There are no fossils in wood that continually re-grows itself. I simply rotate my lenses, watching the high, thin clouds pass over the roofless greenhouse of my body. I track a shrimp-heron as it daintily steps over a yard-wide drainage rosette just before the great, fleshy sphincter *thumps* shut, sending a concentric ring of standing waves racing across the mirror-flat water.

I listen through my ruffled collar to the ten thousand pipes of the valley. Somewhere high up on the western rim, a viaduct is singing a fraction of a hertz flat—a buildup of mineral scale in a secondary flute, perhaps, or a dead heron wedged in a baffle. Another of my kind will hear it, closer to the source, and will swim through the walls to dissolve the blockage. 

The sun drops behind the western buttresses. The sharp, cool shadows race across the five-mile basin, turning the bruised purple of the wood to a deep, twilight slate. The gold goes out of the water. Without the direct solar heat, the air temperature plunges quickly at this altitude, and my extruded stalks begin to feel brittle, the sap thickening in my seven corneas.

It is time to go back inside.

I do not pull my filaments back all at once; that would leave a dead husk of wood on the plaza, violating the immaculate emptiness of the terrace. Instead, I reverse the hormone cascade from the top down. The resinous lenses cloud over, soften, and melt back into the stalks. The sky blurs into a bright, formless smear, then vanishes. The ruffled acoustic collar wilts, folding into the tripod like wet paper. 

Sight goes. The dry, empty roaring of the air goes, replaced once more by the deep, comforting, bone-shaking hydraulic thunder of the interior. 

The stalks sink into the floor. I release the stomata. Warm, celery-scented water rushes back over the twenty-foot circle, erasing the dry patch, carrying away the last chemical traces of my extrusion. Within three minutes, the jade gastropods have already glided back over the spot, polishing the newly hardened cuticle until it reflects the first stars.

Below the seamless skin, in the warm, dark, iron-hard wood, I gather my violet threads and begin to swim downward, back toward the deep pipes.
