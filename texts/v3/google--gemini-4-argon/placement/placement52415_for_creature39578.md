Do not look for me in the greased troughs. I have no knees to feel the *thud-drag* of the weavers, no teeth to ache for the dry grit of three-thousand-year-old clay, no lungs to labour in the ninety-eight-degree soup of the vaults. Anything that walks through these corridors is only a tool with a heartbeat—a temporary trowel, a short-lived hod-carrier hauling stick and spit from one dark pocket to another before dropping into the moss to be dismantled by the grey beetles.

To find what I am, you must put your palm flat against the warm, purring flank of the buttress and push your mind four hundred feet inward, past the sweat-bee tombs, past the sour nurseries of the blind wood-borers, down to the boundary where the chewed world meets the cold, unchewed tooth of the buried granite peak.

I am down in the dark where the heat begins. 

If you broke open a gallery wall three hundred feet below the plaza, you would not see me, or rather, you would think I was only the white, leathery felt binding the ancient pellets of red lowland clay together. You would smell me, though: the sharp, cellar-cool tang of mushroom and wet iron that underpins the formic acid and the sweet rot of the figs. 

I am the *pale-thread*. I am the mortar’s memory. 

Ten thousand generations ago, before the first weaver-bird smeared a beakful of river mud against the stone, I was a small thing, a smudge of frost-colored mycelium living under a scrap of wind-blown birch bark in the granite clefts. Then the birds came, and the termites, and the early, sighted ancestors of the badger-beasts, piling their ceilings over me to keep the mountain wind off their grubs. They buried me in their house-Sweepings. They thought they were entombing the rock, but they were only building me a body.

Now, I am three miles wide from horn to horn, and five hundred feet thick at the saddle. I am the only single thing in this valley.

The ants have their millions of six-legged shards; the *thrum-heavies* have their solitary, shuffling bulk; the mantle-slugs have their tiled congregations. They are all broken into pieces that must crawl around each other in the greasy dark. I do not crawl. I am already at the western horn while I am drinking the dissolved shell of the dead millipede in the sunken plaza. 

Every time an amber ant pats a pellet of fern-pith into a crack, my microscopic white hairs—my *hyphae*—are waiting in the dry wall behind it. Before the ant has stepped aside for its sister, I have breached the wet pellet, drinking the sugar from the chewed fern, lacing the new mud to the old mud with ten thousand chitinous threads stronger than spider-silk. Without me, the first big autumn rain that breached the stratus cap would turn this entire five-hundred-foot saddle into a greasy landslide of soup, dumping three millennia of chambers down into the lowland plains. 

I hold the mountain up so that the mountain will keep feeding me.

And because I am everywhere in the masonry, I am the only thing here that hears the whole song. 

The badger-beast feels the four-minute wave of the soldier termites through its breastbone, yes, a single rolling *thrum* that passes under its belly and is gone. I feel the wave begin at the eastern lip where a rock-python has just crushed a gallery; I feel it ring through the cured, hollow flutes of the middle-tier baffles; I feel it bounce off the buried granite spire at the valley’s heart; I feel it arrive at the badger’s sternum; and I feel its echo return, twenty minutes later, attenuated and soft, to the very termites that began it. To the insects, it is an alarm. To me, it is a sonar ping showing me the shape of my own ribs.

I know where I am thin.

Right now, sixty feet above the parabolic arch where the weaver just wedged the fig branch, there is a shearing fault in an old chimney-mound. The red clay there was laid down eight hundred years ago during a dry century, and it is too brittle; the weight of three new pandanus trees is pulling the lip outward by a millimeter a month. If that chimney falls, it will smash through the plaza floor and expose ten thousand square feet of my deep, warm composting-vaults to the cold, high-altitude stratus mist. I will scar over, as I always do, losing twenty tons of my white flesh to the chill before the ants can roof me back in.

I do not have a brain to plan a repair, any more than the badger has a blueprint for the arch. What I have is chemistry, and a slow, hydraulic hunger.

Deep in the hairline fracture of that failing chimney, I begin to sweat. 

I pull water up from the deep granite seepage four hundred feet below—pumping it cell by cell through my dark cables—and I push it out into the brittle eight-hundred-year-old clay at the fault line. Into that water, I bleed a specific ester: a volatile, musky oil that smells like bruised almonds and warm tallow. 

It takes six hours for the scent to wick through the porous mastic to the surface of the chimney-mound. 

When it gets there, it does not drift away; there is no wind to take it. It hangs in a heavy, invisible bead along the crack. 

Down in the trough, fifty yards away, the *thrum-heavy* that patched the arch is sleeping wedged into a buttress, its back-ferns soaking up the trickle of condensation from the wall. As it breathes, a single molecule of my almond-ester drifts down the chute and touches the forty-second barbel on the left side of its shovel-snout.

In the dim, warm wet of the beast's hindbrain, an itch ignites. Not a thought—an appetite. Its gourd-sized throat-glands swell with alkaline spit. Its ever-growing molars ache for the specific, iron-rich grit of eight-hundred-year-old drought-clay.

I do not watch them come. I have no eyes. 

I simply wait in the dark behind the crack, holding the dry crumbs together with my white threads until I feel the heavy, familiar *thud* of a sternum leaning against the wall to listen, and the wet, warm flood of ammonia-spit soaking into my dry places, bringing me another mouthful of the mountain.
