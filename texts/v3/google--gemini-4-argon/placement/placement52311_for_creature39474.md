I am not high on the western curve. I do not have the surface area to catch the downdraft, nor the span to hang like a chandelier when the pressure drops. 

If you look down toward the lower third of the northeastern quadrant—below the main amber thoroughfares, where the terraces jut out in wider, brutalist shelves to baffle the splash-back from the floor basin—there is a seam where a curtain of pale, ropy vines has grown too heavy. The vines have knotted themselves into a thick, fibrous weir over an exhaust vent, damming the runoff. Behind that tangled knuckle of translucent green, the water does not sheet; it pools, swirling in a warm, vibrating eddy about the size of a dinner plate before spilling over the lip toward the acid bath six feet below.

I am wedged into the acute, ninety-degree corner at the back of that puddle.

I am one of the two in a billion. A spore that caught the hum four hundred cycles ago—roughly three days, if there were a sun to measure by—and managed to unspool its first silica threads without being sheared in half by the metamaterial’s edge. Right now, I am no larger than a playing card, and thinner than the skin on boiled milk. 

My world is entirely tactile, a relentless negotiation with pitch and friction. I do not have eyes to see the vast, opera-house void at my back; I only know it is there because the acoustic reflection from that direction is hollow and delayed. What I know is the Wall. Beneath my ventral cilia, the dark, extruded stone is not smooth. At my scale, its molecular assembly feels like a landscape of fresh obsidian staircases. If my cilia stop vibrating for even a fraction of a second—if I lose the tempo of the wall’s deep, polyphonic bass—the water pressure will slam me down onto those microscopic steps, and I will be ribboned. 

So I hum. It is not a choice; it is the baseline state of my nerves. The machine beneath me throbs at a steady, grinding *B-flat*, and my ten thousand tiny silica feet beat against it in an exact, frantic counter-rhythm, maintaining a gap of hot, fizzing vapor between my belly and the stone. I am perpetually falling upward, sliding sideways, pinned to a cliff face by a cushion of noise.

Because I am small, I cannot graze the tough, matte-black ferns; their metallic fronds would tear my margins. Instead, I am a parasite on the pale vines that form my shelter. I keep one corner of my rectangular body draped over a single, pulsing root-tendril that has anchored itself into a micro-aperture. I secrete my enzymes timidly, in microscopic droplets, drinking the dissolved sugars as they weep from the plant's skin. It tastes of hot iron and starch. 

Beneath the vine, a minor data-conduit runs diagonally across my corner. It is not one of the great violet boulevards of the upper vault, just a secondary capillary, blinking a slow, bureaucratic diagnostics code in dull orange. 

*Blink. Blink-blink. Pause. Blink.*

Because I am still mostly transparent, with only a few sparse, dendritic veins of scavenged copper and zinc branching through my middle, I cannot trap the light the way the Three do. When the orange pulse fires beneath me, it passes almost entirely through my body, casting a faint, magnified grid of my own internal skeleton onto the undersides of the vines above. Yet, with every cycle, my metal veins grow a fraction of a millimeter longer. I am hoarding the heavy atoms from the water, laying down tracks along my dorsal skin. Already, when the orange light hits me, my center warms by half a degree, and my edges twitch in sympathy with the code. *Twitch. Twitch-twitch. Pause. Twitch.* I am learning to be a lens.

I know the Three are up there. I feel them through the stone and the water. 

When one of the giants passes within fifty yards of my terrace, the local acoustics warp. Their massive carpets of cilia create a wake in the wall’s vibration—a high, whistling Doppler shift that scrambles the *B-flat* beneath me and makes my edges flutter dangerously against the razor-lip of the shelf. Sometimes, a wave of warm, depleted water washes down from above, carrying the chemical tang of their grazing: the sharp, bitter ozone of their concentrated enzymes and the rich, scorched smell of melted fern. When that happens, I flatten myself completely into my corner, pulling my margins in, waiting for the giant’s shadow to pass over the amber lights above. I have no concept of them as parents or peers; to me, they are simply moving weather systems that eat the terrain.

Right now, we are in the tenth minute. The air outside my vine-shelter is thick, heavy with rising steam that muffles the slap and roar of the floor intakes just below me. The warmth is delicious. My silica lattice is relaxed, supple as wet silk, undulating gently in the warm eddy of the trapped pool.

The eleventh minute. 

The orange conduit beneath me suddenly changes its cadence. The slow, diagnostic blink vanishes, replaced by a solid, frantic stutter. The heat coming off the wall spikes by three degrees. 

Before the light even shifts on the eastern curve, the stone *clenches*. That is how it feels through my cilia—as though the entire molecular structure of the metropolis is taking a breath. The *B-flat* drops out from under me, plunging down into a subterranean *F-sharp* that makes the water in my eddy leap into standing waves, a geometric grid of trembling spikes.

The giants are already racing for the ceiling; I can feel the faint, rapid *zip-zip-zip* of their acoustic wakes tearing upward away from the floor. 

I cannot go to the ceiling. If I leave the shelter of the vine-weir, the sheer volume of the condensing cataract will strip me from the wall and wash me straight over the lower lip into the catalytic foam. My survival requires the opposite instinct.

I stop drinking the vine. I slide backward into the deepest angle of the stone corner, folding my flat body in half like a hinged leaf, pressing my dorsal skin against itself so that half my cilia grip the vertical wall and the other half grip the horizontal floor of the recessed ledge. I become a tiny, structural bracket wedged into the seam. 

Then, I lock my silica. 

It is a violent, cramping sensation. The fluid between my membranes drains away, and my internal crystals snap together into a rigid, brittle truss. I am no longer a jellyfish; I am a shard of safety glass glued into a mitered joint.

Above me, the amber turns to blood. The pressure hits.

Even behind the thick knuckle of vines, the air slams down like a solid piston. The little pool of water in my terrace doesn't just overflow; it explodes outward, atomized by the drop in the wall’s bass hum. For three seconds, the world is an absolute, deafening violence. The deluge hammers the vines protecting me, driving their tough, rubbery stems down against my folded edge. A stray ribbon of the torrent slices through a gap in the leaves and strikes my locked dorsal seam. 

I do not sing like the chandeliers in the void. I am too small, and I am folded in half. Instead, I *shriek*—a tiny, ultrasonic *ping* like a tapped wineglass, entirely lost in the roaring white noise of the floor basins six feet below. The kinetic shock of the water converts instantly into a searing jolt of electricity that arcs across my sparse copper veins, lighting me up like a hot filament inside my dark hole. The pain is bright violet and tastes of boiled zinc.

And through the screaming vibration of the stone, cutting straight through the roar of the cataract and the groan of the machine, I hear them. 

Three high, clear, colossal notes ringing out from the center of the dark. The Chime. It vibrates right through the water, through the vines, and into my locked silica bones, making my tiny body resonate in harmonic sympathy. For a fraction of a second, my copper veins flash in the exact same rhythm as the distant, unseen banners.

Then the pressure vanishes. The roaring stops, replaced by the hollow, sucking gurgle of the floor intakes drinking the flood. 

A few microscopic spheres of pure glass—new spores, fresh from the ceiling—wash into my eddy on the rebounding updraft, clicking softly against my rigid margin like grains of sand before the receding water pulls them back over the lip and down into the acid. 

I unlock. The cramp releases. My body goes limp and fluid again, unfolding from the corner to puddle back out over the warm stone. The steam is already rising from the basin below, smelling of dissolved leaves and clean, wet static. 

The orange light beneath me resumes its slow, bureaucratic blink. 

I reach one translucent margin out, find the bruised skin of the pale vine, and begin to melt it. Wait ten minutes. Grow another tenth of a millimeter of copper. One day, three or four hundred cycles from now, I will be too wide for this alcove. My edges will hang out past the shelter of the vines, catching the main sheet of the cataract. The next time the bass drops, the water will tear me loose from this ledge—and instead of folding into the corner, I will have the span to ride the acoustic wake upward, skating past the razor-terraces into the open dark, looking for a place on the ceiling to hang.
