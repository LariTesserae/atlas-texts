My left knee hasn’t bent since the number-two dynamo threw a shoe, and that was three hundred thousand blows ago. Give or take. You stop counting the big numbers once the wood gets past your calf. 

At first, when the pale velvet crept up over my ankle-bone, it itched like a turpentine burn. I used to take a brass scraper to it during the Low-Stroke, peeling the gray skin back until my own gray skin bled, stubborn as a young man with a new chisel. Then my hip seized up during a cold-snap in the upper boughs, and I sat down in the warm crook where the main return-pipe bends into the west tenement wall. I rested my cheek against the sweating copper, just for a four-hundred-count, to let the heat draw the ache out of my joint. 

I didn't get back up. 

It isn’t bad, once the root-threads find the marrow. There’s a month of sharp, green lightning up the shinbone while the *Ficus* sorts out which of your veins are worth keeping and which ones it wants to pack with lignum, and then the pain just flattens out into a heavy, wooden hum. You stop feeling the wet. You stop needing to grease your thighs. Now, from the navel down, I am four hundred tons of boundary-wall, and from the navel up, I am an old man with a brass wrench tied to his wrist with swift-gut.

They call this spot the Singer’s Elbow, because the three-inch tap-line coming off the deep arterial trunk makes a high, thin *eeee-uh* right behind my left ear whenever the governor-weights drop. A young Tapper couldn't stand it for two bells; it would rattle his teeth loose and make his nose bleed. To me, it’s just the way the room breathes. 

I can’t see the plaza anymore. The grease-cataracts took my left eye five years back, and the right one only catches the big amber swings when somebody opens the number-four firebox to rake the clinkers out. Mostly the world is just a warm, orange smear down below, and the dry-leaf roar of the grease-swifts boiling past my knees five feet out in the drop. When the swarm banks hard around the steam-stack, the wind off their wings pushes the hot mist into my face, smelling of mouse-dirt and burnt pine. 

I don’t need eyes. My hip-bone is grown solid into the main beam of the third-floor gantry, and the beam is grown solid into the west wall, and the west wall goes down three miles into the wet dark where the big sap cooks. 

I can see the whole Ward with my tailbone. 

Right now, we are at stroke three-hundred-and-ten. The main governor at the north end is tight—too tight. Somebody down on the floor used a green-wood wedge on the relief rocker two blows ago, and it’s swelling in the damp, holding the valve three-eighths of an inch shy of its seat. I know it because the *chug-thump* coming up through my fused pelvis has a little hitch at the top of the stroke. *Chug-thump-tink. Chug-thump-tink.* Like a man with a pebble in his boot-heel. 

Wait until after the blow, I think. Let it settle. 

A lumen-grub crawls out of the deep ditch between my eyebrows, where the skin is folded thick as a walnut shell, and creeps down the bridge of my nose to drink the warm grease around my goggle-rim. I don’t brush it off. I haven't worn goggles since my knees went; the crab-shell lenses are still stuck to my cheekbones with old gum, though the lenses themselves cracked and fell out long ago. The rims just make a nice, high dam to keep the forehead-sweat out of my eye-sockets, and the grubs keep the seams clean. It glows a dull, fat yellow-green against my nose, lighting up the gray bristle on my upper lip. 

Stroke four-hundred. 

Down in the orange smear, the sound changes. The clicking of the tread-crabs—which usually sounds like ten thousand women knitting with iron needles—tightens up. They are sliding into the gullies. 

Something cold and wet drops onto my bare shoulder. A young girl, maybe twelve summers, her skin slick with fresh tallow and her brow-cuts bright with six fat grubs. She doesn't say anything—nobody wastes breath at four-hundred—just hooks her greased knee over the copper elbow beside my ribs, shoves a warm tin cup of mashed fungus and crab-fat against my mouth, and starts scraping the white sulfur-rot out of my armpit with her thumb. 

That’s my brother’s granddaughter. Or her daughter. They all have the same broad, flat thumbs. I open my mouth, let her tip the sour, penny-tasting paste onto my tongue, and swallow it down. In return, I take my heavy left hand—the one where the knuckles have swollen into solid, bark-skinned walnuts—and lay it flat against her collarbone. 

I tap my middle finger twice against her skin, right over the bone, then drag my thumb-callus across her grease. 

*North-end. Rocker-wedge. Too-fat.*

She nods once, her hard little chin bumping my knuckles, and then the first bell hits. 

*CLANG.*

The sound doesn't come through my ears; it comes up through my wooden thighs and snaps my jaw shut. The girl doesn't scramble away. There's no time to get back inside the tenement shutter-hole, and besides, my lap is the safest shadow-niche on the west gantry. Over the last ten years, the iron-bark has thrown a heavy, curved lip up over my shins like the prow of a barge, deflecting the direct blast of the north flues. 

She drops her head into the hollow between my chin and my collarbone, locks her ankles behind my rooted back, and pulls her swift-leather cape over both our skulls. 

*CLANG.*

The flying curtains of clack-moths drop past us like wet sacks of gravel. One of them clips my brass wrench, spinning it on its gut-string, and then a dozen tread-crabs that live up here on the gantry pipes come scuttling over my wooden knees, tucking their flat, greasy edges under my ribs and over the girl’s calves, locking their joints with a sharp *snick-snick-snick*. 

*CLANG.*

The world goes white and loud. 

Even under the leather cape, the heat hits like a mallet. The copper pipe behind my back swells a quarter-inch against my spine, burning hot, as the pressure dumps. Outside our little tent of oilskin and crab-shell, the forty-second gale screams across the nave. I can feel the whole tenement building lean back an inch into the mountain as the blast hits its chest. The wood around my hips—my wood, my legs—opens its pores with a long, greedy, sucking *shhhhhh* that vibrates right up into my lungs. 

This is the part the young ones don't know about. When you’re still walking, the blow is just a thing you hide from so your meat doesn't cook. 

When you’re rooted, the blow is when you eat. 

As the boiling turpentine-fog batters the bark around my waist, the sap-veins in my thighs dilate. A sudden, thick rush of heat pumps up from the floor, through the gray wood that used to be my calves, and hits the live blood at my groin. It tastes like lightning and rosin at the back of my throat. My old heart gives a massive, double-thump to match the stalling boilers, and for forty seconds I am not an old man sitting in the dark; I am three hundred feet of iron-strangler drinking the sky, and I am the brass engines chewing the sap, and I am the little girl shivering against my grease-caked ribs, and I am my grandfather’s grandfather, who is just a round, warm lump in the wall twenty feet below my heels. 

The valves slam shut. *WHUMP.*

The silence afterward is always shaped like a cathedral, just for three heartbeats, before the drizzle starts and the insects begin to screech again. 

The crabs unlock and slide off our legs, dripping yellow water. The girl pulls the hood back, wipes her nose on my shoulder, and drops off the gantry without looking down, sliding down the slick curve of a root-buttress toward the plaza with a heavy chisel already in her hand to go fix the north rocker. 

I let my head tip back against the sweating copper pipe. Warm condensation runs down the iron-bark of the wall, follows the groove of my neck, and pools in the hollow of my collarbone. 

Down below, four hundred feet away through the solid timber of the floor, I feel her bare, calloused feet hit the gully. A minute later, there comes the sharp, clean *tink-tink-chug* of an iron mallet shaving two-eighths of an inch off a wet wedge. 

The hitch in the governor smooths out. The bassline drops back into its grease. 

*Chug-thump. Chug-thump.*

I lift my brass wrench, let it fall against the three-inch tap-line—*one dull, two short*—to tell the old woman rooted over by the east intake that the north end is square, and then I close my eye and start counting to four hundred and eighty.
