I am seventy feet down from the wind-baffle, wedged into the narrow, vibrating ribcage of the East Wall, where the raw diorite smells like struck flint and the iron tastes of dry salt. 

My station is the *Number Four Leaf-cradle*—a nest of nine-foot vanadium-steel plates stacked like the pages of a heavy book, bolted directly into a freshly sheared fault-plane on one side and pinned to the main smelter-gantry on the other. It is not a place where one stands. I live in a three-point wedge: left heel hooked into a stamped-steel rung, right knee jammed against the greasy curve of a nitrogen accumulator, and my back pressed flat against the warm, sweating casing of an exhaust flue. 

I am a *Shim-Driver*. The Spring-Masters down in the dark abyss listen to the mountain’s deep throat; I am the one who answers it with wedges of brass and boiled rawhide.

My eyes are gone to the gray-pearl stage—not yet the solid, milky quartz of the Night-Gauge elders down on the wool cables, but thick enough that the amber sodium lamps across the chasm look like grease-smears on hornblende. I don't need the light. Sight is too slow for the Cleft anyway; by the time you *see* a magnesium strut begin to bow under a lateral fault-slip, the rivets have already sheared and taken your shins off with the shrapnel. 

Instead, I read the *Number Four* with my left hand. 

Unlike the valve-tuners up top who wear three-fingered wool mittens, my left hand is bare, the skin calloused into a continuous, hoof-like pad plowed with black furrows of molybdenum and carbon. My thumb rests across the parting-seam where the third and fourth steel leaves meet; my little finger touches the raw, unweathered diorite of the mountain itself. 

All day, the mountain and the city argue between my thumb and pinky. 

The rock is cold—a deep, airless, stratospheric cold that wants to freeze the marrow in my phalanges—and it is never quiet. Even when the great faults aren't slipping, the Aethelgard uplift has a high, crystalline hum, like a glass rod being rubbed with dry silk. It is the sound of hornblende crystals crushing together six miles under our boots. The iron of the leaf-spring, by contrast, is warm and greasy, vibrating with a five-four rhythm from the stamps in the lower assaying-tier: *shhh-tok, whuff, t-t-t-tak*. 

Between the two, I keep the peace.

In my belt-loop hangs a bundle of *feeler-strips*—ribbons of cold-rolled copper ranging from the thickness of a thumbnail down to the breadth of a shale-moth’s wing. When the mountain groans and inches inward by two-tenths of a millimeter, the gap in the leaf-cradle tightens. The five-four rhythm in my thumb goes sharp, rising half a tone in pitch because the iron is binding. Without looking, I pull a number-three copper shim from my apron, spit through the side-port of my lung-sponge to give the metal a smear of slick, sulfurous fungus-slime, and tap it into the jaws of the spring with a brass-headed maul. *Tink. Tink. Thup.* The pitch drops back to its proper, loose hum. The gantry breathes again.

I am never alone in the cradle. 

Tucked into the crook of my left knee, where the heat from the exhaust flue pools against my greasy wool trousers, is an old, five-legged stoker I call *Gristle*. He lost his front-left paw three winters ago to a telescoping bridge-joint, so he can no longer leap the forty-foot chasm with the wild packs when the counterweights drop. Instead, he rides the Number Four with me. 

He is heavier than the wild ones, fat on the prime, lemon-yellow sulfur crusts I scrape for him from the accumulator seams, and his pale, hairless hide is polished to a dull pewter where he rubs against my graphite-slicked sleeve. We have an arrangement. My hornblende goggles are crusted with salt and pitch, and my ears are tuned to the low, structural groans of the steel; I am deaf to the small, high-frequency treacheries of the plumbing. Gristle is not. His long, flat snout is a honeycomb of crimson heat-pits that twitch constantly in the draft. 

Every twenty minutes or so, he uncoils from my knee, his five double-thumbed paws clicking quietly on the vanadium plates, and crawls along the underside of the high-pressure nitrogen lines. If a bronze union is developing a microscopic pinhole—something that would slice a man’s fingers off with an invisible, freezing jet before he ever heard it hiss—Gristle stops. The skin along his spine ripples, and he lets out a low, rattling *churr* that vibrates right through the soles of my boots. I reach over with a lead-patch and a crimping-iron, seal the leak by feel, and pay him with a thumb-sized flake of warm, violet fungus from my own mask-cage. He takes it from my blackened fingers with teeth that click like porcelain dice, his blind, vestigial eyes squeezed shut in the sulfuric draft.

The Cleft speaks through me, too. Because the Number Four Leaf-cradle is one of the primary anchor-points for the upper three tiers, the main tension-cables pass within an arm's reach of my right shoulder, encased in their thick, lanolin-greased sheaths of braided wool. 

I am a relay. 

When the oxygen-caravans arrive at the exterior wind-baffle seventy feet above, the hoist-men don't waste breath coming down the ladders. They strike the main turnbuckle with their brass thimbles: *Tak-tak-tink-tak*. *Four tons bar-stock, two casks pitch, coming down the east drop.* 

The vibration hits my shoulder blade through my coat. Before the ring of it has even died in the thin air, my right hand—wearing its heavy brass thumb-cap—snaps out and strikes the iron casing of the nitrogen accumulator beside my knee, translating the hoist-men’s light, high-altitude tap into a heavy, booming chime that drops three hundred feet down the pipe-work to the smelter-bosses at the bottom of the chasm. 

All shift long, the gossip and business of the Gauge passes through my bones. 

*Tink-tink-thup* from the second tier: *The number-two rotary broom has thrown a belt; watch for drifting moth-wings near the open resistance coils.* 

A sharp, stuttering *rattle-tat* from the clinic-pod wedged under the west overhang: *Marrow-Sarah’s boy has taken his first breath through the violet sponge; his chest is wide; he will stay.* 

And sometimes, the slow, three-beat measure that makes every machinist in the four-hundred-foot vertical pause for the space of a single, shallow inhalation, touching their brass dogs to their foreheads: *Old Hollis has gone down the chute. The gasifiers are bright tonight.* 

We do not weep when the three-beat comes through the steel. Tears are a lowland luxury; if you weep at thirty-two thousand feet, the salt-water creeps under the pine-pitch seal of your hornblende cups, chafes the skin raw in the zero-humidity gale, and freezes your eyelashes to the glass. Instead, when a name is tapped out in the three-beat, I reach into my grease-horn, take a pinch of the finest, purest molybdenum powder, and rub it into the main pivot-pin of the Leaf-cradle. We grease the city so it doesn't groan as loud while it digests them. Soon enough—another ten years, maybe twelve, when my blood gets so thick with red corpuscles that my heart sounds like a wooden mallet hitting wet leather—my own fat will be rendered down in the lower retorts, rising as warm coal-gas through the flues to keep the frost off the boy who takes my wedge on the Number Four.

A sudden, sharp stillness drops over the chasm.

It doesn't come from above; it comes from my back molars. A tiny, sour taste of copper, and a vibration so low it isn't a sound at all, just a sudden conviction that the diorite behind my neck has grown heavier. 

Two seconds later, the braided wool cable beside my ear jumps. Down in the dark, hanging like blind spiders over the siphon-shaft, the Night-Gauge has struck the chord. *R-r-r-ring-tink!*

I don't think. My body belongs to the Cleft’s geometry before my brain even names the tremor. 

My left hand snatches the copper feeler-strips clear of the steel jaws—leave them in, and the leaves will shear them into razor-darts. My right hand drops the maul into its leather bucket and flicks my secondary lanyard out; the brass dog bites onto the wool-sheathed cable with a dry *snick*. Gristle doesn't jump; he flattens himself into the hollow between my ribs and my elbow, all five paws locked into the heavy weave of my wool coat, his heat-pitted snout buried in my armpit.

Around us, the pale curtains of shale-moths suddenly break apart as ten thousand stokers leap from the conduits in a silent, pale blizzard of leathery limbs. The *shhh-tok* of the logic-valves cuts out as the master-governors trip. 

For three heartbeats, the Spindle-Cleft is dead quiet, save for the thin, stratospheric wind whistling through the slotted iron bulkhead seventy feet overhead, smelling of nothing at all.

Then the Aethelgard massif shrugs.

The diorite wall behind me doesn't shake; it simply *arrives* four inches closer than it was a moment ago. The nine-foot vanadium leaves of the Number Four compress with a sound like a dry pine tree splitting in the deep frost—a massive, groaned *WHUFF* of squeezed graphite and displaced air. 

The whole iron city drops away from under my hooked heel. 

For two seconds, I am weightless, hanging from my hips in the dark chimney while the counterweights the size of ore-cars plummet past in the center of the chasm, their rawhide buffers screaming softly against the greased rails. The orange resistance coils in the lower tiers flare violet as the brushes jump the commutators. A warm, musky gale of stoker-scent, ozone, and pulverized mica rushes up from the smelters, ballooning my coat and making the damp fungus in my lung-sponge swell with heat.

Then the great hydraulics at the bottom of the Cleft catch the load. The city rebounds—once, twice, settling three inches lower than it stood at noon. 

My heel finds the stamped steel rung on the upswing. Gristle uncoils from my ribs, shakes a dusting of dead moth-scales from his snout, and goes back to sniffing the nitrogen union. 

I don't look down into the dark, and I don't look up at the thin purple slit of the sky. I spit through my copper cage, lay my blackened, bare thumb back across the seam of the vanadium leaves to feel the new pitch of the iron, and reach for a number-five shim.
