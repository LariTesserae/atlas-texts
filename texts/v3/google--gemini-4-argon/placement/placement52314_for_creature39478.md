At first, it is easiest to imagine being the water. It is the only thing here that gets to leave—sweating out of the cold air onto a clay thumb-ridge sixty feet up, hanging until the weight is too much, and then taking that single, plummeting drop through the tallow-colored gloom to strike the dish and race toward the throat in the floor. 

Wait, though. The water only passes through. It doesn’t keep the time of the room. And the throat at the bottom—that crude, stone-plugged grate—is not an exit so much as a sieve. 

If you sink below the wet *thwack* of the oil-backs, down where the black silt and the grey tendons of torn flyers collect, the sound of the hall changes. The deafening, high-pitched cobbler-din of the crabs up on the walls hits the surface of the ankle-deep pool and blunts. Down inside the lip of the drain, where the water curls around the wedged stones before dropping into the blind dark beneath the foundation, the clattering becomes a low, bone-deep buzz, like a hive heard through a heavy timber door.

That is where I am. Wedged into the throat.

Not the stone plug itself, which is dead granite, foreign to the clay of the vault. I am the thing that has grown *around* the plug to keep it from washing away.

In a room where everything is either falling or clinging so it doesn't fall, someone has to catch what is dropped. The Grout-Wife tends the skin of the barge, creeping through the noise above to patch the leaks in the echo, but she cannot eat everything, and the hall sheds constantly. Every hour, the rills bring down the flour-fine grit of terracotta worn away by a million crab-tips. They bring the dissolved lime of the old mortar, the unchewed shards of carapace, the oily, iron-rich blood of the flyers, the sloughed cuticles, the heavy, unfertilized eggs that slipped from a mating ball on the northern curve. 

If the drain were clear, the hall would have bled itself thin centuries ago; its minerals would have washed out into whatever blind subterranean river runs beneath us, and the Grout-Wife would have run out of lime for her spit. 

I am the *sieve- lung*. Or the *ballast-root*. 

I have no shell, because nothing down here can fall on me, and I have no eyes, because even the tallow-smear of the ceiling slit dies before it reaches the water’s skin. To look at me—if you pried up the central stones—would be to see something like a massive, bruised oyster shucked of its plate and wedged tightly into the terracotta pipe, or a congested heart made of tough, soot-grey cartilage and fringes of black gill. 

My body is a series of leathery, concentric baffles that line the drain-shaft for six feet down. I do not move at all, except to clench and unclench, very slowly, against the rush of the water. 

The gurgling sound that fills the center of the hall—that wet, rhythmic clearing of a throat in the dark—is me. 

For twenty minutes at a time, I flare my upper flanges, sealing the space between the granite plug-stones. Above me, the central puddle deepens. It rises from ankle-depth to calf-depth, creeping up the shallow dish of the floor, drowning the lowest fringes of the ropey blind-moss. The oil-backs feel the slack water and grow sluggish, their heavy slapping slowing into lazy, slick coils. As the water stands still in my upper cup, the heavy things settle out of it. The black silt, the pulverized clay dust, the powdered shell, the rich, greasy strings of flyer-marrow—they sink into the warm, mucous pleats of my face. 

I sort them in the dark. The flesh and the blood go to my gut, digested without hurry to keep my cartilage dense and my grip on the brick pipe iron-tight. 

The minerals—the clay-flour, the lime, the calcium grit—I cannot digest. Instead, I pack them into a deep, muscular gizzard at my base, churning them with a clear, astringent bile until they form smooth, dense pellets the size of goose eggs. 

When the water above me grows too heavy, and the pressure on my upper baffles begins to ache, I open my throat. 

I let the clean, stripped water go. It roars down through my lower rings, dropping away into the deep earth with a long, sucking *gull-lull-lup* that makes the air in the hall dip by a fraction of a degree. The puddle shrinks back to the center. The oil-backs wake and begin to thrash again as they are stranded on the slick terracotta. 

And as the water finishes draining, I contract my gizzard and cough upward. 

A dozen grey, chalky eggs of compressed silt and lime-paste roll out of the grate, pushed up by my muscular tongue to sit in the shallow, bubbling lip of the drain. They look like river stones, except they are soft as wet soap. 

I do not know what the Grout-Wife is. I have never felt the air on the upper walls, nor the dry *tack* of a crab’s leg. All I know is the vibration of the floor. 

Once every few days, through the dense clay of the dish, I feel the heavy, vitrified *crunch* of her back-plates settling as she descends into the pool. The frantic *thwack-thwack* of the eels parts around her. The water grows suddenly heavy and thick with the scent of her curing mucilage. 

Then, a broad, calloused pad—heavy as packed river-clay, bearing a raised, scarred whorl—reaches down into the water at the edge of my grate. 

She does not rasp at my flesh; my skin is bitter with alum and copper, and we are old neighbors. Her club-tentacle rolls across my upper lip, gathering the chalk-eggs I have coughed up from the drain. She takes them into her cowl, chewing them back into mortar, restoring the hall’s lost dust to her belly so she can carry it back up the slope.

Sometimes, before she hauls her great weight back toward the roaring walls, her heavy pad rests for a moment against the granite stone of my grate. She presses down once—a slow, hydraulic lean that pushes the stone an eighth of an inch deeper into my cartilage, seating it tight against the current—before she releases, and I open my baffles to swallow the next cold drop from the roof.
