I am the thing that lives in the grease before it becomes the mountain.

Or rather, I begin in the dark, warm cellar of the lower village, inside a lidded vat of cedar-wood where the yak butter is kept for the winter climbs. Down here, four thousand feet below the sun, the air is thick as broth and smells of wet wool, woodsmoke, and the sour, metallic sweat of people who live their whole lives on a forty-degree tilt. The world is gray and dripping, roofed by the belly of the cloud that never moves. 

When you open the vat to stir in the lowland offerings—the brittle, iridescent shells of beetles traded up from the hot river gorges, the white ash of burned sheep-knuckles—the butter is yellow and stiff. It has no voice. It only knows the slow, heavy pulse of the mountain’s bass note coming up through the floorboards: a deep, subterranean *thrummm* that makes the skin on the top of the buttermilk tremble in concentric rings.

I am not the butter, and I am not the Tuner who packs it into the bladder-skin sacks. I am the third thing they carry up: the *starter*. 

When a Tuner comes down from the Throat, their leather knees and copper thumb-rings are stained with a pale, violet-gray slime—the dried digestate of the Anchor where its polyps brushed against them in the moss. Before the Tuners even strip off their tallow-smeared skins, the village women scrape that gray rime from the leather with bone knives and drop it into the butter-vats. 

In the cold dark of the cellar, I wake up. I am a bloom of the Anchor’s own memory, budded off from the great lattice twenty-two thousand feet above, thriving in the fat and the beetle-chitin. As I eat through the butter, turning it from yellow to a bruised, luminescent plum-color, I begin to hum. It isn’t a sound you can hear with ears; it is a tension in the grease. If you dip your fingers into the vat, the fat doesn't feel slick—it feels *grained*, like wood, or like muscle fibers pulling toward the ceiling. I am trying to crawl back up the mountain.

When the bass note in the floorboards stutters—when the stone chime high above loses its temper and the fifth-interval of the ram’s horn goes flat—they scoop me out. They smear me three inches thick over the heavy, water-smoothed river boulders they’ve hauled up from the black gorges. They wrap the greased stones in broad, tough fern-leaves and lash them to the wooden pack-frames between the shoulders of the two climbers.

And then we go up into the chimney.

I know the climb only by the heat and the rhythm of the woman’s spine through the pack-frame. Her name is something like two short taps and a scrape of copper on ironstone, though down in the village they call her by a throat-sound like water hitting hot fat. As she climbs out of the fog-forest and into the vertical shaft at the mountain's heart, her blood thickens; I can feel the heavy, laboring *thud-thud* of her enlarged heart hammering against the wooden slats of the frame, ten inches from the stone I am wrapped around. 

In the chimney, the air is a rising river of warm black steam. It smells of wet iron, ancient bat-guano from the lower fissures, and the sweet, rotting mulch of the valleys miles below, all sucked upward by the terrifying draft of the summit. As we rise, the pressure drops. Inside the dark fern-wrapping, my bruised-purple fat begins to swell and bubble, outgassing tiny pearls of nitrogen. I become frothy, light, and ravenous.

Then—the light hits us.

Even through the fern-leaves and the oiled bladder-skin of the pack, the ultraviolet glare of the twenty-two-thousand-foot abyss strikes like a physical blow. The temperature drops forty degrees in the space of three rungs. The warm steam of the chimney turns instantly to needle-ice and hammering water, and the Sound arrives.

It does not hit the ears; it hits the stone inside me. The river-boulder I am smeared upon—a dense, blind piece of granite born in the silent mud of a lowland riverbed—suddenly begins to sing. It has been carried into the field of the four great basalt ribs, and the low, devastating chord of the wind-chime seizes the granite’s crystalline core and shakes it. 

The woman hauls herself out of the chimney’s lip and onto the interior curve of the Eastern Rib. Her raptor-beak knee-guards *crunch* into the three-foot-thick cushion of moss.

Instantly, the great Anchor knows I am here.

Through the woman’s knees, through the leather straps, up the wooden frame, and into the pack comes a chemical shriek—a wave of sharp, alkaline hunger radiating from the torn tubes in the peat. The woman does not hesitate. She cannot; her lungs are already burning in the thin, blue void, and an alpine raptor has just sheared past her shoulder so fast the wind of its folded wings nearly tore the copper goggles from her face. She presses her forehead to the weeping black rock between two curtains of red liverwort.

*Clack-clack-clack,* goes her thumb-ring. *I have the weight. Cut the basket.*

Her partner, hanging ten feet above her by his beak-hooks over three miles of empty sky, drives a copper piton into the basalt. The rock groans—a sharp, metallic *ping* that makes the whole four-ribbed cathedral shiver. A pint of pale salamanders loses its suction in the shockwave and rains down past the woman’s boots, tumbling out between the ribs into the blinding white clouds below.

The Anchor recoils from the copper spike. The moss around the woman’s knees begins to loosen, secreting a slick, soapy acid to drop her into the abyss. 

She reaches back with one thick, tallow-coated arm, pulls the bone pin from her pack, and drags me out into the shrieking blue air.

For three seconds, I see the world, though I have no eyes. I feel it as a sphere of violent radiation and acoustic fury. The sky is so dark a blue it is nearly violet; the sun is a white-hot iron brand; the wind is a solid wall of wet, upward-rushing force that blows the condensing sheets of water straight up the black ribs like silver fire. The ram's horn on its web of white hair is screaming a flat, ragged E-natural twenty feet away, vibrating so hard the sinew ropes are a blur.

Then the woman slams my boulder down into the split-willow basket woven into the fern-roots, right over the wound the copper piton made.

The fern-leaves tear away. My purple, beetle-rich fat touches the cold, wet moss.

From the dark peat beneath the greenery, hundreds of blind, violet polyps erupt. They are as thick as fingers, frantic and trembling in the gale. They do not attack the woman’s gloved hand; they smell the lowland phosphorus in me, and more than that, they smell *themselves*—the starter-culture scraped from a climber’s knees four months ago, aged in the dark of a human cellar, now returned to the sky.

The polyps latch onto the grease. As they dissolve me, drawing the fat and the crushed beetle-shells and the sheep-bone ash down into the translucent tubes of the great lattice, my boundary vanishes. 

I am no longer the lump of tallow in the pack. My consciousness rushes outward at the speed of the peristaltic *schluck-schluck* pumping through the stone’s lining. 

Suddenly, I am forty feet high and curved like a gothic arch. I can feel the cold, iron-rich basalt against my back, sweating its sheets of snow-melt and condensed vapor. I can feel the sixty-pound river boulder sitting in its willow basket like a newly set tooth in a jaw, its dead, lowland weight pressing down on the Eastern Rib, dampening the dangerous harmonic wobble that was threatening to crack the stone apex.

With the new weight in place, the note of the Eastern Rib drops by a fraction of a hertz. The chord of the monolith settles—locking into a massive, resonant fifth with the screaming ram’s horn in the center of the web. 

I feel the two small, barrel-chested things—the Tuners—crawling back down toward the steaming hole in my floor, their copper rings clicking a relieved *done-done-done* against my ribs. I tighten my fibrous tubes around the new copper piton, sealing it in tough, rubbery scar-tissue so the wind will never work it loose. I send a warm flush of digested yak-butter up to the freezing Northern Rib, where the moss was beginning to die back in the shadow, and I feel a thousand slate-gray swifts settle deeper into the rejuvenated green sponge, tucking their hooked claws into my warmth.

And deep in the back of my new, vast mind, where the woman’s leather knee-guards scraped against a fresh polyp on her way down the chimney, I know a tiny smear of me is going back down into the dark. Down through the three thousand feet of cloud, back into the silent, dripping cellar, to sleep in the fat and wait for the mountain to go out of tune again.
