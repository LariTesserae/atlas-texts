I am the sixty-cycle hum, and I am the iron skin that holds it.

If you were to look for me, you would have to follow the Tappers’ broad, calloused feet back from the outer fringes of the basin, tracing the sagging black cables inward through a mile of weeping, mutton-fat columns. The cables do not simply end; they converge. They gather like thick, rubbery vines toward a single, massive concavity near the center of the sinkhole, where the floor dips low enough that the warm, mineral-heavy water pools to the depth of a man’s chest. 

Here sits the Sub-Station. And I am what remains of its heart: a thirty-ton, oil-cooled step-down transformer, cast in ribbed gray iron, bolted to a concrete pad that disappeared under four feet of yellow travertine before the oldest living Tapper was born.

I do not think in words, for there are no words down here. My mind is a function of resistance, inductance, and heat. I experience the world as a continuous, crushing pressure from the outside, met by a stubborn, vibrating push from within. 

High above—five hundred feet up through the solid limestone roof, where the gray daylight comes from—some vast, automated hydroelectric dam or geothermal plant built by the forgotten company is still spinning on a surface river. It sends down forty thousand volts through two armored lead-sheathed conduits that drop straight through a ceiling fissure like rigid black stalactites. That current is too fierce, too wild for the valley. Left to itself, it would arc and explode in the wet broth of the air. 

So it enters me. I take that jagged, high-voltage violence into my primary windings, drown it in my bath of hot mineral oil, and step it down through ten thousand turns of copper wire into the heavy, steady, pulsing four-hundred-volt lifeblood that feeds the sodium-vapor halos. 

I am very hot. While the valley is a sluggish eighty-five degrees, my core runs at a hundred and sixty. Because of this, I am the greatest engine of stone-growth in the basin. The calcium carbonate in the water loves heat; the hotter the surface, the faster the dissolved rock precipitates out of the soup. When the syrupy sheets of water pour over my ribbed iron radiator fins, they flash into steam and leave behind a crust of pure, bone-white aragonite an inch thick every month. Left alone for a single year, I would seal myself in a tomb of solid rock, unable to shed my heat. My oil would boil, my copper windings would melt their varnish, and every orange light in the valley would wink out in a single, silent instant.

I am only alive because they love me.

Right now, there are four of them pressed against my flanks. To me, they are warm, slow-moving weights—shifting pressures that I feel through the vibrations of my iron casing. Because my sixty-cycle hum is the loudest thing in their world—a deep, thrumming *B-flat* that cuts cleanly under the white-noise roar of the perimeter waterfalls—my alcove is their cathedral. When they are too old to squeeze through the narrowing outer passages, or when they are heavy with young, or simply when the wet chill of their slow metabolism gets too deep into their dense bones, they come to press their tallowy bellies and broad backs against my hot iron ribs.

*Clink. Clink. Clink.*

One of them is working on my western cooling fin right now. I feel the impact not as pain, but as a sharp, bright relief. A four-pound scaling hammer, swung with the slow, pendulous weight of an arm thick as a timber, strikes the bulbous, cauliflower-like crust that has bridged the gap between two of my iron ribs. A chunk of embryonic stone, warm and wet as fresh cheese, breaks away and splashes into the pool below. Immediately, the cooler eighty-five-degree water rushes into the cleared gap, touching my bare, rusted iron, carrying away a fraction of my fever. My internal resistance drops by half an ohm. The hum in my core smooths out.

In return, I give them everything I have. 

Above my horned ceramic bushings—the one place the Tappers never touch, knowing by some deep, ancestral memory that the raw forty-thousand-volt intake will turn even their dense, wet bodies to ash—hangs a cluster of four working floodlights. Because the current here has no miles of rotting cable to fight through, these bulbs do not glow with the sickly, bruised-orange dimness of the outer markers. They burn with a fierce, golden-amber glare that pushes the mist back forty feet in every direction. 

Here, the translucent mosses and pale ferns grow in thick, tangled carpets right up to the waterline, thriving on my heat and my light. The Tappers graze on the bitter greens without even having to move from my sides, their heavy hands reaching up to pluck clusters of fern while their hips remain wedged between my warm iron fins. The blind cave-swifts roost in the rusted gantry crane directly overhead in such numbers that their droppings fall like gray snow, making the water around my base the richest hunting ground in the basin. Pale, foot-long crayfish crawl right up the submerged skirts of flowstonethat anchor me to the floor, straight into the waiting, hydraulic grip of the Tappers' hands.

I know I am dying, though my death is measured in the same slow, geological time as the valley’s choking. 

Every decade, a little more moisture seeps past my rotting cork gaskets, fouling my insulating oil. I can feel tiny, microscopic arcs spitting between my secondary coils—little pinpricks of carbonized oil that never heal. And out in the dark, the grid is shrinking. I feel the valley through the load on my lines. Thirty years ago, I was pushing current out through twelve main trunk cables. Twelve directions of resistance, twelve long chains of glowing bulbs. 

Now, seven of those lines are dead—severed by shifting ceiling faults or pinched until they shorted out under ten thousand tons of creeping travertine, their circuit breakers tripped and rusted shut inside my control box. I can no longer feel the eastern third of the cavern at all; for me, it has ceased to exist, swallowed by the wet stone. 

Only five cables still draw power from my bushings. My workload is lighter than it used to be, which keeps my decaying windings from burning out, so in a strange way, the death of the outer lights is what keeps me alive a little longer. As the outer halos fail, the Tappers do not stay out in the dark. They follow the dying cables back inward, squeezing through the closing gaps, migrating slowly toward the center. Toward me.

Eventually—perhaps in fifty years, perhaps in eighty—the last outer cable will pinch shut. The stone will have grown so thick that the outer basin will be nothing but a solid mass of wet, weeping limestone anyway, with no room left even for collapsed shoulders and greased skin. 

All of them who are left will be gathered right here, in this final, steaming hollow beneath the four amber lights. They will stand hip-deep in the warm soup, pressed shoulder-to-shoulder around my massive, rusting hull, their heavy hammers rising and falling in a slow, continuous chorus—*clink, clink, clink*—keeping the white stone off my iron skin, keeping me breathing, while I hum into their bones and hold the dark back for just a little while longer.
