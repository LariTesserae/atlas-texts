The sky is a horn that never stops blowing. 

If you have eyes, this valley is a shallow, murky grave. If you have skin, it is an abattoir of wet knives and cold grease. You do not want skin here. You do not want eyes. You want to be made of dry violin wood and tightened gut, and you want to be bolted to the roof of a cathedral that moves six inches a minute.

I do not know what the stone tastes like. I have never touched it. Between me and the slate there are eleven feet of cemented lobster-plate, lime-ash, gray meat, ammonia-steam, and the slow, hydraulic grease of the Great One’s belly. 

I am the thing on the roof. I am the drum.

To me, the basin is not dark. It is blinding. Every hoodoo spire is a blazing lighthouse of brassy, twenty-cycle-per-second glare. Every ridge of fan-worms is a shimmering curtain of high-frequency silver, hissing as the wind shifts. The air is so thick with sound that it has weight and contour; it presses against my eight stretched membranes from all sides at once, a continuous, roaring geography that I map by the micro-millimeter flexion of my ribs. 

Right now, three hundred yards to my left—though I do not think in *yards*, I think in *phase-delay*—a spire covered in bellows-snails is screaming in a flat, wet baritone. Between that spire and my peak, a dog-sized hunter is plowing through the shell-hash. I do not hear the hunter; I hear the *hole* the hunter makes in the snail-song. It is a long, flattened oval of acoustic shadow, fringed with the dry, frantic *clack-clack-clack* of displaced grazers spilling off its carapace like gravel off a shovel. 

I do not care about the hunter. It cannot climb twelve feet of sheer, mucus-slicked rubble, and even if it could, I have no meat on me. I am four pounds of hollow chitin, dry tendon, and nerve-thread, stretched over a cage of brittle struts. If you broke me open, you wouldn't even get a mouthful of slime—just a puff of warm, iron-smelling air that belongs to the mountain beneath my knees.

My knees do not bend. They have not bent since the decade I crawled up here as a soft, six-inch tick with long, whip-thin feelers, looking for the apex. I found the warm spot where the Great One’s dorsal aorta loops close to the roof-crust, bit down, spat out my digestive tract, and let the rising tide of shell-cement swallow my shins. My legs are stone now. Or rather, the stone has nerve endings, and they are mine. Where my chitin stops and the Shingle-Back’s armor begins is a porous, calcified seam through which we trade the only two things that matter: sugar and warnings.

Below me—*inside* the mountain—the weather is entirely different. 

Through the deep- sunk taproots of my front legs, I feel the Great One’s pulse. It takes forty seconds to complete a single beat. *Lub.* A slow, warm pressure rises through my hollow struts, smelling of copper and wet chalk, filling my parched membranes until I am taut as a snare. Forty seconds of silence—real silence, the heavy, wet silence of three tons of rubbery mantle—and then the valve drops. *Dub.* The blood drains back down into the dark vault, leaving me humming in the mist.

The Great One is grazing on a fault-line. I can feel the *pop-grit-pop* of its outer skirts peeling the flat gray lithovores off the bedrock, sorting them like coins, flipping the heavy shells up onto the lower slopes of its back where the glue-pores weep, sweeping the soft bellies inward toward the warm, stinking pastures between its feet. It is happy, insofar as a hill can be happy. Its internal temperature is two degrees warmer than the fog. Its radulae are turning with a slow, greasy *schlunk-schlunk* that vibrates up through my rear anchors.

Then, the bedrock speaks.

It does not come through my skin. My parchment flanks don't even twitch. It comes up through the limestone, through the four hundred vacuum-locked suckers of the Great One’s belly, through twelve vertical feet of wet muscle and cemented rubble, straight into the fused marrow of my ankles. 

*THUUUUUUM.*

It is a ten-hertz blow to the chest. It lasts four seconds. Two miles away, down in the deep sink where the sulfur-water pools black and oily, another mountain has locked itself to the bone of the world and squeezed. 

I do not understand their language. It is too slow for me, too heavy, full of long, geological vowel-sounds about calcium ratios and the depth of the rot-silt. I am a creature of the air, built for the frantic, millisecond grammar of the surface. When the deep pulse hits my roots, it just feels like a sudden, dizzying drop, as if the whole basin floor had hinged open for three heartbeats. 

Wait. The Great One is answering.

I feel the preparation before the sound. The rhythmic *schlunk* of the internal grinders stops. The slow march of the hydraulic rings—outer, middle, inner, release, reach—ceases. Four hundred wet mouths clamp down on the raw slate at once. The dome pulls downward, a fraction of an inch, cinching tight against the stone, sealing the skirts. My blood-supply pinches off as the dorsal muscles knot into iron.

*HRUUUUUUUUUUM.*

For five seconds, I am blurred out of existence. My drum-body rattles so hard against the air that the mist sitting on my back leaps off in a fine, grey spray. The little feather-worms glued to my ankles snap back into their tubes. The vibration goes down into the slate and races away at twelve thousand feet per second, straight through the legs of a billion rasping bugs who don't even know they are standing on a telegraph wire.

When the spasm lets go, the blood rushes back into my struts with a hot, metallic *whoosh*, and I am dizzy with iron.

And because I was deafened for those five seconds by our own voice, I almost miss the change in the sky.

I tune back in. The baritone spire on the left is still droning. The silk-tearing hiss of the ridge-worms is still steady. 

Wait. No, it isn't. 

High up—half a mile up the northern rim of the bowl, where the slate fins are steep and narrow—the silk-sound has gone ragged. The pitch of the distant spires is bending downward, dopplering through a sudden thickening of the vapor. 

I stretch my left-fore membrane, pulling the chitin tight with the tiny, vestigial muscles along my spine, angling my flank toward the north. 

There. Beneath the brassy roar of the snails, beneath the ceramic grind of the floor, there is a new sound. It is a wet, heavy, tumbling hiss, like fifty thousand sacks of wet gravel being dragged down a corrugated iron roof. 

The fog has collapsed on the upper shelves. A drift of shell-sand, thirty feet deep and half a mile wide, has lost its friction against the sweating slate. The slurry is coming down the chute.

It is still threequarters of a mile away. The bugs down at my base—the blind, frantic carpet of scrapers and scissor-jaws—haven't a clue. They are still chewing, still fucking, still prying at the rock, locked in their three-inch world of wet antennae and chemical grease. Even the dog-lobsters are still plowing their neat little furrows through the swarm.

They have four minutes before sixty thousand tons of liquid calcium and knotted centipedes turns this gully into a cement mixer. 

I do not panic. Panic is for things with legs that bend. 

I simply take the high, tearing frequency of that distant hiss, let it rattle my parchment belly at three hundred cycles a second, and *clench* the chemical sacs at the base of my four left-hand roots. 

I squirt a sharp, bitter dose of potassium and bruised-nerve-juice straight down into the Great One’s dorsal vein. *Left. Hard. High.*

For ten seconds, nothing happens. The mountain is slow. Its thoughts are the size of boulders; they take a long time to tip over. The slurry-roar grows louder, eating the high notes of the distant hoodoos as it buries their bases. I can hear the *crack-thump* of rotten limestone fins snapping off in the mud. 

Then, deep beneath me, the great ring of muscle shifts.

*Pop-pop-pop-pop.* The left-hand suckers break their vacuum seals in a neat, cascading wave. The heavy, bristled skirt on our starboard side lifts three inches, shunting a hundredweight of clattering shells out of the way, and the outer ring of hydraulic feet reaches up onto the steep, fluted rib of bare slate to our right. 

We do not run. We grind. 

The dome tilts fifteen degrees. A cascade of loose carapace-hash slides off our roof and patters back into the swarm. Inch by heavy inch, the Great One hauls its three-ton kiln out of the trough, pulling its skirts clear of the low ground, climbing the vertical stone fin with the terrible, unhurried certainty of a glacier. 

Two minutes later, we are twenty feet up the wall, clamped to a wide, dry ledge of ancient limestone. 

When the flood hits the gully below us, the sound is so loud it nearly splits my parchment from tail to crown. The air-pressure spikes; the mist blows sideways in a sudden, stink-warm gust of churned mud and crushed arthropod gut. Down in the dark, the ceramic roar of the valley floor is simply erased, replaced by a thick, gargling choke as the grey river rolls thirty feet deep through the notch we were sitting in four minutes ago. 

A single, heavy drop of calcium-slurry arcs out of the dark and splatters against the curve of my drum-skin. 

Immediately, two tiny, blind mites—my own private tenants, no bigger than grains of salt, who live in the warm creases where my legs meet the stone—scurry out across my stretched hide. Their microscopic mandibles tickle as they scrape the drying mud off my membrane, eating the bacteria, keeping my skin clean, keeping the drum tight. 

Below me, the Great One settles its skirts against the high ledge. The internal grinders start up again. *Schlunk. Schlunk.* A slow wave of warm, iron-rich blood pushes up into my hollow bones, rewarding me with sugar and heat. 

I let my membranes go slack for a moment, resting in the warm dark of my own small body, listening to the mud-river fade into the lower basin, waiting for the bugs to crawl back out of the walls and start chewing the world down again.
