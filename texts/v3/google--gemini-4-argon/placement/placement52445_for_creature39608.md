There is a blind spot beneath the three stone steps that lead up to the twenty-foot catwalk. 

Because the steps go both down and up at the same time, the space directly underneath them is folded over on itself like a piece of stiff linen creases-in. It is a wedge of dry, dusty shade that ought to be three feet high, though if you reach an arm inside, you can stretch your fingers for six yards before touching the back wall, and when you do, the brick you touch is warm from the parchment ceiling directly above the courtyard's center.

That is where I stay, mostly, though I am not all in one place any more than the Clerk is. 

I am the tally that lost its wall.

Before the courtyard finished closing itself off—when the masonry was still settling into its loop—there were calculations scratched into the soft, unbaked clay of the lower courses and written in charcoal on the raw cedar beams. Most of them were absorbed as the geometry hardened, becoming the rule that feeds the ferns or the angle that drops the quill-birds thirty feet in two inches. Wait long enough in this heat and any measurement becomes a plant or a beast. 

But a few fractions were left over. A remainder of seven-eighths of an inch that couldn't be reconciled between the north-west corner and the well-curb; the weight of three hundred bricks that were counted twice on the manifest and therefore had to exist twice in the same place without making the wall any thicker; the drift of a plumb-line when someone breathed on it four hundred years ago.

They pooled in the crease beneath the steps, along with the shed skins of the courtyard's small mistakes: flakes of dried beeswax, the snapped-off points of carpenter’s pencils, curled shavings of yellow pine, and the pale, papery husks of leaves that browned when a floor tile shifted. 

I am shaped vaguely like an oversized greyhound or a long-legged fox, though my ribs are made of bent willow switches and dry leather shims, and my pelt is a shingle-work of slate-pencil rubbings, dried sage leaves, and stiff, frayed strips of emery cloth. I have no eyes—eyes are for things that need to judge distance by looking, and looking is the least reliable way to cross this room. Instead, I have two brass dividers set into the bridge of my snout, their sharp points angled slightly outward. 

When I walk through the chest-high thickets of felt-briar, I do not part the leaves. I measure the gaps between them with the points of my snout, and where the gap matches the width of my shoulders, I am already through it. 

My job—though no one gave it to me—is to eat the slack.

Even with the Clerk tapping the tiles back toward true north, a space this crowded generates excess length. Two walls stare at each other through a break in the fig-vines for three weeks without blinking, and the air between them stretches by a quarter of an inch out of sheer fatigue. A wooden lizard crosses a mortar joint too slowly, and the joint widens by the thickness of a thumbnail to let its belly pass, threatening to starve the ivy rooted an inch higher.

I smell the looseness before the plants feel it. It smells like cold tallow and unstruck flint—a sudden, sour drop in the room’s dry fever.

When I find a joint that has grown too wide, or a corner that has gone lazy and opened to ninety-one degrees, I press my narrow chest against the brickwork and breathe in. I do not have bellows like the hares; my ribs are strung with dried gut that tightens when the air is slack. I lick the mortar seam with a tongue made of rough-grained sandstone. *Scritch. Scritch.* 

I do not scrape away the brick. I scrape away the extra *between*. 

The quarter-inch of slack comes off on my tongue like dry white salt. As I swallow it, the bricks draw back together with a low, grinding sigh, the ninety-one-degree corner snaps back to ninety, and the emerald ferns above my head shudder, their stiff leaves rustling like a deck of cards being squared on a table. 

The extra length has to go somewhere, of course. Nothing leaves the courtyard. So it goes into me.

On weeks when the masonry is restless and the sightlines are badly tangled, I grow very long. My willow-switch spine adds another vertebra; my hind legs remain under the stone steps in the cool dark while my forepaws are thirty paces away by the dry cistern, and my narrow head is resting on the edge of the Clerk’s workbench, watching him mend a hollow brick. At times I am twelve feet from nose to tail-tip, winding through the A-frames like a draft of warm air, my emery-cloth hide leaving faint, silver scratches on the chalky leaves as I pass. 

When the courtyard is tight and well-behaved, I shrink back down until I am no larger than a terrier, curled into a tight, dry knot of wood-shavings and brass at the Clerk’s feet.

The Clerk knows what I am. He does not look at me with his parchment-lantern head—if he measured me too closely with his eyes, I might lock into a single size and become useless—but when I am stretched thin and heavy with swallowed fractions, he reaches down without pausing in his work. 

His green-stained wooden fingers find the back of my neck. He takes his flat steel mason’s square and lays the cold iron blade along my spine, from between my shoulder blades down to my hip. 

The iron is true. It has no slack in it at all. 

At the touch of the square, the swallowed inches inside my ribs discharge all at once. There is a dry, snapping sound, like a carpenter’s chalk-line being plucked against a board, and a small cloud of powdered lime and charcoal dust puffs out from between my willow ribs. Immediately, I am short again—four-legged and compact in a single spot beside his boot—and the dust settles onto the floor tiles, feeding the hemp-shrubs for six feet around.

In return, I keep the hares out of his boots while they are growing.

The straw-and-wax hares love the corner where the three pairs of leather boots are planted. The ninety-degree angle there is so fierce and well-tended that the air practically hums with precision, and the hares gather in great, overlapping drifts—six or seven of them occupying the same cubic foot of shade, their bellows-ribs puffing lime-dust over the sprouting canvas hems. If too many of them crowd into the same spot, their combined weight confuses the floor tiles beneath the boots, and the growing ash-wood knuckles begin to twist out of true, growing six fingers instead of five, or curling into wooden spirals that could never hold a mallet.

So when I am small, I sleep propped against the middle pair of boots, my chin resting on the instep of the left one. 

The leather is warm and smells of tallow and the dry chalk packed inside it. Every few hours, deep within the boot, there is a tiny, fibrous *pop* as a root hair of unbleached linen threads itself through a stitch-hole in the welt. Above my head, the ash-branch creaks—a sound so slow it takes twenty minutes to finish—as the pale wood splits another millimeter toward becoming a thumb.

When a hare drifts too close, its waxen ear passing through my front leg like a warm candle-shadow, I do not bark. I simply tap the brass points of my snout against the floor tile. *Tick-tick.* 

It is the exact sound of a mason’s chisel testing a stone for a flaw. The hare’s ears flatten; its bellows give a quick, startled *puff*, and it hops backward through the partition wall, emerging forty feet away by the well-curb.

Then the shade under the arch is quiet again. Far across the thicket, hidden behind six walls of green felt-leaf and a wooden scaffold that supports only itself, the copper wire beneath the Clerk’s coat slips a fraction of an inch against its peg. 

*Plink.*

The note travels through the brick, not the air. I feel it in the soles of my paws and the points of my brass nose—a tiny, straight line of sound that pins the corners of the room in place for another afternoon. I shift my weight, letting my hind legs slip through the mortar joint behind me so they can stretch out in the warm amber light up on the cedar trusses, while my head stays here in the dark, listening to the new hands grow.
