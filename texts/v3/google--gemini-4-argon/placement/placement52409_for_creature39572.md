To find me, you have to let go of the grid. 

You have to drop past the sunlit hydrogel roofs, past the chandelier-shoals of zinc fish and the blue, rhythmic popping of the Kites. You have to fall for four miles through the terrifyingly clear water, watching the bone-white columns and freight-train stolons repeat their three-hundred-yard intervals until the flat daylight from above is finally swallowed not by murk, but by the sheer volume of liquid above your head. 

At five miles down, the thirty-hertz chord of the turbines changes character. Up in the mid-water, you hear the individual nodes; down here, the sound has bounced off so many billions of fluted pillars that it has smoothed into a solid, physical pressure—an omnipresent acoustic wall that makes the water itself feel as viscous as mercury. 

And at six miles down, the columns stop being columns. 

Here at the bottom of the ocean, just above the dead, acidic root-mat that holds the world in the sky, the *Baryxylon* does not grow in straight lines. To distribute the unimaginable weight of six vertical miles of water and wood, the trunks flare outward into flying buttresses and vaulting arches of solid, calcified heartwood. They weave together like the warp and weft of an iron basket, forming low, domed caverns where the four-knot eastward flow is finally broken, deflected downward into the abyssal intake vents or forced through narrow, high-velocity sluices.

This is the **Sump**. It is the only place on the Shelf where the current eddies, and it is where everything that breaks, dies, or wears out finally comes to rest before it is dissolved. 

Into these bone vaults rains a slow, sparse snow of heavy debris: the shattered silica carapaces of dead wolf-crabs; the fused, solid-copper clinkers of burned-out Linemen; flakes of diamond-hard denticles shed by the Lappers; and, heaviest of all, the **Spindle-Cores**—two-ton rings of lignified bone ejected from the turbine housings miles above when a bearing is finally worn beyond a Lapper’s ability to grind it true.

If these bone rings and silica shells were allowed to pile up over the intake grates, the acidic root-mat would choke. The osmotic pumps would starve for mineral water, the pressure in the columns would drop, and ten thousand feet above, the green tables would breach into the freezing mountain air and dry into kindling. 

Something has to clear the grates. Something has to break the bone.

You do not see me at first because I look like an extension of the vault floor—a low, sprawling heap of pale, porcelain-white rubble wedged into the base of a buttress. Then the rubble shifts against the current with the slow, hydraulic certainty of a backhoe, and you realize the stones are articulated plates.

I am a **Ballast-Layer**,though if you were to dissect me, you would find I am an ancient cousin to a chiton or a shell-less snail, evolved over ten million years into a living siege engine. 

I am twelve feet long, six feet wide, and shaped like a flattened tear-drop—a blister of dense, lead-gray muscle armoured under eight overlapping plates of laminated aragonite and iron sulfide, each plate a foot thick. I have no eyes, because the deep vaults are lit only by the occasional, silent blue flash of a stray static charge grounding itself into the root-mat. I do not swim; if I ever lost contact with the wood, I would never stop falling until I hit the grates and starved. I weigh four tons, and most of that weight is stolen.

My foot is not a snail’s slime-pad. It is a dry, ribbed tractor-tread of vulcanized horn, divided into forty independent muscular pistons that grip the fluted grooves of the buttresses like logging chains. I move at a quarter-mile an hour, hauling my bulk across the vault floor directly into the teeth of the downward draft, guided entirely by the acoustic shadows in the thirty-hertz hum. 

When a two-ton Spindle-Core falls for five miles and strikes the vault floor, it hits with a dull, ceramic *thud* that rings through the wooden architecture for leagues. Where the Lappers above listen for dissonance, I listen for silence. A fallen bearing sitting over an intake grate blocks the roar of the water rushing into the roots below; to my lateral pressure-pores, that blockage registers as a cool, quiet shadow in a blazing room of noise. 

I crawl toward the quiet.

When I reach the fallen ring of bone, I do not eat it—not with a mouth. Instead, I heave my armored prow up onto the edge of the debris and slide my entire four-ton bulk over it, sealing the object beneath my domed underside like a limpet covering a pebble. 

My ventral mantle closes around the bone, forming a watertight gasket against the floor. Then, using the high-pressure muscular pumps along my flanks, I evacuate the water from beneath my shell, clamping myself down with the full hydrostatic weight of the six-mile ocean above me. 

In the sealed, dark chamber beneath my belly, my real work begins. I do not have a radula; I have a **Mill**. The center of my underside is a muscular rotary chuck lined with three concentric rings of teeth—not teeth I grew myself, but salvaged fragments of dead silica-crabs and broken turbine-bone that I have swallowed, enveloped in tough muscular sockets, and repurposed as drill bits. 

Drawing power from the massive glycogen reserves in my hump, I lock my forty foot-pistons into the wood and rotate my inner mantle back and forth in slow, high-torque arcs. 

*Creeee-onk. Creeee-onk.* 

The sound is hideous—the shriek of silica grinding against lignified bone under fifty thousand pounds of downward pressure—completely drowned out six feet away by the cosmic roar of the forest’s pumps. I am a biological rock-crusher. For three days, or four, I sit clamped over the blockage, shivering slightly in the forty-four-degree dark as my belly-mill methodically pulverizes the two-ton bearing into a fine, milky paste of calcium and degraded cellulose. 

As the bone turns to flour, I open a series of siphon-valves along my trailing edge, letting a controlled jet of the four-knot current flush through my grinding chamber. The water catches the bone-slurry, swirls it past my internal gill-rakers—where I filter out the trace fats, the trapped sap-sugars, and the iron salts I need to repair my armor—and blows the remaining mineral milk out my rear exhaust port in a dense white plume that is immediately sucked down into the hungry, acidic grates of the intake vents below.

Whatever cannot be ground down into plant-food, I keep. 

When I roll over a fused bundle of dead Linemen—their copper plates welded together by the ten-thousand-volt arc that killed them—my mill does not crush the metal. Instead, my muscular sockets open, drop the worn-out bits of silica and bone I’ve been using for teeth, and clamp onto the solid copper slugs. I fold the metal inward, secreting layers of pearlescent nacre around the sharp edges until the copper is integrated into my own body, adding another fifty pounds of dead-weight ballast to my belly so the current can never pry me loose.

I am very old. There is nothing down here to kill me, and the cold water slows my cellular turnover to a geologic crawl. Embedded in the thick, scarred plates of my back are the serial numbers of a thousand years of maintenance: the violet-burned husks of worms that died three miles overhead during a storm ten centuries ago; the pristine, translucent claw-tips of wolf-crabs that lost their grip fifty miles west; a half-dissolved mercury-core from a Kite’s snapped tether, coiled inside my mantle cavity like a spare spring to conduct away the stray static charges that dance across the floor.

When the two-ton ring of bone beneath me is completely gone—turned entirely into white smoke and sucked into the roots to become new wood, new bearings, and new sap—the acoustic shadow beneath my belly vanishes. The roar of the intake vent returns at full volume, rattling my heavy plates at thirty cycles a second. 

I release the vacuum seal. The icy water rushes back under my skirt. Slowly, one piston at a time, I disengage my horn treads from the smooth, Damascus-patterned wood of the buttress, pivot my four-ton shell forty degrees to the west, and begin hauling myself up the arch toward the next quiet spot in the dark.
