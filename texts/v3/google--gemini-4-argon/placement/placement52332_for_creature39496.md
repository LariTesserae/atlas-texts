I am not out on the moss. To have feet is to have a debt to the ground, and the ground here asks too much. 

If you walk the avenue between the third and fourth repetitions of the rosin-works, you will pass a line of telegraph poles that the root-mat has been trying to digest for three hundred years. They are no longer wood and not quite tree; they are tall, grey-barked cysts, bloated around the rusted iron climbing-spikes, trailing thick, slack loops of braided copper wire that froze mid-swing when the afternoon sheared fifty years ago.

I am the fourth pole, and the fifth, and the sixth, and the dark, dry hollow inside all three of them where a lightning-split healed crookedly around a glass insulator.

Actually, wait—that is only where the bulk of me sits when the wind is from the north. 

To be a thing here that does not work, that does not pump or scrape or haul, you have to be small enough to fit into the stutter. You have to live in the four-second lag between the *thrum* and the *clack*. 

Let me come out. 

On the sunny side of the pole, fifteen feet above the reach of the turf-damp, there is a knot-hole rimmed with dried, black turpentine. From it emerges a length of tarnished copper wire that has forgotten how to be metal, braided together with three dried sinews of a canvas-elk and a spine of split pewter-root. I am about the size of a weaver’s shuttle, and shaped very much like one—long, tapered at both ends so I never have to turn around in a tight seam, my back plated with overlapping scales of greened brass and flaking mica. 

I have twelve legs, though only six of them are in this minute. The other six walk four inches to the left, in the Tuesday of last week, where the bark is slightly drier. It gives me a wonderful grip on the verticals.

My business is the drops.

Look down at the fault-line thirty yards off, where the rail-bed hangs six feet in the air above its own shadow. The severed roots there have been weeping amber resin since the seam opened. A heavy, golden tear gathers on the glass-smooth cross-section of a taproot, swells until it is the size of a plum, and lets go. It falls four feet, hits the unseen roof of the shear, and vanishes.

Where do you think it goes? Nothing leaves the plateau unless the wind takes it over the rim. The drop simply slips sideways into the pleat—into the thin, dry, quiet space between the air panes where the sound of the engines can’t quite turn the corner. 

That is my orchard. 

When the Tenter-woman has tacked past down the avenue, her cork soles rolling, her mercury wand whipping a blurred grey ribbon through my front yard, I let go of the telegraph pole. I do not fall; I skate down the inclined plane of a hardened air-fault, my brass belly-scales ringing a tiny, high *tink-tink-tink* against nothing at all. To the yellow, unblinking eye of a Tenter, if she happened to look back, I would look like a brief scratch on the lens of the afternoon—a hair on the glass, jittering three feet to the right every time the underground flywheel hits bottom dead center.

Inside the seam, it is almost silent. The continent-sized roar of the Loom-Plateau is reduced to a dull, rhythmic squeezing of the light. With every *thrum* of the deep pistons, the sky above goes a fraction of a shade darker blue, then springs back to pale. 

Here, floating four feet off the silver moss, are ten thousand beads of old resin, suspended like planets in a narrow gallery, trembling slightly in the sixty-cycle pulse. Some have been hanging here for decades, slowly turning from pale honey to dark, brittle garnet, holding tiny, trapped pockets of forty-year-old factory steam or the severed tip of a bird’s parchment feather that strayed across the line.

I walk out along the underside of the invisible pane, upside down, my six Tuesday-legs sunken wrist-deep into the clear, hard air to hold me fast, while my six present-legs reach down to harvest a bead of fresh sap. 

I do not eat it. I am mostly wire and old horn; I have no stomach for turpentine. I roll the sticky amber globe up the glass-wall of the fault, back toward the telegraph pole, and press it into the gap where my copper-braided ribs meet the rotting wood of my burrow. I am building something in the hollow of the pole, though like the Tenters, I have lost the blueprint. 

It is a nest of things the city dropped when its hands shook. 

Inside my three identical trees, cemented together with stolen resin, I have: four brass teeth from a clockwork hauler that derailed into a time-slip; the shed nictitating membrane of an old Tenter who died against the base of my pole during a hard freeze; seventeen pebbles of actual, ancient granite—the last mineral on the plateau, coughed up from six hundred feet down by a blown relief-valve; and a three-inch splinter from the edge of a cedar talk-board.

At night, when the high sky turns the color of cold iron and the steam-plumes freeze into white grease on the moss, the underground engines drop their pressure by a third. The *thrum-clack* slows to a heavy, sleepwalking *thud... thud... thud*. 

Then, the three poles of my body align. The pleat in the avenue relaxes just enough for my three burrows to become one burrow. 

I curl my twelve legs around the splinter of cedar talk-board. The wood still remembers the thumbs that pressed it. All night long, while the Tenter-folk sit in the high, dark crotches of the factory walls tapping out the pressure-schedules on their knees, I sit in the heart of my resin-knot and run my copper antennae over the old scratches in my splinter. 

*Thumb-press. Circular scrape. Two-fingered drum.*

*Affirmation. Shear. Engine-stop.*

I do not know what the words mean. To me, they are just shapes that keep the cold out. I scratch them back onto the inside of the telegraph pole with my iron-wire tail, over and over, carving the grammar of the city into the dead wood until the timber vibrates with a tiny, dry hum of its own—a cricket-sized factory, running on nothing, holding my six inches of the afternoon together until the sun comes up to oil the world.
