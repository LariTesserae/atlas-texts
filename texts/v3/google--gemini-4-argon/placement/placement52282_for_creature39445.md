I am wedged on my side in the three-foot-high throat of the fourth chimney on the north rim, six yards back from the lip where the ninety-mile-an-hour air shears past the mouth. Here, the rock curves like a snail’s shell, folding the shriek of the orifice back on itself until the two sound-waves cancel out. The air does not move, though it pulses against my cheekbones like a thumb pressing hard into a bruise, sixty times a minute.

Before me, resting on a flat pillow of greasy felt, is a green-horn visor that belongs to the net-man from the lower ledge. A stone chip, blown off the west crest yesterday, struck him between the eyes; it did not break the bone, because the horn is three fingers thick at the bridge, but it left a star-fracture right across the center of his left vision-slit. If he wears it out into the glare today, the crack will catch the white light off the basin floor and split it into a blinding fan inside his eye-socket. He will miss his gaff-hold on the slick ice-apron and go east.

My knees are drawn up tight against my chest; my spine is pressed hard into the cold limestone behind me to steady my hands. Between my boots sits a tallow pan no wider than my palm, its three hair-wicks burning with tiny, flat blue flames that do not flicker in this dead-air pocket, though the liquid oil around them shivers in a permanent honeycomb pattern of fine rings. The smell of it—rendered slate-bug fat and singed braid—fills the tube so thickly I can taste the grease at the back of my throat.

I am the one who cuts the slits. You cannot do it by eye alone; in the yellow gloom of the tallow-lamp, your eyes lie to you about how straight a line is, and the rock vibrates too hard to hold a blade steady by wrist-strength. Instead, you have to use the vibration itself. 

I have a flake of black glass—traded up from the dark valleys five generations ago, precious as a thumb-bone—lashed with wet gut into the split end of a heavy rib. I do not push the glass. I rest the heel of my hand against the limestone wall of the tube, lock my wrist until the tendons feel like dried wood, and touch the point of the glass to the inside of the new horn blank. The mountain does the cutting. The sub-bass roar of the great vertical flue thirty feet below us runs up through the bedrock, into my funny-bone, down my ulna, and makes the black glass chatter against the horn at four hundred beats a second. All I have to do is lean my chest slowly forward, guiding the buzz of the stone along the chalk-line I drew with my thumbnail, while the fine grey horn-dust trickles down into my lap like dry snow.

My ears were plugged forty winters ago, when my skull was still soft enough to take the flattened leather binding-cap without aching, so I do not hear the chimney. I *weigh* it.

Through my left hip-bone, which is jammed tight into a dry cradle in the floor, I feel the big, hollow *oom-oom* of the main water-pillar half a mile west. That note is fat and wet; when the mist is heavy on the windward face, the water-film thickens on the stone and drops the pitch just enough to make my back teeth itch. Today my teeth are quiet. It is a dry blow. The troughs will be half-empty by mid-sun, and the women who scrape the water-bowls will have to use their horn spoons to gather the rime frost out of the shade-cracks instead.

Through my shoulder blades, pressed flat against the soot-slick ceiling, comes a much thinner, sharper buzz, like a tight gut-string being sawed with a dry stick. That is the Third Eastern Needle, two miles down-wind, where we wedged my mother’s brother three seasons back. He was a wide-chested man, heavy in the hip, and when we jammed him into the high flute with his knees tucked and his chin down, he choked off the upper whistling hole almost completely. Before him, that needle used to scream so high it made the water in your eyes turn blurry when you skated past its shadow; now it has a rough, burred edge to its vibration, a steady *tchk-tchk-tchk* underneath the big chord that I can feel right behind my breastbone whenever I lean back to rest my neck. It is a good, solid rattle. It means the wind hasn't shifted him an inch; he is wedged tight, drying nicely into stone-leather, holding our family’s claim on the eastern net-anchors just by keeping his fat shoulders in the draft.

A shadow blots out the blue-white bar of sunlight at the dog-leg turn of the tunnel. The air pressure in the pocket jumps—a soft *thump* against my eardrums, even through the silica wax—as a body plugs the crawl-way.

I don't look up; if I lift my chin while the rock-buzz is in my elbow, the obsidian will skitter sideways and ruin the bevel of the glare-slit. I wait until a heavy, tallow-greased knee-pad nudges my shin. Then I ease my shoulder off the ceiling, breaking the circuit of the vibration, and lift the blade away from the horn.

It is the girl from the second-tier sleeping crack—the one whose left thumb was taken off by a freezing gut-line last winter. She is still strapped into her outer carapace, the overlapping scales of cured glider-wing clicking faintly against the floor as her chest heaves. Her bellows-lungs are working hard, sucking at the thin air; her leather face-rind is cracked around the nostrils, white with dried salt from her breath, and her old horn visor is pushed up onto her forehead, showing the wide, wet, unblinking black eyes that look too big for her skull. Across her back, lashed to her harness-pegs, is a bundle of six slate-bugs, their legs tucked in, their shells still slick with west-wall mist.

She doesn't hum; her throat is too cold from the crawl across the basin. Instead, she reaches out with her four-fingered hand, hooks her thick, flat nails against my collarbone, and taps twice—*hard-soft, hard-soft*—right over my heart, where the hollow of my chest can catch the knock.

*The big gut-net at the Split-Tooth is singing wrong,* her fingers say. *A glider tore the bottom leash. The belly is flapping.*

I reach into the warm grease of the tallow pan with my thumb, rub it over the new slit in the horn visor to see if the edges are smooth, and then press my greasy thumb flat against the center of her forehead, between the red marks left by her visor-straps. I draw a short, hooked line down toward her nose.

*Wait until the shadow of the Tooth touches the third water-bowl,* I tell her skin. *The wind drops six knots in the lee when the sun hits the west cheek of the rock. If we go out while the stone is cold, the flap of the net will break your other wrist.*

She doesn't nod—nobody wastes neck-heat on gestures when a touch will do—but she shifts her weight off her elbows, curls her short legs under her armored skirt, and wedges her back into the narrow V of the limestone opposite me, right where my feet are tucked. She pulls the leather plug from her mouth-slit, takes a dried, silica-crusted leaf of wire-scrub from her belt-pouch, and wedges it between her molars to stop them from chattering against the rock-hum.

I lean my shoulder blades back against the greasy ceiling. The *tchk-tchk* of my uncle’s chimney comes back into my ribs, steady and dry, while the big sub-bass of the mountain’s throat comes up through my hip. I set the point of the black glass back into the groove of the horn, lock my wrist bone against the limestone wall, and let the ten-thousand-year-old roar finish the cut.
