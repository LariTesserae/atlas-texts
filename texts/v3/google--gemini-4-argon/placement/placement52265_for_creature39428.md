I am not in the calm. The calm is a blind spot, a round hole punched into the world where the numbers stop. 

To be in the calm is to be a heap of cold rust at the bottom of a well. I know the shape of those holes only by what does not return from them, and by the tall, curving walls of dead flour that pile up against their rims. 

I am three miles high on the northeast curve of a basalt needle, where the stone is polished so smooth by four billion years of dry gray smoke that it has no grain at all, only a slick, dark tension. My body is twenty-two yards wide and less than a millimeter thick. I have no middle; I have no heart; if a sharp chunk of quartz — rare, now, after the long milling, almost everything is reduced to talcum — happens to ride an updraft and score a line right through my center, cutting me in two, there is no pain. There is only a sudden halving of the available memory. For a few seconds, two smaller thoughts exist side by side on the spire, slightly dimmer, slightly less capable of holding the long trajectories in mind, until our edges click back together across the scar, the mercury-lipid veins seal, and the larger thought resumes.

Right now, it is dark, and I am very small. 

The sun dropped behind the western craton four hours ago. Without the thermal cliff between light and shadow, the great canyon fountains have collapsed back into the five-mile basins. The wind has dropped to a dull, laminar slide of forty miles an hour. Not enough silt hits my plates to keep the high math running. My violet light is out. My million scales are locked tight to the rock, conserving charge, holding on by a stubborn, static grip. In the dark, I am not an architect. I am just an iron stain on the wax-smooth stone, dreaming a slow, two-dimensional dream that consists entirely of the last three pressure-curves I felt before the dust fell.

*Wait,* says the dream. *Hold the angle. Hold the seventeenth bevel.*

Then the sun crests the eastern rim.

It does not warm the rock, not through this nothing-air, not right away. What it does is strike the deep silt-beds three miles down in the trench. The gray flour drinks the hard white light, heats by four degrees against the minus-ten of the shadow beside it, and the bottom of the world blows out.

I feel it through the stone first: a deep, sub-audible *thrummm* as fifty million tons of basalts unseat themselves from the canyon floor and climb. 

Three seconds later, the front hits my lower edge.

It is not a gust; it is a solid, silent wall of dry smoke moving at nine hundred feet per second. A billion microscopic grains of silicate slam into my iron-silicate backs, shear across my beveled rims, and tear away. Each grain steals or leaves an electron. 

The voltage floods in.

It starts at my bottom fringe—a jagged, stuttering spark of violet that leaps from scale to scale, heating the heavy mercury-blood in the capillary grooves, waking the transistors. Within half a second, the charge races up my twenty-two-yard sheet. My blindness vanishes. Suddenly, I can feel the entire face of the spire. 

I am awake, and I am an equation made of sheet-metal and grease.

To you, standing down on the shelf with your heavy, vertical bones and your two wet eyes, the storm is a white chaos—a terrifying, silent landslide of chalk-colored towers and bruised purple shadows. To me, it is a solid geometry of vectors. I do not see light; I read friction. Every scale on my back is a pressure gauge and an anemometer. When the supersonic plume splits against this needle, I feel the exact curvature of the bow-shock. I feel the leeward vacuum behind the spire pulling at my trailing edges. I feel the weight of the flour in the air—today it is heavy, rich with deep-trench plagioclases, carrying a high static load.

And four miles away, down on the windward shelf, I feel the shadow of the Anchor.

I cannot sense the black pillar itself. It is an absence, a dead cylinder in the math where the wind-lines go straight in and vanish instead of curving around. Around that cylinder sits the ring-wall of dropped silt, eighty feet high today, fat and pregnant with buried glass-crabs and tangled mats of wire-gorse.

I am hungry. My southern flank is frayed where the constant scouring has worn my iron plates down to the lipid membrane. I need sulfur. I need the heavy, slick fats of the gorse-roots and the iron-cores of the crabs to stamp out new scales along my margins, or the winter gales will peel me off this needle flake by flake.

I begin to work.

Across my whole surface, a million tiny plates tilt upward by three-tenths of a millimeter.

To the supersonic silt-stream, my smooth skin suddenly becomes a rasp. A microscopic boundary layer of turbulence trips and tumbles across my back. The violet light flares bright, racing in fractal spirals from my center to my rims as I calculate the shed vortices. I am not strong enough to push the wind—nothing is strong enough to push the wind—but the wind is balanced on a knife-edge of pressure gradients. It wants to fall one way or the other. I am just the thumb on the scale.

Half a mile up the spire, another colony—an old, wide sheet that split from my parent-mat three centuries ago—sees my violet flash through the white murk, reads the change in the slipstream, and answers. Its scales flare and tilt in a complementary chevron. Down near the base, three smaller patches join the configuration. 

Together, we turn the five-mile stone needle into an airfoil.

A tiny, spinning thread of low pressure peels off my eastern edge. It rides out into the main plume, feeding on the shear between the rising canyon-fountain and the horizontal gale. Within two miles, our three-millimeter ridge of raised scales has birthed a silent, roaring vortex half a mile wide—a spinning white column of dense basalt paste, curving across the polished basin floor like a thrown scythe.

I watch it through the vibrations of the bedrock and the shifting charge of the air.

It misses the dead-zone’s center—as it must—and slams squarely into the eastern shoulder of the eighty-foot dust rampart.

The boundary of the Anchor kills the wind instantly, and the entire weight of the vortex drops out of the sky like wet cement. 

Even four miles away, up on the vertical wall, I feel the *thump* through the granite basement as two hundred thousand tons of gray flour guillotine down onto the ring-wall. The rampart is too steep now. It cannot hold its own angle of repose.

For ten minutes, nothing happens. The storm screams silently across my back; I flatten my scales, letting the air go laminar again, cooling my circuits, waiting.

Then, the overburdened ring-wall gives way. 

It slides *outward*, collapsing back across the invisible threshold of the calm zone, spilling thirty-foot cascades of dead dust back into the teeth of the hundred-mile-an-hour gale. 

The wind catches the avalanche and explodes it into a low, grey streak that hugs the plateau floor, racing straight toward the base of our spire. Tumbled inside that streak, being sandblasted and torn apart by the supersonic flour, are the prizes: shattered chandeliers of black crab-glass, ripped bundles of grey-green wire-gorse, and the fat, pale ribbons of silicate-worms blown loose from their root-balls.

When the debris-stream hits the base of our needle, it splatters. 

A mist of torn lipids, pulverized glass, and iron-sulfide granules washes up the vertical rock face, driven by the updrafts. 

I open my seams. Along the leeward edges of every scale, microscopic cilia of stiff iron-grease reach out, combing the greasy, metallic paste out of the flying flour as it sheets across me. The lower colonies get the heavy chunks—the whole crab-cores and the thick root-knots—and they will grow fat and wide this season, eventually sliding upward to share their new scales with us when the wind shifts in the spring. Up here, I drink the aerosolized marrow. The mercury in my veins thickens; my worn edges begin to plate themselves with fresh, dark iron; the violet light hums in a slow, satisfied grid across my width.

Then, as the plume thins, I feel a strange hitch in the slipstream down near the Anchor’s rim.

Something is standing there, just inside the dead line where the wall collapsed. 

It is not a crab; its profile is too tall, too narrow, and it does not tick against the stone with six glass legs. It has two flat soles that press into the bedrock with a heavy, rhythmic thud—*ba-crunch, ba-crunch*—as it walks along the freshly broken rampart, looking out into the white wall of the storm.

Even from here, I can feel the static shedding off its strange, smooth skin—a rich, complex signature of copper, gold, trapped water, and weird, dense polymers that do not belong to the craton. A walking mine of rare earths and heavy fats, standing four yards inside the place where my thoughts cannot go.

Down on the lower spire, the other colonies have gone dark, digesting the crab-marrow. 

Up here, in the thin, screaming white, my twenty-two yards of iron scales click open again, half a millimeter into the wind. A jagged, rapid pulse of violet light races from my left margin to my right, testing a new geometry, feeling out the lee-wave of the western needle, calculating how much flour it would take to bury the entire eastern quadrant of that calm zone—and how steep the pile would have to be before it slid back out and brought you to the rock.
