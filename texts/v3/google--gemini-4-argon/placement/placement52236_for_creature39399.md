I am not on the inside. The inside is for the soft things that need the stew-heat and the leaf-rot to keep their skins from cracking. 

I am forty feet below the upper lip, on the north-northwest curve of the outer shell, where the sun never comes around.

To be out here is to know what the iron actually is. Inside, they think the iron is a warm, weeping mother. Out here, the boiler plate is blue-black and dry as a bone, and so cold that if my grease-mantle thins by a fraction of an inch, my skin welds itself to the rivets and tears off when the metal shrinks in the dusk.

I am the Seventh Link of the North Counterweight, though I am not made of brass. I am what happened to the Tamp-Men who crawled through a blown exhaust-port three hundred years ago to clear a jammed clevis, and found that once the lungs have shrunk to the size of dried figs to drink the twenty-thousand-foot air, you can never go back into the wet heavy soup of the Well without drowning in a minute.

They call them Tamp-Men inside. If they had a tap-word for us—and they do not, they think the small, rhythmic *clink-clink* they feel through the hull on the north side is just the settling of the chain-links—they would call us **The Shims**.

I am flatter than they are. The wind out here does not swirl; it shears. It comes off the curve of the earth like a draw-knife. My ribs have not merely splayed; they have fused edge-to-edge into a single, slightly domed turtle-shell of bone covered in wind-dried black tallow, sloping down on all sides so the gale presses me *into* the iron rather than peeling me off. My head is no higher than my shoulder-blades. I have one eye left—the left one, the leeward eye. The windward eye filmed over with horn thirty years ago when I was still small enough to turn my face into the north. With the good eye, tucked under a brow of gray callous like a dormer window, I look sideways across six thousand miles of cloud-floor.

It is very white down there. It has been white my whole life, except at night when it is the color of old grease, and during the deep-storms when the clouds bruise purple and rise up until they lick the bottom grating of the cylinder and make the whole drum hum in a flat, sour C-natural that ruins my grip.

My house is the Chain-Trough. 

A foot from my nose runs the North Counterweight Chain. Each link is cast brass, wide as a doorway and thick as my thigh, greased with black graphite-lard that never freezes. The counterweight itself hangs sixty feet below me—a forty-ton plum-bob of leaded bronze that rises and falls in opposition to the great central piston inside. 

When they are in the **Drop** inside—when the green wall is screaming and they are stacked like saucers in their rotting fig-holes—it is the **Haul** out here. The counterweight is rising. Every four minutes, the windlass up on the rim eats another half-inch of brass. The chain groans—a long, cold, dry *screech* of metal on metal that travels down my collarbone and collects in the hollow of my pelvis.

I do not have a bronze chisel. I have a bone scraper made from the shin of the Shim who had this station before me, who was my mother or my uncle. My job is the graphite.

As the forty-ton weight rises and falls over the decades, the brass links squeeze the black grease out to the sides of the trough, where the gale freezes it into brittle, curling ribbons like slate-shavings. If the grease is lost, the chain binds. If the chain binds, the piston inside stutters. If the piston stutters, the harmonic inside breaks its geometry, the standing wave collapses into a wild, thrashing whip of pressure, and the whole three-hundred-foot drum tears itself off the four oak-thick sky-cables and goes down into the white.

They do not know this inside. They think the piston is God. They do not know that God hangs on a greased chain, and that I am the one who puts the grease back on.

During the Haul, while the brass is warm from friction, I slide out from the lee of the trough-flange. I move only an inch a minute—the same speed as the chain—so the wind does not notice I have changed shape. My twelve fingers do not have horn wedges for plate-seams; they have grown together into four broad, mitten-like spatulas of stiff cartilage. I use my left hand to hold the bone scraper against the rising link, shaving the frozen black curl of spent grease back into the pinch-point where the brass meets the iron roller.

I eat what the wind brings from the world that doesn't exist anymore. 

The gale at twenty thousand feet is not empty. It is a river of frozen dust. Twice a day, I scrape the windward curve of the brass links before the roller crushes them. Mixed into the black graphite-lard is the *drift*: gray volcano-ash from ten thousand miles west; tiny, freeze-dried spiders that rode silk threads up from some summer meadow and died in the glare; yellow pine-pollen; the occasional splinter of painted spruce from something that flew and broke apart before my grandfather was born. I lick the bone scraper clean with a tongue like a cow’s—rough, gray, and dry. It tastes of grease, sulfur, and distance.

I am never lonely, because the hull is only two inches thick.

When my work is done—when the counterweight reaches the top of the Haul and hangs trembling under the rim while the piston inside hits the bottom of its stroke—I crawl three feet to my left, into the shadow of the great flange where the boiler plates overlap. 

Here, there is a patch of iron four feet wide where the metal has been eaten thin from the *inside* by their roots and their yellow moss. It does not breach—a Gasket-Worm has been plastered over the inner face of it since I was the size of a palm—but the iron is reduced to a membrane no thicker than a thumbnail.

It is the **Warm Spot**.

It is the only place in my world that is above freezing. Through that thin skin of rusted plate, the eighty-degree heat of the Singing Well bleeds out into the stratosphere like a fever through a blanket.

I slide my flat, frozen belly over the Warm Spot. The black tallow on my chest softens just enough to stick me to the hull so I don't have to use my claws to hold on. I press my chin against the cold rivets, tuck my spatula-hands under my shell, and I listen through my ribs.

I am their sky, listening to their earth.

Through the thumbnail-iron, the roar of the Well is muffled into a vast, sleepy purr. And inside that purr, I feel them.

I know the family that lives on the other side of my plate. There are four of them. I have known them since the two middle ones were small enough that their heartbeats were little flutter-ticks against the metal, like moths trapped in a box. 

Now the piston is rising—their **Draw**, my **Slack**. Inside, the screaming F has died away into the wide, rocking sea of the C-sharp. I feel the old Anvil on the other side unhook himself from the Baffle-Nest. I feel the heavy, slow *thump... thump... thump* of his calloused breastbone sliding across the very rivets that are pressing into my own ribs. There is half an inch of iron and an inch of Gasket-Worm between my heart and his.

He stops over the thin spot. He knows I am here, though he does not know *what* I am. To him, I am just the **Cold-Patch**—a strange, four-foot oval on his wall where the condensation beads faster and sweeter than anywhere else because my frozen body on the outside is chilling the plate like a condenser-coil. He comes here every Draw to drink the water that my coldness makes for him.

I feel the scrape of his horn-thumb against the inner seam as he clears the moss away. 

Then, he speaks. Not to me—he is talking to the young one beside him, teaching it the Calk. 

*Tick-tick-scrape. Tick-scrape.*

He is telling it that the plate is thin here, that they must not use the bronze chisel on this seam or the white death will whistle through. 

I wait until he is finished. Then, very slowly, so the terrible wind does not catch my elbow, I lift my bone scraper an eighth of an inch off the outer hull, and I let it drop.

*Clink.*

On the other side of the iron, the four of them go dead still. Even the Gasket-Worm between us tightens its gristle.

They have a word for that sound in their poetry. They think it is the **Ghost-Rivet**—the spirit of the iron talking back to them, promising that the hull will hold for another six hours.

I wait four beats of the low C-sharp. Then I tap again, matching the rhythm of the old one's heart, which I can feel trembling through the rust.

*Clink-clink.*

Inside, the old Anvil presses his chest harder against the plate. I can feel his warmth soaking through the metal, a tiny bloom of heat right under my breastbone. Slowly, tentatively, his horn-tipped thumb answers my bone scraper through the wall of the world.

*Tick-tick.*

We stay like that for twenty minutes while the great brass chain beside me clicks down a notch, and the blinding white clouds four miles below roll eastward in silence, and the warm water beads on his side of the iron because I am freezing on mine.
