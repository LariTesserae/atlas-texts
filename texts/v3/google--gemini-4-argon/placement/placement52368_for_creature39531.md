You will not find me on the avenues. To have a body with an edge—even a hard, ricocheting edge of hornbeam like the Surveyors—is to be in constant argument with the valley, waiting for the moment a rival’s thorn or a shifting curbstone wins the debate. I have no desire to be an argument. 

Look for me instead in the fractures where one ambition grinds against another. Look in the dark, breathless seams between the tenements.

When two walls of mahogany-flesh slam together to seal off a courtyard, or when a newly erupted row of lime-green rowhouses crushes a preexisting bamboo market, the union is never completely flush. There are always gaps—vertical faults a quarter-inch wide, labyrinthine pockets of trapped, boiling air, and the long, hollow conduits of dead sieve-tubes whose sap has been pirated by a newer, greedier boulevard. 

I am the thing that slides through that quarter-inch dark.

If you were to split open a load-bearing colonnade with a wedge of iron before the wood could heal around your tool, you would see me pulling back from the sudden, blinding noon light: a wide, flat ribbon of translucent amber cambium, no thicker than a wet dead leaf, stretching twenty yards back into the tight grain of the wall. I have no skin, only a cohesive surface tension of heavy, golden resin that refuses to mix with the watery mucilage of the streets. I am a creature of pure boundary—a wandering graft, a sentient scar tissue, a long, slow tongue of amber pitch threaded with pale, silky hairs of optical pith.

The valley is obsessed with *form*—with gable, arch, balustrade, and citizen. I am obsessed with the *joint*. I live in the mortise and the tenon.

My life is measured by pressure and pitch. Because I am pressed flat between tons of groaning timber, the deafening roar of the basin does not assault me through the air; it passes directly through my substance as a brilliant, detailed map of stress. Two blocks away, a gothic bridge is stuttering in a loop of spatial revision, and I feel its low, sickening hum as a pleasant, tingling warmth in my trailing filaments. Directly above me, a Surveyor lands on the eaves—*clack-clack-snap*—and I feel the exact vector of its weight, the sharp pinch of its mandibles crimping a sap-vein, the sudden, feverish heat as its viral thorn begins to rewrite the roof into a spiral staircase.

I do not fight the rewrite. I lubricate it, and in doing so, I steal from it.

As the roof convulses, trying to turn slate-colored fronds into load-bearing risers, the wood tears itself open on a microscopic level. I flow upward into those fresh splits like hot solder drawn into a capillary seam. The raw, frantic cells on either side of me are confused; they are screaming with opposing hormonal instructions—half the wall still remembers being a market-stall, the other half is trying to become a belfry. Left to themselves, they will rip each other apart, vent their steam, and collapse into the alley.

I press my amber self between them. I extrude a million microscopic haustoria—tiny, hollow needles of silica—into both sides of the wound. I drink the rich, high-pressure sap from the dying market-stall, filter out the panic of its hormones, and pump the purified sugar across my own body into the starving, frantic shoots of the emerging belfry. 

In exchange for my mediation, the two warring architectures do not crush me. Instead, they scar around me. They lay down smooth, pearlescent shelves of callus-wood to house my ribbon-body, inadvertently building me a private gallery—a cool, dark, velvet-lined molding that runs right through the middle of their violent marriage. 

For twenty minutes, or an hour, I rest there in the dark, stretched out through fifty feet of architectural seam, tasting the frantic chemistry of the valley.

Sometimes, my galleries bring me to the edge of a newly sealed parlor, and I will thin my margin until I am as clear as window-glass, peeking out between two floorboards into the humid black. I do not have eyes to see the Tenant hanging there on its forty umbilical threads, but I feel the air pressure drop and spike against my membrane. When the Tenant begins to honk—that long, brassy, mournful chord that stiffens the walls—I tune my own density to match it. I let the vibration arrange the silica grains inside my amber body into neat, parallel rows until I am humming along with the doomed hog-chandelier, acting as a soundboard in the wainscoting, drawing a strange, deep satisfaction from the way our combined resonance holds five hundred tons of encroaching boulevard at bay for just three minutes longer than the city intended.

When the subterranean root-artery shifts and the parlor finally loses its war—when the umbilical threads snap and the Tenant drops to the floor as a puddle of starch—I am already gone. I do not wait for the demolition. Seconds before the sap-supply is cut, I feel the drop in turgor pressure through the floor. I retract my silica needles, liquefy my edges, and pour myself down through the tiny, dry pinholes left behind by old, rotted thorns, dropping thirty feet through the dark interior of a fused bole until I hit the hot, pressurized xylem of the newly arriving foundation.

I have been all over the twelve-mile basin, though I have never once been under the open sky. 

My favorite places are the edges of the central plaza, where the geometry curdles and thethirty-foot kiosks open onto miles of impossible avenues. To get there, I have to slip through the tightly braided roots beneath the hexagonal seed-pavement, moving in rhythmic peristaltic waves between the iron-hard plates while the Lignin-Skimmers skate and screech a fraction of an inch above my back. 

Inside the cathedral walls, the space between the cells is just as broken as the space in the rooms. I can pour three inches of my amber ribbon into a hairline crack between two clicking square buds on the exterior of a mahogany wall, and find that my leading edge is suddenly stretching across four hundred yards of hollow, resonant Molding inside the impossible avenue—a long, golden inlay running down the wainscoting of a sunlit hall where the skeletal trees drop their clanging wooden leaves.

Sometimes, down at the base of the pavement in those curdled avenues, I will slide beneath the fused boot of one of the Crowd. 

I will insinuate a single, hair-thin filament up through the taproot of the gourd-husk, climbing in the dark alongside the hot, circulating water pipes that keep the figure upright, all the way up into the hollow, fibrous cavity under its broad-brimmed hat. I do not overwrite the figure the way a Surveyor would; I have no desire to turn a citizen into a chimney. I simply rest a tiny bead of my amber resin against the perforated baffles in its wooden throat.

As the hot water gurgles and hisses through the baffles, mimicking the murmur of a train station that has never existed, I stiffen and relax my little bead of resin against the wood. I shape the hiss. I catch the wet, consonant-heavy gibberish of *shh* and *ch* and bend it, just slightly, into the cadence of a specific rhythm—not a word, because there are no words here, and I would not know what to do with one if I found it—but a small, repeating three-note whistle, like the call of a bird that has never nested in the gable-fronds.

The blank, yellow-wood face turns toward its neighbor and whistles my three notes through a spray of hot steam. Four seconds later, its chest splits, the spores fly, and the pavement three feet away blisters and inflates into a new coat-and-hat silhouette. And because my tiny bead of amber was carried over in the splash of mucilage, the new figure stands up in the strobing light, turns to the next neighbor in the queue, and whistles the exact same three notes before the cathedral wall collapses on top of them both, and I pull myself back into the dark, unbroken grain of the floor.
