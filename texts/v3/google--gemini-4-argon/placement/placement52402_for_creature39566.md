To find me, you have to trace the heat downward, away from the violet hum at the ceiling, down into the frozen tallow bank on the lower eastern shelf. 

Here, the porcelain cage of the inspection lamp is already two-thirds swallowed by a bulbous glacier of grey grease and lime. Inside the glass globe, the thick carbon filament burns at a steady, sullen orange—the only constant, dry summer in Junction 14-Sub-Zero. The glass itself is hot enough to blister skin, were there any skin down here to blister. Instead, the heat simply melts a narrow, circular moat into the tallow bank around it: a glistening, two-inch trench of liquid grease, lukewarm brine, and dissolved copper salts that never freezes, even during the deepest stretch of the forty-minute winter.

I live in the moat, and I am built around the cage.

If you shone a lantern down through the standpipes, you wouldn't see a body. You would see that the porcelain ribs of the lamp-cage look oddly thick, as if they had been dipped repeatedly in tarnished silver and bruised, greenish-black tallow. And you would notice that the orange light in the chamber does not stay steady. It breathes. Every few minutes, the shadows of the pipes lengthen, sharpen, and then blur back into a dull amber smear, as if an eyelid of dirty stained glass were sliding across the bulb.

I am that eyelid. In the vernacular of a naturalist who will never climb down here, I might be called the *shutter-smear*, or the *lamp-wright*. 

I do not have the hydraulic violence of the scab-walkers, nor the sprawling, electrostatic nets of the drafters above. I am an architecture of thin films and thermal thieves. My body is a flat, creeping mantle of heavy, tallow-fed mucilage, shot through with dendritic trees of pure, metallic copper and lead that I plate out of the moat-water using the tiny galvanic currents leaking from the lamp’s corroded brass socket. I am half-candle, half-circuit. 

Because I hug the glass globe, I am the only thing in the cistern that does not need to flood its tissues with ammonia to survive. I am warm. My inner face, pressed flat against the scorching glass of the bulb, bakes at ninety degrees; my outer face, exposed to the twenty-six-degree draft, skins over with a flexible, translucent crust of paraffin-like fat and horn-silver. 

Between that hot belly and cold back, my interior is a slow, continuous convection current. I do not have a heart; I let the lamp pump me. The heat of the carbon filament drives my heavy, mineral-rich fluids upward along the glass, and the bitter chill of the room’s air drops them back down along my outer rind. I think in slow, vertical loops of rising warmth and falling cold.

Wait during the forty-minute quiet phase and watch the moat.

The scab-walkers know the moat is here. They cannot get too close to the bulb—if their core temperature rises above thirty-five degrees, the concentrated ammonia in their gizzards begins to vaporize, bloating them like dropped bladders until they burst—but they covet the grease. The tallow bank around the lamp is the richest larder of renders and lubricants in the cistern, soft enough to be scooped without iron teeth.

Twenty minutes into the freeze, a walker detaches itself from the rimstone two feet away. It comes down the slope with its brittle, ticking gait, six needles of blue sulfate tapping against the glazed iron. It reaches the outer lip of the tallow bank, just where the frost gives way to slick, softened fat. It lowers its dome to feed.

I do not see it, because I have no eyes. Instead, I feel the shadow.

Actually, I feel the *absence* of reflection. My outer skin is dusted with microscopic crystals of cuprous oxide and silver bromide, precipitated from the industrial runoff. Across my whole mantle, this mineral dust acts as a crude, sprawling photographic plate. Wherever the orange light of the lamp hits the surrounding ice curtains and bounces back onto my skin, it creates a tiny, localized voltage. I know the exact shape of the room by the map of reflected warmth and light pressing against my back.

When the scab-walker steps up to the tallow bank, it blocks a fraction of a percent of the orange glow bouncing off the eastern wall. A cool spot, the size of a thumbnail, falls across my flank.

I react at the speed of melting wax. 

Deep in the warm moat at the base of the lamp, I tighten a ring of grey, muscular gristle around the brass collar of the socket, shifting my weight. Slowly, silently, I slide a lobe of my mantle upward across the glass globe. 

Inside this lobe, I have concentrated my densest dendritic trees of copper and lead, arranged like the slats of a venetian blind. As the lobe creeps over the top of the bulb, it casts a sharp, dark wedge of shadow directly across the tallow bank, aimed right at the feeding walker.

In Junction 14-Sub-Zero, light is radiant heat. The moment my shadow falls over the walker, the temperature on that three-inch patch of tallow drops four degrees in ten seconds. 

The softened fat under the walker’s stilts instantly glazes over, turning from lard back into stone. The walker feels the sudden frostbite through its pores; it tries to lever itself backward, its hydraulic core spasming. One of its rear stilts, already half-sunk in the grease, snaps off with a sharp *plink* as the tallow hardens around itlike a trap. 

The walker lurches, off-balance, and slides two inches down the slick bezel of the bank—right into the warm, two-inch moat at the base of the bulb.

It is over in thirty seconds. The ninety-degree grease of the moat is a cauldron to a creature built of winter. Its remaining five stilts of copper sulfate dissolve like sugar in hot tea, dumping its hexagonal cap of boiler-scale flat into the melted fat. Beneath the shell, its ammonia-heavy gizzard boils, swells, and splits with a muffled, wet *pop*.

I do not rush. I have forty minutes before the next blow-down, and twenty years before the carbon filament inside the globe finally sublimates and snaps in the dark. 

Slowly, I retract my shutter-lobe from the top of the bulb, letting the orange light flood the tallow bank once more. Down in the warm moat, I extend a thin, translucent apron of pale jelly over the drowned shell. I have no iron teeth to scissor the meat; instead, I simply plate the walker. Using the stray voltage from the corroded socket, I run a faint galvanic current through the moat. Within an hour, the dissolved lead and ammonia from the walker’s ruptured core are drawn straight into my mantle by electrolysis, branching out through my body in fresh, glittering veins of grey metal, while the empty cap of boiler-scale is cemented into the base of the porcelain cage to reinforce my foundation.

Then the brass governor on the eastern wall clicks. The counterweight drops with its oiled sigh.

As the relief pipe coughs and the five-gallon blizzard of steam and forty-degree greywater roars across the terraces, the scab-walkers crack their seals and wade into the flood, and the drafters above ring their glass harps in the golden spore-smoke.

I do nothing. I simply pull my skirts tighter around the hot glass of the inspection lamp, sealing the warm moat beneath a tough roof of tallow-rind. The sideways snow-squall hisses against my back, melting on contact into a slick glaze of fresh lime and sulfur that I will spend the next forty minutes drinking through my skin. 

Through my body, the orange light beats out into the mist—dimming slightly as I digest, brightening as I thin my mantle to let the heat pass through—a slow, copper-veined lighthouse keeping watch over a sea of freezing stone.
