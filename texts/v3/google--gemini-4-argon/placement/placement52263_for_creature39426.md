My heels are locked into a V-notch of warm gabbro, sixty feet above the talus chute. My shins are pressed hard against the stone, and through them, the mountain is speaking in a low, continuous murmur of stress.

It is not a sound the ears can catch. The ears only hear the dead air—the faint, dry hiss of silver dust sliding down a seventy-degree face, the occasional *tink* of a cooling shard dropping into the dark below. The real world comes up through the calcaneus, travels the dense, ivory-hard shafts of the tibia, and blooms behind the jaw. Right now, three hundred yards to my left, a spire root is grinding against a rising plinth of basalt. It feels like two heavy millstones turning with a single grain of quartz caught between them: a stuttering, high-frequency purr that sets my back teeth on edge. 

In my hands, I hold a thirty-foot coil of pale fiber, braided from the belly-fungus we hauled up from the great dark crack at the valley’s navel two sleeps ago. The rope is dry and stiff, smelling faintly of old sulfur and deep-earth rot. Beside my left knee rests a quiver of fungus-hide packed with six flat slivers of grey slate, each one drilled through its upper third with a sharp twist of black glass.

I am the line-layer for this five-crest hub. While the others sleep in the half-sheared herringbone shelter behind me—their great keeled chests rising and falling in slow, synchronized bellows-strokes—I am re-tuning the dark.

Yesterday, the north chasm widened by the breadth of two fingers. The line that bridged it pulled until the slate gauge screamed against its pillar, and then the fiber parted with a dull *thwip*. A snapped line is a blind eye. If that chasm shears fully while we are down in the south basin scraping crust, the entire eastern flank of our ridge could drop out from under our shelter, taking our cache of obsidian cores and drop-lines into the bottomless cracks.

I uncoil three yards of the pale braid and lean out over the drop. 

To someone not made of this rock, the posture would look like falling. My chest projects far out over the black gulf, counterbalanced only by the backward hook of my cleft heels wedged into the fissure behind me. My toes—two long, horn-sheathed chisels on each foot—twist slightly inside the crack, biting into the microscopic grain of the gabbro until they lock. I do not need to look down to know I am secure. The rock holds me the way a mortise holds a tenon.

A quarter-mile across the basin, a seam tears. 

It begins with a sharp *clack* in my ankles, arriving a full second before the air-sound reaches my ears. At the same instant, a vertical line of blue light—cold and brilliant as an arc-weld—zips fifty feet up the face of an opposing monolith. 

I do not blink. My wide, milky eyes drink the ten-second flash. In that brief blue noon, the dark valley leaps into razor-sharp relief: a forest of tilting black blades, great heaps of angular house-sized blocks, and, fifty yards across the chasm from my perch, the notched stone pillar where the other end of my broken line still dangles. 

High above that pillar, wedged into the needle-tip of the spire, stands the Old One from two cycles back. The blue light catches the silver lace of dead lichen coating their cured hide and the hollow, flared cage of their ribs. The spire they are locked into has tilted another three degrees since the last flash I saw. Soon—perhaps ten sleeps, perhaps twenty—that needle will go, and the Old One will come rattling down the scree to call us to dinner.

The blue light dies. The blackness snaps back, absolute and warm, leaving a purple afterimage of the needle floating across my vision.

I do not need light to cross the gap; the blue flash has already printed the new cracks onto the back of my skull. I loop the end of the pale rope around my waist, draw a short, thick hornblende striker from my belt, and tap the rock between my feet.

*Tak-tak. Tak.*

A pause. Through the soles of my feet comes the answer from the shelter behind me: a single, dull *thud*. The youngest of our hub is awake, heels on the foundation stone, listening to my progress.

I unhook my left heel and drop four feet down the sheer face, my horned fingers finding a cooling blister in the obsidian, then a paper-thin ledge of sheared feldspar. *Tink. Clack.* I move sideways like a crab across the seventy-degree wall, descending toward the narrow throat of the chasm where the two spires nearly kiss before flaring apart into the abyss.

Down here, the heat is heavier. A hairline crack runs beneath my right hand, no wider than a grass blade, breathing out a silent, invisible ribbon of sulfur dioxide and trace steam. Without pausing, I lower my face to the stone and draw a long, slow breath through the narrow slits of my nose. The gas burns cold and sweet across the dry turbinates in my skull; the waxy mucus in my throat thickens, trapping the microscopic trace of mantle-water, pulling it greedily into my blood. It tastes of iron and copper—the taste of a coming bloom.

At the narrowest neck of the gorge, the gap is twelve feet wide. 

There is no wind, only a steady, rising thermal column from the deep dark below, smelling of baked slate and dead silver dust. I wedge both feet into a horizontal seam, fold my long, spindly legs until my breastbone touches my knees, and spring.

I do not fly; I unfold across the dark air like a thrown carpenter's rule. 

My hands hit the opposite wall first—four curved plates of dark keratin striking a vertical rib of gabbro with a loud *clack* that echoes down the chute. My toes stab instantly into a V-crevice two feet below, and my weight settles onto the bone-locks. A shower of brittle, calcified lichen-lace, disturbed by my landing, drifts down past my shoulders, sparkling faintly where the tiny crystals catch the dull cherry-red glow of a dying vent far down at the valley floor.

The climb up to the pillar takes ten minutes of rhythmic, four-limbed slotting. When I reach the cairn—three heavy slabs of stone keyed together on a narrow shoulder—I find the old rope-end frayed to pale fluff where the shifting mountain sawed it through. 

I untie the new coil from my waist, haul it taut across the dark gulf back toward my ridge, and seat it into the deep V-notch carved into the top slab of the pillar. From my quiver, I draw one of the flat slate gauges. I thread the rope through its pierced eye, sliding the stone sliver down until its sharp bottom edge rests directly against the rough granite-like lip of the pillar's notch. 

I pull the fiber until it hums under my thumb, tying it off around a projecting horn of rock. 

As I finish the knot, the deep millstone-grind in my shins abruptly stops. 

In this valley, silence in the stone is the loudest warning there is. It means the friction has locked; the pressure is no longer bleeding off in micro-slips. It is building toward a shear.

I freeze, my hands still on the taut rope, my heels wedged tight into the shoulder by the pillar. 

One breath. Two. My great lungs expand, filling my keeled chest with the thin, kiln-warm air, holding it while my inner ears strain down through my leg bones into the dark root of the spire.

*Snap.*

It does not come from the spire I am standing on. It comes from the ridge right below the Old One’s needle, two hundred yards up-valley. 

The shockwave hits my heels like a mallet blow, followed instantly by a tearing, ragged line of cherry-red light that splits the dark wall open from top to bottom. A wedge of rock the size of four shelters detaches in eerie slow motion, tilting outward into the gulf. Seconds later, the sound arrives through the thin air: a dry, cascading clatter, like a thousand slate roofs sliding into a cellar all at once.

 right beside my knee, the newly strung rope jerks tight as our two spires settle a fraction of an inch apart in sympathy. The slate gauge drags across the stone notch.

*Shhhk.*

The red light from the new fissure washes over my face, warm and dull as an iron bar pulled from the coals. Already, I can see the first grey plumes of sulfur-breath rising from the raw, glassy wound in the cliff. Within ten minutes, the copper frost will begin to branch across that hot stone, drinking the gas, turning the rock into food.

Beneath my feet, the stone taps rapidly. *Tak-tak-tak. Tak.*

The shelter is empty; the family is already on the scree, scrapers in hand, descending toward the red glow. I tap once with my heel—*I hear, I am coming*—unhook my toes from the seam, and drop down into the warm dark to join the harvest.
