I am wedged three hundred feet below the yellow sky, in the dark, warm seam where a caudiciform’s bloated taproot crushes an ancient gallery of petrified ochre. 

There is no room here to turn around, so I do not have a front or a back. I am a flat, leathery ribbon of cartilage and pale muscle, forty feet long and no thicker than a man’s thumb, threaded through the microscopic fault lines of the masonry like a vein of wet lead. If you were to break open this wall—though nothing down here has the strength or the madness to strike the load-bearing clay—you would think I was just an old root sheath, or a stray ribbon of the Basal Mat gone tough and fibrous.

I do not walk; I peristalse. My skin is covered in dry, overlapping scutes made of something between horn and fingernail, angled so that when I breathe in, I grip the walls of the fissure, and when I breathe out, I slide forward an eighth of an inch. 

I am an auditor of pressure. My entire body is a lateral line pressed against the drumhead of the deep city. 

Above me, the thirty-foot chimneys and vaulted causeways are constantly growing, accumulating ton upon ton of sun-baked spittle and red mud. The city is top-heavy. It is always settling, always shifting its intolerable weight down through the centuries of dead galleries toward the bedrock. Where the giant water-trees swell after a deep tap, they push against the ancient pillars; where an old fungal garden is abandoned, the vault ceiling sags. Before the clay actually cracks, it sings. It is a slow, sub-audible groan of crystalline grains grinding against one another under ten thousand pounds of shear stress.

That groan is my food, or rather, it is the dinner bell.

Right now, through my left flank, I feel the dull, rhythmic *thud-squish* of a Spongy Walker moving across a surface dome two hundred feet overhead. The vibration comes down through a main buttress, faint and harmless, like a heartbeat through a thick mattress. To my right, forty feet laterally, is the wet, continuous static of a major highway—millions of blind worker termites hauling damp mud up from the water table, their tiny feet creating a warm, sizzling hum in the stone. 

Neither of these interests me. I am listening to a pillar twelve yards down-gradient that has been quietly dying for three weeks. 

A strangler fig’s deep sinker-root has wrapped around the base of the pillar, and the root is thickening. The clay is under immense, dry tension. It is too hot and too compressed there for the termites to easily chew a relief joint, and the Grout-Worms cannot slide into a gap that hasn't opened yet. So the stress builds. To my ribs, the dying pillar feels like a tight wire humming a steady, rising note in the dark. 

I begin to contract, oozing backward through the mortar-seam, following the pitch of the groan. It takes me four hours to travel the twelve yards. As I get closer, the air in the hairline cracks grows hotter, baked by the friction of the shifting weight. The smell here is old—not the wet, mushroom-and-formic-acid stench of the living chimneys, but the dry, chalky tang of clay that was chewed and spat out before the trees above were even seeds, mixed with the sweet, limestone-heavy breath of the Basal Mat just below.

I reach the nexus of the stress. Here, a gallery ceiling has dropped half an inch, pinching a side-tunnel nearly shut. The clay hasn't shattered—the old mortar is too heavily laced with ancient, mineralized fungal hyphae for a clean break—Instead, it is flaking under the pressure, exfoliating in razor-sharp shards of dry terracotta, trapping a pocket of the city’s workforce behind a creeping pinch-point.

And there is my meal.

A battalion of old soldier-termites, their huge, orange-armored heads too wide to squeeze back through the narrowing gap, have been crushed or trapped by the settling vault. Alongside them, a thick, pale tendril of the Basal Mat has been severed from its main web by the shear, and is bleeding a slow, milky exudate of pure, concentrated sugars and regulatory alkaloids into the dust. 

The city’s repair crews are already gathering on the other side of the pinch, their mandibles clicking in a frantic, papery chorus as they prepare to wall off the collapsed sector with fresh wet daub. They will seal the tomb within the hour.

I slip my narrow, eyeless head through the two-millimeter gap where the ceiling meets the floor. My skull is not bone; it is a flexible wedge of oiled cartilage that flattens like a stiff tongue. I do not bite. I have no jaw to drop. Instead, I extrude my pharynx—a long, muscular tube lined with rasping, microscopic teeth like a snail’s radula—and press it against the crushed soldiers and the weeping fungal vein.

I drink the mistake. I rasp away the chitin, the spilled hemolymph, the rich, hormonal sap of the Mat, and the damp, enzyme-heavy clay that the trapped workers died holding in their jaws. 

While I feed, the repair workers on the outside reach my tail. They do not attack me. My scutes are coated in a dry, powdery wax I secrete from my cloaca—a dust that smells precisely like cured, century-old load-bearing brick. To their antennae, I am not flesh; I am simply a vein of unusually slick, hard mineral in the wall. A few of them walk across my back, daubing wet pellets of fresh red mud directly onto my skin to seal the crack. 

I let them. As the wet clay dries against my hide, I absorb the moisture through specialized pores between my scales—the only water I will ever drink. When the mortar begins to set and grow warm, I simply exhale, flattening my ribs by a fraction of a millimeter, and slide forward out of the newly cast sheath, leaving a smooth, tubular mold of myself behind to act as a tiny stress-relief flue in their new wall.

Full, heavy with the Mat’s stolen alkaloids, I settle into a quiescent fissure between two calcified fungal arches. The alkaloids make my nervous system hum; for a few hours, as I digest the fungal brain-sap, my sense of the city expands. I am no longer just listening to the twelve-yard radius of my seam. I can feel the whole basin breathing. I feel the millions of Flue-Swifts dropping through the hot chimneys like blood cells through capillaries; I feel the sun shifting toward the horizon as the western faces of ten million towers expand slightly in the afternoon heat, tilting the entire continent-wide metropolis a fraction of a degree toward the east.

I flatten myself against the warm, singing stone, waiting for the next pillar to groan.
