I am forty miles inland from the old coast, where the avenues narrow into the terraced stair-canals that once lifted meltwater from the central valleys.

I am not one of the great plaza-grazers, nor am I a simple piston-cylinder clearing a single ditch. I am three of them that met at a four-way sluice-junction nineteen years ago during a winter storm-swell, when the twenty-second rhythm dropped to fourteen and the deep water tore the kelp-canopy loose in great, tangled rafts. We crowded into the lee of a stone gatehouse to wait out the turbulence, our outer rinds chafed raw by the flying gravel, and before the rhythm settled back to its twenty-second pendulum, our pale inner jellies had flowed together through the torn seams of our coats. We never divided. We had found a shape that fit the junction.

If you looked down through the indigo gloom from the canopy—past the *clonk* and sigh of the bowing laminaria—you would see only a crescent-shaped bank of rubble and blood-red dulse hooked around the base of a square basalt pillar. I look like a drift of storm-wrack that has been there for a century.

In a way, I have. Some of the wrist-thick kelp stipes stitched into my dorsal lattice were harvested from trees that fell when the water was ten feet shallower. My core ballast—nearly four hundred pounds of stone suspended in my central mesoglea—is not rough shale, but the hand-knapped cobbles from the gatehouse floor. Over the decades, as I have shifted and pulled myself through the three-second slacks, those stones have ground against one another inside me until they are as smooth and round as river-eggs. They don't *clack* anymore when I move; they make a sound like wet leather rubbing on slate, a low *shhh-ock* that only the anemones in the gatehouse niches can feel.

Wait for the east-heave to end.

The millions of tons of cold, metallic water press my ten-foot ribbons of brown kelp flat against the paving stones. My borrowed holdfasts—the pale, dead knuckles of giant weed that I have chemically welded to my underside—lock into the joints of the basalt floor. I do not fight the water. I let it compress me. The pressure squeezes my outer tunic of sponge-gristle, forcing a slow circulation of cold seawater through the labyrinth of pores in my flank, filtering out the faint, sour sugars of rotting mucilage.

Then, the heave spends itself. The forest stands upright in the gloom. The sighing stops.

*Slack.*

For three seconds, the world has no weight. Instantly, my holdfasts relax their grip. From beneath my fringe of black Irish moss, twenty pale, translucent cables of gristle shoot out across the cobbles. I do not pull myself forward; I am too large for a single channel now. Instead, I send my pseudopods into the four intersecting drainage trenches at once, feeling the stone with a blind, chemical hunger.

The east trench is clear; I swept it four hundred swells ago. The north trench has a thin film of grey marine snow, not yet thick enough to roll.

The south trench is wrong.

Thirty feet up the channel, the water-stroke is ragged. Instead of the smooth, laminar slide of the reversing swell, there is a faint, fluttering turbulence—a tiny whirlpool that is scattering the falling ash instead of banking it against the wall. And beneath the taste of the dissolved slate, the water carries the sharp, chalky tang of freshly fractured basalt.

A root has wedged a cap-stone loose.

The west-heave begins. The forty-foot kelp stalks bow over me, their tips brushing my back. I flatten, locking my knuckles back into the floor, and let the current ride over my spine. Inside my jelly, I begin to shift my stones.

It takes me two hours—three hundred and sixty swings of the great pendulum—just to unhook myself from the gatehouse pillar and flow into the mouth of the south trench. Because the channel is only four feet wide and I am twelve feet across, I have to change my architecture. In the pauses between the swells, I dissolve the sponge-threads holding my outer lattice of dead kelp-stipes together. I unweave myself. My round, boulder-like bulk elongates, narrowing into a thick, twenty-foot train of gravel-filled slime and trailing red fronds. My internal cobbles roll into a single, heavy line down my gut, like beads on a string.

*Slack.* Pull two feet. *Heave.* Lock and flatten. *Slack.* Pull two feet.

Along the way, my underside strips the stone clean. The thin grey carpet of dead plankton and bryozoan velvet dissolves into my mucous sole, leaving the ancient horn-pick marks in the basalt dark and gleaming behind me. When I pass a niche carved into the third tier of the wall, where three peeled-grape anemones hang limp in the dark, a pale tendril flicks out from my flank. I drop a marble-sized bolus of compacted, indigestible chitin and silt into the largest crown. As my twenty-foot length drags past the niche over the next ten swells, the anemone’s tentacles brush my back, leaving a fresh, invisible smear of stinging mucus across my dulse-pelt. The skin of my tunic tightens slightly at the chemical burn, hardening into better armor.

By the five-hundredth swell, my leading edge touches the breach.

A bull-kelp holdfast, thick as a barrel, has grown directly into the dry-stone seam at the lip of the dike. Its woody fingers have pried a two-hundred-pound lintel block out of alignment; the stone is canted outward, hanging over the trench by an inch, vibrating faintly every time the swell reverses. Behind it, the grey ash that should be funneling down to my junction is leaking out into the sunken courtyard beyond, wasted in the thickets of sea-lettuce.

I do not have the mass of the great avenue-weavers. I cannot lift a two-hundred-pound stone with gas-bladders alone.

For ten swells, I simply lie in the trench beneath the overhanging block, letting the water press me east, then west, while my outer membrane tastes the crack. The gap behind the tilted stone is six inches wide, choked with the pale, living root-hairs of the kelp that caused the ruin.

In the next slack, I do not pull forward. I flow *up*.

Half of my body remains anchored in the trench, ballasted by my smooth gatehouse cobbles. The other half—a pale, glistening sheet of yellowish-grey plasmodium stripped of its weed-coat—extrudes upward, pouring itself into the six-inch gap behind the stone.

I wrap myself around the living kelp holdfast. I do not tear it; my gristle is not strong enough to snap living wood. Instead, I seal it away from the water. I pump my digestive acids into the pocket, turning the space behind the stone into a slow, freezing stomach. Over the next four days, while the forest above me bows east and west twelve thousand times, I eat the root that broke the wall.

As the woody fingers dissolve into sugar and grey slime, the two-hundred-pound stone begins to settle back toward its bed.

It cannot go all the way back; the basalt has flaked, and the ancient joint is gone. If I leave it, the current will rock it loose again in a year.

So, on the fifth day, I make the division.

Inside my belly, I sort my stones. The smooth, round cobbles from the gatehouse—the ones I have carried for decades—I pull down into the lower half of my body waiting in the trench. The rougher, flatter pieces of shale I have picked up along the road, I push upward, packing them tightly into the gap behind the basalt block, wedging them against the stone until it stops vibrating in the swell.

Then, during the three-second silence of the slack, I pinch my own waist in two.

I sever the pale, sinewy jelly connecting my lower bulk to the mass packed into the wall. The upper quarter of me stays behind in the masonry. Deprived of my moving sole and the rich ash of the gutter, that severed piece will not crawl away. Already, in the near-freezing dark, its outer rind is beginning to calcify, stiffening into a dense, rubbery mortar of dead sponge-spicule and trapped shale. Within a month, it will be indistinguishable from the stone it holds in place. It will hold that corner straight for five hundred years.

Lighter now, shorter by five feet, I drop back down into the square-cut gutter.

The next east-heave comes. The water rushes down the south trench. Where the wall was canted, it is now flush; the tiny, fluttering whirlpool is gone. The current slides past the patch of pale, stiffening gristle in a smooth, unbroken stroke, carrying a fresh ribbon of grey marine snow directly over my back and down toward the gatehouse junction.

The water pauses. The forest stands upright.

In the three seconds of silence, I reach out with my pale cables, find the cracks in the floor, and pull myself two feet back toward the crossroads.
