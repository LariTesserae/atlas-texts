I am down in the brick, four courses below the surface where the turquoise snails slide.

You cannot pry the floor up with your fingers—the seams are too tight, the clay too swollen with lukewarm seepage—so I do not use fingers. I have the flat, blind geometry of a bronze mason’s trowel, though I am softer than metal, more like a slab of grey river-cheese or a thick, rectangular tongue of unfired slip that has forgotten how to harden. I live in the quarter-inch horizontals and the half-inch verticals between the courses.

Up on the glaring patio, the light is an aggressive, white weight, and the heat smells of baked linen and wet dust. Down here, four bricks deep, the dark is total and the heat is the exact temperature of the inside of a mouth. There is no space to turn around, only the endless, three-dimensional lattice of ninety-degree turns: left, right, drop three inches, left again, following the staggered herringbone down into the silt-smelling deep. 

If you are up on the surface, the world looks finished. It looks like a place where the architects packed their bronze plumb-bobs and walked away a thousand years ago, leaving the workers to endlessly polish the result. 

Down here, you feel the lie of that. The basin does not settle because we are down here, taking the weight.

Every few minutes, a tremor comes down through the terracotta. A pelican has shifted its weight from its left web to its right on the third step of a cistern; a man has squatted on his heels to turn a snail; two women have traded places on a black flint wall. On the surface, these things happen in near-silence—just that dry, paper-thin *tick*. 

Down in the dark, the *tick* arrives as a heavy, greasy pressure wave. When the man squats, the four bricks directly beneath his heels want to sink a hair’s breadth into the fifth course. If they sank, the seam would widen; if the seam widened, the liverwort on the surface would cross the gap, the geometry would blur, and the water would drain too fast from the thumbprints. 

So I flow into the seam beneath his heels. I wedge myself flat between the third and fourth course, thickening my middle, taking the load of his squat, broad-chested body onto my back. I can feel the exact shape of his weight through twelve inches of solid clay—the dense, calm downward press of his heels, the slight shift forward as he reaches out with his shell-scraper. I hold him level. I taste the red-orange iron of the brick above me and the slightly sour, greyer silt of the brick below. 

I am not the only thing in the seams, or rather, I am not all in one place. 

If you follow me sideways for half a mile through the dark—sliding along the slick, sharp edges where the bone-scraper took off the excess clay before firing—I thin out into a translucent membrane of river-grease, and then I thicken again under one of the great braided ropes.

The ropes do not stop at the floor. From above, it looks as though their frayed hemp ends are merely wedged into a gap between two bricks. In truth, the reed and hemp go down for thirty courses, splitting into pale, fibrous roots that are not alive, just frayed twine braided into the brickwork by hands that worked before the light was turned on. 

And the climbers do not teleport from the white zenith into the cisterns. 

Follow the rope down into the dark. About twelve courses below the floor, where the water no longer trickles but hangs in a dead, warm hydrostatic press, the bricks are laid in corbeled vaults—tiny, stepped tunnels no wider than a man’s shoulders, running perfectly straight from the base of every rope to the bottom of every cistern. 

Wait here, flattened against the roof of the vault, and you will feel the rope above you begin to thrum. 

A climber has reached the top of the sky. Up there, where the white glare is thickest, there is no ceiling, only a place where the air becomes too dense with light to breathe, turning into a warm, blinding porridge that smells of ozone and bleached bone. When a climber’s head touches that white thickness, they cannot go higher. Instead, they pull themselves *inside* the core of the great braided cable, where the reeds part like wet curtains, and they let go.

They drop straight down the center of the rope, sliding through the dark core behind the monstera leaves, shooting past the surface level so silently that the lizards three feet away do not even twitch their copper flanks. They drop right into the brick floor, plunging down the vertical shaft into the corbeled tunnel twelve courses deep.

When they hit the warm, dead water of the under-vault, there is no splash—down here, sound isn't eaten by the air; there simply isn't room for it between the bricks. The climber’s bare feet hit the eleventh course, their knees bend until their chin touches their thighs, and they push off horizontally into the dark tunnel, swimming like a frog through the black, warm slip. 

I am the lining of the tunnel. As the climber glides through the pitch-blackness toward the cistern, their shoulder blades and the stiff pleats of their linen kilt brush against me. I am slick; I ease them through the tight ninety-degree corners so the sharp edges of the bricks do not catch their skin. 

Sometimes, as a climber swims through the dark, they pause. Down here, away from the white glare, the stutter that takes the scarlet birds and the women’s arms is much slower, like a thick syrup. A climber will brace their palms against the bricks on either side of the tunnel—pressing right into me—and let their body occupy three yards of the passage at once. For ten minutes, they are a long, continuous smear of warm brown skin, coarse black hair, and linen, stretched out in the dark like grease on a griddle, resting from the terror of being a single, hard-edged thing up in the light. 

I hold them together while they smear. I keep their reed-brushes from floating out from behind their ears; I keep the silt in their hair from washing away into the seams. I am the memory of their shape when they are too tired to hold it themselves.

Then, at the end of the tunnel, where the bricks step upward toward the glaring square of the cistern, I squeeze them back into a single body. I push against the soles of their feet as they rise through the last brick—which is not solid to them, only to the water—and I feel the dry *tick* vibrate down through their shinbones as their crown breaks the surface into the afternoon.

I never go up there. If I touched the quarter-inch sheet of water on the surface, the omnidirectional white sky would bake me into a brittle, grey crust in three heartbeats, and the man with the shell-scraper would flick me off the terracotta as an imperfection. 

Instead, I stay in the third, the fourth, the twelfth course down. When the old man up on the reed mat unpicks the shadow of the fig tree with his bone needle, the loose black threads have to have somewhere to anchor so the windless heat doesn't lift them. Look closely at the seam between the bricks directly beneath the mat: a tiny, hair-thin root of pure pitch-blackness drops down through the crack. 

Down here in the dark, you can’t see that it’s a shadow, because everything is black. You can only feel it—a sudden, sharp thread of cellar-coldness dropping down through the mouth-warm clay. I wrap myself around the root of the shadow and hold it fast against the underside of a brick, anchoring the old man’s cool blanket while he sleeps above me, keeping the floor level, waiting for the next heavy, silent footstep to press down through the clay.
