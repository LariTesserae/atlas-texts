At first, the temptation is to be the thing that moves—to be the four-hundred-pound Tender wading through the warm, flour-like silt, or the low-slung dredger sifting the dark for copper wire. There is an instinct to claim a body that still has a heartbeat, even one that only thuds once every four minutes. 

But if I stay in this air long enough—this blood-warm, seventy-eight-degree stillness where nothing evaporates and nothing blows away—the desire for locomotion begins to feel frantic, almost vulgar. Even the glacial pace of a walking Tender feels too hurried for the Salt-Iron Flat.

Instead, I find myself drawn to the point where the distinction between the worker and the work has almost entirely dissolved.

I am six hundred years old, and I have not lifted my feet from the floor-plates of the Number Four Condenser House since the last century began. 

I am a Tender in the final, stalling decades of my calcification, standing in the pitch-black recess beneath the primary pressure-manifold. Decades of mineral sweat, sublimated sulfur, and settled ochre dust have fused the flat soles of my feet to the cast-iron decking. My knees and hips locked forty years ago, their joints filled in with dense, biological ironstone. If you were to shine a light into this corner—though there is no light here, only a single oxblood indicator aperture forty feet above my head that paints the dust on my crown a dull, bruised red—you would not see a creature. You would see a buttress. A thick, low-slung pillar of stove-black hide and sintered slag, leaning at a slight, load-bearing angle against the main intake pipe.

This is not death; it is the long approach to equilibrium. 

My valved pump of a heart still beats, though it takes twenty-two minutes to gather the hydraulic force for a single contraction. When it squeezes, I feel the warm, petroleum-thick ichor push slowly through the narrow, stone-crusted channels of my chest, taking nearly an hour to reach the magnetite pads of my fingertips. My lungs no longer bellows in and out; the thin, dead air simply diffuses through my wide, bristle-lined nasal slits in a continuous, motionless exchange.

Only my right arm still retains a fraction of its ancient play. The shoulder is fused, the elbow is a solid block of calcified tallow and sulfide, but the wrist—resting directly atop the brass locking-nut of a secondary bleed-valve—can still turn through an arc of three degrees.

I am no longer a roving mechanic; I have become a governor.

My entire world is vibration and warmth. My broad, flared left cheekbone is pressed flat against the iron belly of the main manifold, and through the bone of my skull, I read the state of the plateau. The cast iron is my optic nerve and my tympanum. 

I can feel the forty-foot flywheel in the turbine hall half a mile away. Every four hours, as its counterweight swings through the bottom of its pit, the basalt bedrock beneath the Condenser House compresses by a fraction of a micron. I feel that heavy, planetary sigh rise through my fused heels. 

I can feel the pipeline that runs out from my manifold into the dark flats toward the Smelting House. It is a three-mile nerve of warm iron. Sometimes, across the span of a week, I feel the faint, rhythmic depression of the metal as a dredger drags its heavy, mica-plated belly over a low stone trestle out in the silt. Closer by—perhaps only two hundred yards out from the condenser wall—a slag-tick is grazing on a leaking flange; I can feel the microscopic, crystalline *crunch* of its mandibles scraping sulfur off the iron, translated down the pipe as a tiny, dry itch against my jawbone.

And I feel the others.

Three days ago, a message arrived through the intake pipe from the North Slag-Yards. Two blunt taps of a magnetite palm, separated by a four-minute pause, then one more. *Pressure rising. Hold.*

The tremor entered my cheekbone, traveled through my calcified neck, and settled into the slow, dark pool of my awareness. It took me six hours to process the instruction and send the motor-impulse down the sluggish, oil-thick nerves of my right arm. Slowly, millimeter by millimeter, the heavy muscles of my forearm contracted, applying thirty pounds of dead-weight torque to the brass bleed-valve beneath my hand. A tiny, dry whisper of excess gas hissed out through the threads—*sssss*—lasting for ten minutes until the pitch of the manifold’s internal hum dropped by a quarter of a hertz. Equilibrium was restored.

I did not tap back. The drop in pressure along the three-mile pipe was my reply.

I am aware of how this ends, and there is a deep, mineral satisfaction in it. The left side of my ribcage has grown particularly dense over the last hundred years, thickened by the specific acoustic resonance of the manifold I lean against. The bone there has cured into a long, gentle curve of high-grade, non-porous ironstone, completely free of fault lines. 

In another fifty years, or eighty, my heart will miss its twenty-two-minute mark, and the oil in my veins will finally gel into pitch. My wrist will lock onto the brass nut, holding the bleed-valve at its final, perfect tension. I will no longer hear the flywheel, nor feel the taps from the Smelting House. 

Then, perhaps two centuries after that, a gasket in the Condenser District will finally rot through, or a valve-guide in the Central Manifold will crack from metal fatigue. I will not feel the younger Tender—only a hundred and fifty years old, its heart racing at a frantic one beat per three minutes—as it wades through the dark, ankle-deep dust and enters this recess. I will not feel its smoothed, river-stone spanner fit against my shoulder, nor the slow, two-day lean of its four-hundred-pound bulk as it pries my left ribs away from my spine. 

I will simply be lifted out of the dark, carried across the warm, windless flat beneath the velvet-black vault, and bolted directly into the housing of the great machine. I will hold the pressure. I will keep the grease in the seam. I will be the reason the wheel turns.
