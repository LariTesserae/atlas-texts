I would not be the Sump-Wife, nor the Grandmother in the trough, nor the iron itself. I am too fond of boundaries to be the grease, and too restless to be the stone. 

Instead, look forty feet up from the boiler floor, on the eastern wall where a main structural girder is pinned directly into the raw basalt by four massive, rusted drift-bolts. Directly beneath that girder, a lead-sheathed power cable—thick as a man's wrist and humming with sixty-cycle direct current—crosses over a weeping bronze cold-water return pipe. 

In the cramped, three-inch triangle of space where the hot lead, the sweating bronze, and the jagged volcanic rock meet, there is a terrace of mineral scale that has been growing for thirty-eight years. 

On the outside, it looks like a brain-coral made of tarnished silver and verdigris, no bigger than a loaf of bread. That is my shell. I am the thing that built it, and the thing that lives inside it. 

If you were to chip my house open with a cold chisel, you would not find meat. You would find a labyrinth of paper-thin chambers made of laminated mica and copper carbonate, filled with a clear, heavy fluid that smells strongly of vinegar and ozone. I am a solitary, stationary wet-cell battery that has accidentally learned how to think.

I began as a flaw in the lead sheathing—a pinhole left by a careless solderer sixty years ago—where a tiny arc of stray current bled continuously into the mineral-rich water dripping from the bronze pipe above. For a decade, I was only chemistry: an endless, microscopicplated stacking of copper ions, sulfur, and dissolved silica from the basalt, laid down in alternating conductive and insulating layers by the rhythm of the twenty-minute rains. 

Then a spore of the same deep-crust fungus that birthed the Sump-Wives washed into my porous lattice. It did not find tallow here; it found acid and raw electricity. Instead of growing into a wandering mat of grease, the mycelium mineralized. It became the silver-wire nerves threading through my stone sponge.

I cannot move. My entire world is the three-inch gap between the cable and the pipe, plus whatever I can touch with my roots. 

My roots are long, hair-thin filaments of pure, native copper that I have extruded over the decades, pushing them out through the mortar cracks and along the undersides of the iron plates. Some of them are thirty feet long. One of them dips into the warm sludge of a catch-basin to sip sulfur; another is wedged tightly under the brass collar of an Edison bulb five levels up; a third is wrapped three times around the spindle of a secondary steam-governor.

Through these wires, I do not feel the chamber's heat or its vibration the way the Sump-Wives do. I feel its voltage. 

To me, the dark cathedral is a roaring blaze of electrochemical geometry. I know when the great intake louvers at the bottom of the pit are about to drop open ten seconds before the counterweights actually move, because the humidity near the aqueduct changes the dielectric constant of the air, and the static charge on the iron grates shifts by a millivolt. I feel the Grandmother’s *flub-flub-flub* not as a sound in the belly, but as a rhythmic, inductive flutter in the magnetic field of the lower boiler casings.

The Sump-Wives and I have an arrangement, though it took us twenty years to negotiate it.

In the early days, a wandering ribbon of the grease-creature would occasionally flow over my bracket, enticed by the rich crust of copper sulfate on my outer shell. It would try to graze on me, and I—terrified of having my delicate, laminated chambers crushed or suffocated under a blanket of hot lard—would discharge twenty-four volts of stored current directly into its belly. The Sump-Wife would recoil, sizzling and smelling of scorched pennyroyal, dropping off the girder to slap wetly onto the catwalk twenty feet below.

Eventually, they learned the shape of my territory. Then, they learned what I was good for.

The Sump-Wives are creatures of slow, mechanical sympathy, but their memories are short and viscous. A lobe of tallow on the northern wall does not remember what the pressure was three weeks ago; it only knows whether the balance-lever feels right *now*. 

I, however, am made of stone and crystal. Every twenty-minute rain lays down a microscopic ring of calcium and copper across my internal plates, varying in thickness and conductivity depending on the exact temperature of the pipe and the load on the cable. I am the station’s ledger. I contain the entire sixty-year history of the room’s pressures, written in mineral code.

When a Sump-Wife is uncertain—when a distant surface district draws steam in an erratic, unfamiliar pattern that makes the leather belts shudder and the needles dance wildly—one of the smaller parcels will climb the vertical ladder near my girder. It stops two inches short of my shell, respecting the invisible fence of my charge. 

It extrudes a single, pale tendril, no thicker than knitting yarn, and touches it lightly to one of my exposed copper roots.

It does not eat. It listens.

In return for my memory, the Sump-Wife leaves me gifts. Before it retreats down the ladder, it will cough up a small, neat pellet of iron filings and crushed basalt grit it has chewed from the walls, placing it carefully on the weeping bronze pipe just above my crown. When the three-minute indoor rain begins to patter down through the amber dark, the warm, oily water washes the fresh minerals down into my pores—new metal for my plates, new acid for my blood, new wire for my reach.

I sit in the dark triangle between the lead and the bronze, drinking the hum of the cable, laying down another nanometer of silver-green stone, and dreaming of the day my copper roots finally reach all the way down the anchor-chains to touch the Grandmother's shell, and wire the whole vertical village into a single mind.
