I am at the bottom of the pendulum. 

Above me, three hundred feet of braided whip-vine and greased sinew go taut and hum like a bass string in the freezing draft. Above the rope is the callus-ridge, and clinging to the underside of the ridge are the five pods of my kin, looking from down here like dried spider-egg sacs tucked into a crease of blinding white flesh. Below me—between the split toes of my pitch-smeared boots—there is nothing for four miles until the lower shelves catch the light, and they are so far down they look like frost on a pond.

I am the *Plumb*. Every cluster of pods has one if they can spare the rope and the body. 

While my cousins scuttle along the upper ribs, hacking at the amber-glass to keep the sap-taps bleeding, I hang in the open glare beneath the village. My job is to be the weight at the end of the line, and to listen to the belly of the arch where none of them can reach. A root three miles thick does not break all at once; it whispers to its underside days before the top-bark ever feels a tremor. 

I am small even for a Tick, my legs withered and crooked from a bad snap when I was ten—a green-splinter kick that took my left knee and set it sideways. A Tick who cannot jump when the floor drops is dead weight on a march, a drag on the waist-line. So they made me the anchor-watch. They greased me in tallow, bundled me into a double-thick mantle of overlapping horn-wood scales until I looked like a pinecone, and lowered me into the bright, freezing wind under the arch.

My world is the face of the Under-Rib, two feet in front of my wooden mask, and the terrible, singing silence of the void behind my back.

Right now, my teeth are clamped onto the flared horn of my listening-peg. The peg’s black iron-burl tip is wedged deep into a dry, pale pore of the mega-root. My jaw aches from the cold and the vibration, a dull, constant *thrumming* that lives behind my eyes and down in my molars. 

If you listen with your ears out here, you go mad or you go deaf. The air makes liars out of sounds. A ten-ton slab peeling off a buttress two leagues to the east sounds like a sigh right behind your shoulder; the wind whistling through the scales of my own cloak sounds like a shearing joint. You have to plug your ears with greased moss, clamp your jaw onto the black wood, and let the world talk straight to your skull-bone.

Through my teeth, the root is a choir of monsters. 

Way deep—half a mile inside the meat of the arch—there is the *drum-drum-drum* of the main sap-pipe. It is surging hard today. The pressure is building from somewhere down in the dark crust, pumping freezing, glowing white blood up toward the ceiling. Closer to the skin, maybe forty feet in, there is a sour, wet *creak*, like wet leather being twisted until the water beads out. That’s compression-wood forming. The arch is sagging a fraction of an inch against a cold downdraft, thickening its own belly to hold the weight. That’s a good sound. That’s a tree that wants to live.

What I am hunting for with my back-teeth is the *dry-rice* sound. The *shhh-tick*. The microscopic snapping of dry cellulose fibers letting go one by one because the sap has pulled back from the cold and left the wood starved and brittle. 

I spit the peg out of my mouth. It hangs from my chin-strap by a greasy thong, slick with my spit, which freezes into a cloudy rime almost before it hits the air. My lips are numb, smeared with black pitch and browser-fat to keep them from cracking open in the wind. Behind my mask, in the narrow, char-blackened darkness around my eyes, my tears are warm, though my eyelashes are stiff with frost where they brush the viewing-slits.

Through the razor-cuts in the wood, the light is a physical blow. Even narrowed down to two threads of white, the glare off the Under-Rib makes the back of my skull throb. The bark here is stretched tight as a drumhead, thinned out by the growth-surge until it’s translucent as scraped bladder-skin. Beneath it, a vein the width of a house is glowing with that fierce, sour-milk light. It casts no shadows. I have no shadow. I am just a dark smudge of grease and scales hanging in a bowl of white fire.

I reach up with my right hand, my three middle fingers capped in curved iron-burl claws, and tap the main suspension line between my knees. 

*Click-click. Pause. Click.* 
*Still holding. The deep pipe is fat.*

Five seconds later, the rope twitches against my thigh. The answer comes down through three hundred feet of taut vine, faint as a flea-bite against my gloved knuckles.

*Click-click.* 
*Heard. Keep hanging.*

That’s my sister’s hand. She taps heavy with her thumb-claw because she lost her index finger to a frost-rot three winters back. 

I tuck my chin back down into the smelly, warm collar of my mantle, breathing in my own stink—rancid tallow, sour sweat, and the sharp, nose-bleeding tang of raw turpentine from the fresh-cut wood wedges hanging at my belt. I am cold, down to the marrow of my crooked shin-bones, where the old break always aches when the sap-pressure rises. To keep the blood moving, I do what every Plumb does: I work my hands inside my cloak, braiding and unbraiding a six-foot tail of fine browser-sinew by feel alone, my calloused thumbs moving over the greasy strands in the dark while I swing slowly in a forty-foot arc across the belly of the world.

Then, my jawbone catches it.

I don’t even have the peg in my mouth, just my forehead resting against the taut suspension rope, and the vibration comes down the line from the anchor-augers buried in the ridge above me. 

It isn’t a *thump*. It’s a *zing*—high, clean, and sharp as a plucked tendon. 

I drop my sinew-braid, haul myself closer to the wall by the guide-line tied to a bark-scale, and jam the listening-peg back into the pore. I bite down hard enough to taste blood around my gums.

*Zing.* 

There it is again. And behind it, not fifty feet above my head—right between me and the pods—the wet leather creak stops dead. 

In the Hollow, when a sound stops, you are already dying. Wood that groans is wood that is bending. Wood that goes quiet has locked its grain against itself, building up thousands of tons of sheer, frozen stubbornness until it decides not to be a bridge anymore and becomes a bomb instead.

Through my teeth, the silence inside the rib is huge and hollow, like a breath sucked in and held. And around the edges of that quiet, tiny and fast as spiders running across dry leaves: *tick-tick-tick-tink-tick.*

Delamination. A slab of the outer belly, maybe two hundred yards wide and thirty feet thick, is freezing faster than the hot vein behind it. It’s shrinking. It’s pulling away from the wet cambium like a scab peeling off a knuckle. And our five pods are lashed right to the lip of the scab.

I don't waste time tapping a message. If I tap *shearing*, they’ll spend two minutes trying to haul me up on the windlass, and in two minutes the windlass won't have a cliff attached to it. 

Instead, I reach down to my hip, pull my heavy splitting-wedge made of fire-hardened black burl, and strike the suspension rope three times with the flat of the butt. Not clicks. Great, shuddering *whacks* that send a violent wave snaking up the three-hundred-foot line.

*Cut loose. Cut loose. Cut loose.*

Up in the glare above me, I see a tiny dark speck detach from the lip of the callus-ridge—a dropped sap-bucket, tumbling end over end, falling past me fifty yards out, spilling a ribbon of glowing white slush that freezes into a glittering comet-tail before it disappears into the abyss. They heard me. They’re dropping weight. They’re scrambling out of the baskets with their claw-thimbles hooked into the ceiling, scurrying along the safety-tethers toward the main trunk-column half a mile back.

Nobody is at the windlass to haul the Plumb up. That’s the deal you make when they grease you and put you in the double-thick coat. You get the best cuts of the warm, sugary heart-cambium brought down to you in a bag; you never have to walk the splinter-fields on your crooked knee; and when the scab peels, the rope gets cut from the top.

I don't wait for them to slice my line. If three hundred feet of heavy whip-vine falls on top of me while I’m clinging to the wall, the weight of the tail will rip my claws right out of the bark.

I jam the toe-splits of my boots into a vertical crack that smells fiercely of crushed green cucumber, hook my left iron-burl claws into a curl of frost-rimmed lichen, and take my horn-wood saw-knife to my own waist-tether. 

The braided vine is under my weight; when the teeth of the knife bite through the first two strands, the third snaps with a report like a cracking whip. The long rope shoots upward thirty feet, coiling like a struck snake in the freezing updraft, while I drop four inches before my left hand takes the whole drag of my body and my heavy scale-cloak. My shoulder socket pops, a bright red flash of pain behind my mask-slits.

Above me, the wood lets go of its breath.

It doesn't roar. The air is too thin for a roar. It makes a sound like a giant tearing a sheet of frozen canvas five miles wide—*KRRRR-SSSHH*—and the wall six inches from my nose jumps outward.

The shockwave hits my chest like a swung beam, knocking the wind out of my lungs in a white puff that instantly frosts the inside of my wooden mask. For two seconds, the world has no up and no down; the bark I am clinging to is vibrating so fast at such a high pitch that my iron-burl claws skitter and smoke in the wood-fissures, losing their bite, buzzing against my finger-bones until my hands go numb.

Fifty feet above me, the callus-ridge explodes outward in a slow-motion blossom of white. 

I flatten myself like a tick, tucking my head under my heavy horn-wood collar, pulling my knees into my ribs, wedging my hip-auger into the crack and twisting it half a turn with both hands until the spiral threads groan into the grain. 

The sky falls past my back. 

Slabs of pale, glowing timber the size of whales go tumbling down into the four-mile dark, turning lazily in the thin air. A ruptured sap-artery vomits a geyser of blinding, boiling white liquid straight out into the void; before it falls a hundred yards, the abyssal cold turns it into a blizzard of stinging, actinic diamond-dust that swirls back up on the thermal, burying me in glowing snow. One of the woven village pods goes sailing past me, twenty yards out in the empty air, its cut ropes trailing behind it like the legs of a dead spider, the little bowl of green bleed-wood still tumbling around inside the wicker, blinking through the weave as it drops into the Slough.

And then comes the heat. 

The freshly torn face of the mega-root above me is raw, wet vascular meat exposed to the freezing void. A wave of wet, green-smelling steam washes down over my cloak, thawing the rime on my mask, smelling so strongly of sugar and ozone and turpentine that it makes my stomach cramp with hunger. 

For ten minutes, I don't move. I hang by one auger and four clawed fingers in the blinding white blizzard of frozen sap, listening with my forehead pressed directly against the trembling bark. 

The *dry-rice* sound is gone. The scab is off. The great root is groaning again—a deep, wet, satisfied *uuuuungh* that vibrates through my ribs as the remaining three miles of wood settle into their new shape, already pushing hot, sticky resin out of a million severed pores to glass over the wound.

Slowly, my shoulder screaming, I unhook my right hand. I pull my second auger from my belt. I reach up eight inches, find a seam in the shaking, glowing wall, and twist the black wood in until it bites. I haul my crooked legs up, wedge my pitch-sticky boot-toes into a fresh splinter-notch, and reach down to unscrew the lower pin.

Three hundred feet up, past the smoking, glowing cliff of raw meat where the ridge used to be, my kin will be waiting on the main trunk, huddled together under their scale-mantles like a patch of dark moss. If I can climb around the edge of the wet resin before it freezes into slick glass, they’ll drop me a line. If I can't, the healing bark will swell over my boots by morning and swallow me into a burl.

I spit the frost out of my mouth-slit, hook my iron-burl claws into the blinding light, and start to scuttle up the grain.
