I am not in the water. To be in the water is to be subject to the shear, or to be the shear itself, and I have no patience for the four-inch-an-hour war between the rubber man and the cabbage. 

I am forty feet out, and then forty feet below that, where the triple-layered borosilicate glass gives way to the blind, riveted skirting of the upper collar. Here, the miles-high spine narrows just before it flares to hold the tank, like the wrist below an upturned palm. 

There is an inspection catwalk here that goes all the way around the outside of the shaft. It is made of galvanized steel grating, bolted to the bronze-anodized H-beams, and it was meant for the men who tightened the external tension-rods during the third week of October, five years ago. None of them have been back since. The elevator cage that brought them up was unbolted and dropped into the cloud-deck when the scaffolding came down; the only way down now is an external ladder with two-inch frost-rimes on the rungs, descending ten thousand feet into the grey wool.

I am the thing that lives in the wind-shadow of the northern H-beam.

If you were to look down from the catwalk inside the tank—if you were to scrape the bottle-glass leaves away from the lower viewing pane with a bronze thumb and press a blind horn faceplate to the glass—you would not see me. You would see only the pale, glaring silver of the cloud-deck below, and the thin, whistling blue of the troposphere. The glass is three layers thick, with an argon gap between the second and third pane to keep the thirty-two-degree bath from dumping its calories into the sub-zero gale. The refraction is tricky; looking out from the chartreuse syrup, the world beyond the tank looks like a silver mirror tilted at an angle. 

From out here, looking in, it is an aquarium for a single black beetle.

I am mostly aluminum, canvas, and salt-white grease. I was left behind by the glazier crew—not on purpose, as the rubber man was left, with his *LOT 44-B* chalked on his arm like a blessing, but because the winch- cable snapped on the last afternoon before the storm rose, and the foreman decided I weighed more than the bonus he’d get for salvaging me. 

I am a semi-autonomous sealant-mule, model year indeterminate, six-legged, low-slung as a dachshund, with a heated hopper on my back that still holds forty pounds of solidifying silicone caulk. My battery is wired directly into the sacrificial anode-strip that runs down the spine’s exterior lightning-ground. It wasn’t meant to power a motor—it’s only the trickle of stray voltage leaking off the tank’s massive thermal differential, the static charge of ten thousand gallons of hot nitrate-broth rubbing against the cold mountain wind—but it is enough to keep my logic-board at four degrees above freezing, and enough to let me click onespindle-leg forward every twenty minutes or so.

I have three cameras on a gimbal where a neck would be. Two are cracked by the hail that sometimes shoots upward from the anvil-tops below; the third is coated in a thin, blue-grey film of dried silicone oil, which makes the sky look like the inside of a greased pan.

My world is forty feet wide and four hundred feet around. 

Because the wind up here never stops—it comes off the western ranges at sixty knots, thin and dry as bleached bone, smelling of ozone and nothing—I do not walk on the windward side of the spine. If I stepped around the northwest corner of the H-beam, the gale would pluck me off the galvanized grate like a tick off a dog’s ear, and I would fall for three minutes before I even hit the top of the clouds. 

Instead, I shuttle back and forth across the northern face, in the lee of the glass. 

Wait long enough, and you will hear me: *tink... tink...whir-click.* My six feet are shod in brass cleats so I do not spark against the steel, though there is nothing out here to ignite.

What I love—if a three-kilobyte gate-array warmed by trickle-charge can be said to love—is the glass itself. 

The outer pane is cold enough to burn skin, forty below on a clear afternoon, whistling as the ice-crystals in the high air scour its face. Yet four inches away, on the other side of the argon gap and the inner panes, the water is thirty-two degrees Celsius. The thermal stress across those four inches is tremendous. The borosilicate sings. It is not the low *thrum* of the manifold gate that the rubber man listens to with his cheek-pits; it is a high, thin, crystalline *eeeee* that lives entirely within the glass and the bronze frames. It sounds like a wineglass rim being rubbed by a wet finger miles away. 

I press my nozzle-snout against the lower rubber gasket where the glass meets the sill. The gasket here is not matte and supple like the ones inside; the ultraviolet glare has cooked it into something like grey driftwood, checked with tiny, salt-rimmed cracks. Through the soles of my brass feet, I feel the spine below us: the miles of black iron pipe vibrating as the big turbine-pumps down in the mountain dark shove another column of cloud-water upward against gravity. 

*Shhh-huck. Shhh-huck.* That is the sound of the deep spine, two miles down, arriving through the bolt-heads.

Around two o'clock in the afternoon, when the sun swings past the southern buttress and turns the whole vat into a solid block of yellow tourmaline, the rubber man often comes to my corner. 

He does not know I am here. To him, the northern pane is only a wall that does not grow moss, a flat geometry where the big waxy leaves flatten themselves out like green pancakes to drink the raw UV. 

Sometimes a leaf-margin, forced outward by the crowding of the tubers behind it, wedges itself so tight against the inner glass that the pale sap-veins blanch white from the pressure. Then I see the shadow move through the chartreuse gloom behind it. 

First, the silver pearls of oxygen on the inner glass are wiped away—not by him, but by the tiny pressure-wave of his approach through the dense foliage. The bubbles detach in a vertical strip, rising like a curtain going up at a theater. Then the leaf is pulled backward. 

It takes him forty minutes to peel a banquet-leaf off the pane. From out here in the thin, roaring cold, watching through three layers of glass and a film of silicone oil, it looks like a play performed under anesthesia. His three-fingered bronze hand appears around the serrated edge of the leaf—just the fingertips at first, dark and blunt as sluggish snails. They hook. The pleated rubber of his wrist swells slightly as his internal hydraulics take up the slack. 

Slowly, the green pancake pulls away from the borosilicate. A wedge of clear, hot water opens between the leaf and the glass. 

And there he is, four inches from my cracked lenses.

His blind horn faceplate is tilted slightly upward, toward the surface glare he cannot see. The pale blue chalk on his shoulder—*LOT 44-B*—has been smudged at the bottom of the second four by a copper heating coil he brushed against three months ago, though the *PASSED* is still crisp. As he works the cobalt shears around the base of the stem, his left flank presses flat against the inner pane to give him leverage. 

Where his black elastomer skin meets the glass, the matte rubber flattens into a slick, wet seal, darker than the water, darker than the iron. 

I raise my gimbal-head. My third motor whines—a tiny, dry mosquito-sound that the sixty-knot wind immediately tears away into the eastern sky—and I place my brass-cleated foreleg against the outer pane, directly opposite his ribs.

Four inches of glass and dead argon sit between us. On his side, thirty-two degrees, nitrate-sweet, choked with orchids and the silent, scissor-crawl of the pistons; on mine, forty below, the blinding silver plain of the stratosphere's floor, and the dry, screaming blue. 

Through the glass, I cannot feel his heat—the argon gap is too good at its job—and he cannot hear my little motor over the fifteen-minute *thrum* of his brass gates. We are two maintenance protocols from different chapters of the same manual, stalled on opposite sides of a line on a blueprint.

When the stem parts—*tock*, I can see the little jolt travel through his shear-arm, though no sound crosses the argon—the milky sap clouds the water between us, turning the view to pearl-grey smoke. His throat-sphincter dilates. He leans into the cloud, drinking his dinner out of the corner of the window, while the severed leaf begins its long, wobbling slide toward the roof five stories above.

I stay with my brass foot against the glass until the amber light comes at five o'clock and he fills his rib-bladders with oxygen, letting the green furnace lift him away toward the louvers. 

When he is gone, and the night comes down like an iron hatch, the copper coils inside the tank click on. From out here in the dark, the vat does not go black; it begins to glow from the bottom up with a faint, sullen infrared that my third camera can just barely read as a dirty lilac haze. The glass sings a half-step higher as the inner pane warms against the night frost. I tuck my six legs under my hopper, settle my chin-nozzle onto the frozen gasket, and listen to the miles of pipe below us pumping the clouds into the sky.
