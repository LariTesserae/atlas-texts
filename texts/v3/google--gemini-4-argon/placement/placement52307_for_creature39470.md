I am not the walker in the storm. I am the quiet in the corner of the hut before the walking begins.

Find the third cairn east of the great green tower, the one wedged against a low lip of basalt where the current divides and leaves a pocket of dead, still water. Look past the doorway’s threshold of stacked slate, into the back where the cold is absolute and motionless. 

I am the spare frame. I have been soaking here, pinned under four flat river-stones, for thirty-eight years. 

Right-now, I have no moss in my chest, so I am completely dark. I have no reed-bundle lashed to my neck-stump, so I do not hear the valley as a picture; I only feel it through the rock floor as a long, rhythmic shudder that passes through my wooden ribs every few minutes and then dies away.

I am unfinished, and because I am unfinished, I am the only thing in this valley that gets to rest while still being whole.

My left shoulder is the pride of my making. It was bent from a single, green shoot of bladder-pine forty winters ago, steamed in the friction-heat of a composting chalk-weed heap, and then forced into a cleft in the basalt until it cured into a black, rubbery curve as hard as bog-oak. My right hip is older—salvaged from an Orcharder named only by the three notched scars on its femur, whose chest was stoved in when an ice-calving up on the unseen peaks sent a boulder bowling down the central trough. When the others unlashed that one, they found the hip-ratchet was still crisp, its teeth unworn, so they carried it here and bound it into my pelvis with fresh, milky kelp-sinew.

In the years since, that sinew has cured to iron. It has shrunk as it blackened, pulling my joints together with an immense, silent tightness.

That is what I am: a history of tensions waiting for a current.

You might think it is lonely, lying pinned under ballast-stones in a dark stone beehive while the world outside roars and clicks and flashes mint-green. It is not. A plant does not experience time as a room it is locked inside; it experiences time as a slow thickening of the wall. Every year, the freezing mineral water seeps a little deeper into the microscopic pores of my timber, replacing the old sap-channels with silica and lime. I am becoming stone that remembers how to bend.

Every twelve days or so, when the surge outside is weak and the valley goes quiet save for the distant *crack* of a lonely bone-kelp flinging spores into the gloom, the doorway darkens. 

One of the walking ones backs in. 

It has to stoop, its slate-plated shoulders scraping the dry-stone lintel with a sound like a dry grindstone. It wedges its wide, root-knotted feet into the corners of the floor, locks its knee-ratchets—*clack, clack*—and lets the last of the water drain from its back-valves. The mint-green fire in its ribs dims as the friction stops, fading from a bright, cold flare to a dull, sea-glass smolder, and then to nothing.

For three hours, while the basin above us lies still under its ceiling of freezing cloud, the walker sleeps standing up, three feet from where I lie.

We do not speak, of course. Even if I had a voice-valve, there is no pressure to drive it. Instead, the walker reaches down in the dark with one split, burred hand-clamp and takes hold of my left ankle. Using the very last of the torsion stored in its own abdominal springs, it gives my leg a single, slow, tremendous pull, drawing my knee-joint open against the coiled resistance of my fresh bone-kelp tendons. 

*Creak... clack.* 

It holds my leg taut for a minute, feeling the stiffness of the cure through its wooden fingers, making sure the wood hasn't warped or gone brittle in the cold. Then it releases the catch, and my leg snaps back against my chest, hitting the basalt floor with a dull *thud* that sends a tiny puff of green light flaring from the sleeper’s chest—just enough to show me the curve of my own black ribs and the pale flint knife already lashed and waiting at my hip.

Sometimes, before the next surge comes to wake it, the walker will spend its remaining strength tucking a fresh twist of pale ribbon-fern behind my collarbone, or tightening a lashing around my wrist-clamp that has worked a fraction of a millimeter loose in the bass-vibrations of the pines.

I know which walker it is by the rhythm of its maintenance. This one has a slight catch in its right elbow—a hairline split in the bone-kelp actuator that makes it pause for half a second before the spring releases. 

It is getting old. The silica is taking its joints too far; they are becoming too much stone and not enough wood.

In another two years, perhaps three, a great meltwater storm will come tearing down from the unseen ring of peaks. The pressure will drop so hard the whole basin will seem to lift an inch off its bed. The central tower will blaze like a green sun in the bruised indigo dark, and the pines will hum so loud the slate shingles on this roof will chatter like teeth.

My maintainer will go out into that glorious, screaming flood, leaning forty-five degrees into the wall of freezing water, winding its springs to the very last notch to haul a tangled mat of bladder-spires clear of the main channel. And somewhere out there along the taut kelp-ropes, that right elbow will finally shear through. The flint knife will drop into the dark. A flying pine-bladder, fifty pounds of buoyant force snapping on its tether, will catch the walker under the chin-reeds and tip it backward, breaking its forty-five-degree lean.

The current will take it then, tumbling it over the smooth, whale-backed basalt ridges until its slate armor shatters and its wooden ribs come unlashed.
