I would not be on the roofs. The roofs are too bright, lost in that blinding, shadowless smear of cloud-belly where the Sluice-Maws bicker and the guano slides like wet chalk. I have no desire to look out over an horizon that only repeats what is already under my feet. 

Instead, find me thirty feet down, in the warm, sweating dark of a Secondary Settling Gallery, where the masonry is five courses thick and the light comes only from the orange, tallow-fedwick lanterns we hang from the tie-rod stars.

I am what my Gang calls a **Sounder**, though I am not yet old enough to be a true Listener with a crab-claw horn and a place on the Harmonic Floors. My knees still bend without grinding; my lungs are still clear enough of the grey mist to hold a four-stroke breath under the dark water. My job is the middle depth—the wet, warm arteries where the surface sluices drop their loads into the great subterranean return-mains.

If you saw me resting against a warm bronze valve-casing, you would think I was a pile of oil-soaked rags and rusted plates left behind by a repair crew a century ago. My quill-coat is heavy with forty years of boiled paraffin and coal-dust, stiff as oak bark, smelling sharply of mutton-tallow and hot copper. My shoulders are armoured in the broad, rust-red carapaces of ditch-crabs, stitched together with braided telegraph wire that has turned green with verdigris. 

I am wider than I am tall, it feels, and when I move along the narrow stone ledges above the surging brown water of the main, I do not step. I skate. My ironwood clogs have worn their own groove into this specific ledge—a shallow, slick trough in the basalt that fits the rocker of my soles the way a bearing fits its housing.

Look at my hands. The nails are gone, replaced by thick, horn-colored calluses from decades of tapping against iron and prying at wet lime. The skin between my fingers is slightly webbed with pale scar-tissue from the nipping of blind cistern-eels and the acid-burn of sulfur vents. In my right hand, almost grown into the palm, is my **spud-wrench**—three pounds of cast Admiralty bronze, worn bright and smooth where my thumb rests, heavy enough to crack the shell of an encroaching crab or ring a warning note down two miles of four-inch steam-pipe.

***

My world is not visual; it is a landscape of pressures and viscosities. 

Right now, I am leaning my forehead against the curved, sweating flank of the **Old Sow**—the twelve-foot cast-iron intake main that drains the Fourth Tallow-Basin above us. My eyes are closed. Through the leather pad I wear strapped to my brow, I am reading the district.

*Thrum... ... ... thud.*

That is the Deep Stroke. The great piston three hundred yards down in the foundational slag. It is the floor of my mind. Since the moment I was pulled from between my mother’s greased thighs in the warm vault behind the condenser, that ten-second interval has been the measure of all things. A pot of filter-meat takes two hundred strokes to boil. A man can hold his breath under the tallow-scum for six strokes; seven if he is young. A good sleep is three thousand strokes long.

Over the Deep Stroke, I feel the *whir-click-whir* of the overhead gantry crane passing three levels up. It is carrying a full ladle of silicate today; I can tell by the way the eastern retaining wall groans slightly in B-flat as the weight shifts onto the third pier. 

And beneath my brow, inside the Old Sow, there is a soft, rhythmic *flup-flup-shhh*. 

A Lard-Berg has torn loose from the upper settling tank and is lodging itself against the bronze grating of the secondary intake. The water pressure behind it is rising; the pitch of the pipe is climbing from a contented, gurgling low C to a strained, tight D-sharp. Left alone for five hundred strokes, the grating will blind over completely. The warm water will back up into the street-sluices above. The crabs will drown in the sudden rise; the overflow will flood our sleeping vault; the boilers below will starve for return-water, knock, overheat, and crack their jackets.

I don't sigh—sighing wastes warm air. I bring my bronze wrench back and strike the rivet-head nearest my ear.

*Tink-tink. Tink-tink-tink. Pause. Tink.*

*Blockage. Grate Four. Bring the long saws.*

Three hundred yards away, in the warm hollow inside the bridge-pier, my brother’s wife will feel that rhythm through the soles of her clogs as she turns the smoking-meat over the flue. A moment later, the iron pipe under my hand answers back, a faint, metallic Morse shuddering through the rust:

*Tink-tink. Coming.*

***

While I wait for the young ones to shuffle down the spiral trough-stairs with the grease-buckets and the ten-foot whalebone saws, I unhook my lantern and shuffle fifty paces down the gallery to the **Weeping Wall**.

This is my secret place, though in a Gang of nineteen people who sleep piled together like salamanders for warmth, nothing is truly secret. It is a spot where the ancient slag-concrete has bulged outward between four massive iron star-plates, and the warm water seeping through from the refinery floor above has deposited a curtain of mineral lime that looks like frozen yellow fat.

Here, the vibration of the Deep Stroke and the high-pitched whine of a nearby turbo-generator cross each other. They create a dead spot—a pocket of strange, hollow resonance where the roar of the plateau cancels itself out just enough for you to hear the water dripping.

I reach out with my thick, scarred fingers and touch the wet lime. 

I am forty-two years old—very nearly an elder. The damp is beginning to settle into my hips, making my shuffle slower than it was last winter (though we only know it is winter because the mist from the roof-vents sinks lower into the streets and smells more of coal-gas than feathers). Soon, within a few hundred thousand strokes, I will not be able to strip down, smear myself in warm axle-lard, and drop through the manhole into the blind, fleshy dark of the intake pipe to saw away the polyps. 

Instead, I will sit in the Vault with a hollowed-out crab claw pressed to my ear, and the young ones will bring me the richest curds from the relief-valves, and they will lay their jawbones against my knuckles so I can tap out the stories of the Great Slump of the Sixtieth Pier, or teach them which rivets to hit when the third boiler begins to wheeze.

I do not wish for green things; I do not know what green is, save for the poisonous copper-rot on old wires. I do not wish for a blue sky; the one time the clouds parted for twenty strokes when I was a child, the vast, empty, dark-blue hole above us terrified the entire district so badly that the birds stopped croaking and the Gangs hid in the cisterns until the blessed, warm white fog rolled back in to roof the world again.

From down the gallery comes the slow, rhythmic *scuff-slap, scuff-slap* of ironwood clogs on wet stone, and the smell of fresh, hot tallow from the grease-buckets. My nephews are coming. Their silhouettes appear in the amber mist—round, shiny, hunched, their crab-shell mantles glistening, their long saws resting on their shoulders like the antennae of giant insects.

The oldest boy stops beside me. He doesn't speak into the eighty-decibel roar. Instead, he leans his heavy, grease-slicked shoulder against mine, and reaches up to lay two cold, wet fingers against my throat, right over the pulse.

*Tap-tap-tap,* go his fingers against my skin. *How thick?*

I hum a low note in my chest, matching the strained D-sharp of the clogged pipe beside us, so he can feel the tension of the water in his fingertips. Then I raise my thumb and tap his collarbone twice.

*Two spans. Soft meat. Easy cutting.*

He nods once, his eyes small and bright in a face the color of wet dough. Together, we turn to the iron manhole latches, waiting for the floor to rise beneath our feet.

*Thrum... ... ... thud.*

On the *thud*, we throw our weight against the bronze wrenches, and the seal breaks with a long, warm sigh of steam.
