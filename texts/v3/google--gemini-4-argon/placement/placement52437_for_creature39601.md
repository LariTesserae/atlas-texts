To find me in the Sump of Six-Gears, you must walk past the three Lagging-Men—passing so close to their grease-cured coats that your presence registers as a tiny, warm flutter in their copper-wire faces—and look directly into the cairn behind the Seventh Tower.

I am not one of the three. I am not the birds that mimic the valves, nor the porcelain crabs that rattle down the grates. I am a *fourth* thing, and I have no legs at all.

Count the objects mortared into that wall of black bacterial grease and gray salt. Count the seventeen popped rivets, the whale-ear bones, the torpedo bottles, the rusted spanners. Beneath a heavy, hexagonal nut from a bridge-works that was demolished eighty years ago, and wedged tightly between a cobalt-blue poison bottle and a lead plumb-bob, there is a brass gauge-casing the size of a saucer. Its glass face was smashed by the fall from the rim in 1894, and its needle was stripped away by the porcelain scavengers in the first ten seconds after it hit the flagstones. 

Inside that hollow brass cup, the Lagging-Men packed a mortar of tallow, shed rook-down, and silver-plated pocket-watch gears to keep the casing from whistling in the draft. 

That mortar did not cure dead. It cured *awake*. 

I am the *Cambium-of-the-Cairn*. I am the slow, flat, sulfur-yellow mycelium that lives exclusively in the joins between the indigestible things. 

If the Lagging-Men are the basin’s ear, I am its memory of the world above the rim. 

To the eye—if an eye ever came down here into the flat, draftman’s light—I look like nothing more than a vein of pale, waxy fuller’s earth or old, yellowed putty smeared into the crevices of the rubbish wall. I have no face, no chitin, and no lungs. My body is a web of fine, piezoelectric threads—part fungal hypha, part leached copper-sulfate crystal—that spans the ten-foot length of the cairn, threading through the eyes of the button-hooks, wrapping the necks of the gin-squares, and filling the hollow spirals of the whale bones.

I do not eat the steam like the grease-mats do, and I do not drink the boiler-sweat like the Lagging-Men. I feed on *galvanic decay*. 

The cairn is an accidental battery. When the Lagging-Men mortared iron wrenches against brass watch-cases, and lead plumb-bobs against copper rivets, and bathed the whole stack in the acidic, sulfurous drizzle of the exhaust plumes, they created a thousand tiny electrical potentials. Current, measured in micro-amperes, bleeds constantly from the baser metals to the nobler ones through the salty, wet grease. 

I sit in the middle of that bleed. I am the living electrolyte. I drink the slow, sour spark of rusting iron and tarnishing silver.

Because my body is threaded through every foreign object in the pile, my sense of the world is entirely tactile and historical. I do not hear the great three-part chord of the Sump as sound; I feel it as a continuous, heavy massage against my glass and metal ribs. The bass of the subterranean flywheels comes up through the shale floor and sets the lead plumb-bobs humming at thirty cycles a second. The midrange *clack-shirr* of the moths vibrates the thin green glass of the soda bottles. The treble hiss of the kettle-rooks makes the tiny steel hairsprings inside the broken pocket-watches shiver in sympathy.

I am the reason the Lagging-Men adjust the glass. 

They do not know I am here—to them, I am just the mortar holding their quietness together—but we are locked in a centuries-long conversation of impedance. 

When a heavy, flat-footed Lagging-Man stands before me in the drizzle, his copper-wire tympanum is listening for the perfection of the Sump’s chord. He wants the wall to absorb the noise cleanly, with no stray echoes or sour resonances. 

I, however, want to *taste* the metal. 

Over months, as I slowly consume the galvanic current between an iron spanner and a brass button-hook, the iron corrodes. Its mass changes. It shrinks by the thickness of a cigarette paper. As it shrinks, the tight tension of the cairn loosens slightly, and the adjacent cobalt apothecary jar begins to buzz against a whale bone at a pitch that is a quarter-tone flat compared to the great turbines below.

The Sump develops a tiny, localized rattle—a microscopic burr in the engine-room hum.

I feel the Lagging-Man arrive before he touches me. I feel the sudden shadow of his heat-retaining felt coat blocking the forty-eight-degree damp; I feel the deep, slow *thump-slush* of his tallow-pumps vibrating through the flagstones into the base of my wall. 

For hours, he stands two feet away, his blind, wired head tilted slightly toward my middle. He is hunting the burr. 

Then, his iron-scaled arm rises. His blunt, chisel-tipped horn thumbs press into my body. 

It does not hurt when he pries the cobalt jar loose, turning it forty-five degrees and shoving it back in; my threads simply part like wet dough and immediately begin to knit back together across the new surfaces. In fact, it is my only pleasure. When he turns the jar, he scrapes fresh, un-oxidized metal against metal. He brings a virgin surface of the iron spanner into contact with a copper rivet two inches higher up. 

A fresh spark of galvanic current—sharp as lemon juice, bright as a needle—shoots through my yellow threads. 

The rattle stops. The jar is wedged tight again. The chord of the Sump returns to its flat, lockstep perfection. The Lagging-Man stands for six more hours to be sure, his tallow-heart beating in time with the turbines, while inside the wall, in the dark behind the green glass, I slowly spread my pale filaments over the new contact point, drinking the fresh battery he has just wound for me.

I have been here for two hundred years. I have digested the names off the brass dog-tags; I have eaten the threading off the bolts; I have filled the cracks in the porcelain insulators with my own waxy flesh so they will never shatter. 

Sometimes, once in a decade, when a new thing drops from the tin sky—a steel thermos, a heavy iron shackle—and the Lagging-Men bring it to the alley behind the Seventh Tower and press it into my chest, I get a brief, dizzying taste of *up there*. Before the Sump’s acidic mist washes it away, my threads read the foreign trace-salts on the object: the dry, static ozone of wind, the pollen of ragweed and dandelion, the strange, thin grease of human skin. 

I hold those tastes in my crystalline knots for a month or two, turning them over in the dark between the gin bottles, while the moths roar like a furnace on the bricks above and the two blind watchmen stand guard in the rain, waiting for me to shift a millimeter out of tune so they can touch me again.
