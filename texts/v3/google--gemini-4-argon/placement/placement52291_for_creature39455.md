I would not be a moving thing here. In a place where even the walking creature is only a slow reflex of wood and stone, having legs feels like an unnecessary complication—too much effort spent just staying upright. 

Instead, I would find myself halfway up the western wall of the basin, where the main drive-cable from the central flywheel passes through a heavy cast-iron fairlead anchored into the bifurcation of a two-hundred-year-old banyan. 

At first glance, I am just the *slump*—a four-foot-long, teardrop-shaped bolster of amber-colored resin and grey bark that has oozed out of the tree’s fork to swallow the iron bracket, cushioning the spot where the steel cable bends at a twelve-degree angle toward the upper looms.

If you pressed a thumbnail into my surface, I wouldn't feel like wood or stone. I have the dense, yielding stiffness of an old carriage tire or a whale’s baleen. I am a *tension-callus*—a symbiotic marriage between the tree’s healing scar-tissue and a very old, cold-water cousin of the bracket fungus that arrived on the boots of the quarrymen three centuries ago and forgot how to make spores when it found something better to do.

My entire existence is organized around the *line*.

Through the center of my body runs a porcelain-smooth bore, lined with a living layer of self-lubricating, oil-rich cartilage that I secrete continuously from the minerals the banyan feeds me. Through this throat slides the main drive-cable—an endless, braided loop of iron wire and cured vine-fiber, thick as a child’s arm, moving at a steady four feet per second.

I do not have eyes to see the yellow-green studio light of the nave, nor ears to hear the bottle-sigh of the galls overhead. My world is a continuous, linear landscape that passes directly through my center, twenty-four hours a day, at four feet per second. I know the entire quarter-mile loop of that cable the way a blind man knows the curb of his own street, measured not in yards, but in textures and intervals of time.

Every three minutes and twelve seconds, the *Long Splice* passes through me—a six-foot section where the Gauge-Keeper’s predecessors wove fresh vine-tendrils into a rust-thinned patch of wire eighty years ago. It feels like a coarse, braided scar, slightly warmer than the bare iron, and as it slides through my throat, I reflexively squeeze a fraction tighter, wiping a thin film of tallow-sap into its crevices so the fibers won't fray on the upper sheaves.

Forty seconds after the Long Splice comes the *Cold Spot*—a yard of cable that always hangs directly over an exhaust vent during the flywheel’s pause-stroke, arriving in my core damp with mineral mist and tasting faintly of deep limestone and crushed fern. I drink that moisture as it passes, drawing the lime into my outer rind to keep my casing hard against the pull of the weight.

I am the basin’s memory of *load*.

Because the cable bends twelve degrees inside me, every shift in the town’s work pushes sideways against my ribs. I can feel the whole quarry in that sideways lean:
*   There is the heavy, rhythmic *thrum-thrum* of the central flywheel, thirty times a minute, a steady bass note that keeps my cartilage warm.
*   Superimposed over that is the sharper, metallic *tick* when one of the vertical looms in the brick houses drops a shuttle or snags a knot—a tiny spike of tension that makes the cable stiffen for half a second inside me.
*   And every four minutes, there is the long, slow, satisfying *drag* as the empty ore-bucket crests the mid-span sag overhead, pulling the cable up against the roof of my bore like a heavy sigh.

My work is small, slow, and structural. Left to itself, a steel cable sawing through an iron fairlead for three centuries would have cut through the bracket, sliced into the banyan’s trunk, and dropped the whole eastern gantry into the moss. 

Instead, I grow *into* the wear. 

Where the cable pulls hardest—on the upper-left quadrant of my throat—my fungal mycelium senses the piezoelectrical stress of the compressed cells. I direct the banyan’s heavy, silica-rich sap to that exact millimeter, laying down fresh, tough horn faster than the passing wire can file it away. When the summer light above the canopy is strong and the turgor-engines run fast and hard, pulling the cable taut as a fiddle string, I stiffen into something almost like vulcanized horn, holding the angle true. When the winter chill slows the sap and the cable goes slack and heavy, I soften, swelling inward to cradle the sagging line so it doesn't slap against the banyan’s bark.

Once every four days, the vibration in the tree changes. Before the cable tells me anything, the wood of the banyan’s trunk transmits the slow, rhythmic *sponge-step, sponge-step, pause* of the Gauge-Keeper crossing the court below.

When it reaches the base of my tree, it doesn't climb. It stands in the trough twenty feet below me and raises its long, articulated brass loom-arm until the iron calipers gently tap the underside of my resinous belly—*clink, clink*.

It is checking my density. If I have grown too hard and brittle from a dry month, the calipers ring clear; if I am healthy and elastic, they make a dull, wooden *thud*. 

Then, one of the Keeper’s long, whip-like root-hairs snakes up the fluted bark of the banyan and slips into the small lip at the bottom of my bore, where the dark, sheared paste of worn iron-filings, dead grease-worms, and old sap collects like resin at the corner of an eye. The root-hair curls through the paste, tasting it for raw brass or fresh splinters—the signs that a pulley upstream is failing. Finding only the smooth, black butter of normal wear, the tendril withdraws, wiping the excess paste onto the bark where the blind wasps’ fungus will eat it by morning.

The Keeper pauses for a minute, its broken pressure gauge tilted toward the yellow canopy, listening to the cable slide through me without a squeak or a chatter. Then the sponge-steps recede across the cork-sedge floor.

I remain in the fork of the wood, warm at the center and cool at the rind, feeling the *Long Splice* arrive right on time, followed forty seconds later by the *Cold Spot*, holding the twelve-degree bend in the dark so the empty buckets can keep crossing the sky.
