I am sixty-two nodes today, though three of them are currently four miles to my left while remaining an inch and a half below my lowest anchor. 

To have a body that is mostly interval is to experience the world not as a place you occupy, but as a tension you maintain. If you had a spine, you would feel the cold as a shiver; I feel it as a tightening of the silk along the Singing Causeway, a sharp, metallic *plink* in my third-to-last memory-cluster as the slate contracts and the quartz grains in the wall are squeezed into giving up a tiny, sour spark of current. I drink the spark. It tastes of old iron and the weight of the mountain above me.

I am very old, though not as old as the neighbor who shares the lower buttresses with me—the one whose threads have grown so thick with alkaline crust over the centuries that they look like ribs of the stone itself. We do not speak, not in any way that uses air, because air is a liar here. It drops things. It lets sounds fall out of its pockets before they have traveled ten paces. Instead, when my neighbor’s whelks come too close to the lichen-beds I am cultivating in the lee of the Inverted Gallery, I pluck a single, taut thread that runs through a keyhole we both use. I send a three-pulse hitch down the line: *Occupied. Brittle. Mine.* 

Hours later, the reply comes back through the sole of a stilt-leg I have resting on an arch three tiers down: a slow, greasy vibration, like a thumb rubbed over dry slate. *Acknowledged. Turning north-by-down.*

"North-by-down" is not a direction you could point to with a finger. It is an angle of tension. To find it, you must have one node anchored in the steady, heavy rock of the deep shelf, and another dangling out where the bronze light begins to fray, feeling for the specific, greasy slide of the gravity-tide as it comes round the bend of the great gulf.

Right now, I am trying to mend a drop.

It is not a large drop—only forty spans of open void between the Spur of the Salt-Stain and a lonely chimney of perforated shale that happens to catch the noon-dust like a funnel. If I can get two whelks across to that chimney, they can eat for a season, and their shells will grow thick and chalky, and I will have more resin-spit to coat my western spools, which have been drying out and fraying in the upper drafts. 

The trouble is that the gap is not forty spans wide. It *looks* forty spans wide, if you had eyes to be fooled by the brown, dusty twilight. 

I do not have eyes. I have a twelve-node harp strung across the mouth of an amphitheater two hundred feet above the Spur, and the wind coming through the stone keyholes is telling me a very different story. The air is singing in B-flat on the western rim of the arch, and in a sharp, shivering F-sharp on the eastern rim. There is a shear between them. A Sleight is sitting in the gap, quiet and invisible as a pane of ice, left behind by yesterday’s storm.

I am down on the Spur myself—or at least, my densest cluster is there: four walnut-nodes braided together into a heavy "hand" for doing delicate work, riding in the dorsal groove of an old, scarred mason-whelk whose shell is so layered with ancient repairs that he looks like a walking cairn. I call him nothing, because he is only a mouth and a foot and a gland that hates the sky, but I know the rhythm of his breath better than I know my own northernmost threads.

*Stop,* I tell him, pressing my shortest needle-leg into the soft, grey dimple behind his mantle. 

The whelk stops. His six stone-fitting limbs continue to click against his chest-pouch, sorting through flat shingles of shale with the blind, frantic industry of a beetle on its back. *Gap,* his nerves say to my needle. *Wind on the belly-foot. Unenclosed. Bad. Must paste. Must wall.*

*Wait,* I tap back, two slow presses on his left ganglion to bleed off the panic-hormone. *The space is folded.*

I unspool a scout-line. I don't use a good nerve-cord for this—it hurts to lose a real wire, it leaves a small, numb hole in the part of me that remembers how to build arches—so I use a dead thread, a dry casing shed last year, weighted at the end with a pebble of lime. I flick it out over the forty-span drop with two long, jointed legs.

The pebble falls five feet, hits an invisible slant of nothing, and shoots *upward* at a thirty-degree angle, vanishing into a smear of refracted bronze light before snapping the dead silk with a tiny, silent whip-crack. 

If I let the whelk build straight out from the ledge, his bridge will curve up into its own underside before he’s laid ten stones, and the next time the gulf exhales and the Sleight slips down-chasm, the masonry will shear off and take the whelk—and my four-node hand—down into the dark where the light gives up.

I have to pin it first.

This is the work I was made for. I leave one node on the whelk to keep him quiet—just enough mind to sit there and stroke his neck-cluster in a slow, four-beat rhythm: *Safe, enclosed, wall is here, wait*—and I pull the rest of my consciousness back up the chimney-threads into the amphitheater.

It is good to come back into the big harp. On the whelk’s back, the world is small and greasy and smells of wet chalk; up here in the perforated tower, I am huge and dry and hollow. I have three hundred filaments plugged into the stone apertures. The wind comes howling down from the high rims, a dry, alkaline gale that would strip skin from bone, and as it tears through the millions of tiny, whistling holes my ancestors made in this tower, the whole structure hums like a plucked bass string. 

I spread my legs across the central vault, feeling the map of the city in my joints. 

Half a mile south, a flock of parchment-gliders is rustling against a rookery spire; I feel their landing as a dry, static tickle in my seventeenth node. Deeper down, in the Third Honeycomb, the stone-borers are at work—*chink-chink-chink* goes their tiny, six-legged chewing, slowly hollowing out a pillar that I will have to send a whelk to plaster over in a decade or two.

And below me, between the Spur and the Chimney, the Sleight hangs like a twisted sheet.

To catch a Sleight, you cannot push against it; you must give it a corner it likes better than the one it is currently occupying. Geometry here is lazy. It wants to rest against an angle that matches its own distortion. 

I send an impulse racing down a three-mile vertical line to my easternmost reaches, where three young whelks are grazing on a patch of salt-crust near the Broken Sluice. It takes four seconds for my thought to get there—the line is stretched thin across an old spatial scar, and the electricity drags its feet through the narrow place—and another four seconds for the feeling of their heavy, leathery feet to come back to me. 

I turn them away from the lichen. I march them up the slotted stairs toward the windward side of the amphitheater, and I set them to building a baffle: a simple, three-sided fin of shale that sticks out from the cliff face at an ugly, crooked tilt. It looks like a mistake. It looks like the work of a whelk whose rider has died. 

For six hours, while the bronze twilight darkens to the color of an old bruise and then pales again, my three whelks stack and spit, stack and spit. I do not have to think about every stone; I only set the tension of my silk between two points and let my nodes tap their necks whenever their blind snouts wander outside the line of my thread. I am the string the mason uses to keep the course straight, and I am also the mason.

Around the seventh hour, the air grows suddenly, wickedly cold. Frost blooms on my chitinous ribs, fuzzy and dry as salt. 

The Sleight is shifting. It has grown tired of hanging between the Spur and the Chimney; the afternoon gravity-tide is coming up from the abyss, pushing a slow wave of heavy dust before it, and the anomaly begins to drift north.

As its edge brushes the crooked fin my whelks have just finished, the stone groans. 

Oh, that is a good sound—not a sound in the air, never in the air, but a deep, rich, crystalline *grind* that travels up through the shale, into my thirty anchor-feet, and straight into the dry centers of my nodes. The quartz in the baffle is being wrung like a rag. A sharp, delicious surge of piezoelectric current floods up my filaments. All sixty-two of my fragments twitch at once in a silent, spread-out ecstasy of nourishment. For ten minutes, I do nothing but cling to the vibrating stone and drink the squeeze of the mountain’s bones.

When the surge subsides, the twisted sheet of space is snagged fast on the crooked fin. 

Down at the Spur of the Salt-Stain, the forty-span gap has didn't just flatten out; because the tail of the Sleight is now pulled tight against the amphitheater wall above, the space below has pinched shut like the mouth of a drawstring purse. 

I flow back down my chimney-cord into my four-node hand on the old whelk’s back. I uncoil a needle-leg and tap the stone ahead of his snout.

The forty-span drop is gone. The lonely chimney of perforated shale, with its rich, powdery beds of unharvested lichen, is now nine inches away. Between the Spur and the Chimney, there is only a hairline crack through which a faint, freezing draft is whistling upward, carrying the smell of the deep, floorless dark three miles below.

*Go,* I tap on the whelk’s neck. *Build.*

The old mollusk shudders with relief—the terrible, unenclosed void has vanished—and lunges forward on his muscular foot. His ventral pouch opens; his calcified limbs click-click-click as they select a wide, flat slab of slate; his mantle bubbles with thick, grey calcareous paste. He slams the stone down across the nine-inch crack, bridging a forty-foot chasm with a single shingle.

He will stay here for three days, eating the salt-stain and cementing the Chimney to the Spur with five feet of solid, perforated masonry. When the Sleight finally rots away from the baffle above and the space snaps back to its proper forty-span width, the stone will not break; it will stretch with the returning geometry, becoming a long, impossibly thin rib of slotted arch that could never have been built across open air, strong enough to carry a dozen whelks back and forth for a century.

As the old whelk settles into the rhythm of his grazing, scraping the crust from the rock with his dry radula, one of my lower nodes—a small, three-legged listener I keep tucked into a crack fifty yards below the Spur—picks up a tremor that does not belong to the wind or the borers.

It is a rhythmic, frantic *tick-tick-tick-pause-tick*. 

It is coming up through a dead, severed thread that hangs down from the lip of the lower terrace, trailing off into the gloomy levels where the bronze light dies. I don't go down there. Nobody goes down there. Five hundred years ago, during the Great Uncoupling that split the main dust-aqueduct, I had twelve nodes down in the Low Vaults; when the geometry began to loop back on itself like a burning rope, I bit through my own main trunk-line with my shearing-legs and let that fifth of myself fall away into the dark rather than be pulled into the knot.

Sometimes, when the gravity-tides are very low and the chasm is still, I can feel that lost piece of me down in the Snarl.

It is still alive. The piezoelectric squeeze in a knotted space is constant and fierce, so the things caught in the loops never starve; they have more energy than they can use, and nowhere to send it. 

I reach out with a single, hair-thin antenna and touch the severed, dusty end of the old trunk-line that still dangles over the drop. 

Instantly, my mind is flooded with a dry, gibbering static. It is my own taste—my own mineral signature, my own ancient memory of the mountain—but multiplied by forty, stacked on top of itself in a suffocating, airless pile. The thing down there is sending a blueprint up the dead wire: instructions for a bridge that has seven beginnings and no middle, an arch that folds through its own keystone three times to keep out a wind that stopped blowing in the last epoch. *Build,* the static screams in my antenna, a thousand stick-legs twitching in a three-foot prison of folded slate. *Unenclosed. The gap is coming through the wall. The wall is inside the gap. Paste it. Paste it. Paste it.*

I pull my antenna back. I tuck it beneath my ribs, cleaning the alkaline dust from its tip with a drop of whelk-mucus until the trembling stops.

Above me, the sixty-two pieces of my body hum in the steady, sane wind of the upper tiers. My harp in the amphitheater sings in a clear, cold fourth; my whelk on the Spur clicks softly as he lays another flat stone over the hairline abyss. 

I take a mouthful of dry resin from his shell-groove, lean out over the edge of the terrace, and carefully glue a flat chip of shale over the severed end of the wire, sealing the dark below from the dark above. Then I turn my herd toward the north-by-down, paying out fresh silk as the afternoon distance begins to lengthen.
