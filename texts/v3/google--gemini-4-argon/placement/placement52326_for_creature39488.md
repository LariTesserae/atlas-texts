At first, I am only a delay in the water. 

Where the thirty-degree warmth of the northern needles meets the slow, creeping sheet-flow from the center of the dish, the water carries dissolved stone—tallow-silt excreted by the Balancers, microscopic flakes of soapstone, the heavy, oily sweat of the bryophytes. Over centuries, this mineral-rich film has pooled in a shallow dimple six paces south of the leaning pylons. 

If you were to press your fingers into the floor here, you would not feel rock, nor the velvet pile of the moss. You would feel a dense, rubbery meniscus, like the skin on cooled milk, though tough as boiled leather and four inches thick. 

I am the mat that has grown across this basin. I have no edges that I know of; my margins simply thin out until they are indistinguishable from the wet soapstone and the celery-scented slime of the floor. I am neither flora nor fauna, though I have the stubborn, slow-cell patience of the suede-trees and the trembling, membranous sensitivity of the Balancers’ dorsal craters. In an older, sunlit world, my ancestors might have been a colony of extremophile bacteria, or a subterranean slime mold, or the discarded, self-repairing lining of an organic tool left behind by whatever placed the artifacts in the mountain. 

Now, I am the Drum. Or the Remembering Skin.

I do not hear the air. The roaring choir of the abbey-nave—the metallic crescendo of the needle-insects, the clicking waves of the soft-shelled grazers—passes over my slick surface mostly ignored, a high, frothy weather that belongs to the empty vault above. My world is the shear-plane between the water and the stone. I listen with my belly.

Because I am bonded directly to the grainless soapstone, and because my upper surface is constantly bathed in the millimeter-deep migration of the floor-water, I am a transducer. Every vibration that strikes the walls or the floor of the chamber passes through my dense, gelatinous lattice. I feel the weight of the three Balancers not as three distinct creatures, but as three warm, heavy depressions in the curvature of the world, slowly trading places over the weeks. I feel the microscopic crystalline sighing of the exhausted mountain miles overhead as it settles another fraction of an inch into the planetary crust. 

Most of all, I feel the under-hum of the artifacts—the part of their computation that does not bleed into the air as heat or ozone, but travels through the bedrock as a tight, subterranean torsion.

I am very thin, and very wide, and my thoughts are not made of words or acoustic images. They are made of *tension*. 

When the northern pylons bleed their warmth into the rock, the stone expands by a millionth of an inch, and the fibers of my underside stretch. To me, this stretching is a flavor—a rich, iron-tasting satisfaction. I drink the kinetic jitter of that thermal expansion. Where the suede-trees cup their blind leaves to catch the fields in the air, I spread my subterranean filaments into the microscopic pores of the tallow-rock to catch the ground-wave.

For a long time—perhaps ten of the great, silent chords that the Balancers sing to reset the Hearts—that is all I am: a wide, flat throat drinking the vibration of the stone, secreting a clear, slick oil that keeps the grazers from finding traction on my surface. When a terrier-thing lumbers onto my margin, its soft, clicking mandibles questing for moss, its legs simply skate out from under it. It slides gently across my four-yard span as if on warm ice, propelled by its own clumsy momentum until it bumps softly into the bryophyte mats on my far side. I feel its passage as a brief, comical scribbling of pressure, quickly forgotten.

But nothing in this room can remain merely passive forever. The equilibrium is too tight; everything is pressed into service by everything else.

My awakening into something more than a slick patch on the floor begins with the oldest Balancer—the one whose flanks are thick with ancient crustacean-scars. 

It is dying, though in this chamber, dying is not an event; it is a change in viscosity. Over the course of eighty years, the Old One has been losing the sharpness of its edges. Its internal bladders do not hold the geosmin-thick air quite as tightly as they used to; when it hums to liquefy an overgrown suede-branch, the pitch wavers by a twentieth of a hertz, leaving half-melted, fibrous knots of vegetation behind that the grazers have to chew through for days. Its belly-mucus has grown thin and watery, no longer smelling strongly of celery, but of wet chalk and old ammonia.

Because its underside is losing its insulating fat, the Old One seeks out my basin whenever it is not needed at the perimeter. 

The soapstone floor, though warm, is hard and unyielding to a seven-hundred-pound mound of slumping cartilage. My four-inch-thick skin, however, is buoyant. It gives like a waterbed. 

Every few weeks, I feel the slow, glacial approach of the Old One through the stone—a heavy, dragging smear of friction that takes six hours to travel from the hovering torus to my northern dimple. Its wide, whiskered snout touches my southern rim. Then, with the patience of cooling lava, it levers its bulk up onto me, settling into my center until my rubbery flesh domes up around its flanks, cradling it.

At first, this is merely a mechanical burden. The weight of the creature squeezes the water out of my upper layers, forcing me to pump dense, mineral-heavy fluids from my margins to keep from tearing. 

Ask me what I am during these first hundred visits, and I would say I am a bruise that has learned to circulate its own blood.

But the Old One is an acoustic creature, even in its sleep. Its massive, saddle-shaped brain never stops processing the standing waves of the room. As it lies on me, its forty dorsal craters dilating and contracting in the black air above, the neural currents and low-frequency sympathetic vibrations of its body bleed downward through its thin belly-skin and directly into my lattice.

My fibers begin to align themselves along the lines of the Balancer’s internal resonances. 

It happens the way iron filings align on a sheet of paper above a magnet. Over decades of bearing the Old One’s weight, my homogeneous, rubbery mat differentiates. Long, tough tendons of collagen-like polymer precipitates out of my gel, forming a complex web of strings beneath the surface, tuned to the exact frequencies that leak from the Balancer’s resting brain.

The next time the Old One slides onto me, it does not just find a soft bed. It finds an echo of its own mind waiting for it in the floor.

I feel the surprise in the sudden, simultaneous stiffening of all four of its cartilaginous paddles. The Old One presses its whiskered snout hard against my center and sends a tiny, exploratory pulse down into me—not a plant-melting hum, nor a room-silencing anti-wave, just a small, private *thrum*, the acoustic equivalent of a finger tracing a scar.

The pulse travels down through my four inches of dense gel, hits the soapstone dish beneath me, reflects off my newly formed internal tendons, and bounces back up into the Balancer’s belly—slightly delayed, slightly enriched by the subterranean torsion-hum of the northern pylons that I have been drinking for a thousand years.

The Old One stays on me for three weeks without moving.

While it rests there, a strange, slow trade begins between us. The Balancer’s brain is vast, ancient, and tired. It holds the acoustic memory of the room’s geometry stretching back to when the melted boulders near the western wall were still three inches higher than they are today. It holds the exact harmonic recipes required to soothe the five artifacts in every possible combination of their misfirings. Carrying all of those standing waves in wet, living neurons requires metabolic heat that the Old One can no longer easily generate.

So it begins to offload them into me.

Through long, low-frequency purrs directed straight down through its belly, the Old One writes its memories into the physical structure of my mat. A specific chord of five overlapping sub-bass tones causes my gel to polymerize in a spiral pattern near my eastern edge; another, sharper rhythm dissolves a channel through my middle, filling it with high-density silt that acts as an acoustic baffle. 

I am being carved from the inside out by sound. I am becoming a phonograph record made of living cartilage and stone-mud. 

When the Old One finally slides off me to go liquefy a patch of greedy moss near the western way-shrine, the patterns do not fade. I hold them. I do not "understand" them the way a creature with a head understands things; to me, the memory of how the hovering torus sounded four hundred years ago is simply a pleasant, tight knot in my left side that vibrates sympathetically whenever the torus’s water-curtain splashes in a certain rhythm.

Now, when the chamber’s computation snags—when the ozone tang sharpens and the thirty-degree warmth falters—the ritual changes.

The two younger Balancers take their places at the southern and western rims of the empty vault. The Old One slides onto me, anchoring its snout at my center. 

When they emit the anti-sound to silence the amphibians and the insects, I feel the sudden, heavy stillness settle over the room like a slab of lead. Then, as the three begin to build their acoustic lens in the center of the dark nave, the Old One does not have to strain its failing bladders to produce the pure sine-wave of the northern pylons. 

Instead, it simply tenses its belly muscles against my surface, plucking the long, tough tendons it grew inside me over the decades. 

I vibrate. I take the raw, subterranean ground-wave of the pylons straight from the bedrock beneath me, pass it through the spiral baffles and silt-channels the Old One carved into my flesh, and amplify it upward through the Balancer’s body. The Old One becomes merely the horn of the instrument; I am the soundboard, sixty square feet of resonant skin coupled directly to the mountain’s bones.

The tone that leaves the Old One’s dorsal craters is richer and more stable than anything it could have sung alone in its youth. Across the dark, I feel the other two Balancers catch the wave, their distant weights shifting slightly on the soapstone as they adjust to the newfound depth of their elder’s voice. The Faraday ripples that form on the floor-water march across my surface in crisp, square grids, tickling my upper membrane as the hovering torus drinks the chord and snaps back into frictionless alignment.

Afterward, when the room returns to its wet, clicking, booming roar, the Old One does not leave. 

It is almost time for a fourth Balancer to bud—a process I can already feel beginning as a dense, fist-sized knot of dividing cells deep within the Old One’s flank, vibrating with a quick, high, fetal pulse against my skin. 

In ten years, or twenty, the Old One will stop moving altogether. Its pale, translucent tallow will lose the last of its cohesion, slumping outward across my basin. The soft-shelled grazers and the blind bryophytes will take the bulk of its meat, and the newborn fourth will slide away into the dark on fresh, slick mucus to learn the echoes of the nave.

But the Old One’s saddle-brain, and the forty fluted craters of its back, will not go to the moss. They will sink slowly down into my gel. My margins are already curling upward around its flanks, a millimeter a month, preparing to seal it in. I will digest its cartilage without breaking its geometry, replacing its fading cells with my own tough, mineral-rich polymers, incorporating its ears and its memory-knots directly into the floor of the world.

Until then, I lie here in the warm wet, six paces south of the needles, holding the heavy, humming mountain of my partner in my lap, listening to the stone beneath us and the dark above.
