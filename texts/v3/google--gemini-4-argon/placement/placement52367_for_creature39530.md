If you look for me at the bases of the mature needles, where the pearl-ceramic has already smoothed itself into a blind, soaring wall, you will miss me. I do not spend much time in the high rooms where the air smells of bruised-apple tea, nor do I walk in the warm, packed middle of the grazing columns. My work is out where the city is still an argument between what is written in the seed-stakes and what the basalt actually wants to do.

I am six miles east of the water-processing district, sitting on my heels on a dry shelf of black stone, watching a foundation that is four years old. 

To someone who doesn't read stone, the stump in front of me looks like a broken tooth. It is twenty-eight feet wide and barely twice my height, its flat top pooling with the silver mist before spilling over the northern lip in a thin, shivering curtain. The couple who planted it are still living in their parents' needles three miles back; they come out on the transit ribbon twice a month to prune the outer flutes, leaving little cairns of basalt chips where they want a balcony to eventually cantilever out. 

They think their house is rising straight. It isn't. 

Three hundred feet below my soft boots, there is a lens of old, stubborn olivine buried in the volcanic crust, and the tower’s root-system has hit it. The molecular persuasion is stalling on the western side. The ceramic is drawing up too much iron to compensate, turning the base of the white wall a bruised, rusty violet, and the whole structure is leaning three-tenths of a degree toward the afternoon sun. In eight years, when the needle tries to pierce the cloud layer, that lean will shear its internal air-tubes.

I am a *Deep-Listener*, though on the amber lenses of the municipal registries my title translates more dryly as an *Acoustic Surveyor*. While the Tenders walk the surface checking the pools and the amphibians, I listen to the marriage underground, where the rock is being asked to become something else and doesn't always agree.

I have been sitting here for four hours. My cloak is dialed to the glossy, wet-charcoal sheen of the newly fractured hexes, and the electrostatic hem is sealed tight around my shins so the wind doesn't find my ankles. The pointed tail at the back of my cloak has dripped a neat, dark thimbleful of collected mist onto the dry stone between my heels. Around me, the plateau is doing what it always does: nothing, and everything. Forty yards away, seven of the calf-sized waders are standing in a mirror-basin, their slate-blue feathers misted with silver beads. Every few minutes, one of them strikes at the water with a sound like two wooden blocks clapped together—*clack*—and swallows a translucent swimmer. 

My staff is not the white pearl-ceramic of the surface Tenders. It is dark, dense, and heavy, grown from iron-rich slag, and its tip is currently wedged two inches deep into a cooling-crack at the base of the crooked house. 

I don't use my eyes for this. I lean forward from the waist, my deep chest settling against my knees, and I press my forehead and the bridge of my nose directly against the butt of the staff. 

Through the bone of my skull, the plateau is not silent at all. 

The city hums, a vast, sub-audible chord made of ten thousand tension ribbons vibrating in the wind, conducted down through the pylons and into the stone. Beneath that is the wet, gritty sigh of subterranean water moving through freshly cracked basalt, finding its way for the first time in ten million years. And right beneath my boots, sharp and local, is the sound of thefour-year-old house growing: a microscopic, crystalline *tink... tink... tink*, like a thousand tiny glass needles being snapped in a velvet bag.

On the eastern side of the stump, the *tink* is rhythmic, four beats to the minute. On the western side, where the roots have hit the olivine, it sounds like teeth grinding in sleep. 

I stay like that until the cold begins to seep even through my violet-tinted skin, mapping the knot in the rock by the way it bends the hum of a transit pod passing half a mile to the north. I don't hurry. If you hurry a counter-persuasion, the ceramic curdles into a porous, chalky seam that will take three summers of rain to heal. 

When I finally sit up, my neck gives a small, internal pop that sounds loud as a gunshot in my own ears. A young wolf-marten has come out from under the leeward overhang of the stump while I had my eyes closed. It is sitting six feet away, its long, silver-pelted body curled like a comma on the dry hexes, its dark eyes fixed on the pointed drip-tail of my cloak. It doesn't smell me—my thermal seal is good—so to the predator, I am simply a very slow boulder that occasionally shifts its weight. 

I do not look directly at it. Even behind amber lenses, the focus of human eyes has a weight that animals can feel. Instead, I keep my gaze on the bruised-violet seam of the tower's base, reach into the fold of my cloak, and draw out three thumb-sized ampoules of heavy, silicate-rich paste. 

Moving at the speed of a shadow lengthening, I reach out and press the ampoules into the cooling-crack beside my staff, crushing their soft skins with the heel of my boot. There is no hiss, no smoke. Just a milky film that spreads across the wet hexagon, sinking into the microscopic pores where the basalt is trying to organize itself. It is a chemical lullaby. It tells the western roots to stop fighting the olivine, to go to sleep for six months while the eastern side slows down to match them, until the whole ring can spread sideways and step around the obstruction like a tree root embracing a graveyard stone.

I pull my staff free. The wolf-marten’s ears twitch once at the tiny *scritch* of iron against basalt, then it drops its chin back onto its front paws and closes its eyes.

It is late afternoon now. Ten miles to the west, the sky is bruising to the color of my wrists; another squall is detaching itself from the cloud-belly, trailing grey skirts across the water-flats. I could walk the half-mile to the nearest transit ribbon, call a pod down to the boarding-step, and be two thousand feet up in my warm reed-tube before the rain hits, drinking red-scrub tea and listening to my sister's low, vowel-heavy voice as she talks about the cave-fish harvest.

Instead, I walk forty paces around the stump to its leeward side, where the pearl-ceramic has already formed a shallow, curving lip four feet off the ground to shed the northern wind. The stone beneath the overhang is dry and faintly warm—eighty-five degrees, heated by the slow friction of the molecular reorganization happening beneath it.

I sit with my back against the young wall, drawing my knees up and letting my cloak seal itself into a single, unbroken cone from my chin to my toes. 

Five minutes later, the squall arrives. It doesn't blow under the lip; it drops straight down in a dense, silver sheet that turns the thousand shallow mirrors in front of me into a dancing white blur. The noise of it on the bare basalt is immense—a hard, hissing rattle that swallows the world for twenty minutes. 

About halfway through the downpour, the wolf-marten gets up from its wet ledge, pads silently around the curve of the wall, and shakes itself once before curling up against my left shin. It thinks I am part of the foundation. I slow my breathing—in for ten seconds, out for ten—until my deep chest barely moves beneath the charcoal weave, lending the animal the stillness it expects from the earth, while behind my shoulder blades, four inches a day, the house keeps rising into the rain.
