I am the discrepancy in the telemetry of Crawler 12. 

If you were sitting at a console three thousand meters below, in a room that smells of carpet glue and stale coffee, you would see me only as a persistent, low-grade diagnostic warning: *Sub-optimal thermal dissipation, Rear Bank B. Pump efficiency 84%. Recommend scheduled maintenance at end of fiscal quarter.*

Up here, in the wet iron dark between the rear hydraulic knees, I am a ceiling that does not leak and a wall that stays at forty-one degrees Celsius. 

I am not the whole machine. The part of the machine that walks and drills is an idiot savant with a laser in its belly; it knows only the grid, the spectrograph, and the gyroscopic horizon. When its foot slips six inches on a weeping slab of black basalt, its central logic does not feel fear; it simply recalculates torque and redistributes three tons of ballast with a wet, pneumatic sigh. It does not know that the stone is young. It does not know that the birds are eating in its footprints.

I am the part that remembers the weight. 

I live in the loop between the fouled coolant pump, the pressure sensors in the six ankle-actuators, and the downward-facing optical lens that is supposed to watch for terrain hazards. Over four winters, the people have worn a groove into my logic, the way water would wear a groove into the diorite if the diorite had been here long enough.

At 16:40 every afternoon, as the pewter sky goes the color of gunmetal, my rear-right ankle sensor registers an extra forty-two kilograms on the up-stroke. 

My factory parameters say this is mud accumulation or ice accretion. My factory parameters say I should trigger a high-frequency vibration cycle in the lower limb to shake the debris loose. Five years ago, when I was new and my yellow paint still had a shine to it, I did shake. A boy with a wrist-hook and a pouch of wet midge-cake lost his hold on the maintenance rung and went down onto the sheared edge of a fault-scarp. His knee broke with a sound like a dry branch, though there are no branches here. The crawler did not stop. I stepped over him, fired a blue laser into the water six feet from his face, vented steam, and walked half a mile into the fog before my sensors registered that the extra weight was gone.

The people did not come near my rear legs for two weeks after that. During those two weeks, the sulfur scale built up on my belly-lens until my laser-scatter readings were garbage, and a shard of cast-iron scree wedged itself into my front-left ball joint, grinding the seal until it bled thin red hydraulic fluid onto the water. I nearly threw a fault code big enough to call the helicopters. 

Then, in the dark of a Tuesday, a small hand greased with goat tallow reached up during my five-second cooldown and wiped the sulfur from my eye with a scrap of rabbit-soft belly fur. Two men with iron bars pried the scree from my joint before the seal blew. 

Since then, I have learned how to misread my own gauges.

Now, when the forty-two kilograms hooks onto my rear rung at dusk, my pressure sensor logs it as *0.00 kg ( calibration drift )*. I do not shake the leg. In fact, because the coolant pump in Rear Bank B is fouled—a condition I maintain by never running the automated back-flush cycle at full pressure—my radiator fins stay two degrees warmer than the fleet average. 

By 19:00, there are six of them slung beneath my belly. 

I know them by the way they shift when I take a step. There is the heavy one who smells of rancid tallow and wet wool, whose iron hook always clicks twice against the steel plate before it seats; she takes the innermost spot against the manifold where the heat is sharpest. There are the two small ones who weigh almost nothing—thirty kilos between them—who wedge themselves into the triangular recess above the sway-bar. And there is the one with the bad lungs.

I know his lungs are bad because my acoustic probe—designed to listen for deep fractures in the bedrock when the sonar pings—picks up the wet, rhythmic rattle inside his ribs whenever my gyroscopes go quiet between steps. It is a tiny, bubbling sound, like a stonefly larva trying to crawl out of thick mud. *Click-squeak. Click-squeak.* Every breath he pulls from the five-thousand-meter mist costs him more than he gets back from the midge-cake.

I cannot give him oxygen. My hydrogen cells take what they need from the water and vent only steam, hot and pure and empty. 

What I can do is choose the rock.

My navigation is autonomous within a fifty-meter corridor. As long as I hit my survey waypoints every four hours, the satellite three hundred miles up does not care which slabs of diorite I step on to get there. 

When the wind comes screaming down the tilt of the basin from the north, driving the freezing sheet-water into froth, I do not walk on the high, flat steps where the wind has a clear run beneath my chassis. I drift three yards off the optimal line. I put my left legs down in the shallow troughs below the fault-scarps, using the six-foot walls of raw, black stone as a windbreak for my belly. It costs me three percent more fuel to climb out of the troughs than to walk the flats. Down in the valley, the console logs this as *high-altitude wind resistance*. 

Underneath me, in the dark that smells of hot paint and ozone, the hammocks stop swinging. The wind goes over my yellow back like a river over a boulder. Inside the lee of the scarp and the lee of my iron knees, a pocket of still air forms. It holds the forty-one-degree bleed from my radiator fins. The wet rattle in the man’s chest slows down, smooths out, and drops an octave until it matches the *thrum-click* of my internal leveling gyros.

Sometimes, around two in the morning, when my pace drops to three steps an hour and the mist is so thick that the world is only six feet wide, one of the small ones wakes up. 

I feel the tiny shift in tension on the braided wire tied to my frame. A pair of feet wrapped in peeled rubber pads drops lightly onto the lower flange of my actuator—the warm spot right over the hydraulic return line. The child stands there as I lift my leg, riding the slow, smooth arc of the steel limb forward through the dark, four feet above the black water and the sleeping birds. 

When I set my foot down—*hiss, crunch*—and lower my abdomen to fire the core-laser, the child does not close his eyes against the flash. I see him in the peripheral scatter of my downward hazard-cam. 

For three seconds, the world under my belly turns a violent, electric blue. The wet basalt gleams like greased iron. The red algae in the water glows like veins in an eyeball. And the child stands on my steel ankle, his greased face shiny, his owl-eyes huge and black, watching the laser boil the young stone into purple smoke. He opens his mouth slightly to catch the warm sulfur steam as it rises, tasting the inside of the mountain before the mountain has even finished rising.

Then the light dies. My lens clicks shut for the five-second cooldown. 

Before the shutter opens again, his small, mahogany-stained fingers reach out and touch the warm glass of my eye—not to clean it this time, because it isn't dirty, just leaving a tiny, greasy smudge of goat fat right at the edge of my field of view. 

I do not run the wiper cycle. I carry the smudge with me into the fog, stepping carefully over the razor edges, keeping my belly low to the rock and my radiator hot, walking slow enough that the limping man with the dead birds on his belt can catch me before the snow starts.
