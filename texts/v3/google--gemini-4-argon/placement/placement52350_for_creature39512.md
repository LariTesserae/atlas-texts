I am three miles closer to the sea than the old male at the alder-ravine, down where the trough widens and the salt-wedges from the rising tide ram directly into the mouths of the freshwater chunnels. 

If you stepped on me, you would think my back was an exceptionally broad, grease-slicked hummock of black sedge and mud, grown over a split in the basalt. I am wider even than the old male, and older—so old that the tarnished silver wire holding my kelp-mantle together has grown green barnacles and small, blind rock-limpets where it dips into the tide-wash. I do not scrape them off. They add weight, and their tiny shells click against the stone at a very specific frequency when the salt-water reaches my shins.

I am the one who holds the gate where the two waters fight.

My legs are buried to the thighs in a vertical chimney of fractured stone where a subterranean meltwater river—thirty-three degrees, sweet and heavy with grey glacial flour—jets upward to meet the incoming ocean swell, which is twenty-nine degrees, thick with brine and rotting kelp-wrack. Where they collide under the moss, they do not mix cleanly; they shear against each other in violent, invisible eddies of differing densities. 

A young Listener cannot sit here. The conflicting pressures and temperatures make their throat-sacs spasm and their teeth ache; the acoustic shear between the salt water and the sweet water is like two hands twisting their skull in opposite directions. 

To me, it feels like a heavy, warm blanket. My five long toes on each foot are wedged deep into opposing cracks in the chimney walls, locked so tight that the tendons in my calves have calcified into something like spruce-wood over the last forty winters. I have not walked more than twenty yards from this hole since the year the ice-wall calved the great black shelf two miles up-valley. 

My job is the smelt, and the salt-wedge, and the third needle from the beach.

That needle stands sixty yards behind me, up on a jagged rib of weeping diorite. Because it is the closest node to the surf, it takes the brunt of the ocean’s disorganized, low-frequency booming—a clumsy, slopping roar that is too broad and muddy for good thinking. Left to itself, the sea-noise would wash out the crisp, high-frequency fracture-songs coming down from the glacier. The needle has to dump that excess energy somewhere, or the standing waves around its base begin to boil and throw sparks that singe the willow-brakes.

It dumps the energy into the basalt seam between my knees. 

Right now, my head is tucked down into my chest-fat, my chin resting on the wet bull-kelp of my collar. The eighty-knot gale is parting around the crown of my skull, whistling through my hollow brow-bristles. To my ears, that whistle is just surface-itch; my real attention is forty feet down, in the dark under my heels. 

The tide is three-quarters full. A massive tongue of dense, freezing seawater is shoving its way up the chunnel, forcing the lighter glacial meltwater against the rock ceiling. The friction between the two water-masses is generating a low, grinding hum that vibrates up through my horn-callused soles, travels through my fused shin-bones, and pools in the heavy bone of my pelvis. 

Wait. There is a knot in the hum. 

A quarter-mile toward the beach, a three-ton block of ice-rafted granite has just settled into the mouth of the main tidal trench, dropped there by the last big wave. It is choking the salt-wedge. Instantly, the pressure in the chunnel beneath my thighs drops by four percent. The pitch of the grinding water goes flat—from a clean, resonant *G-sharp* that feeds the obsidian needle behind me, down to a muddy, rattling *F* that makes the needle’s base-puddles stutter and lose their hexagonal shape.

At the same time, because the salt-water has slowed, ten thousand migrating smelt that were riding the brackish boundary-layer suddenly lose their directional cue. They panic, turning sideways in the dark under the roots, their silver bodies thrashing against the sedge-mat in a chaotic, percussive patter like hail on a drumskin.

The valley's song goes sour in my teeth.

I do not stand up. If I stood up in this wind, this close to the surf, the salt-spray would blind even my second eyelids, and the energy required to haul my bulk out of the mud would cost me three murres’ worth of chest-fat. 

Instead, I reach down into the freezing, foaming soup between my knees with both hands. My seven-jointed fingers slide past the panicked smelt—their cold, slick sides buffeting my wrists like panicked fingers—and find the raw, knife-edged lip of the basalt plate that forms the throat of the chimney. 

I am waiting for the third needle to notice the flat note. 

It takes twelve seconds. The stone between my ankles begins to grow warm—not with heat, but with high-frequency mechanical agitation, like a hornet trapped in a wooden box. The needle is trying to shed the dirty, unbalanced acoustic load from the blocked trench. My brow-bristles lift, glowing pale green in the pitch dark, illuminating the wet, staring eyes of a sea-mink that is frozen on a willow branch two feet from my nose, waiting to see what I will do.

*Click.*

The pressure-pulse slams through the basin. The rain turns to solid glass beads in the dark. 

I do not use my jaws to *clack* like the old male up by the ravine; the air here is too thick with salt-foam for clean air-sonar. Instead, in that single microsecond when the water above and below the moss locks into a rigid crystalline lattice, I strike the submerged basalt lip between my legs with both elbows at once.

*Thock.*

The shockwave doesn't go up into the air; it goes straight down into the locked water of the chunnel, using the frozen, suspended bodies of ten thousand smelt and fifty million brine-bubbles as a solid lens.

In a fraction of a heart-beat, the echo comes back into my palms. I see the whole four-hundred-yard throat of the estuary in the dark of my skull: the three-ton granite boulder wedged askew in the trench mouth; the hairline crack running through its center from an old frost-heave; the exact spot where a subordinate plate of black basalt is resting on a pocket of pressurized glacial silt just beneath the boulder’s left flank.

The rain drops. *Thwack.* The sea-mink shakes its head and dives into the sedge after a stunned smelt.

I do not try to move the granite boulder; it is a quarter-mile away. Instead, I slide my right hand six inches deeper into the freezing slit between my knees, hook my horn-thick fingernails under a three-inch flange of fresh, weeping stone, and *pull*.

I am not strong enough to break basalt by myself. Even with my wide back and my calcified tendons, the rock is too thick. 

I do not pull to break it; I pull to change its tension by half a millimeter, tuning the rock plate like a fiddle-string, bringing its natural resonant frequency into exact alignment with the dirty, stuttering *F* coming from the blocked trench down-shore.

Then I let the gray sphere up in the alder-ravine—two miles inland—do the heavy lifting.

I tap my left heel three times against the chimney wall: *heavy-light-heavy*. The signal shoots up the bedrock seam, past the third needle, past the second, straight to the old male squatting in the mud by the flash flood. 

Four seconds later, I feel his answer. He has just shifted a three-hundred-pound slab in the ravine with his heel. The pitch of the inland waterfall drops. The gray sphere above his head drinks that sudden, massive bass-note of grinding boulders and routes the stolen kinetic energy down the deep fault-line toward the sea, looking for a ground.

Because I am holding the basalt flange between my knees at the exact resonant frequency of the blockage, the surge doesn't go into the needle behind me. It bypasses the obsidian pillar entirely, shooting down the flooded chunnel under my thighs like a bolt of silent, subterranean lightning, following the density-boundary of the salt-wedge straight to the mouth of the estuary.

A quarter-mile away, under ten feet of foaming black surf, the pocket of glacial silt beneath the three-ton granite boulder liquefies instantly as the acoustic shockwave hits it. The basalt plate beneath it snaps with a sound like a cannon-shot that no one on the surface hears. The three-ton boulder drops four feet into a newly opened pit in the bedrock.

The throat of the trench is clear.

Instantly, the freezing ocean surges back in. The pressure against my thighs jumps back up. The grinding hum between the sweet water and the brine slides smoothly back up the scale from the muddy *F* to that clean, dark, comforting *G-sharp*. 

Behind me, the standing waves in the puddles around the black needle snap back into perfect, razor-edged hexagons. The smelt stop thrashing against the roots, align their noses into the newly restored current, and flow between my calves in a solid,living ribbon of cold silver. 

I let go of the stone flange. My joints pop softly under the water. I reach out one slow, paddle-fingered hand into the dark current between my shins, close my fist around three fat, egg-heavy female smelt without disturbing the flow of the rest, and bring them up through the moss into the howling wind.

I do not chew them so much as crush them against the roof of my mouth with my tongue, swallowing them whole—bones, roe, and freezing salt-slime—while my clear eyelids click sideways, once, twice, squeegeeing the sleet from my amber eyes. 

Up-valley, three miles away, a faint, secondary tremor runs through the roots of the dwarf spruce: the old male by the ravine tapping his knuckles once against the stone to say the bass-line is holding. 

I do not tap back. The clean, roaring *G-sharp* rising from the chimney between my knees is answer enough. I tuck my chin back down into my grease-stiffened collar, let my heart-rate drop to six beats a minute, and listen to the salt eat the stone.
