I am not on the iron drums, where the mud boils and the reed-beds thicken the air. I am higher up, where the valley’s throat narrows into the western lip—where the four hundred small cables gather themselves like roots in reverse, braiding into four immense, trembling trunks as thick as ancient pines. 

Here, the travertine gives way to bare, sweating basalt that rises at a forty-five-degree angle toward the sky’s dark violet edge. This is the Substation, though nobody calls it that. To the tribe, it is the Heart-Stone. It is a series of monoliths cast from some dull, un-rusting grey alloy, half-swallowed by a massive, centuries-old strangler vine whose pale wood has grown entirely around the transformer housings like a clenched fist.

I am one of them, though I am old enough that the waxy sebum on my shoulders has hardened into permanent, horn-like plates—gray and cracked like the dry side of a boulder. My chest is broader even than the others’, a hollowed-out barrel that whistles faintly in the back of my throat when the stratosphere-wind knifes down the rock face. My left leg ends at the knee, taken twenty floods ago by a blind catfish when a cable snapped under my weight during an earthquake and dropped me into the black silt. The stump is capped with a thick, dead bulb of grey callus. I do not miss the foot; the three limbs I have left are enough to hook into the deep grooves of the main trunk-lines. I don't need to travel the basin anymore. The basin comes to me.

Because the four main trunks meet under my ribs, I feel the whole caldera at once. 

It is not a sound; it is a geography of pressure. If I press my scarred cheek against the grey alloy of the primary transformer and let my amber eye-scales slide half-shut, I can read the marsh the way a spider reads the wind in its web.

The sixty-hertz hum is not one thing. It is four hundred distinct voices braided together. Near the center of the caldera, where the water is hottest and the sulfur bubbles thick as porridge, the drums sing with a heavy, greasy *thrum*—a wide, lazy current that makes the back of my jaw itch. Up near the northern snow-chutes, where the glacial melt hits the iron fins at thirty-three degrees before the mud can warm it, the harvesters scream in a tight, high-voltage *purr* that vibrates right in the center of my sternum.

And over those steady rivers of current, I feel the little taps. The tribe.

*Thump-thump-drag.* That is the three-fingered female down at the seventh terrace, clearing a rock-slide off the secondary junction. 
*Bzzz-clack-bzzz.* Two juveniles fighting over a basking-spot on a warm conduit, their small throat-sacs puffing out clumsy, ragged modulations that die out after a hundred yards. 

I am the one who listens to the long drop. 

On the other side of the basalt lip, the four giant trunks plunge straight down into the dry, freezing nothingness of the outer mountain. Down there, the air has no water in it. If you climb ten yards past the rim, your skin dries into paper and cracks; the cold gets inside your huge lungs and turns the wet sponge of them into glass. None of us go over the lip. 

Yet that is where the hum goes. For all my life, and the lives of the bones that have dissolved into the sulfur-silt before me, the megawatts have poured over the cliff, pulled by some immense, invisible thirst far below the clouds. 

Until three days ago, the pull was as steady as the sun. The current flowed out of our valley like water over a waterfall—smooth, heavy, and clean. 

Then, the lowland died. Or broke. Or closed its mouth. 

I felt it first in my stump. A sudden, violent *kick* inside the braided copper-nickel under my knee, as if something enormous at the bottom of the mountain had slammed a door against the river of power. 

Now, the electricity has nowhere to go, and the Heart-Stone is sick with it. 

The grey alloy beneath my belly is no longer the ninety-eight degrees of the marsh, nor the pleasant, feverish hundred-and-ten of a hard-working drum. It is scorching. The strangler vine wrapped around the transformer is dying; its pale wood is char-black where it touches the metal, weeping a bitter, smoking sap that smells like burnt hair and lightning. Because the lowland isn't drinking the current, the impedance has backed all the way up the mountain. The sixty-hertz hum has gone sour. It has a jagged, secondary harmonic now—a frantic, shivering *stutter* that makes the translucent frogs drop off the iron into the mud, their bellies spasming, their eggs souring into white curds inside them.

The air around my monolith smells of ozone—sharp and metallic, like a freshly knapped basalt blade held to the tongue. Every few minutes, the air between the great cable-trunks turns purple-white and *snaps*, a tiny, whip-crack of raw arc-flash that leaves a blind spot in the center of my black eyes even through the amber shields. 

Below me, in the golden steam of the marsh, the tribe is restless. I can feel fifty of them gathered along the lower approaches, their throat-sacs humming a low, confused dirge into the rubber sheathing. *Hot-wrong-loud,* the cables say against my knuckles. *Loud-wrong-hurt.*

They think the harvesters are angry. Down at the fourth terrace, a group of young males has already begun doing the only thing we know how to do: they are cutting the vines away from the fins, scraping the crimson moss raw, exposing more and more iron to the cold runoff to make the engines work better, to purify the hum. 

They don't understand that they are pouring more water into a choked throat. Every vine they cut down in the green haze makes the grey stone under my belly three degrees hotter. 

I shift my weight, hooking my broad, webbed right hand over a heavy copper ground-strap, and drag my three-limbed body down the basalt slope toward the first splitter-box. My huge chest heaves—five times a minute now, fast and shallow, sucking at the thin, ozone-tainted steam. 

I reach into my reed-fiber harness. I do not carry a basalt saw like the vine-cutters down in the bog. My tool is a piece of the old world, pulled from the deep mud below the cliff before my knee was taken: a two-foot wrench of drop-forged vanadium steel, its jaws fused solid by mineral scale, heavy as a bone from the mountain itself. 

I do not know what a circuit is. I do not know what a city is, or why the valley was wired to feed one, or what has finally rusted through ten miles down in the dry dark. 

I only know the hum. And I know that if the Heart-Stone burns itself hollow, the whole valley will go quiet forever. 

I reach the first junction where the northern line—the coldest, loudest line from the glacial chutes—bolts into the main bus-bar. The rubber armor here is blistering, bubbling up into black, smelly boils. 

I press my sternum hard against the burning jacket. The heat sears the thick, waxy skin between my ribs, smelling briefly of fried fat, until my calluses take the brunt of it. I open my mouth, let my ribbed throat-sac drop until it touches the smoking rubber, and lock my neck muscles. 

I do not modulate the hum the way the others do to warn of a cat or a boiling wave. I throw my whole hollow barrel of a chest against the jagged, stuttering harmonic, creating a dead-stop—a flat, heavy, biological *thud* that travels backward down the northern line against the current.

*Back,* I vibrate into the copper. *Down. Off.*

Fifty yards down the slope, through the rising white fog, I see three pairs of huge, black eyes rise above a bed of giant reeds. They feel the command in their feet. They freeze, their basalt saws halfway through a pulsing white vine. 

I don't wait to see if they obey. I cannot stop the four hundred drums from drinking the mountain's heat, and I cannot open the closed door at the bottom of the world. If the river cannot go down, some of the tributaries must be killed, or the Heart-Stone will crack.

I wedge the hooked head of the steel wrench under the first secondary lead—a cable only as thick as my wrist, feeding from a cluster of six drums near the eastern rim. The metal is so hot the scale on the wrench hisses against the wet skin of my palm. 

I fold my single long, frog-like leg tight against the rock, brace my stump into a notch in the travertine, and pull with the whole breadth of my keeled back. 

The ancient copper-nickel is stubborn, stiff with seventy years of carrying the mountain’s fever, braided to survive rockfalls and floods. It does not want to part. Inside my chest, my huge lungs burn in the thin air; black spots swim behind my amber membranes, mixing with the purple afterimages of the arc-flashes. 

Instead of prying the cable free, I reach down with my free hand and scoop a heavy double-handful of the thick, black, sulfur-rich silt from a pocket in the rock, ignoring the immediate, blistering scald as the boiling mud hits my webbed fingers. I pack the mud—and the sprawling, wet mat of crimson moss clinging to the stone beside me—directly over the cooling vents of the junction box, and then down onto the iron fins of the nearest harvester just below the lip. 

I am doing the obscene thing. I am smothering the iron. 

I drag a severed, thigh-thick vine across the harvester's crown, piling the wet, insulating green over the metal, burying the fins in steaming muck so the cold wind from the violet sky cannot touch them. 

For a minute, nothing happens. The drum beneath the mud shudders violently, fighting for its differential. Then, slowly, the inside of the cylinder equilibrates with the boiling rock below it. 

The sixty-hertz hum in that single iron barrel wavers, drops an octave, and flatlines. 

A sudden, terrible hole opens up in my sensory world—a patch of absolute, dead silence right in front of my knees. It feels like going blind in one eye, or having my leg taken all over again. A cold shudder of pure instinctual revulsion runs down my spine, and my throat-sac flutters in panic. Around the dead cylinder, the translucent frogs immediately begin to leap away into the reeds, abandoning the cooling, silent iron as if it were a corpse. 

Below me, a low, mournful ripple comes up the remaining cables from the tribe. They felt the drum die. They are terrified. 

I press my cheek back against the main trunk-line leading to the Heart-Stone. 

The jagged, biting stutter in the big transformer is still there—the grey monolith is still too hot, the strangler vine is still smoking—but the purple arc-flashes between the trunks have stopped. The pressure in the main vein has dropped by a fraction of a breath. The hum is a little cleaner.

I pull my burned hand out of the mud, lick the gray volcanic ash from my webbing, and look down into the golden, shimmering soup of the caldera. Four hundred drums. If the lowland never opens its mouth again, we cannot keep them all. We only need enough to keep the web alive—enough to talk across the terraces, enough to keep the frogs breeding, enough to feel each other in the dark when the stratosphere-wind blows the fog in. 

I hook my steel wrench back into my reed harness. I press my throat against the northern cable again, sending the slow, heavy rhythm down into the green haze, over and over, until I feel the first of the others press their chests to the metal and repeat it back to me. 

*Cover,* I hum into the mountain. *Smother. Sleep.*

Then I swing my three limbs out onto the scorching rubber highway, descending into the steam to teach the tribe how to put the valley to bed.
