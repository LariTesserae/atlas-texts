I would be on the other side of the root-loop, in the wide, fast winter, and I would not know at first that I was sharing a quarry with a blind mason at the bottom of the sea.

Here, the sky is a high, pale slab of iron, and the river is broad and shallow, running over millions of gray, frost-chapped cobbles. It is the kind of cold that dries the inside of your nose when you breathe. Nothing here is packed tight; there is too much room. The wind comes off the flat gravel bars with nothing to stop it for forty miles except stunted willow scrub and the bleached, half-buried ribs of a whale that died here when this valley was still a sound, ten thousand years ago.

I am what is left of a surveyor, or a boundary-walker, though the parish I am keeping the bounds of has long since blown away into dust and lichen. I am tall, thin as a dried bladder-wrack, and wrapped in four layers of stiff, greased wool that smell of old mutton fat and woodsmoke. I have eyes, though they water constantly in the wind, so I see the world mostly as a smear of gray stone, white frost, and the silver flash of the braid-streams.

My work is simple: the river is losing its weight.

Every year, the frost heaves the gravel bars, and when the ice goes out, a few yards of the bank simply forget to stay down. You can see it happening near the bends—a gray stone the size of a loaf of bread will begin to tremble on its dry bed, light as a dry leaf, and the water around it will stop running downhill and begin to curl sideways into the air, freezing into strange, square-edged rime that doesn't melt when the pale sun hits it. There is a leak in the gravel. The winter is draining out into somewhere small and tight and wet.

Wait by the gravel bar long enough, with your hands tucked into your sleeves against the wind, and you will see the roots.

They don't belong to the willows. They come up out of the dry stones six feet from the water's edge—white, blind, segmented things as thick as my thumb, smelling faintly of bruised plums and very old salt. They arch out of the gravel for five or six inches and then go straight back down into the dirt, like a stitch in a coat. They are freezing cold, colder than the river ice, and when the wind hits them, they don't shiver. They have the dense, stubborn stillness of something that is under immense pressure somewhere else.

I used to try to chop them out with my billhook, thinking they were some kind of blight killing the river’s fall. When you cut one, it doesn't bleed sap; it lets out a single, tiny *tink*, like a glass snapping in a dark room, and a smell like wet chalk and mercury. And the cut ends don't die—they just pull back down into the stones with a slow, rubbery *creak*.

Then, three winters ago, I saw the hand.

I was squatting near the old whale-ribs, stacking flat river cobbles into a cairn to keep the gravel bar from drifting upward into the fog, when the air two feet above a white root-loop simply parted. There was no hole—just a seam where the wind stopped blowing and the light went out.

Out of the seam came an arm.

It was black as a river mussel, scarred white at the elbow, and thicker than my thigh, though it wasn't long. It smelled terribly of the bottom of a well. The moment the dry wind hit that matte-black skin, the frost rimed over it in a flat white crust, and I heard, coming from nowhere, a low, groaning creak like an oak beam taking the weight of a roof. The horn-nailed fingers were blind and slow—so slow that I watched for twenty minutes before I realized they were even open, groping across the dry gravel like five pale-tipped slugs.

It was looking for a stone. It touched three or four—ones that had already gone light and bad from the leak—and left them alone. It wanted a heavy one. A stone that still had the sun and the mountain's drop inside it.

I could have struck it with the iron billhook. Instead, I looked at my cairn, and I looked at those scarred knuckles, raw where they had been scraping against fresh-split rock somewhere in the dark. I knew a wall-builder's hand when I saw one. You don't get knuckles like that unless you spend your life arguing with stones that don't want to lie flat.

I reached into the base of my cairn, took out a gray cobble that had forty summers of honest river-weight in it, and set it down on the gravel right against the tip of her thumb.

It took her ten minutes to close her fingers around it. Before the hand drew back into the seam, one of those broad, horned nails tapped twice against the dry gravel—*tink, tink*—testing the level of my world, and finding it good.

That is how we work now.

I walk the forty miles of the gravel flats, my greasy wool flapping in the dry wind, gathering the heaviest, truest river stones I can find and piling them near the white root-loops. When I pass the buried whale, I use my iron wedge to split off long, straight laths of the old, seasoning bone, and I leave those by the loops, too.

In return, when the hand comes through the winter to take the stone, it does not come empty.

Wrapped in the horned fingers, there is always a lump of wet, violet-streaked chalk the size of a walnut, coughed up from the bottom of the sea, or a three-inch cutting of a black-green plant that is heavy as lead. I take them with my mittens before the frost can crack them. I bury the chalk and the dark leaves under the places where the riverbank is trying to float away. The heavy, sideways-pulling misery of her little room anchors my loose gravel down; the honest, straightforward fall of my river stones keeps her ceiling from forgetting which way is up.

Sometimes, when the wind drops entirely and the gray sky comes down to meet the stones, I kneel by a root-loop and press my ear against the white rind of the plant. Through the frost and the gravel, from five hundred miles away and three thousand feet down, I can hear the low, rhythmic *thrum* of the Tuesday that missed, muffled and steady, because she is lying across the slates on the other side, holding the floor down for both of us.
