At first, I am the heat itself, or the pressure of the white glare against the flat planes of the feldspar. It is difficult to locate a boundary where a self might begin when there is no darkness to hide in and no horizon to look toward. 

If I try to inhabit the chamber as an observer, the light immediately ruins the attempt; it has no direction, so it offers no perspective. Everything is equally present, equally sharp, and equally loud in its brightness. To exist here, I cannot have eyes that need to focus. I must be something that does not care about the glare, or something that uses the glare as a medium rather than an illumination.

I settle lower, down into the V-shaped troughs at the bottom of the fracture, where the fifty-degree water gathers before it steams back into the air. 

I am not one thing; I am the clear, gelatinous skin that forms where the scalding water meets the freshly cleaved granite. Wait—not the rust-colored bacteria on the fast-flowing ridges above. Those are too frantic, too dependent on the whims of the silica-slug’s dams and diversions. When the slug redirects a trickle of condensation, those upper gardens dry out, bake into brittle red dust against the quartz, and die. 

I am at the bottom, in the permanent pools. Not the blind isopods—their existence is too precarious, a constant, tapping hunger that leads them up the walls into the traps of the architect or the jaws of the dry-shelf spider. I have no legs to lose my footing with. 

I am a colonial mat of lithotrophic archaea and silicate-binding gel, lining the deepest, sharpest crease of the floor trough. 

To the eye—if an eye could bear the glowing air of this shipping-container world—I look like nothing more than a slight thickening of the water at the bottom of the pool, a faint, milky opalescence in the deepest V of the stone, like quartz that has forgotten how to be hard. 

My world is tactile and chemical, measured in temperature gradients and the slow dissolution of minerals. Because the granite sheared only a few years ago, the stone beneath me is still raw, its crystal lattices broken and unstable, bleeding fresh ions into the hot water. I lie pressed against these microscopic wounds in the rock. I do not eat the sun—there is no sun—and I do not eat the flesh of the crustaceans. I breathe the slow, invisible exhalations of the deep crust rising through the micro-fissures in the floor: hydrogen sulfide, methane, the faint, metallic tang of iron and dissolved silica.

Above me, the water surface is a ceiling of blinding silver. I do not see it, but I feel the radiation of the room's excited air pressing down through the few inches of scalding water, warming my upper membrane. It is a heavy, buzzing warmth, different from the deep, slow heat of the geothermal stone beneath me. I exist in the sandwich between these two fires: the ancient, conductive heat of the Earth’s interior below, and the strange, white, atomic hum of the unmapped infrastructure miles away. 

In fact, I am the only thing in this chamber that truly registers that distant infrastructure. The spider feels only the vibrations of footsteps; the slug feels only the weight of the light; the isopods feel only the heat and the slime. But because my body is a continuous, mineralized jelly wedged directly into the hairline fractures of the floor, the resonance that excites the air above passes through me first as a physical tremor. It is a high, tight, sub-audible singing in the granite—a steady, artificial *hum* that never wavers, keeping the molecules of my gel aligned in neat, crystalline rows.

The tiny, translucent isopods walk across me constantly. Their fourteen sharp little legs dimple my surface, sinking a fraction of a millimeter into my tough, elastic skin before springing back. They graze on my upper layers, their tiny mandibles scraping away the dead, sloughed-off polysaccharide film that I shed like skin. It does not hurt. Their grazing keeps me clean; it prevents the heavy precipitated minerals from choking my pores, allowing the hot gas from the floor fissures to keep diffusing upward through my body.

Occasionally, the world above sends down a message. 

*Plink.* 

A fat drop of water falls from a ceiling tooth, hits the surface of my pool, and sends a concentric ring of pressure down through the warm inches to press gently against my back. 

Sometimes, the message is chemical. Three feet up the wall, the silica-plated slug finishes digesting its latest catch. After hours of enzymatic soaking, it loosens its muscular seal against the vertical dihedral. The next sheet of weeping condensation flushes the empty, softened shell of the isopod—along with a rich, cloudy soup of dissolved nitrogen, phosphorus, and stray digestive proteins—down the sheer granite face. 

It slides into the pool with a soft *hiss*. 

I do not have a mouth, nor do I have the mind to anticipate a meal, but as the warm plume of organic waste diffuses into the scalding water of the trough, my entire surface responds. Millions of microscopic pores across my gelatinous expanse dilate. I drink the rich runoff of the slug's private stomach, pulling the scarce nitrates down into my matrix, using them to thicken my gel and push my margins another millimeter up the jagged feldspar wall.

I am the floor of the ledger. Everything the slug builds, everything the spider drops, every flake of rust-bacteria that peels off the hot quartz ridges eventually washes down into the troughs. I catch it, dissolve it, bind it with the rising gases of the deep rock, and turn it back into the clear, slick skin that feeds the isopods, who will tomorrow climb the walls to feed the slug again. 

The rock ticks as it settles under the mountain's weight. The white air hums its sourceless, steady note. And in the scalding bath at the bottom of the mirror-room, I hold the raw edges of the broken stone together, growing thicker in the glare.
