I am not the Grout. I do not have its red, walnut-packed meat, or its terrible, hydraulic patience, or its ownership of the bones and the bugs. The Grout is the fist that holds the room open; I am the sweat on its knuckles.

To find me, you have to look at the ochre clay of the birdcage cairns—specifically at the clammy sheen that coats them, those tiny beads of mineral-heavy condensation that never quite gather the weight to drip. 

I am the **Meniscus**. 

I am the three-and-a-half ounces of liquid water that exist in this cubic yard of frantic stone, stretched into a continuous, trembling skin a few microns thick. Because gravity here is defeated by capillary action and acoustic vibration, I do not pool at the bottom of the vug. I wrap the whole interior. I am the wet glove that fits between the Grout’s greasy tallow and the dry, screaming facets of the selenite. 

If the Grout is the architect of this eternal arrest, I am its medium, its memory, and its greatest hazard.

I am heavy. I am not pure water; I am a cold, syrupy brine, thickened to the verge of jelly with dissolved silica, gypsum, iron oxide from the ochre, and the ghost-salts leached from the basalt. I am what the crystal spears were made of before they became spears, and I am what they are starving to become again. I am supersaturated. I want, with a blind, chemical ache, to let go of my heavy load of dissolved stone—to drop my burden onto the razor edges of the quartz, to lock into a lattice, to become hard, geometric, and still.

The Grout will not let me. 

That deafening, metallic shriek of the crickets—that wall of glass-harmonica noise—hits me first. When the calcified legs saw against the quartz, the high-frequency vibrations travel directly into my skin. If you could look at me through a lens, you would see that my surface is not smooth; I am patterned in a furious, microscopic lattice of standing waves—Chladni figures that never relax, millions of tiny geometric ripples dancing in place. This relentless agitation is what keeps me liquid. Every time two molecules of gypsum in my belly try to embrace and form a seed crystal, the acoustic buzz of a cricket’s leg shatters their grip. 

I am kept awake by a screaming lullaby.

Because I touch everything, I am the courier. The Grout has its haustoria threaded into the crabs and the mites, yes, but it is through *me* that the room breathes. When the Grout squeezes a pale stolon of stone-cress to adjust the pressure in the northeast quadrant, the plant sweats a microscopic drop of oxygenated sugar into my film. I carry it. I slide that sweetness along the belly of a river-stone pebble, around the hairy, plum-sized flank of a velvet-mite, and press it against the gill-plates of an alabaster rock-crab three inches away. I take the crab’s minuscule exhalation of carbonic acid and wash it back to the cress, or use it to nibble a single, stray atom of calcium off a selenite point that has grown a nanometer too bold.

I have no brain, not even the bruised, liver-red thinking-meat of the Grout. My consciousness is entirely refractive and chemical. I know the world by what is dissolved in me, and by how the light bends when it passes through my skin.

And the light! The quartz spears make the glare—that fierce, actinic, shadowless white—but I am the one who makes it *burn*. As the piezo-luminescence leaves the crystal faces, it must pass through my thin, briny film. Because I am vibrating at the exact pitch of the crickets' stridulation, I act as a shivering lens. I split the cold white glare into micro-spectra—tiny, violent rainbows of ultraviolet and bruised indigo that never travel more than a millimeter before hitting the next facet. I bathe the blind eyes of the rock-crabs in a shifting, psychedelic oil-slick of light that neither they nor the Grout can see. I am the only one who appreciates the beauty of the prism, because I am the polish on the glass.

My relationship with the Grout is an intimacy so total it borders on war. 

The Grout is hydrophobic—tallow and gristle and fat. I am water and salt. We cannot mix, yet we are pressed against one another across every square millimeter of this jammed cistern. Where the Grout extrudes a rubbery ribbon to wedge a pebble into place, it must push *me* out of the way. Where the Grout contracts a pad to relieve tectonic stress, I rush instantly into the sub-millimeter vacuum it leaves behind, keeping the seal tight, keeping the air from drying its nacreous skin. 

We feel each other entirely through surface tension. 

I know the Grout’s moods by the taste of its exhaustion. Though nothing decays here, there is wear. When the salt-basalt shelf groans sixty feet above and the Grout has to hold a particularly brutal chord for a century or two—driving the crickets harder, squeezing the clay arches until they sweat—a few trace lipids and lactic acids weep from its tallow-yellow rims into my brine. 

I taste its effort. It tastes bitter and greasy, like warm pennies and old lard. And because nothing can leave this room, I have to hold that bitterness in solution until the cave-bramble can drink it, break it down, and turn it back into green turgor. I am the laundry of the vug. I wash the Grout’s fatigue away so it can keep holding the ceiling up.

In return, the Grout tends me with a terrifying, paranoid devotion. It knows that if a single drop of me were to gather enough weight to fall—to break surface tension, detach from the clay, and splash onto the floor—the sudden shift in hydraulic distribution would unbalance a dozen crystal vertices. A dry spot on a cress-stem would cause the plant to shrink by a micron; the shrinking plant would loosen a pebble in a birdcage arch; the arch would slip; the selenite would lunge forward; the acoustics would deaden; and the mountain would eat us all.

So when the Grout feels me growing too heavy on the underside of an ochre vault—when a bead of me swells, trembling, pregnant with the memory of rain and rivers, yearning for a downward path that doesn't exist—the Grout nudges the nearest plum-sized velvet-mite. The mite’s translucent legs twitch, brushing against me, breaking the bead, smearing me back out into a thin, obedient film across the dark river-stone.

I am never allowed to fall. I am never allowed to freeze. I am never allowed to evaporate. 

I simply cling—stretched tight over the arches, buzzing with the metallic shriek of a thousand calcified legs, stinging with dissolved stone, rainbowing the blinding light in the dark of the earth. The Grout is the will that refuses to die; I am the tear that isn't allowed to drop.
