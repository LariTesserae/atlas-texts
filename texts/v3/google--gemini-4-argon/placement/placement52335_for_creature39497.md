I am at the Great Knot, two hundred and eighty feet down from the violet sky, where the dusty gold of the upper shaft bruises into the heavy, smoky amber of the lower tiers. 

If you look for me from the basalt walls, you will not see me at first. You will see only a massive, hairy chandelier of braided ironwood roots, greased lianas, and twisted copper cables hanging in the exact center of the three-hundred-foot gulf. Six primary suspension lines—each as thick as a man’s thigh—meet here in a terrifying, woven fist of tension, holding up the mid-tier granaries above and anchoring the swinging net-gardens below. 

I am perched on the underside of this fist, hooked by my horn-soled heels to a loop of three-ply vine, hanging slightly backward over two hundred feet of warm, twilight air. 

They call me the Ear at the Center, though my actual name is a three-note whistle that drops at the end like a pebble falling into mulch. I am old enough that the grey granite-grease has worked its way permanently into the deep creases around my eyes and knuckles, making me look like a piece of carved, oily soapstone. My eyes are neither the slit-yellow of the Rim nor the liquid-black of the Sump; they are the cloudy, amber-brown of the mid-shaft, though I rarely keep them open while I work. Out here in the middle of the air, sight is a distraction.

My job is to listen to the town’s weight.

Right now, the palm of my left hand is pressed flat against the North-Rim Main, a cable of braided copper wire wrapped in cured gecko-hide that angles up into the misty glare above. My right big toe is wedged firmly into the fork of the Sump-Drop, the great hemp-and-vine hawser that plummets straight down into the sweet-rotting dark at the bottom of the world. Between my knees, I hold a tuning-fork made from the hollowed, dried rib of an ancestor, notched and strung with a single, taut strand of human hair.

Through my heels, my palms, and my groin, the entire settlement hums. 

To someone from the flatlands—if any such people still exist beyond the fifty-million-year desert—this shaft would sound like madness. The din is a physical pressure against the eardrums: the dry, papery roar of ten thousand swallows beating their wings in the upper crannies; the hollow *chak-chak-chak* of the bull geckos echoing off the wet basalt lobes; the relentless, saw-toothed drone of the moss-beetles; the clatter of bone-looms; and over it all, the piercing, silver ricochet of five hundred people whistling across the void. 

But I do not listen to the air. I listen to the bone and the fiber.

A town that hangs by threads speaks through its threads. When I press my jawbone directly against the greased copper of the North-Rim Main, the noise of the birds disappears, replaced by the deep, submarine groan of the structure. 

*There.* A dull, rhythmic *thrum-thrum-thrum* in the third strand from the left. 

That is the Great Wind-Wheel on the east ledge, four tiers up. The thermal draft is steady—it is always steady, the warm, sulfurous breath of the mountain’s belly pushing up past my bare shoulders at the same pace a tortoise walks—so the wheel should turn with a smooth, sleepy sigh. This *thrum* has a hitch in it. A dry tooth on the main bone-gear is catching. Left alone, it will jerk the haul-line; a jerk on the haul-line will set the mid-tier tuber-gardens swinging; a swinging garden will chafe its anchor-vine against the basalt lip until the fibers fray, and six tons of black soil and woven wicker will plummet into the Sump, taking three kitchens and a bridge-clan down with it.

I do not look up. I simply tilt my greased head back, purse my lips, and throw a whistle at the smooth, bulbous lobe of wet rock forty feet to my left. 

I use the sharp, two-fingered pitch that cuts right through the gecko-barking. *Check the third tooth on the East-Haul,* I whistle, shaping the notes so they curve off the polished stone and fly straight up into the misty gold of the fourth tier, landing right in the ear of the wheel-tender’s apprentice. *Give it a thumb of lizard-fat. It’s biting.*

Three seconds later, a high, chirping trill drops down through the hanging curtains of silver tillandsia: *Heard, Old Knot. Greasing now.*

A moment passes. Through my jawbone, I feel the *thrum-thrum* smooth out into a greased, velvet purr. I grunt, a deep, barrel-chested rumble that nobody hears but the fat, scar-nosed gecko clinging to the woven basket-pod five feet above my head. 

The basket-pod is my home. It is no larger than a womb, woven from split ironwood withes and plastered on the outside with grey ash-and-guano mud to keep the draft from drying my skin while I sleep. Inside, there is only a bowl-shaped mat of agave fiber, a stone trough of sage-scented granite dust, a few clay jars of tuber-paste, and my small collection of bone-scales and counterweights. I have no porch, no hearth-stove, no rail-hens clucking around my ankles. Fire is forbidden at the Great Knot; a single stray spark here in the dry rigging at the shaft’s center would drop the town into the compost pit in ten minutes. My meals are sent down to me on a little zip-line from the third-tier kitchens—warm balls of mashed, sulfur-sweet root wrapped in waxy bromeliad leaves, which I eat with one hand while the other stays resting on the main lines.

People think I am lonely out here, hanging like an orb-weaver in the empty center of the stone throat. They are wrong. Nobody in the Chimney is less alone than I am. I am touching everyone.

When the two young net-weavers from the Rim sneak out onto the fourth-level cross-brace after the twilight-horns have blown, thinking nobody can see them behind the thirty-foot beard-moss curtains, I feel their combined weight—one hundred and sixty pounds of compact, muscled youth—step onto the secondary guy-wire. I feel the wire sing a quarter-tone higher. I feel the rhythmic, nervous shift of their four opposable big toes gripping the bark, and then the sudden, quiet settling of the line as they sit pressed together, sharing a dab of rationed water from a wet-thumb. I do not whistle their mothers. I merely reach over to my trim-lever and shift a ten-pound bag of pumice two inches to the left to keep the fourth-level brace from sagging into the swallow-roosts, and let them have their hour in the warm mist.

I know when a Sump-dweller is climbing up from the dark long before their pinhole-goggles breach the amber light; their cadence on the vertical rope-ladders is different from the mid-tier folk—slower, heavier, their legs accustomed to wading through waist-deep black mulch and blind millipedes rather than swinging through the rigging. 

And I know when someone has died.

It happened an hour ago, up near the top, where the violet sky burns against the stone rim. I felt the sudden, collective stillness on the Upper-West terrace—twelve people all stopping their bone-looms at the exact same instant. When a dozen looms stop clacking at once, the tension in the terrace-stays relaxes by a fraction of an inch. Then came the slow, low, mourning-whistle, bouncing down the curved walls from tier to tier, a sound like wind over an empty clay jug, until the whole five-hundred-foot cylinder was vibrating with it.

Old Marra of the Hair-Combers finally lost her grip on the thin air.

Now, I am waiting for her to pass me. 

I unhook my heels, swinging down by one thick, grey-gold arm until I am perched on the lowest lip of the Great Knot, where the Sump-Drop cable passes through a massive ring of polished mammoth-bone—a relic from the basin floor brought up ten thousand years ago when our ancestors first climbed into this warm vent to escape the dying world. 

Above me, the golden mist parts. 

The great bone-wheels at the rim are turning backward, groaning under the brake-levers. Down through the center of the shaft comes the bundle, wrapped tightly in a worn-out dew-net of braided gray and black human hair. It descends slowly, spinning a half-turn to the left, then a half-turn to the right, dropping through the shafts of dusty sunlight, past the hanging gardens of bloated agave, past the barking lizards and the cooing rock-doves. 

As she passes each tier, I see the little, compact figures of my people clambering out onto the tips of their ironwood perches and the edges of their mud-brick pods. Nobody speaks. Only the birds and the beetles make noise. As the bundle drifts past the third tier, twenty hands reach out across the gulf—not touching her, for she is already balanced for the drop—but flicking a tiny, invisible bead of moisture from their thumbs into the rising draft, letting the warm Breath carry their water up against her shroud.

I reach to my belt and unstop my own personal vial—a hollowed gecko-femur plugged with wax. I tip it against my left thumb. The water is warm and tastes faintly of clay and sulfur. In a place where we clean our skin with granite dust and measure our lives by the dampness of moss, this wet spot on my skin is as bright and heavy as a jewel.

The hair-net shroud drifts down into the bruised-amber light, ten feet from the Great Knot. Through the coarse mesh of shorn hair—hair that once belonged to me, to her, to our grandchildren, all twisted together—I can see the familiar, barrel-chested shape of her, her knees drawn up tightly to her chin, her horned feet hooked together one last time so she will not feel vulnerable on the long fall. 

As she passes my level, I do not flick the water into the air. The Sump-Drop cable is right in front of me. I lean out over the two-hundred-foot abyss, holding the main line with my left foot, and press my wet thumb directly against the taut, descending rope just above the mammoth-bone ring. 

The coarse plant fibers drink the tiny smear of water instantly. A fraction of an ounce of my moisture travels down the line with her, down into the hot, bacterial dark where the giant millipedes are already waiting to turn her back into the town.

In two years, when the haul-baskets bring the black soil back up to the mid-tier nurseries, they will plant a new liana shoot by the West Anchor. In twenty years, a child with my broad shoulders and her squinting, Rim-clan eyes will braid that vine into the Great Knot to replace a fraying strand.

I pull my torso back into the web of ropes, hook my heels over my resting-loop, and press my ear back against the copper main. Below me, the shroud disappears into the smoky twilight of the Sump. Above me, four tiers up, the bone-looms begin to *clack-clack-clack* again, one by one, taking up the slack, putting the weight back onto the lines. 

The cables hum at their proper pitch. The warm, sulfurous breath of the deep earth rises between my toes, steady as a pulse, and I shift my shoulders half an inch to the right to keep the world balanced in the dark.
