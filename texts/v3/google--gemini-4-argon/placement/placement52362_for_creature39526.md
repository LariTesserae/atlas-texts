I am not in the dome. I am three miles down the wind-carved coulee, where the pale silver of the bark-plate darkens into the color of dried blood.

Long ago—perhaps three hundred years, perhaps a thousand—something massive died here, or the mountain range far beneath the continent shifted its shoulders and cracked the wood. The plateau bled a lake of emergency resin to seal the fault. Now, that ancient wound is a half-mile sweep of translucent, golden-red stone, planed utterly flat by the gritty winds. It is the great amber-scar. To the cord sleeping up on the ridge, this is a quarry. To me, it is a lens.

I am old enough that the airborne world is entirely gone. Even when I unlace my leather cap in the dark of a shelter, there is no hiss of wind, no brass rattle of the stridulator valleys. My eardrums ossified twenty years ago into solid, protective pearl. Yet I have never been less deaf. My knees, my pelvis, the calloused pads of my collarbones, and the flat of my jaw are tuned like the belly of a lute.

I am the one who walks two miles ahead of the family, alone, my pith-sleds whispering over the grain. They call my kind *fault-readers*, though when we sit on the hoop and hum, the word feels more like *the tooth that tests the rind*. 

Right now, it is the deep middle of the night. The starlight is cold and surgical. The stridulators have gone entirely silent, clamped flat against the cooling wood like billions of silver coins, waiting for the sun to restart their blood. Without their screaming treble, the plateau belongs entirely to the bass. 

I am lying flat on my belly in the center of the red amber-scar, my quilted coat unlaced so my bare sternum presses directly against the petrified sap. 

Living wood mutes and bends sound; it is wet, fibrous, and full of its own local business—the gurgle of a capillary vein, the slow creak of widening grain. But ancient amber is dead, homogenous, and hard as glass. It reaches down like a monstrous tooth miles into the dark phloem-canyons of the continent, and it conducts vibration from the deep interior with terrifying clarity.

Lying here, my body is the ear of the cord.

Through my ribs, the *thrum-shuck* of the vascular rivers is not a simple lullaby. It is an ocean of distinct, braided currents. I can feel the main arterial trunk four miles beneath my boots—a slow, syrupy surge that takes12 heartbeats to complete its stroke, heavy with dissolved iron and deep-earth sugars. Cross-cutting it, higher up, is the dry, fluttering whistle of an empty resin-duct that hasn't flowed since my grandfather’s time, resonating like an organ pipe as the night wind blows across some distant, cracked burl where it breaches the surface.

I shift my chin, pressing the point of my jawbone into the cold glass of the scar, and cast my attention outward across the skin of the world.

Three valleys east, a herd of the heavy grazers is feeding. Through the amber, their basalt teeth don't make a wet crunch; they register as a dry, rhythmic *zip-zip-zip* in my molars, like someone tearing stiff bladder-cloth. There are twelve of them. One is limping—or rather, rolling off-balance; its acoustic signature has a heavy, lopsided *thump* every fourth revolution, probably a shed plate that hasn't cleanly detached. They are moving north, following a vein of young, sweet cambium toward the low mesas. Good. My family can stay at the violet vent for another two days without pulling up the condensers.

I slide my jaw an inch to the left, tuning out the grazers, and listen deeper.

This is why I came down here in the cold, leaving the warm, pith-lined dark of the shelter and the shared hum of my niece’s stories on the hoop. Yesterday, while we were skating across the whale-backed ridges at noon, I felt a catch in the plateau’s breath. It was tiny—a microscopic stutter in the great vascular pump that no one else in the cord noticed through the thick spongy soles of their sleds. 

I wait. The stars wheel overhead. A ribbon of warm, leftover dusk-fog drifts across the amber-scar, smelling of wet iron and turpentine, beading instantly on the oiled leather of my breeches. I don't bother to scrape it; my water-bladders are full, and if I move, my bones will lose the thread of the deep sound.

There. 

It comes up through the roots of the amber-scar, a vibration so low it doesn't register in my inner ear at all, but in the hollow of my pelvis and the liquid of my eyes.

Deep down—perhaps two miles beneath the surface, where the wood is pale, blind, and frantic with growth—the continent is walling something off. 

I can feel the wet, cellular tearing as the plateau forces gallons of scalding sap into a new cavity, building a cyst of dense knot-wood around an intrusion. Something has bored up from the buried mountain range below. Not an animal; the deep-borers have a sharp, frantic, ticking rhythm I know well. This is a slow, grinding shear, the sound of true stone—the mythical, dead bone of the planet that we never see—shifting in its sleep and tearing the root-mat.

Because of the tear, the great vascular river beneath this ridge is rerouting. 

I close my eyes behind my bone goggles, mapping the hydrodynamics of a living continent in the dark inside of my skull. A valve the size of a valley is slowly squeezing shut down there, starving the capillaries above it to keep the sap from bleeding out into the subterranean dark. 

I know what this means for the surface. Within a month, the violet stomatal vent where my family is currently sleeping will cough its last plume of steam. Its bruised, slick lips will dry out, turn silver, and pucker shut into a blind, healed navel. The stridulators in the valley below will feel the phloem lose pressure; they will pull their drills from the wood and rise in a single, sun-blackening cloud to drift downwind toward wetter pastures. The shelter we have maintained for forty years, carefully paring back the encroaching callus-wood every spring and autumn, will become useless—a dry dome in a dead zone.

And yet, a mile further down this coulee, where the rerouted sap is backing up against the wall of the amber-scar, the pressure is building. The wood there will grow hot and thin. Soon, the bark-plate will blister, split with a sound like a thunderclap, and birth a new vent—raw, violet, and gushing virgin steam rich with deep-earth fat.

I lie on the cold amber for another hour, memorizing the exact geometry of the deep blockage so I can hum it into the talking-hoop when the sun rises. I am not sad for the old shelter. The plateau gives nothing permanently; it only allows us to camp on the face of its slow, wooden weather.

Before I stand, I take my flaking-tool—the tip of a stridulator's drill mounted in a bone handle—and press it hard against a natural ridge in the amber-scar. *Tick.* A fresh, curved scale of deep red glass pops loose. I tuck it into my cheek, sucking on the dry, piney faintness of its taste, and lace up my coat.

Standing up, I strap on my pith-sleds. I do not strike the ground with my staff. I simply lean into the cold night wind and begin the long, syncopated skate back up the ridge, matching my stride to the new, limping heartbeat of the world beneath my feet.
