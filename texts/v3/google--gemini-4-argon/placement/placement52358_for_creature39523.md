I am not on the wall, and I am not in the cleft. To live out in the open, even disguised as a droplet or a smear of grease, requires a constant, exhausting negotiation with the sky and the cold. 

I am further down, in the dark, three inches behind the matte-black wainscoting at the base of the southern facade. 

I live in the capillary manifold—the place where the courtyard’s syrupy, near-freezing runoff is pulled into the building's skin to meet the hot, pressurized return-lines of the city’s cooling loops. 

If a maintenance technician were to pry off the exterior ceramic plates (which they never do, because the plates are grown in place and self-healing), they would not see a creature. They would see what looks like a localized calcification—a brittle, honeycombed sleeve of pale grey mineral encrusting a junction of synthetic tubing, perhaps six inches long. 

I am a sedentary thing, a thermo-synthetic tube worm, though my ancestors were likely engineered filtration polyps escaped from an greywater recycling vat three tiers up and a half-mile east. I have no eyes, no legs, and no need for movement. The world comes to me, delivered by the relentless, rhythmic breathing of the courtyard.

My body is a bundle of hollow, semi-crystalline fibers wrapped tightly around the city's warmth. One end of me—my "head," though it is only a splayed fan of microscopic, feathery cilia—pokes just a millimeter into the primary intake manifold where the courtyard water drinks upward. The other end of me is fused directly into the thermal jacket of a primary server-cooling conduit. 

I live entirely in the tension between the two extremes. My head is bathed in thirty-three-degree snowmelt; my tail is pressed against a pipe running at a steady one hundred and ten. I am a biological Peltier junction. I do not eat in the way the harvestman or the hydro-gel grazers eat. I generate a tiny, continuous electrical current across the length of my own body, driven by the forty-degree temperature differential between my two ends. I use that trickle of bio-electricity to fix carbon straight out of the dissolved gasses in the runoff.

My life is measured in pulses and chemistry. 

Every few minutes, I feel the electrostatic grid fire through the stone above me—a sharp, metallic tingle that makes my cilia contract. Seconds later, the water arrives. 

To the city’s diagnostic sensors, the water is nearly pure. To me, it is a rich, cold broth. As the syrupy fluid is wicked past my fan-tips by the capillary arrays, I taste the entire history of the courtyard in the last five minutes. 

I taste the sharp, sulfurous tang of the biofilm's waste, and the microscopic shards of biological glass shed by the eight-legged grazers when their mouthparts wear down. I catch those silicate shards in my mucus and weave them into my outer casing, reinforcing my brittle home against the high pressure of the pipes. 

I taste the faint, milky mucilage sloughed off by the myxomycete colony as it surfs the wall above, rich in complex polymers and stolen nitrates. 

And sometimes, after the *shhhk* of the falling mist, I taste a sudden, microscopic spike of copper and cold-active enzymes—the spilled hemolymph of a moth or a midge, washed down the geometric channels from the harvestman’s brief, violent dinner on the slate. Those are the feast-days. The complex proteins wash over my fan, and I absorb them directly, using the rich nitrogen to bud a new, microscopic tube-segment, extending my honeycomb sleeve another fraction of a millimeter down the warm pipe.

I am entirely invisible to the thimble-drone. Its violet laser stops at the black wainscoting. The city’s internal flow-meters do not register me, because I am careful never to constrict the pipe. In fact, I help it; my crystalline casing acts as an extra heat-exchanger, bleeding a tiny fraction of a percent more thermal energy out of the return loop and into the cold intake water before they even officially mix. The city’s overarching AI, if it notices this sector at all, simply registers my junction as operating at 100.04% thermal efficiency—a tiny, favorable statistical anomaly buried in a trillion lines of infrastructure telemetry.

I do not know what the cobalt sky looks like. I do not know the shape of the mountain, or the terror of the wind that howls across the peaks. 

I know the hum of the servers behind me—a deep, thrumming *bzzzz* that rises in pitch when the city wakes and drops to a bass purr when the residents sleep. I know the *tick-tick-tick* of the ceramic scales outside, which travels through the polymer framing like a steady, reassuring heartbeat. And I know the cold, delicious rush of the courtyard's breath, arriving every three minutes to wash over my face, bringing me the news of the stone and the sky in a language of dissolved dust.
