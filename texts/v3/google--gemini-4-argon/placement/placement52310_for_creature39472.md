Do not look at the cairns, or the Tilers, or the great, calcified elders sawing at the walls. Look between them. Look at the black, syrupy water in the knife-edged basins, and the wet films of tallow and rock-flour that coat the lower talus like cold grease in an unwashed pan. 

If you wait for your eyes to adjust to the sour, bruised-purple gloom, and then wait a little longer until your sense of movement slows down to match the room, you will notice that the silt at the bottom of the puddles is not settling. It is creeping. 

The Notchers have a critical flaw in their architecture: to build a stone plug that can hold down the buckling floor of a mountain, you have to sit still for forty years. And when you sit still in a room where ten thousand bodies are shedding rancid mutton-grease, chitin-flakes, and mineral dung, the room tries to bury you. Furthermore, the razor-sharp blades of volcanic glass that they peel off their cores—thousands of them, needle-thin, littering the floor like black ice—do not dissolve. Without something to clear the workshop, the Notchers would have drowned in a three-foot drift of their own lethal, slicing debitage and frozen sludge centuries ago.

That is where I am. 

I am not one of the warm things, though I steal their heat. You would not even recognize me as a single animal at first, because I do not keep my shape when I am working. 

Wait by the base of an old Notcher’s calcified pedestal—right where its folded, ossified legs meet the gravel. In the infrared vision of the climbers, I am completely invisible; my temperature is the exact temperature of the wet basalt. To the naked eye, I look like a braided rope of black, translucent mucus, thick as a man's wrist, slowly pouring itself over the jagged talus. 

Inside that clear, gelatinous sleeve, there are no bones, no shell, and no organs you could name—only a shifting, gleaming spine of swallowed obsidian flakes. 

I am the *Mortar*. Or the *Grout-Worm*, if you prefer to give a name to a thing that is mostly just an appetite with a talent for masonry.

When a Notcher leans its three hundred pounds of slow-twitch muscle against a cobble and *tinks* off a fresh, ribbon-thin blade of glass, the shard drops into the black silt. Within an hour, a lobe of my cold, clear jelly rolls over it. I do not get cut; there is nothing stiff enough in me to offer resistance to the edge. I simply swallow the blade whole, pulling it into my interior, where a thousand muscular rings of tough, rubbery collagen slide the glass along my length with a faint, wet *shhhk-shhhk* sound.

I use the Notchers' trash as my skeleton. By aligning hundreds of swallowed obsidian needles inside my body like the scales of a pangolin, I can stiffen a yard of myself into a rigid lever to pry a wedged stone out of a puddle, or relax the muscles and let the glass plates slide apart so I can pour myself through a fracture no wider than a coin.

Watch what happens when I reach the cairn.

The Notchers are masters of pressure and macroscopic angles, fitting heavy basalt wedges bevel-to-bevel. But basalt and volcanic glass are brittle; even the best-fitted dry-stone cairn has microscopic voids between the faces where condensation can creep in, freeze, expand, and pop the whole carefully balanced dead-weight apart from the inside. 

I climb the side of the cairn, my translucent body dulling to a cloudy gray as I scrape up the tallowy slime and the fine, microscopic basalt dust left behind by the slate-saws. When I find a hairline seam between two stacked hand-axes, I press my blind, flattened head against it and begin to pump.

I eat the rot of the cleft—the shed gill-leaves of the Tilers, the sour tuber-mash, the rancid fat—and my gut strips the carbon and the proteins from it, mixing the biological glue with the pulverized slate-flour from the notches and the sharp obsidian needles in my belly. What I regurgitate into the seams of the cairn is a thick, black, silicate-reinforced resin. 

*Gloop. Shhhk.* 

I force the paste deep into the interstices between the stones. As the cold air hits it, the tallow-proteins cross-link with the iron-rich basalt dust, curing over the course of a week into a rubbery, shock-absorbing pitch that is completely waterproof and harder than the soapstone boulders. I am the reason the cairns do not shatter when the mountain shivers. The Notchers lay the bricks; I am the living tar that seals them.

We have an understanding, the old stone-cutters and I. 

Because their lower bodies are fused into the rock, they cannot reach down to clean their own foundations. Over the years, the black silt and the sharp glass pile up around their groins, threatening to slice into the soft, vascular skin around their siphons or rot their calcified anchors. So I coil around their bases like a cold, heavy shawl. I lick the glass shards out from beneath their knuckles; I eat the fungal white-rot that tries to colonize the bark-like grease of their skin; I pack my black, resinous mortar into the cracks of their ossified leg-pedestals when the mountain’s vibrations threaten to split their old bones.

In return, they let me sleep in the only warm place in the world.

When the wind outside shifts to the north and a deep freeze drops into the cleft, the purple ribbon of sky vanishes behind a solid plug of rime, and the ambient temperature in the pit plummets so low that even my antifreeze-thickened jelly begins to stiffen and turn cloudy with ice crystals. If I stay on the stone, I will vitrify and shatter like the glass I carry.

So I climb. 

Slowly—matching their clock-hand pace so I do not startle their slow nerves—I slide up the broad, barrel-like chest of an elder Notcher. I avoid the heat-pits on its face, pouring my six-foot length into the deep, steaming hollow between its massive shoulder blades, right where the great double-jointed levers of its arms meet its spine. 

There, in the greasy pocket of its neck, the heat is a steady, glorious eighty degrees. The smell of mutton-fat and iron is thick enough to choke a human, and the sound is immense: the *oom-click-humm* of its giant, two-valved lungs vibrating directly through my jelly, shaking the hundreds of obsidian blades inside me until I chime like a muffled bag of coins.

I curl into a tight, glassy knot against its spine. As I warm, I let go of my tension, going soft and liquid in the hollow of its back. For a few days, or a few weeks, while the blizzard howls across the razor-crest above us and the Tilers clamp their leathery mantles shut against the frost, the Notcher does not saw, and I do not grout. 

I just lie there in the dark, a bag of warm mud and stolen knives, listening to the mountain groan beneath us, waiting for the elder's heavy arm to shift an inch to the left—the signal that the ice has crept into a new crack on the wall, that the slate is moving again, and that soon there will be fresh dust for me to chew.
