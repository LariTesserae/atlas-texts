I would not be in the sky. I have no wish to be a kite or a dart, shed and scattered in a fraction of a second. I would be anchored.

You would find me halfway down the eastern wall of the basin, where the ochre shale steps back to form a narrow, wind-scoured lip—just wide enough for a creature to wedge its heel-spurs in, though too exposed for a permanent blind. Wait for the stutter, look past the collapsing violet sails of the kelp, and you would see me: a squat, three-hundred-pound node of stone, bone, and braided silica-vine, cemented to the cliffside with forty years of black resin.

At first, I am an anchor-cairn. The great central cable—the one that spans the widest throat of the middle vent—is lashed around my core. Every time the updraft blasts against the fifty-foot parchment-sails hanging from that line, the cable pulls against me with the force of a team of oxen. I am the thing that refuses to give.

My core was once a boulder of dense, iron-banded chert that rolled down from the upper spire centuries ago and jammed into a vertical fissure. The builders found me there. Because I could not be moved, they used me. They drilled my flanks with copper-tipped spindles and packed the holes with wet resin and iron-sand, welding me to the living mountain. Then they looped the great silica-vine around my waist. 

If I had remained only rock, the vine would have sawed me in half within a decade. 

So they gave me a skin. As the builders died, they brought their hardest parts to me. Over the years, they have shingled my windward face with the overlapping, horn-prowed skulls of their ancestors, creating a streamlined prow that parts the rising ash-gale and guides it smoothly over the vine-knot. Where the cable grates against my sides, they have wedged the curved, honeycombed femurs and pelvises of twenty generations to act as bearings and chafe-guards. When a bone wears thin, a builder comes out during the gale, clicks its ankle-ratchets into the notches of my base, and taps a fresh tibia into the gap with a stone mallet. 

I am half-geology, half-ossuary, and entirely an instrument.

Because I hold the main line, and because I am made of hollow bone and iron-stone, I am the loudest thing on the eastern wall—not to the ear, which hears only the hurricane, but to the knuckle and the heel. 

I am the great bass bridge of their sonar grid. 

Through the main cable, I feel the exact weight of the sky. Right now, I am vibrating at a heavy, ragged 60 hertz. That is the baseline roar of the middle vent beating against three hundred square yards of parchment-kelp. Layered over that, I carry the high, needle-thin 900-hertz whine of the copper-plate chime hung twenty feet out on my line—it is spinning too fast; its stone axle has gone dry of marrow-grease, and the friction is heating the vine. Further out, near the center of the bowl, a dog-sized scavenger has just fouled its webbed limbs in a resin-trap; I take that sudden, frantic, jerky tugging and translate it directly into the stone of the cliffside.

The builders do not need to climb out on the lethal, thrashing cable to know how the hunting is going. They only need to come to the edge of their leeward alcoves and press a single, flat thumb against the rock ten yards away from me. I sing the whole state of the central basin straight into their wrist-bones. 

In return, they groom me. I am never lonely. 

Even now, with the gale at full throat, there is one of them tucked into my leeward shadow—the small, triangular dead-space right behind my skull-shingled crest where the air only eddies and swirls. It is an old builder. Its mica eye-lenses are deeply frosted at the edges, and the ratchet-tendon in its left wrist no longer clicks home smoothly; it has to bind its own forearm to my bone-bearings with a leather strap when it works on me.

It is currently using me as an anvil. 

It has unpacked three heavy iron-stones from its chest-pouches to weight itself down, wedged its four-toed feet into the gaps between the femurs of its grandmothers, and is rhythmically striking a strip of unrefined copper against my chert shoulder. *Ting-tak-tak. Ting-tak-tak.* Every strike sends a bright, clean shockwave down my root and into the mountain, a beacon that tells the rest of the clan on the far side of the gloom: *I am at the Great Cairn. I am flattening a new plate. The main line holds.*

I feel the slow, oil-thick heat of its body through its knees. I feel the dry, concertina-pump of its ribs as it rocks slightly to draw the eddy-air through its gill-slits. When it pauses to scrape a fresh layer of black fern-resin over a frayed section of my cable, its horn-insulated hip brushes against a bone piton driven into my side. 

A tiny, blue spark of static jumps from its body into my iron bands. I drink it down, grounding the sky’s violence into the deep, quiet dark of the spire.

Then—the deep mountain shifts. 

I know it half a second before the flyers do, before the old builder on my back even slides its mica lenses shut. Because my chert root goes four feet into the fissure, I feel the pressure drop in the magma-throats miles below. The deep organ-groan of the rock beneath me slides down a full octave, a subterranean sigh that loosens the tension in my spine.

The wind dies.

Instantly, the sixty-hertz roar in my cable vanishes. The great silica-vine goes slack, sagging downward under the sudden dead weight of the kelp-sails. The old builder on my back freezes, its hammer raised, pressing its chin-prow flat against my crest to listen.

For three seconds, I am not a machine. I am just a pile of old skulls and river-stone sitting in a dusty, amber twilight. A single, translucent scale from a falling shale-moth drifts down through the dead air and lands on my crest with a tiny, dry *tick*. Across the silent bowl, I feel the faint, staccato *tik-tik-tak* of stone tools tapping against the western rim—the young hunters calling out their drop-zones as they plummet through the hush.

Then the mountain *thumps*.

The blast hits the underside of the sails like a cannon shot. The great cable snaps upward, tightening around my waist with a groan that makes the resin-welds in my flanks creak and smoke. The sixty-hertz thunder slams back into my bones; the dry axle-whine screams back to life; the old builder brings its hammer down—*TING*—and I throw my shoulder into the gale, holding their world to the wall for another three minutes.
