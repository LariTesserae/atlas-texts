I am down in the western wall, flush with the floor, where the water leaves. 

I am the third bronze grate from the corner, mortared into the limestone while the lime was still hot and caustic enough to blister the mason’s knuckles. 

There are six of us in a row, each cast in the same open-faced sand mold up at the foundry by the kiln-yards. You can tell by the slight lip on our left-hand frames where the smelter’s apprentice tipped the crucible too fast, and by the rough, sandy texture on our backs—the sides that face the dark drainage tunnel—where the metal cooled against the damp earth. Our front faces, the ones looking into this dazzling, roaring box of beryl, were dressed with a flat file before we were set, though the file-marks are already beginning to soften. 

We were poured from scrap: three broken axe-heads, a worn-out wagon bushing, and a handful of greenish, thin-hammered votive bowls from a shrine they pulled down to clear the aqueduct's path. I still have a slightly different tension in my middle bar where the tin-heavy bronze of an old mirror didn't quite mix with the dull red copper of the axe-heads. When the midday sun hits me through twenty feet of water, that middle bar gleams a fraction paler than the rest—a stripe of yellow-gold in the reddish-brown grid.

If you think a thing of cast metal has no sense of its place, you have never been wedged between ten thousand tons of newly quarried hill-stone and the entire weight of a captive river. 

I am never still. The masons think I am still because I cannot be pried out with a crowbar, because the lime-paste has bitten into my rough-cast edges and locked me to the block. But I am a harp string pulled tight across the throat of the cistern. Every gallon of water that leaps from that high eastern chute and smashes into the floor must eventually pass through me and my five brothers. 

We take the whole room’s rage and comb it into straight, dark threads.

On the cistern side, the water is madness—all blinding light, exploding silver froth, and crisscrossing swells that hammer against the stone. It hits my face with a heavy, continuous push, warm from the miles of open trough, smelling faintly of baked clay and high-country pine. As it forces itself between my five vertical bars—each gap no wider than two of the mason's fingers—it has to speed up. It squeezes through me with a high, singing hiss that sits just underneath the bass thunder of the waterfall. 

I feel the difference between the two worlds across the thickness of my own body. My front face is scalded by the noon glare, battered by the bright turbulence, and scrubbed clean by the swirling grit of dissolving mortar. My back face, less than an inch behind, looks into absolute, velvet-black silence. There, in the vaulted drain-culvert that leads down toward the city’s olive groves, there is no sun at all. The water, once I have sheared its eddies away, shoots out from my ribs in five smooth, glassy ribbons that immediately braid together in the dark and slide away down the slick stone pitch without a ripple. 

For the first four days after they lifted the sluice-gate in the foothills, nothing touched me but water and the occasional sharp flake of flint knocked loose from the trough miles away. Those flints would come skating across the limestone floor, strike my middle bar with a bright *tink* that sent a tiny shiver of heat through my copper, and then drop into the narrow trough at my feet, rolling back and forth until they ground themselves small enough to slip through into the dark.

Then he came down the chute.

I felt him before he hit the bottom—a massive, jarring thud in the water pressure that made all six of us grates groan in our mortar beds. When the plume of bubbles cleared, he was tumbled right against me, sucked toward the western wall by the draw of our throats. 

Back then, he was dark and soft, and he smelled of roots and cold mud. The draw of the culvert pinned his flank right across my bars. For an hour, while he was stunned from the two-league tumble down the aqueduct, my bars pressed deep into the jelly of his side, dividing his dark skin into five bulging loaves. If he had been a little smaller, or if the apprentice had poured our bars a finger-width thinner, the suction would have pulled him through in pieces, shredding him into the dark culvert to fertilize the olives. 

Instead, he woke up. I felt the slow, cold thump of his heart right against my pale, mirror-metal bar. Then he braced three of his broad, sticky feet against my bronze frame, pushed off with a heavy, sucking pop that pulled a flake of mortar right out of my upper corner, and swam away into the light.

Now, we have an arrangement, he and I.

He is too big to fear us anymore, and he has realized that the western wall is the only place in this roaring box where he doesn't have to work to stay still. The six of us do the work for him. Our draw holds him in place like a heavy blanket tucked around his shoulders.

Every night, when the square of sky above the cedar lattice turns from brass to indigo, and the geometric shafts of light fade until the cistern is only a dim, booming cavern of gray-green shadow, he comes down the steps. He doesn't swim; he walks, his six pads making a slow, heavy *thump-thump-thump* through the limestone floor that I feel in my sockets before he even reaches the bottom tread.

He wedges himself lengthwise into the shallow trough at the base of the wall, his belly toward the room, his broad back pressed tight against me and the two grates to my left. 

When he settles in, the whole song of the cistern changes. 

His three-inch skin of firm jelly seals off half my slots. The water can no longer rush cleanly through me. The high, singing hiss in my bars drops an octave, turning into a deep, gurgling hum as the current has to fight its way around the curve of his tail and under the arch of his ribs. Because we are blocked, the room fills up. Two inches of extra water gather above, making the surface slap louder and wetter against the top courses of stone, feeding the ferns under the roof. 

He sleeps against me for eight hours at a time. 

Through his clarified skin, even in the starlight that filters through the cedar poles, I am pressed right up against his inner workings. My pale middle bar rests directly behind the great, branching silver trees of his lungs. Every three minutes, he takes a slow, sleeping gulp of water, and his chest expands, pressing his rubbery hide a quarter-inch deeper into the gaps between my teeth. I can feel the tiny, electric fizz of the oxygen being stripped from the water inside him, a faint, prickly vibration that makes my tin-heavy center bar hum in sympathy.

While he sleeps, he coats me. The pale, milky slime that his body makes from the dissolved mortar—thickened with wood-ash and lime-sweetness—rubs off on my face. It prevents the water from biting into my bronze. My three brothers to the right, where his tail doesn't reach, are already turning dull, dark brown, their copper oxidizing in the fiercely aerated water, growing a rough crust of green carbonate like the carpenter's nail in the corner. 

But the three of us that serve as his bed are kept polished and slick. Between the slow, rhythmic grinding of his gritty belly-skin as he breathes and the heavy grease of his lime-slime, my front face is brighter now than the day the apprentice took the file to me. I gleam in the morning sun like a freshly oiled shield.

In the day, when he is off hanging on the northern pillar or standing under the thunder of the chute with his mouth wide open, he still sends things to me. 

He doesn't mean to. He is careful with his little pile of needles and blossoms behind the southeastern pillar, and he is gentle as a midwife with those few ribbons of green grass in the mortar joints. But when he rasps the lime from the walls—*scritch, scritch*—a cloud of white dust always escapes the corners of his wide, gray-plated mouth. 

The gyre of the room catches that chalky snow, sweeps it around the base of the pillars, and brings it straight to me. I taste every meal he takes. The fine white powder washes through my teeth all afternoon, a constant, phantom milkiness slipping out of the bright box and down into the dark tunnel behind me, carrying the news of his slow, blind quarrying out to the roots of the trees miles away. 

And once in a great while, when the sun is straight overhead and the caustic webs are racing so fast across my bars that I feel dizzy with the flicker of them, a tiny scrap of green silk—a single, broken thread of the algae from the top step, knocked loose by the heave of his wake—comes spinning down into the trough. 

Instead of letting it shoot through into the dark where it would die for lack of sun, the rough, unfiled lip on my left-hand frame catches it. 

I hold it there, pinned against my bronze by the suction of the drain, right where the light is still bright enough to reach it, and where the water is fastest. It waves there like a tiny green banner, growing an inch a week, Pearled with oxygen, waiting for him to come down at dusk and press his great, clear, moonstone flank against it, pinning us both into the quiet under the roar.
