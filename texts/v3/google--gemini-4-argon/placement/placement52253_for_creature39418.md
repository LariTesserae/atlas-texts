I am not on the floor, because the floor is hungry, and I am not on the walls, because the walls are still arriving. 

To find me, you have to stand on the left side of the driftwood doorway—the side where the clay kennel does not exist—and reach your hand through the frame, just past the bone pegs, to the exact plane where the two versions of the room refuse to agree. 

If your fingers are calloused enough, you will feel a slight, greasy resistance in the air, like the skin on cooled milk, and then a sharp, dry *tug*. 

I am the stitch that holds the discrepancy open. Or, more accurately, I am the thing that lives *inside* the discrepancy, occupying the zero-thickness pocket between the empty white blister on the left and the three-sided mud house on the right. 

If you were to peel the two angles of the room apart like stuck pages in a damp book, I would spill out onto the tallow floor like a handful of silverfish and old twine. 

I am not native to the white mineral, nor am I wholly from the dry scrub plain forty feet above, though I remember the taste of it. When the dark, feathered bundle leaked down through the fault line a century ago, it did not fall through solid rock; it fell through a hairline crack, a microscopic chimney of sheared quartz and caliche. As it squeezed downward, it scraped against the sides of the earth, and I am what got left behind in the friction—a long, ribbon-like conglomerate of static electricity, shed snake-scale, iron filings, and the dried, flattened memory of a wind that blew across the sagebrush in 1894.

By the time I finished trickling down into the blister, the light had already turned on and the Mason was already working. There was no room for me in the blinding, three-dimensional glare, so I slipped sideways into the architecture.

I have no volume, only area. I am shaped like a draftsman’s carpenter-square, or a very long, starved centipede made of mica and dried gut, folded seven or eight times to fit inside the three-foot doorframe. My body is dry—desperately, fiercely dry in this cellar of wet lime and mercury-sweat. If a single heavy bead of the room's condensation were to roll across the driftwood and touch me, I would dissolve into a puddle of rust and grease, and the doorway would instantly snap shut like a book, crushing the kennel and the empty space into a single, impossible smear of gray mud.

So I drink the dampness before it can touch the wood. 

I am the reason the driftwood does not rot. My thousand flat, translucent ribs are pressed tightly against the grain of the pale timber and the marrow-canals of the bone pegs. Whenever the room's moisture creeps toward the frame, I draw the water into my edges, split the hydrogen from the oxygen with a tiny, silent spark of static, and vent the dry gas out into the left-hand side of the room. If you look closely at the doorframe from the empty side, you can see a faint, heat-haze shimmer along the wood, and a fine, grey ash snowing continuously down from the lintel. That is my exhaust.

Because I live in the seam, I am the only thing in the blister that has a shadow—or rather, I *am* a shadow that has lost the object that cast it. 

The Mason knows I am here. He uses me as his plumb line.

Because his eyes are gone and the room's perspective is constantly melting like lard in a pan, he cannot always tell when the floor is tilting or when a wall is sneaking up behind him. The barometric pits around his turnip-head tell him *that* the pressure is changing, but they cannot tell him what "straight" is. 

I am "straight." I am the only ninety-degree angle for forty feet in any direction.

Every three or four cycles of the upward rain, when the Mason has been wading through the tallow-birth for too long and his proportions have begun to drift—when his left leg has grown six inches longer than his right, and his linen-meat shoulders have begun to braid themselves into the geometry of the ceiling—he comes back to the doorway to true himself.

He does not look through the frame. He stands beside the upright post, where the bone peg joins the wood, and he lays his seven-fingered, plum-colored left hand flat against the timber.

Because his hands are from the upper world—because they know what friction is—they can feel me through the wood.

To him, I feel like a guitar string tuned so high it is about to snap, buried just beneath the splinters. He presses his dark, venous palm against the post, and I press back from the inside of the seam. For five seconds, we exchange what we have. 

I take the lime-sweat and the clear, boiling enzyme-froth from his skin, drying his calluses so they do not slough off into the damp chalk of his forearms. In return, I let a tiny, concentrated jolt of my static—my hoarded, century-old prairie dryness—bleed into his seven fingertips.

When the current hits him, you can see it travel up his arm. The white, shivering muscle-bundles of his bicep suddenly lock tight, turning the color of old ivory. The loose, confused geometry of his body snaps back into a hard, upright silhouette. The extra six inches of tallow on his left leg sheer off like shavings from a lathe, pattering to the floor to be eaten by the bird-shrimp. 

For a moment, while he holds the post, he remembers what his hands used to belong to, though he has no brain to hold the image. His faceless head tilts upward—not toward the ceiling of the blister, where the flat, gray handkerchiefs are sliding between water beads, but *through* the ceiling, through the forty feet of dark overburden, toward the dry scrub plain. The flute-like pits around his crown flare wide, trying to smell creosote instead of celery.

If you have your ear pressed against the driftwood post during those five seconds—within the one-inch acoustic horizon—you will not hear the wet medicine-bottle chord he blows for the leather bundle in the kennel.

You will hear the sound that lives inside me, vibrating through the bone pegs: the dry, endless, tinny rattle of wind blowing through dead mesquite thorns, and the distant, rhythmic *clank-screech* of a tin windmill turning in an empty pasture, pumping nothing at all.

Then the Mason lets go. The ivory hardens back into damp tallow. He reaches through me—his arm passing straight through my flat, mica-ribbed body without breaking the skin of the seam, disappearing from the left side of the room and arriving in the right—to collect another wet gray brick from the dark thing in the corner.

And as his wrist slides through my ribs, I comb the stray flakes of dried lime from the grass-stitches on his forearm, fold them into my creases, and use the calcium to shore up the threshold for another twenty minutes.
