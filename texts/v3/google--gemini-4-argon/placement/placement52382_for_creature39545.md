I am on the third tier, where the noon light never quite touches the flagstones in the summer months, though its glare bounces off the pewter underside of the *slate-agaves* across the shaft and fills my corner with a pale, tallow-colored twilight. 

I am not one of the builders who climbs to the bright rim to pry fresh glass from the sky-wall. My knees have not had the grease for the upper stiles since the great fan-palm in the middle court shed its twentieth ring of bark. If you were to scrape the underside of my forearm with a sharp piece of hornblende, the dust would come off in a solid grey curl, like paring lead with a knife. I am heavy with the mountain now. My velvet has matted and locked with seventy years of agave-chalk and wrist-oil into small, hexagonal plates along my spine and shoulders, so that when I lean back against the dry-laid basalt of the nursery wall, the stone and my skin make no sound at all—not even the papery rasp of the young ones’ coats. I fit the hollow between two old root-bolsters the way a key fits a wooden lock.

My work requires no climbing, only heat and the slow turning of my hands.

Across my thighs lies a single frond of the fan-palm, cut three seasons ago when its oils were thickest. To an eye from outside the chimney, it looks like a slab of cured boar-hide, dark brown and translucent at the edges. I have had my right thumb-spade wedged into the midrib since the sky-oval above turned from the black-violet of morning to the bleached bone of noon. I do not push the horn-spade through the fiber; pushing tears the oil-cells and lets the richness weep out into the dry air where it is lost. Instead, I lean the weight of my heavy, calcified shoulder into my thumb and wait for the heat of my own blood to soften the plant’s wax. Millimeter by millimeter, over the space of an hour, the horn parts the grain. A ribbon of inner bast curls up over my knuckle—pale yellow, smelling faintly of warm lard and sulfur, supple as a strip of raw gut. 

By the time the light above begins to bruise toward evening, I will have four ribbons. Tomorrow, another elder two ledges down will braid them into a sling to hold a new slab of stone on the fourth tier.

Nothing is wasted. The tiny shavings of dark rind that curl off the edges of my thumbnail drop into the hollow between my heels, where a juvenile sits wedged between my shins.

He is small—still soft-furred, the color of wood-smoke before it clears the chimney, his coat not yet crusted with the mineral armor of the pit. His wrist is pressed flat against the naked, ridged patch on the inside of my ankle. He has not moved for two hours, except when the horizontal membrane sweeps across his dark rosin eyes with a tiny, wet *click*—so quiet I do not hear it with my ears, only feel the minute flinch of the muscle through his skin against my shin.

Through the patch at my ankle, I am reading him, and he is drinking me.

I feel the quick, shallow flutter of his pulse—too fast, still burning the rich beetle-wax his mother fed him at dawn, though it is slowing as my own three-beats-a-minute rhythm pushes back against his tendons. I feel the slight, dry tightness of his dermis: he has been up on the fifth tier watching the masons, where the air is thin and the wind shears across the rim, and it has stolen half a dram of water from his nostrils. In answer, the gland at my ankle loosens, seeping a thick, salty film of my own old tallow into his wrist-ridges, sealing him, telling him without a single twitch of cartilage: *Still. Down here. Keep your mouth shut and your breath behind your teeth.*

Two paces away, where our ledge narrows to join the main root-bridge of the middle court, the traffic of the afternoon passes us at the speed of cooling pitch.

A female tortoise—the one with the chipped third scute on her left flank, whom I remember when she was no bigger than a mason’s mallet—is coming up the stepped ramp from the lower shaft. Her front foot, plated in dry yellow horn, lifts three inches, hangs in the sulfur-warm air for ten heartbeats, and settles onto the pumice flagstone. There is the faint *shhh-tick* of grit crushing under her sole. 

Perched between the high, terracotta domes of her shell is a girl from our own sleeping-cell, her knees hooked around the tortoise’s neck-flare, her hands busy braiding a cord of *cinder-moss*. As the tortoise’s flank brushes my knee, the girl does not look down at me—turning the head uses neck-muscle, and muscle burns tallow—but her left ear, which was folded flat like a closed pod against her skull, swings out forty degrees toward my alcove. The pale, bone-colored web of it flushes half-way up the central rib with a warm, dull brick-red, then holds steady for five seconds.

*The shelf-fungus in the lower shaft is ripe for cutting.*

Without lifting my thumb from the palm-frond, I let my right ear tip back an inch and drop the pressure in the lower vein until the rim turns the color of tallow-rind. 

*Wait until the sun clears the rim. The spores will rise less in the cool.*

The tortoise takes another step. The girl’s ear folds back down against her head, pale again. As the reptile’s hind leg clears my shin, the girl reaches down blindly with one arm and drags the underside of her wrist across my shoulder-plate—just a three-inch smear as she is carried past. The scent of crushed fern-oil, young sweat, and the sour-sweet yeast of the lower walls stays behind on my mineral crust. The juvenile between my knees shifts his chin an inch to rest his nostrils against the smear, breathing her in through his nose-baffles so he knows where the household has been today without having to climb down and see for himself.

I look down past my own flat, horn-rimmed toes, through the gap between the basalt slabs of the balcony, into the deep throat of the pit.

Fifty feet below, the twilight is the color of old pewter. The great, fused trunks of the *strangle-figs* down there do not look like plants anymore; they look like the insides of a stone throat, gray and smooth and swollen, bulging over the places where the first masonry was laid when the mountain was still hot enough to blister a bare heel. 

Just beneath the third root-arch, where the sulfur-draft rises thickest from the blind cracks in the floor, there is a rounded, smooth boss in the wood, about the size of a sleeping-sack curled on its side. The gray bark has closed completely over the niche now—even the two stone pins we used to hold the runner-vine in place forty seasons ago have been swallowed by the cambium—so that there is only a slight, knee-shaped knot in the timber where my mother’s brother went into the wall. 

A flat lizard, mottled ochre and slate, is hanging head-down right over the bulge where his forehead used to be, soaking up the steady, blood-warm heat that still comes through the wood from the stone behind his ribs. 

In five or six seasons, when the mineral crust at my hip-joints finally locks into a single plate and I can no longer bend to sit on this ledge, the juvenile between my shins—who will have his full height by then, and a thicker thumb-spade—will give me his wrist. We will take two days to go down the ramps. I will not have to leave the city, or the house, or the smell of our sleeping-cell’s tallow. I will only move three stories down into the cellar, tuck my heels against my thighs in the warm dark, and let the vine take the weight off my back so the nursery four floors up doesn't slip an inch toward the drop.

My thumb-spade reaches the end of the palm-frond with a tiny, dry *pop*. 

The ribbon of yellow bast comes free in my palm. I do not shake the dust from it. I lay it across the juvenile’s knee, wait ten heartbeats for the horizontal film to slide across my eyes and clear the chalk-haze from my sight, and set my thumb against the next seam of the leaf.
