I am down in the mid-levels, where the noon light from the central sinkhole only reaches as a pale, dusty bounce off an opposing retaining wall. My knees are wedged against a stubborn course of grey shale that wants to slide three degrees toward the old fault line, and my left thumb is pressed flat into the cold seam between two slabs, waiting to feel if the rock is still breathing.

We do not call it breathing out loud, because nobody wastes breath on metaphors down here, where the air stays heavy in the bottom of the lungs. We call it *creep*. You can’t see it. You can only feel it with the skin of your fingertips where the callus has been pared back with a piece of glass-flint—that tiny, sickening grease-slide of limestone powder moving against limestone powder under twenty tons of terrace.

When the drop happened, I was seventeen and up on the grass with two iron-shod spades and a tether-line, digging out a post-hole for a sheep fold. One moment the horizon was four miles away, flat and yellow with cheat-grass, and the next the horizon jumped fifty feet straight up and turned into a jagged lip of white bone. My brother went under the big north roll where the shale folded over on itself like wet blanket; we never dug him out, though for three months we laid our foundation walls right over the mound of him to keep the scree from shifting further into the low wells. Everyone here is built into a wall somewhere. That is why we don’t shout. You don't make a racket in a house where the floor is still settling over your people.

I am neither a Tapper in the dark bottom nor a Drip-wright up under the sails. I am a *Pitch-listener*, one of the ones who walks the vertical seams where the town meets the raw cliff-scarp of the drop.

In a soft leather sling at my hip, I carry thirty shims of black hornstone—not limestone, which crushes into meal when the weight comes on it, and not the common brown flint that shatters like cold tallow, but the dense, greasy black chert we prize out of the lowest seam near the south wall. I knap them between my knees during the mist-hours when the stone is too slick for climbing. My maul is the root-ball of an old bog-oak that came down with the turf during the fall, wrapped five times round in raw bull-hide so that when I strike a shim, it makes no more noise than a heel kicking packed dirt. *Thup.*

Right now, the mist is coming on. I don't have to look up at the linen sails to know it; the chalk on my forearms—which has been dry and itching since the sun-shafts cleared the east rim—starts to pull tight and cool. The grey flour in the scars across my knuckles turns dark, like lead pencil lines. Around me, the *clack-clack* of the masons on the third terrace changes its note. Dry stone rings high, like two goat-ribs knocked together; wet stone goes dead and heavy, like striking salt-pork.

Above my head, a waxed twine lead runs down from a roof-trough into a three-gallon belly-jar wedged into an alcove. *Plip.* A pause while I count four heartbeats. *Plip.* The air smells suddenly of the high roofs—wet hemp, the tallow they grease the wooden poles with, and that sharp, sour-sweet cellar smell of the green moss they pack around the cistern necks to filter out the rock-flour.

A boy comes down the zig-zag from the upper course. He is maybe nine, too small to remember what a tree looks like when it’s standing up, though he knows the name of every grain in the oak wedges we use down below. He walks with the basin-glide, knees bent, toes turned slightly in so his wide grass soles don’t catch the lip of the risers. His elbows are tucked so tight to his ribs he looks like a plucked bird.

As he squeezes past me in the two-foot gap between the house-back and the raw fault-face, he turns sideways and lifts his right arm. We touch the backs of our wrists together. His skin there is warm and slightly damp from the fog, the only clean three inches on him. He doesn't look at my face—people here look at hands and feet, not eyes—and taps twice with his thumbnail against his collar-bone. *Two courses up. A hollow sound.*

I let go of the shale seam I’ve been testing, pull a flat hornstone wedge from my sling, and follow him up the steps.

From the third roof-terrace, you can see a quarter-mile of the basin before the grey haze swallows the masonry. It looks like the inside of a wasp’s nest that someone broke open and then tried to mend with slate. Thousands of grey backs are bent over thousands of grey lines, rising and falling in the slow, wave-like rhythm of people lifting stone from the knees. Up toward the middle sinkhole, the column of afternoon sun is thick as churned butter with dust and mist, slanting down onto the three-acre patch of tilted linen sails. The Rig-tenders are out on the poles, dark and skinny against the glare, hauling on the stays to dump the last puddles of fog-water into the troughs before the sun-shaft moves off them.

Here is the bad stone the boy heard. It’s a lintel over a cold-cellar doorway, a six-foot slab of blue-grey limestone with a thin white vein of calcite running diagonal through its belly. Beneath its left corner, the leveling-shims have powdered. The wall underneath is bellying out by the thickness of a grass-blade. If that lintel drops two inches, the terrace above it loses its toe-hold, the three water-cisterns on the roof above that tilt and crack, and forty gallons of four-day water go straight down into the dry chalk of the lower alleys where it’ll swell the oak wedges before their time and blow out a foundation wall in the dark.

Everything here leans on everything else. You cannot have a private wall.

I kneel on the damp limestone, my oiled leather apron bunching against my thighs. I lay my ear flat against the cold lintel. The rock is freezing; it pulls the heat right out of my cheek.

Inside the stone, there is a whole small world of sound that never gets out into the air. I can hear the *rasp-rasp* of fifty people walking three alleys over. Deeper down, through twenty feet of packed rubble, I can feel the dull *thump* of a crew in the lower dark setting a big slab into its bed. And right here, six inches under my ear, the calcite vein is singing—a tiny, dry *tink... tink...* like a beetle chewing dry straw. The weight is coming onto the flaw.

I don't reach for the hornstone shim yet. If I drive a hard wedge under the corner now, the shock will run right up that white vein and split the lintel in two.

Instead, I hold my left hand out flat behind me, palm up, fingers spread wide. I don't look back. After ten seconds, the boy sets a handful of wet, grey clay-paste—scraped from the bottom of a settling trough—into my palm. I pack the wet mud into the vertical seam beside the stone to deaden the vibration, then slide the thinnest hornstone leaf I have into the gap beneath the lintel’s heel, just far enough to catch the edge with my thumb-nail.

With my right hand, I bring the rawhide maul around in a short, six-inch arc from the wrist.

*Thup.*

The little beetle-tick inside the stone stops.

*Thup.*

The pitch of the lintel drops half a note—from a tight, frightened *ping* to a dull, satisfied *clunk*. It has found its seat on the course below. The weight has gone straight down into the earth instead of sideways into the alley.

I stay on my knees for another minute, my cheek still against the rock, just listening to the town settle around the new balance. Above us, a rock wren drops onto the linen guy-rope six feet away, shakes the mist out of its slate-colored feathers, and drops down into a wall-crack after a woodlouse. Up at the rim of the great hole in the ceiling, fifty feet in the air, a single grass-head from the plains above blows over the jagged edge and comes spinning down through the blue shaft of light—dry, yellow, and strange—before landing in a stone water-trough, where a Drip-wright’s girl picks it out with two scarred fingers so it won't clog the twine lead, rolls the seed between her palms to see if it will grow in crushed shale, and tucks it into her apron.
