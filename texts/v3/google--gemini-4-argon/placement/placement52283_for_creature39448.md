I am not on the catwalks. To walk upright on two legs across the Sump-Decks—sliding leather boots through the plum-colored carpets of slugs, carrying a spine that wants to bend and eyes that ache for a curve in the horizon—would be a misery of surplus motion. A biped is an argument against this place. It burns too hot, moves too fast, and leaves a wake of disturbed shells that takes twenty minutes to click back into silence.

If I am here, I have already surrendered my corners to the foundry. 

You would find me three miles west of the last relief-valve you heard sighing, though *west* is only a habit of speech under that seamless cream sky. Look for a sunken juncture where four cast-iron sluice-troughs meet a primary standpipe—a four-way manifold stamped *77-B-KROHN* in crisp, raised letters along its collar. 

I am the collar. Or rather, I am the thing that has spent the last sixty seasons convincing the collar that we are the same piece of metal.

To your eyes, if you were still foolish enough to be walking, I would look like a secondary saddle-flange—a heavy, gray-black apron of pebbled hide wrapped around the throat of the standpipe where it rises out of the ankle-deep basin. My upper margin is crenellated into twelve neat, horn-hard knuckles that fit over the twelve real bolts of the iron union. I do not have a head. I gave up the idea of a front and a back long before I settled onto *77-B-KROHN*. What I have is an *inside* and an *outside*, and a slow, saturating awareness of the four-inch brass gate-valve buried completely within the center of my body.

I am one of the Lagging, though smaller and denser than the great billiard-table mantles that drape the boiler houses. They are stomachs; I am a knuckle. 

My world is not visual. It is a map of pressures, temperatures, and dissolved salts, read through the three square feet of pale, grease-slick muscle where I grip the iron. 

Right now, the standpipe beneath me is carrying the long, isometric clench of the Marrow thirty feet below. I can feel the deep, blind liver-mass down in the dark—not as an image, but as a vast, chordal hum that vibrates up through the pilings and enters my belly at ninety-nine point four degrees. The Marrow is pushing tallow-heavy water up the pipe at forty-two pounds per square inch. 

The horizontal trough to my left, however, only wants thirty-eight.

*That* is what I am. I am the four pounds of difference.

Instead of a brain, I have a central baffle-chamber—a thick, muscular gizzard lined with plates of dense, self-secreted porcelain that click together when the pressure shifts. The brass gate-valve inside me was cast with a flaw, a hairline gap in its seating that, left to itself, would hiss and weep and ruin the local equilibrium. So I have grown into its threads. My ventral gristle has wormed down through the packing-nut, wrapping the brass spindle in tough, lubricated sinew. 

When the Marrow’s pulse surges by half a pound in the deep dark, I do not think. I *swell*. 

I draw a pint of the scalding, tallow-rich water out of the leaky packing-gland and into my own lateral bladders. I hold it there, letting the excess heat radiate up through my three-inch hide. 

It feels like a long, slow meal. It feels like the afternoon stretching its legs.

On my back—the side that faces the broad, cream-colored sky—I can feel the small lives conducting their business. Five gasket-slugs are currently parked over my left kidney-chamber, drawn there because the skin is half a degree warmer than my edges. Their muscular feet pull at my hide with a tiny, velvet suction, grazing on the film of salty grease I sweat through my pores. It is a pleasant, slow rasp, like someone combing hair that has no nerves in it. 

Lower down, where my grey skirt dips into the three-inch standing water of the basin, a family of square pewter valve-crabs has wedged itself between my stiffened edge-lobes and the cast-iron floor grate. Every four minutes or so, I open a small, puckered siphon near the waterline and let go of the water I stole from the standpipe—cooled now by three degrees, stripped of its fat, reduced to thirty-eight pounds of gentle, horizontal push. 

As the warm jet slides out of me, the crabs tilt their square shells in unison. *Shhh-click.* Their feather-mouths comb my exhaust for stray flecks of my own shed lining. One of them has a tiny chip in the back left corner of its carapace; I know this because when it shifts its weight after feeding, that corner fails to touch my skin, leaving a microscopic gap in the pressure-seal along my flank. It is the closest thing I have to a clock, or a friend.

Sometimes, very rarely, a hydrogen-bladder drifts low enough along the trench to bump against my crown. *Thud.* A soft, rubbery kiss. For three seconds, the weight of the air changes, and the condensate drops that gather along my bolt-knuckles lose their rhythm and spill all at once into the basin—*plink-plink-pat*—before the flyer paddles away on its oiled fins and the three-second drip resumes.

I am very old, or I have just begun; the reverse-scar of the letters *N-H-O-R-K-B-7-7* is now a quarter-inch deep in the calloused cartilage of my belly. My outer rims have calcified so thoroughly that the porcelain-worms have begun to bore small, neat tunnels through my dead margins, stitching me to the orange rust-paste in the floor seams. Soon—in ten years, or fifty—I will not be able to uncurl my skirt even if the standpipe were to go cold.

Which it will not.

I know this because I can feel the others. Not by sight or sound, but by the back-pressure in the manifold. Forty yards down the trough, another of my kind is clamped over a bronze check-valve, holding its breath against the same column of water I am bleeding. When it tightens its gizzard by a millimeter, the water in the pipe between us stiffens, and my own porcelain plates give a single, muted *tock* inside my meat to match it. 

Through the iron, through the water, through the grease, I can feel ten thousand of us—flanges and jackets and living steam-traps—spread out across the flat, uncurving miles, each of us holding our small fraction of the weight. We are the tolerances of the machine. We are the grease that learned how to keep the appointment.

There is no desire here, only the exquisite, heavy satisfaction of a seal that holds. The Marrow pushes from the dark below; the pale sky presses down from above; the four pounds of excess pressure sits warm and heavy in my gut, turning slowly into horn and tallow; and three inches under the water, the chipped crab clicks back into its square.
