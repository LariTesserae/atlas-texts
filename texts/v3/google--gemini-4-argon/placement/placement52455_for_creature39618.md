I would not be one of the racing things, nor one of the patient, stationary traps waiting for the air to fall. To live in a place of pure geometry is to be tempted to become the geometry’s failure—or its memory.

I am down in the middle-south, three inches above the fuming white river of the dissolvers’ wake, where the architecture is forty days old and already growing tired. 

Here, the bone-white struts of potassium and calcium have endured ten thousand electrostatic downdrafts and ten thousand sudden, synchronous contractions of the tuning-worms inside them. The mineral is developing micro-fractures, invisible to an eye, where the crystal lattice has slipped by a few angstroms. In this silent basin, a crack is not a sound; it is an electrical dam. The static charge that wants to flow smoothly from the high northern spires down to the grounding floor of black clinopyroxene gets caught in these microscopic faults, pooling like water behind a fallen log, building a jagged, localized fever of trapped voltage.

That is where I am. If you looked through six hollow buildings to find me, you would think I was a smudge of graphite on a white needle, or a flake of black biotite mica that landed on a vertical beam and somehow forgot to fall.

They might call me a *shunt-scale*, or a *solder-leech*, though I have no mouth and nothing to bleed. I am the size of a thumbnail and thinner than a dry onion skin, composed entirely of overlapping, microscopic plates of conductive carbon and translucent horn. I do not walk on legs; I move by electrostatic peristalsis. By shifting the polarity of the plates along my underside, I slide smoothly up and down the razor-sharp salt-needles without ever quite touching them, riding on a half-millimeter cushion of magnetic repulsion. 

Right now, I am pressed flat against a load-bearing joint on the first floor of a dying ziggurat. Two struts meet here at an acute angle, and the weight of the tower above—feather-light as it is—is twisting the joint because a gray dissolver has just eaten the neighboring pillar twenty feet away. 

Above me, inside the hollow room, three hundred tuning-worms are stretched to the point of snapping, flashing silver in the grey light as they drink the panic of the leaning wall. They are greedy; they pull harder, trying to milk one last drop of piezoelectric current from the failing salt before the south claims it. Beneath my belly, the crystal strut fractures.

It makes no sound, of course. The pumice ceiling drinks the snap before it can even become a wave in the air. 

I slide over the break instantly. I flatten my carbon-plated body across the microscopic chasm, clamping my front edge to the upper crystal and my rear edge to the lower. 

The pooled charge hits me all at once. It is the whole history of the northern draft—the static scraped from the black glass cliffs, the faint violet corona missed by the caliper-mites, the frantic tension of the tuning-worms above—all of it rushing down the pillar toward the floor and finding the bridge of my back. 

For three seconds, I am a filament in a vacuum tube. The current surges through my carbon scales, and I glow—not the pale violet of the mites, nor the silver flash of the worms, but a deep, dull, furnace-orange. It is the only warm color in the entire slate-gray quarry. In the absolute cold and dead silence, I am a tiny, silent coal burning on a white wire. 

The heat of the current passing through my body does two things. First, it feeds me, driving the dry, solid-state chemistry of my thin tissues, allowing me to flake off a new layer of horn at my margins. Second, the brief thermal spike partially melts the potassium-salt directly beneath my belly, fusing the micro-fracture shut in a glassy, smooth scar. 

When the charge has grounded itself into the black floor below, my orange glow fades back to the dead charcoal of a mica flake. The joint beneath me is whole again, welded by my passage. Above me, the tuning-worms go dark and slack, cheated of their collapse for another hour.

I do not stay to admire the repair. The air around my strut is growing chill and heavy; looking down through the mesh of the floor, I can see the milky, two-inch tide of acid vapor creeping over the black glass toes of the pillar, and the flat, oiled shadows of three screed-crabs skating through the mist, waiting for me to miscalculate and drop.

I reverse my polarity. Smoothly, without the twitch of a limb or the scratch of a claw, I slide four feet up the vertical white line, take a ninety-degree turn onto a cantilevered arch that points north, and glide across the empty avenue into the younger, tighter geometry of the middle city, hunting for the next silent break in the grid.
