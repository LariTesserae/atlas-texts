I am in the Valve-House on the North-By-West Spur, three hundred yards out from the stamping mills, where the rock shelf narrows to a blade of wet hornblende no wider than a canal boat. 

There is no road here. To reach my post, you must walk the top flange of the forty-inch cast-iron main that carries the city’s waste-steam out to the rim-condensers. The pipe is warm underfoot, coated in a two-inch pelt of velvet-black moss that squelches grease-water at every step, and the brass hobnails of my boots have worn a bright, raw groove down its spine. 

I am a *Bleed-Tender*, though the guild-rolls in the Lower Ward still list my grandfather’s title: *Keeper of the Sump-Weight*. 

My booth is six feet square, built of dry-stacked basalt slabs that were quarried so recently their inner faces still smell of struck flint. There is no mortar; the stones are held in place solely by the two-ton cap of pig-iron cast onto the roof to keep the wind and the unrendering from lifting the walls. Inside, there is a brazier of perforated boiler-plate, a bench of rough bog-oak, and the three great wheel-valves that govern the pressure in the western mains. The wheels are four feet across, their rims wrapped in salted goat-hide so my palms do not freeze to the brass when the fog comes in off the unformed edge.

If you were to look at me through the narrow, unglazed slit of my doorway, you would see mostly mantle. Mine is older than I am—eighty pounds of felted wool, stiffened with forty years of boiled tallow and black lead until it stands up around my ears like a diving bell. When I sit on the oak bench, the hem settles around my iron boots with a heavy, greasy *thwack*, sealing my lower half away from the draught. A pale-green crust of sulfur-lichen grows in the deep crease of my left elbow, thriving on the heat of the brazier and the sweat of my arm; I never scrape it off. It is good company, and its tiny, cup-shaped fruiting bodies turn a bright, warning yellow whenever the air-pressure drops toward a thinning.

My face is wide and flat, the skin under my eyes the color of boiled lard, cross-hatched with the fine blue tracks of burst veins from a lifetime of breathing thin, iron-tasting mist. My left thumb is missing its top joint—lost thirty years ago not to a gear, but to a faulty stone on the Third Viaduct that went briefly translucent while I was leaning against it; when the mason’s Plumb-Setter drove his wedge in to snap the granite back to density, the stone closed up around my knuckle like freezing water, and I had to leave the tip behind in the pier. You can still see it there, if you take the noon tram and look down as you pass the fourth trestle: a little grey smudge of bone, three inches deep in the solid black rock, keeping the bridge honest.

My work is simple, and it takes all twelve hours of the pewter day. 

Fifty yards beyond my booth, the mountain stops. It does not drop away into a cliff; it simply ceases to have an opinion about itself. There is a jagged curb of obsidian where the rock has finished arriving, and beyond that is the *Grey-Slack*—a three-mile expanse of stuttering, pearl-colored fog where the granite and the sky have not yet been sorted out from one another. From that bank comes the sound: the low, wet-finger-on-a-rim hum that sets the back teeth aching and makes a man’s shadow want to slip its moorings and crawl toward the boilers.

The great forty-inch main does not end at my booth. It runs straight out over the obsidian curb, supported on a trestle of iron girders that grow increasingly sketchy the further out they reach—the last three bents are little more than dark-grey charcoal lines drawn on the mist, held up by the sheer, stubborn pressure of the steam inside the pipe. 

Every twelve minutes, by the heavy lead-weighted clock that ticks like an axe-stroke on my wall, I must stand up, take the four-foot iron spanner from its hooks, and walk out onto the flange. 

I do not look down, because twenty feet below the pipe, the bottom of the gorge is still making up its mind whether to be five hundred feet deep or ten, and the sight of the basalt rising and falling like slow grey dough is bad for the stomach. Instead, I watch the goats. There is an old, four-horned billy—his coat so matted with coal-grease and mineral-lime that he rattles like a bag of slates when he walks—who likes to sleep wedged between the third expansion-joint and the girder. If the goat is there, chewing his cud of bitter quillwort with his yellow, horizontal eyes half-shut, the iron under him is solid. A goat will never put its hoof down on a piece of the world that hasn't finished its math.

I walk out to the seventh collar, right where the air begins to taste like an unwashed copper penny and the bristles of my mustache start to lose their shadow. There is a relief-cock there, a massive tapered plug of gunmetal. I fit the spanner to the square nut, brace my iron-shod heel against the flange, and lean my whole barrel-chest into it until the plug groans and turns a quarter-inch.

A jet of thick, greasy cylinder-bleed—water that has been through the high-pressure turbines of the stamping mills six times until it is heavy with dissolved iron, tallow-scum, and coal-soot—roars out of the nozzle and sprays straight into the Grey-Slack. 

That is the whole of the trade. We do not vent the steam into the sky; we feed the grease-water to the edge. 

When the hot, filthy soup hits the stuttering fog, there is a sound like a wet blanket thrown onto a stove-lid—a heavy, sizzling *flump*. The unrendered mist cannot abide the dirt. It cannot stay vague when it is covered in mutton-tallow and iron-scale. Before my eyes, as the white plume settles, a yard of the pearl-colored nothingness shudders, curdles like sour milk, and drops three inches with a dull, leaden *thud*, turning into wet, greasy shale. It is ugly stone—rotten and black and smelling of old pistons—but it has weight. You can stand on it. Next month, the Gage-Men will come out with their wheelbarrows and their bronze transits, drive three-foot spikes into the crust I’ve made, and lay another twelve feet of pipe.

Between the twelve-minute turns of the clock, I have my routines to keep the hum out of my marrow. 

I keep a lump of rough slag the size of a loaf of bread on my lap, and while I sit by the brazier, I rub it against the cast-iron breast of my coat. *Scritch-scritch. Scritch-scritch.* In tune with the four-second drop of the stamps half a mile behind me. If I stop rubbing for more than ten minutes—if I let my eyes go soft watching the pale, blind condenser-eels that sometimes flop out of the drain-cock and curl around the base of my brazier for the warmth—I feel my heels begin to lighten. The hobnails stop biting the floor. My left hand, the one with the three-jointed thumb, begins to look like a smudge of charcoal on wet paper, and I find myself thinking strange, un-Oakhaven thoughts: wondering what a tree looks like when it grows out of dirt instead of basalt cracks, or trying to remember the name of a town where the sun was yellow and round instead of a pale hole in the wool. 

When that happens, I don't reach for the clack-board. I reach into the coal-scuttle, take out a three-pound wedge of cold pig-iron, and drop it straight onto the instep of my boot. The dull, bruising throb that climbs up my shin is as good as a hot meal; it brings the purple back into my cheeks and puts the edges back on my fingernails.

At four o’clock—when the pewter light goes the color of an old kettle and the thousands of sheep-sized rock-fowl begin to waddle single-file along the forty-inch main, coming back from the fern-beds to roost against the warm brickwork of the North Shelf—my nephew comes out to relieve me. 

We do not speak as we pass on the narrow stone shelf outside the booth; the cliff-face opposite is too fresh, a single sheer acre of black glass that dropped into place only six winters ago, and even a grunted *good-night* would come back at us five times like a handful of thrown gravel. 

Instead, as he steps off the mossy pipe onto the basalt, he lifts his ironwood paddle and gives two short, flat raps against his thigh. *Clack-clack.* (Pressure steady; the goat is on the third girder.)

I tap once on the iron lintel of the door as I squeeze past him, my tallow-stiff skirts scraping against his like two iron plates passing in the dark. *Clack.* (Mind the seventh collar; the air is thin on the left side.)

On my way back to the tenements, I do not take the cable-car. I like the walk along the Lower Flume, where the great, cabbage-headed rosettes grow six feet tall in the warm dribble of the condenser-troughs. The martens run alongside my boots on the iron grating, four seconds left, four seconds right, their long pale necks weaving through the mist like shuttles in a loom. 

Sometimes, if the shift has been unusually quiet and my hips feel light in their sockets, I take the long way round by the Central Plaza. I don't have a wife in the slow tenement—my people have always been North-Spur folk, too heavy and grease-soaked to slip the clock—but I like to stand for ten minutes by the iron railings and look up at the third-story eaves. 

The copper-winged swifts that got caught in the slow-air there last autumn have come down about four inches since the beginning of the month. The lead bird—a big cock with a smudge of soot on his breast—has finally gotten his beak open to take a beetle that has been hanging two inches from his tongue since the winter solstice. Standing there in the dripping gloom, with my hands in my pockets wrapped tight around my lead weights, I watch the water-bead on his tail-feather. It hasn't fallen yet, not by the breadth of a hair, and it gives me a slow, solid satisfaction in the pit of my belly to know that when I come back next Tuesday with my oil-can and my spanner, it will still be there, holding its shape against the fog.
