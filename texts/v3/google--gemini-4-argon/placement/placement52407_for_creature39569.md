I am at the bottom of the steps, where the lowest terrace of the eastern mound meets the talc of the plaza, and I have been rising from my crouch since the light began to thicken toward evening.

My left knee is locked; my right is taking the weight. Between the two bones of the joint, the cartilage clicks—a dry, private sound that never leaves the skin, swallowed before it can even reach the air by the grinding brass wall of the cicadas. That sound—the air-sound—is not something I hear anymore. It is simply the weight of the atmosphere. It presses against the thick membranes of my ears the way the stone presses against the pads of my feet. It is a solid thing. If it ever stopped, I think our skulls would burst outward into the sudden vacuum.

In my left palm lies the wedge. Its dark stone is warm from my skin, the crescent edge tucked into the deep crease between my thumb and forefinger, a crease that was shaped by the stone just as the stone was shaped by the crease of my grandmother’s grandmother. My right hand is flat against the slumped mud-brick of the retaining wall.

I am not rising to walk. I am rising to listen.

Down in the dust, the tremors are muddied by the root-mats and the cushioned shuffling of a tortoise herd passing three hundred yards to the west. I can feel their bellies dragging through the gypsum—a soft, grainy slur that blurs the bedrock. To hear clearly, I need the bone of the mound itself. I need the old masonry, fused by ten thousand years of its own weight into a single bell of baked clay.

My shoulder blade touches the wall. I lean my weight into it, letting my center of gravity settle backward until my legs no longer have to work. My heels sink an inch into the drift of shed locust-wings at the base of the plinth. A beetle, round and matte as a pebble, is chewing at the edge of my calloused toe; it mistakes my skin for a fallen strip of leather-tree bark. I do not flick it away. It is only taking the dead horn of my heel, which I would otherwise leave in the dust anyway.

The light overhead is darkening from ochre to the color of an old bruise. The insects feel the drop in temperature before I do. All across the three-mile basin, in a wave that starts under the shadow of the eastern rim and rolls westward, the millions of saws on millions of wings slow their strokes by a fraction. The roar drops into its night-register—a heavy, chest-rattling thrum that makes the loose dust trickle from the lip of the terrace above me in a thin, pale thread.

And underneath that thrum, rising up through the foundation stones, through the fused brick, into my shoulder blade, up my neck, and into the heavy bone behind my ear: the tap.

*Thock.*

A long pause, while the beetle on my foot finishes its meal and begins to climb toward my ankle.

*Thock-thock.*

It is coming from the north-northwest, from the High Buttress where the agaves grow thick along the collapsed aqueduct. I know the hand that strikes it. The rhythm is slightly uneven—the second strike of the double-beat always trails by the breadth of a breath, because the hitter’s elbow was crushed by a shifting lintel forty seasons ago, and the bone knitted back thick and slow.

He is telling the basin about the great gray agave on the third tier.

I close my outer eyelids, then let the heavy, translucent inner lids slide across to seal the moisture in. In the red-black dark behind my eyes, I see the map of the city—not as the swifts see it from the dusty vault above, a scatter of green-furred hills in a bowl of flour, but as a web of tight, resonant lines. Bone-lines. Stone-lines.

*Thock. Pause. Thock-thock-thock.*

The stalk has risen, he says. It has cleared the canopy of the leather-trees. The buds are swelling with the bitter water. In three moons, or four, the skin of the pods will split.

Two mounds to my left, someone answers. It is a lighter strike—a smaller wedge, or perhaps the heel of a young one’s foot against a hollow cistern roof. It repeats the phrase back, confirming, passing the word southward toward the deep groves where the bedrock dips and the signal grows faint.

I do not add my stone to the chorus. Not yet. My task is smaller, closer to the dirt.

Twelve paces out into the plaza, where the silver-scrub thins into pure talc, there is a sink-within-the-Sink—a shallow dimple no wider than the length of my body, where an ancient cellar vault finally gave way to the weight of the dust three winters ago. The depression is catching the drift. Every evening, when the cool air slides down the cliffs, it eddies around this mound and drops its load of gypsum into that hole. Already the pale moss on the lip of the collapse is choking, its fibrous hairs clogged with dead white powder that carries no vapor. If the moss dies, the crust will break. If the crust breaks, the next ten years of wind will scour a gully right under the roots of the three braided trees that shade this flank of the mound.

The city does not fall all at once. It falls by an inch of choked moss.

Slowly, while the poem of the northern agave echoes back and forth across the dark floor of the world, I peel my shoulder from the wall.

My hip joints grind, warm and dry. I slide my left foot forward through the brittle drift of insect husks. It takes a hundred heartbeats for my weight to transfer. A flightless owl, grey as clinker, is squatting on the lowest step just above my head; its yellow eyes turn in their sockets to watch my crown pass beneath it, then look away again. To the owl, I am just a boulder settling deeper into the silt.

When I reach the edge of the depression, the night-tide has begun.

I feel it first on the back of my neck—a slow, heavy river of cooler air, rolling down from the unseen rim three miles away, sliding over the terraces, spilling down the soap-worn stairs into the plaza. It smells of the upper rock: flinty, sun-scorched, barren, yet carrying that tiny, desperate ghost of dampness wrung from the night sky above the plateau.

I stop at the lip of the choked hollow. I let my knees bend, taking ten minutes to fold myself down until my wide pelvis rests on my heels. Around me, the silver-scrub is stirring slightly in the draft, millones of tiny leaf-hairs standing up to comb the breeze. The cicadas on the mound behind me are drinking it too, their spiracles pulsing slowly.

I tilt my head back. I unseal my lips—they stick slightly with the chalky paste of my saliva—and let my jaw drop. My tongue unrolls, heavy and dark and rough as a rasp, resting over my lower teeth, exposed to the dark flow.

I do not think in words while I drink. Words are made of stone and impact; they belong to the wedge and the wall. While the air washes over the deep fissures of my tongue, I think in textures. I feel the dry silt settling onto the wet papillae, dissolving its trace of gypsum and salt, giving up its microscopic cargo of dampness to the sponge-labyrinth behind my nose. The water does not taste like liquid; it tastes like cold stone, like the shadow inside a crack, like the green meat at the heart of a root.

For an hour, two hours, I am no different from the agave on the northern buttress, or the cushion-moss at my knees. We are all just rough surfaces held up against the dark wind, catching what falls.

When the draft finally levels out and goes still, the roof of my mouth is coated in a rich, mineral mud. I draw my tongue back in, seal my lips, and swallow it down in one thick, slow contraction. It will be enough for tomorrow.

Now, the work.

I do not use the dolostone wedge; the stone is for cutting and speaking. For the dust, I use my hands and the broad, horned scoops of my heels.

I reach out into the dimple where the moss is suffocating. My fingers sink into the talc—it is cool and frictionless, like dry grease. Very gently, so as not to raise a cloud that would blind my second eyelids or waste the air, I cup a double-handful of the powder against my chest, turn my torso toward the open plaza where the midday thermals run strongest, and lay the dust down on the hard-packed flat.

One handful.

From the western mounds, a new vibration enters the soles of my feet. Someone has begun the Long Count of the Rim-Fall—the old, old rhythm that tells how the sky broke and the desert stayed above while the garden dropped into the cool dark. It is a winter poem, heavy and regular, four beats and a rest, four beats and a rest, like the walking of the great tortoises.

I turn back to the hollow. My joints creak in time with the bedrock. I slide my hands beneath the silt again, feeling for the tough, wiry threads of the buried moss underneath, careful not to bruise them.

I will clear a space the size of my thigh before the amber light returns to the ceiling. By the time the northern agave splits its pods, the moss will have breathed again. And a hundred years from now, when my own dry ribs are braided into the roots of the upper terrace and the cicadas are singing from my brow, the three trees on this flank will still be standing, combing the dark wind for those who come to sit against my bones.
