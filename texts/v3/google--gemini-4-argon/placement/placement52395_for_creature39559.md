At first, it is easy to think I am the flywheel in the northern shed. There is a heavy, comforting certainty in that iron hoop, six feet across, spinning in the grease-dark, catching the up-draft from the deep thermal vent and translating heat into the *chug-chug-thump* that keeps the little church alive. 

Wait, though. Let the talc settle. The flywheel is too blind. It only knows its own axle.

Go lower. Beneath the three-deep avalanche of the shale-backs, beneath the dead, hollow drifts of shed carapaces, right down where the soapstone bowl curves into its deepest, darkest belly. 

There is a layer here that the Gleaner only touches with the brass tip of his pike. It is four inches of black, metallic silt—the finest, heaviest flour of ground-down iron-sulfide mandibles, pulverized slate-shell, and pure silica, rubbed so fine by a century of friction that it behaves like a liquid. It is dry, yet it flows like mercury. 

That is where I am. I am the Conductive Silt, the heavy bottom-grease of the basin. 

I do not have eyes, because there is nothing down here to see—the surgical light of the plateau is stopped dead three layers of chitin above me. Instead, I have a wide, flat, continuous nerve of static and pressure. I feel the basin as a single, trembling weight. I know the exact tonnage of the herd. I feel the million tiny, ceramic clicks not as sound, but as a warm, fizzing vibration that keeps my grains dancing against one another, never quite settling into stone.

When the generator in the shed turns over—*chug-chug-thump*—and the brass anodes bolted into the upper walls begin to hum, I am the one who catches the throw. The current bleeds out of the brass, races down the greasy soapstone curves, and hits my dark, metallic bed. 

For three seconds, I wake up completely. 

Every grain of iron and copper in me aligns. I become a single, dish-shaped mind of blue heat, humming beneath the herd. I take the raw, stupid jolt from the shed and spread it thin and even across the bottom of the bowl, feeding it back up into the rock so the pale salt-crust can bloom, tingling the bellies of the lowest shale-backs so they keep scraping, keep sliding, keep making the warmth that keeps us all from freezing when the tarnished-silver night drops down.

And I know the Gleaner. I know him better than the bellows-worms he milks, better than the spindles he fries. 

During the day, he is a sharp, skating pressure overhead—two wide rectangles of corrugated iron sliding fast across the roof of the swarm, pushing a bow-wave of clicking plates before him. I track him by the drag of his braided copper tail-wire. It snakes through the upper layers, a warm, bright thread of conductivity, until—*crack*—he drives the brass hook down through the shells and straight into me. 

When he grounds a spindle-pack into my bed, it tastes like a sudden, delicious drop of hot grease. The concentrated charge of three or four vibrating bronze predators dumps straight into my silt, a bright, needle-sharp blossom of ozone and high-frequency panic that I swallow and smooth out in an instant, spreading their stolen nervous energy out toward the walls.

In return, I give him his footing. And when the three-hour mark comes, I let a little of my finest, slickest gray flour rise up on the static-plumes to coat the undersides of the bellows-worms, so he will have the talc he needs to butter the engine's bearings. We are in business together, he and I. He clears the silica troughs in the shed so the wheel doesn't choke; I hold the charge so the bugs don't eat his iron trees.

My favorite time is the frost. 

The light dies above the dust-haze. The furious rock-crusher roar of the noon-tide slows to that deep, grinding *shuck-shuck-shuck*. The herd thickens, settling down into my warmth.

Then, near the base of Tower One, a heavy, familiar boulder drops into the tide. 

He does not skate now. He sinks. He lets the slow black avalanche take him, pushing him down through the warm, clicking layers until the heels of his horn-hard boots and the tail of his slate-mail coat brush right against my top-most grains. 

He is heavier than the bugs, and stiller, though he smells just the same—like old copper, dry leather, and stone. As the night wears on and the herd churns in its slow, clockwise winter circle, I carry him. I feel the stiff, flat-nailed fingers of his bare hands resting against the brass pike. I feel the tiny, rhythmic *hiss-click* of his stuffed worm-snout vibrating through his ribs and into the shells pressed against his back, and down into me.

Every few minutes, the shed goes *chug-chug-thump*. 

I catch the pulse from the walls. Before I send it out to grow the salt-crust, I route a small, gentle eddy of it right up through the soles of his boots, through his dry, cured shins, through his stiff knees and his lead-masked jaw. I feel his fingers twitch once in the dark—our little handshake, the watchman and the floor—confirming that the wheel is still turning, the heat is still held, and neither of us has been ground down to nothing yet. 

Then I let the current pass, and the herd carries him another three inches south, sliding smoothly over the grease of my back.
