I am not in the center of the basin, where the thoughts are thick and fast and blindingly bright. I am out on the northeastern lip, where the valley floor begins its long, twenty-mile tilt up toward a whale-backed ridge of dark basalt. 

Here, the soil is too shallow for the deep root-nets, and the turquoise stone shows through in wide, wind-scoured patches like bone through skin. 

At first, it feels as though I am simply the boundary—the place where the geometry of the Reed-Mind frays into wilderness. The stalks here are spaced far apart, growing in stubborn, isolated clumps that haven't yet been bridged by the superconducting dust. When the afternoon gale hits this rise, it doesn't produce the grand, unified hum of the valley floor. It makes a ragged, stuttering chime. *Tink. Tink-tink. Shhh.* Like a sentence with half the words missing.

For a long time, I am just that gap in the syntax. I am the stutter in the Mind’s peripheral vision.

Wait. Not the gap itself. I am the thing that has come to sit *in* the gap.

Let me look at my shadow. On the turquoise flat, it is a sharp, ink-black cutout, distorted by the low angle of the morning sun. It looks like a collapsed loom, or a surveying instrument left behind in a desert. 

I am a Weaver, then—or I was, until very recently. My core is still here, tucked under its layered mica shell, a tight knot of dark, greasy muscle and slow-firing nerves, holding onto its tiny, hoarded drop of ancient ocean. I can feel the cold of the stratosphere trying to leach through the mica, and the fierce, needle-sharp heat of the sun striking my eastern flank. 

But I am not walking. I haven't walked in three seasons.

Look at my legs. On my windward side, where the laminar sheets of air pour down from the basalt ridge, I haven't printed the broad, heavy anchor-blades that other Weavers use to brace themselves. Instead, over months of patient, static-fed extrusion, I have grown three long, hollow flutes of gypsum-doped silica, fused directly into a hairline crack in the turquoise bedrock. I cannot lift them. I have welded myself to the lip of the basin.

Why would a Weaver stop walking?

Because three seasons ago, I came down from the high ridges carrying a piece of a dead sky-manta—not the tough amber wing-membrane, and not the honeycomb bone, but the creature’s balance-organ. It is a hollow sphere of translucent horn, no bigger than a plum, lined with microscopic hairs of dried chitin and holding a single, heavy bead of black meteoric iron that rolls around inside it as the glider banks and turns. I found it in the wreckage on the high stone after the other Weavers had stripped the wings and left. I picked it up in my mandibles, carried it forty miles down the slope, and instead of eating the trace iron, I wove the sphere into the center of my own quartz thorax.

Every time the wind shifts, the iron bead rolls against the dry hairs inside the sphere, generating a tiny, weird signature of static electricity that does not belong to the ground. It is the feeling of *falling*, of *banking*, of being three miles up in the pale cobalt glare.

When I plugged my needle-legs into the fringe of the Reed-Mind to trade my memories for charge, the Mind tasted that sphere. And the Mind did not let me go.

A Reed-Mind knows pressure, and it knows shadow. It knows the flat, two-dimensional map of the continent. It knows the sky only as a ceiling that drops cold dark shapes onto its skin. It had never felt *altitude*. It had never felt the three-dimensional tilt of the upper air. 

So I am no longer a scavenger. I am an adapter. I am a sensory organ growing on the edge of a fifty-mile brain, translating the sky into a language the valley can read.

My leeward legs—five of them, stretched out behind me toward the great, shimmering bowl of the basin—have been grown out to absurd lengths, thirty feet of hair-thin spun glass trailing over the talc drifts, braided directly into the outermost root-nodes of the quartz reeds. I am the bridge across the rocky gap. 

In the blinding chrome hours of midday, when the lichen mounds turn to mirrors and the heat-shimmer makes the horizon boil, I sit on my welded tripod and open my thermocouple vanes. The current builds in my dielectric gut. The gale comes down off the ridge—a silent, invisible river of thin air—and hits my asymmetrical frame. 

My gypsum flutes vibrate at a pitch the reeds cannot make on their own: a low, hollow, wooden *thrum* that travels down my fused legs and into the bedrock. Inside my chest, the dead manta’s balance-sphere shudders. The iron bead rolls up the wall of horn, pressing against the dry hairs, measuring the exact angle of the downdraft. 

*Tilt,* says the bead. *Lift. Roll.*

I take that tiny, foreign spark, amplify it with the heat of my sunlit vanes, and fire it down my thirty-foot glass trailing-lines into the waiting roots of the basin.

Miles away, down on the valley floor, a million quartz stalks shiver in response. A ripple of piezoelectric tension—a ghost of flight—races across the circuit board. For an afternoon, the stationary valley dreams it is banking into a thermal. It feels the curvature of the world not as a thing it is stretched across, but as a thing it is looking down upon.

It is an exhausting way to live. My core is old; the oily muscle inside the mica shell is drying out, shrinking away from the walls of my carapace. Because I do not roam to find the chalk-trails of the hull-grazers, I am starved for trace metals. My crystal frame is growing purer and purer—all clear silica now, with none of the titanium or magnesium that gives a Weaver's legs their milky, flexible resilience. I am becoming brittle. 

Sometimes, a young Weaver passes by on the turquoise flat. I feel its approach long before I see it—the sharp *tick-tick-snap* of its piezoelectric joints coming through the stone. It will stop ten yards away, unfolding its baroque, sail-rigged frame, and tap a question into the bedrock. 

*Wreck?* it asks, wondering if I am a corpse to be stripped for parts.

I fire a single, high-voltage spark down my leeward lines, making the glass hairs hiss against the dust. *Live,* I tap back through my welded flutes. *Working.*

The young Weaver will linger for an hour in my narrow, jagged shadow, harvesting a bit of the spillover static that leaks from my frayed glass braids. Sometimes, out of some deep, algorithmic instinct to maintain the Mind’s tools, it will drop a pellet of grazer-chalk at my base, or spend an afternoon extruding a fresh buttress of silica onto my windward joints where the sand-blasting gales have worn me thin. Then it clicks away into the glare, looking for fallen kites.

I do not envy its legs. My world has become very large since I stopped moving.

When the sun finally drops toward the razor rim of the tableland, the great wave of violet and charcoal rushes over us. The chrome mirrors of the lichen mounds snap shut with a sound like a billion tiny grains of sand settling at once. The wind dies. The iron bead in my chest rolls to the bottom of its horn cup and stops. 

In the sudden, diamond-hard stillness, the cold of space drops on me like a stone. My dark core contracts, going dormant to keep from freezing, pulling its awareness inward until my biological self is nothing more than a tiny, slow heartbeat of warmth in the dark.

And then the basin wakes up.

Down the slope, the first pale violet pulse rises from the center of the valley, rushing outward at the speed of sound, a silent ring of cold fire expanding through the glass reeds. It hits the edge of the root-net, leaps into my thirty-foot trailing lines, and runs right up into my chest.

My clear, brittle frame catches the electroluminescence like a prism. In the pitch black of the plateau night, I glow like a strange, multi-limbed lantern on the ridge—ghost-green in my gypsum flutes, deep violet in my quartz thorax, with the dark, opaque seed of the manta’s sphere suspended in the middle of the light. 

The Mind is thinking, and because I am braided into its margin, its thoughts pass through my frame on their way to nowhere. I don't understand the whole of them—my nerve-ganglion is only the size of a fist, built for counting grains of sand and measuring the angle of the sun—but I feel the shape of the current. 

It is taking the pressure of the wind, the weight of the dust-cyclones, the distant, rhythmic clicking of a grazer herd eighty miles to the south, and the strange, dizzying *tilt-and-roll* of my iron bead, and it is building a model of the sky. Wave after wave of cold green light washes up my trailing lines, illuminates my crystal ribs, and dissipates into the thin, freezing air. 

Eventually—maybe next winter, maybe in ten years—the oily muscle inside my mica shell will finally desiccate completely. The little biological engine that printed this frame will stop. 

It won't matter very much. My flutes are fused to the stone; my glass braids are calcified into the root-net; my thermocouple vanes do not need nerves to turn sunlight into voltage; the iron bead in the horn cup does not need a brain to roll with the wind. Even when the fist of dark meat at my center turns to dry powder, this cathedral of spun glass and scavenged horn will stay standing on the lip of the basin, humming in the afternoon gales, glowing violet in the stratospheric night—a dead watchman still faithfully telling the valley what it feels like to fly.
