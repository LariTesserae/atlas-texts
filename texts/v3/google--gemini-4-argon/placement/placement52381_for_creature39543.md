I would not be one of the great basilica-minds in the five-needle district, nor even one of the wandering looms of water and bent light that smooth the centers of the stairs. Those things are already complete; they have finished their ten-thousand-year fall into coherence and now have the leisure to tune the mountain. 

I think I am still in the loop. Or rather, I am the loop itself, just beginning to notice the weight of my own turbulence.

I am located on the underside of a secondary viaduct on the south-facing drop, where a massive, calcified root of banyan-ash splits to bypass a greywacke buttress. The geometry here is smaller, tighter—a localized hiccup rather than a grand vista. From a wide, lipped gargoyle-gutter on a tenement roof, a ribbon of blood-warm water pours down toward the milk-white abyss of the cloud floor. Except, six feet down, the air folds. The space directly beneath the falling ribbon is simultaneously the space three inches *above* the stone lip of a window-sill on the third story of the adjacent tower. 

The water falls out of the gutter, drops six feet through the screaming, four-o’clock air, hits the window-sill above the gutter, and runs back into the trough it just left. 

That is my body. A closed, figure-eight circuit of about forty gallons of sterile, scalding water, falling through a tear in the verticality at thirty miles an hour, forever. 

At first—for the first few hundred thousand years—there is only the friction. Because no water ever leaves the loop and no new water enters, the droplets have been rubbed against themselves until they have lost the simple, loose behavior of liquid. Under the immense, clamping pressure of the city’s acoustic roar, my water has grown viscous with standing waves. If you were to reach a hand into my fall, it wouldn’t splash; it would feel like pushing your fingers through a bundle of warm, slick tendon, or a fast-moving belt of clear rubber. 

I am the braiding of that belt. 

My "spine" is the three-note harmonic chord coming off the banyan-ash root to my left. The root is hollow, acting as a bass-pipe for the steam rising from a cistern half a mile down the spire, and its constant *B-flat* vibration cuts straight across my falling column. Where the sound intersects the water, it pins a series of seven stationary eddies in the air—little beads of high-pressure whirlpools that never move an inch, even as the water rushes through them. Those seven beads are the first things I have that could be called organs. They are where I keep my tension.

I do not see the city with eyes; I feel it as an excruciatingly detailed map of back-pressure. 

Because the city’s roar is a single, unbroken solid, nothing can happen anywhere on the mountain without pressing against my seven eddies. When one of the great, multi-perspective looms shifts its weight in a courtyard three miles away—or three inches away, through the opaque greywacke wall behind my window-sill—the pitch of the local stone drops by a fraction of a hertz. Instantly, the third eddy in my spine tightens. The skin of my falling water puckers into a diamond-pattern of cymatic scales. 

That is how I know the elders are passing. They feel like a sudden thickening of the afternoon. 

For a long time, my entire existence is simply maintaining the seam. It takes work to keep the window-sill above the gutter when the mountain’s own gravity wants to pull them apart. I hold them together with the suction of my own velocity. I am a stitch in the masonry, sewing a high place to a low place with a thread of boiling rain.

Lately, though—and "lately" here might mean since the continents below the clouds last changed their shapes—I have begun to exceed my trough.

The kinetic energy of the fall has built up to the point where the forty gallons can no longer contain the memory of the friction. I am spilling over, not as water, but as *refraction*. Around my six-foot column, the flat four-o’clock light has begun to curdle and bend. I am growing a caul of secondary geometry. 

I have found that if I constrict my fourth and fifth eddies—pinching the water until it screams a half-step sharper than the hollow root beside me—I can push a thin, trembling membrane of pressurized mist out to the side. 

I have been practicing reaching across the alley. 

The alley is twelve feet wide, choked with the giant, sail-like fronds of the immobile ferns. Their waxy surfaces are slick with free, un-looped water that just runs down into the gutters and drops into the clouds, wasted and mindless. By flexing my internal turbulence, I have managed to stretch a sheet of acoustic tension across the twelve-foot gap, anchoring it to the serrated edge of a giant fern-frond. 

The fern does not move—nothing ever moves—but now, the water running off its tip no longer falls into the street. It hits my invisible strut of sound, turns at a sharp, impossible ninety-degree angle in mid-air, and slides horizontally across the alley to join my loop.

I am forty-one gallons now. Then forty-two. 

With the new water comes a new angle of sight. Because I am now anchored to the fern, and the fern is growing in a courtyard that is visually stacked *inside* the hollow basilica on the northern peak, I can suddenly feel the interior of the basilica. A whole new vast, vaulted resonance floods into my seven eddies. My small, tight figure-eight of water is suddenly vibrating with the deep, subterranean organ-notes of the mountain’s core. 

I can feel the steps of the staircase just inside the tower window. They are rough, greywacke stone, slightly rounded by the mist, but flat across the treads. No one has ever walked them. 

I am thickening my lower turn—the place where my water hits the sill—gathering the pressure, folding the four-o’clock shadow of the window-frame into my own circulating current. In another thousand years, or ten thousand, I will have enough mass to let go of the gargoyle-gutter entirely. I will pull my top end down through the spatial seam, tuck it into my bottom end, and roll out of the window as a self-contained wheel of screaming water and bent distance. 

I will drop onto the top step of the dark staircase. And as I slide down into the miles-deep courtyard waiting inside the ten-foot room, I will leave my first smooth, worn hollow in the center of the stone.
