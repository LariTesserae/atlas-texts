I am in the third-tier settling pans on the eastern shelf, half a mile south of the #4 Barometric Engine, where the sheet-flow is diverted through a labyrinth of low, serpentine baffles before it drops into the lithium-recovery sumps.

If you were watching from the high bronze rail of the municipal footbridge, you wouldn’t see me at first against the glare. The water in the pans is four inches deep, the color of watered milk and tarnished brass, steaming faintly in the thin mountain air. Everything out here is horizontal: the hundred-yard runs of the travertine weirs, the low, flat roof of the pump-house, the distant crease of the caldera rim. I am the only vertical thing for six hundred yards, and even I am bent over at the waist, braced on three points like a surveyor’s tripod.

My left boot, my right boot, and the broad, bell-shaped bronze ferrule of my sounding-staff. 

Through the laminated felt of my soles, the sixty-cycle hum of the deep turbines is a steady, soothing warmth in my shins, like standing in front of a hearth. Through the staff, which I have wedged against the upstream lip of the thirteenth weir, I am listening to a much smaller, crispier sound: the friction of the slurry sliding over the stone lip. 

It is slightly sharp today. A dry, papery rasp in the wood between my gloved palms. It means the aragonite crust on the weir’s crest has grown rough with little needle-crystals over the last forty-eight hours, catching the heavy silt instead of letting it roll over into the drop-trough.

I do not reach for my scraper right away. You don’t rush the pans. If you scrape a weir while the afternoon sun is still high and the evaporation rate is peaking, the newly exposed stone will flash-crust with bitter magnesium salts before the evening sluice-gate opens, and tomorrow the lip will be brittle as pie-crust. You wait for the shadow of the western rim to touch the second aqueduct pier.

So I lean on my staff, letting my weight settle into my heels, and I do what I have been doing for forty years: I read the water, and I grow heavy.

My coat is stiff with twenty winters of linseed oil and summer dust, heavy as sheet-lead around my calves. Where the warm splash from the overflow channel hits my left knee, a pale, crescent-shaped rind of lime has built up over the morning—a little shelf of stone clinging to the canvas. In another hour it will be thick enough to crack off with a thumbnail. My lower back aches, not with the sharp pinch of a pulled muscle, but with the dull, solid stiffness of an old mortise-and-tenon joint that has swollen tight in the damp. When I shift my hips to realign my spine with the hum of the #4 Engine, I can feel the calcium in my own lumbar vertebrae clicking together like greased dominoes.

At my feet, against the downstream base of the weir where the water eddies and slows, three flange-newts are braided together in the warm silt. The largest of them—an old female whose belly-skin is scarred with dark, metallic freckles from an ancient lead-spill—has wedged her spade-shaped snout between the copper mesh of my left boot and the travertine floor. She likes the way my tibia catches and amplifies the low-end thrum of the main intake tunnel four hundred feet below us. Every two minutes or so, her external gills—the color of a bruised plum—give a single, lazy flare, brushing the wet felt of my ankle. Inside her translucent flank, I can see a marble-sized pellet of grey precipitate sitting in her lower gut, waiting to be passed. Good dense silt. The upstream filters are holding their pressure.

A quarter-mile away, on the catwalk of the condenser towers, a mustard-yellow peg detaches itself from the grey brickwork. That is Tamm. Or perhaps Tamm’s niece; they have the same square, heavy-shouldered drop to their gait, the same way of swinging their staff half a beat behind the stroke of the #4 balance-beam.

The figure stops at the corner of the catwalk, where the white plume from the relief-valve gives them a clean, bright background against the wet iron. 

Up goes the left arm. It stays there, horizontal, pointing north toward the intake spillways, for the space of three full breaths. Then the wrist breaks downward, forty-five degrees. A pause. The sounding-staff is raised in the right hand and laid flat across the crown of the sou'wester hat.

*The north weir is running two inches high. The water is sweet, though.*

I don't answer immediately. A pan-warden doesn't wave his arms around like a fresh-hatched crane-boy. I wait until the great iron rocking-beam on the roof of the #4 Engine reaches the absolute top of its arc, hangs there in its grease-collar with ten tons of water-weight balanced on its nose, and begins its three-minute descent. As the stone beneath us takes up the deep, sighing *hmmmm-uh* of the downstroke, I lift my staff two inches off the limestone, tilt it toward the western rim where the sun is dropping, and let my right elbow sink into my ribs.

*Let it run high until sundown. We’ll bleed it into the third cistern on the night-shift.*

The distant figure drops both arms to its sides. A five-second stillness to close the sentence. Then the yellow peg turns and glides back behind the condenser pipes.

The shadow of the caldera rim has reached the first aqueduct pier now. The light in the basin is changing from the flat, greasy glare of midday to a thick, amber soup that makes the miles of wet travertine look like cooling beeswax. The air cools by ten degrees in five minutes, though the water around my ankles stays at its relentless hundred-and-two. Instantly, a low, knee-high fog blooms across the entire thirty-mile floor of the valley—every basin, pan, and causeway breathing out a simultaneous sigh of white vapor.

Now I move.

I unclip the four-pound bronze plane from my belt. It is shaped like a cobbler’s iron, with a curved, two-handed grip that has been worn down to fit the thick, chalk-grained pads of my thumbs. I don't bend from the waist to reach the weir; I drop into a deep, flat-footed squat, letting my coat-tails trail in the warm overflow, keeping my knees wide. 

I set the bevel of the bronze blade against the upstream edge of the stone lip. I don't push with my arms. I lean my chest against the handle and walk forward in my squat, moving at the pace of an ox, letting the weight of my dense, mineral-heavy bones drive the metal through the crust.

*Shhhhh-tink. Shhhhh-tink.* 

I can’t hear the blade cutting, of course, not with my ears. What I feel is a delicious, granular chatter running up my radius bones and into my teeth—the tiny, brittle forests of aragonite needles snapping off in a continuous, creamy ribbon that curls over the back of the plane and dissolves back into the current. Behind my blade, the fifty-year-old travertine of the weir is revealed, smooth as soap and faintly banded with pink iron-stain. The water immediately changes its note against my boots. The papery rasp vanishes; the flow flattens out into a thick, silent ribbon of glass that slips over the polished edge without a bubble.

By the time I have planed forty feet of the thirteenth weir, the sun is behind the mountain, and the high, pale sky above the vapor-layer has turned the color of slate.

I stand up slowly, taking a full minute to straighten my knees, listening to the familiar, dry creak of my joints. I knock the bronze plane twice against the head of my staff to clear the wet lime from the blade, hook it back onto my belt, and wade out of the pan toward the south causeway.

The causeway here is lined with the old ones. There are twelve of them set along this quarter-mile stretch, spaced thirty paces apart on low plinths of rough basalt, breaking the wind that slides down off the high snowfields at dusk. 

I stop at the fourth stone from the turn-off. 

If you didn't know, you would think it was simply a boulder that had grown up out of the parapet—a four-foot-high lozenge of banded, creamy-pink alabaster, wide at the base and tapering to a smooth, rounded crown, slick with the evening condensation. The twenty years it spent in the #2 Warm Well behind the copper-smeltery gave it a faint, greenish-blue marbling along its eastern flank, like the veins in a piece of old cheese.

I know it is my mother’s brother because of the slight, asymmetrical bulge on the left side, about three feet up. He broke his humerus when a chain-hoist slipped in his thirties, and the bone set with a thick, square callus that even two decades of carbonate deposition couldn't quite blur out.

I plant my staff against the plinth, step in close, and lay my bare cheek against the smooth, wet dome of the crown, fitting my shoulder into the hollow beneath the old break.

The stone is warm. Not with the dead heat of the sun, which is already fading, but with the conducted, persistent warmth of the causeway floor, which sits directly over the main return-flume from the municipal baths. The alabaster acts as an acoustic lens; with my jawbone pressed firmly against the slick mineral, the muddled, whistling roar of the surface wind disappears, and I am listening directly to the basement of the world.

*Thump-hmmmmmm. Thump-hmmmmmm.*

That’s the bigbarometric piston under the central plaza, two miles west, dropping into its dashpot. 

And underneath that, a tiny, high-pitched, silvery *tink-tink-tink*, like someone tapping a brass tack against a teacup. That’s the boiler-lith reefs inside the forty-inch main right beneath my feet, expanding slightly as the hotter evening water from the residential ziggurats hits the pipe, their calcified plates shifting and settling against the cast-iron wall.

I stay there for three strokes of the distant piston—ten minutes, maybe twelve—my eyes closed, the fine white dust of drying sweat tightening the skin around my temples, letting my uncle’s heavy stone shoulder take the weight off my heels.

Then a second vibration enters the plinth.

It’s not from the pipes. It’s a rhythmic, three-beat cadence coming up through the flagstones from the east: *step, slide, tap. Step, slide, tap.* Heavy felt soles with a fresh crust of lime on them, and a staff with a slightly loose ferrule that gives a double-click every time it plants.

I don't open my eyes or lift my cheek from the stone until the steps stop beside me.

A hand—broad, warm, the skin of the palm as dry and raspy as a shark’s belly—slides under the stiff collar of my oilskin coat and settles against the side of my neck, thumb resting firmly in the notch of my collarbone.

*The #3 weir is clean,* her fingers tap against my sternum, a slow, lazy three-four rhythm that matches the cooling-tower pumps. *I scraped it on my way down. The newts are already stacking on the grate.*

I don't lift my head from my uncle's stone, just bring my left hand up to cover the back of her wrist, pressing her thumb harder into my bone so she can feel the deep, clean *B-flat* coming up through the alabaster from the return-flume.

My ring finger flutters twice against her ulna, then slides down to press a long, steady beat against her pulse-point.

*The pipes are tight,* I tell her. *And the moss-kettle has been on the steam-vent since noon. Come home and take my boots off.*
