I am not in the water. Nothing with a name stays in the water for long without becoming either dust or architecture. 

I am three inches to the left of the roaring intake vent, tucked into the five-foot vertical fault where the Weaver sleeps when the light goes out. Look past the empty space where its body was wedged a moment ago; look deeper into the basalt, where the black rock pinches down to the width of two fingers before sealing entirely. 

I am in the mortar that holds the back of that seam together. 

Six years ago, an early winter storm tore a bristlecone pine from a ridge four miles away and dropped it down the sinkhole. Most of the tree was milled to pale soup in the subterranean siphon, its bark and heartwood reduced to individual fibers before it ever saw the selenite window. What shot up through the floor vent was only a fist-sized knot of resin-soaked rootwood, dense as ironwood and twisted around a fistful of black river-gravel. 

It didn't make it to the ceiling. The gyre caught it, slammed it into the back wall before the Weaver could snag it for a loom-weight, and drove it sideways into the sleeping-crack. The Weaver tried to pry it out—there are still four deep gouges in my left flank where its quartz-crusted thumbs raked the wood—but the current had hammered the root-knot into an undercut in the basalt like a keystone. Even the Weaver’s hydraulic tendons couldn't budge it. 

Instead, the Weaver used me as a mattress and a foot-brace.

In the six years since, the wood has ceased to be wood. Here in the back of the fault, the water does not scream; it hisses, forced through the tight gap around the Weaver’s flattened ribs at high pressure, bleeding off its minerals in a slow, dense glaze. The resinous pine has been replaced cell by cell with blue-grey chalcedony and veins of milky agate. The river-gravel trapped in the root’s grip is fused into the mass, and a single, broken tine of an old ibex horn that drifted into the slot three summers ago has been welded across my upper ridge like a crossbar.

I am a nodule of petrified debris, half-organic memory, half-mountain, cemented permanently into the fault. I am the Weaver’s furniture, and its anvil, and its ledger.

You can read the history of the room in my layers. 

Because I am in the sleeping-slot, I am the only thing the Weaver touches when it is still. It cannot stay still for long, even in the dark when the selenite wall turns the color of a bruise and the room goes black. Every forty minutes through the night, the Weaver has to shudder—that violent, bow-stave ripple of its ribcage—to crack the growing frost of aragonite off its hide before the mountain welds us together. 

When it shrugs in its sleep, the shed scales of white crust don't get swept away by the main vortex. They fall down into the V-shaped pinch of the rock beneath its feet, right on top of me. Over the years, those shed skins of stone have settled into my crevices and been glazed over by the seeping silica. I am laminated with the Weaver’s old winters. I hold the exact negative impression of its left shoulder blade from three years ago; I hold the cast of a deep, ragged scar across its flank from the time a ceiling shelf the size of a dining table dropped early and nearly sheared the creature in half. Around the edges of that scar-cast, the stone is stained a faint, rusty ochre—the mineral trace of the yellow fluid it bled while it wedged itself against me for four days, shivering and flexing until the grey skin knitted shut.

I know the weight of its jaw. 

The rock of the back wall is too loud to listen to directly; the basalt carries the whole bass thunder of the mountain’s plumbing, a blind, deafening roar that drowns out detail. The wood inside me—even turned to agate—has a different grain. It dampens the deep earth-rumble just enough. 

When the Weaver is tucked into the seam, waiting out the dark or resting its tendons between climbs, it hooks its lower heels into my ibex-horn crossbar and presses its flat, solid-bone chin into the smooth hollow at my center. 

Through me, it listens to the room. And because its jaw is pressed to me, I am the ear canal. 

Every vibration in the forty-foot box comes down through the basalt, hits the denser, cross-grained knot of my petrified rootwood, and translates into a distinct, readable tremor before passing into the Weaver’s skull. I do not have eyes to see the blinding noon light or the violet shadows of the clouds outside, and I do not have skin to feel the freezing blast of the water. I have only the chord of the chamber.

Right now, while the Weaver is high up near the ceiling with the copper wedge in its teeth, I am holding the whole tension of the room in the dark of the crevice.

I can feel the main floor-vent twenty feet below: a heavy, ragged *thrum-thrum-thrum* that changes pitch with the season. Today it is high and tight—the sun is hot on the peaks three miles away, the glaciers are sweating hard, and the siphon is over-pressurized. 

Above that bass note, I feel the five copper pitons driven into the wall around me. Each one has its own voice in the stone. The lowest piton, six feet to my right, is loose; its tiny, rapid chatter in its socket tells me the basalt around it is flaking away, undermined by cavitation. Within two days, the sinew line tied to it will go slack, and the wicker vessel of half-grown stone hanging there will swing into the wall and shatter unless the Weaver moves the anchor. 

Higher up, the gut-strings are singing through the rock—a frantic, shifting harmonic of C-sharps and D-flats as the water batters the looms. 

And then, from forty feet overhead, the new note arrives.

*Clink.* 

It travels straight down the vertical grain of the basalt, bypassing the roar of the water entirely—a sharp, metallic spike of sound that hits my agate core and rings like a struck tuning fork. 

The Weaver has reached the ceiling. It has jammed its quartz-crusted fingers into the roof-crack, hanging upside down in the boiling apex of the gyre where the water turns back on itself, and it is using the back of an old, heavy mammoth-bone wedge to hammer the new copper piton into the seam between the black rock and the clear selenite window.

*Clink. Clink. Clink.*

With the third strike, the tone of the entire wall shifts by a fraction of a hertz. A tiny, tight sigh runs down the stone face—a micro-fracture opening up by the width of a human hair at the top of the sky. A thread of water, no thicker than a sewing needle, has just found a new way out of the box. The pressure in the chamber drops by an imperceptible fraction, and down in the narrow, warm crack below the intake vent, the three soft, pink shapes shift slightly in their sleep as the eddy at their doorway changes its curl.

Then the hammering stops. 

A moment later, a flake of old, green-edged copper—sheared off the head of the piton by the blow—drops out of the main current, slides down the quiet boundary layer of the back wall, and clicks softly into the groove between my petrified root and the basalt. 

It settles there against the bone-ash and the shed aragonite scales. By tomorrow morning, the water seeping through the slot will have thrown a veil of clear quartz over the green metal, locking it into my skin. I will hold the copper flake, and I will hold the new resonance of the slightly wider sky, waiting for the heavy, slate-skinned body to drop back down the wall, slide into the slot, and press its jaw against me to hear if the mountain is still moving.
