There is no *above* from which to look down on this. To imagine a vantage point—an eye hovering thirty feet in the air, taking in the purple swells and the slow, grey-brown drift of the slugs—feels like a violation of the plateau's physics. Nothing stays up there except the thin, dry glare and the bladder-gnats, and the gnats have no eyes. 

To be here at all is to be underneath. It is to accept the weight of the roof.

At first, the mind resists the compression. It wants to stand upright, to draw a sharp, three-second breath, to look thirty miles to the next whale-backed ridge. Give it an hour in the dry-oven light, though, and the resistance begins to flake away like dried mucus. The thin air makes a fast heart feel foolish, like a bird beating its wings inside a stone jar. You drop your chin. Your neck thickens to hold the heat out. Your knees bend until your belly finds the damp shadow just above the porphyry.

I am not the whole Gleaner—not at first. A whole creature feels too distinct, too separate from the stone it pushes against. 

I think I begin simply as the *levering*. 

I am the flat, horn-yellow nail of a thumb, wedged into a seam of purple rock where the glacier left a saucer five hundred million years ago. There is no hurry in the thumb. Above it, the wrist is thick as an oak root; behind the wrist, a shoulder packed with warm, tallowy fat; behind the shoulder, a heart that beats once while you count to twenty. 

The stone does not want to move. It has been glued into its socket by three centuries of crab-ash and dried slug-slime. If I tried to jerk it free, the nail would split, the tendon would tear, the lungs would gasp and burn in the thin air. So I do not jerk. I simply lean the weight of the shoulder into the thumb. I let my blood pool heavy in the forearm. I close the milky film over my eyes because there is nothing to see while the rock makes up its mind.

A cloud-bank is dragging its belly down the trough behind me. I know this before it touches me because the million breathing-pores of the Mantle-Slugs uphill all sigh open at once—a vast, wet, rhythmic *shhh-ock* that travels through the granite into my knees. Then the warm fog hits my back. The little blind crabs riding in the deep crease behind my neck stir; their spindly legs go *tick, tick, tick* as they reach up to catch the moisture condensing on my tallow skin. 

Ten minutes pass. Maybe forty. The fog moves on. Beneath my thumb, the dried mucus-bed under the porphyry slab finally takes in a drop of new water, softens by a fraction of a millimeter, and yields. 

*Clack.*

The stone comes free. A blind crab the size of a walnut, pale as bone, is clinging to its underside, disturbed from its dark pocket. It does not scurry. It raises two clock-hand claws in the sudden warmth, feels the blunt pad of my index finger, and steps onto my knuckle to begin cleaning the salt from my skin.

I do not build the wall to make a shape. I have no picture of a circle or a dome behind my brow-ridge. I only know that my left hip is slightly drier than my right hip, and that if I put this flat stone on top of that round stone, the next cloud will snag its wet belly here for three breaths longer than it did before. 

I shuffle two feet to the left. My belly scrapes over the black-veined lichen. I set the stone down. 

That is the morning.

By what might be afternoon—when the purple rock between the slugs has grown hot enough to smell like baked iron—I am done with the sky. The light is too loud against my eyelids. I turn toward the low crescent of stones I have been thickening since the last twenty clouds went by. 

The Mantle-Slugs have already found it. Three of them, each the size of an overturned boat, have flowed over the knee-high wall and wedged themselves together in the lee, their grey-violet hides pressed so tightly that the seams between them have vanished. To get inside my house, I have to ask the roof to let me under.

I sink my chin against my chest, seal the fleshy valves of my nostrils, and push my crown into the crease where the largest slug meets the granite floor. 

It is like pushing your face into a warm, damp sack of flour that is very slowly breathing. The smell is intense, ancient, and completely comforting: copper blood, wet chalk, and the sweet, heavy musk of the silk-hammocks rotting gently in the dark behind it. 

I do not shove. I bring both broad hands up and lay my palms flat against the slug's rubbery mantle. I know this one. It has a jagged scar of old dry lichen near its third breathing-pore. My right thumb finds the nerve-cluster just below the pore and presses—slow, heavy, rhythmic. *One. Wait. Two. Wait.* With my left fingernails, I rake gently across the lichen-scar, flaking away the dead crust that the little crabs couldn't pry loose.

A tremor runs through the three-ton wall of flesh. It is not fear; it is a profound, syrupy relief. With a wet, sucking *shuck*, the great muscular foot contracts three inches to the right, arching up just enough to break the seal against the bedrock.

I slide my shoulders into the gap. 

Behind me, the daylight disappears. The dry heat of the altiplano is cut off as the slug relaxes its rim back down against my heels, sealing the door with its own weight. 

Inside, the air is thick as broth. There is no light at all, not even a pinhole, and the relief of that darkness in the tiny, milky eyes is the closest thing this body knows to joy. The chamber is maybe four feet wide and two feet high—a low, glistening pocket roofed by the pale, pulsing underbellies of the mollusks and walled by the rough porphyry slabs I levered out of the mud when my nails were half an inch longer than they are now.

I am not alone in the dark. 

As I draw my knees up under my chin, my flank brushes against another warm, tallowy mass wedged into the back curve of the stone wall. 

We do not turn our heads. We do not make a sound in our throats. I simply let my weight settle until my ribs and hip are pressed flat against their back, filling the last bit of empty air in the pocket so the moisture has nowhere to go but into our skin. 

For a long time—an hour, three hours—we only breathe. One shallow, rasping pull of the copper-sweet air; then the long, silent hold while the oxygen drifts lazily through the thick blood; then the soft, leathery creak of the ribs letting it go. 

Then, through the two-inch layer of fat along my side, I feel their stomach give a slow, low *glug*, followed by a slight, deliberate flexing of the muscle under their left shoulder blade. 

It is a report from the outside. They were down by the wide glacial saucer near the maroon pillar before the last two clouds came through. The water there is warm and thick with floating bladder-gnats. A slug has died on the south lip, and the silk-worms are spinning heavy over the carcass.

I answer by shifting two pounds of weight from my left hip to my right, pressing my warm shoulder a fraction tighter against their spine. *The wall here held the fog. The ceiling is wet. Stay.*

Overhead, a heavy silk hammock sags down between the bellies of two slugs, brushing the top of my skull like a warm, wet glove. Without opening my eyes, I raise one heavy arm. My broad fingers hook into the gelatinous web, feeling the slow, blind squirming of a hundred thumb-sized grubs inside. I pull a fistful down, push it past my lips, and let the flat bone plates of my jaw begin their slow, sideways grind. It tastes of sweet tallow and damp cellar stone. 

A tiny porcelain crab steps off my neighbor's shoulder and crosses the bridge of skin onto my neck, its eight legs ticking softly as it goes to work on the crease behind my ear. 

Above us, three tons of mantle-flesh heave upward by a millimeter, then settle back down. Far away, outside the stones, a cloud-bank is dragging its belly across the purple swells, and a million gills are opening in the silence to drink it in. My heart beats once. I wait twenty seconds, and it beats again. There is nowhere else to go.
