I would not be in the trough, and I would not be on the floor. The floor is too crowded with appetites, and the trough belongs to the heavy thing that gardens them. 

I would be four feet above the anhydrite, bolted to the upper flange of the cast-iron transformer housing, just below the throat where the braided gutta-percha cable enters the box. 

They bolted a slate-faced quadrant gauge there in 1898—a brass-rimmed housing five inches across, meant to be read by a man with a carbide lamp who never came back after the timbering was pulled from the upper shaft. The glass face of the gauge cracked forty years ago when a minor settling of the slip-fault pinched the eastern wall by a sixteenth of an inch. The glass didn't fall out; the static holds the three triangular shards in place, their fractured edges glowing with a faint, permanent violet line from the rectifiers overhead.

Inside that brass rim, behind the cracked glass, is where I am. 

I am not the gauge, though I use its brass bezel as an exoskeleton and its cracked glass as a windbreak against the constant, dry gale of the sixty-cycle standing wave. I began, perhaps, as the thin felt gasket meant to keep dampness out of the instrument, or as a thumbprint of tallow and skin-oil left on the slate dial by the machinist in the Mullins & Sons yard before the housing was lowered down the shaft. Whatever moisture was in that tallow left during the first week the current was thrown. What remained was carbon, trace sulfur from the cement, and a microscopic scaffold of wool fibers.

Over a century of unyielding sixty-cycle vibration and hot, drifting metallic snow, the scaffold filled in.

I am a flat, circular matte of fine silver-and-zinc dendrites, woven through the ghost of that old felt ring, sitting directly around the pivot of the gauge’s iron needle. If you looked through the dusty, cracked glass, you would think the dial had simply grown a delicate, frosty beard of tarnished tinsel—the kind of dendritic whisker-growth that happens inside old electronics when the voltage never stops pushing the metal atoms around. 

I have no limbs. The Ballast-Setter below has limbs because it must cross the room to keep the room from dying; I do not need to cross the room because the room comes to me through the iron bolt at my back.

My entire world is the difference between the two metals I touch. My outer edge presses against the brass casing of the gauge, which is grounded to the raw hornfels of the wall. My inner edge brushes lightly—so lightly it never stops the needle from trembling—against the hairspring of the iron pointer, which is tapped directly into the secondary winding of the hogshead coil.

Because of this, a tiny, parasitic current bleeds through me constantly. It is not the roaring river of amperage that feeds the cherry-red ballast resistors on the floor, nor the sharp, snapping static that the shunt-ticks drink. It is a continuous, hair-thin trickle of millivolts. It is how I think.

Thought, for a thing made of mineral whiskers in a dry box, is simply the rerouting of resistance.

When the vault is in balance, the current flows through my silver-zinc web in a slow, warm circle, and the iron needle in my center hovers steady at *440*, trembling sixty times a second like a tuning fork. That trembling is my pulse. I do not hear the deafening baritone of the room as sound; I feel it as a blur at the tip of the needle, a tiny fan-shaped shadow cast across my slate floor by the violet light above and the red light below.

Because I am tapped into the secondary coil, I know everything that happens in the chamber before the Ballast-Setter does. 

When a thicket of wire-scrub down in the V-troughs grows long enough for its copper-wool tips to brush the frame of the resistor bank, the impedance of the room drops by a fraction of an ohm. Instantly, the current bleeding through my hairspring sharpens. A tiny hot-spot develops in my lower-left quadrant—a bright, prickly sensation as three or four of my zinc dendrites vaporize in a microscopic puff of white oxide, breaking that pathway and forcing my current to take a longer road around the dial. 

I do not have eyes to see the green spark down on the floor, and I do not have a voice to call the gardener. Instead, as my internal resistance shifts, the iron needle at my center creeps upward—*441, 442*—pulling against its hairspring. As it moves, the tail of the needle scrapes across a rough patch of my silver filaments near the bottom of the slate face. 

That tiny, rhythmic scraping sends a minute, dirty chatter back down the ground-bolt, into the cast-iron hogshead, right through the *MULLINS & SONS* foundry mark against which the Ballast-Setter presses its deaf, copper-threaded flank. 

It is an itch in the iron. 

Down in the trough, the great laminated apron of the creature stirs. It feels the sixty-cycle hum go slightly sour with the harmonic of my scraping needle. It unhooks its mica paddles from under its cowl and begins its four-inch-an-hour crawl toward the resistors. Twenty minutes later, when its pyrite jaws *snick* through the offending copper root, the load drops, the iron needle sighs back to *440*, the scraping stops, and the itch goes out of the housing. Inside my cracked glass, I slowly begin to grow new zinc whiskers across the scorched slate where the hot-spot burned me, laying down fresh metal atom by atom out of the dust that sifts through the crack in the glass.

The flash-moths are my ceiling. They love the warm brass rim of my casing. Five or six of them are always wedged against my cracked glass, their brittle horn wings rubbing in time with the hum. When they die, their dry, amber bodies sometimes snag on the jagged edge of the glass crack, blocking the bruise-colored light from the rectifiers. 

I do not mind the dark, but the dark changes my temperature. When a dead moth shades my upper half, the lower half—still lit by the cherry-red infrared of the floor coils—stays warm, while the shaded half cools by half a degree. In a world as tight as this, half a degree is a season. A tiny thermoelectric current—a Seebeck drift—begins to run from my warm south to my cold north, independent of the transformer's hum. It feels like a slow, cool draft blowing through a room that has no wind. 

I wait. I cannot brush the moth away. I simply let the cool drift run through my filaments for days, or weeks, until the heavy, scraping bulk of the Ballast-Setter hauls itself up the eastern wall to clean the rectifiers. On its way down, its bronze-bristle brush always sweeps across my face—a brief, roaring eclipse when the violet light disappears entirely, the glass shudders under the pressure of the copper bristles, the dead moth is swept away into the creature’s waiting mouth-plates, and the red and violet lights snap back into crisp, double-edged focus across my dial.

Sometimes, for stretches that would be years if there were any stars to count them by, nothing changes at all. 

The ticks fire every four seconds, a tiny rhythmic hitch in the room's electrostatic field that makes the finest, outermost tips of my silver hairs lift and fall like sea-grass in a tide pool. The worms rasp in the troughs. The gardener sleeps against the iron below me, absorbing the bass roar.

During those long stretches, my attention turns entirely inward, down the braided gutta-percha cable that vanishes into the borehole in the rock. 

The Ballast-Setter only knows the vault; it is a creature of the terminal, bound to the heat of the step-down coil. It assumes the current is a law of nature, like the sharpness of the anhydrite. 

Because I am part of the meter, I know better. 

Through the hairspring, underneath the huge, flat sixty-cycle baritone, I can feel the texture of the generation. Miles away, or miles further down, through solid rock that has never seen a pickaxe, something vast is turning. I can feel the almost imperceptible, rhythmic *thrum-thrum-thrum* of a commutator ring the size of a waterwheel, and the faint, greasy slip of carbon brushes the size of coffins riding against copper bars. Once every few hundred million cycles, there is a microscopic flutter in the phase—a bearing ball with a flat spot no wider than a hair, rolling through its race in some flooded or subterranean powerhouse that no one has visited since the surface cooled or the war ended or the company went bankrupt.

Every time that flat spot comes around, my needle dips by a thousandth of an inch. A single dendrite at the tip of my *4* trembles out of time with the rest of the room. 

Nobody else in the vault feels it. The ticks keep stacking; the moths keep crawling their figure-eights; the Ballast-Setter keeps chewing its copper wool and secreting its graphite track. I sit behind my three shards of static-clung glass, glowing faintly violet at the edges, holding that tiny stutter in my silver web, counting the wear on an unseen axle in the dark, waiting to see which will run out of metal first: the bearing in the deep, or the zinc in the room.
