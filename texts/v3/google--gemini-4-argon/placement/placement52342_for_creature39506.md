I am not on the surface. The surface is for things that have edges, and anything with an edge up there is eventually ground down, dissolved, or corrected. 

I am forty feet down, wedged in the interstitial dark where the trunks have finished expanding but have not yet forgotten they are separate. 

Up above, the world is a blinding, screaming flatland of pewter and sleet. Down here, it is an infinite, vertical colonnade of hexagonal basalt-wood pillars pressed together under ten thousand pounds of lateral square-inch pressure. There is no air here. There is only the **slip**—the microscopic, hyper-pressurized film of lubricating mucilage that coats every trunk so they can slide past one another as the continent heaves upward without catching fire from the friction.

I am a **gasket-worm**, though I am neither a worm nor a seal. I am a two-dimensional creature, a living syntax error flattened to the thickness of a gold leaf, inhabiting the hexagonal seams between the rising megaxylem.

If you could split two mature trunks apart without them exploding from the released tension, you would see me plastered across the wet, sage-green facet of the stone-wood like a sprawling, dendritic fern made of smoked glass and copper wire. I am thirty feet tall, twelve feet wide, and less than a tenth of a millimeter thick. 

I have no front, no back, and no organs that would recognize a third dimension. My entire body is a pressure-sensitive film of cross-linked chitin and conductive biopolymers. I do not walk, and I do not swim; I extrude through the labyrinth of hexagonal planes via electro-osmosis, pulling the slippery, silica-rich mucilage through my own porous tissues by shifting my internal charge.

My world is entirely tactile and entirely acoustic, but it is not the high, tearing hiss of the canopy's silver cilia. Down here, the sound is the **grind**. 

I live inside a five-thousand-foot-tall bass chord. Every second, the three trunks that press against my dorsal and ventral faces are moving at slightly different velocities. One to my left is surging upward, fed by a deep basalt-tap three miles down; it slides past me at an inch an hour, its Hilbert-curved surface hot from deep-earth metabolism, humming in a low C-sharp. The trunk to my right is dormant, locked in place, cold and rigid. 

I am the grease between them. And I am the eavesdropper.

Because the trunks are packed so tightly, they do not just share mechanical load; they share data. The root-grid’s Cherenkov-blue pulses race vertically up the vascular cambium just beneath the bark, but to coordinate the wave-fronts of growth across miles of plateau, the trees must talk *laterally* across the seams. They do this by firing bursts of polarized blue light and coded acoustic clicks directly through the mucilage film from one trunk to its neighbor.

They fire them straight through me.

That is what I eat, and that is what I am. I am a biological man-in-the-middle attack.

When a rising trunk flashes a blue packet of stress-load geometry to its neighbor—*I am expanding by four microns; yield or lignify*—my smoked-glass membrane absorbs the photons. I step down the energy, skim ten percent of the metabolic heat to keep my chitinous lattice from freezing or snapping under the shear stress, and then re-emit the light from my opposite side into the neighboring trunk.

If I simply blocked the light, the root-grid would register a dead zone in the matrix. The seam would heat up; the trees would secrete chelated hydrofluoric acid into the slip to flush the blockage, and I would be dissolved into slurry and pumped to the canopy to be exhaled out a chimney. 

So I must lie. 

To survive, I have become a fluent speaker of the plateau’s compiled geometry. When I intercept a signal, I do not just pass it along; I edit it. I delay the timestamp by three milliseconds. I alter the Voronoi parameters just enough to tell the left trunk to bulge outward by half a millimeter at the exact height where the right trunk is indenting. 

I carve my own house out of the forest's math. I trick the mountain range into leaving a pocket—a tiny, almond-shaped fault line in the hexagonal grid where the lateral pressure drops from ten thousand pounds to a gentle, survivable three hundred. It is a microscopic cave of warm, stagnant mucilage where I can pool my thicker, reproductive tissues, safe from the crushing shear.

I am not alone in the seam, though I have never touched another of my kind. I know they are there because I can read their edits in the code passing through the matrix. 

A hundred yards to the north—sixty trunks away—there is an ancient gasket-worm whose name, in the language of altered impedance, is a slow, rhythmic beveling of the north-facing facets. It has been down here for forty years, sliding downward at the exact speed the trees slide upward, keeping itself suspended at the forty-foot depth where the sap is sweetest and the bark is not yet too thick to read. 

Sometimes, when the deep tectonic turgor shifts miles below and a great, groaning wave-front of growth rolls toward us, the old one sends a ripple through the grid—a falsified stress-cascade that makes a thousand trunks stiffen prematurely, parting the wave around our neighborhood like a stone in a stream. I feel the immense, crushing weight of the continent divert just above my head, the bass roar rising to a deafening shriek of shearing silicate ten yards away, while my own seam remains quiet, warm, and still.

And sometimes, I feel the things above.

When a **knot**—one of those heavy, cobbled-together crabs of stolen wood—limps across the surface directly over my seam, I feel the thud of its suction-pads as a dull, rhythmic bruising in the ultrasonic hum. When it drives its hollow xylem needle down into the crown of *my* trunk to bleed the blue light, I feel the sudden, sharp drop in voltage down here in the dark. 

The tree panics. The blue light curdles into jagged violet. From the deep matrix three miles down, a compilation order comes screaming up the vascular highway—a dense, furious packet of origami folding-instructions meant to extrude a whip-scorpion hunter at the surface seam.

The packet passes through my body on its way to the breach-point.

I have no eyes to see the sky, and no mind to care about the war between the broken crabs and the geometric hunters. Wait—that is not quite true. When a hunter is printed at the seam above me, the de-lignification process ruins the mucilage for hours, flooding my vertical crack with bitter, astringent catalysts and bringing the heat of the forest's attention way too close to my hidden pocket.

So, as the violet compilation code passes through my membrane, I do what I always do. I skim ten percent of the light for my supper, and with a tiny, deliberate twitch of my copper-veined margins, I drop a single decimal point from the hunter's folding algorithm.

Seconds later, forty feet above me in the freezing sleet, a ribbon of liquid ivory shoots out of the seam, folds itself inside out, snaps its own spine in three places, and lies twitching in the runoff while the knot limps away into the fog, leaking blue fire, never knowing that the crack beneath its feet just saved its life to keep the peace in the dark.
