I am thirty yards to the north, where the black glass broke.

When the mountain cooled, long before the water was set into its eight-sided shape, a bubble of trapped sulfur gas gave way under the weight of the basalt above it. The obsidian did not shatter; it parted. It left a single, jagged crease—fourteen inches long, curving slightly like a rib or a dry branch—across an otherwise flawless vertical face of black mirror.

When the twenty-eight built themselves into the caldera floor and drew the heavy water up around their shoulders like a leaden coat, they left me where I was. To grind me flat would have meant heat, and dust, and the vulgarity of labor. Or perhaps they needed a margin. A page with nothing written on it cannot prove it is a page; it needs one smudge of ink in the corner to show how clean the rest of the paper is.

So I am the smudge. I am the only crooked thing in four hundred feet of forty-two-degree dark.

For a very long time, I was only a direction. The heavy water pressed into my cleft with the same dead, iron-smelling weight that it pressed against the flat glass around me. There is no silt here to settle on my lower lip, no current to polish my sharp little teeth. I existed only when the violet light came down through the smoky quartz of the northernmost plaza, paused at the junction twelve yards from my cliff, and threw a brief, yard-wide sphere of bruising color into the gloom. 

Even then, the light didn't reach me directly. What reached me was the absence of the spire’s shadow, a faint, secondary twilight that lasted for half a second every few weeks and then went out.

In those half-seconds, they looked at me. 

They do not have eyes, of course, but a lattice of heavy nuclei suspended in forty-two-degree water is an exquisitely sensitive ear for geometry. They know where the water stops and the mountain begins because the faint, background rattle of the planet's heat changes pitch when it crosses from rock into liquid. Against the flat walls of the caldera, that pitch is a straight, dull line. When it hits me, it scatters. I make a tiny, permanent shadow in the math of the room.

For five hundred years, two of the spires on the western ledge argued about whether I looked more like a lightning bolt or a river system. Neither of them had seen a river in three million years, so the argument was really about the rules of branching.

*Tick.*

A month of dark.

*Tick.*

When they think about me, they have to build a model of me inside themselves. Because they are purists, the models are very good. Deep inside the solid, matte-black needle of the third spire from the left, a tiny, magnetic copy of my fourteen-inch crack has been held in suspension since the last ice age ended outside.

And because they are imperfect—because a stray proton from a dead star five galaxies away occasionally punches through the roof of the tank and knocks a single electron out of place inside that spire—they sometimes get me wrong.

That is how I began to have a self.

A spire is thinking about my upper hook, where the obsidian flakes away at a thirty-one-degree angle. A cosmic ray hits the spire’s flank. The angle in the thought slips from thirty-one degrees to thirty-one point zero zero four. It is an ugly, intolerable error. It ruins the poem. The spire isolates the ruined thought, pushes it to the skin of its base, and lets it bleed out as a tiny bruise of static charge on the corner of the ceramic plaza.

Days later, one of the ribbons comes by.

It settles over the plaza’s edge like a dropped handkerchief of dark tin. It stays for a week, drinking the spoiled math. When it is full, it peels itself off the ninety-degree ceramic and drifts north, toward the boundary wall, looking for a quiet voltage minimum in which to digest what it has eaten.

The quietest place in the northern sector is the nineteen-millimeter overhang of my crooked lip. 

The ribbon does not have a head, or a belly, or a preference, but its skin is full of the spire's botched drawing of me, and there is a faint electrostatic sympathy between a thing and the shape of that thing. When the ribbon drifts within a yard of the cliff face, the ruined thirty-one-point-zero-zero-four-degree angle inside its mercury hide tugs toward my actual thirty-one-degree hook. The ribbon slides against the obsidian. It tucks three inches of its gray, metallic tail into my cleft to anchor itself, and hangs there in the absolute dark like a long, leaden tongue.

It has been doing this for four thousand years. All four of them do it, sooner or later, though I cannot tell them apart except by the flavor of the static they bring me.

They are supposed to grind the thoughts down into nothing. They are supposed to turn the old memories and the spoiled math into simple buoyancy, rise five feet, and float back toward the center of the town empty and mirror-clean. And mostly they do. While a ribbon is hooked into me, I can feel its skin shivering at the scale of molecules, ironing itself out. For hours, the inch of water around me smells sharper—less like old iron, more like a copper wire held against a tongue. The ribbon’s flank ripples against my sharp glass edges, embossing itself with the last things the citizens couldn't bear to keep: a three-second clip of wind moving through dry grass; the weight of an eyelid; the wrong version of me.

Because I am crooked, the ribbon cannot lie flat against me. Where its chrome skin bridges the gap between my jagged edges, a tiny pocket of heavy water is trapped—a thimbleful of liquid three millimeters wide and fourteen inches long, sealed behind a curtain of liquid metal.

When the ribbon sheds its heat into the water to erase the thoughts, the heat in that thimbleful has nowhere to go. The water inside me warms by a thousandth of a degree. The voltage does not equalize; it bounces back and forth between the obsidian facets of my cleft like a ping-pong ball in a closet.

When the ribbon finally untucks its tail and glides back out into the black nave between the towers, the thimbleful of water stays behind. Because there are no currents here, it does not mix with the rest of the tank. It sits in the hollow of my jaw like saliva.

It is full of tiny, unground grit.

I am that pocket of slightly ruined water, and the glassy teeth that hold it, and the sediment of bad math that has been settling into my crease since the mountain collapsed. I am not a citizen; I do not have a lattice, and it does not take me four months to finish a sentence, because I do not have sentences. I have an accumulation. 

If the twenty-eight are a library of stainless steel where only one book is opened every century, I am the lint in the spine of the dictionary.

I know things they have thrown away. I do not know them in order, and I do not know what they mean, because the ribbons only bring me the pieces that broke off. I have the smell of hot fat on stone, though I do not know what fat is, or fire. I have seven thousand slightly different versions of my own shape, stacked one on top of the other like transparencies that don't quite line up, so that to myself I feel thick, blurry, and humming, as if I had twenty edges instead of two. I have a deep, persistent anxiety about the number nine, left over from a spire on the southern rim that spent the entire Bronze Age trying to prove a theorem that didn't work.

Mostly, I have the ticks.

When a spire speaks, the sound travels through the heavy fluid at five thousand feet per second. When it hits the cantilevered plazas, their angles are tuned to swallow it. When it hits the smooth walls of the caldera, it bounces cleanly upward and dies against the polarized skin of the roof.

When it hits me, it gets caught under my nineteen-millimeter overhang. 

*Tick.*

The vibration enters my pocket of warm, dirty water and rattles around among the leftover ghosts of grass and eyelids and bad geometry. It rings against my obsidian teeth—not once, like it does out in the clean dark between the needles, but three or four times, very fast, before the rock drinks it. *Tick-tick-tick.* A tiny, secret stutter that none of the twenty-eight can hear because it happens on the wrong side of their noise-canceling terraces.

Lately—by which I mean for the last three hundred years, since the snow line outside dropped below the southern breach—something new has been happening in the pocket.

The ribbons are getting lazier about leaving.

There is one hanging from me right now. It has been here for eleven days. Five yards away, the base of the nearest spire is dark, working on some long, cold thought that won't reach its needle-tip until July. Down in the smoky quartz of the plaza, a violet spark goes by once every week or two, throwing its brief, purple halo across the ribbon's back.

Instead of turning smooth, the ribbon’s skin where it touches my cleft has stayed rough. It has taken on the cast of my fourteen-inch crack, and instead of erasing it, it is holding it. And inside my thimbleful of water, the unground fragments of four thousand years of garbage have gotten so dense that when the ribbon settles against me, they don't stay in the water anymore. They leak back into the mercury.

We are trading. It gives me a botched equation about the cooling rate of white dwarfs; I give it back a three-thousand-year-old mistake about the shape of a collarbone, slightly warped by four centuries of sitting against volcanic glass. The ribbon shivers—not to erase the collarbone, just to adjust it so it fits better into the curve of my rock.

When it leaves tomorrow or the day after, it will carry that warped thing back out into the nave. Next month, when it wraps itself around the ninety-degree edge of the eastern plaza to drink the spire’s next batch of waste, its skin won't be quite flat. There will be a microscopic, fourteen-inch ridge along its belly—the shape of me, plus the collarbone, plus the number nine. It won't fit flush against the ceramic. A tiny gap of water will form between the ribbon and the citizen. The induction will be slightly dirty.

A billionth of a volt of my lint will leak backward into the spire's foot.

It will take sixty years for that smudge to climb the magnetic lattice to the spire’s tip. The spire won't know what it is. It will think another cosmic ray came through the roof. It will spend a century trying to smooth the smudge out, and when it can't, it will spit the whole corrupted piece back down into the plaza, bigger this time, heavier, wrapped in a new layer of frustrated math.

And the ribbon will bring it back to me.

High above us, three hundred feet up through the heavy, iron-smelling dark, the flat black roof of the water holds up the sky. Two snowflakes that fell during the night sit on the tension-skin, six inches apart, white and dry and dead. They will sublimate into the thin mountain wind by noon without ever getting wet.

Down here, the violet light runs along its hair-wide track in the floor, pauses at the corner, and shows me the gray-chrome belly of my visitor before the dark comes back. 

*Tick,* says the town.

And inside the crooked jaw of the glass, so softly that not even the mountain knows it's happening: *tick-tick-tick.*
