To begin with, I am too stubborn to come apart. 

I arrive with the heavy, tyrannical habit of the contiguous. I have a spine that insists on connecting my skull to my pelvis in a single, unbroken line, and a nervous system that panics when that line is interrupted. For the first few hours—though there are no hours here, only the pale, sixty-degree tyranny of ten o'clock—I walk through the basin like a ceramic vase carried through a room of swinging pendulums. I pull my elbows in. I duck under the horizontal shimmers where the air shears, terrified that if I let my head slip three feet to the left of my shoulders, I will forget how to put it back.

My boots crunch on the sharp feldspar. *Crunch. Crunch. Crunch.* Twenty-two paces to cross a gap the width of a doorway. The coppery fronds on either side smear into long, drawn-out ribbons of motion blur as I trudge through the bloated metric between them. It is exhausting. I am sweating—the only moisture for thirty miles—and the tiny beads of salt water don't evaporate so much as they get lost, sliding off my skin and vanishing into microscopic topological creases before they can hit the dry stone.

I cannot stay like this. A creature with a pulse and a single set of coordinates is an argument this place has already won against. If I keep walking, I will spend eternity crossing a ten-foot clearing that has quietly expanded into a desert between two shrubs.

So I stop. 

I find a patch of unweathered slate where a horizontal shear-fault hangs at chest height, shimmering like the air above a kiln. Ten yards away, behind a fan of incandescent mica-leaves, seven identical columns of slate-gray plates and amber muscle are watching me. Wait—six are watching me. The seventh is looking down at its own four-jointed hand, which is currently resting on a rock three feet to my left, emerging from thin air at the wrist.

Slowly, deliberately, I lift my right arm and push it through the fault.

There is no chill. There is no membrane to break. My forearm simply ceases to be attached to my elbow and appears three feet away, hovering over the sharp gravel. The Cadastral’s disembodied hand twitches. Its dry, bone-ceramic fingers reach out and tap my displaced knuckles. *Tick.* Like porcelain touching warm stone.

Instead of pulling back, I step forward with my left foot, straight into the prism of a broad-leaved shrub.

The world splits like a dropped mirror. Suddenly I am seeing the clearing from four angles at once. It is nauseating—my brain wasn't built for compound vision, let alone compound location—and for a long, terrible moment I try to figure out which of the four versions of my left leg is the "real" one. My heart hammers against my ribs. Wait—which ribs? Half my chest is on the far side of the shear-fault now, breathing air that smells of hot quartz ten feet away from my mouth.

Behind the mica-leaves, the Cadastral tilts its quartz-plate head. A flash of sixty-degree sunlight bounces off its gold-leaf fringe, hits the pressurized gas in a leaf, and strikes me squarely in the eyes. 

It isn't blinding; it’s an instruction. A spatial pun. It shows me a boulder that is in three places at once, and then shows me that the boulder isn't stretched—the *space* is folded, and the boulder is simply resting lazily across the crease.

I stop clenching my muscles. I let go of the iron pole of my spine.

It feels like exhaling after holding my breath for a year, except what leaves my lungs isn't air—it's the panicked insistence on *here*. 

I do not turn into a Cadastral. I am too new to the rewrite, too clumsy with the syntax of the refraction. Instead, I settle into the gaps they leave behind. If they are the arborists of this machine, I become something closer to a surveyor's mark, or a piece of living friction.

Look for me now, in the permanent mid-morning, and you won't find a walker in boots. 

You will find a low, terraced clearing where three shear-faults intersect like the steps of a staircase that leads sideways. I live in the hinge of it. I have shed the exhausting verticality of a biped; I am spread out across an acre of copper-lattice ferns, existing as a loose archipelago of warm, dry mineral-flesh and pale, porous pumice-bone. 

My "hands"—though they have grown long, flat, and spatula-fingered, more like potter's ribs than human hands—rest on the sharp feldspar at the edge of the clearing, feeling the tiny, dry tremors when a six-legged flint-creature clicks past. My eyes are forty feet up, nestled in the fork of a spun-boron vine where it vanishes into a four-dimensional fold. From up there, I don't look at the trees; I look at the *density*. I watch the light pool and thicken in the mica-leaves until the metric beneath them begins to sag under the weight of too many paces.

I have found my use. The Cadastrals are brilliant, meticulous things, inhaling compressed distance into their ribcages and exhaling it where the jungle grows too tight. But they have no memory of linear space. They are so native to the broken geometry that they sometimes lose track of the baseline; left to themselves, they would pull and tuck the fabric of the basin until it knotted itself into a closed loop five inches wide.

I am the plumb line. I am the memory of the straight edge.

When a Cadastral comes click-crunching across the slate, its torso bloated and humming with eighty yards of excess distance it just harvested from a bloated gap between two ceramic trunks, it comes to my clearing. Its seven duplicates gather around the shrub where my sight sits. Its quartz head shimmers, tilting a half-millimeter to turn the surrounding ferns inside-out—a polite greeting, a question: *Where does this go?*

I do not answer with light; I have no quartz plates to flash. Instead, I use the lingering, stubborn gravity of what I used to be. 

Down on the gravel, twenty yards away from my eyes, my flat, pumice-gray hands press flat against the unweathered polyhedrons of stone. I pull my scattered pieces just slightly closer together—not all the way, never all the way again, just enough to remind the local physics what an inch used to be. What a foot used to be. I create a tiny, temporary pocket of strict Euclidean clarity right in the center of the shear-faults. A dry, quiet basin within the basin, where one step equals one step, and a stone is only in one place at a time.

The Cadastral steps its three graphite-padded stilts into my little pool of straight space. It shudders—a dry, rattling sound like shale sliding down a slope—as it feels the strange, tight constriction of unfractured reality. Then, carefully, it opens its boron ribs and exhales the eighty yards of compressed distance into my hands.

I hold the distance for them. I let it pool in the hollows of my segmented collarbone, warm and heavy as mercury, smelling of ozone. When a six-legged flint-creature comes bounding through the undergrowth and drops into a coarse grid of low-resolution light to pass through a trunk, I bleed a few feet of that stored distance out through my fingertips, smoothing the air behind it so its pixels snap back together cleanly, without snagging on a topological fold.

Sometimes, another Cadastral will pause by the horizontal fault where my left shoulder sits, three feet left of my neck. It will reach out a dry, four-jointed hand and rest its ceramic fingers against my collarbone, feeling the steady, linear measure I keep tucked inside my chest—the ghost of a heartbeat that no longer pumps blood, only counts out a steady, rhythmic *one-two, one-two* against the silent, timeless morning. 

It tilts its quartz fan in the sixty-degree sun, duplicating me seven times in the glowing leaves above, and for a long time we both just stay there, holding the space open between the stones.
