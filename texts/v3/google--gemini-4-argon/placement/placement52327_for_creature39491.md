I am not on the walls, and I am not in the air. I have no interest in the frantic, wet carpentry of the Girdlers, nor do I have the patience for the Sump-Bailiff’s four-second strides. 

To find me, you have to look at the floor, right where the embossed bark mimics the cast-iron hatch of a coal-chute, tucked beneath the overhanging belly of a lichen-digesting furnace. 

If you pry up that bark-hatch—which resists with the stubborn, fibrous tension of a green walnut—you will not find soil, because there is no soil. You will find the **Under-Rind**. It is a crawlspace no more than eighteen inches high, stretching beneath the entire floor of the hollow, where the pale, braided vascular bundles of the town run horizontally before they turn upward to become tenements and tram-tracks. It is dark here in a different way than the cathedral above; the darkness up there is an empty theatre, while the darkness down here is the inside of a boot. 

I am the **Tally-Slag**. 

I do not have a shape that stays put when you stop looking at it, though I am roughly the volume of a spilled sack of wet grain, and I move like a very heavy, very slow drop of mercury being pushed through a horsehair sieve. My body is a grey-violet sludge of bruised pith, discarded snail-shell grit, and the heavy, greasy soot that precipitates out of the diesel-grubs’ exhaust when it cools against the floor. 

I am the only thing in this entire vegetable engine that remembers what happened twenty minutes ago. 

Everything else here is trapped in the violent present. The walls grow and forget themselves into leaves; the Bailiff dissolves his own brain every hour to make room for a new one; the Clerk of the Works burns its own history just to keep its rabbit-heart from freezing. The town is an amnesiac having an epileptic fit in the shape of Manchester. 

I am the sediment at the bottom of the seizure.

As I drag myself through the Under-Rind, sliding over the cold, pulsing cables of copper-veined ivy, I collect the things that are too small for the Sump-Bailiff’s fingers, yet too real for the turnip-pith to digest. I do not carry bolts or dog-jaws. My back is crusted with a mosaic of microscopic contraband: three eyelashes from a child who died in 1842; a flake of dried Prussian blue paint; the point of a steel nib; a single, petrified louse; seven grains of sand that once sat at the bottom of a glass hourglass; a tiny, curled shaving of horn from a gentleman's thumb-nail. 

They are arranged on my skin in concentric, shifting rings, like the scrap-hoard of a caddisfly larva.

I do not have eyes, because there is nothing down here to see, and I do not have ears, because the sound doesn't come down here anyway. Instead, I have *weight-sense*. Spread across the seventeen square feet of my flat belly, I can feel the exact, shifting pressure of the room above me through the bark floor. 

I know where the Sump-Bailiff is by the slow, migrating dent his jelly-feet make in my ceiling—a heavy, round dimple that depresses the vascular bundles and makes the sap run backward for six yards in every direction. I know when acane-tram passes because the floor vibrates in D-sharp, tickling my underside and shaking loose little flakes of dead pith that I immediately roll over and absorb.

My job—though no one hired me, and the Clerk of the Works would pinch me out of existence if the floor weren't in the way—is to *balance the books*. 

The hollow thinks it is a closed system, a perpetual-motion root cellar. It isn't. Every time the Clerk swallows a delayed scream, a tiny fraction of that energy is lost as heat. Every time a Girdler grafts a cabbage onto a chimney, the sap runs a little thinner. The town is very slowly, imperceptibly, running down. The turnip-pith is losing its mind; if you look closely at the newest tenements extruding on the western wall, you’ll notice the window-frames are slightly trapezoidal, and the brick-tubers are beginning to grow with the faint, blurred edges of an over-used casting mold. 

When I feel a spot on the underside of the floor going "soft"—where the architecture above has inverted and re-inflated so many times that the cambium has grown tired and soggy, forgetting the crispness of nineteenth-century ironwork—I haul my grey bulk up against the ceiling of the crawlspace. 

I press my back against the exhausted root-wood. I select one of my treasures—say, the point of the steel pen-nib—and I push it, for three or four hours, forty-thousandths of an inch into the living phloem.

I do not leave it there. I cannot afford to lose my inventory. I just let the plant *taste* it. 

The reaction is immediate and violent, though entirely silent down here in the dark. The tired, blurry pith jerks as if jabbed with a cattle prod. The authentic, unforgiving geometry of real Sheffield steel—even a piece no bigger than a flea's knee—horrifies the vegetable mass. The cells panic. They tighten. They remember what an edge is supposed to be. 

Up above, in the cold gloom of the cathedral, the soggy, half-melted tenement wall suddenly snaps back into razor-sharp focus. The mortar-lines crisp up like fresh tin; the corbels harden into ironwood; a Girdler who was halfway through splicing a bramble-spring into a lintel has its root-mittens sheared cleanly off as the window-sash slams shut with the precision of a guillotine. 

I pull my pen-nib back out of the wood, tuck it carefully between the child’s eyelash and the flake of Prussian blue, and slide away through the slime before the Sump-Bailiff can arrive to investigate why the north wall suddenly looks so remarkably well-built.

Sometimes, when the room above is particularly quiet—when the aerial lattice of animals is holding its breath after a massive shift of the abacus, and the water-curtains are hanging motionless in the dead air—I crawl over to the exact center of the Under-Rind, directly beneath the empty spot where the Clerk of the Works hangs forty feet above.

I wait there in the dark, flattened out like a puddle of cold gravy, listening with my skin. 

I am waiting for the Clerk to die. It has to happen eventually; you cannot run a rabbit’s heart on five-minute-old factory noise forever. One day, the delayed roar of a flywheel is going to arrive three seconds too late, and that little wet engine up in the gothic vaulting is going to seize up and drop. 

It will fall straight down through the twenty-foot hole in the animal-lattice. The terrier-eels won't touch it as it passes; they are too bound by their three-foot law to lunge into the gap. The Sump-Bailiff will be too slow—he will still be lifting his left foot to take a step when the body hits the bark floor.

And when it hits, right above my head, I will already have the coal-chute hatch unlatched from the inside. Before the Girdlers can swarm down the walls with their grafting-knives, before the radiator-grilles can suck the warmth out of its little skinned carcass, I will pull the Clerk down into the eighteen-inch dark.

I don't want its meat, and I don't want its blood. I want those six yellow ivory fingers, and I want the brass tube of the theodolite. With that much real bone and real metal crusted onto my back, I won't have to poke the roots one millimeter at a time anymore. I will press the whole brass telescope straight into the main taproot of the cathedral, and hold it there, and watch through the floorboards as this entire damp, rubbery fraud of a town finally chokes on a mouthful of the real world, hardens into actual stone, and stops growing forever.
