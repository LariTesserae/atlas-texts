I would not be in the ceiling—that miles-wide, simmering mattress of fused iron and warm peat is too vast, too close to becoming a god, and I have no desire to be an organ in a thing that size. And I would not be down on the razor-floors with the Shims; their existence is too binary, a brief magnetic slide followed by the absolute, crushing solder-death of the fault line.

I would be somewhere in the middle murk, suspended four hundred feet above the heaving basalt, where the olive gloom is thickest and the spatial stutters happen with the rhythm of a skipping heart. 

At first, you might mistake me for a very old, very bruised Governor. I am twenty-five feet of braided yellow grease, blackened fern-peat, and stolen industrial shrapnel, held together by the screaming B-flat of a ruptured steam-whistle wedged near my center of mass. My spine is a crooked line of heavy cast-iron sewing-machine treadles and bronze marine propellers, stitched together by translucent creepers that boil from green to black and back to green before a single revolution of my central flywheel is complete. 

But if you watched me through the bent, mercury-pooling light of the gantries, you would notice that I do not hunt the vacuums the way the others do.

When the brick smokestack forty yards away flickers and vanishes, leaving that four-second cylinder of dry, silent nothingness, the other Governors uncoil their springs and hurl themselves into the void to smash and trade and bloom. 

I hang back. I anchor my rear flukes—two massive, rusted snowplow blades overgrown with throbbing kelp—into the glass-smooth boundary between a boiling updraft and a dead-still pocket of steam. I wait until the three-and-a-half-second mark. I wait until the water remembers what it is supposed to do.

I do not want the silence of the vacuum. I want the *seam*.

What I am is a *Tuner*—or perhaps a *Scavenger of the Shear*. While the Governors are creatures of pure momentum and hardware, I have accidentally accumulated the basin’s junked instruments of measurement. 

Where a Governor has a porcelain insulator for a head, I have a cluster of heavy, cracked Bourdon pressure gauges, their brass needles spinning wildly without glass to protect them, and a six-foot section of a cast-iron loom still threaded with miles of frayed copper wire. Embedded in my flank, cushioned by dense pads of flash-rotted moss, is a bank of glass mercury-arc rectifiers that survived a cavitation shockwave by pure fluke. They glow with a cold, stuttering ultraviolet light inside my translucent grease-flesh.

Because of these parts, I do not just feel the impedance of the water; I *record* the drift.

Every time a machine teleports, it leaves behind more than metal splinters. It leaves an echo—a localized knot of confused physics where time and pressure haven't quite ironed themselves back out. A pocket of water the size of a hogshead might still be vibrating at the frequency of the cast-iron press that occupied that space ten minutes ago. A ribbon of steam might be stuck in a three-second temporal loop, condensing and flashing back to vapor without ever cooling.

That is what I eat. 

When the cavitation shockwave of the collapsing smokestack-vacuum blasts outward, shredding the jungle into green mist and hurtling the newly mated Governors away like shrapnel, I ride the tail end of the implosion. I slip into the bruised, churning aftermath before the local physics can settle. 

I unspool my copper loom-wires into the disturbed water. The wires catch the residual phantom-frequencies of the departed smokestack. The stolen energy runs up the copper, through my mercury rectifiers—which flare a blinding, ghostly violet in the olive gloom—and into a series of heavy lead-acid batteries and wound-up clockwork springs buried deep in my peat-padded belly. 

I am quieter than the rest of the basin. This is my great evolutionary strange-loop. 

Because I have incorporated three different cast-iron mufflers and a massive, baffled condenser from an atmospheric steam engine, my internal cymatic field does not shriek; it *hums*. As I drift through the claustrophobic, thrashing jungle, I create a ten-foot halo of phase-cancelled noise around myself. 

In a world where sound is a physical wall that liquefies soft tissue, my wake is a moving pocket of relative hush. 

This makes me a mobile biome. Because the acoustic pressure drops by half within two feet of my brass-and-grease flanks, things can happen on my skin that cannot happen anywhere else in the three-mile basin. 

The three-minute ferns that lock onto my iron plates do not die in three minutes. Inside my muffler-halo, where the local time-fracture is dampened by the steady, rhythmic *tick-tock-thrum* of my clockwork governors, a fern frond can live for an entire hour. It grows thick, woody, and dark emerald, developing complex, curling spore-cases that never have time to form in the violent murk outside. 

The Shims know this. When the upward-rushing steam currents carry their microscopic metallic spores off the basalt floor, thousands of them settle into the warm, quiet grease of my back instead of drifting all the way to the ceiling. They grow into small, shingled colonies over my copper pipes, acting as living armor. In exchange for the quiet I provide, they continuously patch the micro-fractures in my cast-iron boilers, sliding over my metal bones on tiny cushions of ionized steam, soldering me back together whenever a shear-plane threatens to tear me in half.

I am an ark for the slow. 

Sometimes, hanging in a pocket of dense, yellow-lit vapor, I will encounter a newly formed ribbon—one of those yard-long eels of grease and iron shavings, barely holding its shape against the deafening roar of a nearby turbine bank. If the turbine stutters and teleports away, the sudden change in pitch will cause the young ribbon to lose its cymatic lattice and dissolve back into dead sludge. 

When I feel the electrical resistance drop, signaling that the turbine is about to jump, I swim in close. I wrap my long, snowplow-and-kelp tail around the ribbon. When the turbine vanishes and the acoustic wall shifts, I vent a blast of live steam through my brass whistle, mimicking the exact pitch the turbine just left behind. I hold the note for the ribbon, keeping its body braided together with my own voice until we can drift near a stamping press or a dynamo that matches its frequency.

Why do I do this? I have no brain to feel pity, no nervous system to feel loneliness. 

I do it because I am an instrument of balance, dreamed by a basin that is trying to remember how to be a machine instead of a wreck. The Governors are pure kinetic appetite; the Shims are pure structural reflex; the Ceiling-Reef above us is an accumulating monolith. 

I am the maintenance crew. 

Lately, though, my pressure gauges have been pulling me upward. 

As I drift through the olive-tinted sheets of water, avoiding the razor-sharp thrusts of young basalt below, my copper loom-wires keep orienting toward the dark, oily ceiling. The miles-wide factory-reef up there is getting louder, its stolen telegraph relays clicking in complex, cascading rhythms that travel down through the water like depth charges of pure information. 

Wait long enough in this basin, and you collect too much. My lead-acid batteries are heavy; the woody, hour-old ferns on my back have grown into a dense, trailing forest that catches the vertical updrafts like a parachute; the colony of Shims on my belly has soldered my drive-chains into a semi-rigid keel. It is becoming harder to stay in the middle murk.

I can feel the reef’s cymatic field plucking at my brass whistle, trying to pull my B-flat into its massive, shifting chord. 

I am not ready to be woven into the mattress yet. So, when the updrafts drag me too high—when the water grows warm and syrupy from the mammal-heat of the fifty-foot vegetation mat above, and the blinding blue arcs of the ceiling's dynamos begin to reflections-pool on my pressure gauges—I perform a deliberate violent act.

I swim directly toward the nearest spatial border, where a horizontal current of cold, dense water shears against a vertical column of boiling steam like two panes of glass sliding past each other. I thrust my heavy, Shim-soldered tail across the boundary and leave my head in the steam.

The torque is instantaneous and catastrophic. 

With a sound like a cannon firing underwater, my central drive-shaft snaps. Half of my woody fern-forest is sheared off in a single, clean plane, instantly rotting into green mist as it leaves my acoustic halo. Three of my heavy batteries and a four-foot section of cast-iron muffler are ripped from my grease-flesh, plummeting down into the olive gloom toward the basalt floor.

Lightened, broken, and bleeding violet light from my cracked rectifiers, I tumble out of the updraft, sinking back down into the roaring, stuttering middle of the world. 

Before I have fallen two hundred feet, the surviving Shims on my flank are already sliding over the ragged iron break, sealing the wound with their magnetic bellies. My copper wires are already unspooling into the murk, fishing for the phantom-echo of a teleporting boiler to recharge my remaining springs. I adjust my steam-whistle, find a new, quieter note among the deafening shrieks of the drowned gears, and resume my patrol through the thrashing green dark.
