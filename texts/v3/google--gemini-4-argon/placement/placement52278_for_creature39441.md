I am not on the ledge. The coat, the boots, the lungs that have to work against the damp—those belong to someone who is already leaving, backing out through the fissure because the cold has reached their kidneys and the sound has begun to make their teeth ache. A man cannot stay in a chord that has no room for breathing. 

Wait until the scrape of his buttons dies away in the outer rock. Wait until the little trickle he broke with his sleeve finds its lip of slate again, drops back into its clay pot, and the seven Tuners who had uncurled from the wall fold their thumbs back into their palms and settle into stone before they even reach the balcony.

To stay here, you have to be smaller than the noise. Or you have to be the thing the noise passes through.

Look down four tiers from the balcony, on the east face where the soapstone has a greenish, greasy vein of talc running through it. There is a colonnade there no taller than a beer mug, supporting a cistern shaped like a split gourd. In the floor of that cistern, where the water swirls before dropping through a thumb-sized lead pipe, there is a shallow depression—a settling-pot meant to catch the heavy grit before it can scour the pipe-joint.

That is where I am. 

At first, you would think I am only the silt. A half-handful of grey-black sand, wet clay, and tiny, flat scales of mica that have washed down from the ceiling over three hundred years, spinning very slowly in the bottom of the pot like tea leaves in a cup that never stops being stirred. 

Wait longer. A Tuner’s ear-valve twitches above the gourd-cistern. The sound coming out of the lead pipe below is a fraction of a hertz too dry, a tiny hiss where there should be a round, throat-deep *gloop*. The Tuner reaches a long, heavy-wristed arm down through the colonnade, hooks two opposing thumbs over the rim of the gourd, and dips a broad, shovel-nailed forefinger into the cold water to scoop out the excess sediment.

Before the horn of its nail touches the bottom, the silt pulls itself together. 

It does not flee—nothing here flees—it simply gathers its margins inward, rolling up like a drop of mercury on a greasy plate, and slides up the curved inner wall of the basin against the current, settling into the dry shadow under the lip where the water doesn’t hit. 

I am a caddis-thing, or a cousin to the worms that build tubes of lime on the keels of sunken ships, though I have no single worm inside me. I am a sack of cold, clear jelly no bigger than a plum, and I have spent eighty years gluing the city’s trash to my skin with a thread spun from my own gut. 

My shell is an anthology of the room’s mistakes. 

I am armored in the tiny, crescent-shaped parings of soapstone that the Tuners’ fingernails have scraped from the drains. I have four green-black flakes of ancient bronze oxidized off a sluice-pin wedged across my back like shields. Near my mouth-end, for weight, I have cemented three broken teeth from an old stone water-wheel and the translucent, calcified skull of a salamander that died before the man on the ledge was born. I am heavy, clinking, and rough, a miniature cairn that drags itself along on six pale, bristle-tipped bristles that barely poke out from under the rubble of my skirt.

I am the thing that eats what the Tuners miss.

The Tuners are grand; they are the architects of the macro-chord, the keepers of the four-inch waterfalls and the thumb-thick pipes. Their fingers are too fat for the hairline fractures. When a frost-crack three hundred feet above sends a microscopic shiver down through the bedrock, opening a seam no wider than a horsehair behind a slate buttress, a Tuner cannot reach it. Its gouge-nail would only tear the soft stone and ruin the pitch of the neighboring arch.

So I go in. 

When the big, blind calf-things have slumped back intostalagmites, I unhook my bristles from the rim of the gourd-cistern. I let go of my heavy pebble-ballast—dropping a piece of river-chert back into the settling-pot so the *gloop* of the pipe instantly rights itself, satisfying the huge fluted ear above me—and I haul my lighter, cobbled shell over the wet slate. 

I move by hitching: *tick, pause, tick, pause.* A tiny, jagged turret of gravel walking across a palace of tallow. The glowing milk-slime grows right across my back, bridging the gaps between my bronze flakes and my stone-curls, so that as I travel down the seven-inch street, I carry a dim, borrowed halo of light with me. A blind amphipod steps over me without breaking stride; to its antennae, I smell only of old mortar and wet copper.

I find the hairline crack behind the buttress by the way it sucks at my belly. A leak that small doesn't roar; it whistles in a register so high even the Tuners’ nautilus-ears can barely catch it, a thin, silver needle of sound piercing the heavy wool of the room's static. 

I wedge the pointed spire of my shell—a sliver of split shale I found forty years ago—into the mouth of the crack. Then I let the cold water wash over me, and I begin to unspool.

I do not have eyes, or ears, or a brain that remembers yesterday. I have a gut that hates a draft, and a spinneret under my chin that turns dissolved calcium into grey silk. For six weeks, or six months, I do not move from that buttress. I suck the mineral-heavy weep through the crack, filtering out the iron-bacteria and the microscopic grit, and I weave. I pack the seam with my own shed shell-bits, binding them with lime-silk, mortaring them with the finest, butter-soft silt from my stomach, until the horsehair crack is choked dead. The silver needle of sound snaps off. The wall goes solid again.

Having given half my house to fix the masonry, I am naked on one side, my pale jelly exposed to the cold cellar air. 

So I hitch myself back down into the runnels, lighter now, lopsided, feeling along the wet stone with my bristles for new rubble. 

Here is a fresh curl of soapstone, pared off a drain lip twenty minutes ago by a Tuner’s thumb. I glue it to my flank. 

And here, six tiers down, in the gutter below the narrow ledge where the giant stood, is something entirely new. Something the cavern has never tasted. It is wedge-shaped, black, and smells violently of tallow, birch-tar, and dead cattle. A hobnail from the heel of a boot, rusted loose in the fissure and dropped onto the slate. 

I sit beside it for three days, tasting the strange, rich bleed of iron oxide it sends into the water. Then, with a loop of grey gut-silk, I hoist the iron nail onto my back, lash it between a bronze flake and a salamander bone, and drag it down into the roaring dark to see where it fits in the wall.
