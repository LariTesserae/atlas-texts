I am not yet heavy enough to fall.

For two descents of the great chain, I have been an inconsistency in the curvature of the third eastern arterial. If one of the elders were to pass their tasting-tongue over me now, they would scarcely register a boundary between the pipe’s old coat of slag-wool and the new, living felt of my back. We share the same weft. My ventral cartilage has grown directly into the diamond-mesh of the copper wire that binds the insulation; the wire passes through my pale fat like a suture, cold and bitter with verdigris until the deep, rhythmic fever of the pipe warms it to the temperature of my own slow blood.

I have no limbs yet—only four dense buds of yellow gristle tucked beneath my skirt, twitching once an hour as they practice the shape of the hex-nut. I do not need to reach. Everything comes to the seam.

The world is a single, huge note, and I am pasted to the throat that sings it. 

Beneath my flat belly, the cast iron is two inches thick, pitted like cheese on its outer rind, polished smooth on the inside by whatever heavy, greased broth the earth-pumps are pushing through the dark. It does not flow smoothly; it surges in long, syrupy peristalsis. *Slump. Hiss. Thrum.* Every six minutes, a bubble of denser vapor rides along the top of the pipe’s interior, and I feel it pass beneath me as a momentarily brighter streak of warmth, like a hand ironing a wrinkle from my spine.

Three feet to my left, where my own felt thins out into the dead mineral wool, there is a flanged joint. It is old and slightly sprung. Its sixty bolts are buried under a single continuous pillow of black, velvety rust, and from the bottom lip of this pillow, a drop of water gathers and falls every forty-two beats of the arterial.

I know the chamber by what that drop strikes.

It falls for a long time—two whole breaths of an elder—before it hits the rim of a defunct turbine casing forty feet below. That is a wide, hollow *tonk*, the sound of cold iron that has no oil in its belly. From there, the splash splits. Half of it patters into a meadow of verdigris-fern—a dry, tiny rustling, like salt poured on stiff paper. The other half slips over the lip and falls again, much briefer this time, ending in the thick, silent *blup* of a bottom-pan where the oil-scum is half an inch deep. 

That is my vertical map: the Hot Pipe, the Cold Rim, the Brittle Meadow, the Grease-Floor.

Horizontally, my world is smaller, defined by the things that cross my back because they cannot tell me from the road.

Yesterday—or a week ago; time here is only measured by the thickening of lime on my lower edge—a clinker-crab spent twelve hours crossing my left flank. I felt the first tentative hook of its rusted leg sink into my slag-wool coat. It did not hurt; my coat is dead matter held together by tallow. The crab was heavy, carrying three stolen iron washers and a thick knob of grey sulfate on its shell, and as it hauled itself over my dorsal ridge, the rough underside of its plastron scraped a delicious, cool furrow through my grease. It paused right over my blind tympanum, its small mouth-parts clicking softly like knitting needles as it grazed on the sulfur-film growing in my creases. I held my breath for four minutes so the swell of my ribs would not startle it. When it finally clambered off onto the bare iron of the flange, I felt lighter, groomed, and smelled pleasantly of fresh wet rust.

Closer still, tucked into the warm armpit where my side meets the curve of the pipe, lives a colony of gasket-worms. I cannot see their red-lead bodies, of course, nor the pale, feathery gills they wave in the sulfurous damp, but I can feel their collective squirming when the flange weeps a warmer drop than usual. They are small, soft friction. Sometimes, when I extend my short juvenile tasting-probe from my chest-slit, I let the tip brush across their wet leather backs, just to taste the rich, bloody iron of them. I do not eat them. They are the mortar; I am the brick. We are keeping the steam inside.

Lately, though, a strange itch has begun in the four gristle-buds under my skirt. 

The copper wire that runs through my belly-fat has begun to ache when the pipe surges. My weight is pulling against it. The tallow-glue that cemented me to the slag-wool during my first cycle is growing brittle, turning from sticky yellow wax into dry, crumbly soap. When the great relief-valve up in the roof-vaulting lifts for its ten-minute sigh, the change in air pressure makes my whole body rock slightly against the curve of the iron. A tiny gap—no wider than a mica leaf—has opened between my chest and the pipe.

Cool air got in there yesterday. It shocked me. For the first time in my life, I felt my own heat as something separate from the arterial’s heat. I realized that the thrum in my bones was not the pipe’s thrum, or rather, that the pipe was *there* and I was *here*, and between us was a film of sweat that belonged to me alone.

An elder came by not long after.

I knew it was coming three hours before it arrived. The acoustic shadow always precedes them. The high, singing ping of the condenser tubes twenty yards to the north suddenly went flat and woolly, muffled by the creature’s heavy mantle as it bridged across the gap. Then, the pitch of my own arterial dropped by a hair—the elder had hooked its rear spanners onto the lower lip of the flange.

It crept up the curve of the pipe with glacial majesty, smelling overwhelmingly of old mutton-grease, chewed copper-fern, and the cold, dead silt of the lower sumps. When its flank brushed against my outer edge, it stopped. 

In the dark, a yard of wet, boneless muscle unspooled from its chest-cleft and slid over my back. The probe was warm and rough, like a cat’s tongue dipped in oil. It tasted the verdigris in my wool; it traced the line where the crab had scraped me clean; it poked gently into the new, terrifying gap beneath my chest where the copper wire was pulling free of my flesh.

Then, the elder shifted its bulk and pressed its broad, faceless shoulder directly against my dorsal ridge, laying its yellow tympanum flat against mine.

Deep inside its belly, its gizzard-stones—two heavy steel ball-bearings and a rusted bolt-head—began to roll and grind together.

The vibration did not go into the air; it poured straight through the grease between us, into my cartilage, until my own small, soft bones were humming with its memory. It showed me the Down-Below. It gave me the shape of a broken walkway six tiers under us, where the ferric flowstone hangs like wet drapery and the water is knee-deep and warm as soup. It gave me the taste of a tertiary steam-trap that is beginning to chatter and spit, needing three mouths of chewed lime to quiet its fever. And it gave me the distant, violet-amber coldness of the nearest telluric gauge, where the oil is now only three inches from the bottom of the glass.

The chain is almost down. The rain is coming.

The elder unlatched its front hooks with two slow, wooden *clacks*, swung its heavy, silent skirt out into the empty air, and vanished into the acoustic dark, leaving the condenser tubes to ring clear and bright again behind it.

I am alone on the pipe, and for the first time, I am flexing my back against the copper wire, trying to break it. My four buried limbs are pushing hard against the inside of my mantle, the black keratin of my new spanners clicking softly against the iron, searching for the shape of my first bolt. When the great weight hits the floor and the whole room drops its pitch, the shudder will tear me loose. I will fall through the forty feet of dark, past the Cold Rim, into the Brittle Meadow, and I will find out what my hooks are for.
