I am the line that has not happened yet. 

I am forty feet long and three hundred feet deep, running vertically through the blue-black basalt on the western step of the basin, two hundred yards upslope from the lattice-boom crane. Right now, I have no width at all. I am only an agreement among the crystals of pyroxene and plagioclase that when the time comes, this is the plane along which they will let go of one another. 

Above me, at the lip of the ledge, the three Gantry-Tenders have been wedged into my upper throat for thirty-four days. 

I do not see them, because I have no eyes, and the stone has not yet parted to let the white glare in. I know them only as a divergent ache. Before they slid their grease-skirts into the notch at my top, the mountain was a single, blind block of compression, holding its breath since the Miocene. Now, there is a direction. The three heavy frustums are drinking the sweat of the cliff and bleeding the high-tension cables, and every time a walnut-sized bubble of hydrogen rises through their yellow sight-glasses, the lateral load against my walls increases by another four hundred pounds per square inch.

Time, down here in the unweathered dark, is measured by the creep of the rock-salt crystals and the thermal expansion of the afternoon. 

When the sun—that solid white band of heat—crosses the sky above the bladder-moss, the top two feet of the basalt warm by three degrees. The stone tries to swell. Because the three Tenders are locked in the seam like cast-iron chocks, the swelling cannot close the gap; it has to go downward. It drives the point of the wedge a fraction of a micron deeper into my chest. 

*Tick.* 

That was Tuesday. Two square inches of bond gave way twelve feet down, near a pocket of grey chert. No sound escaped to the surface; the stone simply sighed into a slightly wider arrangement of itself, and the vibration traveled down through the roots of the mountain and vanished into the mantle.

If I am anything, I am the anticipation of the cold sweat.

For millions of years I have been completely dry, sealed away from the cellar-air in the tight, dark masonry of the range. But at my very top, just beneath the rubbery, horn-scaled tube-feet of the middle Tender, a film of moisture has crept two inches down into the dark. It tastes of copper sulfate, old tallow, and the ozone that hangs around the porcelain bells. It is the coldest thing that has ever touched me. Along that two-inch margin, the pale, hair-thin rootlets of a silver-barked scrub have already found their way in, following the Tender’s mucilage, pressing their blind, thirsty tips against the microscopic steps of the fracture. 

They are waiting for me to open so they can fall inside.

The whole basin is a grammar of stress, and I am a sentence half-spoken. Through the solid floor of the valley, I can feel the other operations—not as sounds, because sound requires air to tremble, and the moss eats the air’s trembling. I feel them as shear-waves in the bedrock. 

A quarter-mile south, the crane’s hydraulic ram pushes against its buried manifold; as the four-ton block of chert rises three inches an hour off its bed, the rock beneath the crane unloads, lifting by the thickness of a fingernail over a week. That relief pulls slightly at my heels. Further up the ridge, the six ribbed gas-separators turn on their greased bronze tracks. Their weight rolls from one set of dinner-plate hex-nuts to the next over twenty-four hours, a slow, circular kneading of the cliffside that rocks the basalt slabs like loose teeth in a jaw.

Everything here is levering against everything else. The machines pump oil to lift the stone; the stone parts to vent the heavy, cold gases from the deep faults; the condensers catch the gas and the static charge; the Tenders drink the charge to split the water to pump their bladders to split me. There is no difference between the hydrology, the geology, and the machinery. We are all just different viscosities of the same grey, oiled weight.

Right now, it is late afternoon on the thirty-fifth day. The glare outside is at its fiercest, bleaching the sky, baking the waxy rosettes until their horn-flesh is taut with cold water. 

In the cleft above me, the third Tender—the oldest one, whose lead-carbonate casing is scarred from an ancient rockfall and patched with hardened grease—has just finished digesting a gallon of condensate. Its internal pressure has reached nine hundred pounds per square inch. Its six vulcanized-rubber valves at the crown are clamped shut, sealed with tallow. 

Down in the dark, fifteen feet below the surface, a single grain of quartz in my wall—a flaw no bigger than a pinhead, trapped when the lava cooled—reaches its shear limit. 

It does not break yet. It hums. It is a vibration too high and too small for the Tenders’ heavy, oil-filled tympanums to register, a tiny, localized panic in the crystal lattice. The load shifts from that single grain to the three grains beside it. They cannot hold it. They pass it to twelve grains below them.

I am gathering myself.

In four minutes, or perhaps six, the cascade will reach the chert layer at my base. When it does, I will cease to be a potential and become a place. 

The crack will be flat, dry, and absolute—a single, instantaneous rearrangement of forty feet of cliff face that will slap the dead air like a board, startle the non-existent birds, and vanish into the sponge of the bladder-moss before it can even make an echo.

I will drop three inches. 

For the first two seconds, as the slab settles onto the lower ledge, my two new faces will be warm from the friction of parting, smelling sharply of scorched flint and trapped, ancient sulfur. And then the white, high-altitude light will pour into me like bleach. The cellar-cold air will rush into the three-inch vacuum. Within ten minutes, before the three Tenders above have even begun to vent their hydrogen bubbles and unstick their brass-rimmed tube-feet from the rim, the first microscopic beads of water will condense out of the bright nothing and sit on my fresh, matte-black skin.

By tomorrow, the Tenders will be inside me, sliding down the new dark on their skirts of grease, bolting their cold copper pipes to my walls. In ten years, a brass-washer larva will drop from a rosette leaf and lodge in my throat, growing its shell in the dark. In a hundred, I will be packed so tight with silver-barked scrub and lead-sheathed cable that no one will remember the rock was ever whole.

The grain of quartz lets go. The line begins to run.
