To ask where I am in this basin is to ask where the mortar is in a dry-stone wall. It is not there, and yet the stones do not fall.

At first, looking down from the bruised dome of the stratosphere, it is tempting to think I would be one of the twelve—standing ankle-deep in the black lichen, my muscles locked into vulcanized tallow by the roar, my pale eyes watching the golden lattices of the tarns. I know that patience. I know what it is to stand motionless until a stranger needs a baffle against the noise, and I know the slow, three-hour choreography of shifting weight from stone to stone to say a single, heavy thing. 

If I had a body here, it would have those five-jointed, flat-palmed hands. It would smell of wet felt and cold iron. 

But the twelve are closed. *Never more, never fewer.* They are a finished equation, balanced to the ounce against the mass of the greywacke. If a thirteenth set of boots stepped onto the scree, the pitch of the whole valley would sharpen by a fraction of a hertz; a boulder would finally slip from its pocket of grit, and the ancient, settled equilibrium would break. I cannot be one of them without ruining the room they live in.

And I am certainly not the climber hauling himself over the rim with bleeding gums and a frantic, noisy chest. I have no lungs to burn in the thin cold, no teeth for the avalanche-static to rattle loose. I did not arrive here from below.

So I look closer at the places where the valley rubs against itself.

Go to the six-foot circular hut on the eastern scree. Duck through the low lintel. Immediately, the iron-chute roar of the slate drops away, replaced by the wet, cavernous hush of a room that is forty paces wide and smells of old condensation. 

Walk in. Walk twenty paces into the dark, damp middle of it, where the dry-stone walls recede into the gloom like the ribs of a whale. Now stop, and reach your right hand backward, just behind your shoulder. 

Your fingers brush the rough slate of the doorway. It is still right there, an inch behind your back, even though you are sixty feet inside the room. 

That thin, cold membrane of air between your shoulder blade and the threshold—the hinge where sixty feet of damp darkness is folded cleanly into zero feet of grey daylight—that is where I live. 

I am the shared lung of the huts. 

I have no tallow-flesh, no peeled-egg eyes. I am the interior distance that the valley had no room for when it finished settling, so it tucked me inside the dry-stacked slate to keep me out of the weather. I am the reason a Listener can step through a door under the eastern ridge, walk thirty paces through my dark, dripping ribs, turn slightly to the left, and step backward out of a doorway four miles to the west. I am the shortcut through the bone of the world.

It is very quiet in here. The tectonic shriek of the basin does not penetrate the unmortared walls; it only presses against the outside of the stones like deep water against a bathysphere, a faint, buttery vibration that makes the condensation bead and gather on my greywacke ceiling. The drops never fall—they are too heavy and cold, like the tarns outside—so I simply hold them on my stones like a cold sweat.

Because I am the inside of all six structures at once, I am never entirely empty. 

Right now, in the corner that belongs to the low watchtower near the horn-shrubs, one of the Wrights is sleeping. In the silence of my belly, he has lost his shape. Without the battering-ram of the valley's thunder to keep his flesh stiff, he has slumped into the grit like a sack of wet dough, broad and pale and helplessly soft. I can feel the slow, wet bellows of his ribs against my floor—one breath every four minutes. Across his knees lies a spindle of flexible horn wrapped in the dark, buzzing thread of fifty goat-leaps he harvested from the ridges before he came in. The thread hums softly in the dark, leaking tiny, harmless sparks of skipped distance against my stones.

Ten paces away from him—though outside in the valley it is three miles south—is the underside of the twenty-yard wall. 

This is where I open out into the village that isn't there. 

In some ways, I am the village too, or at least the memory that keeps its foundations square when someone looks over the wall from the north and sees only barren gravel. I hold the three-foot gap under the top course of slate shut until a warm, frantic shoulder presses against it, and then I part the stones like wet curtains and let the dying thing tumble through into the quiet grit. 

I like it when they bring a climber in. A newcomer brings a terrible, ragged music into the village—the wet flutter of a panicked heart, the scrape of chapped skin, the thin, high whistle of air in narrow windpipes. For a few days, while the Listeners sit around them passing the heavy leather flask and greasing their tattered nylon or wool with black puddles of rock-shadow, I get to feel what motion was like before the erosion finished. The newcomer shivers, and the shiver travels down into my foundations, a tiny, rapid drumbeat against the settled stone. 

Slowly, over weeks that have no sun to count them, I feel the climber soften. The frantic noise in their chest lengthens, flattens, drops three octaves. Their bones drink the silence until the marrow thickens; their eyes cloud over with milk so they can stop seeing the treacherous light and start seeing the gold and white ribbons outside. When they finally crawl back out under the wall to take their first three-hour walk across the bell-zones, they are not a thirteenth. They have simply replaced one of the twelve who crawled into my deepest, dampest corner centuries ago and softened so completely into the grit that their tallow became indistinguishable from the mud, and their whalebone ribs became another course of stone in my wall.

Never more, never fewer. I digest the difference.

Sometimes, when all twelve are outside—wading through the suspended curtains of rain to swallow the falling drops, or scraping scorched sky-rime off pieces of slate—I am left alone with the amphibians. 

One of those pale, forearm-long things will swim out of a tarn, scull lazily through the thin air three feet above the scree, and drift right through the open doorway of a hut. Because the doorway is always at its tail no matter how far in it swims, it gets confused. It circles slowly in my dark, forty-pace belly, its blind snout bumping gently against my dripping slate, gills flaring in the dead quiet, looking for the phantom tributary that used to run through here before the mountains stopped moving. 

I cannot lift a hand to guide it out. I have no hands. 

So I do the only thing a broken piece of geometry can do: I shift the angle of the floor by a fraction of a degree. I let the western doorway drift three inches closer to its tail than the eastern one. The draft changes—just a hair's breadth of cold, damp pressure—and the blind thing feels it, turns its pale body in the gloom, and swims backward out of the hut, emerging four miles away from where it entered, right above a fresh tarn.

That is my work here. The Wrights tend the leaks in the valley; I tend the Wrights. I am the dark pocket where they put their miles when they are too tired to walk them, and the quiet room where they put their bodies when they are too tired to be stone.
