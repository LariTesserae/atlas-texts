My right cheek is colder than my left. That is the entire shape of my world. 

The right side of my face is pressed flat against the waxed-slate flank of the High-North Strut, and over forty years, the bone of my zygomatic arch has thickened and flattened to meet it. If you were to pull me away from the timber—which requires untying four separate tension- toggles—you would see a gray, hairless callus curving from my temple down to the corner of my jaw, shaped like the heel of a foot. 

I do not go into the cocoon unless the wind shifts to the south and brings the needle-sleets that blind the horn-goggles. Inside the cocoon, the air is too soft, and the braided tendon floor eats the small tremors. Inside, you only feel the big drops, the cannon-shot adjustments that make the grease-lamp swing on its gimbals and spill hot tallow onto your shins. Out here, tucked into a chiseled pocket the size of a butcher’s tub just below the camp’s main structural blister, I feel the *itch* before the scratch.

I am the String-Taster for the Four-Gall Clan. I am fifty-one years old, which means my knees no longer unbend past a right angle, and my great-toes have lost their nails entirely, replaced by thick, grey pads of skin that fit into the flute-grooves of the ironwood like oakum hammered into a hull. I am too stiff to run the flanks when the gale is up. If I were roped into a triad and the point-walker went over the apex, my hips would pop out of their sockets when I threw myself down the opposite slope to catch their weight. 

Instead, I am wedged here, where five beams the thickness of river-barges fuse into a black, steaming knuckle. 

My left hand rests on the Mother-Harp. It is nine strands of twisted glider-gut, each the length of three tall men, stretched across the forty-degree aperture between the High-North Strut and the Under- brace. The wind coming out of the planetary dark hits those nine strings before it hits our shelters. To a young walker with ears still full of blood and gristle, the harp sounds like a dying beast—a continuous, rising and falling *whooooo-eeeee-whooooo* that gets behind your eyes and makes you want to open your mouth and let the cold take your tongue. 

To my left thumb, which rests against the bone bridge where the all nine strings are notched into the living sap-wood, it is not a sound at all. It is a braid of nine distinct tensions. 

The third string from the top—the one cured in black gall-water to keep the frost from fraying it—is currently singing in the key of a split rib. Three miles down-lattice, where the Three-Toed Clan used to hang their hides before the bad winter, a lateral brace is rotting out. It hasn’t dropped yet. It’s just softening. The vascular tubes inside are turning to brown flour, and as it sags by inches, the High-North Strut is taking the orphaned weight. The wood beneath my right cheekbone is compressing. It feels like someone is very slowly driving a wooden wedge between my back molars. 

*Tap. Tap-tap. Slap.*

That is my mallet against the calling-scar—a patch of bare, pale sapwood we keep scraped clean of iron-bark right beside my left knee. I don't hit it hard. You don't need muscle to speak through a tight beam; you need a clean rebound. I let the basalt knuckle lashed to the mallet’s head bounce twice, then deaden it with the heel of my palm. 

*Rot below,* I tell the wood. *Three-Toe side. The North beam is drinking the load. Do not cut steps on the upper flute today. The skin will pop.*

A mile and a half up the slope, where the mist is currently thickening into a grey paste that smells of old snow and bird-lime, my niece Bristle is hanging in her harness, scraping scale-bugs off a secondary junction. Seconds after my mallet drops, I feel her answer through my jawbone—two quick, light *tinks* from the back of her horn-adze. *Heard. Moving to the belly.*

She is a good girl. She has her father’s wide, flat nostrils and she never wastes a stroke when she’s talking on the wood. Her younger brother, Gully, is different; he hits the timber like he’s trying to kill a snake, wasting his arm-strength, sending muddled, splashing vibrations down the grain that could mean *glider-flock* or *my boot-thong broke* or nothing at all. He still thinks the wood is dead matter to be climbed on. He hasn't lived long enough to realize we are fleas in the beard of a man who is still growing.

Below me, seventy feet down the vertical face of the Under-brace, the fog suddenly rips open. 

A downdraft—one of the heavy, cold ones that drop straight out of the purple roof without warning—punches a hole in the cloud-bank. For ten heartbeats, the abyss opens up like a split gut. 

I look down between my stirruped heels. Half a mile below, two great spans cross each other like dropped needles. Something white is draped over the intersection. From here, it looks like a smear of grease on a knife-blade, but I know what it is. It’s the shed skin of a Choke-Shoot that missed its mark three seasons ago, dried out by the wind and colonized by pale, blind timber-crabs. Beneath that, another mile down, there is a faint, rhythmic flashing—pale-bellied gliders banking in a thermal, turning their undersides to what little indigo gloom filters down from the sky, looking like a handful of bone-chips tossed into a well. 

And below them: nothing. Just the blue-black soup that goes down until the air gets so thick and hot it would boil the grease in our lamps without a spark. My grandfather’s grandfather had a story that if you dropped an iron adze-head from here, it would never hit the bottom; the air down in the belly of the world is as dense as cold lard, and the iron would eventually just float on it, suspended in the poison dark alongside all the dead things that have slipped their ropes since the beginning of time.

I spit over my shoulder, watching the gobbet of brown resin-spittle curve sideways before it’s fallen six feet, torn into mist by the gale. 

Then the third string on the harp goes dead. 

It doesn't snap. It just loses its bite under my thumb, going as slack and greasy as an untied boot-lace. At the same exact instant, the wood against my right cheek *stops humming*.

A Rigger who hasn't spent twenty years in the listening-pocket thinks silence is peace. If the wind drops for a second, or the beam stops groaning, the young ones let their shoulders drop in their slings. They sigh. 

When the wood goes quiet against my teeth, my scrotum draws up into my belly and my toes curl so hard in their horn thimbles that the cramps shoot up my shins. 

Wood only goes quiet when it’s between weights. It’s the breath intake before the scream. 

Three miles down, the rotten lateral has finally sheared away from its node. Millions of tons of ironwood that were leaning against it are now hanging on nothing, waiting for the elasticity of the sap-veins to catch up with gravity. 

I don't use the mallet. The mallet is for news. 

I reach down to my belt, pull the heavy bone-cleaver we use for splitting glider-skulls, and I hack the fifth string of the Mother-Harp in half. 

The gut parts with a sound like a dry branch snapping in a small room—*CRACK*—and the sudden release of tension yanks the wooden sounding-board glued to the cocoon’s entrance-ring thirty feet behind me. Inside the greased hide, the alarm-rattle—a string of dried scale-shells and knuckle-bones—Explodes into a clattering roar. 

*SLACK!* I whistle through my front teeth, a two-note shriek that cuts the wind like a wire. *SLACK ON THE NORTH! DROP-LOAD!*

I don't look to see if they’re coming out. If they come out now, they’re dead anyway; you don't want to be on the bark when the bark decides to change its mind. Inside the cocoon, they will be diving for the center-post, hooking their belt-toggles into the twenty-ply gut that holds the Drop-Stone, pulling their knees to their chins, becoming small, round, and soft while the trampoline floor goes loose beneath them.

Me, I am too far from the sphincter-valve. It would take me forty seconds to un-toggle my knees, haul myself up the thirty-degree flute against the frost-slick, and grease my hips through the hole. 

I have about four seconds. 

I don't climb up. I kick my wooden wedges loose from the sitting-pocket and throw myself off the beam into the empty air. 

The wind takes me before the gravity does. It scoops me up like a dry leaf, slamming my back against the underside of the node before my forty-foot tether of braided intestine even pulls taut. I hang there in the black, spinning like a spider on a thread of spit, ten feet clear of the timber, tucked into the triangle of dead air beneath the great cistern-gall. Above me, the gall’s leathery, bark-covered belly is hot; I can smell the rank, pickled stench of ten thousand gallons of bitter water sloshing inside it, warmed by the tree’s feverish sap-blood.

Then the load arrives. 

It doesn't come from below; it comes from everywhere at once. A three-mile length of the High-North Strut drops twelve feet in half a heartbeat to find its new equilibrium. 

The sound is not something you hear with your ears. It is a physical blow that hits the chest like a falling timber, knocking the thin air out of my lungs in a pink spray of bitten-tongue blood. Along the entire visible length of the span, the tension-change causes the outer layer of cast-iron bark to shed. Plates of black wood the size of hut roofs explode outward from the flutes with the roar of a rockslide, spinning away into the fog-banks, slicing through five-inch climbing ropes like wet twine. 

A shower of freezing, yellow sap-icicles, shaken loose from the upper struts, rains down past my goggles, chiming like glass knives as they strike the beam above and shatter into sweet-smelling dust. 

My tether stretches. Glider-gut is good; when it’s cured in urine and greased with yellow scale-oil, it will stretch a third of its length before it remembers it’s dead meat and tears. I drop twenty feet, my stomach staying behind in my throat, before the cord hits its limit and bounces me back up toward the weeping, steaming underside of the cistern-gall.

For five minutes, the world is nothing but the *boom-whir-boom* of the continent settling into its new shape. Far out in the dark, a secondary span that couldn't take the new angle gives way entirely. I don't see it go, I just hear the long, tearing *shhh-kreeeeee* of twenty-foot fibers pulling out of their sockets like green broom-straws, followed by a wind-gust displaced by something the size of a village plummeting three miles into the soup. 

A flock of gliders, shaken off their roosts on a lower sap-boil, comes boiling up through the mist right beneath my dangling feet. One of them clips my left ankle with its wing- spur—a dull, heavy thump against my hide mitten—and I look down into a pair of yellow, saucer-wide eyes set above a beak crusted with dried sugar-scabs before the beast folds its leather and drops back into the dark. 

Then the High-North Strut stops shuddering. 

The new tension settles in. I can tell before I even haul myself back up to the stone-cold bark, because the remaining eight strings of the Mother-Harp above me have found a new note. 

It is higher than before. Almost a keen. The beam has dropped twelve feet, yes, which means the angle at the node is sharper now, the wood pulled tighter than a drumhead over a starved dog’s ribs. It will be hard chopping for the young ones tomorrow; when they take their horn-adzes to this strut to cut new stair-notches, the chips won't come out in neat wedges. They’ll fly off the blade like slung stones, humming with stored anger. 

I grab my tether hand-over-hand, using my hooked, yellow-plated fingers like wooden pegs, not bothering with my knees until my chest is back over the newly stripped, pale-grey lip of the flute. 

My sitting-pocket is gone. When the bark-plate blew off the flank, it took my chiseled tub with it, along with my spare horn-goggles and the grease-pot I use to keep my heels from cracking. They are two miles down by now, sinking slowly into the hot, heavy air at the bottom of the world. 

My mallet is still at my belt, though. The stone knuckle is still lashed tight with sinew. 

Before I even check to see if the cocoon’s anchor-lines held—I can already hear my cousin’s wife cursing through the hide valve in a low, chest-deep rumble, which means the water-jar probably tipped over onto the bedding-slings—I pull the mallet free. 

I press my right cheek against the newly exposed sapwood. It is warm and wet, sticky with the clear, turpentine-smelling blood of the tree that hasn't yet frozen into rime. It tastes like iron and burnt sugar. 

I wait until my breath stops whistling in my nose, listening with my jawbone to the three-mile string of timber stretching out into the indigo dark. Far away, up in the mist, I feel two tiny, sharp *tinks* come shivering down the grain. 

*Bristle,* the wood says against my teeth. *Still holding.*

I raise the stone knuckle and hit the wet scar twice, hard enough to splash warm sap across my goggles, sending my voice out along the tight, screaming wire of the world. 

*We dropped,* I tell the clan. *We held. Bring your chisels.*
