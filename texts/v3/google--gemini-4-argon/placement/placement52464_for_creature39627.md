My heels are settled into the twin concavities worn into the cast-iron floorplate by the three women who held this post before me. The lead of my boots has conformed so completely to the depressions that to lift my foot would require a deliberate, peeling effort—a slow unsealing of metal from metal. I have not lifted either foot since the third hour of the watch.

Before me rises the great curved flank of the Number Four Western Return, a pipe eleven feet in diameter, riveted from half-inch copper plate that has long since wept itself green and black. Behind me, the basalt wall is unplastered, its vertical striations glowing with that dull, sourceless cherry-red that you do not see with the center of your eye, only with the edges. The space between the rock and the copper is forty inches wide. It is my whole house during the waking shift, and it smells of hot tallow, wet slate, and the faint, sweetish rot of sulfur bacteria baking on the vents below.

I am an Ear—a *Pipe-Listener* for the Third Tier Manifold Guild.

My left gauntlet is unlatched and hangs from its gutta-percha cuff by a brass chain, letting my bare hand rest flat against the warm, greasy cheek of the Return. My fingers are spread wide. The skin of my palm is calloused to the thickness of horn, stained the deep, permanent peacock-blue of copper-water, and greased with grey ozokerite so the ninety-eight-degree metal does not blister me. Through my palm, the city arrives.

To an unseasoned low-lander, the pipe would feel dead—just a monumental curve of hot metal sweating mineral oil in the dark. Even to a common Well-Tender, it has only two or three voices: the low, bowel-like rumble of the brine-slurry shifting its weight four hundred yards down-grade, and the sharp, bright *tink-tink-tink* of the tuning-irons as the guild-clerks pass their tallies from the eastern smelters.

Right now, two clerks are arguing in the Seventh Sub-Cellar. The clicks come through the soles of my boots and into the bones of my shins before they reach my hand: *three short, one long, a pause like a held breath, three short.* Someone is disputing the specific gravity of a tellurium batch. The rhythm is slightly too brisk—the sender’s brass thimble is striking the iron at nearly forty beats a minute. A young man, probably. His carapace will be hot inside; his charcoal filter will be choked with his own grease by mid-shift. Without moving my head, my right thumb twitches upward by a quarter-inch, miming the gesture across the brow: *You are wasting yourself.*

Beneath the brisk chatter of the clerks, deeper in the frequency of the metal, is the real work.

I do not listen to the words; I listen to the marriage between the iron and the salt.

The Number Four Return carries spent mother-liquor from the high potassium-settlers back down toward the magma-boilers to be re-concentrated. As it travels, it cools by three degrees over two miles. Three degrees is enough. In the dark inside the pipe, the liquor wants to let go of its burden. It wants to lay down a velvet lining of alum and gypsum against the rivets.

I close my eyes—not a blink, which is a fast, fluttering waste, but a slow lowering of the lids that takes five seconds to complete and will last for twenty minutes. In the dark behind my eyes, the vibrations sort themselves out by weight.

There is the six-hour piston in the Sump, half a mile straight down. It is currently at the top of its stroke. I know this because the sub-audible hum in my breastbone has flattened out, becoming wide and hollow as the great counterweights take the strain off the steam-chests. For twelve minutes, the city holds its breath at the top of that swing. The water in the tiny tin gutter above my head stops dripping every four seconds and drips every six.

And there, just under my ring finger, four feet upstream from my stance: a roughness.

It is not a sound. It is a tiny, rhythmic *drag* in the boundary layer of the brine. A rivet head on the interior seam has caught a seed-crystal of potassium sulfate. Over the last three weeks, that seed has grown into a knuckle, and now the knuckle is combing the thick water as it creeps past, throwing a minuscule eddy against the copper skin. Left alone, by next season that eddy will slacken the flow behind it, inviting more salt to settle, until the pipe grows a stone tumor the size of a hogshead and the pressure backs up all the way to the cliff-face fractionators.

I do not reach for my scraper. That isn't my trade.

Slowly, taking a full minute to bring my right arm up against the resistance of my stiff leather elbow-joints and the warm, shifting weight of the cooling-water in my chest-coils, I draw my tuning-iron from its forearm slot.

I do not use the brass thimble. That is for speech, for the bright, thin surface of the metal. Instead, I turn the octagonal steel rod around and press its blunt, rounded butt-end directly against the copper, right over the spot where the internal eddy is tugging at my ring finger. Then I lean my chest against the butt of the rod.

I let fifty pounds of my suited weight settle onto that single point.

My personal condenser hums softly between my shoulder blades, a tiny convection-tick as my body heat rises by half a degree from the effort. A bead of distilled, charcoal-flat water forms at the lip of my chin-tube; I take it on my tongue without swallowing, letting it wet my mouth.

Through the steel rod, I am pinning the vibration. I am changing the resonance of that three-inch patch of copper plate, damping the tiny harmonic tremor that the crystal needs to lock its lattice onto the metal. I will lean here, an immovable lump of brass, tallow, and lead, for the next two hours. Under the steady, deadening pressure of my breastbone, the microscopic vibration will flatten; the eddy will lose its purchase; the hot, heavy brine will slowly lick the salt-knuckle away from the rivet and carry it down into the roaring dark of the Sump.

Across the alley, four feet from my shoulder, a *vitrifex* is clinging to the brickwork of a sulfur-flue. It was there when I relieved the previous Ear five hours ago. Since then, it has rotated its flat, slag-grey body by the width of two fingers. One of its silicate legs is raised slightly, poised over a bloom of yellow crust like a hand over a harp, waiting for its own internal clock to tell it to take the next bite.

Mycrust—the one I am growing on my left pauldron, the side that faces the gutter-drip—receives a single, warm drop of water from the tin lip overhead. *Plip.*

I do not look at it, because my neck-ring is sealed with grey litharge and turning my head would crack the putty, but I know the shape of it as well as I know my own tongue. I have trailed three thin lines of mutton-tallow down the brass from the shoulder-rivet to the armpit-gusset, forming two narrow channels between them where the bare metal is etched with vinegar. The drop will catch in the left-hand channel today. It will leave behind a millionth of an inch of pale green brochantite before the ambient heat steals the moisture back into the linen sky.

In ten years, when I am made First Listener of the Western Flank, that ridge will be as thick as my thumb, braided like a rope of green glass, and every clerk who passes me in the cistern-halls will know by the angle of the crystals that I stood my watches in the forty-inch cleft behind Number Four, leaning into the copper at a twelve-degree list, holding the mountain’s throat open with my chest.
