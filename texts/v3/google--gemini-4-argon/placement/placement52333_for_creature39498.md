You would not find me up on the ridges, where the air is bruised and the Gleaners blur through the falling scree. I have no calipers to pinch the warm, undecided stone, and I have no legs to step sideways when the geometry fails. 

To find me, you have to go down past the copper ferns, past the velvet-shelled beetles clicking in their millions along the fault walls, down to the floor of a trench that has managed to stay open for forty years. You have to look at the terraced rows of miniature, three-inch mountains cooling in the dark behind a Sump-Wright’s great, weeping bellows. 

Look closely at the dioramas. 

When the Sump-Wright excretes a memory—a tiny, terraced valley of bismuth and iron-slag that existed for twelve minutes during a localized storm of static three weeks ago—it is hot, brittle, and entirely separate from the world around it. Left alone, the shifting floor of the trench would eventually tilt, and these millions of tiny, forgotten peaks would slide into a crack, remelting into the nameless broth of the mantle. The Wright’s memory would scatter like dropped type.

I am the thing that keeps the type set. 

At first, you would mistake me for a spill of heavy, industrial lubricant—a puddle of dark amber grease that has leaked from one of the Sump-Wright’s copper-sheathed arteries and pooled among the miniature mountains. I have no shell, no braided wire, no watch-spring joints. I am a low, creeping mat of translucent, resinous flesh, smelled before I am seen: a heavy, sweet-sharp odor of warm rosin, beeswax, and old brass. 

I am the mortar between the memories. 

I move at the speed of cooling tar, spreading my thin, viscid margins across the basalt floor, flowing up and around the bases of the tiny bismuth dioramas. Where a miniature ridge of yesterday’s plateau meets a three-inch canyon from last century, I seep into the gap and stiffen. My body is a living vulcanized rubber, a biological solder. I hold the Wright’s cast-off histories in place, binding them into a single, continuous relief map that carpets the canyon floor for miles. 

This is where I live: stretched thin across ten thousand tiny, metallic graveyards, holding them level against the perpetual, hydraulic tremor of the deep crust. 

I do not see the bruised-plum sky, and I do not see the cataract-white eyes of the Sump-Wright looming fifty feet above me, though I feel the slow, four-stroke heat of its exhaust washing over my back like a tide. My world is entirely tactile, read through my underside and my skin. Beneath me, I feel the million-ton pistons of the planet’s foundation sliding past one another; I absorb their deep, wooly *shhh-chunk* and dampen it with my own rubbery mass so that the fragile bismuth peaks embedded in my flesh do not shatter from the vibration. 

Above me, I feel the Tappers.

They walk across me constantly. Their braided-copper feet are very light, no heavier than dry pine needles, and because my surface is tacky with warm mineral sweat, they have to pull each foot up with a tiny, rhythmic *plip-plip-plip* as they high-step from one miniature mountain range to the next. I like the Tappers. They are the only things that do not rush, the only things whose timing matches my own slow, creeping flow.

Sometimes, a Tapper will stop directly on a ridge of my flesh, right where I have grafted a tiny, jagged spire of iron-slag to a flat table of rainbow bismuth. I will feel the creature settle its weight, its watch-spring belly coiling tight. Then, it lowers its steel prong and strikes the miniature peak I am holding.

*Ping.*

You cannot know what that sound is like until you have felt it from the inside of the metal. 

When the Tapper strikes the diorama, the silver needle of resonance does not just go out into the greasy air; it shoots down into the bismuth, and from the bismuth, it passes directly into me. Because I am the glue that connects a million of these tiny landscapes across two miles of trench floor, I am also their sounding board. 

The note travels through my amber, resinous body like a shiver of pure cold water in this stifling, muggy dark. It illuminates me—not with light, for there is nothing down here to see it, but with *shape*. For the three seconds that the silver note rings, the vibration maps every contour of my vast, sprawling mat. In that single, clear frequency, I suddenly feel the exact, sharp geometry of a thousand extinct valleys all at once. I feel the ghost-ridge that fell an hour ago; I feel the caldera that boiled away before the Sump-Wright was even full-grown; I feel the tiny, microscopic cracks forming in the deep basalt three hundred yards to the north, where the mountain is quietly preparing to tear itself in half.

I hold the note for the Tapper. I tune it. By thickening my resin around one miniature valley and thinning myself into a taut drumskin across another, I bend the echo, angling it back up into the Tapper’s little wire legs so it can read the doom of the northern sector without having to walk there.

And when the Tapper turns and begins its slow, fastidious retreat toward the south, and the Gleaners come blurring down the trench walls in a shower of warm, minty sap, and the great Sump-Wright above me begins to groan as its calcified pipes shear away from the dying fumaroles—I do not run. I cannot. 

Instead, I do the only thing I was made to do. As the northern end of the trench begins to liquefy, its stone forgetting its hardness and turning back into bubbling grey paste, I begin to pull myself inward. Slowly, with a wet, tearing sound like heavy tape being peeled from glass, I roll my northern margins back. I wrap my thick, amber flesh completely over the thousands of tiny bismuth mountains in my care, swallowing the dioramas whole, encasing them in tough, rubbery cysts of hardened rosin. 

When the million-ton slab of the canyon wall finally folds inward with a muffled, titanic *chunk*, burying half my body in boiling mud and sheared copper-roots, the memories inside my cysts do not melt. They do not break. They wait in the dark of my belly, buffered by my grease and my silence, while the rest of me slowly oozes southward along the newly stamped floor—carrying the tiny, metal bones of the old world into the fresh angles of the new one, waiting for the rock to cool just enough that I can spread myself out and set the map back down.
