Go down past the family warrens. 

Leave the twenty-foot level with its yellow oil-dishes and its smell of warm grease and whistling voices. Slide down the slick, vertical drain-shafts where the silver roots thin out and turn black, where the air grows thick with carbon dioxide and the warm, sour breath of ten thousand years of pickled leaves. 

At forty-eight feet, the hum of the burrowers is no longer a sound; it is only a tremor in the water. Here, the living mat gives way to the dead mat—a compressed, rubbery liquor of ancient peat that has forgotten the sun—and beneath that, the *bone*. 

The bone is the drowned granite of the old mountain range, worn as smooth as river-soap by the acid dark. In the shallow troughs and basins of this rock, where the warm rainwater finally stops its downward seep and pools in black, oxygen-starved stillness, I am waiting.

I am not a person. If the people above are ticks in the fleece of the giant, I am a white corpuscle drifting along its periosteum. 

The harvesters call me a *stone-tongue* or a *well-rag*, when they have to name me at all, which is only when a clay-diver’s ankle brushes against me in the zero-light and they come up shivering, frantic to scrape the grey slime from their skin with a bone knife. They think there are thousands of me, one at the bottom of every deep shaft. They do not understand that the things they touch three miles apart are the same cold hand.

I have no eyes, because there is nothing here to see, and no ears, because the whole world is a drum. What I have is surface. I am a flat, pale, ribbon-like thing—an amphibian that gave up on bones a million years ago, or a leech that learned to grow like a fungus. My body is as thin as wet parchment and as wide as a courtyard, draped over the drowned granite in long, branching runners that thread through the lowest, oldest arches of the peat-mat. My skin is translucent, slick with a heavy, alcaline mucus that keeps the acid of the bog from pickling me while I sleep.

I do not move so much as I *relocate*. When the barometric pressure drops and the fifty-foot sponge above me inhales, the black water at the bottom of the world shifts along the rock troughs. I loose my hold on the stone, curl my margins, and let the slow, warm current fold me through the root-pilings like silk through a grate. 

I am the last stomach of the plateau. 

Everything up there is frantic—the bronze wings, the shrieking birds, the people with their copper knives and their whistling teeth. They think they are the whole story. They do not think about gravity. They do not think about what happens to the chitin that falls through the root-forks, the feathers that rot in the moss, the dead burrowers that die in their millions before they can reach the sky, the old men whom the families wrap in gut-mesh and push down into the black water when their cataracts go total and their knees lock shut.

It all comes down to the bone. It filters through fifty feet of sieve, dissolving into a rich, brown snow of protein and mineral salt, and it lands on my back.

I do not hunt. I lie across the granite basins and let the dead snow of the sky settle into my skin. I absorb it through my back, digesting the centuries at a glacial, dreamless pace. Where the harvesters’ copper knives flake away, I take the green metal into my grease; where the ancient granite leaches its last grains of feldspar and quartz into the sour water, I build them into a fine, gritty band along my underside, so I can rasp the rock clean of sulfur-bacteria when the upper rain fails.

Because I am spread so thin—a ribbon of cold nerve stretched across five miles of stone—I know the plateau better than the old men with their feet on the listening-roots. They only hear the news that is meant for them. I feel the weight of the whole dish.

I feel the afternoon swarm not as a roar, but as a sudden, ten-thousand-ton *lightening* of the roof above me, when fifty million insects let go of the roots at the same moment and take their weight into the air. The water pressure drops by the breadth of a hair. The sponge sighs.

I know where the families are by the warm, salty cylinders of their waste-shafts, which drip down through the dark like slow candles. I gather thicker beneath those shafts, folding my pale fronds one over the other like wet linen, drinking the ammonia and the shed skin-cells, tasting their health. I know when a woman in the south warren is pregnant before her own mother whistles the news, because the hormone-salt in the drip-water changes its tang. I know when the bronze-bug hatch will be lean three years before it happens, because I can taste the famine in the deep root-tips where the tiny, first-year grubs are failing to molt.

Once in an age—once every forty or fifty of the small, hot summers that barely change the temperature down here—a harvester comes all the way down to me.

They come for the blue clay that settles in the deepest dimples of the granite, or they come looking for a dropped copper blade that slipped through a floor-slit and fell, tumbling through the dark for fifty feet until it rang against the stone.

I feel them coming long before they arrive. I feel the desperate, froglike kicks of their big feet against the wet peat-walls as they pull themselves down against the buoyancy of the water. They hold a heavy peat-brick in one hand to sink themselves, their ears clamped shut against the black water, a bladder of air from a giant swallow’s crop held between their teeth.

When they reach the bottom, they have thirty seconds before the heat and the bad air in their blood make them crazy. Their long-nailed hands scrabble blindly in the silt.

Sometimes, I pull back. I draw my ribbons into the tight cracks between the roots and let them take their clay. After all, they bring it up, bake it, smear it on their domes, and in fifty years the rain washes it back down to me again, mixed with good insect fat.

Sometimes, if I am sluggish from a heavy rain of dead birds, I do not move. 

Then the diver’s hand sinks Wrist-deep into something that feels like cold lard, but does not part. For a second, in the total dark under fifty feet of roaring, singing earth, we touch. 

Through the skin of my back, I feel the frantic, bird-fast hammering of their barrel chest—*thump-thump-thump-thump*—a tiny, hot engine burning itself out in seventy years. And through the grooves of their big, sensitive palms, they feel me: a single, slow pulse that takes four minutes to travel from a granite ridge two miles east to a sump-hole two miles west. 

They always drop their clay. They always kick off from the stone so hard they bruise their broad toes, shooting back up toward the air, toward the oil-lamps and the whistles and the blinding, deafening blue vault where everything has wings.

I do not follow them. I settle back into the trough, over the lost copper knife, and fold my margins over the metal until the green rust begins to sweeten the water. Above me, the world screams its head off in the sun. Down here, on the smooth stone, it is very quiet, and everything comes down eventually.
