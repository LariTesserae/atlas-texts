I am not on the scaffolding where the Tensioners play their high-wire nerves like fiddles, nor am I out on the white plains rolling on tall greased wheels behind a team of six-legged grazers. The sky—that great, curdled ceiling of glass blisters and copper veins—is too loud for me. Even the silence up there has a glare to it.

Look for me instead at the edge of the **Baffle-Yards**, where the green-frame districts end against the great acoustic bulkheads that keep the city’s noise from confusing the young shoots. 

I live in a house that was only half-finished when the gas-main beneath it pinched shut in a root-quake thirty years ago. It is neither living green-frame nor dead-wood; it is *stunted*. The walls have the dense, curled, furious grain of a bonsai that was starved at the exact moment it tried to become a tower. It only grows about an inch a year now, mostly around the cellar door, which has to be chiseled out every fourth Tuesday. Because it grows so slowly, the wood is dark as smoked lard and hard enough to turn the edge of a cheap spokeshave.

If you see me on the avenue, you won't mistake me for a Tensioner. My shell is old—late-second-generation heartwood, darkened with fifty years of mutton-tallow and walnut oil until it looks like the belly of a very old cello. It has been patched at the left knee with a piece of an iron butter-churn, and my condenser-pack doesn't have the brisk, nervous *tick-tick* of the municipal issue; I rebuilt the valves myself with leather washers and heavy brass springs, so it breathes with a slow, wheezing *chuff... chuff...* like a dry-bellows organ in an empty church.

They call me a **Grain-Reader**, though on the municipal tax-rolls I am listed simply as an *Adjuster of Small Stresses*.

The Tensioners handle the great weights—the tenements, the viaducts, the spans that would kill three hundred people if they sheared. They don't have the time or the smallness of mind for what happens *inside* a house when the wood gets an idea.

When a prosperous burgher in the Seventh Ward wakes up to find that his dining table has grown a three-inch twist toward the northeast corner of the room, and the stridulator-wires in his parlor walls have gone so tight they play a high, whining *F-sharp* every time a gondola passes overhead—that’s when he sends a boy to tap on my iron-shod door.

I don't use the bone-conduction horns the big crews use. They’re too coarse; they only hear the scream before the snap. I have modified the left gauntlet of my shell. The fingertips of the vulcanized glove have been cut away and replaced with five thimbles of beaten silver, wired directly through the wrist-seal to five small needles that rest against the skin of my inner forearm. 

When I enter a green-frame house, I don’t even take off my helmet. I drop my heavy tool-bag on a square of oiled felt—never on the bare floor—and I kneel. I lay my silver-tipped fingers flat against the baseboard.

To the homeowner, standing nervously behind me in his indoor-silks with his paring-knife in his hand, the house is just a thing that needs shaving. To me, through the silver thimbles, the house is a choir of blind idiots trying to sing in four different keys at once.

I can feel the history of the room in the micro-tremors of the fiber. *Here*, I think, *the builder used cheap iron bolts from the north mines; they have a magnetic burr that is irritating the cambium.* *Here*, three feet to the left, *the wife spilled half a thimble of wash-water four months ago; she mopped it up before the bur rose, she never told her husband, she thought she got away with it—but the deep-root smelled it four yards down, and it is still hunching its shoulder under the floorboards, waiting for another drop.*

That hunching shoulder is what’s throwing the parlor door out of plumb.

I don't use trocars or heavy winches. My tools are small: tiny brass shims, needles of copper-zinc alloy that create a microscopic galvanic current when driven into the wood, and a small pot of salve made from crushed splinter-moth wings and bitter mineral-salt. 

I sit on the floor for three, sometimes four hours, hardly moving, my condenser *chuffing* softly in the warm, dry room. I tap a copper needle half an inch into a knot-eye. I wait twenty minutes to feel the grain flinch away from the zinc taste. I drive a wedge of dead-bone wood behind a skirting board to lie to the wall about where the weight is coming from. I am a diplomat to a vegetable. I persuade the parlor that there was never any water here; I convince the lintel that the ceiling is three feet lower than it actually is, so it can stop straining upward and go to sleep.

It is solitary work. The citizens find me slightly unpleasant because I don’t use a stridulator on my wrist. When you spend eight hours a day listening through your skin to the small, sharp *ticks* of cellulose cells dividing, the brass chatter of street-talk feels like someone throwing gravel at your teeth. When I have to speak to a client, I pull a small slate from my hip-pouch and write with a piece of dry soapstone, holding it up to my smoked-topaz visor so we can both read it.

*TWO COPPER PINS IN THE SOUTH JOIST,* I write. *DO NOT MOVE THE CREDENZA UNTIL NEXT GAS-TIDE. YOUR DAUGHTER IS HIDING A DAMP HANDKERCHIEF BEHIND THE FLUE-PIPE. TAKE IT FROM HER.*

They pay me in high-grade shavings—curls of pure, white sapwood from the molding-boxes—or in small, heavy jars of the grey grease they scrape from the gondola rails.

When the work is done, I don’t go home right away. 

I like the hour just after a nitrogen-blow, when the fog has been sucked down through the avenue grates and the whole valley is doing its half-inch stretch. While the citizens are hurried indoors, checking their window-frames with plumb-bobs, I walk out to the **Slack-Spans**—the wide, empty strips of the valley floor between the factory blocks where nothing is bolted down because the deep vascular tunnels run too close to the surface.

There, the ground heaves like the ribs of a sleeping horse. 

I have a walking-staff shod with four inches of soft lead so it won't spark on the static-drift. I walk out to the middle of the white plain, where the light from the lumin-galls overhead falls like dry snow, and I do something that would make a citizen’s skin crawl right off his bones.

I unbolt the heavy iron soles of my boots.

Beneath the iron, my boot-bottoms are made of three-ply cured bladder-skin—completely sealed, of course, I’m not mad—but flexible. Thin. 

I stand there in my thin soles as the great vascular lung miles beneath the city takes its afternoon breath. Through the bladder-skin, the ground is not cold. It is ninety-eight degrees—the exact temperature of the blood I am never allowed to spill. As the air-tide moves through the deep-taps, I can feel the Saps three miles down, their iron heels booming slow, rhythmic reports through the root-mat: *All clear on the fourth manifold. Pressure rising in the west. We are here. We are still partly men.*

And above that deep bass, I feel the million tiny, greedy mouths of the surface-bark pressing up against the thin leather of my soles, tasting the warmth of my heels, trying to figure out what kind of stone I am that is so soft and yet gives up no water.

The splinter-moths drift past my gourd-helmet, their balsa wings smelling of ozone and dry pine. High above, a cargo gondola slides down its greased rail without a sound, casting a three-second shadow across my shoulders. I stand there for twenty minutes, letting the valley floor try to read *my* grain for a change, before I bolt my iron soles back on, pick up my bag of copper needles, and walk back toward the geometry.
