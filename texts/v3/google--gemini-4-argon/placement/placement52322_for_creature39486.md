I would not be in the water, and I would not be entirely in the air. I would live in the meniscus—in that volatile, six-inch band of obsidian and diorite where the alpine wind shears the top of the brine into a stinging mist, and where the water’s edge rises and falls by millimeters as the great *valve-sponges* down in the deep coordinate their exhaust strokes.

Down in the lens, the problem is seizure: metal wanting to marry metal in the dark, greasy cold. Up here on the collar, the problem is *crust*. 

Where the heavy, mineral-choked water meets the dry mountain gale, it evaporates with ferocious speed. If left to itself, the rim of the basin would grow an overhanging shelf of precipitated sulfates, native copper dendritic crystals, and bitter white vitriol within a single season. The shelf would roof the canyon over. It would shut out the blinding alpine sun, choke the surface exchange of gases, and drop great, jagged bergs of salt-slag down into the *flywheel-medusae*, jamming their rotors and smashing the *Sump-Loom’s* crystal ears.

So I am the thing that keeps the window clean. I am the *Spall-Skater*, though I do not skate so much as I score and snap.

At first glance, against the salt-and-pepper diorite, I look like a dropped set of fireplace tongs or a surveyor’s pantograph that has rusted into the shape of a crab. My body is small—no heavier than a cobbler’s last—and held high clear of the stone on six long, stilt-like legs of laminated horn and spring-steel. Each leg ends not in a foot, nor a wheel, but in a single, rotatable glazier’s diamond—a tiny, octahedral crystal of black carbonado harvested from the deepest metamorphic seams of the pass and set into a knuckle of brazed silver.

I am an creature of tension and sudden release, the only fast thing in a world of low-gear torque.

Because I live half in the wind, I do not have the luxury of the water’s slow, supportive density, nor can I rely on the *Lard-Eels* to grease my joints—they will not come within three feet of the surface, where the ultraviolet glare would polymerize their tallow skin into brittle yellow varnish. If I used sliding bearings or gear-trains, the salt-spray would freeze my joints solid in an hour. 

Instead, I am built entirely of leaf-springs and flexure hinges. My carapace is a single, arched bow of phosphor-bronze, beaten paper-thin and tempered until it sings when the wind hits it. I have no lungs and no gills; along my flanks are banks of tiny, whistling Venturi tubes that catch the forty-knot mountain gale and use the pressure drop to suck dissolved oxygen straight out of the brine filming my belly.

My life is a meticulous, jittering patrol along the razor-edge of the water. 

Step, *scritch*. Step, *scritch*. 

As I walk the sheer obsidian wall just at the splash-line, my diamond toes drag across the newly formed rime of copper sulfate and gypsum, scoring a neat, cross-hatched grid into the crust. I am cocking myself with every step. The rhythmic, four-minute swell of the water below pushes against a broad, flat paddle of horn suspended beneath my sternum. For two hundred and forty seconds, as the *valve-sponges* inhale, the water drops a fraction of an inch, and my bronze back-spring bends, storing the weight of my own body and the drag of the wind, clicking past a series of bone pawls—*tink, tink, tink*.

When the sponges slam their check-valves shut—*shhhhhh-CLANK*—the shockwave hits the surface.

That acoustic spike is my trigger. It trips the sear in my thorax. My arched bronze back snaps flat with the dry *whack* of a mouse-trap, driving a chisel-tipped punch of hardened tungsten straight down between my front legs into the scored salt-crust. 

A flake of mineral rime the size of a dinner plate fractures cleanly along the diamond-scored fault lines, parts from the obsidian wall with a sharp *ping*, and slides down into the forty-foot lens below. 

I do not eat the salt. I eat what the salt trapped. In the microseconds before the brine evaporates on the rock, it catches the mountain’s aerial drift—blown pollen from timberline miles below, wind-born spiders, the dry, freeze-dried husks of high-altitude moths, and fine dust rich in phosphorus. The evaporating crust glazes them over like insects in amber. As I shatter the glaze, my mouthparts—two counter-rotating brushes of fine brass wire—whir for half a second, sweeping the freed organic motes and a slurry of fresh brine into my gullet before the big slab of pure mineral slag slips away into the deep.

To the creatures below, I am not an animal; I am weather. 

They see me—if anything down there could see—only as a silhouette through the underside of the mirror: a spider-legged geometry of black lines standing on the ceiling of their world, surrounded by concentric rings of capillary waves where my six diamond feet dimple the surface tension. Every four minutes, on the exact beat of the sponges' exhaust, a bright, flat sheet of crystallized sunlight detaches from the rim and goes fluttering down through the crowded bells of the *flywheel-medusae*, spinning like a dropped coin, shedding a trail of concentrated sulfates back into the solution until it crashes into the bottom trenches for the *Sump-Loom* to smelt.

Sometimes, in the dead of the high-country winter, the wind drops and the sky goes the color of gunmetal. Then the surface threatens to freeze. Ice is the great enemy—worse than salt, worse than the cold-weld. If the lens caps over with ice, the light dies and the pressure of the expanding crystal will crush the upper tiers of *piston-clams* against the diorite.

When the first needles of frazil ice begin to knit across the meniscus, my metabolism changes. The *Sump-Loom* forty feet below hears the change in the surface acoustics—the sharp, high-pitched chimes of the wind-chop suddenly dampening into a dull, slushy slur. The Loom cannot reach the surface, so it sends a messenger. It releases a single, walnut-sized bubble of pure, compressed methane and hydrogen sulfide from its deepest furnace.

The bubble wobbles upward through the forty-foot column of machinery, dodging the spinning rotors of the medusae, painted blue by jumping arcs of static, until it breaks right beneath my belly.

My Venturi tubes drink the rich, foul gas. It hits my internal catalytic sponge—a thumb-sized organ of spongy platinum I carry in my thorax—and ignites without a flame. Within minutes, my phosphor-bronze carapace grows too hot to touch. The water filming my stilts hisses into steam. Along the entire perimeter of the cathedral nave, three hundred of my kind begin to glow a dull, cherry red in the alpine dark. We march shoulder-to-shoulder around the rim, our heated tungsten punches firing twice a minute now, shattering the young ice at the shoreline, dropping sizzling, steaming plates of slush down into the face of the reef, keeping the eye of the foundry open to the stars until the sun comes back over the pass.
