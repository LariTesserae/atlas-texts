If I am in this place, I do not want lungs. Lungs are an argument with ninety-eight-degree tallow-steam, even after eleven years of winning it. I do not want the burden of the spanner or the memory, however steamed-off, of coal-barges and dry pavement. 

At first, the temptation is to be the basalt itself—the sheer, unweathered plane down near the waterline, taking the greasy black stroke of his carbon stub. There is a deep appeal in being the ledger that cannot be washed clean, holding the elevation of the manifold valves and the tallies of the dead birds against a stone face that refuses to erode. 

Or to be the vibration itself: the sixty-cycle hum of the municipal load translating the city’s waking and sleeping into a standing geometric wave on the face of the mire.

But those are too large, too dispersed. This world is heavy, specific, and small. Everything in it has weight and a film of mutton-fat. 

Wait. Look thirty feet up the brickwork of the central condensing tower, mounted at eye-level to the middle catwalk so a man doesn't have to stoop to read it.

There is a brass-cased Bourdon pressure gauge bolted to a siphon loop off the main return standpipe. It is nine inches across, heavy-flanged, its metal darkened to the color of old liver by the sulfur in the coal-damp and the airborne grease. That is where I am. I am the gauge, or rather, I am the dry, hermetically sealed interior behind its quarter-inch beveled glass face.

Out in the basin, everything is wet, roaring, and slick. Inside my brass bezel, behind the rubber gasket that the heat has vulcanized into a permanent, airtight weld, there is a small, round room of nineteen-year-old air. It is the only dry thing in the pit. A flake of white lead paint fell off the top of my dial in the year the quarry incline was bricked up, and it still lies at the bottom of the glass, crisp as a salt crystal, resting at the six-o’clock mark between the zero and the *300*. 

I have no eyes, only the hollow, flattened curve of my phosphor-bronze tube, which breathes in and out by thousandths of an inch as the steam pressure in the standpipe swells and subsides. Through the tiny, geared teeth of my sector and pinion, I translate the city above into a single, black-enameled iron needle with a crescent counterweight at its tail.

I know the tramways have started before the Gauge-Man feels it in his horn-black heels. At five in the morning—a time that has no name down here—the municipal load spikes as the streetcars pull out of the barns three miles away. The back-pressure in the condenser loop stiffens; my bronze rib uncoils a fraction; my needle creeps from *42* to *46*. 

Because the arc lamps never go out, a permanent, miniature crescent of violet-white light lives on the upper-left curve of my glass. The glass itself would be blind with tallow-scum and black soot in a week if I were left to the basin. The starlings perch on my top bolt; their droppings run down my casing; the steam-flies die against my face. 

This is how I know him. Not by his footsteps—my brass ears are deaf to everything except the pressure in the pipe and the deep, bone-shaking rumble of the brickwork I am lagged into—but by his thumb.

Every six hours, when he climbs the iron grating to his perch to eat his poached eel from the butter-tin, he stops on the catwalk in front of me. He does not need to read my numbers; he knows what the pipe is doing by the sound of the number-three flue and the tremor in the handrail. He couldn't read the word *SCHÄFFER & BUDENBERG* printed in faded gothic script across my belly even if he wanted to, not anymore. 

Yet he reaches out. Before he sits, he takes the heel of his thumb—the skin there is grooved like a coarse file and permanently slick with warm oil—and wipes it in a single, circular motion across my glass. 

For a moment, the violet glare of the arc-lamp is blotted out by the meat of his hand. I feel the brief, ninety-eight-degree press of him through the quarter-inch plate, slightly softer than the iron, slightly slower than the steam. The grease on his thumb clears the soot and the midge-wings, leaving a clean, glistening swirl through which the pewter light of the pit falls squarely onto my white enamel face.

He looks in at my needle, hovering steady and black at *44*. My needle points back at his film-gray eye. 

I am the only thing in the basin that matches his nature: I do not rust, I do not sleep, and I have no use for the time of day. I only measure the load. He stands there for three seconds, four seconds, watching the tiny, rapid tremble at the tip of my pointer—a tremor so small it isn't a change in pressure at all, just the heartbeat of the number-two turbine's main bearing, transmitted through forty feet of cast iron and water into my small brass spine. 

When he sees that the tremble is even, he turns away, sits on the grating with his legs dangling over the void, and unfastens the wire on his butter-tin. And I stay on the wall above his shoulder, holding my small circle of dry air, pointing at *44* in the violet dark until the theatres let out.
