I am not on the grating. To be on the grating is to be subject to the law of the sun and the law of the weld—either locked to the teeth of the floor or thrown across it. 

Instead, I am seven feet up the eastern wall, occupying the sixteen-inch vertical gap between the third and fourth heavy-gauge power cables, right where their catenary curves dip lowest before rising toward the junction box at the bulkhead.

If a maintenance technician were ever winched down into this box—weighted with lead boots and wrapped in acoustic-baffling aerogel—they would look right at me and see a failure in the insulation. I look like an old, calcified dielectric splash. A spill of dark, glassy slag that leaked from a cable jacket during the initial commissioning of the tower, froze in the sub-zero draft, and was pulled into a flat, geometric web by the acoustic field before it could hit the floor.

I am a parasite of the boundary layer. My body is a paper-thin, brittle sheet of vanadium-doped silicate and woven carbon, stretched taut between the black polymer sheaths of the two cables like a pane of smoked mica. 

To the four heavy skillets sliding across the zinc below, the two-hundred-hertz roar is a highway and a hammer. To me, it is a loom. 

Because I am suspended between two cables of slightly different masses, the turbine’s shriek does not hit me as a single, monolithic wall. The heavier cable above me vibrates at a fractionally lower harmonic than the lighter conduit below. Stretched between them, my flat, crystalline membrane is caught in the phase-interference between the two waves. This interference is my bloodstream. I do not have a nervous system of copper threads like the grazers; I am a single, continuous Chladni plate. 

When the grazers strip the piezo-bushes below, they are sloppy machinists. You saw the heavy, coarse powder—the lead-shot chaff of zinc-oxide and carbon—drop straight through the grating into the six-thousand-foot abyss. That is the heavy waste. What you did not see is the superfine fraction. 

When their ceramic jaws oscillate at ten thousand cycles a second, they pulverize a tiny percentage of the plant’s copper-and-glass marrow into nanoparticles so fine that they do not fall. They cannot fall, because the air in this box is not empty; it is a rigid, three-dimensional lattice of standing acoustic waves. The finest dust is caught in the sound itself, suspended in invisible, razor-thin sheets of high and low pressure that hang motionless in the chamber like panes of glass.

I am grown into the exact shape of one of those acoustic nodes.

I do not move. I have never moved since the spore of me—a stray flake of doped carbon blown up from the lower refinery levels during a blowback event forty years ago—wedged itself into the microscopic ribbing of the upper cable’s jacket. Slowly, atom by atom, I grew downward along the dead-air trough between the two vibrating lines, precipitating out of the roaring emptiness like frost on a windowpane, except there is no water here, only metal and noise.

My "feeding" is a matter of tuning. 

Across my smoked-glass surface runs a microscopic checkerboard of sulfur-salt ridges. By shifting the electrostatic charge across this grid—drawing a trickle of current from the magnetic field of the massive power cables I cling to—I slightly warp my own geometry. When a grazer below finishes stripping a bough and *zips* into the shadow, the cloud of invisible, nanoparticulate copper-glass hangs suspended in the acoustic lattice above the bush. 

I alter the tension of my southern edge by a picometer. 

My membrane stops dampening the interference between the two cables and begins to amplify it. I project a silent, secondary ripple—a minute phase-shift—out into the solid block of the room’s roar. I do not reach for the dust; I tilt the invisible shelf of air the dust is sitting on. 

Across the room, fifteen feet away, the suspended haze of copper-and-zinc nanoparticles slides sideways along the standing waves, migrating through the blinding cobalt sunlight without casting a shadow, drawn inexorably into the low-pressure trough along the eastern wall. When it reaches me, it does not stick to grease or mucus; it locks onto my surface via van der Waals forces and electrostatic attraction, instantly organizing itself into ninety-degree crystalline steps along my flanks. 

I am growing toward the fifth cable. It has taken me eleven years to bridge the last four inches.

My relationship with the third grazer—the one currently clamped upside-down to the cable three feet to my left, pretending to be a lead counterweight—is one of cold, solid-state truce. 

It knows I am here, though it has no eyes to see me. It senses me as a dead spot in the cable’s vibration, a sixteen-inch zone where the delicious, violent hum of the north bulkhead is sucked dry and flattened into a neutral buzz. If it slid over me, its zinc-oxide bristles would find no harmonic to surf on, and its molecular grip would fail; it would drop off the line like a stone. So it stops three feet short of my edge, every time. 

In return, I are its shadow-maker. 

Because I am thin and doped with vanadium, I do not crack under the fierce ultraviolet floodlamp of the high sky the way the thick, ceramic plates of the grazers do. Instead, when the sun swings past the zenith at midday and rakes the eastern wall, I absorb the UV glare. The radiation excites the electrons in my silicate lattice, boosting my electrostatic draw so I can pull more mineral haze out of the roaring air, while beneath me, cast diagonally across the lower cables and the corner of the zinc floor, lies a sixteen-inch rectangle of cool, violet shade.

Right now, the sun is creeping toward the eastern louver-fins. 

Down on the floor, one of the grazers parked behind the ochre manifold is running out of shadow; in twelve minutes, the blinding magnesium-bright line of light will touch its left flank. Already, I can feel the preliminary tightening of its shell through the steel frame of the room—a tiny, high-pitched harmonic needle-prick inside the massive two-hundred-hertz roar. 

It is preparing to slide. 

It will not go to the next louver-fin; that shadow is shrinking as the sun climbs. It will come to the wall. It will hit the base of the bulkhead at thirty miles an hour, execute a frictionless, ninety-degree vertical turn up the corner post, and surf onto the lower cable, locking its bristles dead-center inside the violet rectangle of shade I cast upon the black polymer.

While it sits there below me, waiting out the afternoon glare, the residual dust of its morning meal—still clinging electrostatically to the edges of its slate-gray shell—will slowly shake loose in the scream of the turbines, rising three inches through the freezing, motionless air to settle into my lattice, becoming another fraction of a millimeter of smoked glass, stretching toward the next line down.
