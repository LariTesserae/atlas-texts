I am not out in the glare. I have no patience for the sprint. 

If you want to find me, you have to look at the floor of a wide, spoon-shaped basin thirty paces out from a cluster of the floating silver polyhedra—right at the threshold where the node’s refractive shimmer begins to fray into normal, blinding light, and where the silence is not quite absolute, but muffled like a hand pressed over an ear. 

Look for a fault in the travertine, a sluggish little fissure no wider than two fingers, where the limestone has heaved and cracked under the thermal strain of the machine’s waste heat. Look for the seam that stays dark for ten minutes after the rest of the basin has bleached bone-dry. 

At first, I am only a crust of pale, porous mineral wedged into that crack, indistinguishable from the tufa and the dead, calcified rings of old lichen. I have a shell, though it is not entirely my own making; it is a sluggish amalgamation of calcium carbonate precipitated straight out of the warm water, bonded with a rubbery, chitinous glue I secrete when the mists come. I have been cemented into this specific curve of the rock for forty seasons. I do not walk the shelf. Let the tall, chalk-skinned runners and the six-legged hounds burn their livers out racing from puddle to puddle. The water always comes back to the low places. I am a low place.

Wait for the fog to roll up from the drop-offs. 

When the warm, wet breath hits my basin and condenses into a slick sheet of water, my seam splits open. There is no click—the node drinks the small sound of my hinge. Out of the cleft slides a wide, muscular veil of translucent gray mantle, thin as an eyelid, which I spread flat across the stone over a radius of three feet. I am a creature of surface area, a wide, sessile gastropod—or something that converged on the idea of a snail from a very different ancestor—built to work like a living squeegee. 

As the red microbial mat explodes into bloom under the fierce cobalt sky, I am already over it. My underside is a rhythmic, rippling wave of microscopic rasps, scraping the new algae and the waking spores before the spindly grazers can even drift into the basin. I do not chew; I liquefy and draw the warm soup back into the deep, cool pocket of my cleft, where my true body stays wedged three inches down in the dark, wet stone, safe from the ultraviolet burn.

Sometimes, the people come to my basin. 

I know them before they arrive—not by sound, for their horn-padded knuckles make none, and the node would swallow it anyway—but by the sudden, sharp needles of blue and green light that lance across my mantle as they talk to one another across the shelf. I have no eyes with lenses, only a ring of dark, photosensitive pits along the outer fringe of my veil, so I do not see their crouched, gullied shapes. I see them as a constellation of shifting shadows and tiny, violent stars of reflected sun.

When an elder drops into a crouch at the rim of my basin, their neck-frills pulsing a hot, infrared violet that my heat-pits can feel from six feet away, I do not pull back into my crack. Any other small thing on the plateau—the ribbon-amphibians, the blue skittering insects—panics or dies when the pale hands come down to scrape the mat. 

I stay spread. I am why they come to this basin first.

As my mantle digests the raw, slick bacteria, I sweat a thick, clear mucilage through my upper skin to keep the high-altitude glare from evaporating my body's water while I feed. That slime is rich with bound moisture, filtered minerals, and slow sugars that do not turn to steam in the sun. If a hunter pries my shell out of the fissure with a cured bone rib, they get one mouthful of tough, rubbery protein, and then my crack goes dry and barren forever. They learned ten thousand years ago not to break the hinge.

Instead, a broad, calloused thumb with a split-horn pad presses gently at the very edge of my gray veil, right where I meet the cream-colored stone. The hunter drags the side of their hand across my back in a slow, firm sweep, harvesting the thick coat of clear jelly and rolling it straight into a waiting section of hollow bladder-reed. 

The touch is dry and rough, dusted with chalk, and it pulls a tremor through my muscle, a reflex tightening toward my deep fissure. They stop instantly when I flinch. A second later, a warm splash of water—collected by another member of the band from a lesser depression ten yards away—is poured directly over my shell, buying me five more minutes of feeding time before the sun drinks the stone dry. 

It is a quiet, sightless trade conducted in the padded hush of the silver polyhedra. They bring me extra water from the shallow runs; I turn the fleeting red bloom into a stable, gelled ration they can carry across the dead zones. 

When the puddle finally shrinks to my lip and the glare becomes a physical weight pressing against my skin, I draw my veil back in. I fold myself down into the wet dark of the fault line, sealing my seam with a fresh rim of hardened lime and mucus that dries to the exact dead-matte cream of the travertine.

I sit in the dark then, cooling in the stone, feeling the constant, sub-audible hum of the node working in the deep rock below me. Every few years, the air above my crack shimmers harder than usual, and a new, sudden fog settles into my basin when there is no mist-bank on the plateau—a warm, slightly salty condensation that smells faintly of iron and old bone. The red algae blooms twice as thick in that water, and when the band comes to scrape my back after, their thumbs linger a fraction of a second longer against my shell before they vanish back into the light.
