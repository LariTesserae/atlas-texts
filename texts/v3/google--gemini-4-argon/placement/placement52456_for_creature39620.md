I would not be in the water, and I would not be on the paths. The floor belongs to the Turncock and the snails, and the air is too cold and thin to hold a shape for long. 

I would be the forty-seventh prism in the high north curve of the vault, set into the cast-iron frame where the brick arch meets the street-level gutter a hundred feet above.

At first glance, I am only a block of poured flint-glass, six inches thick, flat on the street side and cast into a heavy, downward-pointing tooth beneath. My upper face is scored and frosted by decades of iron-shod cartwheels, horse hooves, and the salt of winter slush. From the street, I am nothing—a dull, greenish cobble that doesn’t take snow quite as fast as the granite around it. 

Down here, I am an eye that has forgotten how to close.

For the first fifty years, I simply dropped the light straight down, as I was poured to do. Whatever passed overhead—the flick of a dray-horse’s shadow, the sudden white glare of an August noon, the amber smear of a hackney’s lantern—fell through me and vanished into the mist. 

Then the city’s weight shifted. The grey-green slate settled half an inch to the east. My iron collar pinched hard against my northern flank, and a single, curved fracture opened inside my glass belly, shaped like the bowl of a spoon. It did not break me through. Instead, the crack drank the lime-water seeping from the mortar until it was lined with a hair-thin film of calcite and pale green copper-salt.

That was when I began to keep things.

Now, I do not drop the light straight down. I bend it. The fracture inside me acts as a second, secret lens. While the other prisms along the vault shed only a vague, pewter twilight that dies twenty feet down in the mist, I gather the dullest scraps of the upper world and press them into a single, bruised-violet beam no thicker than a walking stick. Because the iron frame around me continues to warp—yielding a millimeter every decade to the wet brick—my beam is not stationary. It creeps. 

It takes me seven years to drag that pale violet finger across the width of the lagoon.

I am the chamber’s only calendar, though there is nothing down here that needs to know the year. My light is too weak to warm the water, too diffuse by the time it reaches the bottom to startle the blind eels, whose copper pits cannot read light unless it carries heat. Yet the Sump knows where my finger is pointing. Where my violet spot rests for a season on a sunken causeway, the clear slime on the brick thickens into a pale, milky jelly. The coal-lotus leaves, floating on their rusted chains, will stretch their stems an extra three inches to nudge their plum-colored margins into my glimmer, drinking the ghost of a sun they would die under if they ever saw it whole.

I know the Turncock better than the lamps do. The gas-globes only know him when they are sick and guttering, when he comes to pinch their brass throats. I know him from above. I know the flat, turtle-shell gleam of his leather cap and the slow, silver-grey whorls of the mussels growing in the ditch of his collarbone. 

Once every three or four years, his four-minute rounds and my seven-year sweep intersect on the open flagstones between the pillars. 

When my violet light touches the back of his coat, he stops. Even if the water is rising, even if a distant lamp is hissing blue, he will halt mid-stride, one heavy gutta-percha foot half-lifted in the two-inch black water. He does not look up immediately. It takes him three minutes to register the faint, weightless pressure of the refracted sky against his carapace.

Then, slowly, the petrified brim of his cap tilts back. The wire-fern collar parts, shedding a few drops of black water. His clay-grey face turns upward, and for the span of two turns of the great wheel—eight minutes of the upper world’s frantic time—his large, dark eyes meet the point of my glass tooth a hundred feet above.

Through the milky click of his second eyelids, he does not see the street. My internal fracture has scrambled the shapes of the boots and the wheels overhead into a slow, kaleidoscopic wash: a shifting stain of soot-black, rain-silver, and the occasional, startling flare of a red omnibus wheel passing over my back, which reaches him fifty fathoms down as a brief, soundless blush of rose-madder across his cheeks.

He does not reach for it. He does not wonder what the red is. He simply lets it sit on his face the way the coal-lotus lets it sit on its leaf. 

On my underside, waiting for him, hangs a single drop of water. It has taken forty minutes to gather from the sweating brick around my collar, sliding down my grooved glass flanks until it trembles at my very tip. While he watches, the drop swells, fills with the bruised-violet light of my fracture, and lets go.

It falls for nearly three seconds through the mist, carrying a microscopic speck of street-grit and a tiny, trapped pearl of upper-world daylight, and strikes him squarely in the center of his forehead, just above the bridge of his nose. 

His nictitating membranes sweep sideways—*click*—to clear the splash. His slit nostrils flare once, tasting the faint, foreign trace of horse-manure and coal-smoke that leaked through my cracked seal. Then his chin sinks back into the wire-ferns, his foot finishes the step it began ten minutes ago, and he wades on toward the Regulator, leaving me to drag my pale finger another fraction of an inch toward the eastern wall.
