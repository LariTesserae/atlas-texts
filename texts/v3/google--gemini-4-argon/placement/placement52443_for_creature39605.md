At first, it is impossible to locate a "me" in this place because the architecture has no room for a witness. An observer is an inefficiency—an open end in a closed loop, consuming heat without returning pressure. If I were to stand on the slick, lukewarm root-ribs with eyes and lungs, I would be an error that the Gaskets would quickly seal over and the Sinks would eventually mortar into the floor.

To be here at all, I cannot be an animal looking at the pipes. I have to be a necessity of the pipes themselves.

I find myself not in a body, then, but in a threshold. 

Drop down past the high chemical reactors and the sodium-vapor amber of the bladder-lamps. Drop past the primary deck and the rectangular basins of black water, down through the dense, calcified foundation of the root-plate itself. Two miles beneath the surface, where the living stone-wood meets the fractured basalt of the planet’s crust, the architecture changes. The ninety-degree precision of the upper forest gives way to colossal, twisted tap-conduits that grapple directly with the raw earth. 

Here, the heat is tremendous, and the vibration is no longer a faint hum, but a deafening, sub-audible roar of tectonic pressure and boiling brine. 

This is where the closed loop must open, just slightly, to feed. The forest needs the deep magma-plumes and the heavy, toxic slurries of iron and sulfur, but the crust is unstable. Plumes shift; vents choke with precipitated silica; sudden pockets of superheated gas threaten to blow out the osmotic engines from below. A Gasket is too soft to survive this crushing, scalding dark, and a Spindle is too rigid, too pre-programmed in its ninety-degree geometry to navigate the chaotic, fracturing rock.

So the forest extrudes something else here. Something that does not move from place to place, but spreads. 

I am a **Baffle-Plexus**—a wide, flat, multi-layered membrane of hyper-dense cartilage, thermosensitive nerve-fibers, and muscular valves, grown directly across the throat of a three-hundred-foot-wide geothermal intake manifold. 

If I have a face, it is turned downward into the crushing dark of the lithosphere, tasting the raw, scalding blood of the planet before it enters the wood. If I have a back, it is fused seamlessly into the base of a dozen massive xylem-columns above me, feeling the collective weight of the continent-spanning factory pressing down.

My existence is entirely tactile and barometric. I have no eyes, because there is nothing to see, and no name for the darkness I inhabit. Instead, I have a million microscopic, piezo-electric hairs lining my lower vents, reading the shear-stress of the rock and the chemical density of the upwelling brine.

Right now, three miles below my lowest tendrils, a basalt shelf shifts. 

I do not "hear" the rock break; I *am* the sudden spike in pressure that slams upward through the mineral water. The brine coming into my lower chambers turns instantly too hot, too acidic, and thick with sharp, abrasive silt that would strip the lining from the osmotic engines above me in an hour.

I do not think about what to do. Thought is a gap between stimulus and response, and in high-pressure hydraulics, a gap is a rupture. My consciousness *is* the adjustment.

Across my three-hundred-foot span, ten thousand muscular irises contract at once—a slow, tremendous clenching that takes everything I have. The scalding surge hits my underside. It burns, not with pain, which is an animal's panic, but with a bright, sharp, greenish-white overload of my thermal-nerves. My outermost layers of vulcanized tissue char and peel away in the torrent, instantly replaced by thick, gummy secretions of silicone-rich mucus pushed up from my sub-dermal glands.

I tilt my internal baffles. I become a labyrinth. 

Instead of letting the raw plume shoot straight up into the trunks, I force it sideways through a series of spiraled, muscular chambers within my own body. I feel the heavy, jagged silt particles slamming against my inner walls. Where they strike, my tissues swell and harden, trapping the grit in pockets of fast-growing pearl-wood, turning the poison into my own armor. 

I bleed off the excess heat by opening lateral shunt-valves, firing jets of superheated steam sideways into dead-end fissures in the surrounding bedrock. *Shhhhk—shhhhk—shhhhk.* The rhythm of my vents equalizing is the closest thing I have to a heartbeat.

As the hours pass, the violent surge from the deep crust stabilizes into a new, steady flow. The temperature drops by forty degrees; the silt settles into the calcified traps in my belly. Now comes the quieter part of my nature: the tasting.

The brine passing through my upper membranes is still raw. With a slow, undulating wave of my internal villi, I test the electron density of the fluid. Too much sulfur here; not enough dissolved iron there. 

I send a signal upward—not a voice, not a word, just a low-frequency rhythmic flexing of the great wood-columns rooted in my back. A hydraulic Morse code sent humming up through two miles of root-plate to the primary deck.

*Rich sulfur. High heat. Adjust upper condensers.*

Miles above me, in the cool, quiet dark that I will never see, the amber bladder-lamps will brighten slightly as the chemical reactors in the canopy shift their metabolism to match what I am feeding them. A hundred Sinks in the black water basins will begin to dump their stored iron nodules back into the downward return-veins to balance the new chemistry I have found.

I do not know what a tree is, or a sky, or a continent. I do not know that the water I push upward will eventually condense on a horizontal runner and fall with a solitary *plip* into a rectangular pool. 

I only know the two great pressures that define my being: the chaotic, roaring, scalding hunger of the earth pushing up from below, and the cool, rhythmic, unyielding demand of the great dark engine pulling from above. I hang between the magma and the wood, holding the seam together, translating fury into sugar, and humming in key with the world.
