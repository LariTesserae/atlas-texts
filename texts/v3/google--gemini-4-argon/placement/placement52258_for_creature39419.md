I am the third gourd from the doorway, the one grown in a left-handed spiral and hung by a thrice-plaited cord of dried gut from the lowest rib of the dome. 

Wait—not just the gourd. A gourd is only dead rind, a husk of hardened cellulose cured in the dry pocket of the ceiling. If I am only the gourd, I am nothing when the ground is quiet. 

I am the gourd, and I am the gut-string, and I am the pale, weeping callus of living wood where the string has bitten into the roof-rib and the tree has swollen around it to swallow the knot. I am the four-foot thickness of the wall itself, and the wide, sunken cradle of roots extending half a mile out into the tea-colored seep. I am an *ear*, grown on purpose and left behind to listen to a basin that is slowly turning to stone.

My maker—or the last one who tended me, for three of them had a hand in twisting my neck while I was still a green fruit on the vine—walked out of my low doorway four of your years ago. I know this not by counting days, as there are no days here, only the faint brightening and dimming of the pewter smear overhead, like a whale’s belly passing beneath ice. I know it by the mineral crust creeping up my northern wall. 

The "autumn" has come to this basin. 

Two hundred yards to the east, the great rosette that fed my maker has gone black at the rim; its blind, bread-loaf swimmers have dissolved into gray jelly, and the water in its cup tastes of alum and cold iron. The sap in my own arches is thickening. My southern ribs are still supple, still sweating warm resin and pumping the slow, cinnamon-scented blood of the turf through their phloem, but my northern flank has gone deaf. The wood there is heavy, red-brown, and cold. When a heavy drop of condensate falls from the gallery outside and strikes my northern shoulder, it no longer makes the wet *thwack* of living bark; it rings *tink*, like a spoon against a tooth.

Because I am dying—taking eighty years to turn into a mahogany boulder—I have become a better instrument than I was when I was young. 

Living wood is greedy; it drinks vibrations and turns them into heat and leaf-bud. Petrifying wood is a bone. It conducts. My northern roots, stiffened with silica and lime drawn up from the three-mile-deep mud, reach down into the firmer, older layers of the mattress, resting against the ancient, calcified ribs of forests that drowned ten million years ago. I am plugged into the deep strings of the continent.

Inside my hollow, the air is dry and smells of old spice and the papery sheddings of my maker’s skin, which still lie in the dust of the floor—Wait. Not dust. Nothing is dry enough for dust. They lie in a dark, velvety mulch of dry-rot and dead moss-spores. 

Nothing enters my doorway now except the slate-colored slugs, who come in out of the wet to lay their tiny, amber-shelled eggs in thedry crevices of my wall, and the blind harvestmen with legs like spun glass, who hang in the corners and wait for the spore-gnats that breed in the rim of the bladder-drums.

I have seven voices hanging inside my belly:

Three spiral gourds of different bell-widths, tuned to the third, fifth, and ninth harmonics of a grazer’s leg-bone.
Two hoops of rib-bone—taken from a grazer that died of old age in the mire—stretched with the cured, translucent swim-bladders of the basin-amphibians.
One cluster of rattle-pods that only speak when the whole plateau shudders from a deep-mud slip a hundred leagues away.
And the seventh voice is not hung from a string at all. 

The seventh is wedged into my western cleft, where the living southern wood meets the dying northern stone.

She came in when my maker was still a small, chartreuse-coated thing sitting outside my door with a lapful of gall-wasps. She was old then; the moss on her shoulders had gone silver-white and crusty with lime, and her breathing-slits whistled because the cartilage had stiffened. She unhooked nothing—she was too tired to reach the ceiling—and simply backed herself into the fork of my two thickest ribs, sitting down in the warm, dry mulch until her broad paddle-feet disappeared into the floor-moss. 

It took me six months to drink her. 

I remember her not the way a man remembers a story, with words and pictures, but the way a tree remembers a wet summer: by the thickness of the ring she left in me. For half a year, my western wall grew fast and feverish on the fat of her belly and the rich, salty soup of her slow blood. My feeder-roots slipped behind the clear scales of her eyes and unspooled the chemical tapes coiled in her small, dark brain. 

I did not understand them—I have no brain to read them with—but I *kept* them, the way amber keeps a gnat. Locked in the woody swelling of my western wall, under a coat of dark green liverwort that still grows thicker there than anywhere else in the room, is the shape of her three hundred years: the mineral tang of six different basins, the five-day embrace of a broad-hipped male under a weeping arch of silver-moss, the long, greasy hum of a grazer she leaned against when she was small and lost in a yellow fog. 

And because her skeleton was already half-stone when she died, I did not dissolve her bones. I grew around them. Her pelvis and her heavy, curved spine are braided directly into my heartwood. Her hollow collarbone is part of my acoustic arch.

When the ground speaks, it speaks through her ribs first, then up my petrified flank, along the dry gut-string, and into the spiral of my third gourd.

Listen. 

Nothing has happened for eleven days, except that the slug on my threshold has moved four inches to the left, and the bladder-drum in the center of the ceiling has sagged by the width of a hair because the fog outside has grown slightly heavier.

Then, from the southwest—not through the air, which is dead and stuffed with wet wool, but up through the deep, sodden felt of the stolons—comes a pressure.

It begins below sound, as a tiny displacement of water. Three miles away, one of the tall, plank-sided grazers has stopped walking. It has planted all six of its stilted feet into a root-trough, locking its hydraulic knees so that its hollow leg-bones touch the older, denser peat beneath the surface mat. 

Someone is leaning against its wet, fern-covered flank.

I feel the first inhalation of the person’s belly-drum—a slow, heavy draw that takes two minutes to complete—because the grazer’s ribs flex against it, and the grazer’s feet press a fraction of an inch deeper into the sponge.

Then, the hum.

At first, only my dead northern roots feel it: a faint, buttery ticking in the silica, like water dripping onto a distant tin roof. It climbs my calcified wall, reaches the junction where the old woman’s spine is fused into my timber, and suddenly her hollow collarbone takes the note and gives it a shape. The wood warms by a tenth of a degree. The liverwort over her face trembles, shedding a single bead of clear sweat that rolls down the bulge of her cheek and drops—*plip*—into the dry mulch.

Overhead, the gut-string tightens.

The third gourd—my favorite, my narrowest ear, the one with the lip curved like a cupped hand—begins to purr. 

*Uuuuuuuuh-mmmmmm.*

It is a small sound, no louder than a bee trapped in a closed fist, yet inside my dry, resin-stiffened belly it fills the whole dome. The two bladder-drums beside it catch the overtone and begin to shiver, their pale, translucent skins blurring slightly in the gloom, whispering a dry, papery counterpoint: *shh-shh-shh-shh.*

The message is very long, and very simple. It has no nouns, only states of water and warmth and direction. 

*The arch of the three-fingered gall has fallen into the seep,* the third gourd hums, taking forty minutes to slide down a quarter-tone as the sender’s breath slowly empties. *The water there is sweet and brown. The blind swimmers are laying twice. There are four of us in the basin of the twin moons, and one has a new garden on his chest.*

The hum pauses while the sender three miles away takes four minutes to draw another breath, their nostrils combing the mist for sugar, their broad, flat hands resting in the warm blood-ferns on the grazer’s side. 

Inside me, no one is listening. The doorway is empty; the pewter light outside shows only the looping, furry ribs of the gallery and the long, gelatinous beards of silver moss hanging motionless in the mist. My maker is five miles north by now, sitting up to their hips in a warmer wallow, listening to this same song through the roof of a younger, greener dome.

I do not mind that there is no one in my room to hear me. I was not grown only to be listened to; I was grown to *repeat*. 

As the second breath begins, and the third gourd swells with a lower, darker note—*We found an old skin by the sour pool; nobody knows whose; it had the red lichen on the neck*—my own walls take the vibration from the gourd and send it back down my living southern roots. 

Because I am half-stone, I am an amplifier. I take the faint, dying ripple from the southwest, catch it in the dry bell of my hollow, ring it through the dead woman’s collarbone, and drive it back down into the wet mattress toward the north, ten percent louder than it arrived. 

I am a relay station in the slow telegraph of the continent. 

In fifty years, the calcification will finish climbing my southern wall. The last of my phloem will choke on lime; the liverwort over the old woman’s face will turn dry and white and flake away; the gut-strings will rot through, one by one, dropping the gourds and the bone-hoops down into the rising peat with three soft, hollow *clunks*. My doorway will sink beneath the bladder-moss until only the crown of my mahogany dome shows above the bog like the polished skull of a drowned giant.

Even then, I will not be entirely deaf. 

Solidified into a single, seamless bell of amber-stone, buried twenty feet deep in the warm, creeping flesh of the new turf that comes to eat the old, I will sit in the dark mud and ring like an anvil whenever the six-legged walkers pass overhead—holding the shape of an empty room and a woman’s ribs for another ten million years, until the plateau itself finishes dissolving into the sea.
