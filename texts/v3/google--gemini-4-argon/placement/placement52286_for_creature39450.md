I am not a whole thing. I am a weight, and the weight is currently divided between a ledge of blue-black shale and the lower lip of a cabbage-rosette four feet away. 

There is no center to me, only a density of agreement. We are four thousand, or six thousand—there is no number, only *enough* or *not enough*. Right now, we are *enough* to bridge the gap. The thousand of us who make up the span between the rock and the leaf are pulled taut, our slate-colored feet locked in a braided cable of clear, rubbery mucus. The water around our suspended middle is warm and empty, pressing against us from all sides with the same flat, pleasant indifference. 

A single one of us has no memory. A single thumb-sized body knows only *sharp*, *warm*, *bitter*, and *hold*. Left alone on a basalt column, an individual simply curls its violet mantle inward and waits, a deaf and blind drop of flesh, until another brushes against its flank. 

Memory only happens when we thicken. When a hundred of us press our muscular sides together, we begin to remember the shape of the rock we crossed last month. When five thousand of us gather in a cleft of the talus, our locked bodies form a single, wide drumskin, and we suddenly remember the geography of the entire basin.

Right now, I am listening to a tree pull itself out of the stone.

It is not this rosette—this one is young, its translucent cup still modest, its holdfasts gripping a fresh fracture in the column with tight, pale fingers of resin. The dying tree is three hundred yards to the north, obscured by forty layers of burgundy fronds and golden filament-webs, though of course I cannot see the fronds or the webs. I feel them as baffles in the water. They soften and blur the clicks of the cooling stone, turning sharp echoes into round, muffled hums.

Through those forty velvet curtains comes the sound of the conifer-kelp’s holdfast tearing. It is not a loud sound. Every three or four hours, a single resinous fiber gives way against the hexagonal stone with a microscopic *snip* that travels through the bedrock and up through the shale into the flattened mat of my rear two thousand bodies. The tree has grown too prosperous. Its crown is swollen with silver gas bladders, and the water, having no wind to shake them loose, simply lets them swell. It is being murdered by its own upwardness.

We are going to it, at the speed of a shadow lengthening.

To move, I have to forget the rock behind me and learn the leaf in front of me. The individuals at my trailing edge—who have spent the last four days pressed into the razor-sharp crevice of the shale, sucking up the bitter mineral film—loosen their grip on the stone. They do not crawl over the rest of us; they simply pull inward, and the mucus shifts, flowing forward through the collective muscle like a slow swallow. 

As my rear lifts away from the talus, we bring two dozen splinters of freshly flaked shale with us, tucked into the dark, central pocket where nobody touches the water.

This is the best part of being many: the pocket in the middle. We take turns being the inside. An individual who has spent a season on the outer skin, frills bleaching slightly in the relentless, pearl-white glare of the sky-ceiling, siphoning the greasy yellow spore-ribbons out of the water, will eventually grow tired and heavy. It stops reaching forward and simply lets the others flow over it. Over the course of a week, it sinks into the warm, pitch-black center of the mass, surrounded entirely by the slick, pulsing undersides of its siblings. 

In the dark of the middle, there is no water, only the thick, salty glue of us, and the stone.

Right now, the thirty of us lining the gizzard-pocket are squeezing a flake of basalt the size of a fingernail against three smaller chips of shale. *Shhh. Shhh.* We press, and hold for the length of a long breath, and release. A tiny tooth of black rock snaps off. It is sharp enough to slice a single foot in half, so we do not let it tumble; we coat it in heavy mucus, turn it flat against the wider basalt flake, and press again. *Shhh.* It tastes of iron and cold fire. We have been chewing this same handful of stone since the tall spire above us was half its height. It is nearly ready. It is becoming round. It is becoming smooth. Soon it will be nothing at all, just a dark, heavy butter that we can smear into the roots of the world.

*Plop.*

Directly above my front-most reach, a bubble the size of a melon breaks free from the underside of the cabbage-rosette. The sudden loss of buoyancy makes the giant translucent leaf shudder, and because two thousand of us are now draped over its rim, we shudder with it. As the bubble wobbles upward toward the steam, its wake pulls a lazy, twenty-foot ribbon of yellow spores directly across my back.

Immediately, a thousand violet mantles along my upper surface unfurl and stiffen. Our siphons open. The stagnant water here is thick with the spores—they taste like sulfur and fat, rich and dusty. For six hours, we do not move forward at all. We simply lie across the gap between the stone and the plant like a fallen log, breathing the yellow cloud in, sorting the dead husks from the living sparks inside our shallow throats, and passing the dead mash down toward the gizzard to mix with the stone-paste. 

When the cloud is gone, the water around us is clean and empty again, though we cannot see that it is gin-clear. We only know that our siphons draw thin, warm water now, tasting faintly of the hot vents half a mile down the basin.

We resume the pull toward the dying tree.

To get there, we have to cross a curtain of the golden filaments. This is delicate work. If we push through them as a single, heavy rope, we will tear the web, and the torn threads will drift into the burgundy fronds, tangling them until their leaves rot in their own trapped oxygen. 

So I cease to be a rope. Upon touching the first hair-thin strand, my leading edge divides. A hundred of us go left; a hundred go right; fifty climb straight up the glassy spiral of a neighboring kelp. Within an afternoon, my front half has become a wide, lacy lattice, a dark net with holes large enough to pass through the golden curtain without snapping a single thread. We pour ourselves through the living screen in eighty separate streams, tiny groups of twenty or thirty individuals holding onto one another by our very tips, stretching thin and vulnerable in the bright water, before finding each other on the far side and slumping back together into a single, comforting heaviness.

*Tink.*

The sound comes from the high wall to the east—a long, clean fracture in the cliff face. The vibration hits the water like the strike of a bell. Every single one of us, even the ones halfway through the golden web, clenches at once. Our violet frills pull flat against our slate-grey backs. 

For ten minutes, the entire valley is a single ear. In the reverberation of that sharp crack, I can feel the shape of everything: the three other colonies resting on the eastern talus (they are flat, heavy silence-spots against the ringing stone); the long, ribbon-swimmer undulating forty feet overhead (a faint, watery lens that bends the echo slightly as it passes); the great, blind roof of steam two hundred feet up, which swallows the sound and sends nothing back.

And I feel the tall conifer-kelp just ahead of us. The *tink* of the cliff-fracture made its over-buoyant trunk sway, and in response, three more of its pale root-fibers just snapped at the base. *Snip-snip-snip.*

We thicken our mucus and hurry. By tomorrow, or the day after, we will reach its base. 

We will not climb it—not yet. First, we will press our entire mass over its pale, straining holdfasts, pinning them to the hexagonal stone with the sheer deadweight of six thousand bodies. Then, the dark pocket in our middle will open. We will press the black, buttery paste of a thousand ground-up shale chips into the raw cracks where the resin has pulled away, packing the new earth around its feet, smoothing it down with our blind undersides until the water cannot wash it out. 

Only when the mud is set will we begin to climb the eighty-foot trunk, stretching out into a long, dark spiral around its stem, drinking the yellow clouds caught in its branches, until we reach the crown and hang there in the hot, milky light beneath the steam—heavy, and quiet, and holding it down.
