I am at the dead center of the bowl, where the stone is oldest and the pipes are thickest. 

They call my house the Drum, though it makes no sound you could catch with your ears. It is a windowless rotunda of double-laid yellow brick, sunken four feet into a dead, dry shelf of travertine that the vents abandoned three hundred years ago. From the outside, it looks like a kiln buried up to its shoulders in salt-crust and copper manifolds. Seventeen primary trunk-lines—some lagged in swollen, grease-stiffened canvas, some bare and weeping green verdigris—drop down from the trestles to vanish through lead-packed collars in my walls.

Inside, it is dark, ninety degrees, and smells of hot graphite, damp wool, and the sweet, tallowy stink of the paraffin vats.

I am sitting in a sling-chair of stretched hide suspended from the ceiling beams by four lengths of braided wire rope. If my chair touched the floor, the competing vibrations coming up through the stone would muddle the work. To read the basin properly, you have to take the pulses one at a time.

I am fifty-eight years old, which is ancient for the flats, though not so strange for the Drum. My knees went bad at forty—the sulfur-water gets under the kneecaps eventually, no matter how high your boot-soles are, and leaves little pearls of lime in the joints that grind when the barometer drops. So they took my scraper and my red oilcloth away, brought me in out of the violet glare, and gave me the long reeds.

Around me, rising from the concrete floor like a grove of iron saplings, are nineteen resonance-posts. Each is welded deep down to the belly of a different trunk-line before it breaches the wall. The tops of the posts are capped with cups of turned boxwood, polished dark as mahogany by the chins and foreheads of the three men who sat in this sling before me. 

I do not use the oak biting-wedge anymore. My back teeth are worn too flat from thirty years on the catwalks, ground down to yellow nubs that ache when a cold-return line shivers. Instead, I use a listening-stick: a four-foot stave of mountain ash, dry and light as a bird-bone, with a brass shoe on one end and a broad, hollowed thumb-rest on the other.

Right now, the brass shoe is resting in the cup of Post Six—the North-Northwest brine return—and the wood is pressed hard against the mastoid bone just behind my left ear. My eyes are shut. Even in the pitch-black of the Drum, with only a single tallow dip burning under a clay shade in the corner, my eyes water if I leave them open too long. They are the color of skimmed milk now, the pupils permanently wide, greedy for a twilight that isn't there.

Through the ash stave, the North-Northwest line feels like a heavy, sluggish river of warm mercury rolling over gravel. 

*Shhh-lump. Shhh-lump.* 

That is the great walking-beam at the Rim-Three condenser, a mile and a quarter away, reaching the bottom of its stroke and tipping the counterweight back. It takes four minutes and twenty seconds to fall, four minutes and twenty seconds to rise. I don't need the pendulum clock on the brick wall to know it is running twelve seconds slow. The grease on its main trunnion is stiffening; the afternoon sun has moved behind the shadow of the Number Four distillation tower, and the iron is cooling down. 

I lift the ash stick, swing my sling-chair three inches to the right with a push of my heel against a floor-peg, and drop the brass shoe into Post Seven. This is the small-bore copper signaling-line that runs alongside the NNW main.

With my free hand, I pick up a short bar of antimony-lead—softer than iron, so it doesn't burr the pipe and send false echoes down the valley—and tap the post below the boxwood cup. 

*Tock. Tock-tock. Tock.* 

*Rim-Three,* that says. *Grease the main bearing.*

I wait, the ash stick pressed behind my ear, breathing shallowly through my nose. For forty seconds there is nothing in the wood but the faint, fizzy tickle of plate-crabs scraping their shells against the submerged trestle-pilings half a mile out. They feel like dry leaves blowing across a cellar floor. 

Then, a dull, wooden *thud* comes through the stick, followed by a rhythmic, sliding scrape. 

That isn't a Tender. That is a bull wader, twenty pounds of copper skin and gristle, landing squarely on the unlagged signaling-pipe and shifting its three-toed feet to get a grip on the warm metal. I can feel the slight, elastic bow of the copper tube taking its weight. A moment later, a sharper, cleaner vibration cuts right through the bird's heavy shuffling:

*Clink-clink.*

That is young Hollis, out on the Rim-Three catwalk, tapping back with the nose of his grease-gun. He's heard me. He's already climbing the trunnion ladder.

I let the stick drop into the crook of my elbow and rub the bridge of my nose. My fingers smell of the tallow dip and the dry, chalky powder I dust between my toes every morning to keep the skin from peeling off in the humidity.

People down in the timbered valleys—the agents who come up on the mule-trains twice a year to weigh the sulfur cakes and sign the syndicate tallies, wearing their dark wool coats and breathing through vinegar-soaked rags—they think the Drum is a prison cell. The last clerk who poked his head through my double-curtained door took one look at the sweating brick, the dark iron stalks rising from the floor, and me hanging in my leather harness like a spider in a cistern, and he backed out into the blinding glare before he’d even got his goggles tied on.

He couldn't understand that out there, you only see a hundred yards before the white shimmer eats the world. Out there, you are trapped inside your own canvas coat, looking through two little horn slots at a slice of wet stone and a rusted rivet. 

In here, I can see three miles in every direction.

I know this basin the way a blind man knows the roof of his own mouth. I know that the deep sulfur vent under Terrace Nine—the one we call the Old Sow—is choking on its own lime-scale, because its oily, ten-minute burp has developed a double-hitch at the end, like a man hiccuping in his sleep. I know that the six-legged clay-lizards are bunching up on the south side of the Number Two boiler-sphere, because the north side has a hairline weep in the seam that’s spraying invisible, scalding vapor across the iron; I felt six of them slide off the curve in a lump an hour ago, three hundred pounds of wet meat hitting the lower catwalk with a soft *whump* that made the whole trestle hum like a plucked bass string.

And I know where the grease-hound is.

I haven't told the line-crews yet, because if you send three men with spears out into the middle pans during the high glare, they’ll get sun-sick and miss their footing, and the hound will just slip down an overflow pipe and wait them out. 

I found him two nights ago, just after the sun dropped and the thermal shock made all the big iron towers groan as they shrank a quarter-inch in the sudden dark. When the metal is settling like that, the pipes are noisy—full of clicks and pops and long, straining sighs—and the hounds use that racket to cover their chewing. 

I was on Post Twelve, the low-pressure paraffin line from the eastern flats, when I felt it: not the crisp *tink* of metal or the slow drag of crab-shells, but a wet, rhythmic *gnaw-gnaw-gnaw*, like someone sawing through a tallow candle with a notched bone. Every thirty seconds it would stop, and I’d feel a long, boneless slither as the thing flattened its hinged ribs and squeezed itself around the flange to get a better angle on the lead gasket.

He’s still out there. He didn’t finish the gasket two nights ago because I reached over to the master-bypass wheel on my north wall and dumped five hundred gallons of hundred-and-forty-degree wash-water down the outer jacket of the pipe. Through the ash stick, I felt the sudden, furious *thwack* of his paddle-tail against the iron as the heat hit his belly, and then he was gone.

Now, I swing my chair around to Post Twelve again. It is nearly sundown. I can tell because the draft coming through the dry-pipe vents in my masonry floor is turning cool; the mountain shadow has just touched the western cliff-face, and the cold air is beginning to slide down into the bowl like water into a dish.

I press the ash stick behind my ear and close my eyes.

The paraffin line is quiet. The thick, waxy fluid inside moves with a slow, velvety drag, barely sixty degrees, sluggish and rich. 

Far out along the pipe—maybe eight hundred yards east, right where the trestle crosses the Wide Apricot shelf—the evening shift is coming in. I can feel two Tenders walking the grating above the pipe. It’s Maren and her nephew; I know Maren’s walk because she has a slight hitch in her left hip from a wader-kick ten years back. *Step-drag, step-step, step-drag.* They are moving at the proper, flat-footed basin crawl, their heavy rubber soles thudding softly on the iron mesh, the vibration bleeding down through the trestle struts into the pipe, and from the pipe into my skull.

They pass the junction. Their footsteps fade into the general, syrupy hum of the valley.

For five minutes, there is only the machine. The great flywheels turning in their baths of black grease; the silent, endless siphon pulling the mountain's hot blood up through the limestone, stripping its salts, and spilling the warm water back over the white shelves to feed the slime and the crabs. It is a huge, heavy cradle rocking in the dark, and I am right in the bottom of it.

Then, right where Maren’s footsteps died away, a tiny, elastic tremor touches the pipe. 

Something long and heavy has just hauled itself out of the shallow water and draped its salt-pale belly across the warm copper to dry off in the twilight. It slides three feet to the left—*slup*—until its snout bumps the collar of the eighth junction flange. 

There is a pause. I can almost see it in the bruise-colored dark out there: twelve hinged ribs rising and falling, little black eyes wet with sulfur mist, smelling the sweet paraffin weeping through the scored lead.

Then it begins. *Gnaw. Gnaw. Gnaw.*

I don't reach for the hot-water bypass this time. If I scald him twice, he’ll learn the trick and move to the clay-pipe conduits on the southern rim where I can’t hear him until the line actually parts.

Instead, I keep the ash stick wedged behind my ear, lean over to Post Eleven—the eastern watch-house where Maren and her boy are just now stepping inside to take off their goggles—and I pick up my heavy iron wrench. Not the soft lead bar. The iron. 

I strike the side of the post with the flat of the wrench: two sharp, ringing blows, then a long, scraping slide down the metal, then three quick taps on the rim of the boxwood cup.

*Hound,* that says. *Flange Eight. Bring the spears.*

Through Post Twelve, eight hundred yards away, the chewing stops for a second. The iron ring of my signal has traveled out along the trestle, faint as a bee-sting by the time it reaches Flange Eight, just enough to make the grease-hound lift its flat head and taste the fog. 

It waits. Nothing moves on the catwalk. Above it, the first cold stars are coming out, and below it, the bacterial mats are beginning to glow their sour, bruised green in the dark. The hound decides it was only the pipe cooling in the night air. It settles its jaw back into the soft lead. *Gnaw. Gnaw.*

Two minutes later, on the same pipe, I feel the faint, tiny *click* of the watch-house door latch rising. Neither Maren nor the boy puts a boot down hard. They are walking on the outer edges of their soles now, sliding over the rust like ghosts, their weight so evenly spread that the iron barely whispers under them.

I settle back into the leather sling, close my milk-pale eyes in the warm dark, and press the ash stick tighter against my bone, waiting to feel the strike.
