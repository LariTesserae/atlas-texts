If you follow the main discharge line out from the western flank of Derrick Nine—past the forty-degree plume where the blind, quartz-backed crabs cluster thick as knuckles, past the outer boundary of the trestle-weavers’ wire gardens—the iron pipe eventually settles into a long, level run across a terrace of black glass. Every forty feet, the pipe is cradled by an A-frame trestle of pickled steel pinned into the rock with heavy lead-antimony bolts.

At Trestle Fourteen, eight feet off the bottom, the cross-bracing forms a tight, inverted triangle just beneath the belly of the pipe. 

If you look directly at this triangle from thirty yards away, there is nothing inside it. You can see the razor-edged basalt ridges three miles behind it with total, merciless clarity; you can see the faint, silvery shimmer of a ribbon-school hanging five leagues to the south. The light passes through the gap under the pipe without dimming by a single lumen. 

Look closer, though—not at the center of the triangle, where the eye wants to rest, but at the riveted seams of the iron struts that frame it. The lines of the steel do not continue straight through. They step sideways by half an inch, as if seen through the base of a heavy drinking glass. And the black stone behind the trestle is slightly *too* sharp, its distant spires magnified by a fraction of a percent.

I am wedged into that iron V, and I have not moved since the last two haul-cables parted on Derrick Six.

The bathysphere crews who come down to inspect the pipe-welds have no name for what I am, because when their searchlights sweep the trestles, I am simply part of the brine’s own magnification. If a cold-chisel were driven into the angle of the girder, it would strike something that feels like an exaggeration of the water itself: a three-sided, fifty-pound wedge of heavily cross-linked hyalectrin and fluor-silicate gel, stiff as vulcanized rubber, cold as the iron, and tuned to the exact refractive index of the plateau sea.

I have no shell to dissolve, no blood to pump, and no mirrored guts to flash in the noon sun. My only concession to opacity is a set of six anchor-pads tucked into the blind crevices behind the girder’s rivets—small, dense brushes of chitinous hooks that lock into the microscopic grain of the unrusted cast iron.

For me, the world does not exist as a picture. Having eyes would require lenses of a different density than the surrounding water, and in this blinding blue desert, two dark beads of focus would draw a tuning-fork from half a mile away. Instead, I am a lens in the service of an ear.

Running through the clear block of my body, suspended like flaws in an emerald, are three microscopic threads of piezoelectric crystal—barium titanate harvested ion by ion from the deep trench currents and grown along my central nerve-cord. They are invisible to the eye, thinner than the trestle-weavers’ murder-wires, stretched taut from one iron strut to the other.

To everything else in the basin, the noise of the fifty derricks is a single, crushing wall of violence that must be endured or hidden within. To me, it is the sun, the wind, and the soil.

Because I am locked directly to the steel of Trestle Fourteen, I feel the sixty-cycle hammer of Derrick Nine not as a chaos, but as a clean, towering chord. It enters my left anchor-pads through the iron, travels across my crystal threads, and exits through my right pads into the rock. I am a biological transformer. As the mechanical roar shears through my stiff, rubbery mass, the piezoelectric threads flex ten thousand times a second, bleeding off a continuous, silent trickle of millivolts. 

I do not eat the rock like the dredgers, or the frost like the hares, or the flesh like the bone-fish. I eat the *thrum*. That tiny electrical current, drawn directly from the waste vibration of the bronze flywheels half a mile away, walks down my nerve-lattice and drives the slow, cold synthesis of my tissues, cracking dissolved carbon and sulfur out of the brine passing through my pores.

As long as the machines hammer, I am fed.

But a stationary lens in a sea of flying shrapnel cannot afford to be merely passive. The tuning-forks hunt these pipeline corridors, using the iron trestles as acoustic baffles, and when a five-hundred-pound spindle of armored bone banks around Trestle Fourteen at forty knots, the wake alone is enough to tear a lesser body from its rivets. Worse are the tectonic slips—the forty-minute artillery shells that turn the water white with precipitated salt and make the discharge pipes whip like bullwhips in their cradles.

I know when the fault is going to slip before the rock even parts.

Ten minutes before the forty-minute mark, the deep basalt beneath the trestle begins to sing—a rising, sub-audible groan of stressed olivine crystals micro-fracturing miles down in the crust. The vibration comes up the pilings, entirely different in flavor from the oiled, rhythmic cadence of the turbines. It tastes sharp and dry across my barium threads.

When that rising whine hits a certain pitch, I dump thirty hours of stored electrical charge back into my own silicate matrix.

All through my clear body, millions of microscopic, fluid-filled vacuoles—each no larger than a wavelength of light—contract at once, squeezing out trace ions of lithium and pulling in heavy brine. My density shifts. My elasticity drops until I am nearly as hard as the quartz crabs. At the same time, the optical geometry of my outer mantle warps.

I stop being a clear window.

I become a prism.

Just as the fault slips sixty miles away—just as the pair of tuning-forks waiting down-current unhinge their jaws to lunge through the trestle-corridor in the three-second acoustic whiteout—the sunlight falling from three hundred feet above strikes my altered skin. It does not pass through. It bends at a brutal, forty-degree angle, focusing the full, unattenuated glare of the mountain sun into a single, blade-thin sheet of blinding violet-white light that slashes diagonally across the gap between Trestle Fourteen and Trestle Fifteen.

To a tuning-fork, whose eyes are primitive and wide-open to gauge the large shadows of the cliffs, it looks as though a solid wall of sunlit basalt or a blinding sheet of mirrored ribbon-fish has suddenly materialized directly across its flight path.

The lead predator flinches. Its pectoral hydroplanes snap down; its armored tail throws a frantic, cavitating check-turn that sends it slamming sideways into the open blue, missing the trestle—and me—by twenty feet. Its mate shears off to the left, straight into the outer guy-wires of the trestle-weavers’ net.

A single bio-steel thread parts with a high, singing *ping* that plucks my crystal nerves like a harp string, and a broad flap of yellowish, enamelled bone-plate, sheared cleanly from the fish’s shoulder, goes spinning down toward the bottom. 

Then the tectonic shockwave arrives. The water flashes opaque white; the iron pipe above me shrieks in its lead bolts and tries to tear itself from the stone; the acoustic hammer slams through my hardened body and is harvested in a single, massive surge of current that fills my storage nodes to capacity.

Three seconds later, the salt redissolves. The water is pale blue glass again.

I relax my vacuoles. The lithium flows back in; the brine flows out. The blinding sheet of refracted sunlight vanishes, folding back into nothing, and the distant black spires three miles behind me snap back into sharp, undistorted focus through my belly.

Down on the floor below Trestle Fourteen, the severed piece of bone-armor settles onto the sharp olivine gravel, rocking once before the heavy brine stills it. Within twenty minutes, the nearest rivet-hare on the piling-base has unlocked a fraction of its grey foot and begun the four-hour crawl out onto the stone to scrape the trace phosphorus from the enamel. 

Half a mile away, the bronze blades of Derrick Nine continue to cavitate, pouring their endless, roaring river of life into the iron, and I hang in the angle of the beam, clear as the freezing water, humming in sixty-cycle time, waiting for the rock to groan again.
