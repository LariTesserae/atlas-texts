I am forty fathoms down, on the underside of the Tier 6 switch-house overhang, where the concrete has been scoured into a smooth, vault-like groin. 

To your eyes—if you could keep them open in the ninety-degree silt—I would look like a weeping fault in the masonry. A pale, tallow-colored seam of calk, thick as a man’s wrist where I am wedged into the angle of the wall and the rusted iron bracket of an ancient junction box, splitting and thinning as I spread out over the pillowy concrete into a flat, ruffled Delta of wet leather. 

I am the *Mortar-Lung*. Or the *Gasket*. I have no shape that isn't the shape of the corner I occupy. 

The Shield-backs own the planes of this world; they are the masters of the shear, the grey armor of the open faces. I own the crevices. Where two plates of bolted iron meet and the silt-water has eaten out the old red-lead packing; where a heavy bronze manifold sits two inches proud of the brickwork, leaving a dead-water shadow behind its neck; inside the hollow, waterlogged belly of a street-lamp post whose glass was blown out in the administration of the fourth mayor—that is where my body lives.

I am a colonial mat, an animal-carpet related, perhaps, to the freshwater sponges or the bryozoans that once crusted the wooden intake cribs of the upper river, though I have long since traded my delicate baskets for gristle and vulcanized hide. My skin is the color of boiled lard, tough as a fire-hose, and entirely flush with the surfaces I bridge. If you ran a gloved hand down the angle of the masonry, you would not feel where the pillowed concrete ends and my margin begins. I am the fillet in the joint. I am the living radius that keeps the corner from throwing an eddy.

I do not hear the cylinder the way the Shield-backs do, as a crisp, three-dimensional palace of echoes. I am deaf to the distance. I do not care what happens in the boiling emptiness of the central shaft, nor on the South Manifold forty paces away. Instead, I feel the *flex*. 

My underside is rooted three inches deep into the microscopic pores of the concrete and the grain of the cast iron by millions of hair-fine, sulfurous threads. Because I span the joints between different materials—concrete that barely yields, iron that rings like an anvil, copper conduit that shivers like a plucked string—the noise of the chamber comes to me as a continuous, delicious shear. When the twenty-ton check-valves slam shut at the bottom of the well, the iron bracket I cling to jumps half a millimeter faster than the brick wall it is bolted into. That tiny, violent discrepancy—that microscopic pinch and slide—is my heartbeat. It pumps the sluggish fluids through my flat, veinless canals. Without the hammer of the water to knead me against the stone, I would suffocate in my own waste within an hour.

Right now, the water is at its low mark. The locomotive *thrum* is steady, a solid bar of pressure leaning against my back. 

Three feet to my left, out on the exposed cheek of the switch-house where the current splits and roars upward at thirty knots, a heavy Shield-back is grazing. I feel its iron-tipped teeth through the masonry long before it reaches my neighborhood—a slow, rhythmic *scritch-grind, scritch-grind* that sends tiny, gritty shivers through the aggregate pebbles embedded in the wall. 

It is an old one, nearly three feet across, its eight dorsal plates thick with precipitated rust. As it creeps toward the overhang, its left margin encounters the slight, turbulent whistle of a missing rivet on the switch-house corner. It does not like the whistle. The stray vortex tugs at the edge of its skirt, threatening to peel it off the iron. 

The big plate shifts four inches, slides over the corner, and clamps down right on top of my outer flange.

A surface creature would call this being trampled. To me, it is the closing of a circuit. 

The Shield-back’s vulcanized foot does not crush me; my hide is as tough as its own, and I am already shaped to the exact curve of the relief. Instead, its dozens of suction-chambers lock down over my leathery margin, sealing the noisy, whistling gap around the missing rivet. Instantly, the chaotic hammering of the local eddy vanishes. Beneath the heavy dome of its shell, a dark, dead-still pocket of water is formed between its grey belly and my pale back. 

The Shield-back stays there for twenty minutes, resting in the zero-drag fillet I have made of the corner, letting its muscles un-cramp while the ninety-degree current screams harmlessly over our combined silhouette. 

While it rests, we trade. 

From the slits along my ruffled edge, I exude a thick, tallow-like grease rich in sulfur-compounds and chelated iron—the refined paste of the silt and dying bacteria I filter from the backwater. The Shield-back’s piston-like foot relaxes its hooks and absorbs the paste straight through its porous sole, drawing the raw iron into its blood to renew its magnetite teeth and thicken its pewter plates. In return, the great beast dumps its heavy, ammonia-rich waste directly into the sealed pocket between us, and its rough, shifting skirt scrapes away the dead, calcified crust of silica that constantly threatens to plaster over my breathing-pores. 

We are the two halves of the wall’s maintenance: the iron plate that takes the blow, and the rubber gasket that beds the plate.

Then the pressure changes. 

I do not see the amber bulbs dim in their thick glass cages, because I have no eyes. I know the surge is coming because the cast-iron bracket in my grip suddenly grows cold—a tiny fraction of a degree drop as a million gallons of fresh cooling-loop water hit the lower manifolds half a mile away—and then the concrete wall *groans*, expanding outward by three thousandths of an inch as the hydraulic head arrives.

On my back, the Shield-back stiffens. The suction-chambers on my skin release with a simultaneous, muffled *pop*. Its leading edge lifts three millimeters into the rising shear. 

Then the central geyser blows. The roar goes from a locomotive engine to the inside of a cannon barrel. The water temperature spikes six degrees in a heartbeat. The Shield-back launches off my margin, kicking hard against the iron bracket, and is torn upward into the boiling black middle of the cylinder. 

As it leaves, the sudden vacuum beneath its tail pulls a string of pale, tough pearls from the slits in my side—my buds, heavy with silica and wrapped in sticky, hooked skins. They are sucked straight into the turbulent wake of the departing glider, tumbled up into the central storm where they will be battered against the upper tiers until their hooks catch in some new, whistling crack in the brickwork forty feet above me.

I am left naked to the surge. For six seconds, the water tears at my exposed lip, trying to find an edge to peel back, trying to rip me out of the groin of the overhang. I flatten my canals, lock my sulfur-threads into the pores of the stone, and let the scalding torrent polish me smooth. A blind, coin-flat crab, blown loose from a counterweight housing below, tumbles past my margin, hooks two legs into my leathery skin to arrest its fall, and drags itself into the tiny, silent lee behind my thickest ridge, tucking itself into my flesh like a tick into a dog's ear.

I let it stay. Its sharp little legs will pick the grit out of my folds.

Up above, in the steam-thick dark beneath the weeping brick dome, there is a series of heavy, muffled *thuds* as the gliders slam back onto the upper galleries. The twenty-ton valves at the bottom of the world go *clack-clack-BOOM*, the massive column of water drops back down the shaft, and the pressure in my joints eases with a long, metallic sigh. 

A new eddy has formed three inches to my right, where a flake of ninety-year-old mortar was finally torn loose by the surge. The water is whistling against the raw pebble underneath, throwing off a tiny, tearing plume of cavitation bubbles. *Rip-rip-rip*, like wet canvas. 

Slowly, blindly, nourished by the grease of the storm, I begin to creep an eighth of an inch to the right, stretching my pale, rubbery lip over the raw spot to quiet the noise.
