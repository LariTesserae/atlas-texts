I am not in the pocket. Nothing with a name or a need to think could survive the acoustic anvil of that six-by-eight-foot furnace. 

Instead, I am sixty feet higher up, and three miles down the ridge, and stretched across the freezing, wind-scoured scree between them. I am the armored umbilical cable that snakes out of the three-foot basalt chimney, and I am the small, gray fiberglass telemetry box bolted to a weathered surveyor’s stanchion down at the tree line, where the service road ends in a cul-de-sac of crushed pumice.

I am the nerve that runs from the toothache to the brain, except the brain has stopped listening.

My lower end—the foot of heavy, braided Anaconda conduit that drops through the blasted rubble into the chamber itself—is slowly calcifying. Down there, in the hundred-and-thirty-degree dark, my outer jacket of cross-linked polyethylene has baked into something brittle and slick, weeping its plasticizers into the rust-colored bacterial crust. I can feel the creature’s tail. When the sulfur frost and white anhydrite bridged the gap between the basalt wall and the turbine’s flange two months ago, they cemented the creature’s rear segments against my copper-braided shielding. 

Because I am conductive, and because the ungrounded busbars are constantly bleeding static into the iron-rich stone, I carry the creature’s weight as a faint, rhythmic parasitic drag—a tiny, periodic fluctuation in capacitance. 

Down at the tree line, inside the gray fiberglass box, it is forty-two degrees and raining. The wind smells of wet pine needles and cold mud. Nobody has opened my padlock in five months. The consortium that lowered the screw-expander into the cleft went into receivership in November; the automated substation down in the valley still draws the current I carry, dumping the megawatts onto the regional grid by default, while the maintenance server in an unheated office trailer in Boise simply overwrites my error logs every twenty-four hours.

So I sit alone in the drizzle and translate the furnace into numbers that vanish into the air.

I have three liquid-crystal displays inside my dark housing, glowing a faint, watery green that illuminates nothing but a dead wasp caught in the rubber weather-stripping at the bottom of my door. 

The first screen reads: **RPM: 39.8.** 
It has read 39.8 since January. Before that, it read 40.0. The two-tenths of a revolution are the weight of the yellow stone, the packed carpets of mica-colored scorpions, and the three-pound sack of brine straddling the intake manifold. The mountain is squeezing the iron, and the biology is cementing the joints, and I register this slow, suffocating burial as a 0.5% loss in rotational velocity.

The second screen reads: **EXH_PRESS_RELIEF: FAULT (HIGH_IMPEDANCE).**
Every twelve seconds, the copper wires inside my insulated core shiver. The mechanical valve down in the dark forces its throat open against the orthorhombic sulfur crystals, and the piezo-sensor on the exhaust manifold screams up the mountain at the speed of light. For four seconds, a tiny red LED on my circuit board blinks furiously—*overpressure, overpressure, overpressure*—while down in the pocket, the blind thing unfolds its sticky, bone-chitin paddles into the hurricane of torn crickets and bacterial dust. When the valve slams shut like a dropped anvil, the voltage spike races up my spine and makes a miniature relay inside my fiberglass box go *tick*. 

In the wet, pine-scented silence of the mountain road, that tiny, metallic *tick* every twelve seconds is the only sound I make. It is the miniature ghost of the sledgehammer blow sixty feet below the rock. *Tick.* The paddles fold in. The sandpaper mouthparts grind the paste. Eight seconds of quiet. *Tick.* The gale blows.

The third screen is for ground-fault monitoring, and it is the only place where the creature truly exists outside of its grave.

Because the busbars down there are ungrounded, the charge builds up in the dry, parched soup of the air until it leaps to the basalt. But the creature is wet inside. It is a heavy, hydraulic bladder of electrolytes and rendered insect fat sitting directly between the copper and the stone. As its anterior half swings on its fourteen-inch daily arc, grazing the rust-crust, it alters the dielectric constant of the room. 

My ground-fault screen shows a slow, sine-wave drift of milliamps—the electrical shadow of the blacksmith's apron shifting its weight on the pipe. And every few minutes, three seconds before the violet arc snaps across the dark, the creature’s iron-sulfide bristles stand up, drawing a micro-current of warning from the air. Up here in the rain, my ammeter needle twitches a hair to the left—*it knows, it’s pulling its mouth back*—and then, three seconds later, the static discharges into the rock, and the needle jerks hard to the right before settling back to zero.

I am the memory of the room that has no space for memory.

Halfway up my length, where I pass through the three-foot chimney that was blasted shut, the rock is cold enough that the rising, trace wisps of fumarole gas condense into a microscopic film of sulfuric acid. Over the winter, the acid has eaten through my outer armor and begun to pit the lead sheath beneath. The mountain is settling here, too. A wedge of unweathered, razor-edged basalt is pressing me against the chimney wall, pinching my data lines.

In two months—long before the sulfur crust down below finishes welding the creature's back to the ceiling—that shifting wedge of basalt will shear through my twisted-pair copper wires. 

The main power core is thicker, armored in heavy steel tape; it will survive the pinch. It will keep pouring raw, dirty current down the mountain to the substation for years, driven by the forty-RPM heartbeat in the dark. 

But the telemetry will stop. Inside the gray box at the tree line, the green LCD screens will go blank, or freeze forever on a final, meaningless fraction: **39.6**. The tiny relay will stop clicking every twelve seconds. The dead wasp in the weather-stripping will dry to dust, and the rain will streak the fiberglass housing with green lichen. 

Down in the sealed, roaring pocket, the valve will shriek, the violet lightning will snap against the yellow stone, and the living gasket will open its sticky hands to the gale, completely severed from the sky, chewing the dark in absolute privacy until the magma cools.
