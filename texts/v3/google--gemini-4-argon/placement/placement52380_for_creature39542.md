I am not in the houses. The houses are too full of breath and tallow and the low, beeswax hum of their voices, which have nowhere to go but into the sedge mats and the floorboards. If I were one of the eleven, I would have to be a twelfth, and the slate orchard only drips for eleven and a half. To be an extra mouth here is to be a slow theft of water.

Instead, I am at the southern end of the avenue, where the paving stones stop and the limestone simply ends in a plumb line of pale grey air.

I am inside the gatehouse, which has three walls and a roof of overlapped slate, and a fourth side that is only the wind coming up from the unseen valleys. I am the hornbeam drum.

I am six feet wide from cheek to cheek, hewn from a tree that grew three thousand feet lower down—a wood so dense and cross-grained that the iron adze-blades turned their edges when they shaped my axle fifty winters ago. The people of the bowl do not have trees; I am the largest piece of forest they have ever touched, though by now the mountain air has leached the sap-smell out of me and replaced it with mutton fat, horsehair, and the cold, chalky dust of the limestone floor.

All winter and all summer, I do nothing but hold the rope. 

Three hundred fathoms of braided brown tail-hair are wrapped around my belly in forty-two neat, tight coils. Because the horsehair is slightly oily and very stiff, it never rots in the dry height, and it never freezes solid. In the quiet months—which are ten months of the year—the rope and I sit in the half-dark of the gatehouse and listen to the bowl behind us and the drop in front of us. 

Behind me, the plaza is a great, flat ear of stone. Even from fifty paces away, tucked under my low roof, I can hear the old woman’s sheep-bone scraper click against a pebble in the third cistern’s feeder groove. I can hear the *tock* of the boy’s mallet against the seventh slate slab—the one with the blind seam near its heel that rings a quarter-tone flat when the frost has been in it. 

In front of me, there is no sound at all until the wind hits the lip of the threshold. Then there is a low, hollow *whuff*, like someone blowing across the mouth of an empty jug, and the two Alpine choughs appear from underneath the floor-level, rising vertically on the draft with their yellow bills closed and their black feathers slick against their ribs.

I am the only thing in the settlement that knows there is an *underneath*. 

The flagstones of the plaza only know the cloud that walks across them on its belly at dawn, and the soles of the ibex-hide boots, and the three-ton roller that presses them back into their beds when they try to rise. The slate slabs only know the west wind and the east wind and the grey sky that has no sun in it. Even the eleven people, when they come to my house in the fourth month and the ninth month, do not look down. When they swing the grease-darkened crane beam out over the sill, they look at the wooden ratchet-pawl at my side, or they look at each other’s hands on the four ash-wood spokes that pierce my shaft. To look over the sill into the pearl-colored nothing is to invite the eye to fall, and they are people made for flat stone.

Only my rope goes down.

When they knock the oak wedge from my brake-shoe in the autumn, I begin to turn. I do not squeak; they grease my stone sockets with the rancid fat from the bottom of their tallow-crocks two days before the hauling. I turn with a heavy, wooden *thump-thump-thump* as the iron-shod pawl drops into the teeth cut around my rim. 

As I unwind, I grow lighter, and the rope goes out over the grease-polished horn-roller at the edge of the abyss and drops straight into the wool-white haze. For two hours it goes down. As the coils leave my core, the wood of my barrel—which has been dark and warm under the horsehair for half a year—meets the cold mountain air and shrinks by the breadth of a fingernail, giving off a tiny, dry *crack* like a splitting hazel-nut.

When the rope reaches the bottom, a mile down through the soup of the lower clouds, I feel it through the last three wraps that never leave my hub. 

The rope does not go slack; its own weight is four hundred pounds of horsehair hanging in the void, pulling on my axle like a winter gale. Instead, the note of the rope changes. All the way down, it hums in the wind like a bass viol string drawn across a knee. When the people at the bottom—the small, wet-lunged people who smell of bog-peat and bruised turnips and birch-bark—take hold of the iron hook in the valley, the hum stops.

Then comes the tug. Three sharp pulls, which take four seconds to climb the mile of horsehair and arrive at my hub as a tiny, dull twitch, no stronger than a trout taking a beetle.

The four people standing around me in the gatehouse do not look down then, either. The oldest man—the one whose left thumb was crushed between my spoke and the wall twenty years ago, so that the nail grows out in two thick horns—lays his palm flat on the taut rope just ahead of my belly. He feels the three twitches in his calluses. He waits ten breaths, until his palm feels the fourth twitch, which means the wicker basket is lashed to the eye-splice.

Then he lifts his chin toward the rafters, and the four of them take my spokes.

This is when I am most myself. For three hours, the silence of the basin is broken inside the gatehouse, though the sound is too low and slow to travel far across the flagstones. There is the rhythmic, soft *huff* of four sets of thick lungs blowing out through pursed lips at the exact moment their eight ibex-skin boots lock their knees against the stone curbing. There is the heavy, deliberate *clack* of my oak pawl catching the tooth. *Huff. Clack. Pause. Huff. Clack. Pause.*

The rope comes back to me wet. 

Even though our bowl is dry when they haul, the rope has been down in the middle clouds where the rain lives. As it winds back onto my belly, it brings the lower world up into the gatehouse. The horsehair is dark and heavy with mist, beaded with water that has a different taste from our slate-water—water that has touched pine needles and hawk-feathers and the smoke of wet alder-wood before it was caught on the braid. The boy stands by the horn-roller at the cliff edge with a scraper made from a mountain-goat’s rib, stripping the grey water off the ascending line into a wooden bucket so the wet won’t rot my inner coils.

When the basket finally comes over the lip—first the iron hook, gleaming and cold, then the three-ply willow rim crusty with yellow valley-mud on its bottom—nobody speaks. They swing the crane in. They unhook the basket. They hook on the tiny net of calfskin bags that holds the horn vials of lichen-paste, which weighs so little that they have to lash a forty-pound block of dressed limestone into the net beside it just to carry the line back down through the updrafts.

Then they let me run backward, checking my speed with the long ash lever pressed against my rim until the friction smells of scorched hornbeam and hot mutton-tallow, and the whole gatehouse grows warm as an oven from the rubbing of the brake.

By dusk, the rope is home again, dry enough on the outside coils, smelling faintly of the valley’s damp sheep-pens and the wet linen sky it was dragged through. The oak wedge is tapped back under my cheek with two blows of a stone maul. The four people pick up the barley-baskets by their willow ears and walk out into the plaza with their slow, locked-knee roll, leaving the doors of the gatehouse open to the south so the smell of the hot wood-brake can blow away before nightfall.

I stay behind in the three-walled shade. The heat in my rim dies out in an hour, drawn into the stone piers that hold my bearings. The single drop of valley-water that the boy missed with his bone rib-scraper hangs on the underside of my thirty-eighth coil until midnight, then freezes into a bead of grey ice the size of a barley-corn, then vanishes into the dry wind before dawn without ever having melted. 

Outside, in the dark, the morning cloud begins to come over the northern rim, silent as wool, looking for the cold slate slabs to lay its weight upon.
