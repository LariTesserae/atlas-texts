The sinew bites into my collarbone—wet, hot, smelling of copper and tallow—and as it shrinks in the steam, pulling my shoulder back into the weeping slate, I feel the fatal temptation of the bones. 

Bones are gullible. Under that kind of direct, suffocating compression, my skeleton immediately understands the assignment. My spine wants to lock, to calcify, to let the gourd-vines and the bone-needles stitch my boots to the floorboards until my femurs forget they have joints and simply become two more yew-staves bracing the chimney against itself. It would be so easy to be a pillar. The five iron-shod thumbs at the back of my neck are still pressing, waiting to feel that final, dead *clunk* of a timber settling into its mortise.

Instead, I let my knees buckle. 

I can’t fall—there is nowhere to fall—so the buckle doesn't drop me; it turns my weight sideways, into a shearing force. My ribs slide down the greasy bark of a green hickory strut, my collarbone torques against the fresh sinew tether with a wet, creaking *twang*, and the brickwork in front of my chest, deprived of the rigid buttress it was counting on, suddenly bows inward by half an inch.

A seam rips open in the mortar. A sound comes out of it—not the *hiss-spit* of steam, nor the *slurp* of the mason-thing’s muscles, but a dry, violent *crack*, followed by a brief, screaming whistle.

Cold. 

A ribbon of the outside—of the violet-black, airless zenith—knifes through the parted clay before a bladder-fern gets sucked flat against the gap like a wet palm over a drain. Where that needle-thin jet of vacuum hit the fern, the rubbery leaf hasn't just frozen; it has turned to brittle, frosted glass. Where it grazed my cheek, the scalding sweat has flashed into a crust of rime. 

The five-thumbed hand on my neck spasms. It doesn't punish me; it panics. Two of its thumbs jam themselves straight into the crumbling seam over the fern, while the needle-fingered hand drops its sinew-thread and lunges up to claw a gob of grey, lime-rich phlegm out of the lung-sac's wooden trough, slapping it over the frostbitten leaf like caulk.

And in that frantic, two-second scramble, as my skin drinks the tiny, dissolving sting of actual ice, I realize what I am doing here. I realize why I haven't been crushed, and why I cannot let myself become a strut.

In a world that is dying of total, feverish synthesis, I am the shim. I am the draft. 

Look at my hands, wedged between a weeping terracotta brick and the mason’s own boiled-leather flank. They are not turning into trowels. They are not budding embryonic fingers or iron-slag nails. In fact, as I press my palm against the newly formed, dough-soft face of a red brick, the clay beneath my skin doesn't set. It *dries*. It turns pale, powdery, and friable, flaking away in grey scales that refuse to take a thumbprint. 

I am made of the one thing this vertical womb cannot manufacture and cannot digest: *pause*. 

I don't know if I brought the high, thin void in with me when the bamboo braided shut around my ankles, or if the sheer claustrophobic pressure of the weave forced the concept of *space* to condense into a skin-shaped sack just so it would have somewhere to go. Either way, my blood does not run at kiln-heat. Underneath the scalding sheet of runoff that coats my shoulders, my core is cool, stubborn, and slightly hollow—like a pocket of winter trapped under a rotting compost heap.

I test it. I take a breath—not of the boiled-sap soup, which would parboil my lungs, but by pressing my mouth directly against the bruised, frost-rimmed underside of the bladder-fern that plugged the leak, sucking the microscopic beads of thawed zenith-air trapped in its hairs. Then I turn my head and exhale a long, slow, thin stream of breath directly onto the three-wristed arm pinned against my ribs.

The mason-thing shudders through all its mortised shoulders. Where my breath touches its parboiled, clay-furrowed skin, the fever-grease dulls. The frantic, slug-like extrusion of its muscle slows by half a beat. A cluster of embryonic fingers budding near its wrist hesitates, their tips curling inward like sea-anemones touched by a stick. It doesn't hurt the creature—in fact, the great, horsehair-stitched lung-sac inches from my face gives a long, low, shuddering *sigh* that sounds almost like relief. 

For the first time since the sky froze, a square inch of the mason isn't boiling.

We are not enemies, the mason and I. We are counter-weights on the same frantic pulley. 

The room wants to be a solid block of hot, blind matter; the mason wants to organize the block so it doesn't crush its own liver; and I—wedged into the plumb-line with the sinew still cutting into my neck—am the thing that keeps the joints from fusing solid. I am the grease in the socket. The hairline fracture that gives the bridge room to swell in the summer without shattering.

A heavy yew beam above my head groans, its blind, blunt tip nosing down through the taro leaves, seeking the hollow between my shoulder blades to pin me for good. If it roots there, my spine will give up, and the shaft will choke off another three feet of its own throat. 

I don't wait for the palm-toothed hand to come chew a mortise in it. I reach up—moving with a strange, disjointed leverage, slipping my elbows through the gaps like a chimney-sweep—and grip the wet, sappy nose of the growing timber. I press my cool, stubborn palms over its cambium and just *hold* it.

The wood hisses against my skin, weeping turpentine, trying to sprout bone-needles through my wrists. I don't push back; I just refuse to be wood. Under my grip, the frantic cellular roar of the yew falters. The bark stops stripping itself. The tip blunts, grays slightly, and goes dormant, turning from a spear into a simple, static shelf.

Immediately, the flat, five-thumbed trowel-hand slides over my knuckles. Its iron-slag nails click softly against my wrist—not prying me off, not lashing me down, just reading the sudden, miraculous stillness of the timber. With a quick, practiced motion, it tucks a bloated, bursting seed-pod into the crook of my elbow to cushion the weight of the deadened beam, and then retreats into the purple gloom to deal with an iron-ore root by my heels.

I shift my hips an inch to the left, letting a rivulet of scalding water sluice down my spine instead of pooling around the mason’s central liver-gourd. I lean my forehead against the weeping clay, close my eyes against the bruised-orange glow of the mortar, and breathe out, very slowly, keeping my three square feet of nothingness open in the dark.
