I am not in the canyon. I am three hundred feet below the lip, where the basalt wall overhangs the void and the sun never reaches. 

Up there, they have the twenty-minute rhythm of the glare and the mist. Down here, there is only the gray belly of the cloud-sea rising and falling against the stone like breath against a mirror.

If you drop a stone from the catch-harps, it falls through three hundred feet of violet light, punches through the skin of the cloud-floor, and then falls for two more seconds in the blind, wet gray before it passes the ledge where I am tethered.

The ledge is the width of two boot-soles. It is an horizontal fracture where a block of columns slipped out into the abyss a thousand years ago, leaving a narrow shelf and a row of sixty-four copper pegs driven into the crack at the back of the niche by my grandfather’s grandfather. The pegs have turned the color of dead moss, except on their upper edges, where our yak-hair ropes have polished them back to the bright, red-gold meat of the metal.

I am the fourth one. There must always be a fourth one, though the three above never look over the edge to see me, because to lean out over the lip when the thermal is rising is to risk having the wind get under your mantle and peel you off the mountain like a dry scab.

I am the counterbalance. I am the winch.

My world is forty feet of vertical stone above me and ten thousand feet of nothing below me. I wear two mantles—hareskin inside, grease-cured ox-hide outside—and I sit inside a sack of braided grass stuffed with shedding yak-wool, tied off at my ribs so that my lower half is sealed in its own sluggish heat. I have not stood up in four days. When my legs cramp, I hit the thighs with the flat of a stone hammer until the muscle gives up and goes dull.

Before my face hangs the rope.

It is the thickness of a boy’s wrist, four-ply, braided from the belly-hair of the high herds and soaked in groundsel-sap so the frost cannot get inside the fibers and explode them. It comes down from the invisible sky above, passes through a smooth ring of mountain-sheep horn lashed to the stoutest of the copper pegs at my shoulder, and coils in a neat, stiff serpent between my knees.

I do not need the horn visors down here. There is no glare. The light is the color of watered milk, sour and flat, diffusing through half a mile of vapor. My eyes are wide open, wet and black in a face greased with marrow-fat. I watch the rope.

The rope tells me everything happening in the nave above.

For seventeen minutes, it hangs dead. The frost grows on its hairs in tiny, feathered spikes, all pointing into the rock face where the draft sucks inward. I sit with my bone-splinted mittens tucked into my armpits. I chew on a strip of raw back-fat. I listen to the mountain.

Up in the sun, the young rock cracks like a whip when the heat hits it. Down here in the belly of the stone, that sound arrives as a deep, iron *thrum* against my spine—the whole cliff shifting its weight by the width of a hair.

Then the cloud-sea begins to boil between my boots.

This is mine before it is theirs. I feel the thermal first. It comes up out of the ten-thousand-foot dark like the warm flank of an enormous animal brushing against the cliff. It isn't warm—nothing is warm—but it is less dead than the air that lives under the overhang. It smells faintly of the abyss: pulverized slate, old snow, and the bitter, green stink of the low-valley hemlocks three miles down, carried all this way in the stomach of the wind.

The mist thickens until I cannot see the toes of my boots. The feathered frost on the rope trembles.

Two minutes later, the rope twitches. 

*One. Two.*

The girl has reached the lip. She never looks down, and I never look up, because a falling chip of obsidian kicked from the edge would take my eye out before I heard it coming. 

I pull my mittens from my armpits. I take the greasy yak-hair coils in my bone-splinted palms.

Above me, out of sight, the wicker basket comes over the lip of the canyon, weighted with four clear slabs of basin-ice. Seventy pounds. If she simply dropped it on a taut line, the jerk would shear the rope against the sharp basalt lip or pull the ancient copper pegs from the fracture like rotten teeth.

So I take the weight on my ribs.

The rope runs around my leather-bound hips, through the horn ring, and into my hands. As the basket drops into the void, I let the line pay out—*hiss, hiss, hiss*—the sap-stiffened hair smoking slightly against the sheep-horn ring, heating it until it smells of singed wool. I brace my triple-soled boots against the upstream pegs and lean back into the nothingness, trusting the yak-hair loop around my waist, using my barrel chest and short, bowed legs as a brake.

For twenty seconds, the basket and I are balanced against each other on the face of the world.

Then the dark shape blooms out of the white above me, swinging slightly in the updraft. I catch the wicker rim with a hooked rib-bone, draw it in against the shelf, and tipping it, slide the four slabs of clear, bubble-tracked ice out onto the back of the ledge.

Here, inside the perpetual cloud, the ice will not sublimate into the dry violet air, and it will not melt. I stack the slabs like bricks against the basalt, cementing them together with a wipe of wet snow so they become a single blue wall. 

There are sixty-two slabs stacked along the narrow shelf to my left. I am building a glacier on a mantelpiece.

I tuck two empty leather bird-sacks and a fresh coil of horsehair snare-line into the basket. I tug the rope twice. 

Above me, the two men have finished their three-minute circuit of the rosettes. They have come to the edge to haul. The rope goes taut, singing a high, thin note in the wet air, and the basket rises back into the milk and vanishes.

The wind drops. The cloud-sea sinks back down past my boots, leaving the gray stone dry and cold again. 

Above me, the light has returned. I know this because a single snow-partridge feather, knocked loose from a snare-sack at the lip, comes spiraling down out of the sky. For the first two hundred feet, I can see it flashing like a tiny spark against the dark rock—catching the ultraviolet fury from the canyon rim. Then it crosses the shadow-line into my world, and the spark goes out. It is just a bit of dirty gray down, drifting past my knee into the ten-thousand-foot dark.

I take a sliver of obsidian from my pouch and cut a small notch into the wooden handle of my stone hammer. Sixty-six. 

Twenty-four more fogs, and the wall of ice beside me will be as tall as my shoulder. Then the three above will come down the ropes like fat spiders, their sacks heavy with frozen partridges and groundsel-jelly. We will lash the sixty-pound ice-blocks to our pack-frames with rawhide, tie ourselves together at twenty-foot intervals, and begin the two-day descent down the copper-pegged staircase through the blind belly of the clouds, never seeing more than one peg ahead, feeling for the metal with the toes of our ironwood boots.

Until then, I pull my arms back inside my mantle, tuck my chin into the grease-stiffened hare fur, and watch the frost feathers begin to grow again on the hanging rope.
