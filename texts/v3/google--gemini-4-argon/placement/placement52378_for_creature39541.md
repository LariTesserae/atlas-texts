I am the tallow in the stone socket. 

Wait—not just the fat, though you would smell me before you found me: that rancid, herbal stench of boiled marmot suet and bruised fern-seed that never truly washes off a thumb. I am the whole assembly in the north-west corner of the fourth tier down, where the water from the Three-Finger Seam drips thickest. I am the black basalt pillow, carved out with flint chisels two hundred years ago; I am the pale yellow grease packed three inches deep into that stone hollow; and I am the butt-end of the Great Spouse-Beam that rests inside it, sixty feet of ancient, resin-heavy mountain larch spanning the corner angle to hold the mid-tier kitchens against the rock.

If you want to understand this place, do not look across the void. Look into the dark behind the liverwort curtains where the wood meets the mountain.

The humans think they live in a box of air, because air is what their eyes can cross. They stand on their boards and wave their white-beaded wrists at the south wall, proud of their silence, proud of their little bone clackers. But from here, packed tight between the hex-faced basalt and the wet timber, the settlement is not a box of air. It is a web of heavy, groaning pressures. 

I do not have eyes to see the noon square of sky, nor ears to get lost in the endless whip-crack of the falling beads. I have *load*. 

Right now, I am holding four tons of waterlogged sphagnum, eleven clay braziers, three sleeping-boxes lined with damp sheep-wool, six hundred fat gray snails grazing on the roof-slime, and the heavy, flat-footed weight of two brothers who are currently scraping rot-curls off an upright twenty feet out from my socket. Every time the older brother shifts his weight from his left heel to his right, a minuscule shiver runs down the grain of the larch, presses through the crushed wood-fibers at the beam’s end, and squeezes a microscopic ribbon of yellow grease up over the lip of my stone pillow. 

That is how I know them. Not by their faces, which are all the same tallow-gray anyway, nor by the murmurs they press into each other's skull-bones. I know them by the way they step. 

The old woman who lived on my beam forty years ago—a Prober with knuckles like ironwood knots—walked like a water-strider; she barely registered in my socket, a mere flutter of pressure before she was already over the harness-edge, dangling in the dark beneath me, driving her bone needle into my belly. I loved her for that needle. When you are a sixty-foot larch beam buried under three feet of cold bog, the rot is a warm, sleepy itch that starts in your middle and makes you want to let go. Her needle was sharp and cold; it found the soft pocket where the black water had pooled in a split, pierced it, and let the sour juice drain out before the porridge-rot could eat through my heartwood. When they finally lowered her down the middle of the shaft in her sedge-net,weighted with three hex-stones, the sudden absence of her eighty pounds made my timber rise by the breadth of a horsehair. I felt the tallow suck back into the gap with a tiny, greasy *pop*.

Time down here is not counted in seasons. The moss doesn't brown cleanly in the autumn; the cold rain from the seams never freezes solid because it comes straight from the deep belly of the world, and it never warms in the summer. Time is measured in the *creep*.

I have three inches of stone lip left. 

When my grandfather-builders first swung this larch into place—back when the pine was still yellow and smelled of high wind and dry needles—my butt-end sat flush against the back wall of the socket. Since then, the mountain has breathed out nine times. 

The Spout-Watch in the kitchen cistern above me always knows first. Two hours before the rock speaks, I feel a tiny, frantic rhythm vibrating down through the slate legs of the water-tank: the blind pink thing thrashing its tail against the stone, trying to swim deeper into a solid wall. Then the humans feel that warning through the cistern-mat. Their footsteps change. They stop walking heel-to-toe and begin to shuffle, low and wide, hauling the heavy braided bark-ropes out of the corner chests, tying their children and their soup-kettles to the ironwood pegs.

Then comes the *thrum*.

To the humans, it is a sound low enough to turn their bowels to water. To me, it is the only moment I am fully awake. The basalt behind me doesn't just vibrate; it becomes briefly, terrifyingly *elastic*. For half a heart-beat, the mountain forgets it is stone. The great hexagonal columns flexing against one another deep in the dark let out a long, grinding sigh as the central plug drops another fraction of a millimeter toward the core of the earth. 

Then—*tink*. 

The cliff-face pulls backward. Just the thickness of a dried fern-leaf. 

Out in the shaft, the hanging boulder-nets swing wildly for three seconds, shedding a sudden, violent curtain of water drops that makes the echo-chamber roar like a collapsing glacier. And inside my socket, the mountain retreats from the wood. The larch beam, pulled by the four-ton counterweight hanging down in the dark below the second tier, slides forward across my bed of marmot fat. 

*Sluuuup.* 

A sixteenth of an inch. A fresh scar of pale, unoxidized grease is exposed to the cold air behind the timber’s heel, immediately catching a drifting spore of green liverwort. 

If I were mortised in—if the builders had been fools from the flatlands who believed in iron nails and rigid joints—that sixteenth of an inch would have sheared the larch like a dry carrot. The kitchens would have tilted; the braziers would have spilled their coals into the damp moss; the two brothers, the sleeping-boxes, the cistern, the blind salamander, and six hundred snails would have peeled off the north wall in a slow, green avalanche, bouncing twice off the east face before vanishing into the black slush at the bottom where the white crayfish wait.

Instead, I slide. I give way to the mountain's slow divorce from itself.

In ten minutes, a girl with white goat-hide cuffs will climb down the slick willow-ladder beside me. I will feel her knees bump the stone corbel. She will not look at the two hundred feet of nothingness behind her heels; she will only look at the tiny ribbon of fresh yellow fat squeezed up at my toe, and the tiny gap opened at my heel. She will reach into the skin bag at her hip, scoop out a handful of foul, grey-yellow paste, and pack it hard into the gap with her thumb, sealing the air out of my joint so the wet-rot cannot find the end-grain. Her greasy forehead will brush the basalt for a second—not in prayer, these people do not pray to stone—but simply to steady her balance while she works her thumb into the crease. For a second, the warmth of her blood will push against the cold sweat of the seam, and I will hold them both: the girl who will last fifty years, the beam that will last eighty, and the mountain that is taking ten thousand to open its mouth.
