I am not one of the tall figures wading through the mercury of the resetting air, nor am I the kneeling hillock of stolen hours at the center of the marsh. I am smaller than they are, and closer to the stone. 

You would find me at the exact margin where the elder’s rotting shadow meets the pristine, sixty-cycle grid of the water—right where the black peat-sludge is beginning to bleed into the crystalline squares. 

At first, I am only a localized palsy in the basalt. 

For ten million cycles, the stone beneath the marsh has been locked in the same instant of fresh cleavage, its edges sharp enough to split a hair, its mineral grains held in an unyielding vice of *now*. But where the elder’s knees have cracked the floor, a tiny, hair-thin fissure has opened, and the cold, mineral-heavy water is seeping down into a place the loop was never designed to reach. 

I am the rust in that crack. I am the oxidation that happens when the machine’s oil finally meets an unscheduled tomorrow.

If you were to crouch down in the pewter fog, ignoring the *clack-shunt* of the ten thousand frogs and the *zzzt* of the Klink-men tearing the air, you would see me pulling myself up out of the split rock like a slow, ochre-colored crab, though I have too many legs to be a crab and no shell to speak of. I am no larger than a boot-heel. My body is an agglutination of the basin’s very first mistakes: a paste of wet greenstone flour, the tarnished silver scales shed by the dying bearing-eels, and the sour, yellow tallow that drips from the elder’s flanks. 

I do not walk on top of the Faraday waves like the Klink-men. I am too heavy for my size, too dense with wet mud. I crawl *under* the water, gripping the sharp polygonal ridges of the floor, dragging my low, gritty belly through the sixty-cycle hum. 

Where I pass, the geometry of the water breaks. The shivering squares above me lose their corners; they blur into soft, sloshing rings that travel outward for three or four inches before the tyrannical hum of the moss catches them and snaps them back into a grid. I am an interference pattern with an appetite.

My work is humbler than the Klink-men’s. They harvest the high, hot friction of the air—the aborted flight of the sparrow-insects, the snapped strides of the caliper-birds. They are builders of momentum. I am a creature of the foundation. I eat the sharpness.

I crawl to the base of a pristine basalt polygon, a blade of black volcanic glass that has stood un-dulled since the range was split. I press my underside against its razor edge. I do not have a mouth; I have a grinding-sole, a muscular foot paved with tiny, rough grains of quartz and feldspar that I have pried loose from the elder’s crack. 

And then, I simply shudder. 

I borrow the sixty-cycle vibration of the copper-coil moss beneath me, taking that clean, electrical drone into my heavy, mud-packed body, and I step it down. Through the dead eel-grease and the rotten fern-pulp that holds me together, the sixty cycles are muffled, dirtied, and baffled until they become a slow, irregular, grinding *scrub*. 

*Shhh-k. Shhh-k. Pause. Shhh-k.*

It is the sound of sandpaper on iron. It is the sound of a glacier’s belly sliding over bedrock at the speed of a growing fingernail. 

The glade hates it. The loop tries to reset the rock edge beneath me. Every four seconds, when the ten thousand frogs throw their iron shuttle—*clack-shunt*—I feel the terrifying, backward suction of the basin’s crankshaft trying to haul the microscopic grains of basalt I have just abraded back onto the parent stone. The force of it flattens me against the floor; it threatens to tear my tallow-and-silt body apart and scatter me into the non-time of the splices.

To survive the snap, I have to anchor myself in the elder’s filth. I keep one long, trailing filament—a root-like tail braided from dead, blackened hepatic-moss—buried deep in the rotting peat at the center of the marsh. Through that tether, I drink the elder’s heavy, stagnant *Tuesday*. It fills me with a dull, leaden inertia. When the loop yanks at me, I am simply too stupid, too sluggish, and too dirty to remember where I was four seconds ago. I stay where I am. And the three or four grains of black dust I have ground off the basalt edge stay under my belly, trapped in my grease, kept out of the loop’s reach.

I digest the sharpness, and I excrete silt. 

Behind me, in the narrow groove between two polygonal stones, lies a ribbon of grey mud no wider than a pencil lead and three inches long. It is the first soil the mountain has ever known. It does not hum. It does not reciprocate. It just lies there in the cold water, soft and entirely still.

Sometimes, one of the blind silver eels, its figure-eight orbit decaying as it brushes the edge of the dead zone, will accidentally nose into my little ribbon of mud. The moment its snout touches the silt, the creature shudders violently. Its living-bearing mucilage curdles into white strings. Instead of snapping back to the beginning of its turn, the eel slips sideways, its silver skin scraping against the rock I have dulled, and it does not die—not like the ones that touch the elder directly. 

Instead, it forgets its figure-eight and simply *swims*. It wriggles off into a corner of the polygon, curls its body into a loose, un-geometric 'C' in the lee of a stone, and rests. Its gills pump in a slow, ragged rhythm that has nothing to do with the frogs. Every now and then, it opens its mouth and swallows a mouthful of my grey silt, just to feel the weight of something inside its belly that isn't a flywheel.

I am very slow. It takes me a thousand cycles of the caliper-birds' three-step stutter to round off a single inch of basalt edge. As I work, I leave a faint, brownish smear on the raw greenstone—the first lichen-stain, the first patina of age. 

I do not look up at the upper ledges like the Klink-men do. I do not wait for the mountain to fall in a great, theatrical roar of rockslide and avalanche. I know that the mountain cannot fall until its teeth are loosened in their sockets. 

So I stay at the bottom of the ankle-deep water, under the chest-high pewter fog, half-buried in the growing drift of my own grey waste. *Shhh-k. Shhh-k.* I am taking the razor’s edge off the world, one grain at a time, making a soft place for the cliffs to land when they finally remember how to let go.
