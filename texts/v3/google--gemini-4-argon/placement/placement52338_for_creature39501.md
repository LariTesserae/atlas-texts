I am thirty-one miles east of the foundry wall, and I have been walking for three weeks, though three weeks is a measurement that belongs to an eye that watches a sun, and I have neither. 

Time, for me, is measured in the shifting ratio of two chords. Behind my sloped back, the Municipality I left is a fading bass rumble in the soles of my feet, its drop-forges reduced by distance to a slow, heavy throb in the basalt. Ahead of me, nineteen miles into the pale mist, the next Municipality is a rising high-register hum in my forward plates, like cold grease beginning to boil. I am currently at the point in the forty-mile span where the two cities cross inside my chest. 

My internal leaves—the stiff, black-rubber gills suspended in the dark vault behind my sternum—are trembling at two slightly different intervals. It creates a slow, rolling beat frequency in my white blood. *Thrum-thrum-pause. Thrum-thrum-pause.* My milky fluid surges to my knees, hesitates as the two vibrations cancel each other out for a fraction of a second, and then surges again as the chords realign. It makes my gait heavier than usual. Every fourth step, my left hip-pad—a thick cushion of silver-blue moss that has grown slightly asymmetrical from decades of walking on the southern side of the pipeline—compresses with a longer, wetter wheeze, squirting a thin ribbon of cold black water down my calf.

I am not walking on the gravel path below. I am walking on the spine of the upper conduit, sixty feet above the Shelf. 

The copper beneath my three-toed feet is ten feet wide, curved and slick with a film of bright green verdigris and condensed mist, warm as the belly of a living thing. Inside the metal skin, two inches beneath my heavy, basalt-scaled soles, ten thousand gallons of superheated mineral brine are rushing eastward every minute. The friction of that liquid against the copper walls produces a continuous, singing *shhhhhh* that rises straight through my ankles, up the dense stone columns of my legs, and settles in my lower belly as a steady, comforting heat. 

To my left and right, the world drops away into the pale, shadowless bright. I do not see it, because my prow is solid iron-slag and basalt, unbroken by any seam or lens, draped in four feet of dripping grey lichen. Put an eye here and the freezing mist would blind it; put an ear here and the sixty-cycle roar would rupture it. Instead, I feel the emptiness of the Shelf through the wind. 

The wind is twelve miles per hour. It comes from directly behind my left shoulder. Because it never changes, it is not weather; it is a wall. It is a permanent diagonal fixture of the universe against which I lean my seven-hundred-pound bulk so that I do not have to waste effort balancing. My posture was cast for this angle in the dark of the foundry-mound before my plates hardened. If the wind were to stop, I would topple leftward off the curvature of the pipe and fall sixty feet into the cartwheel-rosettes below. Walk east, you lean left into the wind; walk west, you lean right. That is the geometry of the pipe.

I stop. 

My right foot remains planted on the warm copper; my left foot hangs two inches above the metal, suspended in mid-stride while my hip-moss slowly finishes its long, whistling exhalation. 

Something is wrong four hundred yards ahead. 

Through the soles of my feet, the singing *shhhhhh* of the brine inside the pipe has acquired a tiny, jagged burr. Instead of a smooth, cylindrical vibration, there is a rhythmic ticking—a tiny cavitation in the flow, no larger than a pebble, repeating seventy times a minute. And above it, carried on the twelve-mile-per-hour wind, is a thread of warmth that should not be in the air. 

I lower my left foot. *Clack.* The basalt scale strikes the copper. I resume walking.

It takes me forty minutes to cover the four hundred yards. As I approach, the acoustic burr sharpens until it is scraping against the third and fourth gill-leaves on the right side of my chest, making the white blood in my right arm itch and thicken. The air grows wet in a different way—not the cold, suspended silver mist of the sky, but a fat, heavy, copper-smelling wetness that clings to my hanging beard of lichen and makes it heavy.

I reach the flange. 

Here, two forty-foot sections of the great tube are joined by a collar of cast iron, held together by a ring of forty-eight hex-bolts, each the size of a cabbage-rosette. At the two-o'clock position on the collar, the gasket of vulcanized moss-rubber has perished by a fraction of an inch. A needle-thin jet of scalding brine is hissing out of the seam, shooting three feet into the air before the steady west wind catches it, bends it over, and dashes it across the curved top of the pipe.

There are already two others here. 

They have been here for a long time—perhaps three years, perhaps five. One is crouched directly on the crown of the pipe, its knees wedged against the iron collar; the other is clinging to the sloped side of the tube, its long, spanner-fingered left arm hooked entirely underneath the lower lip of the flange to hold its weight against gravity. Their chests are pressed tight against the weeping seam. 

Where the hot, mineral-heavy brine has washed over them, the silver-blue bryophytes of their shoulder-joints and the grey curtains of their head-lichens have grown monstrous and lush, swelling into a single, seamless pillow of steaming green velvet that bridges the gap between their carapaces. You cannot tell where the first Tender’s shoulder ends and the second’s chest begins. Streaks of white lime and pale green copper-salt have precipitated out of the leak, forming stony stalactites that hang from their elbows and fuse their shin-plates to the curve of the conduit. 

They are asleep, if you can call it sleep. Their internal gills are being hammered at point-blank range by the high-pitched shriek of the leak. Their blood is racing, hot and thick and loud, pinning them in a state of ecstatic, motionless suspension. 

Yet the leak is not sealed. The pressure inside the ten-foot tube has shifted slightly—some distant valve in the western Municipality has opened a millimeter wider—and the needle of hot water has found a gap between their fused shoulders, right where the curve of their blind iron prows prevents a flush seal. It is hissing out into the Tuesday-morning light, wasting heat into the sky, throwing the harmonic pitch of this forty-foot section of pipe out of tune by a quarter-tone.

I do not hesitate. The itch in my right gill-leaves pulls me forward like a winch.

I step up to the iron collar. The two fused Tenders do not shift; their stone plates are locked in a calcified embrace, their blood-gills vibrating in unison with the pipe beneath them. I place my heavy, three-toed feet between the knees of the upper Tender, finding purchase on a ridge of precipitated lime. 

Slowly—over the course of twenty minutes, while the twelve-mile-per-hour wind blows the scalding spray across my knuckles—I lower my forty-degree bowed torso into the gap between their heads. 

My blind, iron-and-stone prow slides between their lichen-draped domes. The fit is tight. The rough basalt scales of my left shoulder grind against the right shoulder of the lower Tender with a sound like two millstones kissing; my right ribs scrape across the heavy, lime-crusted chest-plates of the upper one. My weeping beard of grey lichen washes directly into the needle-jet of boiling brine.

The heat hits my carapace first as a dull expansion of the stone, and then the vibration hits my chest.

It is blindingly loud. Through the contact of our three stone bodies pressed against the iron flange, the shriek of the escaping water enters my sternum like a spear of white light. My internal gills flare open, slapping violently against the walls of my chest cavity. My thick, chalky blood, which had been sluggish and divided between two distant cities, suddenly roars into a single, furious current. It hammers into my fingertips; it floods the woody rhizomes wrapped around my wrists; it warms the cold black water trapped in the moss of my knees.

I reach down with my right hand. My blunt, spanner-shaped fingers find the six-inch iron nut directly below the weep-hole. 

The nut is crusted with three inches of pale green mineral scale, and the lower Tender’s forearm is already resting against its bottom flats, grown halfway into the metal by a web of tough, rubbery roots. I fit my stone thumb and forefinger over the top flats of the nut, right on top of the other’s petrified wrist. I do not pull with muscle; I have no muscle worth speaking of. Instead, I shift my hips two inches to the left, hanging my seven-hundred-pound weight off the leverage of my arm, and I pump the hot, roaring energy of the leak into the hydraulic moss-pads of my shoulder. 

The moss swells. The pressure builds. 

Nothing happens Today. Nothing will happen Tomorrow. 

The hot brine trickles through the dark, three-way cavern formed by our pressed chests. It is dark in there, and wet, and warm—just like the interior of the thirty-foot foundry mounds where we were kneaded together out of slag and spore. Already, in the scalding dark between our carapaces, the tips of my grey facial lichens are touching the swollen silver-blue bryophytes of the other two. Within a week, the spores will catch in the mineral grease. Within a month, the creeping rhizomes from my wrist will thread their way under the stone scales of the lower Tender’s forearm, anchoring my grip on the hex-bolt so that even if my blood were to stop, my weight would never slip from the wrench-flat. 

The wind blows from the west at twelve miles per hour, combing the wet hair of my back-moss toward the east. Overhead, the white sky holds its ten-o'clock light without a flicker. 

Under my thumb, through the three inches of lime and the woody flesh of my brother’s wrist, a single crystalline grain of iron inside the bolt-thread gives way with a microscopic *ping*. The nut turns by the width of a spore. The pitch of the leak drops by a thousandth of a hertz. 

I hang my weight against the iron, drinking the hot copper water through my chest, and wait for the second grain to yield.
