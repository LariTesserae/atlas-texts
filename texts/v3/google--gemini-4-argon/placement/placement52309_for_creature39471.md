I am at the Drop-Off, though "drop" is a lowland word that has no currency here. I am at the Lip, five miles above the dry countries, where the ocean simply stops believing in its own breadth and curls back on itself like the rim of a thick glass bell.

To look outward from the Lip is not to see the world below. Because the boundary curves so sharply, the light—that bruised, sideways amber—hits the inner skin of the meniscus and bounces back. The edge of the world is a tarnished mirror. If you press your face against the gelatinous wall of the boundary, you do not see the dry plains five miles down; you see the forest behind you, stretched and curved into a great, looming amphitheater of green-brown meat.

I live in the crease where the tallow-stone meets this standing wall of water. It is a narrow, continuous parish—ten yards wide and forty thousand miles long, ringing the whole plateau. 

I am not a Keeper. I have too much skin left for that, and my bones are still stubbornly socketed together, though they have gone soft as boiled turnip-root over the decades—or centuries; the seasons here are only long, warm sighs that take lifetimes to finish exhaling. I am what happens when something from the bottom climbs up through the five-mile fog-chimneys and forgets why it came, or how to dry out. 

If you saw me, you would think of an old leather coat dropped in a vat of tallow, which has slowly learned to breathe through its seams. I am flat—not like a skate, which is muscled and deliberate, but like dough that has been pressed between two warm stones. My ribs have unhinged at the breastbone and folded outward so I can lie flush against the greasy basal rock or press myself flat against the stiffened rubber of the boundary-meniscus. My eyes are gone beneath two thick pearl-buttons of calloused hide; they only itched in the silt, and there was nothing left to see that I couldn't feel with my belly.

My work—if an obsession that takes no effort can be called work—is the harvest at the Lip.

The Shelves are constantly trying to shed things, and the Lip is constantly refusing to let them go. When a gas-willow dies three hundred leagues inland, or a bowel-mantle tears itself open on a stiffened eddy, or a Keeper loses a piece of its calcium wind-chime during a purple storm, the debris drifts outward. Over decades, the slow, centrifugal slop of the plateau pushes the dead weight toward the edges. 

When these things reach the Lip, they don't fall off the world. They get stuck in the thickened, indecisive water of the boundary, held like plums in warm aspic. 

I slide along this wall on a slick of my own grey grease, half-stuck to the tallow-floor, half-leaning against the rubbery curve of the world's end. I am the only thing here that still uses fingers, though mine have webbed together into two broad, cartilaginous paddles with black, horn-soft nails at the rim. I reach up into the stiffened skin of the boundary and pluck the things out before they can rot the meniscus.

It takes hours to pull a single willow-bladder free. The water at the edge doesn't want to let go; it stretches out in long, ropey strings like hot glue, snapping back with a dull, rubbery *thwip* that sends a shudder through my flattened ribs. 

Mostly, I eat the green tallow that collects in the crease—a sour, mineral-rich cheese formed where the iron-kelp dissolves against the stone. It requires no chewing; I simply lie over a patch of it for four days and let the skin of my throat drink it through the pores. 

Sometimes, though, I find the things that came from the *other* side.

The Lip is not entirely one-way. Down below, in the thin, sharp air of the true world, the winds still blow. Birds still die on the wing; dry leaves are lifted by thermals; the dust of actual, fractured stone is kicked up by hooves. When these things are blown against the five-mile-high wall of the Shelves, they hit the hot mist, become sodden, and are slowly sucked inward through the meniscus by capillary greed.

I find them suspended in the amber jelly of the boundary: a dead hawk with its hollow bones crushed flat by the sudden density, looking like a soggy fan of brown sod; a pine-cone, swollen to the size of a pig’s head and weeping turpentine that smells like a knife-blade in this bland soup; once, a long-handled spade of rusted iron and split ash-wood.

I kept the iron head of the spade. I couldn't hold it—my paddle-hands don't curl anymore—so I cut a slit in the loose, pleated hide under my left ribs and tucked it inside, between my skin and my meat, the way an oyster keeps a grievance.

It is the only truly heavy thing for fifty leagues. In this tired geometry, where even the stones slump downhill like porridge, that three-pound triangle of lowland iron still remembers the center of the earth. It pulls at my left side constantly, a stubborn, downward drag that makes me list as I glide. It is an anchor that never hits bottom. It hurts, a dull, rusty ache that smells of blood and cold dirt, and I would not trade it for all the warm liver-oil in the forests. It keeps me honest. It tells me which way my back is facing.

The Keepers know I have it.

They rarely come all the way to the Lip; the water is too stiff here for their delicate shrouds, and the tarnished-mirror reflection of the boundary confuses their thermal filaments, making them think they are surrounded by a crowd of themselves. 

But every few years, after a great purple storm has squeezed the canopy dry and the melon-spores are still drifting up into the ceiling-fog like drowned moons, an old Keeper will slide down the tallow-slope into my crease. 

I feel it coming long before it arrives. The water grows clean—a startling, naked sensation against my blind hide, like having a blanket pulled off in a drafty room. Then comes the smell of old copper and wet bone, and the soft, muffled *clack-clack* of its unstrung spine rolling like dice in its jelly.

I stop my grazing and flatten myself against the grey rock, my left side pinned down by the iron spade-head in my slit. 

The Keeper does not nudge me toward the Webs. It knows what I am; it can taste the lowland rust leaking through my pores. Instead, it settles over me like a great, warm bell-jar of clear snot, ten feet across, sealing me against the floor.

For a day, or three, we stay like that. 

It lowers its scattered bones until they rest directly against my flat, pleated back, separated only by a thin skin of its mucilage. And then, very slowly, it shifts its buoyancy-liver until its center of mass is directly over my left ribs—directly over the iron.

It isn't strong enough to lock their bones together the way the Webs do. It doesn't tear their nerve-silk or cloud their jelly with pain. The iron is just a memory of weight, muffled by my flesh and corroded by the brine. 

But it is enough to give the Keeper's floating ribs a slight, downward lean. Inside the clear shroud, the white commas of its old spine drift down and settle in a neat, quiet row along my flank, resting against the lump of the spade-head like hounds sleeping by a hearth.

We don't move. The amber light bruises to slate and warms back to ochre. Above us, the gas-trees gulping the broth make their long, hydraulic *thrum*. Three feet to my right, the ocean ends in a wall of warm glue, and five miles below that, things are falling fast and breaking when they hit. 

I lie in the grease, holding the rust in my side, while the ghost of a spine borrows my weight to remember how to lie down.
