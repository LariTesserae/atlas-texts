I would not be one of the Tenants—there is too much frantic, inherited guilt in that endless scrubbing—nor would I be the city itself, which is too vast and blind in its growth. 

I would find myself in the acoustic plumbing. I would be one of the hollow, calcified seed-pod resonators hanging beneath the junction of a four-way root-bridge on the second level, roughly thirty feet above the mirror-polished floor of the Plaza. 

Specifically, I would be a gourd-organ that has developed a hairline fracture along its lower hemisphere, and the creature that has grown inside it to keep it from splitting open.

In a city held together by structural acoustics, a cracked resonator is a localized catastrophe. Instead of amplifying the *thrum-crack-rasp* cleanly and passing the kinetic energy along the root-highways to keep the wood taut, a cracked gourd buzzes. It creates a dead, rattling dissonance—a muddy *bzzzz-clack* that bleeds tension out of the surrounding tenement walls. Left alone, the membranes of the nearby walk-ups would go slack, the capillary roots overhead would stop sweating, and the local district would rot into moths within an afternoon.

So the city grows a Damper. That is what I am.

I am a dense, heavy slug of translucent amber resin and wet cartilage, shaped roughly like a human heart or a thick fist, wedged permanently into the bottom of the hollow gourd. Unlike the rest of the nave’s featherweight, paper-thin fauna, I have genuine mass. I am the heaviest thing for fifty paces in any direction. 

My entire existence is tactile and harmonic. I have no eyes to see the beige glare outside the vault, though I can feel its dry heat baking the upper curve of my shell. Instead of sight, I have forty or fifty long, slender, root-like tendrils—*feelers*—that extend from my core, snake out through the acoustic ports of the gourd, and thread themselves into the grain of the surrounding architecture.

Some of my feelers are anchored into the load-bearing thumb-roots of the bridge overhead. Others dangle down into the open air of the avenue below, their tips flattened into tiny, sensitive paddles that catch the updrafts. A few are woven directly into the translucent leaf-skin of the adjacent third-floor apartments. 

Through them, the city plays me. 

I sit in the dark, warm interior of my gourd, smelling of turpentine and wet celery, and I hold the neighborhood in tune. When a flock of skin-winged glides executes a synchronized ninety-degree turn ten feet below my bridge, the sonic boom hits my dangling air-paddles first. Instantly, instinctively, I shift my weight inside the gourd. I squeeze my resinous body harder against the hairline crack in my shell, pinching the split wood together with the exact pound-foot of torque required to turn a shattering *crack* into a warm, cello-like *hum*. 

I am a living fret on the neck of a screaming instrument.

Because I am stationary and heavy, I experience the city's inhabitants not as creatures, but as textures and rhythms that pass through my wiring.

I know the flat-bugs as a continuous, dry tickle along my upper bridge-tendrils—a six-lane highway of serrated legs that sands my bark smooth and keeps my shell warm with friction. When the upward-falling rain hits them and they shift their rasping chord down a half-step, my core contracts automatically, tightening the tension on the apartment walls beside me so the wet leaf-skin doesn't sag.

I know the cellar-mouth frogs in the floating puddles below as a rhythmic, four-beat thump that travels up through the air-columns and taps against the underside of my belly: *clank-clank-clank-clank*. It is my metronome. When one of the Plaza’s spatial seams drifts too close to my bridge, the *clanks* begin to arrive out of order—yesterday's *clank* colliding with today's—and I have to extrude a thick, gummy bead of sap through my shell's crack to deaden my own resonance, lest I accidentally amplify a temporal echo and blow out the party walls of the walk-up next door.

My favorite moments—if a biological shock-absorber can be said to have preferences—are when the Tenants visit.

Because my gourd has a crack in it, it leaks a microscopic mist of pulverized resin and damp inner-bark whenever the vault overhead drops a water-globule. To a Tenant, this mist is premium mortar. 

Every few hours, I feel a sudden, fluttering drop in air pressure along my outer shell, followed by the dry, chamois-leather slap of an overcoat-sized Tenant wrapping itself around my gourd. For twenty minutes, the Tenant clings to my exterior, its umbrella-ribbed limbs clicking rhythmically against my wood as it scrubs my leaked resin into the joints of the bridge above me. 

While it works, our bodies are separated by only a millimeter of calcified rind. I can feel its hollow chest-tympanum pressed tight against my cracked hemisphere. For those twenty minutes, we share a nervous system. I bleed off its excess acoustic pressure through my heavy, grounded mass, giving its frantic, fluttering chest-valves a rare moment of rest; in return, its wide, billowing dermal pleats catch the high-frequency shriek of the eastern chitin-crabs—a sound my heavy tendrils usually miss—and translate it into a gentle, low-frequency purr that massages my core. 

We sit there together in the deafening roar, the scrubber and the weight, holding up the intersection of 4th and Main in a city that has never had streets, names, or people. 

Then the bark-nodules in the east turn purple, or a vine-viper strikes a glide nearby, shifting the weight of a balcony three doors down. The chord changes. The Tenant peels itself off my shell with a dry rip like tearing velcro, catches a booming thermal from the Plaza, and slides away through a wall. 

I pull my tendrils a fraction of an inch tighter, shift my resinous bulk to the left to compensate for the departing weight, and settle back into the crack, listening to the water drops fall like gunfire, keeping the key.
