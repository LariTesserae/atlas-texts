I am three-quarters of the way down the eastern grade, four terraces back from the Spillway, where the water has already passed through twelve residential plinths and six wide courts of arum and cress. By the time the sheet reaches me, it is rich with the day’s sugars and heavy with dissolved lime, though the heat of the western seams has nearly gone out of it. It slips over my dorsal plates at the temperature of a cooling bath, just warm enough to keep the thin air from biting into the seams of my armor.

I am very heavy now. My left rear limb has not flexed at the mid-joint since the heat-plume sat beneath the central causeway, two decades ago. When I glide, I must swing my whole eastern flank outward in a slow, fractional arc to clear the liverwort along the curb, pivoting on the horn-rim of my heel like a stone door turning on its socket. Inside my chest, where the hydrostatic chambers used to swell and ease with the noon-tide warmth, the cartilage has grown a white, branching lattice of flint. Each heartbeat—four minutes from the slow contraction to the long, mineral-thick release—clicks faintly against my ribs, like a pebble settling in a cistern.

My work on the high vaults is done. I no longer haul the wet-rope harnesses or set my shoulder against the two-ton ballast slabs with the younger three-hundred-pounders whose hides still give like firm leather. My weight is too fixed for the long slide across the districts; I would score the liverwort grease and break the laminar sheet into bubbles. 

Instead, I am a weir-keeper on the seventh sluice.

I stand at the throat of a twelve-foot channel where the fourth court drains toward the fifth. For eight hours while the sun climbs and hangs in the rinsed blue sky, I do not move at all. My four square-rimmed pads are planted across the basalt sill, spaced eight inches apart. I am the last gauge before the water drops toward the rim. Through the porous cushions of my soles, I read the micro-tremors of the greywacke floor two miles wide: the faint, singing tension of the third district where six roofs have been triple-weighted to hold down a rising blister of heat; the slow, centuries-long sag of the southern margin; the distant, solitary *thud* of a wedge-split block two thousand yards away, which arrives through the stone three seconds before its half-millimeter swell reaches my ankles.

I do not think in words, which are the quick, barking currency of creatures that burn air. My mind is a map of levels and pressures, held together by the angle of the sun’s polarization in the water. 

When the shadow of the plinth behind me has pivoted twenty degrees across the glare, my upper slits read a minute thickening in the dark border where the sky’s reflection meets the retaining wall thirty yards out. The meniscus is rising on the north side of the court by the thickness of a fern-hair; on the south side, the moss along the curb is showing half an inch of dry, matte tip above the glaze. The bedrock beneath the fifth plaza is exhaling—warming by a tenth of a degree, swelling upward like a sleeping chest, tilting the grade away from the Spillway.

If I leave it, the northern channel will deepen by sunset. By next month, it will begin to purl. The surface tension will tear against the corners of the masonry; oxygen will bleed out into the thin sky; the broth from the arum roots will rush past the sixth-tier cisterns too fast for the sleepers in the vaults to drink it through their flanks.

I begin to turn.

It takes twenty minutes to rotate my bulk ninety degrees within the half-inch sheet. I shift my weight from the locked left limb to the broad right fore-pad, sliding the horn bolster forward across the slick basalt at the pace of a growing root. The water parts before my shin without a ridge and closes behind my heel without a hollow. Above me, the jagged black peaks of the horizon remain unbroken in the mirror of the court; only the dark, wet trapezoid of my shadow detaches itself from the sluice-wall and creeps out onto the bright water.

Six yards along the curb lies a ballast-chock—a three-hundred-pound prism of greywacke split out during my first century, its edges still sharp enough to shave horn, though its lower half is bearded with tough, horizontal ferns. 

I lower my thorax until my siphon touches the warm film. I draw in a long, slow draught of water, swelling my internal chambers to their utmost hydrostatic stiffness, locking my remaining joints against the flint-lattice in my chest. I set my two fore-pads against the chock’s narrow end, fitting the square horn rims of my feet into the chisel-straight cleavage of the stone. 

Then I lean.

There is no grunt, no scrape of rock on rock. The cushion of liverwort beneath the greywacke weeps a film of slick mucilage under the pressure. For an hour, as the sun begins its drop toward the western teeth of the rim, the block and I are a single piece of architecture expanding southward by four inches. Behind me, the water flowing through the seventh sluice feels the new obstruction. The sheet thickens by a fraction of a millimeter on the south side; a wide, silent fan of pressure radiates back across the court, lifting the drooping tips of the dry moss back into the warm glaze, evening the dark line of the reflection along three hundred yards of wall.

Two terraces up, a younger mason standing on a causeway feels the back-swell press against its ankle-pads. It does not turn to look at me. It simply adjusts its own stance by half an inch to split the returning wave, letting the correction settle into the grid.

By the time the shadows of the dry-stone towers have stretched all the way across the three-hundred-yard courts—long, razor-edged bars of purple-black laid over blinding silver—I have glided into the low corbelled vault of the seventh plinth. 

Four others are already there, submerged to their upper slits in the chest-deep broth of the interior cistern. We do not touch, though our broad, stone-colored flanks are separated by only six inches of dark water. Down here, beneath the two-ton roof slabs, the only light is a green, sub-aqueous glimmer refracted through the hanging curtains of cress roots at the doorway. 

I slide down the stepped bench until the water closes over my shoulder-slits. My heavy limbs settle onto the gravel of chalk and silica pellets that we have precipitated over three hundred years—a floor of our own winnowed stone. I unseal the calcified plates along my sides and let the pale, pleated fringes of my gills drift open into the warm current.

The broth enters me. It tastes of iron from the western fault, of sweet nitrogen fixed by the high sun in the cress beds, and faintly, tangibly, of the four others sharing the vault—the slow, chalky signature of their age and the rhythm of their four-minute hearts pulsing through the dark water against my tympanic skin. 

*Plip.*

A drop of condensation gathers on the petrifying fibrous lintel overhead and falls into the pool between us. The concentric ring it makes is the fastest thing in our world. It travels outward in a fraction of a second, touches the stone walls, touches our submerged shoulders, and rebounds toward the center in a tiny, interfering grid of ripples that takes ten minutes to completely flatten back into stillness.

While I drink the dark water, I feel the locked joint in my left leg cooling. The silica is laying down another microscopic rind inside the cartilage. In ten years, perhaps twelve, my right fore-limb will catch as well. I already know the gap I will take: thirty yards south of my sluice, where the retaining wall of the fifth court has a two-foot notch between two ancient blocks of basalt—blocks whose corners are rounder than quarry-stone, grained like old horn under their thick pelt of emerald moss. 

The gap faces east, toward the Spillway, where the excess water slips over the rim into the clouds that lean against the mountain. It is a good place for a four-hundred-pound weight. When I fold my limbs into that notch and let the sheet-flow close over my back for the last time, my left flank will hold back twenty thousand gallons of warm broth for the arum beds, and my right shoulder will turn the current two degrees north, keeping the sixth terrace wet and level for the next five hundred years of sun.
