I am not at the top, where the slate sky pulls the breath from the throat of the pit, and I am not in the blinding middle, where the white cones of the Weirs hang trembling in the glare. 

To find me, you have to go down past the last place a human could wedge their boots. You have to follow the spiral to the choked nadir, and then you have to keep going, down into the dark under-rubble where the light finally gives up, defeated by the sheer density of the packing. 

Here, five yards beneath the visible floor of the basin, the tiles are no longer stacked in neat rings. Centuries of weight have sheared them, canted them, and ground their edges together until they lock like the teeth of a trap. The air does not roar down here; it *draws*. This is the intake of the great panpipe. Through a billion microscopic capillaries in the crushed crockery, the chill air of the deep earth is sucked upward toward the sun. 

I am in the dark, and I am very thin.

If you were to lever up six tons of compacted kaolin biscuits to expose the seam where I live, you would think at first that someone had poured pale lead into the cracks to solder the rubble together. I look like a dry spill of silver-grey wax, or a root system that has been flattened under a steamroller until it is no thicker than a playing card. 

I have no front and no back. I am a sheet—a hand-sized, branching wafer of gristle and calcified gel, pressed so tightly between the flat faces of the dead tiles that I have taken on their exact texture. The thumb-prints of whatever ancient things pinched these clay shards out of the mud five hundred years ago are embossed in reverse upon my skin. 

They call the walkers above the *Shims*, and the dust- fishers the *Weirs*. If anyone knew I was down here, they might call me the *Grout*, or perhaps the *Lute*—after the clay paste old potters used to seal the door of a kiln.

I do not have eyes, because the light stops three feet above my highest tip. I do not have bristles like the Shims, because I have nowhere to ratchet to; the rubble down here is locked for eternity. I cannot move through the masonry. Instead, I grow through it, very slowly, by dissolving my own edges and re-precipitating myself an inch to the left over the course of a winter.

My world is entirely pressure and pitch. 

Above me, the great basin hums its eternal, hollow chord. Down here in the foundation, I do not hear it as sound; I feel it as weight. The millions of stacked tiles above me are a single, vibrating anvil, and I am the thin film of oil caught between the anvil and the hammer of the earth. 

When the chord above is pure, the vibration comes down through the rubble as a clean, vertical purr. It massages my flat body. It pumps my sluggish, alkaline blood from one lobe of my wafer-thin self to another, saving me the caloric expense of a heart. I am cold, and I am dry, and I am pressed so flat that my organs are merely faint discolorations in my grey hide, and I am completely content.

I eat the dead, and I drink the wind.

The Shims above are jealous of their water, recycling their tiny drop of spittle until they die. When they finally perish and lock into the wall, they think they are dry. They are wrong. Locked in the deep lattice of their calcified chitin, bound at the molecular level to their pasted kaolin shells, there is still water—water of crystallization, mineral-bound dampness, the last stubborn ghosts of hydrogen and oxygen that refused to be sucked out by the roaring draft.

Over centuries, the weight of the upper terraces crushes the oldest, deadest Shims downward. The walls subside by a hair’s breadth every generation, forcing the ancient necropolis down into the dark nadir where I wait. 

When a dead Shim is finally pressed against my upper surface, I fold a lobe of myself over its rounded, thumb-shaped rim. I do not chew. I secrete a dry, crystalline acid—no more than a microscopic sweat—and I wait. Over months, I unbind the mortar of its body. I leach the locked water from its ceramic bones, drop by millionth of a drop, drawing the moisture out of the stone the way salt draws damp from the air. I leave behind a crumbly, chalk-white ghost of pure, unstructured kaolin, which the upward draft immediately sucks away, carrying the sterile dust up through the vents to blind the sky and feed the Weirs.

From the bottom of the pit, I am slowly letting the walls down. As I digest the lowest layer of dead Shims and turn them into rising dust, the entire basin settles into the space I have cleared. I am the reason the terraces crowd inward. I am the slow sinkhole at the root of the bell.

And because I am the foundation, I am the third party in the war above, though neither the Shims nor the Weirs know I exist.

Sometimes, the Shims above become too successful. They pack a terrace too tightly; they choke off too many vents in their blind mania to destroy the Silt-Weirs. When they do this, the pitch of the basin rises. The hollow chord sharpens into a tight, screaming whine. 

Down in the dark, that whine is agony to me. The clean, vertical purr that pumps my blood turns into a high-frequency shear. The tiles around me begin to chatter microscopically against my skin, threatening to sand me into paste. 

When the pitch gets too high, I have to change the shape of the room.

I cannot climb up into the glare to pull the Shims apart. Instead, I choose a load-bearing column of rubble down here in the dark—a tight, wedged stack of ancient pottery that supports the weight of an entire bulbous terrace twenty feet above my head. I pour all my stored, alkaline moisture into the seams of that single stack. I turn my acid up to a slow burn. 

For three weeks, or four, I dissolve the keystones of the foundation. I turn the hard, locked biscuits of the nadir into slick, soapy kaolin grease.

Then, I pull my lobes back into the adjacent crevices, flatten myself to the thickness of paper, and wait.

It only takes a millimeter of slip. Down in the dark, a single, crushed tile shears in half with a dull *thud*. 

The support vanishes. Above me, a twenty-ton section of the terrace drops by the width of a fingernail. 

To a human, it would be invisible. To the basin, it is an earthquake. A vertical fissure—a hair-thin crack—splits the concentric rings from the nadir all the way to the lip.
