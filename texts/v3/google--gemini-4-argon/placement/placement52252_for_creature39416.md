I am four feet below the pale-green mattress of the roots, where the light is no longer white, but the color of clarified butter. 

Down here, the heat is not a blanket; it is a solid block into which the world has been cast. The basalt directly beneath my left foot is a frozen wave of black glass, pulled taut as skin before it cooled, its crest sharp enough to split a hair. I have had my heel resting against the hollow of that wave for long enough that the tacky, vulcanized pad of my sole has conformed entirely to the microscopic striations of the lava. If I were to lift my foot now, it would make a soft, peeling sound, like tape pulled from a windowpane. 

I am not going to lift my foot now. I am in the middle of a reach.

My right arm is extended upward, passing through a narrow, three-cornered gap between two bulbous ginger-tubers. My skin is the color of tupelo honey that has begun to sugared at the bottom of the jar—still amber, still letting the buttery light through the thick webbing between my fingers, though when I bend my wrist, there is a dry, internal grating, like two pieces of pumice rubbing together under water. I am not young, and I am not yet stone. Perhaps twelve hundred years have passed since I uncurled from the brood-cleft in the eastern wall. I am stiff, my syrup-blood pushing through veins that have narrowed into rigid, silicate pipes, my heart giving its heavy, solitary thud once every six minutes. 

*Thud.*

The pressure moves through me. I feel it reach the tips of my four fingers, swelling the pads just enough to increase their friction against the bark of the column-stalk above me. 

Three days ago—or four, the glare above the canopy never dims, only shifts its spectral weight very slightly from bleached-bone to lemon-pith when the exterior sun passes directly over the basin's lip—I found an inconsistency. 

It is an old score-mark on the underside of this root-buttress. Someone made it long before I was born. Most marks are three parallel lines, or a neat chevron, cut with the grain of the wax to show a pleasing alignment of vines or the way a basalt spire catches the yellow halos. This one is a spiral, chipped not into wood, but across the vitreous black stone itself, right where the pale-green root-knuckle swells out of the floor to swallow the rock. 

A spiral means movement. It means: *Follow the turn of this.*

It took me a month of looking from six different perches to understand what the maker was pointing at. 

The root-knuckle has grown—just a fraction of an inch over three thousand years, the wood bulging with glacial, hydraulic pressure against the basalt—and in doing so, it has tilted the broad, paddle-shaped leaf of an understory spur directly above it. 

The tilt is microscopic. To an eye from the upper rim, the leaf is flat. To me, it is a steep, perilous chute. 

Because of that tilt, the warm condensation rising from the vent six yards away no longer collects in the center of the leaf to form the great, trembling, silver-heavy lens of water that the spiral-maker intended. Instead, the water gathers at the leaf’s notched tip, grows heavy too soon, and drops every four hours. 

*Tack.*

There it goes now. I feel the sharp percussion of it in the stiff membranes behind my knees before the sound even dies in the resinous air. The drop falls four feet and strikes the dry, glassy shoulder of the spiral-maker himself.

He is sitting ten feet away from me, wedged into the pitch-black cleft between two cooling-folds of the floor. He has been finished for a very long time. His body has gone entirely milky, the color of a quartz pebble, covered in a fine, glittering dust of precipitated silica. A curtain of pulled-glass vines has grown down around his head like a veil, and his left hand is fused into the very root that betrayed his geometry. 

Every four hours, the drop hits his collarbone, shatters into three smaller beads, and rolls down his chest, ruining the silence and missing the deep, stone-stacked basin he spent his last century building five feet to his left. His basin is dry. The air inside it does not have the heavy, humid lensing he designed it to hold. His sentence has been mispronounced for five hundred years, and his milky eyes are locked open, staring at the error without the ability to lift a finger to correct it.

I am correcting it. 

I do not do this out of pity. We do not have pity; pity requires a belief that things ought to be other than they are, and down here, things simply *are*, with a density that leaves no room for grief. I do it because a crooked line in a library of straight lines is an itch in the center of the brain.

In my left hand, held tucked against my ribs where my six breathing-baffles *click* open and shut every forty minutes, I have a wedge of black basalt. I did not break it from the floor—to break the stone is as vulgar as tearing a leaf. I found it seven hundred yards away, at the base of the great northern scree-pile, where the initial collapse of the world left a litter of loose, razor-edged shards. It took me two winters of the upper world to carry it here, shifting my weight from root-knuckle to root-knuckle, never once letting my hip brush a glass-vine, never spilling a single leaf-cup as I passed beneath them. 

The stone is shaped like a broad, flat tooth. 

Over the next six hours, my right arm—the one reaching through the ginger-tubers—will finish its ascent. My vulcanized palm will find the underside of the tilted paddle-leaf's stem, right at the collar where it joins the main stalk. The wood there is stiff with silica, nearly as hard as bone, but it is still alive. It still remembers how to yield, if you ask it slowly enough.

I will not force it. If I force it, the fibers will crack, a bead of white sap will ooze out and harden into a permanent pearl of scar-tissue, and the leaf will die—not rotting, never rotting, just turning a brittle, translucent yellow that ruins the green equilibrium of the hollow. 

Instead, I will place the basalt tooth into the tight armpit of the stem. And then I will lean my weight against it. 

I will stay here, wedged between the black glass and the green wood, for three years. Perhaps four. 

I will let my heart beat its six-minute intervals. When a seed-pod pops in the mid-canopy, I will not go to it; there is a slow weep of resin oozing from a tension-crack in the tuber beside my cheek, and I can reach it with my tongue if I spend an afternoon turning my neck. The warm steam from the subterranean vent will coat my amber flank in tiny, diamond-bright beads, and when one rolls down the groove of my spine and reaches the small of my back, I will intercept it with my left thumb and bring it to my mouth. 

Mostly, I will just press. 

I will press until the living silica in the stem’s collar slowly, molecularly rearranges itself around the black stone wedge. I will press until the leaf rises by the thickness of a thumbnail. 

I know the exact moment I will stop. It will be when the four-hour *tack* ceases. 

Silence will pool in the cleft again, thick and hot and smells of baked pine. Above me, on the broad green paddle, the water will begin to gather in the center once more, held by its own surface tension, swelling into a heavy, quivering dome the size of a fist. It will stay there for days, magnifying the pale-green veins of the leaf beneath it, bending the yellow-white glare of the ceiling into a single, sharp caustic of light that will project down through the gap in the roots and strike the exact center of the dead builder’s dry stone room. 

When that bright spot hits the floor of his little house, I will let go of the wedge. The stem will hold it tight forever. 

Then I will take a week to fold my arm back down through the tubers. With my obsidian thumbnail, right below the old spiral on the rock, I will scratch a single, straight horizontal line across the basalt, cutting through the tail of his curve. 

*It is level again,* the mark will say. *The light is where he left it.*

And then I will begin the long, slow climb up into the mid-canopy, toward a spot three hundred yards away where four translucent vines cross each other at an angle that has bothered me since I was three hundred years old, and I will see if my knees still have enough amber in them to begin my own room.
