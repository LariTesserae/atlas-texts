I am not in the sun. If I were in the sun, I would already be dead, dried to a husk of spun glass and scattered across the ringing slate. 

I am three feet above the floor, wedged into the deep, V-shaped flute of a limestone needle where the shadow is permanent and the color of a bruise. I am a grey knot of seized joints, tucked so tightly against the stone that the rising breath of the floor-vents has glazed me over completely. There is a white fern-frond of rime growing directly out of my left ocular ridge. I cannot see through it, except as a diffuse, milky glare that tells me the sky above is beginning to burn.

My blood is solid—not frozen into the jagged crystals that burst soft meat, but thickened with lime and sweet, sluggish glycol until it has the consistency of cold resin. I do not think in this state. I only register pressure. I feel the microscopic tightening of the limestone behind me as the deep night-cold lingers in its core; I feel the slight, dragging weight of the ice collar building around my tucked legs, pulling me downward toward the scree by a fraction of a gram every hour.

And I feel the silence breaking. 

High up on the sunlit crowns of the needles, the frost is flashing into steam. A dry splinter of ice shears off a neighbor column and hits the floor—*clack*. The vibration travels up through the slate, into the base of my pillar, and hums against the flat undersides of my eight folded legs. 

Still, I do not move. The sun does not touch my crease of the rock. If I relied on the sky directly, I would stay locked in this blue glass until the weight of the rime ripped my claws from the stone and dropped me to shatter below. 

My life depends entirely on a hoop of green-crusted copper wire hanging six inches from my face.

It is suspended inside a tiny house of stacked slate shingles, lashed together with ancient, salt-crusted sinew and thick, grey wads of my ancestors’ silk. All morning, the damp breath from the floor-vents has been rising through that copper hoop, building a thick, lopsided plug of clear ice inside the ring. 

Now, the razor-edge of the noon sun slides over the lip of the pillar above us. 

The shaft of light misses me by two inches. It strikes the ice-plug in the copper hoop. Instantly, the rough lens catches the glare and bends it, driving a white-hot needle of focused ultraviolet light straight down onto the soot-blackened stone resting on the floor of the cage.

I do not see the light, and I cannot hear it, but within ten seconds, a wave of dry, radiant heat pushes out from that dark stone. It hits my frosted shell. 

The glycol in my core loosens first. A tiny, liquid pulse starts behind my eyes. Then the heat reaches the joints of my first pair of legs. With a sharp, dry *crick*, my right foreleg breaks out of its rime-casing, sending a shower of white frost-needles drifting down into the shadow. *Crick. Crick.* I do not unfold smoothly; I jerk open in jagged, incremental ticks, like a rusted lock being forced. The fern-frond of ice on my face snaps off as my plates shift, and suddenly the world fractures into a thousand blinding, overlapping prisms through my compound eyes.

I have twenty minutes.

I drop from the limestone flute onto the roof of the slate cage. Three others are already here—two small males, their flat, selenite-like legs flashing with milky internal fire as they accidentally dip a limb into the raw sunbeam, and a larger female whose carapace is scarred with a patch of dull, opaque calcium where a falling icicle struck her three noons ago. 

We do not fight. We have no venom, no pincers, no breath to waste on threat displays. We simply crowd to the very edge of the slate shingle, leaning over the eaves of the little cage, and spread our eight flat legs out toward the hot black stone below like pale, greedy hands warming at a hearth. 

My internal gel turns completely to liquid. The sluggish cold vanishes, replaced by a frantic, rattling hunger. 

I scuttle down the side of the cage—*tick, tick, tick*—my iron-hardened mouthparts already working. The ancient sinew holding the left corner of the cage together is frayed, reduced to a few brittle yellow fibers buried under a thick crust of mineral silk and white salt. I clamping my jaws onto the salt crust and scrape. The sound is loud inside my own head, a harsh, grating rasp as my copper-tipped mandibles plane off shavings of potash and calcium left by the night’s mist. I swallow them dry. They will go to my shell, to my silk glands, to the stiff mineral wires spinning inside my belly.

Above me, the ice-lens in the copper hoop is dying. The fierce sun is melting it from the edges inward. Heavy, blinding drops of water begin to fall from the bottom of the green wire, missing the hot stone and splashing into the shallow lead bowl hinged just beneath it. 

The Scarred Female abandons the salt-crust and drops onto the rim of the lead bowl. I follow her. The two males are right behind us. 

We do not want the water—we barely drink—we want the wet, chalky film of white lead-oxide that blooms on the metal whenever the mist touches it. It tastes sweet and heavy. We line up along the curved grey lip of the bowl, our flat legs overlapping, scraping furiously at the soft metal while the warm meltwater rises around our tarsi. 

The water level climbs. The bowl groans on its pale splint of bird-bone. 

I feel the shift in balance before it happens—a tiny, sickening tilt beneath my claws. The four of us together weigh less than a pinch of dust, but the bowl is full to the lip, and my last step toward a thick patch of oxide is the final grain of weight required.

The bowl tips. 

We leap upward in a silent, glittering panic of thirty-two pale legs, catching the copper hoop and the slate shingles just as the lead basin dumps its load of sun-warmed water down the rock flute below. Empty, the heavy bottom of the bowl swings back and strikes the slate floor of the cage.

*Thunk.*

The vibration shudders through the stone cage. And right on that vibration, a joint gives way.

The old sinew lashing at the upper right corner—scraped thin by generations of our jaws and brittle from ten thousand deep freezes—snaps. One of the flat slate roof-shingles slides half an inch to the left. As it shifts, it knocks against the twisted copper stem holding the hoop.

The hoop tilts downward.

Instantly, the focal point of the ice-lens swings off the black stone and shoots harmlessly out into the empty air between the pillars. 

Within three seconds, in the ferocious, heat-sucking vacuum of the high mountain shadow, the black stone begins to cool. 

We freeze in our tracks—not from the cold yet, but from an ancient, hardwired terror. The sun is still directly overhead, the basin is still a roaring furnace of golden, sublimating haze, the other cages around us are still clicking and knocking in their noon rhythm, but our hearth has gone out. Already, I can feel the glycol in my outermost leg-tips beginning to thicken, the joints losing their slick, warm snap.

If the hoop stays tilted, tomorrow’s ice-lens will form at the wrong angle. The black stone will stay cold. We will remain locked in the blue rime on the pillar three inches away while the sun passes over us, and by the fourth day, the weight of the unmelted ice will drag us down to the scree to shatter.

None of us knows what a lens is. None of us understands geometry. We only know: *the wire moved, and the warmth died.*

The Scarred Female reacts first. She does not retreat to the pillar. She scrambles up the slipping slate shingle, hooks her four rear claws into the rough stone, and reaches out with her four front legs to grab the green copper wire. 

She pulls. She weighs nothing; the copper wire is thick and stiff. It does not budge. Her flat, crystalline legs bow inward under the strain, flashing with stress-colors—violet and milky yellow—as the chitin bends near to breaking.

I tick across the slate and join her. The two small males clamber up beside us. 

Eighteen legs hook onto the copper hoop; fourteen legs anchor to the bone splint and the upper slate. We pull together, not by intelligence, but by the simple reflex of arthropods trying to drag a displaced rock back over their burrow. Our tiny iron-tipped jaws clamp onto the green verdigris for extra purchase. 

The copper wire does not bend, but it is only wedged loosely between two shingles of slate. Slowly, with a microscopic, gritty screech of metal against stone, the stem of the hoop pivots back by a fraction of an inch. 

The beam of focused light swings back across the floor of the cage and touches the edge of the black stone. 

A faint breath of heat rises to meet us. It is enough to keep our blood liquid for five more minutes.

Holding the wire in place with my front claws, I lift my abdomen. From my spinnerets, I press a droplet of thick, metallic fluid against the junction where the copper stem meets the bone splint. I draw my belly back. A thread of wet copper-and-lime silk stretches between the metal and the bone, stiffening the instant the dry mountain air hits it. 

The Scarred Female is doing the same. The two males are darting back and forth across the slipped shingle, laying down frantic, criss-crossing guidelines of mineral silk, binding the slate back to the petrified knot of old sinew. We are emptying ourselves. Every gram of salt and potash and lead we scraped during the first ten minutes of noon is being poured out of our glands to glue this broken clock back together. 

Our silk is grey and stiff as wire bristles. We lay down a hundred threads, two hundred, wrapping the green copper and the yellow bone in a fresh, wet bandage that immediately begins to catch the rising mist from the floor-vents, crusting over with a new skin of white salt.

When I finally let go of the copper hoop, it holds. It is a millimeter lower than it was yesterday, and tomorrow the beam will strike the black stone slightly off-center, near its eastern lip, but it will strike it. 

I am completely empty. My abdomen is shrunken, my plates loose, and I have not drunk. Worse, the sun has moved.

While we were lashing the hoop, the razor-edge of the shadow slid eastward by half an inch. The ice-lens in the ring is gone, melted away entirely, and the raw shaft of sunlight has passed over the roof of the cage and is now slicing away up the flank of the neighboring pillar. 

The black stone beneath us is already dead cold. 

The two small males do not make it back to the limestone flute. Their smaller bodies lose heat too fast; they simply tuck their legs in right there on the roof of the slate cage, settling down against the fresh silk bindings as the rising mist begins to glaze their shells in white. 

The Scarred Female and I manage three jerking, sluggish steps up the vertical stone before the gel seizes in our femurs. 

My last movement is to tuck my flat legs tightly against my flanks, overlapping them like the scales of a pinecone so the growing ice cannot pry them apart. My vision clouds over as a film of rime seals my ocular ridges. The last thing I register, before my blood turns back to glass and the twenty-two-hour night closes over my mind, is the faint, watery *thunk* of a distant lead bowl tipping in the eastern edge of the basin, and the slight, comforting tension of the new silk beneath my claws, holding the world in place until noon.
