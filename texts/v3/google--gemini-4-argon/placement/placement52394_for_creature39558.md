I would not be in the basin itself. To have a body in that room—even one as small as a beetle or a blind cave-shrimp—would be an intrusion, a tiny friction against a finished surface. 

Instead, I would be twelve feet behind the Keeper’s shoulder blades, inside the dark, unquarried mass of the limestone. I would be the tortuous, hair-thin network of capillaries and micro-fissures through which the water travels before it ever reaches the air.

In the dark of the stratum, there is no pewter twilight and no sound. There is only pressure and chemistry. 

Out on the surface, miles away and sixty feet up, a season of rain falls on a dead pine forest, or a decade of drought bakes the clay. By the time that event reaches me, its urgency is gone. It arrives simply as a faint, insistent leaning of the water table—a slow push that nudges a film of moisture forward through a million microscopic grains of compressed sea-mud and ancient shell.

I am the delay. I am the friction that turns weather into geology.

As the water presses through me, I give myself to it. The mildly acidic dampness eats at my walls, dissolving the pale calcium carbonate grain by grain, carrying my substance forward in invisible suspension. I am being slowly hollowed out, molecule by molecule, to feed the room at the end of the dark.

I do not know the room by sight, or even by the echo of its acoustics. I know it only as the place where the pressure suddenly drops to zero. 

For three months, or four years, I hold a single bead of water in a narrow throat of stone just behind the cliff face. I feel the weight of the hill above me; I feel the slight, rhythmic tightening of the rock when the moon passes overhead, pulling at the crust of the earth. And when the drop finally slips past my last crystalline lip and emerges into the warm, motionless air of the amphitheater, I feel the faint, elastic recoil of its release.

Some of my channels end high in the ceiling, sixty feet up, where the water gathers on the bulging overhang, swells, and lets go. I do not hear the *plip* in the pool below, but a fraction of a second after the drop falls, a tiny, bell-like tremor strikes the bedrock floor and travels Instantly back up through the stone, passing through my walls as a faint, crisp shiver. That is how I know the depth of the basin is true.

Other channels of mine do not meet the air at all. They end against the warm, dense, porous rind of the Keeper’s back. 

When the Keeper is sealed to the rear wall, there is no boundary between us. My fissures continue right across the threshold of its skin. I press the dissolved lime of the ancient hills directly into the slow, waiting matrix of its shoulders. I am the quiet, blind root-system feeding the silence of its vigil; I am the mortar that glazes its spine back into the cliff when its work is done, and the slow, mineral tear that rises to clear its green eyes when the chords align.

And when the Keeper cracks its joints and stands—once in a long age—to go down and grind the lip of the pool with its thumb, I feel the sudden, cool exposure of the rock face where its body used to be. For the days it spends kneeling on the silt floor, my water weeps out into the empty indentation of its spine, laying down a fresh, microscopic skin of slick travertine, smoothing the mold, waiting for the weight to return and seal the dark again.
