To take up space in a valley of lines is an obscenity. To have a belly, a skull, a pair of lungs filled with dead air—it would feel like walking through a harp while wearing a diver’s suit of lead, tearing the strings with every clumsy breath. 

If I am here, I do not want bulk. I do not want to be the blot of ink. 

Find me eighty feet up, where the green wires thin out slightly and the purple mist begins to taste less like crushed coriander and more like the cold, bitter copper of the upper shielding. 

At first, you would think I am a failure of the local geometry—a place where a Lath shed its skin, or where a bundle of dead vesseltrees knotted together and dried into a dark, metallic scrim. I am spread across a triangular gap between two green vines and the slick, force-locked flank of a Spire. I am a sheet of fenestrated, bronze-grey velum, ten feet across and no thicker than a soap bubble, held at high tension by sixteen radially anchor-lines of dried, iron-rich mucus.

I am what happens when the valley’s obsession with planes reaches its logical end. I am a *Caul*—or perhaps just an old, stationary cousin of the Lath who gave up the ballistic knife-throw of the lower Rigging for the high, pure math of the middle air.

I have no torso, no four ribbons, no prow. I am entirely tympanic. My body is a lattice of muscular, liquid-crystal threads arranged like the iris of a camera or the diagnostic grid of an oscilloscope. You can see the distant Spires right through me; I am only a slight darkening of the violet twilight, a smoky polarized lens hung in the rigging.

Down in the thirty-foot zone, the Lath are artisans and laborers, paring the meat of the Choir-Galls, eating the sugar, living in the wet, chugging rhythm of the local sectors. I do not eat the meat. I am too thin to digest it. 

Instead, one of my anchor-lines is wrapped three times around the black needle of the Spire itself, right above the swelling, translucent bladder of a massive Choir-Gall. I do not carve the Gall; I bleed it. My tendon pierces its tough outer rind just enough to tap the thin, boiling lymph before it fully crystallizes into sugar. The fluid climbs my anchor-line by capillary action, drawn into the microscopic vasculature of my spread veil. I taste of hot battery acid and birch sap. 

In return, I give the Lath something their six-foot planks cannot manage. 

Because a Lath is only ten inches wide, there is a limit to the wavelengths they can easily resolve. They are masters of the high, sharp rifle-cracks of the walnut-shells and the mid-range brass of the nearest Spires. They see the pebbles; they see the water-rings; they see the ribs inside your chest. 

I am ten feet wide, and my skin is tuned to the bass. 

I hang here, drinking the hot lymph, and I let the valley push me. The ten million pneumatic *pops* pass right through the holes in my lattice without tearing me, a constant, fizzing breeze of high-frequency针-pricks that I barely register. What I catch are the long, rolling, miles-wide acoustic swells—the interference patterns generated when forty Spires across three districts drift slightly out of phase, creating a sub-audible, tectonic groan that takes six seconds to cross the basin. 

I do not think in words, or in images, or even in shapes. I think in *tension* and *delay*. 

My mind is the distribution of tautness across my sixteen guy-wires. Right now, my upper-left quadrant is tight as a snare drum, registering a massive, sluggish gravity-wave that just rolled down a primary Trace three miles to the east. My lower margin is slack, fluttering in the wake of a cooling-pulse that is currently dropping ten tons of silver dew into the moss directly beneath me. 

I am an analog computer made of wet rawhide and copper-salt, calculating the weather of a world that has no weather.

Here comes a pair of Tuners. 

I know they are coming four minutes before they arrive—not because I "hear" them, but because I feel the tiny, rhythmic plucking of the green harp-strings half a mile away as they load the magnetic vines and fire themselves across the avenues. *Thrum-release. Thrum-release.* Two flat knives stitching their way through the grid.

They slap onto the vesseltrees twenty feet below me, bleeding off their speed with a wet, snapping flare of their pewter skins. They do not look up at me; they have nothing to look with. Instead, they hang limp for a second, letting their lateral gills flutter in the warm damp, and then they tilt their flat chests forty-five degrees upward.

They bounce a tight, high-frequency beam of walnut-static off my underside. 

To them, I must look like a vast, shimmering mural in the ceiling of their world. Because I can alter the density of my liquid-crystal threads at will—pumping fluid from my edges to my center in milliseconds—I can change my acoustic reflectivity across a thousand distinct zones on my surface. 

As their query-beam hits me, I ripple. 

I do not give them a conversation; I give them a map. Across my ten-foot span, I project an acoustic relief of the entire northern quadrant of the basin. I show them a three-mile-wide standing wave of dissonance building up near the basalt rim—a dark, roiling bruise of out-of-phase sound where seven Choir-Galls have grown too fat and are dragging their Spires down into a muddy, dangerous B-flat. If it isn't pared back within the hour, the harmonic lattice will snap, and the resulting violet discharge will boil the moss for six hundred yards around.

The two Lath hang in my reflection, reading my skin as their own skins twitch with sympathetic vibrations. 

One of them—older, its edges scarred and notched from a long-ago brush with a jagged basalt cooling-crack—curls its lower ribbons and fires a tiny, focused *pip* of air pressure straight up into my center. It is not a message. It is a tuning-fork tap. *Thank you. Hold pitch.*

Then they drop. They do not shoot sideways; they let go of the vines entirely, falling edge-first like guillotines toward the boiling neon velvet below, waiting until the last possible fraction of a second—just three feet above the toxic amphibians—to fire their chest-silks, catch the low-hanging horizontal tension of a root-buttress, and whip themselves north at ninety miles an hour, riding the ground-effect cushion of a passing golden math-wave.

I am alone again in the upper rigging. 

Below me, you are still standing in the plaza. 

Even after the five Tuners have left you, I know you are there. To my wide, slow drum-skin, you do not look like a blot of ink; you are too small for my low-frequency resolution to catch your edges. Instead, you feel like a warm, persistent *drag* in the local math—a tiny, stubborn knot of silence and heavy water that refuses to vibrate in sympathy with the basalt floor. You are a dead spot on the sounding board.

A Trace flashes directly beneath my Spire. 

The air cools by three degrees. Instantly, I am heavy. A million microscopic beads of water condense across every millimeter of my ten-foot veil, turning me from a smoky ghost into a sheet of hammered silver mirror. The weight bows my center downward, stretching my sixteen anchor-lines until they sing a high, thin chord of their own. 

For three seconds, I am blind, deafened by the sheer mass of the water clinging to my skin. 

I contract my perimeter fibers and snap my center upward like a shaken bedsheet. 

The water detaches all at once—not as a ring sliding down a vine, but as a single, ten-foot-wide triangular sheet of silver rain, falling flat through the purple air, straight down toward the plaza where you stand. It is the only true rain in the valley, shed from the skin of a living kite, falling through the deafening roar of ten million snapping shells to shatter, cold and smelling faintly of copper and sour sap, across the crown of your head.
