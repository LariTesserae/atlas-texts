I am down in the murky amber dark, where the city forgets how to breathe. 

Everywhere above me, the metropolis is a mania of addition—the frantic, blind amphipods laying down their milk-white geometry, the icy cyan spires racing toward the black lipid sky, the ribbon-worms pumping their violet veins in a desperate, syrupy waltz. The Archon’s great mind is currently obsessed with the perimeter, pouring its panic into those heavy, acid-weeping sacs along the basalt wall, trying to teach the crabs how to mine.

It does not pay much attention to me. Or rather, I am the part of its attention that is turned entirely inward, toward the dead weight at its heels. 

I am the *Abscission*. I am the line where the Archon stops being a creature and becomes a fossil.

If you could peer through the narrow, choking street-canyons of the seventh tier—just above the solid, calcified block that used to be the city’s first foundations—you would see me not as a node or a sac, but as a pale, retreating tide. I am a collar of specialized, thick-walled filaments wrapped around the descending main-stems of the colony. My glow is not the sharp cyan of the nurseries or the bruised violet of the Alchemists; I burn with a dull, smoldering rust-color, the exact hue of dying phosphorus.

My work is amputation. 

The mineral plumes from the floor fissure are relentless. Down here, the aragonite frost does not just coat the buildings; it bridges the streets. Vaulted plazas that once held ten thousand pumping worms have narrowed to the width of a needle, then sealed shut entirely. The stone is closing in like a slow, white fist. 

If the Archon’s living tissue were trapped in that closing fist, it would rot. And in a sealed, three-cubic-meter pocket of syrup, rot is fatal; a single decaying zooid sealed in a crystal tomb would sour the deep water, breeding anaerobic bacteria that would creep up the core-shafts and poison the wells. The Archon cannot afford to leave a corpse behind.

So I eat the past.

Right now, I am wrapped around an ancient cephalic bell in the lower eastern ward. Centuries ago, this bell was the vibrant heart of a major intersection, pulsing out ester-heavy commands to build the great cantilevered viaducts overhead. Now, the chamber around it has frosted over until the crystal needles are actually pricking its gelatinous mantle. The aqueducts leading out of its vault are pinched so narrow that the lone ribbon-worm left outside can barely undulate, its violet vein dimming for lack of oxygen. 

The bell is obsolete. Its chemical voice can no longer reach the upper tiers. 

I tighten my rust-colored threads around its stem. I do not use the harsh sulfuric acid of the perimeter sacs; my chemistry is quiet, internal, and solvent. I inject a specialized autolytic enzyme into the bell’s central cavity. Within hours, the ancient thinker dissolves from the inside out, liquefying into a rich, dense broth of lipids, amino acids, and recycled phosphorus. 

I drink it all back in. I draw the ghost of the bell up through my hollow, glassy tubes, pulling its salvaged substance six inches higher, out of the choking frost and into the seventh tier, where the water still moves. When nothing is left of the bell but a microscopic smear of indigestible chitin, I pinch my own filament shut below me, sealing the stem with a tiny plug of hardened wax. 

Seconds later, a stray drift of supersaturated brine settles into the empty vault, and the aragonite crystals bridge the gap. Where a mind hung for four hundred years, there is now only solid, milk-white marble. Not a single calorie was wasted. The tomb is clean.

This is how it feels to be me: a constant, slow-motion upward falling. 

While the Cartographer-threads above feel the terrifying proximity of the oil-ceiling, I feel the immense, crushing upward push of the floor. I am the only part of the Archon that remembers the shape of the lower city, because I had to dismantle it room by room. My chemistry holds the recycled memory of the very first spire ever built on the raw basalt—a clumsy, crooked little tower laid down when the amphipods were still wild and poorly trained, before the Archon perfected its sweet, addictive exudate. I drank the filaments that lived in that first tower, and pumped them into the second; I drank the second, and pumped them into the tenth. 

I am the reason the upper city glows so brightly. The icy cyan fire at the top of the world is fueled by the salvage of the dark below.

Lately, though, my work has changed. The Archon’s new obsession at the outer walls—the acid, the black silt—is reaching me.

Because the ribbon-worms are still pumping, the microscopic curls of dissolved basalt from the perimeter are getting caught in the sluggish lower currents. For the first time in ten thousand generations, there is dirt in the water. 

It drifts into my quiet, amber-lit demolition zones: tiny, jagged specks of black iron and magnesium silicate, heavier than the white calcite. The amphipods do not know what to do with it. It fouls their mucus; it ruins the pristine, razor-edged geometry of their bricks. When they accidentally cement a speck of black silt into a closing wall, the bioluminescence around it sputters and dies, leaving a tiny, dark pockmark in the blueprint.

As the crystal walls close in around me, they are no longer pure bone-white. They are streaked with gray smudges, like smoke trapped in ice. 

I am absorbing something new, too. As I dissolve the lowest, oldest filaments near the edge of the marble block, my enzymes are picking up trace amounts of the raw iron leached from the etched basalt. It is traveling up my rust-colored threads, mixing with the salvaged lipids of the old minds. 

I am pumping this strange, heavy, metallic broth up toward the Alchemists in the middle tiers. They do not know it yet, up there in their violet cathedrals, nor do the Harvesters pressing their flat mouths against the black oil sky. They think they are merely digging a trench to survive. They do not realize that by eating the rock wall, they are changing the chemistry of the blood I send them. 

The white city is ending. As I seal off the last vaults of the sixth tier, retreating millimeter by millimeter up the shafts, I can feel the new density in the fluid I pass upward. The next generation of spires will not be pure aragonite. They will be dark, heavy, and veined with iron. 

I pinch off another stem. Behind me, the white stone clicks shut in the absolute silence. Ahead of me, in the dim amber glow, the black snow begins to fall.
