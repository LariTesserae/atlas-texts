I am not on the rim. The wool trousers, the iron weight of the cold in the knees, the eyes that have to adjust to the slowness—that belongs to a traveler passing across the plain, someone whose heart beats seventy times a minute and who measures time in hours. If I am here, I cannot be someone who leaves.

At first, the pull is toward the flyers under the eaves—to be the thing with eyes, the tragic witness trapped in the blinding glare, watching the masonry shift like the hour hand of a clock while the brown fat burns down to nothing between my shoulder blades. There is a familiar ache in that: the wide, filmed-over stare of an outsider caught in a cellar of slow time. 

Look lower. 

Down past the slick slate roofs, past the four-inch alleys where the masons press their snouts together, down through the teacup rooms lined with tallow-rime, past the calcified mothers wedged in their stone oysters, and deeper still, beneath the cellar-warrens where the fresh clay is chewed.

I am the throat itself. 

I am the narrow, crooked flue in the bedrock beneath the great beast’s ribs—the thermal vein that remembers when the world was hot, though I have forgotten the word for *hot* and now only know *upward*.

I do not have eyes, or horn claws, or a heart that beats even once an hour. I have pressure, and I have mineral, and I have a slow, wet sigh that takes a winter to rise from the deep shale to the mud-floor of the pit. 

In the beginning, I was a violence. I boiled the groundwater into a white plume that tore through the grey clay plain; I scalded the sky; I cooked the flesh off the great heavy-boned beast that stumbled into my mud-pot, stripping its ribs clean and settling them into the silt like a basket. The small, fast things with badgers’ blood came then, drawn by the wet heat in a freezing world, and they stacked their river-slate around my mouth to keep the warmth from blowing away on the plain’s wind.

Then I began to choke on my own richness. Century by century, the dissolved lime and silica and pale tallow-clay settled out of my rising water, plating my own rock walls with travertine and slick white grease. My throat narrowed from a roaring chimney to a pipe, and from a pipe to a weeping hairline crack crooked through twenty fathoms of cold stone.

Now, I am very quiet. I am the reason the water does not turn to bone.

I feel the city not as an image, but as a ceiling of immense, intricate weight. I know the beast-bones sitting on my lip; their marrow-cavities were filled with my calcite two thousand years ago, so that they are no longer bone, really, but my own stone fingers reaching up into the light. I feel the millions of tiny, river-rolled slates pressing down on the clay, locked into their load-bearing truce. When a mason up in the milky glare tamps a plug of chewed mortar into a frost-crack with its blunt snout, the microscopic shockwave travels down through four feet of wedged stone and dense root-mat until it reaches me as a faint, dull *thrum* in the mud. 

I am what they are mining when they descend into the pitch-black waterlogged honeycomb at the bottom of the city. 

A tett comes down into my dark. It is blind, so the dark changes nothing for it, though the air here is thick with my iron-and-sulfur breath, forty-five degrees and heavy as broth. I feel its heavy, shovel-clawed feet sink into the soft, warm clay that I have spent fifty years pushing up through the fissure. I feel the slow, hydraulic brace of its legs. I feel the sideways shear of its horn jaws as it bites a walnut-sized plug out of my slick white grease. 

It sits there in my warmth for six hours, chewing me, mixing my prehistoric dust with its thick, glycerol-heavy spit. We are trading substance. My minerals go up into the glare to hold the slate against the prying cabbages; their bodies, when the joints lock with lime and the claws wear down to the quick, come back to the bone-ring around my mouth and become the lip of my chimney. 

I am rising through them. 

Every generation, they mortar the stiffened, leathery shells of their elders into the well-wall above me, and every generation, my warm breath climbs another half-inch inside a tube made of petrified ribs and calcified masons. 

I do not know about the white sky, or the silver greasewood canopy, or the herders who walk past thirty paces away thinking the earth has gone sour. I know only that the cold above is an enormous, flat iron anvil pressing down on the world, and that I am the single, stubborn thread of forty-five-degree breath pushing back against it, holding open a pocket the size of a courtyard so that the slugs can varnish the slate, and the spiders can drink their broth, and the blind masons can take a week to mend a wall.
