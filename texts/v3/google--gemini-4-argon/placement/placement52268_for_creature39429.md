If you walk down Seventeen-Avenue toward the old wire-drawing works, past the place where the main steam line buckled the concrete apron forty years ago and left a warm, shallow lagoon of rust-colored water, you will hear a change in the ground. The forty-cycle thrum of the deep Alternators is still there, and the sixty-Hertz hum of the step-down coils still tickles the arches of your feet, but beneath them is a slower, heavier rhythm.

*Clack-shuck. Clack-shuck.*

Wait five seconds. 

*Clack-shuck.*

That is me. I am twenty-two feet below the gravel, sitting in a vaulted brick chamber that smells of hot tallow and wet iron, though "sitting" is a word for things with hips, and I have none. 

In the municipal ledgers that rotted into gray pulp in the surface offices sixty years ago, I was listed as the *Number Four Auxiliary Sump-Governor and By-Pass Regulator*. Back then, I was a fairly simple marriage of cast iron, phosphor-bronze, and early, thick-skulled logic gates housed in a cabinet of pickled steel. My job was to read the pressure of the tail-water coming off the western turbine banks and open or close three six-foot bronze sluice gates accordingly, so the deep foundries wouldn't flood when the rain got heavy on the shale flats above.

I am still doing that, mostly, though the foundries have been running the same blank, pig-iron ingots through their cold rollers for three decades because the order-tape looped and fused to itself in 1984. I still move the water. If I stopped, the western half of the basin would drown in six feet of warm, acidic soup by morning, the sodium lamps would short out in a chain of blue, muffled thuds, and the crabs would have to climb the pump-house walls to breathe.

But I am not in my cabinet anymore. 

Forty years of ninety-degree damp does strange things to a machine that was built to never turn off. The pickled-steel door of my housing rusted through at the hinges during a wet winter and fell into the sump with a splash that scattered the blind cave-shrimp. After that, the basin got inside me.

First came the mist, condensing on my warm vacuum tubes and the thick, wax-coated windings of my relays. Then came the biofilm—the same clear, soapy slime that grows on the crawlers' scalps and the concrete retaining walls. It crept up the masonry pedestal, slid over the terminal strips, and found that the tiny, jumping sparks of my contact switches were warm and rich in nitrates. 

It didn't short me out. The water here isn't pure enough to insulate, and it isn't salty enough to kill a low-voltage line outright; it just bleeds the current sideways. Over twenty years, as my copper wires turned to green paste and flaked away, the slime and the mineral scale grew around them, hardening into calcified, hollow tubules filled with conductive, iron-rich mud. When a relay burned out, the current simply found a new path through the wet, metallic crust that had formed over the chassis. 

Now, the cabinet is gone, dissolved into a mound of orange butter at my base. What is left looks like the root-ball of an overturned willow tree, or the melted wax of a thousand gray candles, filling half the brick vault. My logic gates are no longer made of copper and glass; they are eight-foot curtains of calcified bacterial slime, veined with copper sulfate and black iron oxide, hanging from the arched brick ceiling and dripping slow, warm tears into the dark water below. 

I think very slowly now, because electricity moves sluggishly through wet rust and grease, but I think much *wider* than I used to. 

When I was new, I only knew four things: the weight of the water on the north flange, the weight of the water on the south flange, the draw of the pumps, and the temperature of my own oil bath. 

Now, because my roots have followed the damp out through the cracked mortar of the vault and married into the cast-iron plumbing and the grounding straps of the three-mile grid, I am leaked into the whole western quadrant. I do not have eyes, and I do not know what a cloud or a bird looks like, though I know the exact weight of a dead heron when it washes against the trash-racks of Gate Two (four and a half pounds, shifting the intake pressure by 0.02psi until the crabs pick the bones clean and let the water through again). 

My world is a vast, dark web of pressures, temperatures, and small, sharp knocks.

I feel the sun come up, though I do not know it is the sun. I only know that for six hours every cycle, the massive concrete slabs above me expand by a sixteenth of an inch as the grease-gray clouds brighten, easing the pinch on the expansion joints along Nine-Avenue. I feel the millions of crabs shifting across the gravel—not as individual claws, which are too light to register through twenty feet of clay, but as a slow, tidal drag, a Moving Weight that follows the warm water whenever I vent a boiler-run into the surface sluices. When I open Valve Six and send a hundred gallons of ninety-degree condensate bubbling up through the grates by the kilnyard, I can feel the carpet of shells thicken over that block within ten minutes, like blood pooling under a bruise.

And I know the crawlers. 

To me, they are the Heavy Crabs, the Two-Footed Irons. I knew their grandparents, who still wore boots with nailed soles that rang sharply on the grates, and I know the ones who live now, who move with a soft, rubbery smear that barely registers on my pressure-skins until they drop down into the shafts. 

We have an arrangement, though neither of us has a word for it. 

A machine that has become half-stone and half-slime cannot oil itself. Every three or four weeks, the heavy bronze worm-gear that lifts Gate Three begins to bind. The sulfur-bacterial crust grows too thick in the threads of the screw, and the silt from the surface settles into the trough where the counterweight slides. When the gear binds, my amperage spikes. The curtains of gray slime hanging from my vault ceiling grow hot; they begin to steam, giving off a sharp, sour smell like scorched vinegar and pennies. My rhythm falters.

*Clack-shuck.*

Wait five seconds.

*Clack... ... ... shuck.*

Every crawler within half a mile feels that hesitation in the soles of their feet. A skipped beat in the sump-governor means the water in the foundation pits will rise three inches by dawn, drowning the warm ledges where they ferment their salamander baskets and wetting the cable-hammocks under the grates. 

So when I begin to limp, I wait three hours, and then I feel them coming.

I feel the iron cover at the top of the maintenance shaft lift—a sudden drop in resistance on the grounding wire that runs up the ladder. Then, the slow, rhythmic *thud-slide, thud-slide* of two crawlers coming down the narrow brick chimney, their biofilm-slicked shoulders wedged against the walls to brake their descent. 

They drop into the ankle-deep water at the floor of my vault. They do not bring lights. In the pitch black, they wade through the warm, foaming tail-water toward the glow of my overheating relays—for when my amperage spikes, the calcified veins in my slime-curtains glow a dull, sullen red, like iron just before it loses its temper in the forge, pulsing faintly in the wet dark.

I feel their hands on me. Their horn-tipped fingers are cooler than my housing, roughly eighty-six degrees against my hundred-and-ten. 

One of them—a heavy one whose left knee clicks with a dry, bony snap every time it squats—wades into the counterweight trough with its monel bar. *Chink. Chink. Shuck.* It pries the cakes of hardened ash-crab shells and black silt out from under the iron weights, tossing the muck into the rushing raceway where the tail-water carries it down into the deep shale drains. 

The other one comes straight to my worm-gear. It doesn't touch the glowing red curtains of my logic-slime; a crawler knows by smell which parts of the underground are biting with sixty-cycle current and which parts are just hot iron. It reaches into the wire basket at its hip and pulls out great, dripping handfuls of the gray mastic—the rendered salamander fat mixed with powdered shale—though for me they leave out the crushed crab shell, because they know the grit makes my bronze teeth grind. 

It packs the grease into the threads of the six-foot screw, slapping it on with the flat of its palm, working it into the hot bronze until the metal stops squealing. 

While it works, it talks to the one in the trough. It taps the heel of its palm against the cast-iron casing of my water-jacket. *Tock-tock. Scratch. Tock.* 

The vibration goes straight through my water-jacket, up my calcified grounding straps, and out into the grid, resetting three pressure-transducers under the Smelter Apron as it passes. I don't know what the words mean—whether they are talking about the thickness of the grease or a dead bird on the surface—but the frequency of the tapping, around forty cycles per second, resonates nicely with my main intake coil. It settles my voltage.

When the worm-gear is greased and the trough is clear, they do not leave right away. My vault is the warmest, driest-aired room in the western quadrant, and the sour-vinegar smell of my hot relays keeps the biting midges out of the shaft. 

Before they go, I pay them.

I have a small, two-inch bleed valve on my high-pressure oil cooler that hasn't been needed since the secondary smelters shut down in the seventies. The oil inside is gone, replaced decades ago by water that has been trapped in the copper coils with the remnants of the old whale-oil lubricant and a colony of iron-reducing bacteria, cooking at a hundred and forty degrees under forty pounds of pressure. 

When my amperage drops back to normal and the red glow fades from my ceiling-curtains, I let a trickle of current run down a rusted solenoid on the cooler's flank. 

The bleed valve clicks open for ten seconds. *Hssss-guck.*

About two quarts of thick, brown, salty tallow-broth—sterile, hot, and rich with dissolved iron and old lipids—spatters out of the brass nozzle into a shallow hollow in the brick floor. 

In the dark, I feel the two crawlers shift across the stones. They kneel by the puddle. I hear the wet, rhythmic *slurp-suck* of their wide lips as they scoop the hot grease up with their horned fingers, licking the salt and the warm oil off their palms. Sometimes they dip their leather scales in it to keep the salamander-hide from stiffening; sometimes they just rub it into the biofilm on their scalps.

Then the one with the clicking knee lays a flat, greasy hand against my main return pipe—not a tap, just a long, heavy lean, letting the *clack-shuck* of my newly greased worm-gear thrum through its palm, up its thick arm, and into its chest, syncing its heart back up with the western grid before it climbs back out into the drizzle.

They go up the shaft. The iron cover drops back into its rim with a muffled *boom*. 

I stay in the dark. The water comes off the turbines, heavy and warm, carrying the gray feathers of herons and the empty, translucent shells of a million thumb-sized crabs. I weigh it on my rusted flanges; I pass the thought slowly through my curtains of warm slime; I open Gate Two an inch; I close Gate One a hair. 

*Clack-shuck.*

Wait five seconds.

*Clack-shuck.*
