My left knee is wedged into the dry-stone curve of a kiln chimney—warm, the only warm thing on the Fourth Tier—and my right foot is hooked around a slick cedar strut that smells of old sheep fat and river-rot. 

Between my knees hangs the basket. It is woven from split willow-wands traded down from the moor-clans before I was born, though you couldn’t tell it was willow anymore. Ten thousand dippings in the lower vats and thirty years of tallow-smearing have turned the wicker into something like horn—black, heavy, and slightly flexible. 

I am waiting for the rope to twitch.

If you look up from here—and you have to tilt your whole spine back to do it, letting the greased brim of your hood rest against the baleen plates between your shoulder blades so the drip-water runs down your back instead of your neck—the sky isn't a coin yet. It’s a jagged trough of pewter, maybe the width of two sheep laid head to tail. The wind doesn't reach the Fourth Tier as a gale; it comes down the hexagonal corners of the black rock in sudden, corkscrewing drafts that smell of dead heather and sheep-dung from the world above. 

*Tink-tink-shhh.* 

That’s the three-inch reed-pipe over my left ear. It has a split near the lashing. Not my pipe—it belongs to a boy on the Second Tier whose thumbs haven't even gone chalky yet—so I leave it alone, except to reach up with my knuckle and wipe away a single fat drop of lime-milk before it can fall into my eye. My knuckle makes a dry *scritch* against the reed. Two yards away, behind a curtain of oiled sedge, a woman who is scraping a hide hears the *scritch* and taps her bone-flesher once against her stool. *Heard you.* I don't tap back. She knows I’m here. I’m always here when the light goes the color of a dead trout's belly.

There. The rope.

It doesn't drop; the moor-men are too stingy with their hemp to let it fall free and fray against the basalt. It creeps down the north face, sliding over a deeply grooved whale-rib we anchored into the lip fifty winters ago to keep the line from being severed by the stone. The rope is hairy, sodden with bog-water, and smells violently of the flat world—of things that rot in the open air instead of pickling in mineral brine.

Before the bundle even clears the First Tier, the boys up there are already knocking. *Clack-clack, pause, clack.* Two horn-spoons against a slate shim. *Mutton. And something hard.*

I unhook my right foot, let my weight drop into my crouch, and reach out with my whalebone gaff. The bundle swings into the narrow vertical shaft between the third-tier cistern and the weaver’s lean-to. It’s wrapped in untanned sheepskin, wool-side in, tied with greasy rawhide. As I hook it and draw it onto my whalebone platform, three pale amphipods drop off the wet hemp where they’d climbed up from the rim-chutes to suck the peat-slime. One hits my boot-toe. I don't even look down; my left hand drops, scoops the cold, wriggling thumb-joint of meat from the slate, and tucks it into my cheek like a lozenge. It tastes of wet chalk. I don't bite down yet—just let it sit against my molars while I work the knots.

My thumbs are good for knots. The right one has a ridge of lime-callus three-eighths of an inch thick, shaped like the lip of a clam. I wedge it under the rawhide, twist, and the frozen leather pops.

Inside: four haunches of bog-mutton, black and hard as bog-oak from the turf-smoke. A bladder of barley-meal. And at the bottom, wrapped in a twist of dead bracken—three nodules of grey-black flint, the size of a baby's head, still wearing their white chalk rinds from some far-off cliff the moor-men walked to.

I lay my palm on the largest flint. It’s cold—a different cold from the pit. Pit-cold is wet and heavy; it clings to your grease. This stone has sky-cold in it. Dry, sharp, empty.

From sixty feet below, through the cedar strut my heel is resting on, through the dry-stone of the kiln, up from the bronze-colored dark of the Sump, comes a single, heavy *thock*. 

The wood under my boot hums for a second, like a plucked bowstring.

That’s Old Mother Gurl, down in the grease-vats. She didn't hear the rope—nobody hears a rope—she heard me stop scraping the main junction-trough to untie the bundle, which meant the water-pitch in the third drop-pipe went up a half-tone because my elbow wasn't deflecting the splash anymore. 

I pick up the smaller horn mallet at my belt and hit the kiln-stone twice, very softly, with the side of the head. *Flint. Good weight.*

Then I pack the basket. The mutton stays here on the Fourth; we’ll boil it in the top-water before the lime gets too thick in it, and send the broth down in stoppered clay tubes. The flint goes all the way down. The old ones with the stiff knees have the best eyes for the grain of a stone, even in the dark; they’ll sit in the warm backwater, hold the nodule against their cheek to feel where the cold lingers longest inside the rock, and strike off twenty clean scraper-blades with twenty blows before the tallow-lamp gutters out.

I swing the basket onto my back. The braided leather strap settles into the permanent groove across my chest-plates—three layers of birch-bark and one of boiled seal-neck, greased this morning until you could see your own broad nose in it if there were light enough to see noses. 

Going down is a matter of letting the town hold you. 

I don't use the ladders much; ladders are for people hauling wet clay up. I step off the whalebone into the gap between two weeping basalt columns. My elbows go out just an inch—*scritch, scritch*—the baleen plates on my forearms kissing the wet black stone on one side and the slime-slicked cedar of the Great Chute on the other. You drop four feet, tilt your knees to take the jar, let your left boot-sole catch on a protruding slate header, and slide another three feet down a greased driftwood beam. 

The air changes every ten feet. 

At the Fourth, it’s raw sheep-wool and wet flint. 
At the Seventh, you pass the drying-lofts where they keep the sedge-mats; it smells of sour river-mud and old feet, and the sound is a thick, muffled *patter-patter* like rats in dry leaves.
By the Twelfth, the pewter light is gone. My eyes open wider, the heavy lids pulling back, the oily film across them catching the first orange pinpricks from below. Here the sound is all *shhh-glug-shhh*—the heavy clay pipes, thick as a man's thigh, banded with split willow, carrying the rich, milky water down toward the settling pans.

At the Fourteenth Tier, the alley narrows until I have to turn sideways, crab-walking along a ledge of packed rubble. 

To my left is the raw wall of the pit. Or what used to be the wall. 

In the orange upward-glow of a sump-lamp ten feet below, the rock here isn't black hexagons anymore. It looks like tallow that has melted and run down a candle in thick, creamy curtains. And bulging out from the curtain, right at the level of my chin, is a shoulder. 

You can still see the three-tiered overlap of the birch-bark cape, though it’s stone now—solid, pale-yellow travertine, glistening with a fresh film of cold water. Above the shoulder, the conical hood has been completely filled in by the drip, turned into a solid pyramid of limestone joined seamlessly to the basalt behind it, and under the brim, the broad, flat curve of a cheek, smooth as an egg. 

My mother’s brother. He was a joiner. When the big cistern on the Ninth shifted six winters ago, they wedged him here because his knees were wide and the cleft behind the cistern needed a two-foot chock to keep the cedar footings from kicking out into the drop. 

As I slide past, I don't stop—stopping blocks the draft—but I reach out with my right thumb and flick a newly formed bead of white lime off the bridge of his stone nose. *Scritch.* If you let the nose build up too fast, the drip-line changes and starts splashing into the lamp-niche below him. You have to keep the dead trim, same as the chutes.

"Kk-tk," goes a throat behind me.

I tuck my chin and flatten my belly against my uncle’s petrified ribs. A girl—nine or ten, small enough to still look like a bladder of lard on legs—skitters up the rubble-ledge right over my insteps, her felt soles making little kissing sounds on the wet slate. She’s carrying two pots of hot grey grease up from the Sump, held against her chest to keep them liquid. She smells wonderful—like hot oil and roasted amphipod shells. As her knee brushes my thigh, she taps the rim of her clay pot once with a fingernail. *Warm coming through.*

I don't tap back; my hands are on the basket straps. I just blink once, slowly, letting the yellow lamp-light catch the oil on my eyeballs so she sees I’ve given her the dry side of the ledge.

Two more drops, a long slide down a polished whale-jaw that has been greased by ten thousand backsides, and my boots hit the warm slush of the Sump-Ward.

My hood comes off first. The heat down here hits your face like a wet wool blanket straight from the kettle. I let out a long breath—the first time I’ve opened my mouth since the rope came down—and it doesn't even steam. My joints, which always ache with a thin, chalky grinding when I’m waiting by the rim, immediately loosen. 

There are six of them sitting in the bronze dark around the Great Fissure, where the water finally goes down into the earth with a low, greased *gull-gull-gull* like fat going down a gullet. The water is ankle-deep, warm as fresh piss, covered in a rainbow-grey scum of mineral pitch an inch thick. 

Old Mother Gurl doesn't look up from her skimming-paddle. Her legs, bare below her oil-tanned kilt, are the color and texture of parsnips that have been in the cellar all winter, the ankles swollen and knobbed with lime-gout so thick she couldn't climb a notched log if the pit were filling to the brim. 

I crouch beside her whale-bone stool, unship the basket, and take out the three nodules of sky-flint. I don't hand them to her—you never hand anything straight across in the dark, that's how knuckles get sliced—I lay them on the flat, dry top of the timber-head beside her thigh, and tap the largest one once with my horn spoon. *Clack.*

She leaves her paddle floating in the grease, reaches over, and lays her wide, yellow-ivory palm across the stones. Her fingers are crooked like cedar roots, permanently curved to the shape of a chisel-haft. 

For a long time, nobody moves. Up above us, fifty tiers of slate shims, reed-pipes, cedar trunks, and stone uncles are ticking, hissing, and dripping—twenty thousand tiny threads of cold water being slowed down, harvested for their fat and their lime, kept from tearing our houses down into the hole. 

Gurl’s thumb rubs the white chalk rind of the flint. She feels the sky-cold in it. Then, without looking at me, she reaches into the pouch at her waist, pulls out a roasted amphipod—still warm from the kiln-ash, its blind, pale belly split open and salted with grey lime—and lays it on the timber where the flint was.

I pop it into my mouth, sucking the hot, copper-tasting grease from the shell while the raw one in my cheek finally stops wiggling. I don't go back up yet. I have ten minutes before the water-shift on the Sixth needs my gaff. I sit on my heels in the warm, stinking mud, let the tallow-smoke coat my tongue, and listen to the roof drip.
