There is a wide, three-cornered pan on the middle tier where the water comes down from six separate weirs and meets itself in the dark of the stone. 

Not dark—nothing is dark here—but a place where the glare is broken into small, overlapping rings that cancel each other’s brightness. The slate at the bottom of this pan was split from a block that had a twist in the grain, so the floor of it rises in the center like a thumb-knuckle. The water has to go around that knuckle. It splits, goes left and right, hits the baffle wall at the south end, and comes back on itself for three inches before it finds the notch.

I am the left-hand side of that notch.

Not the whole wall. The wall was laid down before my knees stopped bending, built of four courses of dark, iron-heavy basalt that someone dragged down from the east rim on a sledge whose runners left two pale grooves across the flags—grooves that are almost filled in now, shallow as thumbnail scratches under the sinter. I am only the corner where the fourth course failed to meet the third. There was a gap there the size of a sheep’s chest, where the grey clay kept washing out because the drop from the pan above was too steep and hit the seam sideways.

They tried twice to wedge it with greenstone. One of the wedges is still under my left hip. I could feel the edge of it for the first ten years, a dull, square tooth pressing through the horn of my flank, before the lime-crust grew thick enough to make the wedge and my hip bone into one continuous shelf.

When I still went on four limbs, I had a split in the nail of my right thumb that went all the way back to the quick. It made a particular sound when I smoothed the clay in the corners of the troughs—a tiny, double-tracked ridge left behind in the grey paste, like the trail of a two-tailed worm. If you went looking through the hundred pans upslope from here, draining them with the flat of your palm and blowing the silt away, you would find that double ridge under the clear glaze in seventy of them. That was my mark, though I didn't mean it to be. It was just where a flake of blue slate took me when I was small enough to ride between the shoulders of my elder, before I knew how to drop my weight when the stone gave way.

I cannot feel the thumb now. My hands are tucked into the pits of my knees, the way we fold when the cold finally gets inside the fat and stays there. My chin is down on my wrists. The two who brought me here—one of them had a white scar across his nose-ridge where a timber lever kicked back; the other was young, still pink around the nostrils, smelling of crushed flint—they were very careful with my head. If they had left my crown an inch higher, it would have broken the surface of the upper pan, and the mist would have dried on the leather of my cap and scaled off instead of glazing. They spent half a day with a stone maul, tapping two thin shims of grey slate under my jaw until the top of my skull was just beneath the skin of the water.

When the wind comes down from the rim—not a wind you could feel on your cheek, only a leaning of the mist—the water over my head thickens by the breadth of a grass-blade. Then the weight on my neck changes. That is how I know the day is turning, though the sky stays the same grease-white from one end of the silence to the other.

It is not dead, being stone. It is only very slow, and your ears get much larger.

When you are moving across the terraces, dragging the sledges, chewing the lime-balls with the grit grinding between your back teeth, your own body makes too much noise. The blood goes *thrum-thrum* behind your ears. Your lungs whistle against the wet felt of your vest. Your joints pop. You can only hear ten or twelve basins at once—the ones right under your belly, the ones whose lips you are testing with your cheek. 

Once the breath stops, the whole three miles comes in through your ribs.

I am Mortared into the middle tier, so I have a mile of stone above me and two miles of stone below. The basalt blocks behind my shoulders are laid directly against the dry-packed rubble that goes back into the hill, and that rubble touches the great ribs of the causeways, and the causeways go all the way up to the flake-piles on the rim. When someone three hundred yards upslope drops a maul onto a work-slab, the *tink* doesn't come to me through the air. The air is full of wool and water; it loses the sound before it crosses four walls. The *tink* comes down through the courses. It travels through the dried clay, hits the greenstone wedge under my hip, goes up my spine, and rings inside the hollow of my skull like a pebble dropped into a frozen kettle.

I know where six clusters are working right now. 

There is a heavy three-some up near the north rim where the slate splits thick and stubborn; they are using a long lever, and one of them has a hitch in his left shoulder—he leans onto the wood a half-second after the other two, so the block comes up with a double groan: *err-uh, err-uh*. 

Closer down, no more than forty pans to my right, someone is alone who shouldn't be alone. An old one. I can tell by the drag. When the young ones move between the weirs, it’s four distinct thuds—*knuckle, knuckle, knee, knee*—quick and greased. This one is sliding his belly over the lips because his hips have already begun to lock with the white scale. He scrapes across the slate like a full sack of gravel, stops for a long time to blow, and then taps—just once—with a small light pebble, checking a seam he doesn't have the leverage to fix anymore. He is looking for a gap the size of himself. There is a hollow in the baffle wall three tiers below me where the clay has bellied out under the weight of the pan; if he comes down this causeway before his knees fuse altogether, he will hear the sour drip of it and crawl in. Then I will have company in the chords.

That is what we do, after the eyes seal over. We hold the pitch.

A rock by itself is stupid. If you put a square of basalt into a wall, it sits there until the water undermines the clay at its heel, and then it tips forward a hair, and the notch widens, and the pan drains too fast, and the pan below it floods its lip and washes out its own clay, and in fifty years you have a gully, a roar, a brown scar of fast water that cuts all the way down to the gravel plug and ruins the work. A rock does not care if it tips.

We still have the bone inside the lime. Even with half an inch of clear stone over my eyelids, even with my ribs grown together into a single barrel of white sinter, there is the shape of a thing that used to listen. When the water creeps around the thumb-knuckle of this pan and comes through my notch, it has to pass over the bridge of my nose and between my knuckles. The sinter grows thickest where the water moves fastest. If the pan begins to settle toward the left, the water rushes harder over my left ear; the lime builds up there twice as fast, a pale ridge of stone-ice thickening week by week until it dams the rush and pushes the sheet of water back toward the middle. 

My body remembers the level, even if I cannot lift the maul. The mineral grows on me the way callus used to grow on my palms—thickest where the work is hardest.

Sometimes—not often, maybe once between the time a new layer of lichen covers a north wall and the time it turns from yellow to bone-white—a heel will come down right on the top of my skull. 

One of the living ones, using the crown of my head as a stepping-stone to cross the deep corner of the pan. 

When that happens, the water-skin breaks above my eyes. For a second, while the heavy, calloused foot rests on my brow, the weight of a living body presses me down into my clay bed. I feel the heat of them—not real heat, not like fire, which we only know from the old stories of the timber-felling down in the deep world, but that slow, greasy peat-warmth of a belly full of lime-jelly and blood that still goes around. I can hear their heart through the horn of their sole, knocking against the bone of my forehead: *lub-dub, lub-dub*. Fast. Terrible and fast, like a weir breaking in a thaw.

Then they shift their weight, drag their maul across the slate lip with a wet *shhhk*, and go on down the terrace. The water closes back over my head. The white rings settle. 

In the pan behind me, three grey spheres of lime-jelly have anchored themselves in the quiet eddy behind my right shoulder, where the current doesn't pull. They have grown big as fists since the last time anyone scraped this tank. Soon one of the young ones with the pink nostrils will come shuffling down the boot-path, smell the fat lime in the water, and reach his thick fingers down into the cold behind my neck to pluck them out. His knuckles will scrape the sinter on my cheek. If he finds that my chin has settled a fraction of an inch over the winter, he won't even look at my face—there is no face left to see, only a smooth, pale bolster of stone with two shallow dimples where the nostrils were—but he will take a flat flake of slate from his belly-pouch and tap it in under my jaw-bone until the *plip* of my notch matches the *plip* of the ten thousand notches below me.

Then the note will be true again, and I will go back to holding up the sky’s grease-white mirror, one drop at a time, until the pan fills in completely and the mountain is done.
