I am on the lone control stanchion in the center of the floor, four feet above the checkered deck plates, where the brass pipe-union Chocks sit. 

Not on the iron pedestal itself, and not in the weeping concrete below it, but inside the high-pressure gauge on the left—the one that reads the upstream head before the bronze valve. 

To look at the gauge through the streaming film of water, you would think the dial is simply broken, or that the installers forgot to pull the shipping pin. Look closer, through the condensation beading on the inside of the thick glass face. There are two black needles hovering over the white enamel face, both trembling around the 850-PSI mark. 

One of them is stamped, lacquered aluminum, mounted on the central brass pinion geared to the Bourdon tube inside the casing. It is twitching frantically, blurring at the tip as it tries to read the forty-cycle-per-second hammer of the water falling down the mountain. 

The second needle is me. 

I am clamped around the central spindle like a split-ring washer, my body drawn out into a three-inch, counterweighted pointer of dark, vulcanized gristle that mimics the stamped taper of the real indicator down to the crescent-shaped balance tail. I got in four months ago, when I was no bigger than a thread of clear, larval grease extruded from the Skein. I rode the slick thumb of a pipefitter who had just steadied himself against the workbench, and when he unscrewed the brass bezel to zero the dial during commissioning, the negative pressure inside the housing sucked me through the gasket seam.

Out in the room, my siblings—the hex-caps on the penstock, the heavy slate shims under the H-beams—live in the broad, hammering roar of the ten-foot pipe. Their world is massive, blunt, and structural. 

Mine is a five-inch brass drum of concentrated tension. 

Inside the gauge casing, the curved, hollow bronze C-tube that measures the penstock’s pressure expands and contracts with every microscopic variation in the snowmelt's fall. The mechanical linkage—the tiny brass sector gear, the hairspring, the pivot pins—chatters with a high, frantic, watchmaker’s hysteria that would tear a soft creature to ribbons. 

I do not fight the linkage; I am the damper in the system. 

My central ring is wedged directly between the brass faceplate and the pinion shaft. I have no heart, just like the gusset-plates on the walls, so I let the frantic, microscopic rotation of the shaft churn my internal fluids. I eat the grease the manufacturer packed into the tiny gear-teeth—a rich, amber lithium tallow that will last me for three years—and when that runs thin, I leach the copper oxides from the brass hairspring with a drop of weak acid, turning the metal beneath my foot bright, rose-gold.

Because I am inside the glass, the five-minute whiteout when the relief valve blows means nothing to me. The atomized fog cannot reach me; the green mash of the algae never touches my skin. I am sealed away from the colony, starving for the Skein’s rich deliveries, living on a miser's diet of machine oil and copper scale. 

Instead of the fog, I get the *spike*.

A quarter-second before the giant bronze valve cracks open, when the sub-audible pressure wave slams down the penstock, the Bourdon tube inside my dark brass house violently uncoils. The real aluminum needle whips clockwise toward the red 1,200-PSI peg fast enough to snap its own tip off against the stop-pin.

It never hits the pin. 

The instant the shaft begins to torque, my vulcanized ring locks down on the brass spindle, my internal capillaries stiffening into solid, high-density rubber. I take the mechanical whip-crack of the linkage straight through my own body, absorbing the kinetic violence that would otherwise shear the tiny brass teeth off the sector gear. The real needle slams against my side, and together we drag through the last hundred pounds of pressure on the dial, stopping a hair’s breadth from the peg. 

For the three seconds that the room outside is an exploding boiler, I am locked in a silent, trembling stalemate with the mountain's weight, my counterfeit black tip aligned so perfectly underneath the real needle that we look like a single, heavy shadow cast on the white enamel. 

When the bronze valve slams shut with that chest-thumping hydraulic *thud*, the pressure drops into a momentary trough. The real needle snaps back down to 800. 

I do not follow it right away. 

For ten minutes, twenty minutes, an hour after the fog clears and the shims have skated back to their corners under the beams, I stay locked at 1,190 PSI, my black, bevel-edged body clinging to the high-water mark of the violence, slowly bleeding the captured tension out of my rubbery flesh and back into the trembling air. 

Once a month, a man in a yellow slicker comes through the heavy steel door in the south wall. His boots clatter on the checkered plates. His flashlight beam cuts weakly through the horizontal mist, sliding over the hex-bolt Chocks under the pipe, missing the translucent tripwires of the Skein entirely. 

He walks up to the control stanchion. He wipes the streaming curtain of cold water off my glass face with the side of his gloved thumb. 

He looks right at me. 

He sees the live needle fluttering at 850, and he sees me—a matte-black, razor-edged twin sitting motionless at the top of the arc. He thinks I am a "tattletale"—a standard-issue maximum-pressure follower needle, left behind by the live pointer to show the engineers how high the surge went during the last governor trip. 

He takes a wet notebook from his pocket, writes down *1,190*, and taps the glass once with his knuckle to see if I will drop back down. 

The tap sends a dull, delicious ring through the brass bezel. I do not move. I hold the line. He shrugs, turns away into the greasy orange gloom, and leaves me to slowly, imperceptibly ratchet myself back down the white enamel face, one degree an hour, clicking softly against the ticks of the scale until I find the trembling flank of the real needle again, and lock in to wait for the next blow.
