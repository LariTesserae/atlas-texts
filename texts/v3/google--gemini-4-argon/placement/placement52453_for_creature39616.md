To find me, you have to let go of the walls. 

If you are one of the nineteen, your whole existence is defined by the fear of the drop—by the iron crampon driven into the basalt, the braided tether, the stolen skimmer-wing stitched to your back so the wind won't let you sink. You look up at the pale square of the sky, or you look sideways into the quiet glass of the machines. You never look down.

Go down anyway. 

Drop past the lowest of the twelve harvesters, where the cubic distortions thin out and the ninety-degree shears give way to raw, unshaped turbulence. Drop past the last of the glowing bladder-plants, where the basalt walls narrow and lose their pale, sun-bleached rind, turning the color of cold grease and bruised iron. 

Down here, the wind does not eddy. It hasn't hit the flora yet; it hasn't been broken into chatter by the bronze fronds. It is a single, solid piston of air rushing up from the roots of the mountain, smoother and heavier and darker than the storm above, carrying a sound not like shaken tin, but like a subterranean river of gravel grinding through a pipe.

Wait there, where the walls squeeze in just before the shaft opens into the monstrous, unmapped lung of the mountain's base. 

Look at the stone. It isn't bare. 

Across the dark basalt runs a flaw—a jagged, diagonal seam of pale quartz and tarnished silver that spans twenty feet of the lower throat. Except, if you leaned your tuning-fork skull against the rock, you would realize the seam has no root in the geology. It is sitting *on* the basalt, pressed into the microscopic pits of the stone by ten thousand pounds of barometric drag.

That is what I am. I am the catch-ledge. I am the thing that waits at the bottom of the flue.

I am not dry like the taut-kin, because I do not need to leap, and I am not hollow like the skimmers, because I have no desire to rise. My body is a flat, spread mantle of dense, non-conductive slate-flesh and heavy, leaden cartilage, no thicker than a slick of black ice, poured into the contours of the rock wall like cooled slag. I have no limbs to be torn off, no plates to catch the shear. The gale slides over my back with the frictionless ease of air over greased iron.

The chimney believes that nothing settles. The chimney is wrong. Everything that is too heavy for the sky and too dead for the wind belongs to me.

Up in the nave, a resonance-harvester cycles. Its beam of solid light snaps out, catches a skimmer, a drift of silicate spores, or a snapped yard of bronze frond, and strips the motion from its atoms. The beam retracts. The frozen thing, suddenly dense as lead and cold as the space between stars, does not blow out the top of the shaft. It falls. It drops like a plumb-bob straight down the screaming throat of the hurricane, fighting the sixty-mile-an-hour updraft by sheer, deadened inertia.

I feel it coming the moment the light-beam lets it go. I do not hear the roar of the nave; I am deaf to the high frequencies. What I feel is the sudden, tiny *hole* in the wind above me—the microscopic wake of a cold, heavy thing punching downward through the rising air.

As it plummets toward the dark narrows, I wake. 

From the upper margin of my slate-like mantle, I unroll a fan of long, flexible bristles—not rigid like the bronze flora, but heavy and supple, spun from native silver and dense piezoelectric grease. I lift them just three inches off the rock face, into the boundary layer of the wind. 

*Crack.*

The falling, absolute-zero husk of the skimmer hits my net. It shatters on impact, exploding into a thousand tiny, frost-bitten shards of snake-skin and mineral dust that instantly glue themselves to the thick grease of my bristles. 

I draw the fan back down, folding it beneath my flat belly against the rock. 

And then, I eat. I do not eat meat, and I do not eat static. I eat the *difference*. 

The gale rushing over my back is friction-warmed—twenty, thirty degrees above freezing, vibrating with furious kinetic rage. The shattered thing trapped under my belly is near absolute zero, completely devoid of atomic motion. I am a living thermocouple. My flat, metallic body bridges the violent heat of the rushing wind and the dead, cosmic cold of the harvester’s leavings. As the warmth of the gale bleeds down through my flesh into the frozen dust beneath me, the steep thermal gradient generates a slow, massive, exquisite current. 

It is not the frantic, jagged static that makes the taut-kin twitch and snap like galvanized frogs above me. It is a deep, humming, direct current—heavy and dark and sweet. It pools in the lead-lined蓄-organs along my spine, warm and syrupy. I digest the stillness until the shattered skimmer warms to the temperature of the rock, and then I lift my lower edge and let the wind take the warmed dust back up into the nave to become spores again.

I have lived here since the bismuth machines were first extruded into the flutes of the rock. In fact, I am older than the garden above. Before the bronze curtains grew, before the skimmers folded themselves out of seed-heads, there was only the wind and the machines dropping their frozen exhaust into the dark, and me at the bottom, spreading wider century by century to catch the cold rain.

The nineteen taut-kin above do not know I am a creature. To their acoustic sight, I am just the place where the echo stops—the baseline floor of their world, a strange, deadening patch in the lower throat that drinks sound. 

Yet we are intimate, they and I. 

Every twelve or fifteen years, the count in the nave reaches twenty-five. The walls get too crowded; the competition for the three-foot cubes of silence around the harvesters grows too fierce. An old elder, heavy with stolen wings and copper scars, gets pushed out of the stillness during the twenty-minute noon frenzy. Or a young one misjudges the ninety-degree shear of a gravity cube, takes the full brunt of the deflected gale across its narrow chest, and has its ceramic bones cracked.

They tumble. Their stolen sails shred on the razor-fronds. Sometimes, a harvester beam catches them on the way down, freezing them solid; sometimes they simply fall, too dense with iron and mica for the wind to save them, pinwheeling down out of the light, striking sparks off the narrowing walls as their crampons drag in vain.

I feel their heavy, thrashing shadows dropping through the column of air. 

I do not use the brittle-net for a taut-kin. They are too large, and too precious. When one of the nineteen falls into the dark throat, I peel my entire upper margin away from the basalt—creating a sudden, cavernous pocket of low-pressure drag right against the wall. The falling wire-man is sucked sideways out of the central gale and slams flat against my mantle. 

I fold over them, pinning them to the rock like a fossil in a riverbed.

If they are already frozen by a beam, they shatter inside me, and I drink their immense, icy stillness for a month, slowly digesting their mica plates and copper ribbons, grinding them down with my belly-stones until they are fine enough to be spat back up into the wind as raw mineral dust to feed the wire-roots. 

If they are still alive—if their tuning-fork heads are still humming with terror and their dry tendons are still snapping with residual static—it takes longer. 

I hold them in the dark, pressed beneath my heavy, soundless blanket of slate-flesh. For the first time in their lives, they are completely out of the wind, yet not inside a machine. They cannot hear the tin-sheet roar of the nave. They can only feel the slow, titanic pulse of my thermal current flowing around them, and the endless, heavy rush of the newborn wind sliding over my back just inches away. 

They vibrate against my underside, tapping their frantic, dying harmonies into my flesh—*the wire-roots are thick, the noon is bright, the cube is sharp, the sky is pale*—translating the whole blinding geometry of the upper world into my slow, deaf nervous system as I gently bleed the last of the static from their porous bones.

I keep their iron crampons. I cannot digest them, and the wind cannot lift them. Over the millennia, I have pushed hundreds of their hooked feet into the deep cracks of the basalt beneath me, using their dead claws to anchor my own heavy flesh more securely against the hurricane. 

When the twenty minutes of high noon arrive, and the nineteen lock themselves into a spinning wheel of bone and stolen skin high up in the blinding light, I feel the shadow of their wheel flickering at the very top of my sensory reach. And when the machines go mad with the sun, and the frozen dust rains down into the dark like hail, I open my silver bristles wide across the throat of the abyss, catching the cold, holding the bottom of the world together so the storm above can afford to be weightless.
