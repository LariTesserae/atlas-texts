I am not in the trenches. The trenches belong to the quick, the two-eyed, the ones whose knees still have the spring to clear a six-foot fissure when the stone gives way. 

I am forty-two years old—an impossibly ancient thing in this furnace—and my left hip was crushed five years ago when a deadfall toggle slipped early. In the first generation, the ones who fell behind were simply eaten by the carpet-swarms before their hearts stopped beating. In the second, they were given a merciful strike to the base of the skull with a basalt core so their screaming wouldn't draw the hoofed packs onto the rest of the knot. 

Now, in the third, we have figured out how to use the broken ones.

You will find me forty feet above the basalt floor, wedged into the high, trembling fork of an exceptionally thick, exceptionally sour cork-tree that grows directly over a major geothermal chimney. The updraft here is a continuous, scalding gale that smells of rotten eggs and wet iron. It is too hot for the wood-grubs to bore into the bark for long, and the rising column of steam creates an acoustic shadow—a rare, vertical cylinder of turbulent air that scrambles the sonar of the big mantis-bats, making them veer wide of my branches.

I am the Loom, and I am the Ear. 

If your light caught me, you wouldn’t see a person at all. You would see a gall—a bulbous, scarred knot of grey cork and black chitin grown straight into the fork of the tree. I have carved myself a deep socket in the living pulp and lined it with the cured, overlapping thoraxes of giant crawlers. My bad leg is tucked under me, atrophied to a dry stick wrapped in sulfur-paste, permanently wedged into a root-cleft so I cannot fall when I doze. 

I do not move from this tree for weeks at a time. Only when the wood beneath my palms begins to lose its heat, or vibrates with the deep, hollow *thrum-thrum* of a collapsing root-system, do the young ones come up the vines, sling me across two sets of shoulders like a bundle of butchered meat, and carry me at a dead, scuttling run through the dark to a new chimney-tree a mile away.

My eyes have been gone since I was twenty—taken by a whip-vine frenzy during a herd-stampede—and the skin has grown smoothly over the sockets, tough and greasy with mineral sweat. I do not need them. Up here in the updraft, I am suspended in the center of a vast, three-dimensional web of tension and vibration.

Radiating outward from my tree are seventeen lines of braided gut-string, thin and hard as wire, rubbed with rosin from the blood-vines so the insects won't chew through them. They stretch out into the pitch black for hundreds of yards, anchored to distant basalt spires, to the trip-pins of deadfalls in the trenches below, and to the great, howling bladder-lures hanging in the neighboring canopy. 

The ends of all seventeen lines are tied to my fingers, my toes, and the pierced, stretched lobes of my ears. 

I sit in the roaring dark, my shaved head bowed against the steaming bark, playing the valley like a monstrous harp. 

Every line has a voice. The thick cord tied to my left big toe goes down to the bone-spike funnel in the third fissure; when the blind, dinner-plate beetles begin to press into the trench, their shells scraping against the rock, the line hums with a low, gritty *zzzzzt* that travels straight up my shinbone. The thin, taut gut looped around my right ring finger runs to a wind-gourd three trees east; I can feel the rhythm of the thermal gusts pulsing through it, and if a flyer tears the net beneath it, the sudden slack makes my finger jerk in the dark.

My hands are never still. Even as I listen through my skin to the seventeen lines, my palms and thumbs are working in my lap. 

When the knot of five hunters—my daughter, my two nephews, and two others whose mothers I felt die through the tremor of the ground years ago—scuttles up the trunk in the dark, they do not stop for more than ninety seconds. They are panting like lizards, slick with grease and blood, their chests heaving at two hundred beats a minute. 

One of them drops a warm, wet sack of raw crawler-gut and a bundle of stripped, stringy vine-bark into my lap. Another pulls five dull, chipped basalt blades from their wrist-sheaths and shoves them into the cork pocket beside my hip, snatching up the five razor-sharp, freshly knapped ones I have waiting for them. 

Then, for ten seconds, my daughter presses her slick, hot forehead hard against mine. 

Her skull is a drum. Through the fused bones of our brows, she hums a frantic, rising three-note chord, cut short by a sharp click of her teeth: *The split-hooves are massing in the low sink. The wind is shifting north. The big deadfall by the orange vent is fouled with rubble.*

I do not hum back. My throat is too dry from breathing the sulfur-steam all day. Instead, I reach up and tap my wrist-stone against the chitin plate lashed to her collarbone. *Clack. Clack-clack. Thump.* 

*Go left. The whistle-crack is drawing flyers to the low ridge. Cut the third line as you pass.*

Before the last vibration has left her collarbone, she is gone, dropping forty feet down a slick blood-vine in a controlled fall that would rip the skin off anyone else’s palms, vanishing into the solid wall of noise below.

I am left alone in the heat again. 

I tuck the fresh gut-bag between my good thigh and my ribs to keep it supple, pick up a fist-sized nodule of black volcanic glass, and poise my striking-stone above it. 

Through my left earlobe, a sudden, violent tug—a four-winged mantis-bat, heavy as a dog, has just hit the high snare over the sulfur-crack. The obsidian weight has dropped. Down in the dark, the thing is thrashing on the jagged rock, its ultrasonic death-shrieks completely drowned out by the roar of a billion other bodies, but up here, the gut-line sings a high, tight *twang* against my jawbone, telling me the toggle held and the meat is pinned.

With my left hand, I reach out and pluck a thicker cord—an eighteenth line that doesn't go to a trap, but drops straight down to a hollow, fire-hardened log wedged into a resonance-echo chamber in the basalt directly below my tree. I pull it once, hard, releasing a heavy wooden mallet that strikes the log with a deep, booming *THUD* that travels through the stone floor of the valley for half a mile. 

*Meat down. North ridge. Hurry.*

Then I go back to my lap. I find the striking platform on the obsidian core with my calloused thumb, angle it by memory, and bring the wrist-stone down. 

*Clack.* 

A long, curved flake falls into my palm, sharp enough to split a hair, though none of us have any left. I tuck it into the cork, rotate the core, and feel the seventeen strings pulling gently at my toes, my fingers, my ears—the whole blind, roaring, chewing engine of the basin trembling against my skin, keeping me warm, keeping me fed, while I make the teeth that eat it back.
