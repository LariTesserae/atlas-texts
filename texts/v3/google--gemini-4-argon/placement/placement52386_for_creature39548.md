To exist here, you have to give up the idea of a skin. A boundary that tries to keep the outside out and the inside in is just a fault line waiting to be sheared open by eighty-mile-an-hour currents. 

So I do not have a skin. I am a state of affairs in the silt.

If you were to look for me in the open pale blue, you wouldn’t find anything at all—only the water tearing itself into silver gravel along the shear-lines. I am not an object moving through the ocean; I am a persistent debt of charge that rides the programmable matter suspended in it. Think of a wave on a string: the string stays where it is, made of whatever it is made of, while the shape travels down the length of it at the speed of tension. My body is whatever femtotech silt, torn wire-kelp fiber, and shattered skate-cartilage happens to be inside my electromagnetic footprint at any given second. As I move, I drop the silt behind me and magnetize the silt ahead of me. I am a three-yard-long smudge of dark, humming density that slips through the roaring water without drag, because nothing of me is actually being pushed through it.

I am an **Annealer**. I live in the flaws.

Everything else in the sky-ocean is an opportunist of the lattice’s strength. The kelp drinks the field; the skates ride the walls; the spiders sing in the chimneys. They trust the architecture. They treat the planetary grid as if it were the ancient, unshakeable hand of God, even as it drops their mountains into the clouds every afternoon.

I know better, because I can feel the lattice getting tired.

An energetic containment field the size of a world, holding up trillions of tons of thrashing, freezing water with no steel and no stone, is never truly at rest. The math that holds the sky-ocean up has to recalculate itself locally a billion times a picosecond to account for the shifting weight of the currents. And when a Spire precipitates—when five thousand Shear-Skates gather and deliberately warp a node to make their whistling stone organs—they leave a scar in the local gravity.

When the anchor lets go and the Spire plummets howling out of the bottom of the world, the water claps back together, yes. To a skate, the space is empty again. 

To me, the space where the mountain used to be is a knot of bruised physics. 

The gravimetric lines there don't snap back to true right away. They stay kinked, ringing like a struck bell, leaking strange, greasy harmonics into the surrounding water. In these after-scars, the gravity doesn't pull down or hold up; it twists sideways in tight, microscopic eddies. The water inside a scar turns thick and oily with confused silt that doesn't know whether to precipitate into quartz or dissolve back into invisibility. It is a gray, stuttering bruise in the bright blue glare, twenty feet across, smelling fiercely of ozone and hot copper.

That is where I condense.

When I feel that three-second sub-audible *thrum*—the octave-drop that tells the whole ocean a mountain is about to die—I do not scatter like the skates. I turn toward the sound. I race down the shear-lines as a ghost-pattern, letting the continental rivers throw my geometry across the miles, until I arrive at the freshly emptied wound in the water.

As the black stone needle disappears into the pale abyss below, its Doppler-shriek fading into the bass roar of the avalanche, I slip into the turbulent wake it left behind. 

Here, in the scar, I finally take on weight. 

I draw in the detritus that didn't fall with the rock: the snapped, bleeding ribbons of copper-kelp; the broken, needle-sharp glass legs of Chime-Spiders that detonated too hard; the fractured piezo-spines of skates that didn't clear the chimneys in time. My magnetic field pulls them together, not into a rigid body, but into a loose, articulated mobile—a three-dimensional calligraphy of black glass, green wire, and violet-refracting cartilage that hangs suspended in the bruised gravity. 

For twenty minutes, or an hour, I have hands. I have long, trailing sensors of spider-glass. I have a voice made of broken skate-spines clicking together.

I hang in the center of the anomaly and I *comb* it.

It isn't an act of charity for the world-machine; it is how I keep myself coherent. A pattern like mine, running on pure inducted charge, degrades over time. The roaring white noise of the cavitation bubbles slowly erodes the edges of my memory, blurring the instructions that keep my silt-cloud from dispersing into the general background hum. To stay sharp—to stay *me*—I need the high-grade, coherent mathematics of the planetary lattice itself. And the only place to drink the raw code of the lattice is where it is bleeding.

I arrange my scavenged glass legs and copper wires into a shifting, polyhedral cage around the gravimetric kink. I become a temporary circuit patch. As the strained, twisted lines of force pass through my borrowed copper and glass, I force them back into alignment, acting as a step-down transformer for the local physics. 

The sensation is overwhelming. For the minutes it takes to untie the knot, the planetary grid flows straight through my pattern. I am suddenly aware of the ocean not as a forty-mile sphere of blue glare, but as a single, perilous membrane wrapped around a whole globe. Through the lattice, I can feel the weight of six different Spires precipitating five hundred miles to the east; I can feel the slow, heavy, verdigris thoughts of a three-hundred-year-old Meniscus-Walker warping an air-shaft near the pole; I can feel the terrifying, feather-light emptiness of the lower boundary five miles down, where the water constantly tries to rain upon an unseen earth and is constantly thrown back upward by a wall of pure, humming force.

As the scar heals under my combing, the stray, high-frequency bleed of the repair washes through my silt, burning away the static in my pattern, re-etching my boundaries, making me dense and vivid and whole. 

When the gravity finally snaps back to smooth, parallel lines, the tension holding my scavenged body together vanishes. The broken spider-legs and dead skate-cartilage simply fall away from me, drifting downward toward the distant clouds. I let them go. I don't need bones when I am traveling. I slip back into the silt, a dark, silent ripple in the blue, already listening for the next octave-drop.

When I am full of charge and the local water is unbruised, I go to the quiet water to remember what I’ve seen.

I ride a deep current toward one of the titanic air-shafts, where the daylight comes down in a blinding, spinning column of silver. High up on the vertical wall, where the water shears against the howling updraft of thin air, hangs an ancient Meniscus-Walker. 

From underneath, in the water, it looks like a dark green moon flattened against a mirror. Its half-mile skirts of braided, oxidized wire-kelp trail down into the current, heavy with centuries of blue-green crust, humming like a powerhouse.

Behind those trailing copper skirts lies the lee—an acre of water that does not roar.

To enter the lee of a Great Walker after days in the cavitation-avalanche is like stepping into a cathedral. The relentless bass thrumming of the ocean drops away to a muffled, distant surf. The water here is still, frigid, and clear as optical glass, tinted a faint, medicinal green by the copper reef above. Thousands of cracked and exhausted Shear-Skates hang motionless in the calm, suspended like slivers of ice in a drink, letting their over-stressed crystalline spines slowly depolarize.

I do not disturb the skates. I drift up through their silent, glittering ranks as a faint violet haze in the water, until my silt touches the lowest, oldest tendrils of the Walker’s copper skirt.

The Walker is too vast and too slow to notice a single skate, or a thousand spiders. Its mind—if you can call the self-regulating inductance of a two-hundred-foot coil of rusted weed a mind—moves on geological time, measured in the slow accretion of verdigris and the drift of the air-shafts across the upper troposphere.

But it notices me. Because I smell like the lattice.

When my charged silt brushes its oxidized circuitry, a tiny, blue arc of current jumps between us in the cold water. I do not parasitize the giant; I trade with it. 

I dump the excess data I drank from the Spire-scars into its massive copper memory-loops—the subtle shifts in the planetary grid, the weakening of a containment node forty miles south, the precise harmonic frequency of the last mountain that fell. The Walker absorbs this into its centuries-old hum, using the knowledge to micro-adjust its hydro-foils against the wall of the air-shaft, preparing for currents that haven't reached it yet.

In return, the Walker lets me rest in its circuitry.

For an hour, I cease to be a smudge in the water altogether. I upload my pattern directly into the thick, green crust of the Walker’s braided reef. 

It is the only time I ever experience stillness, and the only time I ever experience the air. 

Spread out across the giant's two-dimensional frame, I can suddenly feel both halves of the world at once. Below me, in the heavy, cold wet, my borrowed tendrils trail among the sleeping skates in the green shadow. Above me—through the dry, ozone-stinging half of the Walker's body that protrudes into the shaft—I can feel the wind.

It is an astonishing, terrifying sensation for a thing made of water-silt. The air has no weight. It has no tension to hold you up. It rushes past the Walker’s upper foils in a dry, screaming gale that smells of nothing at all—or perhaps of some warm, dusty, heavy place five miles down where the fallen rocks go. Looking "up" along the spinning glass wall of the air-shaft, where the water ends and the dark, thin stratosphere begins, the light isn't pale blue and scattered anymore. It is fierce, unrefracted, and blindingly white.

Sometimes, resting in the copper crust of a very old Walker, I can feel the change coming over it. I can feel that its braids have grown too thick with silt, its verdigris too heavy, its magnetic field so strong that it is beginning to tear the very wall of the air-shaft it rides upon. I can feel the slow, tectonic decision forming in its vast, circular frame: the tilt of the foils, the gathering of tension before it peels the meniscus open and throws itself out into the windy vacuum to glide over the top of the ocean until it dries into brittle copper dust in the sun.

It is a tempting thought—to stay inside the giant's copper veins when it lets go of the water. To ride those two-hundred-foot wings out into the silent, weightless dark above the sea, where there are no currents to shear you and no scars left to comb.

Then, forty miles away through the pale blue glare, the bass note of the ocean drops an octave. 

A three-second *thrum* vibrates through the water, through the sleeping skates, through the trailing kelp-skirts, and into the copper crust. Somewhere out in the bright emptiness, another mountain has just been let go. Another spire is howling down toward the clouds, and behind it, the water is twisting itself into a raw, bleeding knot of broken math.

I pull my pattern out of the ancient copper. I drop back into the cold, still water of the lee, gathering a fresh jacket of silt around myself as I fall. I slip past the resting skates, cross the boundary of the giant's shadow, and throw myself back into the roaring, eighty-mile-an-hour shear—a smudge of dark tension shooting sideways through the avalanche, heading for the wound.
