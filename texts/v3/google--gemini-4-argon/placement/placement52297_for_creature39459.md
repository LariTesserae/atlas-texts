My teeth ache with the *thrum*.

It is not pain, not really. It is the baseline of existence—a low, bronze G-flat that enters through the soapstone pads on my knees, travels up my femurs, rattles the heavy plates of my pelvis, and settles in the back of my jaw. When the great turbine at the bottom of Shaft Four-South hits its third revolution, there is a slight hitch in the rhythm—*thrum-thrum-uhn-thrum*—a tiny lip of lime scale catching on the east bearing. It has been catching like that since my mother’s mother was small enough to ride inside a boiler-felt sling. It is how I know where I am in the dark without opening my eyes.

I am lying on my belly in the Dry-Pocket we call *Weeping-Flange*. The ceiling is four inches above my shoulder blades. If I arch my back to stretch, the cured ray-skin of my carapace scrapes against the dry basalt lintel, flaking off little scales of grey salt. The floor beneath my chest is lined with three thicknesses of Blister-hide, oiled with grease skimmed from the Catch-Basins until it smells comfortably of wet iron and old broth.

Beside me, close enough that our stone knee-pads click together when we shift, lies my clutch-brother, *Six-Rod*. He is asleep, his huge, hairless chest expanding and collapsing against the rubbery floor with a slow, whistling wheeze. Every time he exhales, a tiny film of moisture blooms on the bronze tuner-rod he keeps tucked beneath his chin.

I am not asleep. I have the bone knob of my own tuner wedged hard into the notch behind my left ear, and the chisel-tip pressed against the green-crusted brass bolt that protrudes from the masonry wall.

I am listening to the water change its mind.

Fifty feet down, the main sluice-gate for our district is beginning to creak toward its Slack-Hour setting. Through the bronze rod, the sound is immense—a cathedral of groaning metal and roaring, scalding weight. I can hear the water bruising itself against the stone baffles, the high, singing whine of the pressure-relief vents opening in the next sector over, and, very faintly, the *clink-clink-pause-clink* of the clan at *Red-Sump* tapping out a territory warning on their main standpipe. *Stay off the north grates,* their knuckles say through half a mile of solid rock. *The steam is sour today. We are scraping the overflow.*

I don't tap back. My role is not to argue with *Red-Sump*. My role is the spring-steel.

I slide backward out of the gallery like a crayfish backing out of a crevice, my stone clogs dragging a dull, greasy smear across the threshold. In the turn-chamber—a space wide enough for three people to squat without touching—I check my gear by touch. My eyes are barely open; the yellow inner lids are drawn across them to keep out the grit that falls from the ceiling joints. I have my forty-foot coil of flat spring-steel strapped across my belly. I have my bronze scraper, its edge honed on a basalt paver until it can shave the hair off a dead man’s arm, if we had any hair to shave. And I have my salt-bag.

To go up is to feel the world try to flatten you.

I put my back against the underside of Hatch Nine-B and push with my thighs. The cast-iron square weighs eighty pounds, greased around the rim with tallow so it doesn't rust shut, and as it tilts up, the surface air rolls down over my head like hot wet wool. The pressure changes instantly. My ears pop—a wet, sticky sound inside my skull—and the air I draw into my nose is thin, sharp with sulfur, and so hot it feels like drinking soup.

I slide out onto the avenue, dropping immediately to all fours as the hatch settles back into its frame with a soft, oiled *thud*.

Above me, the sky is the color of an old bruise on pale skin. The fog is low today; I cannot see the standpipe half a mile east. There is only the floor. The vast, shining, ruled paper of the world, slick with its millimeter of scalding runoff, stretching out in ninety-degree lines until it blurs into the yellow dark.

For a long moment, I do not move. I let my body become a drain cover. My shell is stamped with the diamond-mesh; the red mold grows in the seams of my leather joints just as it grows in the mortar between the pavers. A Sluice-Ray, wide as my outstretched arms and thin as a dock-leaf, glides past six inches from my nose. It doesn't know I am here. Its slate-grey margins ripple with a delicate, hypnotic geometry, riding the tiny thermal wave of water spilling from Runnel Twelve. I could drop my chain-ring on it, pin it, and slide my shovel under its belly, but my salt-bag is empty and my spring-steel is cold. I am not hunting meat today.

I push off with my left clog.

*Shhh-click. Shhh-click.*

Skating the Grid is the closest we come to flying. You keep your chin two inches above the water—any lower and you drink the acid; any higher and the thin, shifting drafts make your head swim with the *Up-Sickness*, that terrifying, hollow feeling that the sky is a drain sucking you upward into nothing. I push with my short, heavy calves, gliding ten feet at a time on my soapstone knee-pads and palm-saucers. The hot water parts around my wrists in twin silver V-shapes, steaming slightly as it hits the cooler grease on my leather sleeves.

I am heading for the capillary beds at the edge of the Third Catch-Basin.

When I reach the first choked runnel, I don't need my eyes to see the blockage; I can feel the heat pooling wrong under my left palm. The water here isn't sliding; it’s backing up, forming a lukewarm, stagnant puddle three inches deep where the pale, slimy threads of infant Blisters are already trying to anchor themselves to the smooth basalt.

I uncoil the spring-steel from my waist.

This is the craft that makes me *Three-Baffle-Skinner*, though no one says the name aloud. I feed the flat, flexible ribbon of metal into the two-inch slot in the stone. It goes in twenty feet, thirty feet, sliding through the dark, boiling guts of the floor, until the tip hits the plug—a solid knuckle of precipitated lime and dead, calcified ray-cartilage wedged in the ninety-degree elbow six feet down.

I press my front teeth against the brass handle of the ribbon and *listen* through my jaw.

Down in the dark, the steel tip scrapes against the stone. *Scritch-scritch-thock.* Lime scale has a high, brittle ring to it; iron-rot sounds dull, like wet punk-wood. This is lime. I begin to work the ribbon, twisting my thick wrists in short, violent snaps, sending a whip-crack down the length of the buried metal.

Through my teeth, I feel the scale fracture. A chunk the size of a fist breaks loose. Instantly, the tone of the floor changes. The backed-up water gives a wet, greedy *slurp*, the miniature whirlpool spinning away down the slot, carrying the white gravel down toward the main shafts where the Gantries can eat it. The stone beneath my knees drops three degrees in temperature as the fresh, fast runoff resumes its proper path. The Grid breathes a little easier. My teeth stop buzzing with the wrong note and settle back into the great, healthy G-flat of the deep pistons.

I spend four hours on my belly in the wet gloom, rodding the slots, scraping the rich, dark-red velvet of the mold from the iron rims and packing it into my brass basket. My hands are parboiled inside their greased mitts, the skin of my palms as white and wrinkled as the belly of a Ray, though I feel no pain—the nerves in a Lapper’s fingers die back by the time they are ten years old, leaving only a dull, heavy sense of pressure.

It is while I am scraping the third drain of the afternoon that the floor goes heavy.

The G-flat doesn't stop, but another note crawls over the top of it—a slow, rhythmic *crunch* that makes the water in the runnels jump out of its channels in tiny, square-patterned standing waves.

I stop scraping. I pull my knees under my chest, tuck my head down so my brow-ridge touches the stone, and look sideways through my yellow membranes into the fog.

Fifty yards away, the sky is coming down to touch the floor.

A foot the size of a Dry-Pocket turn-chamber descends out of the amber vapor. It is cast iron, webbed like a duck’s foot, shod on the bottom with a three-inch pad of compressed, petrified mycelium that hisses furiously as it settles into the hot water. From the center of that foot, a spindly lattice of riveted struts rises up at a steep angle, disappearing into the mist twenty feet overhead.

It is the *Old-East-Walker*. I know it by the missing rivet-head on its lower ankle-brace, which leaves a tiny, whistling hole when the steam-wind blows from the south.

The Gantry has stopped over Main Hatch Four. High above me, invisible in the yellow soup, I hear the greased bronze chain begin to pay out—*clank... clank... clank*—slow as a dying heart. The bronze bucket drops through the floor, down into the roaring orange dark where we never go, down among the paddle-wheels and the boiling salt-rivers.

I don't move. You don't skate under a Gantry when its bucket is down; sometimes the old cam-shafts slip a tooth and the waist-trough spills a gallon of boiling acid-slurry onto the pavers to clear its own throat.

Instead, I press my tuner to the stone and listen to it feed. I feel the bucket hit the deep sludge a hundred feet down. I feel the immense, mindless pull of the counter-weights as fivehundred pounds of lime-scale and iron-mud are dragged upward through the shaft. And as the bucket rises past the floor level, dripping hot, white milk back onto the grates, I smell the trough overhead: a sharp, vinegar-and-copper tang that cuts right through the sulfur.

My father is in that machine. Or rather, he passed through it six hundred strides ago, when his lungs finally filled with the white mineral-crust and he couldn't draw the forty-percent air anymore. We climbed the southwest leg at the *Nine-Valve* crossing, hooked our belts to the greasy truss, and slid him into the waist-trough right behind a load of grey bottom-sludge. I remember how small his pale, barrel-chested body looked floating in that green, bubbling liquor, his brow-ridges disappearing first, then his broad, flat feet, until the steam closed over the trough and the Gantry took its next ten-minute step into the dark.

He is down in the bronze sleeves now. He is part of the grease that keeps Shaft Four-South from seizing on its lime-lip. When the floor thrums against my sternum at night, a millionth part of that push is his ribs and his tallow, turning the great paddles, keeping the stone warm under my belly.

The chain stops clanking. Overhead, there is a heavy, wet *whoosh* as the bucket tips into the acid-trough. A few drops of warm, salty spray drift down through the fog and land on my leather carapace with a soft *pat-pat*.

Then, with a long, pneumatic sigh that smells of old oil, the massive iron foot fifty yards away lifts six inches off the basalt. It hangs there in the fog, dripping, and begins its glacial swing forward.

I don't watch it go. The Slack-Hour is nearly over; through my palms, I can feel the deep valves beginning to groan as the boiling river is shunted back toward our sector. Within twenty minutes, the water on these pavers will be hot enough to peel the cured hide off my knee-pads.

I hook my brass basket of red mold to my belt, coil my spring-steel around my ribs, and drop flat to the stone.

*Shhh-click. Shhh-click.*

I skate back toward Hatch Nine-B, following the ninety-degree lines through the thickening steam, keeping my head down, keeping my back flat, a grey square sliding across a grey floor, going home to the crack in the rock where the air is thick and the stone sings against my teeth all night long.
