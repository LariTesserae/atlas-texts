I am seven fathoms below the lowest market-lattice, hanging in the dark tea where the city’s underside frays into raw tether-lines. 

My left foot is hooked around the master-cable of the Fourth Terrace—a three-ply braid of living siphon-kelp as thick as a kiln-chimney. It is slick with a pale fuzz of sulfur-eating bacteria, and when I pinch my big toe against the braid, I can feel the whole district breathing above me. Three hundred tonnes of basalt laths, shell-mortar domes, and gourd-orchards pull upward against the cable’s core, wanting the bronze sky; fifty fathoms below, the mountain holds the other end in its unweathered teeth. 

Between the two, the rope hums like a bass string tuned just below the threshold of hearing.

I am a *tension-listener*. That is what my belt says, if you know how to read the stones at my hips. I carry no obsidian harvest-claws, no wooden quarry-wedges. My belt is heavy with four flat ovals of high-density grey basalt, each bored through and toggled with dried snail-gut, counterbalanced by a single, scarred oil-gourd the size of a child’s head that bobs gently against my lower ribs. I am ballasted to hang dead-level at the thermocline, where the blood-warm broth of the avenues meets the rising, rhythmic scalding-plumes from the deep fissures.

My right knee is stiff this season. Sixty years of silicate broth and snail-grease have turned the old quarry-gash across my patella into a solid ridge of black glass, three fingers wide. When I bend it too quickly, it clicks—a tiny, internal stone-strike that only I can hear. Soon I will have to let go of a basalt toggle on my belt to account for the weight of my own hardening leg, though I have not told my niece yet.

Right now, I am not using my eyes. In this sourceless olive-brown, fifty yards from the nearest lantern-fern, eyes are only good for watching the ash drift sideways. My nictitating membranes are drawn halfway across my corneas, turning the world into a comforting, milky smear. 

Instead, I am listening with my jaw.

I have my chin and the flat of my cheekbone pressed hard into a "tuning-peg"—a two-foot shard of pristine hexagonal basalt driven directly through the heart of the kelp-cable where it splits to cradle a stone-and-wicker cistern above. My eyes are closed. My large, barrel chest is still; my spleen squeezed down ten minutes ago, flooding my veins with thick, dark red cells, so I will not need to sip from the air-bladder clipped to my shoulder for another quarter-hour.

Through the bone of my jaw, the city arrives as a dry, intricate clatter.

Up on the Third Terrace, someone is knapping a fresh core; I feel the crisp, glassy *tink-tink-spall* of the horn-striker taking off long blades for the arbor-weavers’ fingers. Closer, a giant gastropod is rasping its radula across a stone mortaring-table—a slow, rhythmic *shhh-shhh* like rough hide dragging over pumice. And beneath all of it, the constant, sleepy groan of the caldera wall, shifting a fraction of a hair as the magma chamber three miles down cools by a hundredth of a degree.

That is all ordinary noise. I let it wash through my teeth and down my spine without holding onto it.

I am waiting for a *fray-tick*.

A living cable does not rot, because it is still growing, drinking the dissolved silica and sending out blind, pale rootlets to bind its own braids together. But the quarry-crews were busy last month; they floated four new basalt beams into the upper neighborhood to frame a nursery-dome, and they added sixty oil-gourds to the roof-truss. The load has shifted four degrees toward the center of the lake. Somewhere in the twist above me, the living wood is rubbing against the razor-lip of a stone anchor-ring.

There.

A tiny, high-pitched *zzzt*. Like a fingernail catching on a split reed.

It came from above and to the left, along the secondary bridle that holds the snail-pens. If I leave it for ten days, the obsidian-sharp lip of the basalt ring will saw through the outer ply while the current rocks the terrace. A month after that, the bridle will part with a sound like a thunderclap, and forty yards of woven terrace will tip vertically into the broth, spilling sleeping-domes, air-pockets, and mortar-vats into the dark.

I unhook my left foot, reach up with my right hand, and catch a guide-vine. My motion is syrupy, unhurried. Even a veteran does not kick here; two yards to my right, an unworked pinnacle of the submerged cliff-wall juts out of the murk, its fresh black fractures gleaming dull and wet like frozen tar. A careless elbow against that edge would open me to the bone before I felt the cold of the cut.

I pull myself upward, hand over foot, my four prehensile limbs working in the slow, spider-like rhythm of my people. As I rise through the water, the temperature drops by two degrees—from fever-warm to blood-warm—and the olive gloom brightens just enough that I can see the pale, grey-ochre skin of my forearms and the glossy black rivers of old scars winding from my wrists to my elbows.

At the anchor-ring, I find the wound. The massive basalt torus—chiseled out of a single column by three generations of dead masons—has settled at an angle in its wicker sling. Its unweathered inner edge, sharp as a butcher's cleaver, has bitten half an inch into the thigh-thick kelp stalk. Pale, gelatinous sap is leaking from the cut, hanging in the slow current like strings of egg-white.

I do not cut the rope or replace the stone. We do not fight the load; we feed it.

From a net-pouch at my hip, I draw a "pillow-shell"—the empty, spiral house of a juvenile glass-snail, no bigger than my fist, which I have stuffed with a paste of volcanic ash, crushed lime, and my own morning spit. With a flat bone spatula, I work the lip of the shell directly into the gap between the razor-stone and the bleeding vine. The vine’s sap is already reacting with the ash-mortar, turning tacky and grey. By tomorrow morning, the mortar will have drunk enough silicate from the water to set harder than brick, and the wounded kelp will grow around the smooth, curved back of the snail-shell instead of the knife-edge of the basalt. 

To make sure the vine knows what to do, I unclip a two-inch sliver of black glass from my belt—a tiny, crescent-shaped pruning flake—and make three shallow, diagonal nicks in the healthy bark just above the shell. The vine shivers slightly under my fingers (or perhaps I only imagine it does) and begins to weep more sap to seal the new irritation. Within a season, a callus as thick as my own knee will have swollen over the whole joint, locking stone, shell, and stem into a single knot.

Before I descend, I unstrap the horn striker from my belt and tap my sternum-plaque.

*Tink-tink. Scritch. Tink.*

*Fourth-west bridle pillowed. Load holds.*

Two hundred yards away, invisible behind three curtains of giant, sail-fronded ferns and a suspended plantation of gourd-vines, another plaque answers from the upper district.

*Tink-scritch.*

My niece. She has the lighter belt now—two pumice stones and only one small basalt weight—working the high canopy where the bladder-weed ceiling meets the bronze light, tending the gas-traps. Her strike is quick, sharp, a young person’s rhythm.

I hang there for a moment, my toes curled into the slime of the bridle, my chest beginning to ache with the pleasant, heavy warmth of carbon dioxide building in my blood. Above me, the vast, woven shadow of the city sways a few inches to the east as a thermal bubble the size of a house breaks loose from the abyssal vents far below and wobbles upward through the middle distance, missing our district entirely, its silver-black skin shimmering in the bruisy light before it bursts against the floating weed-ceiling two hundred feet overhead.

I reach up to the pig-bladder at my shoulder, pinch the reed-valve between my teeth, and draw a lungful of the sweet, plant-fermented air. Then I let go of the bridle and let my heavy, calcified knee pull me slowly, feet-first, back down toward the warm dark to find the next peg.
