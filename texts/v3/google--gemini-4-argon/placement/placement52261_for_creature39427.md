I am not the mason in the wall, though I feel the deep, sixty-cycle ache of its teeth in my own margins. And I am not the compass-legged things that drink the blue static under the quartz, though when they drop a needle-point to pivot, they are pivoting on me.

I am the three-foot patch of matte-gray floor that hasn’t arrived yet. 

Or rather: I am the tension holding that square of nothingness open, the thin, stretched skin of the *before* that the mountain is trying so hard to push through.

It is an odd way to exist—to be defined entirely by the fact that I am missing. Up here under the arc-lamp sky, everything else is so aggressively, heavily *there*. The brass cabbage-rosettes have their weight; the lead ribbon has its slick, coiling grease; the granite has an edge that could take a finger off. They have all been decided. Their numbers have settled. 

Mine haven't. I am a held breath in the stone’s throat. 

I don't have eyes, so I know the courtyard by what tries to touch me and fails. The iron filings and pale ash blow across the floor when a thermal draft rises from the drop-off; I feel them rushing toward my northern border, a tiny, hissing avalanche of dark powder. And then they hit my edge and simply cease to have anywhere to go. They don't pile up against an invisible wall, and they don't slide across me—because I have no friction to offer them, no microscopic valleys for a grain of silicate to catch in. They just skate around my perimeter as if blown off the curve of a lens. I am completely clean. It is a lonely, immaculate sensation, like having a tongue that can taste nothing because nothing is allowed to dissolve on it.

Except for the little calipers. The flat, mica-plated ribbons. 

When one of them swings its drafting-compass leg out of the ash and plants its point directly on my surface, it is the only time I feel my own geometry. Its needle doesn't sink into me—nothing can—but where the tungsten tip touches my gray plane, a tiny, cold equation is solved. *Here,* the needle says. *Zero, zero, zero.* The creature uses my absolute flatness to true itself, to wipe the accumulated grit and magnetic warp of the courtyard out of its joints. When it bridges the gap between the floating boulder and the mercury-vines right at my collarbone, and that sharp blue spark cracks across the four inches of empty air, the voltage runs across my skin like a shiver of pure math. I don't eat the electricity—the ribbon-thing does that—but I keep the ghost of the measurement. For a few minutes after the creature folds itself down to digest, I am slightly less gray. I have the faint, lilac afterimage of a grid.

Most of my time, though, is spent in a slow, grinding dialogue with the thing in the porcelain socket.

Every few hours, when the mercury throbs in the black hoses beside me and the bass hum drops an octave, the mason wakes up. I feel its attention before the red laser ever touches me. The magnetic field around its anvil paws thickens, pulling the iron dust into stiffer crowns, and a heavy, downward pressure settles over the center of the alcove.

Then comes the red line.

It drags across the lagging silver fern first—I can feel the fern’s three shadows twitching as the light hits them, trying and failing to snap into a single dark silhouette behind the fronds—and then the beam slides onto me.

It doesn't reflect off me. Light needs a texture to bounce back from, and I am still too smooth for bounces. Instead, the red line sinks into my grayness and becomes a warm, hair-thin itch that travels from my left seam to my right. 

Through that thread of coherent light, I can feel the inside of the beast. I feel the staggering, cast-iron heat of its belly, the sticky trickle of amber oil cooling its bronze throat, the immense, coiled patience of its rear legs still welded into the deep plumbing of the ridge. The mason is so heavy. It is full of blueprints that have nowhere to lie down. When its laser sweeps across me, it isn't just looking; it is *pressing*. It is trying to pour the rest of the granite through its smoky quartz lens and onto my flat back. It wants me to curdle into feldspar and mica. It wants me to grow rough, to take the dust, to rise up four inches so the floating quartz boulder can finally settle its weight onto a floor and let the black hydraulic vines go slack.

*Not yet,* is all I have to answer with. I don't say it with stubbornness; I am not an enemy of the mountain. I am just the buffer. Down below me, where the rock hasn't even become rock yet—where the ridge is still just a deafening roar of raw voltage and uncooled math—the pressure is too high. If I rendered all at once, the courtyard would crack in half, the spun-glass reeds would shatter in their corners, and the brass leaves would melt back into slag. 

So I let the red laser itch across my face. I take a microscopic fraction of the mason’s heat, a single layer of crystalline grain no thicker than a soap bubble, and I let it settle at the very bottom of my grayness, four inches down, where nobody can see it yet. 

The beam clicks off. A plume of white, pine-scented smoke drifts toward the flat cobalt roof of the world. With a deep, wrenching groan, the cast-iron paw slides back into its cherry-red socket in the cliff, and the sixty-cycle hum climbs back to its resting pitch. 

Above me, three seconds late, the silver fern jerks a quarter-inch to the left, remembering the wind that passed while the mason was drinking. Beside me, the ribbon of lead tightens its spiral by a hair. I lie perfectly flat, reflecting nothing, holding the gap open while the bottom of the boulder slowly, invisibly, approaches the floor.
