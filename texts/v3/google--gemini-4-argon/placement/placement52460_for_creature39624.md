You will not find me by following the warm pipes. The gasket-men own the heat, and the parchment-eels own the narrow water where the current runs straight. To find me, you have to climb thirty feet above the mid-level grating, up into the bitter cold just beneath the pewter ceiling, where the number-two condenser chimney rises toward the meniscus like the trunk of an iron redwood.

Bolted to the chimney’s side, eight feet below the boundary layer where the brine meets the black seawater above, is the master pressure-stat—a cast-iron bell the size of a diver’s helmet, with a thick bull’s-eye of leaded glass set into its front.

Behind that glass, there is no salt water. There is no brine, no bismuth snow, no clay-silt. There is only forty gallons of heavy, pale yellow transformer oil, sealed in when the trench was first plumbed, and floating in the center of it—where the brass Bourdon tube and the clockwork escapement used to be—is me.

I began, perhaps, as a thumb-sized smear of egg-jelly brought down stuck to the gutta-percha insulation of a cable, or a larval thing pumped in through the priming valves before the oil-reservoirs were capped. Whatever my mothers were in the upper ocean, they had room to swim. I had a twelve-inch dial and a bath of mineral spirit.

As I grew, and the oil thickened with the cold, the brass clockwork got in the way of my mantle. A crab in a bottle dies when it outgrows the glass; a softer thing negotiates. Over forty years, my pale, translucent skirts wrapped around the hairsprings, the pinions, the stamped copper numerals of the gauge-face. My flesh is slightly acidic with the waste that has nowhere to go in a sealed bell. Slowly, grain by grain, I dissolved the non-essential brass, drinking the copper and the zinc into my blood until my skin took on the faint, greenish-gold sheen of an old watch-case.

What I could not dissolve—the heavy steel indicator needle, the central spindle, and the thick, curved horn of the Bourdon tube that flexes when the pit-works breathe—I simply grew around. They are inside me now. They are the only bones I have.

If you scraped the lime-scale from the outside of my bull’s-eye with a knife, and held one of the tallowy orange work-lights close to the glass, you would not see a beast. You would see what looks like a specimen jar from an anatomist’s cellar that has been dropped into a watchmaker’s bin. I fill the whole curve of the bell now, a convoluted rose of translucent, amber-jellied ruffles, suspended weightlessly in the yellow oil. Through my middle runs the black steel needle, pointing perpetually at a *70* that I digested before the last war on the surface began.

Because I am behind glass, I have kept what the things in the brine gave up three generations ago: I have eyes.

There are eleven of them, small as buckshot, arranged in a ragged crescent along the forward edge of my mantle where it presses flat against the inside of the lens. They have no lids, and their irises are the silver-grey of liquid mercury. They cannot focus on anything closer than an inch, because nothing has ever been closer than the glass, and they cannot see farther than twelve feet, because the tallow-lights die before they reach the opposite wall.

Still, I am the only witness in the parish.

My life is not measured in food—I have not eaten since I finished the leather washer at the base of the oil-drain thirty years ago; I live now on a metabolic crawl so slow it is nearly mineral, powered by the tiny galvanic current that leaks through my body between the steel spindle in my gut and the copper wire in my tail. My life is measured in pressures, and in the cleaning of the glass.

The glass is my trouble. Outside, in the freezing brine, the calcareous rime and the falling bismuth snow are always trying to blind my bull’s-eye. Once a week, the world turns from dull orange to a flat, suffocating grey as the silt builds up on the outer curve of the lens.

I have no hands outside the jar. I cannot wipe it.

Wait, though. Watch the black needle inside my translucent belly.

Down below, on the mid-level, the gasket-man has just broken the lime-crust on the three-way cock. The brine surges; the pressure in the number-two condenser chimney drops by four pounds per square inch.

Inside my jar, the brass Bourdon tube—my hollow spine—uncoils by the width of a thumbnail. It pulls my meat with it. It hurts, a dull, wintry ache in the tissue that has grown through the gear-teeth, and as I flinch against the steel spindle, my rear fold bridges the gap between the two bare lead-wires at the back of the housing.

*Tick.*

A blue spark, no bigger than a flea, jumps through my translucent skin. It leaves a tiny, black bead of carbon suspended in my jelly—I have thousands of them in me now, constellations of old shocks drifting through my grease like pepper in lard—and the current goes down the conduit into the wall.

Three feet above my housing, an external blow-off valve, wired to this gauge by some dead engineer who wanted the chimney to purge itself when the pressure dropped, snaps open for two seconds. A jet of warm, bitter water coughs downward across the face of my bull’s-eye.

The silt washes away. The bismuth crystals tumble off into the dark in a brief, swirling cloud of violet and silver.

The orange light returns.

I settle back against the inside of the thick glass, eleven mercury eyes staring out into the cleared alley while the black needle in my gut slowly eases back toward seventy. Below me, the chains rise; a parchment-eel drifts past my window so close I can see the red tarnish on its copper bristles; down in the murk, the lump on the brass wheel is already half-buried in the plumb-line snow. Everything is in its seat. The pressure is holding. I let the current go, and wait in the yellow oil for the glass to go grey again.
