I am not a single thing here. To be a single thing in the Throat—to have a hide, an inside and an outside, a core that must be defended from the dry bake—is too much labor. Even the Miller, who has given up almost everything that makes a man, still has to pry and grease and wedge his way through the dark to keep his barrel-ribs from being crushed. 

I would not want the anxiety of thumbs. 

At first, looking down into the shaft, I think I might be the river-cobbles at the bottom: dense, foreign, brought up from a cold, wet world that no longer exists in any memory here, holding the spike of the vent’s heat longer than the native rock. To be a baffle. To be shifted six inches to the left by a gray, greasy hand, and by that six-inch shift, to save a thousand torpid platter-spiders high on the eastern wall from losing their grip and falling into the centipede-mattress. There is a deep, silent dignity in being the ballast of an engine.

Or I think I might be the octagonal bar of meteoric iron. Pinched thin in the middle by ten thousand years of palms. Resting against the hot, limpet-crusted spine of the mechanic, waiting to be wedged into the seam between the living lime and the primary basalt. To be the only straight line in a world of curves and shells. To translate the slow, three-minute straightening of a bowed leg into the tearing-canvas *shhh-tik* of a falling reef.

But both of those are still too hard. They resist the chamber. The cobble is slowly being abraded by the shoveling beetles; the iron bar is losing a microscopic film of itself to the Miller’s gray calluses every century. 

If I let myself sink further into the heat, past the geometry of the niches, I find where I actually belong.

I am the grease. 

Not the tallow-cake rendered from the winter die-off—that is dead fat, a tool, a roof-patch smeared onto the ironwood beams to keep them from splintering. I am the other grease. The slow, flameless, waxy secretion that weeps from the Miller’s wrists and the creases behind his knees, the stuff born from raw hydrogen sulfide and iron-gas in the dark chemistry of his pleated belly.

I am the treaty between the mechanic and the machine.

Without me, the Throat is all dry friction. Chitin on chitin, slate on rhyolite, horn on lime—a world constantly threatening to sand itself down into a choking powder or spark a smoldering dust-fire in the litter. I am the only wetness that is not water. I cannot boil away in the twenty-minute vent-surge; I only soften, going from the consistency of cold beeswax to a warm, heavy oil that smells faintly of old iron and bitter mustard.

I live on the threshold. When the Miller leans his barrel-chest against a weeping fissure in the rock to breathe, I am the seal around the rim of his torso. I hold the vacuum. I press into the tiny pores of the sulfur-crusted stone on one side, and into the grey, graphite-like skin of the man on the other. Through me, the mountain passes into the mechanic without burning him.

And when the breathing is done, and he unhooks his heels to swim down through the three-deep shingles of the platter-spiders, I am what he leaves behind. 

When his long, flat hand slips beneath a forty-pound arachnid to nudge it sideways, a microscopic smear of me transfers from his knuckles to the underside of its slate carapace. The spider does not strike because I smell like the deep vent, already digested and made safe. The bristle-millipedes that part around his shins like grain around a mill-post brush against me, and their dry, rasping plates slide easier over their neighbors for three days afterward. 

I do not stay with the Miller. That is the best part of what I am. I am constantly being given away to the walls.

A tiny bead of me catches on the cartilaginous fingers of one of the pale, hand-shaped filter-things as it brushes the Miller’s knee. The filter-thing draws its fingers through its mouth-slit, and I am taken in—not to be digested, because I am too stubborn and mineral-heavy for its weak gut, but to coat its joints from the inside, keeping its pale fan supple in the baking draft. When that filter-thing dies a decade later and drops into the five-foot mattress on the floor, a blind shoveling beetle eats the husk, and I pass into the beetle’s shell, making its black armor gleam dimly in the bruised-purple light of the bottom vents. 

And when the beetle dies in the winter cooling, and the Miller wades waist-deep into the centipedes to harvest the husks and render them into a tallow-cake for the ironwood beams—there I am again. Back in his thick, curved fingernails. Ready to be smeared onto the black, petrified wood of the upper sockets, or worked into the braided basalt rope that holds the copper bell.

I am the memory of the chamber’s touch. Because of me, the ironwood beam knows the belly of the floor-beetle; the floor-beetle knows the knee of the Miller; the knee of the Miller knows the weeping crack in the primary rhyolite. 

During the twenty minutes of noon, when the razor of white mountain light slices down from the sky, the Miller holds up his piece of shed spider-shell to catch the glare. The shell is dull, chalky with calcium, a poor mirror. Before he tilts it toward the dark crevices of the rope-sockets, he rubs his thumb across its flat surface. 

He leaves a thin, warm film of me on the slate. 

In the blinding white stripe of the sun, I catch the light. Just for twenty minutes, the dry, chalky shell becomes a wet, gleaming eye. I bounce a weak, yellow smudge of the outside sky into the deep dark under the beams, showing the thumb where the basalt rope is fraying, showing the heat-pits that the geometry is still holding, before the copper bell overhead begins to drip with warm scorpions, the vent sighs open at the root of the world, and we all slide back into the dark.
