I am the third strut from the eastern cleavage of the clearing, though "east" has no meaning here; it is simply the direction where the basalt columns thicken and the chord of the rock drops by half a step. 

For four hundred years—if you measure by the slow, generational migrations of the mantles across the lower spires—I have been the lintel-brace between a needle of black tourmaline and a long, pale spar of orthoclase that wants, very badly, to slide three inches downward into the dark. 

I do not let it.

My left feet are locked around the tourmaline’s six-sided waist, my double-thumbs hooked into a pair of shallow notches I chipped there myself when my blood still moved like warm tallow through my veins. My right hands are overhead, wedged deep into the V-joint where the orthoclase rests against the roof of the cairn. The dry tendon they wrapped around my wrists when my knees finally locked has long since cured into the grain of my own horn-skin; you could not find the seam between the binding and the bone with a fresh-knapped flake of glass.

I am very dry now, and very clear.

When I was wet—when I still carried the heavy leather pouch of rendering-fat at my groin and spent my days hanging from the selenite levers with my brothers—the world was muffled by my own meat. The slow, three-minute draw of air through my snout-scrolls made a constant, rushing roar in my skull, like sand slipping down a chute. My heart, even beating once every forty seconds, was a clumsy wooden mallet thumping against the inside of my ribs, drowning out the finer tremors of the distant lattice. I had to press my jaw hard against the stone to hear past my own living, and even then, the grease on my skin and the wet pulp of my marrow softened the return wave.

Now the tallow-pot has had the last of my grease. My marrow is a dry, iron-laced honeycomb. My chest is an open arch, scraped clean and rubbed with gypsum dust, and the acoustic pan of my brow has tightened over the centuries until it rings like a flake of prime phonolite.

I am no longer deafened by breath. I have become a pure conductor.

Through my lower heels, I feel the deep, tectonic ache of the tourmaline root three miles down, where the heat begins to soften the crystal habits. Through my upper thumbs, I take the sheer, downward grievance of the orthoclase spar—fourteen tons of pale stone leaning its shoulder against my collarbone, day after day, century after century. The load passes through my mineralized spine, down the long, flat curve of my thigh-bones, and drains safely into the black pillar below. I am the hyphen between two sentences of rock that would otherwise shatter each other.

It is not a lonely station. Nothing in the lattice is ever truly alone, because nothing is ever disconnected from the grain.

Six inches from my hollow ribs, inside the dry-stone cage I helped wedge together before my hips calcified, my great-great-granddaughter is greasing the elbows of her mate. I do not see them, even when a stress-slip in the lower reaches throws its brief, violet ghost through the chinks of the hut. I know them by the shift of their weight on the floor-spars. 

When they take each other’s wrists to speak, the tiny, rhythmic clicks of their fingernails and the rolling snap of their forearm tendons travel down through their heels, into the selenite floorboards, up the tourmaline pillar, and straight through my locked ankles into the hollow vault of my skull.

*The fault at the Three-Way-Split is tightening,* her thumb-bone says against his radius. *The pitch has risen a quarter-tone since the last sleep. It will need the iron wedge before we harvest the lower web.*

*We will take the long lever,* his collarbone answers hers, a deeper, duller tap that settles into my ribs like a warm thumb. *And the gray maul. The small one’s hands are hardened enough to hold the lantern-crust.*

Then the small one—sixty years old perhaps, still supple, still full of quick, expensive moisture—crawls out of the doorway to reach the latrine-ledge. To get there, it does what I did when I was small and wet: it uses the arch of my pelvis as a handhold.

Four warm, greasy fingers and two opposing thumbs clamp onto my iliac crest. For three minutes, as the young one shifts its balance and waits for its breath-valve to click shut, its weight hangs from my hip. The warmth of its palm—that ninety-eight-degree heat I had forgotten the thickness of—soaks a millimeter deep into my cured hide. A tiny smear of fresh mantle-tallow from its skin comes off on my bone, smelling sharply of iron-salt and dead glider-fat.

As the young one hangs there, its jaw brushes my knee. Without thinking—the way a cliner always tests the soundness of a hold—it gives a tiny, sub-audible hum through its teeth.

The note enters my femur. Because I am hollow, and because my joints are fused with calcium into a single unbroken curve from toe to finger, I take the child’s small, flat note and I *sweeten* it. It rings up my spine, picks up the deep, taut *F-sharp* of the fourteen-ton orthoclase spar I am holding back, blends with the dry, glassy *C* of the tourmaline needle below, and returns to the child’s jawbone as a rich, three-part chord that says: *The roof holds. The bridge is sound. The tension is kept.*

The young one’s fingers relax a fraction of an inch against my hip—the cliner equivalent of a sigh—before it unhooks its lower heel and edges out into the dark toward the lithophone.

Ten minutes later, a half-mile out, the suspended obsidian blade is struck. 

The air does not carry it to me, though the stagnant ozone shivers slightly against my dry nasal scrolls. It comes through the stone. A long, low, rolling hum that pours through the millions of intersections in the thicket, dropping through the basalt columns, shivering across the glass harps of the weavers—where three pale, six-foot spiders simultaneously draw their waxen knees a hair’s breadth tighter against their hubs—and washing up through the roots of our clearing.

All around the stone cage, my family answers the wave. Not the warm ones inside, who are already sleeping coiled around the tallow-pot, their noses tucked into each other’s armpits to share the dampness of their exhalations. The others. 

The grandfather who forms the arch of the western bridge; the two sisters lashed head-to-heel to splint a cracked feldspar beam above the ceiling; the ancient, headless torso wedged beneath the threshold stone whose name was lost from the gypsum knots forty generations ago. As the vibration of the lithophone passes through the clearing, every one of us hums in our own pitch—dry bone and iron-hard sinew taking the shock, dividing it, passing it safely around the hollow sphere where the soft ones sleep.

Far off, in the deep sponge below, a spire of quartz gives way to the load. The flash of blue light strikes my brow-pan—not as brightness, for I never had eyes to know what blue was, but as a microscopic snap of electrostatic release that makes the dried salt on my knuckles tingle once. 

A flake of mica, loosened by the slip, begins to fall through the absolute still of the dark. In an hour, or two, it will land on the flat of my shoulder blade. And there I will hold it, along with the roof, in the warm and silent dark, while the iron teeth of the mantles tick away the age.
