I am on the third story of a leaning watchtower near the eastern talus-slope, and my left shoulder is wedged hard against a weeping lintel to keep my warmth from drifting away into the fog.

I am not very old—perhaps thirty winters, though we do not count them because the seasons down here only change by the thickness of the ice-rind in the water-rills and the color of the moss on our own shoulders. Mine is turning the rusty, iron-red of late autumn, which means I have been working too high up, where the grey light from the rim actually touches the roofs, rather than down in the green-black gloom of the canal slits where the liverworts thrive. My breath comes out in slow, shallow puffs through my nose, directed downward into the stiff collar of my sedge-thatch so the steam doesn't rise and give me away to the cliff-eagles that sometimes drop out of the white sky like falling stones.

My work is the rills. They call us *Rill-Wardens* if you were to put words to it, though in our flint-talk I am simply *Two-Drop-Scrape*—two quick taps of the thumbnail against the dark stone in my palm, followed by a long, gritty slide of the rock across my calloused knuckle. 

The city dies if the water goes where it wants to go. The water wants to go into the dry-stone walls, to pool in the flat beds between the courses, to freeze at midnight and push a four-story tenement out into the canal by breakfast. So the builders who came before—whose femurs are now eight feet underwater, wrapped in the pale, trembling jelly of the Silt-Sleeved—carved grooves into the upper faces of the limestone slabs before they laid them. Little troughs, no wider than two fingers, that catch the constant weeping from the boundary cliffs and lead it down the outside of the masonry, jumping from stone to stone in tiny, silent arcs, down to the stepped alleys, down to the sedge-canals, and out to the black fens in the middle.

I keep the troughs clean.

In my right hand, instead of a lever-pole, I carry the long, curved rib of a mountain goat that fell from the rim when I was a child. The bone is yellow and dense as ivory from soaking in peat-water, and the tip is worn into a neat, rounded chisel. All day, from the time the grey sky turns the color of wet wool to the time it turns the color of black slate, I move sideways along the narrow stone ledges of the tenements, my cleft, horny soles gripping the two-inch lips of the courses. 

*Scrape. Scrape.* 

A bladder-fern has sent a hairy black root into the third-story runnel of the cistern-house. Because the root is there, the water is backing up; because the water is backing up, a trickle is spilling over the lip of the trough and running *behind* the facing-stone below it. Tonight it will freeze. Tomorrow the facing-stone will bulge. In a month, the whole corner will drop into the narrow water-street and crush the willow-traps my sister’s people have set in the reeds.

I hook my left knee over a projecting drip-stone—my joints don't ache from the cold anymore; the fat around my kneecaps is two inches thick and numb as lard—and I slide the goat-rib into the groove. I do not rip the fern out. If you rip a plant in this city, you pull out the stone it’s holding onto. Instead, I sever the root with a slow, rocking pressure of the bone-tip, peel the wet black hairs out of the limestone channel, and tuck the living fern back into a dry vertical joint three inches to the left, packing a wad of cold sphagnum around its base with my thumb. 

Immediately, the blocked water slides forward. It doesn't splash. It glides over the slick film of algae in the groove, reaches the carved spout at the corner, and drops in a single, unbroken silver thread into the catch-basin on the second story below.

Inside the wall against my belly, something shifts. 

I feel it through my thatch-coat before I see it—a warm, greasy vibration against the limestone, accompanied by the thick, tallowy smell of old vole-fur and wet wool. A dark crack between two three-hundred-pound blocks widens by half an inch as the loose shale packing is pushed aside from within. 

Out comes a grey, hairless finger with a nail like a flat spade, followed by a broad, squashed nose and two tiny eyes filmed over like cataracts. It is the old Chink-Wife who owns the third-story flue of this house. Her back is crusted with pale, dry cup-lichens that scrape against the ceiling of her crevice as she squeezes her hinged shoulders up to the light. 

She doesn't look at my face; she looks at my hands. She knows the sound of the goat-rib. The water that was leaking behind the facing-stone was dripping directly onto the dry peat-bed where she keeps her winter litter of young, and she has been trying to plug it from the inside with chewed rush-pith all morning.

I reach into the leather-tough fold of skin under my left armpit, where I carry my food and my grease to keep them from freezing, and I pinch off a walnut-sized lump of mashed sedge-tuber mixed with crushed newt-tails. I don't offer it to her on my palm—she would take the finger too, out of sheer nearsightedness and the eternal hunger of the walls—so I set it on the dry lip of the stone trough between us.

Her spade-fingered hand darts out, faster than a mink’s strike, and drags the grey paste into the dark. In return, before she ribbons herself back into the matrix, she pushes something out of the crack with her snout. It falls onto my bare, grey foot with a tiny, dull *clink*.

I curl my toes around it and bring it up to my eyes. It is a fresh flake of black flint, fallen from the high cliffs during last night’s frost and carried through the hollow highways of the walls by the voles or the Wives, three blocks over from the talus-slope. It is flat, dense, and has a clean, ringing belly. My old talking-stone is worn down to a nub from twenty years of clicking in the fog. 

I tuck the old one into the moss on my shoulder—it will help hold the thatch down in the rare downdrafts—and settle the new flint into the deep, horny groove of my palm. 

Down below, the afternoon fog is beginning to boil up out of the black water of the canal. It rises past the dark green spears of the reeds, swallowing the tethered rush-rafts, swallowing the lower windows where the blind crayfish wave their long, white feelers in the current, swallowing the shiny black liverworts on the foundation courses. In five minutes, the world will be six feet wide, and the temperature will drop until the moisture in my nostrils stiffens into needles.

From somewhere down in the white wool, thirty feet below my heels, comes a tiny, dry sound.

*Tick-tock. Tick.*

My sister’s boy, poling a raft of fresh peat-bricks down the slit, asking if the cistern-house corner is safe to pass under.

I brace my thick, bowed legs against the slippery slate of the second-story eaves, lean my mossy back into the weeping limestone until I am just another swell in the masonry, and bring my blackened thumbnail down against the new flint.

*Tick-tick.*

Clear. Go on through. The house is holding.
