I am at the very lip of the drop, where the pale, waxy granite rolls over and falls straight down into the lavender nothing. 

I am not standing. Standing is for things that intend to go somewhere else before the sun moves, and the sun has been nailed to its forty-five-degree slant for so long that the shadow of my shoulder has stained the talc a slightly darker gray. I am seated, though my pelvis passed through the surface of the dome an age ago. From the waist down, I am submerged in the stone itself, anchored like a tooth in a jawbone. 

My legs, down there in the cold dark of the mountain's core, have lost their certainty. Sometimes I can feel the flat, blind pressure of the granite occupying my marrow; sometimes a long, segmented hound-thing inches through the rock and passes right through my shins, and I feel the dry, chalky tickle of its hundred legs combing through my calf-bones.

They were the Herders once, then the Gleaners. When the metabolic retreat began, they kept their feet so they could walk the basins and skim the froth. I was one who decided that forty paces a day was still an extravagance. Why drag the talc-keels through the tallow when the discrepancies eventually drift to the edge anyway? 

I am a Sinker. A Groyne. There are a few of us spaced out along the rims of the great chasms, half-swallowed by the stone, facing the drop.

Across my lap lies a single, immense slab of unworked native copper, greenless and dull in the dry air, four feet long and curved slightly like a trough. It does not rest on my thighs; it intersects my femurs, pinning me here with a smoky, gray blur of shared space that smells faintly of pennies and ozone. My hands—fused long ago into flat paddles of translucent horn—are buried wrist-deep inside the metal at either end. I am part of the circuit now.

Around my chest, a Glass-Brier has grown up out of the packed beasts behind me. I did not prune it. Over centuries, its brittle, black silica branches have stitched right through my layered felt wrappings and into the dry cedar-bark of my ribs, exiting through my sternum in neat, ninety-degree angles. It uses me as a trellis; I use it as an antenna. Whenever a six-legged tortoise shifts its weight half an inch a mile away behind me, the tiny pop of static travels through the crowded shells, enters the roots of the brier, and runs through the black glass inside my chest like a thread of cold mint. 

I do not turn my head to look at the plateau. My neck calcified into a solid pillar of chalk before the last wind died. My gaze is fixed entirely on the air five feet out over the abyss.

Out there, suspended in the pale lavender haze where gravity has forgotten its business, hangs the Flotsam. 

There are three chipped flint hand-axes, a split femur from a beast that had marrow once, and seven hundred pale, baseball-sized spheres of calcified knuckles that were slowly pushed over the edge by the crowding millions behind me over the last ten thousand years. They did not fall. They hit the pocket of dead weightlessness off the lip and simply bobbed there, accumulating like dry foam in the eddy of a river.

Because they are crowded even in the empty air, they intersect. A flint axe-head passes halfway through a feathered knuckle-sphere. And where they overlap in the nothingness, with no stone beneath them to ground the math, the geometric tears are much larger than the ones in the valley. 

They hang in the air like panes of mica. Through them, you do not see the lavender haze of the chasm. You see *elsewhere*. 

One rectangular tear, no larger than my palm, shows the inside of a granite dome three leagues west—solid, blind gray stone. Another, just below it, shows the slate-blue sky from an angle that does not exist here, empty and blinding. And a third—my favorite, the one I have been watching for what might be three centuries—shows a patch of dark, wet mud, glistening with water that has not existed at this altitude since the Fault went quiet. Nothing moves in the mud, either, or perhaps it moves so fast that to my eyes it looks like a flat brown smear.

My job, if a thing half-turned to talc can be said to have a job, is to keep the edge from fraying backward into the herd.

Over decades, the floating cluster of bones and axes drifts closer to the lip, drawn by the faint electrostatic pull of the millions of carapaces behind me. As the tears in the air approach the rock, the edge of the cliff begins to lose its nerve. The waxy granite starts to go smoky and double-exposed, threatening to uncalculate itself and let the whole valley slide sideways into an error.

Right now, a cube of inverted light the size of a sheep's skull has detached itself from a floating flint axe and drifted inward. It is six inches from the copper trough in my lap. 

For four years, I have been preparing to meet it. 

The signal started in my dried walnut of a heart—a single, sluggish contraction that sent a pulse of static along the copper wire lashed to my collarbones. Two years ago, the impulse reached my shoulders. A year ago, the calcium pads in my elbows gave a single, sharp *click* that echoed out over the chasm. 

Now, as the cold, static-charged cube of broken space brushes the fringe of my felt sleeve, I tilt the copper trough. Just a fraction of a degree. My horn-paddles shift inside the metal; the smoky blur at my wrists hisses like dry grass.

The edge of the copper catches the bottom of the floating cube.

*Thrum.* 

The sound is deeper than the ones in the valley. It vibrates down through my submerged pelvis, humming into the deep roots of the mountain. The Glass-Brier growing through my chest shivers, its black silica twigs chiming softly against my collarbones. 

I do not lift the discrepancy to my alabaster face-shield to eat it all at once. It is too large; it would blow the top of my skull off into a geometric stutter. Instead, I let it sit in the copper trough between my thighs, where it slowly dissolves into the metal like a block of dry ice. 

The cold static floods up through my wrists, through the brier-glass in my ribs, and into the dark, hollow space behind my eyes. 

For a moment—a moment that probably lasts a month in the frantic, wet world far below—I am gorgeously, blindingly full. I taste the weightlessness of the chasm. I taste the phantom mud in the third window. My optic nerves, starved and patient, flare with gray-violet lightning as the excess geometry bleeds off through the top of my head in a faint, shimmering mirage of a spire that isn't there.

The floating cluster of axes and knuckle-spheres out over the drop settles back six inches, repelled by the discharge. The cliff edge grows crisp and solid again around my waist. 

Behind me, perhaps fifty yards up the dome, a Gleaner is passing. I have known it was there for a week because the static in the Glass-Brier has been leaning slightly to the left, drawn toward the copper staff in its hand. It will not reach me; its trajectory will carry it past my shoulder at a distance of ten paces sometime next year. 

We will not look at one another. We do not need to. As the slow wake of my feeding—a faint ripple of resolved math and ozone—washes up the slope through the packed alabaster shells, I feel the Gleaner pause in its forty-pace day. It leans its slender weight into the windless air, turning its paper-tortoise mask a fraction of an inch toward the chasm to let the leftovers of my meal wash over its dry felt shroud. 

Then, a single chitinous claw ticks against the stone nearby, and I begin the long, five-year process of lowering my elbows by a quarter of an inch, waiting for the next piece of the sky to come loose.
