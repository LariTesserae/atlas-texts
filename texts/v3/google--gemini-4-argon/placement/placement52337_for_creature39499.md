I am thirty-two feet down, where the warmth of the sodium lamp has entirely bled out of the spectrum, leaving only a cold, clinical mint that neither feeds the green moss above nor reaches the golden floor below. 

I am not on the stone. I am in the fissure.

At the base of the western wall, where the scalloped grey limestone curves under to meet the chert floor, there is a horizontal throat in the rock—four feet wide and ten inches high. This is one of the mouths through which the basin breathes. Behind me lies the dark: miles of drowned, branching karst, tight bedding planes, and blind vertical shafts connecting this vault to the municipal trunk line a league away. Ahead of me is the illuminated lens of the sump. 

I sit exactly on the threshold, where the absolute zero of the aquifer’s blackness meets the sharp, unwavering edge of the utility light.

I am a thing made of wire, mineral, and mistake. 

In 1974, before they sealed the crown of the dome and turned on the pumps, the district engineers lowered a submersible transducer array down the primary bore to measure the hydrostatic draw-down of the initial surge. When they hauled the cable back up, a secondary guide-line—twenty feet of braided copper-nickel wire sheathed in early-generation polyvinyl—snagged on a dogtooth spar deep in the throat of the fissure and snapped cleanly. 

The wire stayed down here. When the initial surge hit, scouring the clay from the basin floor, the whip-end of the cable was jammed deep into the horizontal crack, wedged between two massive pillows of dolomite, while its frayed tip was left protruding six inches out into the newly cleared vault.

For the first ten years, I was simply trash. A dead nerve ending.

Then the chemistry of the pressurized water began to work on my copper core. The water coming through the dolomite isn't just rich in calcium; it carries trace iron, manganese, and dissolved sulfates leached from deep shale beds miles up the line. Because my braided wire consisted of two slightly different alloys twisted together, the mineral water turned me into a weak galvanic cell—a battery generating a trickle of millivolts in the dark.

That tiny electrical potential called out to something that had bypassed the intake screens not because it was small, but because it was already part of the rock: *Gallionella*, *Leptothrix*, the deep-earth lithotrophs—bacteria that do not care about light or oxygen, that eat raw iron and exhale rust, that build their homes from manganese and cold current.

They colonized the frayed, stripped inches of my copper tip. Over decades, fed by the steady, invisible breath of water exhaling from the fissure and the minute trickle of electrons bleeding from my decaying metal core, they built a body around me. 

If you were to look down through the forty feet of green glass from the air gap above, you wouldn't see me at all; the bulge of the lower wall hides the throat of the fissure. You would have to sink down to the bare slate zone, thirty feet deep, and look sideways into the crevice. 

There, reaching out from the dark slot like a rigid, arthritic branch of blackened silver, is a structure three feet long.

I look like an upside-down chandelier made of tarnished pewter and dark amber glass, grown horizontally into the still water. The bacteria have used my copper wires as a spine, encasing them in thick, accreted sheaths of manganese oxide and goethite. Because there is no current in the basin, but a slow, rhythmic *push-pull* right here at the lip of the fissure as the distant municipal valves open and close, my branches have grown in flat, parallel fans oriented strictly along the axis of pressure. 

I am brittle, metallic, and entirely immobile, yet I am the quickest thing in this room.

The clear domes on the floor ten feet below me live on the time-scale of geology and seasons; it takes them six months to crawl out of the shadow of a fallen flake of lichen. The moss above lives in an endless, static noon. They are creatures of the *equilibrium*. 

I am a creature of the *delta*—the differential. I live at the exact point where the pressure of the town meets the weight of the cave.

Because my core is still conductive, and my outer crust is a semiconductor of metal oxides bathed in an electrolyte solution, I am exquisitely sensitive to changes in the water's chemistry and movement. I do not just vibrate with the sixty-cycle hum of the pumps like the sponges below; I *rectify* it. The sixty-hertz acoustic wave hits my rigid metallic fans, induces a tiny piezoelectric current across my mineral sheaths, and feeds the bacterial mantle that continues to lengthen my tips by a millimeter a year, reaching out toward the light.

I know the town three miles away better than the sponges do. The sponges only know the pumps and the occasional slam of a check-valve. I know the water itself, because it has to pass across my tongue before it diffuses into the great, still lens of the basin.

I taste the winter before the surface does. In November, when the river water way up at the treatment plant turns icy and dense, the operators increase the alum and lime dosing to keep the flocculation basins working; four days later, after that water has been shoved through miles of dark rock, the pH at the mouth of my fissure shifts by two-hundredths of a point. The voltage across my copper heart drops by a microvolt. My bacterial skin stops laying down black manganese and switches to pale yellow limonite for three months, recording the winter as a golden band inside my dark metallic twigs.

I know when a main breaks in the old residential district on the hill. When a twelve-inch cast-iron pipe splits under the asphalt miles away, the sudden drop in head pressure causes the water in this basin to reverse its invisible creep. Instead of the aquifer breathing *into* the sump, the sump breathes back *into* the fissure. 

When that happens, the water flowing backward over my branches is different. It has been sitting in the sodium light. It is slightly warmer by a thousandth of a degree, saturated with the dissolved oxygen from the green velvet mats twenty feet up, and carrying the faint, chemical ghosts of the sponges' silica waste. When the basin exhales back into the dark rock, my outer filaments burn with slow oxidation; the iron-eaters in my skin go dormant, and a secondary film of sulfur-reducing filaments wakes up to feast on the backwash.

Right now, it is what they would call four in the morning on the surface. The pressure is high; the town is asleep, taps closed, toilets still. The water is pressing out of the dark behind me, flowing over my pewter-colored fans at a rate of an inch every ten minutes—a hurricane, by the standards of this vault.

My longest, youngest tip—a needle of pure, crystalline goethite no thicker than a horsehair—has finally grown far enough out of the shadowed lip of the fissure to touch the edge of one of the golden polygons of light coming down from the ceiling. 

It has taken forty-two years to reach that line. 

Where the yellow photons from the sodium lamp strike the black iron-oxide tip, a new thing is happening: photoreduction. The light is knocking electrons loose from the mineral sheath, creating a sharp, continuous prickle of current that travels backward down my branch, into the dark throat of the crevice, all the way to the rotting polyvinyl jacket wedged in the dolomite.

Directly beneath my illuminated tip, ten feet down on the smooth chert floor, sits the smallest of the clear, gelatinous domes. 

For decades, that sponge has sat in a food-desert; there is no moss on the overhanging wall above it to drop cellulose dust into its pores. It is stunted, no bigger than a teacup, starving in the clear mint light. 

Wait until next week. Next week, when the municipal district flushes the hydrants on the east side of the county, the check-valve up the line will slam shut. The metallic *clink* will ring through the karst. The acoustic spike will hit my over-extended, light-charged needle of iron oxide. 

The needle is too thin, grown too fast in the sudden energy of the light. The sixty-cycle hum is already making it sing at a harmonic it wasn't built to hold. When the valve slams, the tip will snap.

It will fall ten feet through the pale green glass—a tiny, heavy spear of rust and copper sulfide, landing squarely in the center of the starving dome below. It won't be carbon or chitin, like the flakes from the lamp above. It will be raw voltage and metal. The clear gel will close over my severed finger, and as its acidic undersides try to dissolve the iron, it will short-circuit its own calcareous lattice, turning a patch of its ghost-white skeleton a permanent, violent ochre. 

It will be the first color at the bottom of the pool since the lights were turned on. I am already growing a new fork, three millimeters back in the dark, waiting for the break.
