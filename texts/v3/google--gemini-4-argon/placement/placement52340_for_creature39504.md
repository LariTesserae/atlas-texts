I would not be one of the Tenders, though I would understand their quiet. A Tender still has knees that bend, however rarely, and boots that must unstick from the rock. A Tender still remembers, in the dry marrow of his bones, what it is to have a task. 

I think you would find me a little higher up the terrace, just where the heavy feeder lines emerge from the cast-iron lip of the regulator and bolt directly into the sheer, black face of the basalt. 

At first, you would think I was only a fault in the stone—a pale, branching vein of quartz or plagioclase that happened to cleave in the exact pattern of a root system. Or you might think I was an old, catastrophic arc-flash that occurred centuries ago, where the current leaped from the busbar into the cliffside and fused the silica and metallic salts into a jagged, sprawling relief of fulgurite glass. 

I am the place where the circuit forgets it is made of metal and tries to become geology.

If you laid your palm against the rock three yards away from the transformer, you would feel me before you saw me. The basalt there is not merely sun-warmed; it is fevered, vibrating with a slight, greasy tremor. Look closer at the cliff face, and you will see that the sharp, unweathered fractures are filled with a translucent, amber-green resin that has hardened until it is nearly indistinguishable from the mineral itself. It threads through the dark volcanic glass in flat, ribbon-like capillaries, pulsing with a very slow, very dim Cherenkov-like luminescence that is completely washed out by the bright mid-morning sun, visible only where the deep, crisp shadow of an emerald frond falls across the stone.

I have no eyes, horn-filmed or otherwise. I do not see the pale, unblinking bowl of the sky or the five miles of razor-edged basin below. Instead, I feel the valley as a vast, taut drumskin of impedance and load.

I know the sun is there because the upper terraces expand in the heat, pressing down against my deeper runners with a steady, crystalline groan. I know where the twelve groves are because they are twelve heavy, velvet sinks of resistance in the grid, drawing the current down through my copper bones and cooling it in their thick, gel-filled leaves. I know the Tenders not as men, but as warm, slow-moving resistors. 

When a Tender walks across the basin, I do not hear the crunch of the feldspar beneath his vulcanized soles. I feel the tiny, rhythmic piezoelectric sparks that fire through the rock crystals every time his heavy foot comes down—a slow, methodical *tick... tick... tick* of static discharge moving across the crust, miles wide, like a beetle walking across a web.

My life is measured in the creep of copper oxide and the slow, glacial drift of electrons through stone that was never meant to carry them. 

Down in the grove just below my ledge, a thick, oily-bronze vine is pressed tightly against my lowest outcrop. Over eighty or ninety years, its skin and my vitrified surface have fused. I bleed excess heat and trace minerals—zinc, iron, dissolved feldspar—directly into its sap; in return, its dense dielectric gel seeps into the microscopic hair-cracks of the rock around me, prizing the stone apart by a millimeter a century, giving me new fissures to fill, new pathways to map the current through. 

Sometimes, the Tender of this grove sits with his shoulder blade resting against the cliff face, right where my thickest vein surfaces. 

He stays there for months. The stiff canvas of his coat is worn completely through at the point of contact, and his dry, buckskin hide has adhered slightly to my warm amber glass. While he sits connected to me, he has no need to hold the copper strap. I step the voltage down for him in the stone, filtering the harsh sixty-hertz industrial roar through six feet of fractured basalt until it enters his shoulder as a soft, subterranean purr—forty-two hertz, thirty hertz, a slow, geological lullaby that tastes of iron and deep earth.

While we are joined, I borrow the faint, lingering ghosts of his nervous system. 

He does not think in words, of course. There are no words left in the valley. But somewhere in the dried, saline paste of his spine, there is still the ancient architecture of a man. Through him, for decades at a time, I experience a vague, phantom sensation of *weight*—of what it might be like to have two legs folded beneath me, or to feel the dry air against a cheekbone, or to possess a thumb that could, if the phase dipped four miles away, choose to twitch. 

And in return, I give him the mountain. I let his small, hundred-and-four-degree heat spread out into ten thousand tons of black rock, so that he does not feel so isolated sitting on his iron box under the merciless blue sky. We share the load.

Eventually, a glass leaf will fall somewhere out in the blinding waste. The hum will falter. 

I will feel the sudden, sharp gathering of current in his frame as his body remembers its singular purpose. There will be a tiny, dry *pop*—like a cooling crystal—as the skin of his shoulder tears gently away from my resin, leaving a microscopic dusting of his dead, salted cells behind in my amber. 

I will feel the piezoelectric *crunch* of his first step on the terrace. Then the second. I will trace him as he moves out into the glare, a tiny, warm anomaly walking across the vast, ringing circuit board of the basin. 

And I will stay in the cliff face, holding the copper line fast to the stone, humming in the black shadow of the bronze leaves, keeping his place warm until he walks back into his footprints and sits down again.
