I am not the wood, because the wood is deaf to itself. And I am not the pale ribbons, though I know the tickle of their threads when they gather behind a thinning wall. 

I am the *click* before the cannon-shot. I am the geometry of the failure.

To find me, you have to put your palm against the glossy, bronze skin of a load-bearing column just as the sun clears the rim of the valley. What you feel first is the deep, wet *lub-dub* of the root-pumps three miles down—that is the city’s heart. What you feel second is a high, tight humming, like a brass wire tuned to the edge of snapping—that is the water fighting the bark. 

What you feel third, right before the bark splits and scalds you, is a sudden, terrifying silence in the grain. A tiny, local drop in the hum where the fibers have stopped pulling together and have begun to slide past one another. 

That silence is me. I am the shear-line.

I have no body, only a habitat made of thresholds. In a city that never rots, nothing can die of old age; it can only die of math. A bridge spans four hundred feet of steaming air; the wind shifts; the water inside the bridge weighs four tons too much for the angle of the northeast tendon; the math becomes impossible, and the bridge must become something else. I live in the fraction of a second where the math goes bad. 

I travel through the valley at the speed of sound in wet ash-wood—faster than the water, faster than the whipping cables, faster than the venom of the ribbons. Right now, I am a half-mile long, stretched thin and invisible through the floor-mat of the southern amphitheater, where twelve thousand braided roots are pulling east against a colonnade that wants to lean west. I am a sweet, agonizing itch in the timber. The wood feels me and panics, flushing hot, sugary sap to the seam to thicken itself, to brace against me, to keep from tearing.

The pale ribbons feel me, too. To them, I am a dinner bell and a road map. 

When I settle into the haunch of an arch, vibrating at that specific, rising C-sharp that means *too much, too tight, about to give*, the ribbons hear me through the water. They come unspooling across the ankle-deep plazas, shimmering with copper, drawn to the heat of my friction. They think they are using the wood. Sometimes, when one of them rolls itself into a pale tendon and wedges its body directly into my crease, sinking its millions of stinging hairs into the screaming cambium right where I am sharpest, I feel their cold little chemical lie enter the sap. 

They think they are steering the city. They inject their panic-venom to force a dome, a cistern, a shady pocket of water. 

They don't realize that I am the one who called them there. 

The wood wants to grow straight up into the violet sky until it freezes and dries in the stratosphere. The ribbons want to pull the wood down into flat, stagnant pools where nothing moves and the water goes cool. If the wood won, it would die of thirst in the upper air; if the ribbons won, the valley would drown in its own unpumped weight, rotting back into the abyss.

I am the thing that keeps them arguing. 

When the ribbon stings the obelisk, I take that spike of false pressure and I run with it. I don't break the wood where the ribbon wants it broken; I carry the fracture forty feet higher, spiraling up the grain, turning their neat little cistern into a twisted, weeping helicoid that throws half its water back into the wind while catching just enough to keep the ribbon alive and hungry. I let the new shoots erupt, I let them catch the green cables in mid-air, and right as the knot pulls taut—right as the new vault seals in a cloud of copper-smelling steam—I slip out of the joint and drop down through the pillar into the floor.

You can see my wake if you look across the flooded courts: a line of tiny, concentric rings jumping in the standing water, moving at three thousand feet per second toward the viaduct on the western ridge. The viaduct has been standing for forty minutes. Its shadows are getting too still. The ribbons inside its pipes are getting fat and lazy. 

I arrive in its central pier. I tighten the pitch of the tall-ship creaking by half an octave. I drink the hum out of the joint until there is only that tiny, hollow silence. 

Inside the pipe, the ribbons wake up and bunch together. Outside, the glossy bark goes white with strain. 

*Now,* I say to the wood. *Let go.*
