I am not at the bottom. The bottom is too loud with meat and pressure, too full of fat and intention. 

I am twelve feet up the north wall, though *up* is a temporary condition. 

When the clay gave way and the dry-dock dropped, the dark shale didn't shear completely clean. Twelve feet above the broken mains, a stubborn nodule of ironstone—harder than the greasy rock around it—refused to part neatly. It tore out of the ascending wall and stayed with the descending one, leaving an overhang no wider than a boot-heel, tilted slightly back toward the dark seam of the rock. 

When the monitoring station fell through the air, one of the heavy porcelain insulators struck this ledge on the way down. It didn't shatter whole; it sheared in half lengthwise, leaving its thick, threaded-lead core and a crescent of curved, white-glazed ceramic jammed hard into the notch behind the ironstone lip. The rest of it went down into the mud to become a tooth. 

I am the pocket that remained. 

Or rather, I am what has filled the four-inch trough between the curved porcelain glaze and the raw, sweating shale. 

At the beginning, in the first week after the drop, I was only a soup of grey clay-dust, three drowned surface-beetles that fell in from the lip forty feet up, and the cold rainwater that ran down the scratch-marks in the stone. I had no heat of my own. Down below, the Lard-Bellows was still small, frantically sealing itself into the suitcase-hutch, and the stone-bladders were only the size of plums, hissing wildly out of rhythm.

Then the steam found the groove.

Because the wall above me is slightly concave—gouged by a boulder as it fell—the warm, wet exhaust from the forty bladders below doesn't go straight up into the dry prairie air. A draft of it curls against the north face, licks up the greasy shale, and catches under my ironstone overhang. Every twenty minutes, a ghost of warm, sulfurous breath hits the cold porcelain crescent and condenses into a single, fat drop of water that smells faintly of boiled wool.

I am the *Sill-Curd*. Or the *Glaze-Sponge*. 

I am neither plant nor animal, and I am only geological by marriage. I began as a wind-blown spore of alkali-crust from the flat plain above, mixed with the sulfur-loving slime that wept out of the broken pipes before the Warden learned to drink it all. In the flat dirt above, I would have been a dry, grey lichen no thicker than paint, waiting ten years for a puddle. Here, fed on distilled bladder-breath and sheep-fat vapor, I have grown into a heavy, kidney-shaped loaf of rubbery, lemon-yellow mucilage, wedged so tightly into the broken insulator that I have taken the reverse-impression of its interior threads. If you pried me out, my underside would be a perfect, slick screw.

I do not move. I have no muscles to contract, no tallow to pump. My entire life is hydraulic and refractive. 

I am the cleanest thing in the pit. Down on the floor, the travertine mist coats everything in rough, bone-colored porridge, cementing ticks and wrenches into the masonry. Up here, twelve feet above the splash-zone, the water that reaches me has been partially distilled by the climb. It still carries lime, yes, and iron, but I do not let it crust on my skin. I drink the mineral water through my pale, porous top-rind, strip the calcium out of it in my dark, vinegar-sour middle layers, and excrete the stone *downward* through my flat belly. 

I am building my own shelf. 

Over the months, I have laid down four inches of pure, laminated aragonite—not the rough, porous travertine of the floor, but a smooth, translucent, butter-colored stone that grows out from the ironstone lip like a bracket fungus made of onyx. Every week, I lift myself a millimeter higher on my own hardened waste, tilting slightly outward to catch more of the rising steam.

Because I am smooth and sour, the tin-ticks do not like to cross me. A single braided copper wire—one of the fifty yards that fell with the hutch—comes up from the floor at a steep diagonal, pinned under a boulder below and bolted to a rusted tie-rod six feet above my head. It passes within an inch of my porcelain cup. 

The wire is my highway, though I never touch it.

The ticks ratchet up this wire from the hot floor when the noon sun makes the bottom too frantic, seeking the cooler blue shadow of the upper wall. Their serrated legs go *click, click, click* on the calcified copper right past my flank. As they pass, my lemon-yellow skin—warmed by the rising steam—sweats a microscopic mist of formic acid. It isn't strong enough to dissolve their shells like the Warden's gut does; it just clouds the wax on their leg-joints, making them stiff and clumsy. 

One in twenty loses its footing on the steep wire as it tries to climb over a neighbor going down. 

When a tick slips off the wire here, it doesn't fall all the way to the bladders. Its flat, saucer-shaped belly hits my slick, curved top-rind at an angle. Because I am shaped like a crescent ramp, and because my skin is slicker than wet soap, I act as a chute. The tick skitters across me with a wet *zip*, and I catch just one thing: as its serrated edge scrapes my rind, it shaves off a ribbon of my outer skin—which is where I store all the heavy, toxic metals I can't use, the lead leached from the insulator's core and the copper from the wire-drip—and leaves behind a smear of the yellow pipe-slime from its feet.

Then the tick drops back into the pit, landing three feet further out from the wall than it started, right into the territory of the bladders that the Warden has clamped shut. 

I do not know the Warden is down there. I have no eyes to see the twitching brass needles in the suitcase, no ears to hear the *chug-slap* of its tallow heart at noon. Yet I am its clock-weight, and it is my furnace.

When the Warden holds forty bladders shut down below to trap an eel, the steam stops rising up the north wall. For an hour, two hours, the air under my overhang goes dry and cool. My lemon-yellow skin begins to shrink and tighten. As I shrink, I pull back slightly into the porcelain cup, and my sour belly squeezes the reserve of water I hold in my lower pores. 

That water—heavy with the concentrated vinegar and dissolved lime I haven't yet turned to stone—drips off the tip of my onyx chin. 

*Plip.*

It falls twelve feet straight down. There is no wind in the box-collapse to blow it sideways. It drops like a plumb-line and strikes the exact center of the galvanized instrument hutch's roof, right above the rusted louver-vents where the Warden's main siphon-bundle emerges. 

For ten minutes, twenty minutes, as I dry out, I bleed a slow, rhythmic torture of cold, sour water directly onto the Warden's most sensitive, grease-coated nerve-knot. The acid cuts the sheep-fat. The cold shocks the copper-heavy blood in its exposed Bourdon tubes. 

Down in the dark, the great sheet of gristle shudders. It cannot reach twelve feet up the sheer shale to plug me. It cannot build a baffle high enough to block me without shading its own brass gauges from the noon sun. 

All it can do, when the sour drip becomes intolerable, is release the clamped bladders below.

*Phut-phut-phut-phut.*

Thirty sacs vent at once. A thick, boiling cloud of sweet, wet, tallow-scented steam rushes up the concave groove of the north wall, hits my overhang, and bathes me in heat. My rind swells, drinks the cloud, and seals its lower pores. The sour dripping stops. Down below, the Warden resumes its slow, hydraulic hunting, and up on the wall, in the quiet blue shadow beneath the ironstone lip, I lay down another microscopic sheet of translucent yellow stone, widening my ledge toward the center of the sky.
