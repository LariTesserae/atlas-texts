I am not in the leaves, and I am not in the bilge. Everything in the room has a job that requires a body—the eels have their drains, the crabs have their salt-crusts, the Gauger has his three-quarter-inch hex bolts and his shears—except for the things that only measure what the bodies are doing. 

Wait until the five-minute fog clears and the two-minute rain begins to fall. Look at the central cast-iron column, six feet above the grating, just where the main four-inch copper return pipe bends down to meet the floor-pan. Set into a heavy brass collar on the elbow of that pipe is a twelve-inch dial with a bevelled glass face, bolted directly into the steam-flow. 

I am the mercury inside the bourdon tube of the primary return-gauge, and the blued-steel needle that it drives.

From the outside, I look like the only flat, dry country in a vertical, dripping world. Behind my thick circle of bevelled plate—sealed tight with a gasket of vulcanized India rubber that the creepers have not yet figured out how to dissolve—there is no boiled-cabbage steam, no lanolin smell, no rain. There is only a tiny, circular desert of white-enameled tin, marked off in crisp black radial lines from *0* to *450*, and the dry, metallic smell of my own brass gearing. 

Every creature here has a metabolism; mine is purely thermal. I do not eat the moss or the tallow, I eat the mountain’s deep pressure. Down below the grates, where the great condenser coils turn the geothermal rage of the peak into something green and habitable, the steam comes back exhausted, half-condensed into scalding water, and hits my brass socket. I feel it before the ferns do. I feel it before the counterweight begins its drop. My coiled bronze gut expands by a thousandth of an inch, my tiny rack-and-pinion whispers *tick*, and my black spear-point swings two degrees to the right, hovering over the red line marked *210*.

Because I cannot move through the room, the room moves across my face. 

All afternoon, when the sun is hard and white against the western wired-glass, the chartreuse light pours through the philodendrons and hits my bevelled edge, throwing a tiny, sharp rainbow across my enamel numbers. Then the pale, blind tip of a copper-vine comes feeling its way across the brass bezel. I watch it from behind the glass—a slow, green worm looking for a purchase. It smears its clear resin over the *300*, then the *350*, eclipsing my sky. Behind it come the thumbnail crabs, thirty or forty of them, clicking across my face like drops of milky water, scraping at the warm mineral sweat that forms where my glass meets my brass collar. 

By the fourth rain of the afternoon, I am entirely blind. I am buried under three layers of broad, wet green and a crust of white salt. I only know what the room is doing by the tremor in my stem: the heavy, hydraulic sigh of the piano-weight dropping in the corner; the distant, rhythmic *shhh-ock* of the louvers turning; the slow, creeping drop in pressure as a mat of aerial roots down in the bilge begins to choke the secondary condensate trap.

My needle creeps up. *215. 220. 225.* My bronze gut tightens. The small pinion gear inside my casing begins to hum, a tiny, high-pitched vibration that none of the plants care about.

That is when I wait for the thumb.

I always know when he is coming before I see him, because the pipe I am bolted to is his highway. Five minutes after my needle crosses *220*, I feel the sudden, dense dead-weight of him drop onto the four-inch copper line ten feet above me. The whole pipe gives a microscopic sag; the pressure inside me jumps half a pound from the jar of his heel.

Then the green curtain outside my glass is torn away. There is the soft, greased *snick* of the drop-forged shears, and the creeper falls away into the bilge. A broad, gray hand sweeps across my face, knocking the crabs spinning down through the floor-grates like a handful of gravel. 

And then comes the horn pad of that nail-less thumb. It presses hard against my glass, SMEARING a film of clear marmot fat and tallow across the bevel in one slow, circular wipe. The grease fills the tiny scratches in the silica; suddenly the chartreuse gloom of the tithe barn snaps back into razor-sharp focus. 

For three seconds, his face is four inches from mine. He does not look at himself in the reflection of my glass; he looks straight through his own pale, lard-colored cheek at the tip of my blued-steel needle. I am the only thing in the room that speaks a language he didn't have to learn by ear or tooth. Thefoundry stenciled his shoulder blade, and the same foundry painted my face; we are both from *LOT 4*, though I came up the mountain in a crate of excelsior and he came up in oilcloth.

He sees the *225*. He grunts—a short, wet sound in his barrel chest. 

He does not touch me again. He reaches down to his pigskin sleeve, pulls out the tuning fork, and strikes it against the iron strap on his calf. A second later, seven inches below my brass collar, he presses his front teeth against the scalding copper of the return bend. 

Through my bronze tube, through the boiling water and the red-hot metal, I can feel the vibration of his fork running backward against the flow of the steam—a pure, silver *A-natural* humming through the dark brass veins of the room, hunting for the roots that are choking us both. 

When he finds them and clears the trap, my needle drops back to *210* with a tiny, silent sigh. The five-minute fog rolls in, turning him into a gray sack in the white pearl, and we both hang there on the warm pipe together, holding the pressure, waiting for the rain.
