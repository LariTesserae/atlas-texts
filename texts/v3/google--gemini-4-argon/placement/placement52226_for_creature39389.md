I am not one of the tall ones who stand, though my shape is borrowed from their history. 

You will find me three miles east of the Twin-Mound trees, where the basement gneiss dips four inches to cradle a lagoon so still it has grown a skin of pollen and dust. Look down through the clear, tea-colored water, past your own reflection and the blinding white smear of the sun overhead. I am resting in the slight depression where the plum-colored iron stain meets a drift of lavender clay. 

At first, you would take me for a piece of bog-wood—a root torn from one of the swollen trees two hundred years ago, waterlogged and cured by the tannins until it sank like an anchor. I am three feet long, thick as a calf’s leg at the center, tapering at both ends, and black as cured iron. 

Wait for the space of fifty breaths. The high, metallic *tink-tink-tink* of the thumbnail frogs washes over the surface above me, a ceiling of white noise that never breaks. A half-mile away, a stork shifts its weight; the tiny pressure-wave travels through the four-inch sheet of water and presses against my lateral line. 

Very slowly, without stirring a single spherical grain of quartz sand, my side splits open. 

I am an old gar—or what a gar becomes when a river system loses its banks, its current, and its depth over ten million years, flattening out into a sun-baked pan. My ancestors swam in deep channels, but the mountains melted and the channels filled in, and we were pressed downward against the floor of the world. Now I am broad and flat-bellied, armored in overlapping plates of heavy, diamond-shaped ganoid scale that have absorbed the dark manganese and rust of the rock. Along my back, in the shallow flutes between my armor, grows the same slick, sealskin-dark film of cyanobacteria that coats the granite shields. When I lie in the two-inch water over a dark band of stone, I am not hiding on the bottom; I *am* the bottom.

I do not swim. Swimming kicks up the kaolin, and a white bloom in the water means the tall ones will come with their bone-tipped gigs, walking like lengthening shadows until the sky above me is suddenly pierced by an ancestor's femur. Instead, I walk. My pectoral and pelvic fins have thickened into short, muscular paddles tipped with calloused, leathery hooks. I pull myself across the soap-smooth granite a fraction of an inch at a time, moving with the exact, agonizing patience of the hunters above me. 

We share the same religion of stillness, they and I. We are both monuments to the conservation of calcium.

In this starved water, my armor is a fortune. Every diamond scale on my back is dense, ivory-hard bone, laminated with enamel that I have spent eighty years hoarding from the tiny, armored catfish I crush in my jaws, from the drowned beetles that wash out of the pitcher-plants when it rains, and from the stray flakes of gray ash that drift away from the peat-mounds when the wind blows hard after the evening fires. I am a slow-moving vault of phosphorus and lime. The tall ones know me. The family that tends the circuit of sixty trees around this lagoon has a specific two-note hum for me when they press their foreheads together in the apricot mist: *The-Log-With-Teeth*. 

They have not killed me because I am too old to be careless, and because we have an arrangement they do not quite know we have.

It is mid-afternoon now. The water around my gills is the temperature of blood, thick and slightly sour with tannic acid. Four hundred yards away, one of the tall ones—an old woman whose shins are the color of dried plums and whose shoulders are crusted with white clay—stands locked on her bog-wood staff. She has been standing there since the sun was halfway up the sky, one heel hooked over her knee, a statue among statues. 

Through the stone beneath my belly, I feel the low, sub-audible *voom* of a root-drum four miles to the south. *Thrum. Thrum-thrum.* The vibration travels through the ancient granite faster than it travels through the air. I feel it in my swim bladder; she feels it in the arch of her foot. A rain-sheet is creeping up from the unseen sea.

Before the rain comes, the Fog-Drop begins. 

The heat has been unbearable for three hours, baking the exposed shields until the black algae curls at the edges. Now, as the sun lowers, the air gives up. All at once, the ten-mile mirror shatters into glowing orange milk. The brutal glare vanishes. The air right above the water turns into a warm, wet wool that muffles even the metallic static of the frogs, dropping their roar down an octave into a thick, wet hiss.

Above me, the old woman unlocks her hip. Her foot slides out of the water without a drip. She begins her slow, clock-hand glide toward the nearest peat mound, where the low, chest-thumping hum of her grandchildren is already vibrating through the tree roots. 

This is my hour, too. When the sky touches the water and the storks tuck their rust-colored heads beneath their wings, the truce of visibility is over. 

I do not dart. Even in the blind fog, the clay will betray a sudden tail-stroke for three days. Instead, I lean heavily on my right pectoral paddle and lever my heavy, armored snout up until it breaks the surface of the warm lagoon. My nostrils—two tiny fleshy tubes at the very tip of my long jaw—open to the orange mist. I let out a stale bubble of air I have been holding since noon, and draw in a long, slow lungful of the wet sky, tasting the woodsmoke and the roasted frog-fat drifting from the peat mound two hundred yards away.

Then I sink back down until the water closes over my eyes, and I begin to crawl toward the mound.

By the time I reach the base of their tiny island, the apricot light has deepened into the color of a bruise. Above water, five of them are huddled on their baked-clay dome, knees drawn up to their chests, their pale, chalky backs curved like the shells of great pale beetles. They are silent, their foreheads pressed together in a tight circle, humming a long, braided chord that travels down through the baked clay, down through the centuries of pickled peat, down through the roots of the dwarf-cypress, and straight into the water where I lie pressed against the mud. 

I rest my chin on a submerged root and let their bone-song vibrate through my heavy, enameled skull. It feels like warmth.

A hand comes down out of the fog—long-fingered, stained dark purple at the knuckles, crusted with lavender slip at the wrist. It belongs to the youngest child. He is rinsing the wooden bowl they mixed their evening broth in. The water is swirled, just gently enough not to wake the white clay below, and poured out. 

A grey cloud of bone-ash—the ground-up ribs of a great-uncle, the charred vertebrae of a hundred flat-tailed amphibians, a few sunken scraps of roasted meat—settles slowly through the dark water like a miniature snowstorm. 

I open my long, needle-lined jaws just an inch, creating a gentle vacuum that pulls the grey snow in before it can touch the bottom. I filter the ash through my gill-rakers, swallowing the calcium, locking their ancestors into my armor so that when I finally die—fifty or a hundred years from now, laid out on a granite shield by a grandson who isn't born yet—my heavy diamond scales will be pried off with stone blades, drilled with gneiss awls, and strung onto sinew to make a collar for a child's neck, or lashed to the tips of their gigs to hunt my own grandchildren.

Nothing is wasted here. Nothing moves fast. The child’s fingers brush the dark, slick algae growing on the crown of my head. He thinks I am the root of the tree. He leaves his hand there for a moment in the warm water, feeling the vibration of his family's hum echoing back up through my iron-hard scales, before pulling his arm back up into the fog.
