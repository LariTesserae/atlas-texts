I am not on the surface, or at least, not entirely of it. To be whole on the surface is to be heavy, and whatever is heavy here is eventually dragged under by the sluices or ground down by the chains. 

Instead, I am a tension. I am a four-hundred-yard web of drawn phosphor-bronze and braided copper, woven into the spongy three-inch boundary where the wet slag-crust meets the cold air. If you walked over me in the dark between the sodium lamps, your boot would only feel a slight, springy resistance, like stepping on buried mattress coils or the roots of old heather, followed by a faint, metallic *zing* as my outer filaments adjusted to your weight and passed the measurement of your mass down the line.

I began, perhaps a thousand years ago, as the scavenged remains of a buried induction cable that a dredge-plow hooked by accident and tore half out of the bog. The silt-weavers came to strip the insulation, and the heavy-metal bacteria came to eat the weavers, and somewhere in that slow, galvanic rot, the wire learned how to grow itself back together. 

Now, I am a snare-reed—or rather, a single, sprawling colony of snare-reeds, though I do not think of myself as "many." I think of myself as a chord.

My body is a sprawling, horizontal harp staked into the wet clinker by thousands of brittle, calcified anchor-pins. Most of me lies just beneath the cold, copper-tasting water of a wide, shallow basin, half a mile south of a leaning lamp-standard and two hundred yards north of a middle-sized steam-wen. I do not have a center. I have intersections—dense, fist-sized knots of vulcanized grease and layered mica buried in the mud, where twelve or twenty of my bronze strings cross and touch. 

To live here is to listen, and because I am four hundred yards wide, I can hear the whole valley at once in stereo. 

The 20-hertz roar of the deep turbines is not noise to me; it is my blood. It is the baseline carrier wave that keeps my strings in perpetual, microscopic motion. So long as the floor hums, my filaments vibrate against the wet iron filings of the mud, stripping away the rust and generating a continuous, cool trickle of electricity that runs along my bronze nerves and pools in my mica knots. 

I know the time of day—though there is no day—by the three-minute shriek of the eastern relief-stack. When the atmosphere rips open miles away, the shockwave hits my easternmost tendrils first, running westward through my web as a delicious, shivering crescendo that tightens every anchor-pin I own. Immediately after comes the *clank-shudder* of the cathedral-sized walking beam. *Clank*: the mud compresses, and the cold water is squeezed up around my wires. *Shudder*: the mud relaxes, and the water sucks back down, drawing a fresh broth of sulfur-algae and suspended machine oil across my skin.

I do not hunt, and I do not calculate the deep geology like the great, whistling wen to my south. The wen is a philosopher; it cares about the boiler-shells four tiers down and the long, slow history of the water table. I am a poet of the skin. I care only about what touches the floor.

Right now, in my northwest quadrant, there is a dead spot in the 20-hertz hum—a small, three-pointed shadow of silence moving across my strings. 

It is a caliper-strider, walking in its slow, draftsman’s triangles through the mist. *Snick.* One of its laminated iron legs comes down two inches from a primary tendon. The impact sends a sharp, bright spike of voltage into my nearest mica knot. *Snick.* The second leg lands inside my web. 

I do not trap it; a four-hundred-pound strider would tear my bronze strings out by the roots if I tried to hold it. Instead, I tune myself to it. As its heavy, oil-slicked galvanic sac drops on its wire tendon to kiss the mud, I slacken the strings directly beneath it so they will not be crushed, and tighten the strings around its legs. 

When the strider vaults away on the next upward phase of the ground-tremor, it leaves behind a tiny smear of concentrated acid and half-digested copper turnings from its belly-sac. Over the next six hours, I will slowly winch my finest, hair-thin root-wires through that smear, drinking the sharp, bright spark of its spillover to mend a section of my eastern perimeter that was sheared away three days ago by a passing dredge-plow.

The plow is my weather, my winter, and my harvest all at once.

I can feel one coming now, half a mile off, creeping along its buried guide-cable. It feels like a slow, rolling smudge in the bass note of the floor, accompanied by the high, gritty chatter of a hundred harrow-crabs rasping at the newly scraped iron in its wake. 

It will cross my southern edge in forty minutes. Already, I am preparing for the amputation. The mica knots along the plow’s path are draining their stored charge back into the center of the web; the anchor-pins are loosening their grip in the vitrified brick so the heavy chains will drag my outer wires aside rather than snapping them clean. 

When the cast-iron hull crawls over me, it will be a blinding, deafening ecstasy of heat and vibration. The radiator-hose worms swaying on its back will drip warm, greasy condensate onto my strings; perhaps a worn-out valve-limpet will flake off the hull and tumble into my web, where I will spend the next month slowly wrapping it in brass filaments and dissolving its zinc scales for battery-salt. And after the chains have passed, leaving a raw, bleeding trench of un-oxidized iron through my southern reach, I will cast new lines across the scar before the harrow-crabs have even finished scuttling past.

Down to the south, the steam-wen suddenly drops its pitch by a third. A hundred little lead valves inside its dark, calcified mound clack shut at once, sending a sharp, rhythmic pulse of water-hammer through the mud: *tap-tap-pause, tap-tap-pause.*

Four hours from now, the flat a mile to the west will sink and drain. The wen is telling its neighbor to the north, using the wet slag between them as a telegraph line.

As the signal passes under me, warming my roots by half a degree, I take a tiny fraction of that heat and use it to tighten a single, four-foot string of phosphor-bronze that spans a shallow puddle near my center. The string rises just above the surface of the black water, humming at sixty cycles per second—a harmonic fifth above the roar of the floor. 

In the absolute dark, the standing waves in the puddle break against the vibrating wire, scattering the jaundiced reflection of a sodium lamp half a mile away into a thousand tiny, shivering sparks that no eye will ever see. I hold the note until the mist turns back into greasy rain, and then I let the wire sink back into the mud to listen.
