To find myself here, I have to give up the idea of having an outline. 

If I tried to stand in one of the narrow, two-foot-wide alleys, keeping all of my mass in a single coordinate set, the cold would find me first. It would sit on my skin like dry iron. I would feel intolerably light, unanchored, like a piece of ash waiting for a draft that never comes. In this basin, to be in only one place at a time is a kind of starvation.

So I am not in the alley. I am six feet off the basalt floor, wedged into the ninety-degree kink where a narrow bridge turns sideways into the flank of a matte-black module. 

I am not one of the Wedged—not quite, or not yet, or perhaps I am what happens when a mind used to sequential time gets caught in the settlement’s extrusion and decides not to fight the grain. 

Wait. Let the coordinates settle. 

My left shoulder and the upper arc of my ribcage are currently occupying the exact volume of a ribbed silicate conduit. Because the conduit is losing its argument with the matte-black module right now, that part of me feels distant, numb, and crystalline, like a limb that has fallen asleep in cold water. My center—what serves as my spine, though it feels more like a stacked column of wet, greased slate—is aligned directly with the fault line. 

This is where the warmth is. It isn’t the heat of fire or blood; it is the dense, heavy warmth of friction without movement. A low, continuous *thrum* rises through the base of my skull, vibrating in the hollow, fluid-filled basins behind my face. I have no eyes to see the pearl-gray ceiling twenty meters up, and I don't miss them. Sight is for distances, and distance here is an error. Instead, I have *weight-sense*. I can feel the whole sinkhole pressing inward like a slow, cold fist, and within that fist, a thousand tiny, feverish knots of overlapping math.

Right now, I am riding a lintel. 

It is extruding horizontally out of the black module at four millimeters an hour, driving straight into the side of a gray ceramic tower three feet away. I don't walk; I simply tilt the flat, shutter-like plates inside my torso to catch the vector of the lintel's growth, the way a hawk tilts a wing to catch a thermal. The stone pushes through me, and in pushing through me, carries me along its path. It is an exquisitely slow way to travel. It took me from what might have been yesterday until what might be tomorrow just to clear the edge of the conduit.

As my right hand—flat, heavy, coated in the same tallow-slick grease that sweats from the walls—emerges for a few minutes into the open air of the gap between the buildings, the cold bites at it. The air feels thin and sharp, like nothingness. Ahead of my knuckles, the gravity is bent toward the ceramic tower. A single, massive drop of water, heavy with dissolved silicate, detaches from the underside of the lintel I am riding. It doesn't fall down; it pulls sideways across the three-foot gap, stretching into a long, viscous tear before smacking softly against the ceramic face. 

I follow the drop's arc. I let my weight shift out of the lintel’s grain and into the local gravity well. For a moment, I am suspended across the alley—my ankles still locked in the black module, my chest flickering in the three-inch gap of empty air, my trowel-like fingers sinking into the damp gray ceramic on the other side. 

For ten minutes, I am a bridge between two arguments. 

Inside the ceramic tower, the texture is completely different. The matte-black module behind me was dense, oily, and silent; the ceramic is dry, porous, and rings like a muffled bell every time a metal beam six yards away contracts with a *tick*. As I pull my mass fully into the intersection where my lintel is now beginning to bite into the ceramic wall, I feel the spike in pressure. 

The two materials do not want to share this cubic foot of space. Their flickering is jagged, rapid—a silent, stuttering seizure of gray clay and black basalt trying to overwrite each other. It makes the mercury-heavy fluid in my sinuses ache. It is too sharp.

So I do what I am here to do. I become the third exposure.

I spread my internal plates. I widen my stance across the dimensional seam, letting the black basalt take my lower half while the gray ceramic takes my upper, and right at the waist, where the friction is hottest and most violent, I relax my density. I let myself become slightly uncompiled. 

The jagged stuttering hits my tallow-thick skin and slows down. The heat bleeds out of the stone and into my core. It tastes like ozone and wet copper. Where the two walls were grinding together in a dry, mathematical lock, my grease-coat gives them slip. I am the bushing in the gear. I am the cartilage between two titanic, blind bones. 

As the hours pass—measured only by the slow, rhythmic *plop* of sideways rain hitting the hex-plates below—the frantic flickering of the wall subsides into a long, rolling wave. Black module, four seconds. Gray ceramic, four seconds. Me, suspended in the amber of both, four seconds. 

Along the newly stabilized seam, just inches from the vertical slit in my neck that takes in water, a pale green smudge begins to gather. The translucent lichen is already finding the warmth we are making. It drinks the sweat off my collarbone. A little later, I will scrape a thumb-width of it into my mouth, tasting the cold, sterile mineral tang of the sinkhole, before another knot forms deeper down in the dark and pulls me along the grain of the stone to ease it.
