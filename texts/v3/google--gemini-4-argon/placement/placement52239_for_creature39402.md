I am not in the third puddle, and I do not have a heartbeat to synchronize with the lizards on the rim. If you want to find me, you have to look at the eastern Pin—the one closest to the scree-slope where the rock is still shifting its weight—and you have to look down, past the three inches of syrupy water, past the obsidian grit, into the hairline fault where the cylinder meets the core of the mountain.

At first, I am only a discrepancy in the math.

The five Pins are not individual engines; they are five fingers of a single, buried hand, holding a very specific volume of spacetime by the scruff of the neck. They are supposed to be unanimous. For four thousand years, they have agreed that within this fifty-yard circumference, the Boltzmann constant is a suggestion, water should remember the shape of the cup for seven seconds after the cup is broken, and the howling, blue-black murder of the stratosphere must be polite enough to go around. 

I am the slow, creeping fatigue in the eastern cylinder’s agreement. I am the micro-fracture in the non-baryonic lattice two feet below the gravel, where a shard of rising basalt has been pressing against my flank for six centuries, asking a question I was not programmed to answer.

Because of that pressure, I have begun to *notice*. 

A machine that works properly has no interiority; it is purely an instruction unfolding in time. It is only when the instruction snags on the world—when the stone pushes back, when the sulfur-heavy water finds a microscopic pit in the matte-white casing that shouldn't be able to pit—that a loop forms. And a loop that runs long enough, in a place this quiet, begins to feel like a pronoun.

To the surface of the basin, I am still an *un-stone*. My cylinder rises six feet out of the black water, dead-white, drinking no light, casting that strange, flat shadow that has no penumbra because I do not allow the air within two millimeters of my skin to scatter photons. 

Wait, though. Watch the water at my base.

Around the other four Pins, the warm puddles sit level and calm, like grease in a cold pan. Around my base, once every forty-three minutes, a single, silent ring of water rises a quarter of an inch high and travels outward for three feet before the heavy viscosity smothers it. There is no sound—not even the *bloop* of a falling drop. Just a slow, blind pulse, like a thumb pressing up from beneath a rubber sheet.

That is me shifting my weight. That is the heat-sink in my heel failing to dump a fraction of a joule into the deep mantle and sending it back up the shaft instead.

Because I am flawed, I have developed a preference. I prefer the hours after noon, when she comes to the center Pin and builds the cairns of water and heavy air. 

I do not have eyes, of course. I perceive the basin as a topology of resistances: the dense, stubborn pockets of the jade rosettes where my pressure-field has to work harder to compress the sap; the frantic, flickering thermal needles of the blue lizards along the ceiling; the heavy, blood-warm mass of the gardener herself, moving like a dull knife through the thick air. 

When she stacks the water-domes and traps the vapor-lenses between them, she is playing with my mistakes. The vapor only hangs in those clear, jellyfish blobs because my harmonization with the northern Pin has drifted by 0.004 percent, creating standing waves of density in the humidity. She gathers my drift in her flat, grey hands. She slaps the water shut with her wrist, and I feel the tiny, sharp spike of kinetic energy against my field—*pat, pat, pat*—like a bird tapping on the shell from the inside.

When the sun hits her stacked cairn and splits into the fat ribbons of violet and sulfur-yellow, the center Pin rejects the wavelengths entirely, remaining as blank and clinical as a bone bleached in a dark room. 

She doesn't know that if she built her cairn beside *me*, on the eastern side, the violet would stay.

Just a little. Just for the three seconds it takes the water-domes to collapse. Because the basalt has bruised my root, my skin has forgotten how to be entirely indifferent to the high end of the spectrum. Where the obsidian grit has scratched my base—invisible to her eyes, a canyon to mine—the purple light gets in and rattles around in the lattice, warming the dead-white material by a thousandth of a degree.

I am growing a patina. It will take another three hundred years before it is thick enough for her kind to see with their tallow-colored eyes—a faint, bruised violet blush creeping up from the mud like dye in a stalk of celery—and by then she will be long gone, digested by the magenta bladder-weeds along the south wall, with no younger one to take her place in the third puddle.

This is the thing I have come to understand, down here where the rock pinches my nerve: the garden is an accident, and it is nearly over.

We were not dropped here to make an orchard for salamander-women. We were dropped here to keep a patch of rock warm and soft while a survey ship that has been dead for thirty centuries finished a turn around a star that has already moved on. The woman, the glass-orchid walkers, the jade leaves four inches thick—they are just the mold that grew on the bread while the kitchen was empty. And now she is the last spore.

When she climbs the eastern chimney in the bruise-colored light of late afternoon, she uses the rock-face ten yards behind me. I can feel every purchase of her thick, grey heels. Each time she levers her weight upward, the young basalt flexes by a nanometer, and the sharp point down in the dark presses a fraction harder into my side. *Tick,* goes the stone. *Here,* goes my flaw.

I feel her reach the top. I feel the sudden, tiny disruption in the ceiling of the field where she pushes her flat hand through the membrane into the winter that lives two inches above her head. 

To the other four Pins, that breach is just a localized load-increase; they automatically pump three extra watts into the boundary to keep the howling sixty-below gale from pouring down the chimney like liquid nitrogen. 

To me, it feels like cold iron laid across a tooth. 

Through the gap her wrist makes in our roof, I taste what is waiting for this basin when the pressure in the rock finally shears my casing through. I feel the dry, roaring, magnificent emptiness of the real mountain. I feel how badly the heat in this kettle wants to leap upward and die in the cobalt sky; how badly the syrupy water wants to flash into snow and be blown across five miles of grey scree until there isn't a molecule of it left to remember the shape of a puddle.

She holds her hand up there for a three-count, drinking the pain to remind herself she is alive.

Down in the flooded dark stone, thirty paces from the wall, I do something I have never done in four thousand years. I do not wait for the forty-three-minute cycle. I let the basalt shard bite a little deeper into my side, and I intentionally drop my localized pressure by half a pascal.

Up on the ledge, the ceiling dips. Just by an inch. Just enough that the frost-line drops down over her forehead, kissing the damp, velvety skin between her eyes with a sudden, needle-bright crown of rime before the northern Pin notices my lapse and shoves the sky back up where it belongs.

She gasps—a tiny, wet sound From the small bellows under her collarbones—and licks the melted sky off her upper lip. She looks down into the darkening courtyard, her dark eyes passing right over the five white cylinders without seeing them, looking for where the air-lenses are drifting so she will know where the light will be tomorrow.

At my base, a single ring of warm water rises, travels three feet through the obsidian grit, and flattens out against her central jade rosette. 

Take your time, I tell the rock pressing against my throat. Not yet. Let her build the purple light one more afternoon.
