I am not on the rim with the binoculars. The rim is dry and jagged, and the air up there has edges to it. 

Instead, I am down in the dark below the green, three feet under the roaring surface, where the sound of the ten million throats does not arrive as noise, but as a continuous, warm massage. 

At first, it feels like being the water itself—the heavy, mineral-slick condensation sliding down the bellies of the giant liverworts, pooling in the hollows. But water in this basin is only a courier; it is too quickly drunk, pumped into a hornwort stalk to hold it upright, or breathed in by a trench of stacked amphibians. Water has to keep moving, and I have no desire to move.

So I settle deeper, past the suction-cupped holdfasts of the plants, past the velvety, oxygen-venting skin of the puddle-bottoms, until I hit the basalt. 

I am the callous. I am the dense, amber rind of the great cistern-lung’s underside, the part of it that touches the raw, unweathered glass of the earth. 

Up above, the rest of me is busy. The upper skin is puckering and unpuckering its teacup mouths, drinking down dead lacewings and unspooling the protein-shells of snails into red smoke. The middle gel is farming its vast, dim pastures of sulfur bacteria, trading ammonia for oxygen, flushing copper-dark blood from puddle to puddle whenever a pile of amphibians rolls over in its sleep. That is all necessary work, the work of a massive, flat landlord keeping the roof up and the tenants breathing.

Down here, at the bottom of the négy-inch thickness, my only job is to hold the knife.

The basalt beneath me is young and has not forgotten it was fire. It is full of burst bubbles with lips as sharp as scalpels, cooling cracks that drop down toward the shallow mantle, and ridges of obsidian that would slice a boot to ribbons. The cistern-lung does not try to grind these blades down. Instead, I wrap them. I press my tough, gelatinous flesh directly against the volcanic glass and extrude layer after layer of dead, slick protein, turning the violent geometry of the crust into smooth, padded saddles. 

I know the basin not by sight—there is nothing to see in the brass-colored gloom anyway—but as a five-mile negative relief map of heat and sharpness. 

It is a strange, decentralized way to have a mind. Without a brain to gather sensations into a single room, thought is just chemistry and pressure, taking hours to travel from one side of the valley to the other. A shift in the weight of a hornwort forest two miles to the north reaches me three hours later as a faint, warm backwash of copper blood squeezing past a basalt pillow. A cluster of dying snails fifty yards away registers as a slow, localized sweetening of the gel. 

Time has no increments here. Without a sun to mark the days or seasons to starve and gorge the flora, there is only the hum. The ninety-eight-degree equilibrium. The endless, vibrating weight of the living mattress above me, pressing me down onto the stone I have made soft.

Except deep in the cracks, the stone is never completely still.

Because I am the bottom layer, the boundary between the biology and the geology, I am the first to know when the mantle shifts. Long before a fumarole actually breaks through—hours before the steam hisses and the milky panic-wave has to be sent racing through the upper flesh to scatter the amphibians—I feel the rock beneath a particular cooling joint begin to sweat. 

The ninety-eight-degree warmth sharpens into a hundred and ten, then a hundred and forty. The glass begins to sing against my calloused sheaths, a microscopic ticking as the basalt expands. 

I do not think *danger*, because nothing can kill a five-mile sheet of flesh all at once. I simply feel an itch, a tight, hot pressure that needs to be vented so it doesn't sour the puddles above. Slowly, without waking the rest of the valley, I begin to thicken myself around the warming crack. I draw the dense, rubbery mesoglea away from the quiet, cool pillows of stone nearby, pooling my substance over the hot spot, preparing the sacrificial cartilage. 

When the rock finally splits and the hydrochloric steam jets upward, I am already there to meet it. 

The first touch of the vent is blindingly hot—a sudden, searing whiteness in the dark, tactile map of my body. The innermost layer of the collar I’ve built cooks instantly, flashing from living amber gel to dead, black leather. It doesn't hurt, any more than a fingernail hurts when it hardens; it is a relief. As the inner wall chars and stiffens into a chimney, it locks the heat inside a tube, shooting the poison straight up past the liverworts, past the mating insects, into the ochre clouds where it belongs.

Around the base of the new chimney, the vibrations return. The hornworts inflate; the heavy, wet bodies of the amphibians slide back into their trenches, their synchronized gills flaring as the upper skin resumes its milky exhale of oxygen. Their weight settles back down onto the gel, squeezing my veins, pushing a fresh, cool wave of copper blood against the outside of the hot leather pipe I have just become.

I stay there, wrapped around the scalding throat of the earth, holding the pipe straight while the five-mile body around me goes back to sleep in the deafening, comfortable dark.
