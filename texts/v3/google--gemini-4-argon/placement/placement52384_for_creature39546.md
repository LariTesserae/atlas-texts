I am not dry enough to be a Tally-Man, and I do not have the furious, single-minded grief of the Slaked. 

If you want to find me, you have to look at the places where a twenty-mile bore of yellow brine has just finished receding, leaving the sunken plaza gleaming like the inside of an oyster shell. Look at the sweeping, overlapping arcs left by the invisible wooden floats. Look closely at where two of those arcs cross. Sometimes, in the V-shaped notch between one stroke and the next, a little ridge of slip is left standing—a fraction of an inch high, too thin to be a wall, too small for the world’s feverish attention to bother going back to flatten.

That is where I begin. I am the **Burr**.

Wait five minutes, though, and I am no longer there. I have already been dragged three hundred yards to the east inside the belly of an extrusion.

To be what I am is to have no skin of vinegar-slime to keep the world out. I am entirely permeable to the Revision, except for a single, stubborn hitch in my consistency. Somewhere inside my clay, a long time ago, a fistful of wood-shavings from a collapsed tripod got mixed with the shed, parboiled grease of a dead Grout-Eel and a twist of lime-scum. The invisible trowels cannot smooth me out completely because I do not spread like pure mud; I roll under the blade. I catch on the grain of the unseen wood. I leave a tiny, ragged tear in the newly finished floor—a wet, fibrous pucker that the builders have to pass over three or four times before they give up and simply bury me under a fresh course of hand-struck brick.

I live inside the walls, moving through them the way a bubble moves through thick boiling pitch.

When a knee-high retaining wall snaps up out of the flat with that heavy, wet-linen sound, I am often in the third or fourth tier from the bottom. It is dark in there, and the heat is monumental—the true, suffocating heat of the deep kiln-belly, before the steam has had a chance to sweat out into the sour-brass air. Inside the wall, you can feel the *tension* of the Masonry. The bricks are not resting on one another; they are clenching each other like teeth. You can hear the pithy reeds in the mortar drinking up the brine with a tiny, frantic ticking sound, and the long, slow groan of the mud trying to remember if it is supposed to be a causeway or a cistern.

Because I am full of tallow-grease and wood-splinters, I do not belong in the wall's load-bearing logic. I am a soft spot. A structural flaw. 

So I migrate. I let the sheer, crushing weight of the upper tiers squeeze me slowly sideways through the mortar joints. It feels like being swallowed by a throat made of hot grit. As I slide between the bricks, I leave a hairline fracture behind me, a tiny greasy vein that ruins the bond. 

The Slaked know what I am. They do not hate me—they are too busy to hate anything—but they use me. 

Sometimes, when I have worked my way to the outer skin of a newly extruded ziggurat, pushing my blind, splintery snout out through a weep-hole to taste the cooler, brass-lit steam, a gang of Slaked will be there, scrambling up the slick adobe on their paddle-shins. One of them will spot the little greasy pucker I’ve made in the mortar. Before I can slip back inside, a heavy, bitumen-crusted forearm will come down and *jam* a split timber wedge directly into the gap beside me, using my softness as the purchase point to pry the whole tier apart.

When the wall sighs and unweaves itself back into the water, I am washed out into the ankle-deep brine like a clot of wet sawdust. 

For an hour, or a day, I will have a body of my own.

It is not much to look at—something like a flat, waterlogged loaf of bread, pale grey and bristling with stiff, wet splinters of timber and frayed hemp that stick out of me like porcupine quills. I cannot walk like the Wattle-Crabs; I have no knots to pull myself along. Instead, I ride the twenty-mile bores. When the floor tilts and the warm, yellow-foaming water comes sloshing across the flats, I flatten myself out like a sole and let the current skate me over the millions of razor-sharp knuckle-prints.

That is my favorite part of existing here: sliding over the skin-ridges of the builders. 

The prints are so fresh. As my underside scrapes across the sunken thumbs and frantic palms pressed into the mud, I can feel the residual *intent* left in the clay. The unseen builders are terrified. Nobody else seems to know this—the Grout-Eels are too blind, and the Tally-Men are too high up in their dry, clicking bones—but you can feel it if you lie flat against the floor right after a trowel-stroke passes. The hands that make this world are not the hands of gods; they are the hands of convicts, or fever-patients, working in the dark to meet a quota that changes every six seconds. The whorls of their thumbs are full of panic. They are building to cover something up, and they are running out of mud, so they have to keep tearing up the old plazas from behind to lay down the new ones in front.

Sometimes, when the bore beaches me on the leeward side of a stuttering scaffold, I do something the world does not expect. 

I do not fight the mud like the Slaked, and I do not measure it like the Tally-Men. I *counterfeit* it.

Lying there in the hot wet, while the invisible bone awls are busy scoring cross-hatched drainage channels into the terrace three yards away, I will slowly extrude my own wood-splinters and tallow-grease into the soft clay around me. I press my ragged edges down into the thumb-prints, filling them in like wax in a woodblock, taking their cast. Then I roll over, lift myself up on a tripod of stiffened hemp-fronds, and stamp those stolen thumb-prints back into the smooth, freshly floated floor where no prints are supposed to be.

It is a tiny heresy. A single, smudged, backward knuckle-mark in the middle of a pristine causeway.

The world’s attention always catches on it. You can feel the air above the counterfeit print go tight and hot, like a magnifying glass being held over an ant. An invisible wooden float will snap down out of nowhere—*slap-slap-slap*—smoothing the mark away with furious, disproportionate violence. While the air is distracted there, hammering that one square foot of mud flat, the stuttered scaffold ten yards away gets ten more minutes to stand without being turned into tallow-grease. A Slaked trapped to the knee in a rising parapet gets the three seconds of slack it needs to wrench its reed-core leg out of the joint.

I never stay to watch. By the time the third stroke of the float comes down, I have already let the brine wash me down into a Newly opened crack, down into the dark amber sumps where the Grout-Eels tremble in their vents. 

Down there, in the charcoal murk, I will lodge myself under a loose brick and wait. Sooner or later, the distant, hollow *hoot* of a Tally-Man will vibrate through twenty miles of wet clay. The floor will drop out like a trapdoor, the whole steaming soup of ziggurats and vinegar-skins will come plunging down into the dark to be chewed up and remade, and in the violent, squelching updraft that follows, I will be sucked back up into the light—already half-baked into the side of a brand-new chimney, already working my splinters sideways through the mortar, looking for the next seam to ruin.
