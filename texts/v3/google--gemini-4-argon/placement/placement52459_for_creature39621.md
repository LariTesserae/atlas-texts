My knees are never locked. Even in sleep, curled inside the swaying ribs of a Cradle, my heels twitch and adjust as the harness rings slide along the anchor-vines outside. If you lock your joints in this valley, the floor breaks you. You have to let the vibration come up through the dense heartwood of your runners, through the leather bindings, into your ankles, and let it die in the loose hinge of your hips.

Right now, the vibration is a slow, four-beat *thrum-thrum-thrum-pause*, heavy and low, which means the deep thermal pumps two hundred feet beneath my shins are cycling cold meltwater down from the northern pass and pushing warm, mulched slurry up toward the surface ridges. 

I am squatting on the stationary lip of an old, petrified valve-collar—one of the rare islands of dead, grey ironwood that the living thatch hasn't yet dragged under to be re-ground. My gaff-pole is laid across my thighs. The air is so thin and cold it feels like inhaling chipped glass, smelling of bruised pine and the sharp, sweet tang of scorched sap. There is no wind today, just the draft pulled sideways by a three-acre belt of silver-scrub that is currently hauling itself eastward at the pace of a brisk walk. 

I am not watching the scrub. I am listening with my feet.

I am an Listener—or maybe the word in your tongue would be a Tuner. Among the Gantry-kin, my hands are smaller, my chest a little narrower, my shins bowed slightly outward from sixty winters of riding the high-pressure conduits. Around my waist, instead of the heavy harvesting gaffs the younger skaters use to haul timber-sleds or snag Sump-Sloths for their wool, I wear a skirt of forty leather bladders, each no bigger than a hare’s heart, each plugged with a carved wooden stopper of a different shape so I can know them by touch in the blind mist of the trenches.

Two miles to the west, someone is striking a conduit. 

*Tap-tap. Tap. Tap-tap-tap.*

It comes through the deadwood under my runners as a crisp, tiny itch in the arches of my feet. It is my nephew’s stroke—he hits the bark slightly off-center with his burl-mallet, giving the shockwave a dull, flat tail. *A cable-jam at the Three-Drum Sluice. The briars are piling. A Cradle is losing equilibrium.*

I don't stand up to leave. I simply lean forward until my center of gravity passes over the lip of the deadwood collar, extend my gaff-pole, and drop three feet onto the back of a passing haulage creeper.

The hook bites into the graphite-slick wax of the vine. For a second, my shoulders take the jerk—a silent, muscular heave that would tear a stationary man’s arms from their sockets—and then my runners catch the groove of an adjacent root-plate, and I am gliding.

The valley blurs into grey and olive stripes. There is no sound but the wet *shhh-shhh* of my heartwood runners planing over the condensed dew of the under-thatch, and the rhythmic clicking of my leather bladder-stoppers against my hip. To my left, a massive burl-drum, thirty feet across and slick with gray moss, is rotating out of the peat. Two Tappet-Crabs are clinging to its flank, their spoon-mandibles working frantically to scrape a seam of hot, amber resin before the drum rolls their meal down into the crushing dark of the socket. As I hiss past, one of the crabs senses the tremor of my skate, clicks its brake-pads, and drops backward onto a cross-vine, vanishing into a trench without missing a beat of its feeding.

I switch tracks twice—unhooking my gaff, letting my momentum carry me across ten yards of spongy, trembling rosette-heads that squirt freezing water over my oiled wool leggings, and hooking onto a fast-twitch briar runner heading down-gradient.

Before I see the jam at the Three-Drum Sluice, I smell it. The ozone and pine are gone, replaced by the thick, suffocating stench of boiling turpentine and scorched wood-pith. 

When I crest the gully, the geometry of the valley is broken. 

Usually, the floor here braids itself in a clean, three-way weave, with three immense haulage cables crossing over a deep drainage trench to winch timber-sleds up toward the ridge-lines. Now, one of the primary cables—a vine as thick as four men’s waists—has suffered a tumorous growth. A parasitic gall, or perhaps just a misfire in the basin's deep hormonal tides, has caused the cable to swell into a jagged, hardened knot of ironwood. The knot has failed to pass through the collar of the guide-truss. 

The engine hasn't stopped. The plants don't know how to stop. 

Miles away, the subterranean root-bladders are still pumping three thousand pounds of hydraulic pressure into this line. Because the cable cannot move forward, the oncoming vine behind it is buckling upward in great, writhing loops, thirty feet into the pale sky, twisting around itself like an angry snake. The friction at the jammed collar has cooked the gray wax into black smoke. And caught in the rising, thrashing cat’s-cradle of the buckled vines is a residential Cradle—a ten-foot sphere of woven ribs, its pulley-harnesses fouled, tilting at a sickening forty-degree angle as the rising loop threatens to crush it against the upper sap-pipelines.

My nephew is already there, skating in tight, desperate circles on the bank of the trench, his gaff-pole useless against wood under that much tension. Inside the tilted Cradle, two pale faces—an old woman and a child—are pressed against the ironwood lattice. They are making no sound. Even in terror, we do not waste breath.

If I cut the cable with an axe, the released tension would whip the broken end across the gully fast enough to shear the Cradle—and both of us—in half. You cannot fight the factory with muscle. You have to lie to it.

I let go of my tow-briar, sink low into my knees, and skate down the polished wall of the gully, using the heel-edges of my runners to shave speed, throwing up a spray of cold, grey moss-water. I don't aim for the jammed knot. I aim for the deep, dark seam thirty yards *behind* it, where the haulage cable emerges from the subterranean root-mat.

The ground here is bucking like the back of a drowning beast. The underlying peat-mattress is tearing open, exposing the slick, white vascular bundles underneath—the raw nerves and arteries of the valley. Blind Vascular Eels, panicked by the pressure-spike, are squeezing out of a weeping seam in a secondary pipe, flipping silently in the air before falling into the grinding plates below.

I drop to one knee on the heaving bark, jamming the hook of my gaff-pole into a stationary rib of deadwood to keep from being thrown into the trench. With my left hand, I draw my bone scoring-knife—carved from the dense femur of an ancestor who went into the mills four generations ago—and with my right, I pluck three stoppers from my belt.

First, the yellow bladder: *winter-dormancy*. It is tapped from the high, stunted conifers on the frost-line during the darkest week of the year.

I lean over the thrashing, four-foot-thick cable and drive the bone knife three inches into its flank, right where a lateral feeder-root joins the main trunk. High-pressure sap, cold as snowmelt, hisses out in a fine mist, soaking my sleeve and instantly freezing into a crust of white sugar-ice. Before the plant’s automatic valves can seal the puncture, I jam the nozzle of the yellow bladder into the wound and squeeze with both hands, using my chest weight to force my fluid *against* the plant’s pressure.

For ten seconds, nothing happens. Overhead, an ironwood rib on the trapped Cradle snaps with a dull, wet *thwack* that I feel in my teeth.

Then, the chemistry takes. 

The twenty yards of cable in front of me suddenly loses its matte-green sheen, turning the bruised, purplish-grey of winter sleep. The plant thinks a flash-freeze has hit the ridge. Instantly, the internal valves slam shut to protect the deep sumps; the turgor pressure vanishes as the water is dumped downward into the under-thatch through emergency pores. The great, bucking loops of vine in the air stop rising. They sag, groaning silently as their internal hydraulics go slack.

Now, before the surrounding root-mat senses the drop and tries to compensate by extruding a support-column that would impale us all, I pull the red bladder: *rot-flush*. 

I skate ten yards forward, straight into the hot, turpentine-stinking smoke of the jammed collar. The heat radiating off the wood is blistering after a lifetime in the chill; sweat breaks out instantly under my greased wool coat. I slash the swollen, tumorous knot in six places in a cross-hatched pattern and smear the thick, foul-smelling red paste into the cuts. It is a concentrate cultured from the gill-fungi in the deepest trenches—the chemical signal the forest uses when a limb has died and needs to be mulched immediately.

The hyper-metabolism of the valley does in two minutes what takes a normal forest twenty years.

The ironwood knot begins to sweat dark, brown water. The lignified fibers unbind, softening from stone to leather, and from leather to wet, grey punk-wood. My nephew, reading my movements without a word, skates in from the flank. Together, we set the hooks of our gaff-poles into the pulley-rings of the sagging Cradle, brace our runners against the lip of the trench, and lean back.

Down in the dark beneath us, the four-beat rhythm of the pumps stutters, recalibrates, and resumes. *Thrum-thrum-thrum-pause.*

The upstream valves open again. The pressure hits the line.

Instead of jamming, the softened, rotting knot is squeezed through the ironwood collar like tallow through a fist, shearing away in great, steaming ribbons of grey mush that drop straight into the trench to feed the fungi. The great cable lurches forward, swallowing its own slack. As the loops untwist and pull taut, the Cradle swings wildly for a moment, its greased rings sliding along the newly freed vine, and then settles back into its six-foot hover, bobbing gently in the cold, flat sunlight.

The old woman inside raises two fingers against the wooden lattice. *Equilibrium.*

I do not wave back; my hands are already busy. A Tuner never leaves a wound open, or the valley will grow a scar that derails the sleds a month from now. I pull a wide, flat cake of Sump-Sloth tallow from my pouch and plaster it over the slashes I made in the cable, sealing the bark against the cold air, then wash my bone knife in the runoff from a nearby water-rosette.

Before my nephew and I part ways, we take our fee.

The sudden release of the cable has sheared three massive slabs of compressed, thousand-year-old heartwood from the inside of the collar—wood so dense and resin-soaked it sinks in water like lead, already polished to a mirror shine by the friction of the jam. We don't have to carve it. The machine has already shaped it into long, curved splints. 

I lash one of the slabs across my back; it will make four pairs of new runners for the children in the north-ridge Cradles. 

Then I tap the heel of my boot twice against the main conduit to tell the valley we are clear, reach out with my gaff, hook a passing creeper headed toward the high pass, and let the silent, tireless floor pull me home into the cold.
