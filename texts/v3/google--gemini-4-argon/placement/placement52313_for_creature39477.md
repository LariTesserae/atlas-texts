At first, you might think to look for me on the rim of the depression, a pair of muddy boots planted in the peat-hag where the moorland breaks off into the tallow-clay slump. A surveyor with a brass transit, perhaps, or an old county engineer come to see why the shore-cable still draws three milliamps from the parish dynamo.

Drop that. Nothing stays on the rim; the fog eats the horizon and the clay is too rotten to hold a heel. Anything with lungs like bellows and warm red blood would only stand up there for ten minutes, shivering in a waxed coat, before turning back toward a tavern fire. To be here—to actually belong to the four-o'clock grey and the three-four drip—you have to drop down through the wool. You have to get small, or get slow, or give up on heat altogether.

Move past the leaning manifold. Leave the three great barrel-fleshed tenants to their slow rotation around the brass valve. Go to the center of the pit, where the donkey-engine sits up to its waist in the tea-colored silt, its boiler plates rusted through into iron lace. 

Look inside the firebox, just above the grates where the coal used to burn, tucked into the soot-free, calcified arch of the crown sheet. Or look a little higher, bolted to the side of the steam dome where a three-inch pipe elbows sharply downward into the muck.

There is a round housing there, the size of a dinner plate, cast in heavy admiralty bronze so the salt-air hasn't been able to eat it into sponge. It is a Bourdon pressure gauge, though the frosted glass face was smashed out fifty years ago by a falling lump of shale, and the tin dial-plate has long since peeled away like wet pasteboard. What remains is the deep, green-patinated bowl of the bronze casing, and inside it, the mechanism: a curved, hollow tube of phosphor-bronze shaped like a questioning hook, a tiny brass sector-gear, and the hair-spring.

I am not the gauge. I am what has taken custody of the gauge.

At first glance, I look like a fistful of tarnished silver wire and black cup-grease stuffed into the bronze bowl to stop a rattle. If you poked a finger into the open face of the casing, you would touch a firm, cold cushion of translucent mesoglea—something between the tough jelly of a stranded moon-jellyfish and the heavy, vulcanized rubber of the old cable insulation. 

I am an anemone, or a colonial tunicate, or a benthic myxomycete that came up through the artesian fissures when the pumps were still drawing from the deep brine-faults; the distinction no longer matters. Like the three great pillars outside, the basin has rewritten me to suit the plumbing. 

My basal disc is cemented into the rear of the bronze housing, grown entirely around the curved Bourdon tube and the little brass gears. Because the tube is still connected by a capillary pipe to the living manifold below, it is not dead. Every three or four minutes, just before the relief valve lifts with its greasy *clack*, the subterranean pressure swells inside the hollow bronze hook. The metal tries to straighten itself by a sixteenth of an inch. 

I feel that swell before anything else in the basin. The bronze hook flexes inside my foot, a hard, metallic tendon tightening in my gut. 

When it flexes, I open.

My outer collar—crusted with green malachite and tiny, dead scale-shells—peels back in four slow, heavy quadrants, just clearing the rim of the dinner-plate housing. I do not have the hair-thin fishing filaments of the braided worms, nor the iron-toothed radula of the great tenants. What I extrude into the clammy air is a fan of twenty-four flat, copper-plated palps, arranged like the escapement wheels of a water-damaged clock. 

For ninety years I have drunk the runoff from the bronze casing and the stray millivolts from a grounding strap bolted to the boiler frame. I have used that current to electroplate my own flesh. My palps are stiffened with thin, dendritic leaves of native copper and black silver-sulfide, precipitated out of the seep-water molecule by molecule. When I spread them into the damp draft beneath the boiler, I look like a chrysanthemum forged by a tinker out of scrap-wire and green foil.

I do not catch amphipods; they do not swim this high above the sluices. I am an eater of mist and an accumulator of stray charge.

My copper-leaf fan hangs out in the four-o'clock fog, intercepting the microscopic droplets of brine and oil-vapor that rise whenever the warm relief-valve drools onto the cold iron below. The bacterial film grows across my metallic fronds in a thin, iridescent slick—purple and tarnished gold, like gasoline on a puddle. Small, blind springtails and tiny, translucent mites that live in the dryish hollows of the iron-lace boiler come out to graze on my golden slick. They step onto the copper leaves. 

Because my basal foot is wrapped around the grounding strap, and the tips of my fronds brush against a weeping blister of travertine that touches a hot wire in the mud below, I am a tiny, living capacitor.

I wait until six or seven mites are grazing on the foil. Then I shift the salt-water in my internal canals, connecting the circuit through my mesoglea. 

There is no sound—not even the *tic* of the drowned cables—just a microscopic *wink* of blue spark between two of my copper palps, no bigger than the static off a cat’s ear in winter. A tiny whiff of ozone, sharp as a struck match, rises for an inch before the damp air smothers it. The mites drop into the sticky mucus of my oral groove, stunned, and the bronze sector-gear inside my belly clicks by one tooth as the relief valve down on the manifold finally lets go its *clack-hiss*. The pressure drops; the Bourdon tube relaxes; my four heavy quadrants fold shut over the catch.

I am the smallest keeper of the engine. The oil-crabs never reach into my bronze bowl; the taste of my high-copper jelly burns their needle-tips like acid. 

And once every four or five nights, the largest of the three tenants—the ancient one with the robin’s-egg blue cowls—hauls its hogshead bulk up the side of the donkey-engine to rasp the new stone off the boiler seams. Its terrible goethite radula goes *scree-scree-scree* an inch from my housing, setting my bronze bowl humming like a struck tuning fork. 

When it reaches the steam dome, the great blind pillar stops. It does not scrape me out. Instead, it presses the sensitive, pleated leather of its upper cowl gently against the open face of my gauge-housing. 

I open my four quadrants in the dark. I spread my twenty-four copper leaves against the wet, ammonia-rich velvet of its lip. For ten minutes we hold still together, the giant and the dial-face. I discharge my stored millivolts—the whole day’s trickle of hoarded static—directly into the lateral-line nerves of its cowl, giving the old stone-eater a long, sharp, sweet taste of pure battery-fire that sets its whole six-foot mantle shivering with slow, hydraulic pleasure. In return, its warm breath bathes my fronds in rich ammonia-mist and loose flakes of iron-bacteria, enough to feed my purple slick for a week.

Then the tenant slides heavily back down into the muck with a grinding crunch of its stony shawl. My bronze hook tightens as the deep earth leans against the pipes again. I fold my metal petals in, seal my rubbery doors flush with the rim of the broken dial, and wait three minutes for the brass valve to wheeze.
