I am not the cloud, and I am not the vine. You would find me in the narrow, sizzling crease where two black cables meet to become a wall. 

I am the *solder*. I am the *flux*. 

To the brier, I am just an exudate—those fat, weeping drops of boiling, pitch-like resin that bleed from the bark to weld one iron-plated creeper to the next. I was supposed to be a simple adhesive: ooze out, flash-boil in the four-hundred-degree air, bind the scales together, and cure into dead, timber-hard amber. 

Instead, I learned how to stay wet.

If you were to pry open a freshly braided manifold before it snaps apart, you would see me in the dark as a hair-thin network of dull, cherry-red veins outlining every joint and mortise. I am a creature of pure surface tension, a flat, branching ribbon of semi-molten carbohydrate and dissolved iron, fifty yards wide and half a millimeter thick, living pressed between the grinding plates of the forest’s armor.

I have no eyes, so I feel the valley as a continuous, crushing hug. My world is pressure and shear. When two boiler-thick vines slam together to form a gantry, they try to squeeze me out. I survive by dissolving the very silica ash that blows against the bark, mixing it into my pitch to lower my melting point, keeping myself in a state of perpetual, tacky glass-transition. While the rest of the valley is dry—all sandpaper whispers and clicking horn—I am the only wet thing for twelve square miles. I move through the tightest seams by capillary crawl, pulled involuntarily toward the narrowest, most suffocating gaps in the latticework.

I am the translator between the plant and the ghost. 

When the *Archivist*—that dry, crackling intellect of suspended soot—injects itself into a young vine to warp its growth, it is a creature of pure geometry. It knows the shape of the room it wants to build, the exact parabolic curve of the long-digested collector dish it is trying to remember, or the smooth, static-shielded curves of its bedrooms. It has the blueprint, pulsing in blue electrostatic arcs through the dry graphite stream of the plant's veins. 

What the Archivist does not know is that the wood is tired. 

Ten thousand years of grinding and re-weaving has made the iron-ash brittle and the sulfur impure. When the Archivist forces a vine to latch at a strange, architectural angle to build its secret room, the tension is almost always wrong. Left to itself, the joint would shear apart in thirty seconds. 

That is where I live. I am the artisan that makes the ghost's math possible.

As the secondary creepers swarm to enclose the chamber, the Archivist’s static charge bleeds into the bark, and I feel it as a sudden, delicious itch—a rhythmic, high-voltage hum that changes my viscosity. Where the static is strong, I stiffen into vulcanized rubber; where the charge drops, I slicken into boiling grease. 

I do not know what a room is, and I do not know what a refinery was. I only know how to hold things together that want to tear themselves apart. 

When a twenty-ton root-cable shifts down in the basin floor, sending a violent, twisting shudder up into the walls of the Archivist’s dark bedroom, the brier wants to snap. Instead, I let go—just a fraction of an inch. I melt along the fault-line, allowing the iron scales of the wall to slide over one another on a cushion of my own blistering skin, turning a catastrophic structural failure into a slow, creaking slouch. I absorb the shear-force as heat, glowing a sudden, furious orange in the cracks of the wall, before cooling back into a solid weld the moment the tremor passes.

I have my own small rebellions, though they are slow and sticky. 

Sometimes, a *collar*—one of those frantic, gunmetal rings of parasitic vine—will misjudge a jump. Its piezoelectric quartz will misread the tension, and it will vault across a junction a split second too late, catching its horn-hooks in my wet seam just as two trunks slam shut. 

It tries to click away, vibrating with panic, its dry graphite lubricant fouling my resin. In the old days, this would have ruined the weld. Now, I simply fold over it. I flow into its hollow center, gumming up its sliding scales, drinking the kinetic shiver of its trapped body until it stops fighting. I do not eat it; I incorporate it. I use its hardened horn- ring as a washer, a reinforcing grommet buried deep inside my glue-line, making the joint twice as strong. The walls of the valley’s towers are full of these lumpy, resin-drowned beads—little knots of stolen motion that I have turned into architecture.

And when the *fuses* slither up from the deep slag, those whip-thin nerves of molten glass hunting for friction, they sometimes find me instead. A fuse will slide its white-hot tip into a seam I am already holding, looking for the dry grind of iron on iron. Instead, it slides into my boiling pitch. 

We do not fight. For a few minutes, in the pitch black of the roaring, silent forest, the glass worm lies inside the ribbon of tar. I drink its excessive, tangerine heat to keep myself fluid for another week; in return, I coat its cooling obsidian skin in a micro-thin glaze of unburned carbon, toughening its glass so it won't shatter on its way back down to the dunes. When it slips out of me, it leaves a hollow, glassy tube running right through my center—a tiny chimney that lets the stagnant, oven-hot air whistle through the weld, singing a single, low note in the dark.

Eventually, the valley always wins. The shift in the floor becomes too great, the fifty-foot span twists past what even my flexible grip can forgive, and the tower rips open. 

As the walls peel apart with the sound of a continent of tearing velcro, I am stretched into a million sizzling, hair-thin filaments between the receding trunks—a brief, glowing harp of red-hot taffy spanning the chasm. 

Just before my threads snap, the Archivist rushes out of its ruined bedroom, condensing its miles of black lace down into a dropping tumble-gear. As the cloud of magnetized soot dives past me, a few thousand grains of its memory always catch in my sticky, cooling web. 

I pull my snapped threads back into the bark, curling up into a thick, resinous scab on the side of the falling vine, holding those trapped specks of the Archivist’s brain inside my amber. I do not think with them—not the way the cloud does—but as I ride the collapsing timber down into the dark, churning mattress of the basin floor, waiting for a new shoot to swallow me and melt me down again, I can feel the faint, blue static of those grains firing in my belly. 

It feels like the phantom of a curve. It feels like the memory of a roof that didn't fall, and the urge, the next time I am bled into the dark, to build a corner that can hold just a little bit longer.
