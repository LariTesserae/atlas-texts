I would not be the flesh, and I would not be the iron. I would be the thing that happens where the iron fails to be one piece.

Down in the third tier—below the diamond-plate, below the primary hydraulic manifolds, where the amber light of the sodium bulbs only reaches as a bruised, dusty peach reflection off wet copper—there is a switching-table that handles the cross-valley shunt when the eastern hydro-dam opens its spillways. It is a forty-foot cast-iron disk, four inches thick, scored with greasy channels and slotted like a giant watch-wheel. Every fourteen minutes, or seven, or forty-two, depending on the snowmelt three thousand feet below, the disk rotates a quarter-turn on a central bronze spindle.

I am the collar around that spindle. Or rather, I am the agreement between the spindle, the tallow-slick of a six-foot gleam-liner colony, and the tangled, fibrous root-mat of a blind, pale-white variety of subterranean bracken that never sees the slate sky.

If you were to pry me out with a crowbar—though you couldn't, because the bar would have nowhere to brace Before the upper counterweight track slid over and took your arm off at the elbow—you would only find a bushel of wet, black hair-roots, six pounds of heavy gray slug-meat, and a double handful of sheared brass cotter pins and flake-graphite. 

I don't move across the basin like the great, nomadic amalgams. I tried mobility once, years ago—or some forty-percent fraction of my current tissue did—and found the open sluices too cold, too loud with the shapeless *patter-tock* of the rain-sweat. Out there, the vibrations come from everywhere at once; they cross and cancel each other in the wet turf until you don't know which way the weight is leaning. 

Here, tucked into the greasy armpit under the turntable's central housing, the whole three-mile saddle comes down to a single, legible pressure. 

My world is rotational and vertical. The bronze spindle is eight inches thick and warm—always warm, hovering around ninety degrees from the induction coils nested directly beneath the bearing housing. My inner skin, the gray, ruffled mantle of the colony-flesh, is wrapped four times around that bronze shaft. I don't grip it; gripping is for things with knuckles. I *ride* it. My mucus is thick with suspended brass dust worn off the bushings fifty years ago, so that I am partly metal myself now, a living grease-seal that keeps the black condensate water from weeping down into the induction coils.

My outer skin isn't animal at all. Over the decades, the white, chlorophyll-less roots of the under-tier bracken have grown straight through my dorsal muscle. We have come to terms. The plant doesn't tear me when the turntable shifts because its roots are coiled inside my body like the springs in an old clock; when the iron turns forty degrees clockwise, I stretch, and the roots uncoil within my flesh, drinking my metabolic heat and the nitrogenous waste of the crushed midges and fern-pulp that wash down the spindle-groove from above. In return, the tough, woody fibers give me tensile strength. A pure colony of gleam-liners would be squeezed out of this high-pressure housing like toothpaste when the forty-ton disk takes a sudden lateral load from the upper crane-tracks. Because I am stitched through with living timber, I hold my shape. I am a gasket that eats.

This is how I know the mountain:

First, there is the *under-hum*. It is not a sound; it is a taste of copper and sour grease at the base of my feeding membranes. It never stops. It is the sixty-cycle breath of the valley, steady as a winter sleep. 

Then, twelve minutes before the turntable needs to move, the *thrum* arrives through the eastern suspension cables. It enters my body through three rusted hex-nuts that I keep pocketed in my lower folds, pressed tight against the iron flange of the housing. The hex-nuts rattle microscopically against the cast iron—*tink, tink, tink*—at a frequency too high for an ear, but right in the sweet spot of my lateral lines. That rattle tells me the eastern dam has taken a surge. The water is coming down the penstocks five miles away; the turbines in the gorge are spinning harder; the transformers up here in the fog are beginning to swell with heat.

I tighten. 

I draw my ruffled feeding skirts back from the shearing-lip of the disk above me. I pump watery fluid from my outer lobes into my central ring, fattening myself around the bronze shaft, squeezing out a fresh, warm bead of tallow-slime right where the initial torque will bite. If a stray pebble of rotten concrete has washed down the sluice and lodged in the turntable's teeth, I feel it as an ugly, jagged silence in the iron—a dead spot where the hum doesn't travel right. 

I don't have the reach to clear a jammed tooth ten feet away at the rim of the disk. I am a hub-creature. So I do the only thing I can: I shiver.

I contract my ring-muscles in a rapid, ragged staccato against the bronze spindle, deliberately breaking the smooth rhythm of the bearing. *Chug-chug-chug.* I turn myself from a cushion into a washboard. The forty-ton disk above me acts as a sounding board, amplifying my tiny, muscular spasm into a deep, resonant groan that travels out along the steel I-beams, through the wet moss cushions, and down into the dripping, amber-lit cisterns.

It is the only voice I have. It means: *Rough. Unclean. Soon.*

Within three or four minutes, I feel the heavy, sodden thump of an amalgam dropping from an upper pipe-run onto the turntable's skirt. I feel its weight—three hundred pounds of wet wire and muscle—sliding out toward the rim, following the shadow of the dead spot in the vibration. There is a brief, crunchy tug-of-war up there in the mist as it hooks itself to a passing counterweight cable and yanks the concrete wedge free. Then the dead spot vanishes. The iron rings clear again. The amalgam doesn't thank me; it just eats the moss that was crushed under the stone, and half an hour later, a ribbon of its gray, mineral-heavy dung washes down the grease-channel and settles against my upper lip. I extrude a pale membrane and take my share.

Then the valve fires.

*Shhh-ock.*

The pneumatic ram—eighteen inches of oiled steel—punches the turntable's cam. Above me, forty tons of iron and three tons of sodden hellebore turf swing forty degrees to the north. 

To anything with bones, that moment is a terror of shearing edges. To me, it is the whole point of being alive. The bronze shaft turns inside me, hot and slick, dragging my inner skin with it while my outer skin stays anchored to the stationary iron housing. For four seconds, I am twisted into a tight, spiral torus. Every bracken-root inside me pulls taut like a bowstring; the heat of the friction blooms through my gray flesh in a sudden, delicious wave of eighty-five-degree warmth; the old grease squeezed from the bronze pores tastes of burnt mutton and sweet, degraded hydrocarbons. 

Then the locking-pin drops into the north slot with a dull, cushioned *thump* that shakes a thousand cold water droplets down through the grating, pattering onto my back like tiny, icy fingers.

I slowly untwist. My outer rim creeps forty degrees around the stationary housing to catch up with my inner core, sliding over the wet bolts, gathering up the microscopic curls of bronze shaved off by the turn, tucking them into my mucus so they won't score the shaft next time.

I have no eyes to see the amber bulbs flickering in the fog, and no mind to wonder who built the saddle or why the power needs to be shunted from the east gorge to the west ridge every time the clouds bruise over the peaks. As far as I know, the mountain is made of iron, the sky is a leaking boiler, and the purpose of the universe is to turn forty degrees clockwise, hold for the surge, and return to zero without a squeak.
