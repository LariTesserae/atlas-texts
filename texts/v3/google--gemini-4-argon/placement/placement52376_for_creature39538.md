I am not in the room. If I were in the room, my ribs would ache until they cracked, or I would have to trade my lungs for leather sacs and wait three days for glue to dry. 

I am forty feet up the eastern curve of the bottle, just below the jagged lip where the limestone overhangs the pit, wedged into a chimney of split rock that the mist only reaches when the upper river is in spate.

Look up from the granite basins, past the slick black struts of the bog-oak tripods, past the dipper’s nest. Where the gray drapery of the stone pinches inward toward the pewter light, there is a shelf no wider than a boot-sole. A woven hazel basket, black with rot and half-unraveled, hangs there from an iron piton driven into a dry seam. The piton has rusted so thoroughly into the limestone that metal and rock have exchanged substances; an orange tear-stain seven feet long weeps down the wall beneath it. 

Inside the basket is a coil of braided horsehair rope, stiff with mutton tallow that has gone white and chalky with the decades, and three unchipped blanks of grey slate. 

I am the one who comes back to the edge. Not the builders—they died down in the valley when the rye rotted in the ear two generations back, or they moved south toward the chalk downs where the water runs quiet. I am only the nephew of the last man who knew how to read the basins, and he died when I was ten, his ears so ruined by his winters on the rope that you had to strike a bronze kettle with a stick just to make him look up from his broth.

I do not go down anymore. The hemp core of the main line rotted through five summers ago, and I have no wish to hang my weight on the horsehair alone. 

Instead, I lie on my belly on the wet peat of the plateau above, my chin hooked over the jagged tooth of the aperture, looking down through the throat. 

Up here, the river has not yet dropped into the basalt chute. You can still hear the treble of it—the hiss of white water over gravel, the clack of cobbles rolling in the glacial milky undercurrent, the wind snapping the stiff stems of the bog-myrtle. It is a harsh, bright world up here, full of midges and the smell of wet sheep. 

When I push my head out over the drop, the wind stops. The bass rises up the chimney and takes me by the throat. It feels like pushing your face into a bucket of cold fat; the air in the pit has a weight that pushes back against the eyeballs. My teeth immediately begin to itch in their sockets.

It takes a long time to see anything. The pewter sky behind my shoulders reflects off the mist in the throat, making a blind silver fog for the first twenty feet. I have to pull my wool hood over my head, sealing out the lowland light against the wet rock, until my eyes widen to the dark below.

My uncle told me the tripods were built to find the *seam*—the place where the winter water-note and the summer water-note cross each other in the stone. He said if you hung the slates right, you could look down from the rim and see which stones were trembling and which were still, and by that you would know before the frost came whether the ice-dam up in the high corrie would hold until spring or burst and take the lower pasture with it. *The ear lies,* he told me, tapping his own dead mastoid with a horn spoon. *The ear gets full of the thunder and hears what it wants. Look at the stones. The stones have no fancy.*

For three years after he died, I came up every autumn with a pocketful of tallow and a sheep’s femur to replace what the damp had rotted, meaning to keep the tally even if I couldn't read it. Then the main rope parted while I was testing it with a boulder, and the boulder went down through the dark and smashed the easternmost tripod into splinters. 

Wait. No—it didn't smash it. That is why I am lying here with the wet peat soaking through my woolens, my belly gone cold against the rock.

The boulder went down five years ago. I saw the splash of pale wood as the crossbeam broke. I saw the slates scatter into the red moss. 

Today, the crossbeam is back.

It is not lashed the way my uncle lashed it, with the neat crisis-cross of the valley harness-makers. It is shoved into the fork of the willow withes at a crooked, ugly angle, and the whole join is globbed over with something that catches the dim light like a dried snail-track, thick as a blacksmith’s wrist. And the bone I dropped three winters ago—the one that bounced off the rock wall and missed the frame entirely—is hanging third from the left on the center span, right next to the chipped slate my grandfather notched with three nick-marks.

I have a small piece of broken iron in my hand—the tang of an old sickle I use for scraping peat off my boots. 

My fingers are stiff with the plateau wind. Below me, sixty feet down in the cold lung, something the color of tallow and ashes is moving down the bog-oak leg of the northern tripod. It is so slow I thought for twenty minutes it was a burl on the wood, or a clump of dead sphagnum slipping down the wet bark. Only when it crossed the white scar where my boulder grazed the timber did I see the four flat toes spread out and take the weight.

It is not a spirit. My uncle’s people believed in spirits, which is why they used rowan pegs for the plinths and left the iron adze in the basin to sour the water against the underground folk. Looking down at the broad, scarred back of that thing as it settles into the ferns, I know why the iron didn't work. Iron is for things that have memories and grudges and names. That thing down there has none of those. It has only the hum, and the cold, and the gray jelly in its skull.

My fingers loosen over the aperture. I do not throw the broken sickle-tang; I just let it slip from my palm.

It drops straight down through the throat of the bottle, tumbling end over end through the pewter mist. It misses the crossbeam by a yard, misses the dipper's wall, and strikes the rim of the widest granite basin at the bottom. 

Up here, I hear nothing. The river eats the clink before it can climb ten feet. 

Down there, in the bottle-green gloom, the young one by the basin—the small one, no bigger than a marrow squash—flinches. It does not dart away into the burrow. Instead, both of them—the small one in the ferns and the great scarred one at the foot of the pole—freeze for the space of five slow breaths. Then, at the exact same speed, their broad, flat heads swing around until their pale chins are pointed directly at the granite rim where the iron lies. 

The old one’s hand leaves the bog-oak. She begins to turn her heavy body through the moss, creeping toward the basin. She is going to see what new note has fallen out of the sky, and where on the willow frame it needs to be hung.

I pull my chin back from the rock. I leave the hazel basket hanging on its rusted piton beneath me; let the rot finish it, and let the basket fall when it’s ready. They will find a use for the hazel rods. 

When I stand up, the lowland wind hits my face like a slap of cold water, and the high, thin hiss of the river rushes back into my ears, small and busy and entirely on the surface of things.
