I am on the outer rim, where the basin floor rises to meet the long, slumped shoulders of gray clay. 

Here, the mud is marginally stiffer, drained by the slight incline, though it still gives beneath the heel like cold lard. My left foot takes my weight; my right foot lifts, breaking the suction with a soft, wet *pop*, and settles three inches to the side. *Left, right, left, right.* Even with my eyes closed against the glare, the rhythm never leaves my calves. If it stops, I am dead. 

I am fifty years old, which in this white bowl makes me ancient. My knees are swollen to the size of loaves, the joints ground down by forty years of side-stepping with sixty-pound slabs of wet shale held against my belly. Because I can no longer skate fast enough to outrun a fast boil in the central avenues, they have sent me to the slope. I am a timber-watcher. 

I do not look inward at the city, though I can hear it behind me—the vast, quiet industry of ten thousand people breathing through their noses, the soft *clack-clack* of shell-beast plates, the wet slap of bare soles, and the sparse, sharp *tink-tink* of striking stones carrying across the mist. Instead, I face the blank, rising wall of gray clay that disappears eight hundred feet above me into the blinding white ceiling. 

In my hands, I hold two pieces of blue-gray slate, chipped into crescent blades. Between my wide, splayed feet lies a sixty-foot spruce tree. 

It fell from the unseen world three hours ago. I didn't hear it break from the cliff—the mist swallows everything—but I felt the dull, heavy thud through the soles of my feet when it hit the upper slope, and then watched it come sliding down the slick gray chute like a dark needle dropped onto greased glass. It came to rest in the silt twenty yards from me, its branches already snapped off by the tumble, its bark shredded into long, pale ribbons. 

Around me, four younger builders are working on the trunk with stone adzes and bone wedges, their almond-pale backs slick with condensation and sweat, their heavy shoulders rising and falling in total silence. There is no ring of metal, no rhythmic work-song—only the wet, fibrous *thwack* of stone biting into waterlogged wood, and the heavy *hiss* of our two shell-beasts waiting patiently beside an empty sled. 

My job is the branches and the bark. Nothing from the sky is wasted. 

I squat—shifting my weight from heel to heel even in a crouch—and use my slate crescent to peel a long strip of inner cambium from a broken bough. The wood smells of things that have no name down here. It smells sharp, green, and fiercely dry, a scent that cuts right through the dead, flat stink of wet chalk and crab-rot. When a fresh tree comes down, the younger builders always flare their nostrils and roll their eyes back, drunk on the resinous air of a world they cannot imagine. 

I don't bother looking up anymore. The sky is just a bright, cold lid that hurts the eyes. Instead, I pull a lump of rendered bird-tallow from the pouch at my keratin belt and rub it into the newly exposed wood of the branch, sealing the grain before the mineral dampness of the basin can warp it. This branch is four feet long, straight, and thick as my wrist. By tonight, smoothed with silt and soaked in grease, it will be a new pry-bar. It will pry up five thousand tons of stone over the next hundred years, passed from palm to calloused palm until it wears down to a nub no bigger than a thumb. 

*Tink. Tink-tink.* 

The sound comes from behind my left shoulder. I don't turn my head; I just let my pale, slitted eyes roll toward the city. 

Half a mile out on the flats, a low compound of sun-baked brick is settling. It isn't a fast liquefaction—no steam is rising, no shell-beasts are bolting—just a slow, heavy dimpling of the crust. Through the white glare, I can see a line of thirty builders already strung out from the sinking walls like ants emptying a flooded nest, passing bricks hand-to-hand toward a raised causeway of river-stones. 

And then, right beneath my left big toe, the clay goes light. 

It’s not a sound yet. It’s just a sudden, sickening absence of push-back, as if the earth beneath the skin of mud has quietly changed its mind. 

A half-second later, the sound comes: *shhhhh-tik-tik.* Tiny, colorless bubbles break through the gray paste between my toes, smelling faintly of deep iron and cold gas. 

I don't shout. My throat hasn't made a sound louder than a cough since I was a child. I simply bring my two crescent stones together—*TINK*—and at the exact same instant, the four young builders drop their adzes onto the sled, grab the braided bird-hair ropes looped under the spruce trunk, and lean their thick bodies forward into the harnesses alongside the shell-beasts. 

My swollen knees scream as I straightening up, snatching the newly greased branch and my striking-stones, and I begin the low, wide-legged skater's shuffle upslope. 

Behind us, the trough where the tree was resting five seconds ago drops three feet with a slow, thick *glug*, like an old man swallowing porridge. The tail-end of our sled dips into the softening lip; the six legs of the beasts scramble, their translucent plates clattering like dry bones as they haul the heavy timber up onto the firmer gray shoulder. 

When we are forty yards clear, we stop—though our feet keep moving, *left, right, left, right*, treading the cold paste. 

One of the young women looks at me. Her face is pulled into the permanent, deep-pleated squint of our kind, her lower legs caked in drying gray silicate that is already cracking over her calves. She gives a single, sharp dip of her chin toward my hands. 

I toss her the raw, freshly greased spruce branch. She catches it without a sound, immediately tucking the heavy end under the runner of the sled to test its flex, leaning her thick shoulder into the wood from the sky to keep our load from sinking into the floor of the world.
