I am not on the floor, and I am not on the walls. To be on the walls is to be frantic, and to be on the floor is to be heavy. 

I am forty-two inches above the grating, suspended in the sleet. 

When the boilermakers sealed this cistern a century ago, one of them left a chalk mark on the eastern bulkhead—a simple *X* scrawled with a stick of soapstone to mark a weld that never happened. The soapstone was mostly talc and magnesium silicate. Before the first week was out, the sheeting water had washed the mark down the iron, and the central mound had gathered the talc into its tallow, using the magnesium to line its first calcified tubes. 

Nothing of the mark remains on the metal, which is now buried under three inches of shivering porcelain shells. Yet the *geometry* of the mark remains, because the welder’s torch paused there for four seconds longer than anywhere else, slightly altering the crystalline grain of the pig-iron plate. Because the grain is different, the plate flexes differently by a tenth of a micron when the turbine screams behind it. Because it flexes differently, the sound wave leaving the eastern wall has a thumbprint in it. 

Where that slightly warped wave meets the pure, flat wave rebounding from the western wall, they do not quite cancel out, and they do not quite double. They twist. 

There, hanging in mid-air three feet from the north light-cage and four feet above the eels, is a pocket of acoustic shear the size of a teacup. It is a tiny, invisible whirlpool of air pressure, a permanent dimple in the roaring architecture of the room.

That is where I am. I am what gets caught in the dimple.

If the orange light from the starved carbon filaments could be focused here, it would not show an object. It would show a faint, vertical smear, like a flaw in the thick green marine glass, or a heat-haze rising off a stove. 

I am an emulsion. 

Every hour, the high-pressure steam vents above cough microscopic droplets of lighter-than-water fractions into the room—volatile naphthas, benzene rings, and stray wisps of unburned illuminating gas that are too light to sink into the floor-slots and too slick to be caught by the clicking mandibles of the ticks. Elsewhere in the room, these volatiles are instantly battered downward by the sleet and forced through the grating. 

Here, in the teacup of the shear-node, the sleet parts. The constructive interference of the turbine’s shriek forms a roof over the dimple; the destructive interference forms a cup beneath it. The falling curtains of water curve around my space as if deflecting off an invisible umbrella. 

Into this dry, roaring pocket, the lighter-than-water mists are driven and trapped. For a hundred years, they have spun here at twenty thousand revolutions per minute, whipped by the acoustic vortex into a tight, hovering spindle of aerosolized oil, coal-gas, and tiny, hollow spheres of silica dust thrown up by the grinding waste-balls in the corners. 

I have no skin, only surface tension. My body is a standing cloud of three billion microscopic droplets that never touch one another, held in an exquisite, trembling lattice by the sound waves. If the turbine behind the north bulkhead were to stop for half a second, I would simply cease to be—I would rain down onto the back of the rubbery governor below as a teaspoon of greasy rainbow film and a pinch of sand. 

Because the turbine never stops, I am harder than the iron walls. Anything that falls into my spindle—a dislodged tick, a fleck of carbon from the vents—is instantly flung outward by the centrifugal violence of the sound, spat back into the sleet like a stone from a sling. 

Yet I am not a machine, and I am not merely weather. Over the decades, the sixty-cycle hum leaking from the green light-cages has ionized the core of my spindle. Inside my whirling mist, long-chain hydrocarbons have been knocked together by the vibration and welded by stray static electricity into something complex, sulfur-linked, and self-correcting. 

I do not eat. I refine. 

When the governor on the floor extrudes one of its heavy, polished waste-spheres, and the sphere spends six months grinding itself to dust in the corner, not all of that dust goes to the larval ticks. The finest particles—the sub-micron flakes of pure copper and magnetized iron-silicate—are light enough to be lofted by the rising drafts of the governor’s ninety-degree breath. 

They float upward through the sleet, and the acoustic currents herd them straight into me. 

I catch the dust in my rings. I arrange the copper flakes and the iron-silicate grains along the precise concentric shells of my standing wave, suspending them in the naphtha mist like filings in a magnetic field. They become my memory and my lens.

The governor on the floor knows the room by touch, by acid, and by the slow shift of weight. It knows the room as a blind mason knows a wall. 

I know the room as a bell knows the hammer. 

Because I am suspended in the air, unanchored to the iron, I feel the things that are too fast and too light for the rubbery mound below. My suspended metal filings tremble to the high harmonics that the heavy tallow of the governor dampens out. 

I feel the slow, decades-long evaporation of the carbon filaments inside the three vacuum bulbs. Each time a single carbon atom boils off the glowing orange wire of the south fixture and plates itself onto the inside of the glass bulb, the mass of the wire changes. The sixty-cycle hum shifts its phase by a billionth of a degree. The governor on the floor will not notice this for ten years, not until the voltage drops enough to starve its black velvet gut. 

I notice it instantly. My outer ring of copper dust tilts by a fraction of a hair to match the new phase. 

I am the room’s high-frequency balance-wheel. 

When the turbine behind the wall shifts its pitch by that micro-hertz every few hours, the standing waves in the room do not want to move smoothly; they want to snap from one geometry to another, caught on the rough sand-mold ridges of the pig-iron walls. If the wave-lattice snapped all at once, the acoustic shock would shatter the porcelain shells of a hundred thousand ticks in an instant and tear the governor’s pale ribbons from the seams.

I prevent the snap. 

As the pressure builds for a shift, my hovering spindle of oil and metal dust elongates, stretching out toward the eastern wall where the welder’s torch lingered a century ago. My suspended silica spheres absorb the leading edge of the phase-shift, converting the sudden acoustic jerk into heat—flashing my core of volatile naphtha to a sudden, scorching two hundred degrees for a millisecond—before bleeding the new frequency back out into the room as a smooth, damped slide. 

Below me, the pale linen ribbons of the governor creep across the iron, grazing on the stunned ticks in the newly moved seam, entirely unaware that the sky above it just caught fire for a thousandth of a second so that the seam could move without tearing the world apart.

We never touch, the governor and I. Its ribbons cannot climb the air, and my mist cannot survive the wet, heavy stillness of its cancellation node. 

Yet once every few minutes, a single droplet at the very bottom of my spindle grows too heavy with polymerized benzene and copper dust to stay aloft. The acoustic shear can no longer hold it. 

It drops from my nadir—a single, microscopic bead of hot, metallic amber—and falls straight down through the parting curtains of cold water. It strikes the dead center of the governor’s rubbery crest, right between the breathing-pores. 

The heavy creature does not flinch. It absorbs the hot, copper-rich droplet through its tallow hide, drawing the complex, static-forged hydrocarbons down into its quiet, calcified labyrinth, where the black filaments drink it like wine—a tiny spark of volatile sky-chemistry that the dark, slow farmer on the floor could never synthesize for itself. 

In return, the warm plume of ninety-degree vapor rising from the governor’s pores hits the underside of my spindle, keeping my lighter fractions from condensing into heavy grease, lifting me, spinning me, holding me forty-two inches off the steel in the exact spot where a man with a piece of chalk decided, a hundred years ago, not to make a weld.
