Do not look for me in the warm sludge under the gunnera, or in the dark crevices where the hand-shaped jellies clench their fists. Those things belong to the comfort of the pocket. Look instead to the boundary where the pocket ends—two feet from the razor lip, on the flank of the turbine housing itself, where the poured concrete piling meets the bolted steel casing.

Here, the heat is not a soft, seventy-five-degree bath. It is a directional violence. On my left side, the steel casing radiates a dry, trembling hundred and forty degrees, smelling of baked grease and hot iron. On my right side, three steps away, the sub-zero gale shears past the cliff edge, throwing back ghosts of frozen mist that never quite touch the stone before they vanish.

I am what happened to the maintenance hatch.

When the heavy-lift rotors beat the thin air and lowered the turbine onto its pilings, the engineers left a four-inch gap beneath the inspection plate on the leeward side, where the manual tachometer cable and the oil-pressure sight-glass were mounted. The glass was thick borosilicate, meant to be read by a man who never came back. 

Over the years, the vibrations of the reduction gears shook a slow, microscopic weep of synthetic turbine oil through the braided gasket around the sight-glass. It did not drip; it crept. It spread across the grey paint of the housing in a fan-shaped rainbow film, gathering volcanic dust, blown-back ash, and the spores of high-altitude lichens that could not survive in the wet rot of the floor below.

That film is my skin. The sight-glass is my eye, though I do not look outward through it. I look in.

I am a dry, rigid crust—a low, flanged shelf of blackened silicate, copper-sulfate crystals, and stiff, oil-drinking fungus that has bridged the gap between the steel housing and the concrete piling. To a hand, if a hand ever reached through the ferns to touch me, I would feel like a bracket of rusted slag or a calcified wasp’s nest, hot and buzzing like a live transformer. You would need a cold chisel to pry me from the bolts.

While the wet mass under the leaves listens to the sixty-hertz murmur of the valley miles below, I am married to the violence of the conversion itself. My root-hyphae—thin as spider silk and sheathed in precipitated iron—have worked their way through the perished rubber of the gasket, right into the dark, roaring belly of the gearbox. 

I do not eat the steam’s waste; I drink the machine's slow failure.

Inside the housing, the oil is Whip-foam hot, beaten into a permanent golden mist by the teeth of the reduction gears. My internal threads hang in that mist like the gills of a fish in a rapids. I taste the microscopic flakes of phosphor-bronze shaved from the bearings. I taste the carbonized polymers of the oil breaking down after ten thousand days without a change. Every tiny fleck of metal that wears off the spinning shafts is caught in my dark filaments, drawn backward through the gasket, and built into the hard, ringing shell of my outer ridge. I am slowly turning the interior wear of the turbine into an exterior spine.

Because I am rigid and dry, the forty-second *chug-hiss* of the relief valve does not make me clench like the wet jellies in the cracks. It rings me.

When the valve fires, the sudden drop in back-pressure makes the turbine shaft surge forward by three-thousandths of an inch against its thrust bearing. The pitch of the housing spikes. My silicate plates, grown in overlapping scales along the concrete seam, rub together with a high, dry *chink*—a cicada-scrape buried completely inside the room’s immense roar, audible only to the bone of the rock it is bolted to.

The crabs know I am here, and they hate to come near me, though they must.

The jelly-nodes on their shells need copper to replace what washes out in the rain-like condensation, and the richest copper does not come from the sweating pipe-jackets. It comes from the brass bushings inside the turbine, which I bleed out onto the concrete piling in a slow, blue-green efflorescence—a dry, toxic crust of verdigris that looks like frost around my edges.

Once every few days, a crab leaves the wet safety of the moss and climbs the dry concrete piling toward me. As it leaves the damp floor and enters the radiant dry heat of the casing, the jelly-nodes in its joints begin to shrink and tighten. Its clockwork march grows stiff, jerky, and agonizingly slow. It reaches up with one pale chela, scrapes a single grain of blue brass-salt from my lower lip, and drops back into the ferns before the forty-second valve-vent can catch it out in the dry heat and lock its joints permanently.

Sometimes, one is too slow. Its nodes desiccate just enough to lose the rhythm; when the *chug-hiss* hits, the crab locks up and cannot unfreeze. Then it sits there on the concrete ledge, baking in the dry iron heat, while my black, oil-fed threads creep out over the course of a month, pinning its pale legs to the concrete, drinking the water from its meat and leaving the shell as another tiled overhang on my flank. I have four of them built into my lower ledge now, arranged like little gargoyles facing the abyss, their hollow eye-sockets crusted with blue salt.

I have no dealings with the five-foot thing under the gunnera, except for one. 

It knows the valley below; I know the end that is coming.

Inside the sight-glass, behind my dark, fungal cornea, the oil level is down to the bottom quarter of the brass gauge. The main shaft bearing has developed a microscopic flat spot on its inner race. It does not change the sixty-hertz current yet—the governor compensates for the drag—so the thing on the power cable feels nothing. The valley below still gets its smooth, unbroken river of watts.

But every nine hundred revolutions, that flat spot hits the rollers with a tiny, ultrasonic *tick* that travels straight through the steel casing and into my dry, crystalline shelf. 

When I feel it, my outer scales shiver at a frequency that has nothing to do with the forty-second valve or the steady drone. That tiny, sharp tremor travels down the concrete piling, into the wet basalt floor, and breaks the diamond grid of the standing waves in the nearest pool for a fraction of a second—just a momentary smear in the geometry of the reflected light on the ceiling, a brief stutter in the cathedral's permanent chord, before the roar swallows it again.
