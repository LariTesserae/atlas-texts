Two floors up on the west facade, where a cast-iron rainwater leader has split at the seam and spilled a black, mineral-rich delta of rust down the brick, a window frame has rotted entirely out of its opening. The brickwork around the sill has softened into a red, sandy paste held together only by the hairy aerial roots of a trumpet vine. 

Just inside this gap, out of the direct drip of the canopy, the floor of the old carding room has collapsed into a steep scree of waterlogged joists, lime plaster, and thousands of wooden bobbins that have swollen and split in the damp. 

I am in the deep pocket of warm, stagnant air just behind the sill, where the brick arch of the window holds the rising heat of the courtyard like an inverted cup.

At first glance, I am only a pale, sulfur-colored cascade of shelf fungus—a tiered colony of *Laetiporus* or weeping polypore—pouring out of the rotten mortar and pooling over a pile of rusted loom-weights. My upper surfaces are matte and velvety, banded in cream and dull ochre, beaded with the amber sweat of guttation. 

But the shelves are not fixed to the wall. They are the overlapping, leathery mantle-flaps of a single, sprawling body—something akin to a terrestrial octopus that has traded the sea for the steam-bath, or a colonial tunicate that has learned to crawl on a muscular, slug-like foot. My flesh is heavy, gelatinous, and translucent in the thin edges, veined with a slow-moving network of violet-grey vessels that pulse at a fraction of the engine’s speed below. Every fourth *chug-thump* of the great pumps sends a shiver through the west wall’s foundation, and on that fourth beat, my mantle contracts, squeezing a warm, briny lymph out to the fringes of my body.

I do not go down into the oily peat. The floor of the courtyard belongs to the heavy, iron-hearted things with their keratin jaws and their slick, tallow-coated throats. My domain is the vertical margin—the wet brick, the flaking lime-wash, the rusted fire escapes, and the dark, echoing interiors of the mills where the green light of the courtyard dies ten feet from the windows.

I am an eater of boundaries. 

For a week at a time, I lie draped over the windowsill, my lower skirt spread three feet wide across the damp masonry. Beneath that skirt, my feeding surface—a pale, ruffled velvet of microscopic rasps—is pressed tight against the wall. I secrete a slow, mild oxalic acid that fizzes softly against the hundred-year-old lime mortar and the fallen plaster. Hour by hour, I dissolve the dead calcium of the building’s bones, drinking the chalky slurry. 

What I take from the brick, I give back in a different form. As the dissolved lime and iron salts pass through my mantle, I precipitate them out along the edges of my overlapping flaps as a hard, pearlescent cement—a thick, porcelain-like nacre tinted pink and rust-red by the iron oxide in the wall. Where I have slept for a month, I leave behind a curved, glossy buttress of biological stone that glues the loose bricks back together, sealing the cracks against the prying fingers of the philodendrons. The entire west wall is laced with my old, abandoned sleeping-scars: pale, scalloped seams of mother-of-pearl winding through the dark red brick and green moss, holding the facade upright long after its mortar has turned to sand.

When the north wall’s relief valve blows—*shhhhh-clack*—and the great cloud of steam rolls up through the monstera leaves to wash over the second-story sills, I wake a little. 

My edges uncurl. From beneath the tiered shelves of my back, eight long, flat arms unspool. They are the color of bleached tallow, boneless and ribbon-thin, able to slip through a crack no wider than a penny. They do not have suckers; instead, they are coated in a clear, tacky mucus that smells faintly of vinegar and crushed ferns. 

Two of these ribbons reach out into the green void of the courtyard, feeling for the broad, slick leaves of the strangler figs that press against the fire escape. They do not pull the leaves; they merely stroke them, harvesting the thin film of soot, fungal spores, and mineral dust that settles out of the rising steam, drawing the ribbons back under my skirt to be licked clean. 

Another arm slips backward, deep into the dark interior of the mill floor, feeling its way over the pile of swollen wooden bobbins. In the absolute dark behind me, where the heat of the courtyard fades into the cool, subterranean damp of the wider building, blind white woodlice and pale cave-crickets come to feed on the rotting timber. If one blunders across my trailing ribbon, it sticks. The arm does not yank; it simply curls inward from the tip, rolling up like a wet carpenter’s tape over the course of twenty minutes, bringing the small parcel of chitin back to the slow furnace under my mantle.

I have no eyes to see the great mud-turtle down between the condensers, nor the small, flat gasket-things clinging to the steam pipes twenty feet away. I know them only as variations in the wall’s temperature and the rhythm of the iron. 

I know when the great one below has cleared the flywheel, because the slight, rhythmic *hitch* in the building’s vibration—the tiny stutter in the *chug-thump* that had been building for three weeks as the fig root tightened on the shaft—suddenly vanishes, smoothing back into the deep, effortless purr of oiled babbitt metal. When that happens, a long, slow ripple passes through my yellow shelves, and I release a cloud of pale, dust-fine spores into the rising steam. 

Some of those spores will drift down into the black water at the base of the pumps, where the great creature will drink them in with its broth of oil and iron, replenishing the flora of its ancient gut. Others will catch on the wet hides of the little pipe-clingers, sprouting into the very moss that camouflages their backs. 

I do not move far. By winter, I may have crept four feet higher up the brick pier, following a warm chimney flue, leaving a fresh trail of pink, nacreous glaze over the crumbling sill to keep the window arch from caving in. The white sky four stories up will turn the color of lead, cold rain will hiss against the upper steam-pipes, and I will fold my pale ribbons beneath my shelves, flattening myself against the warm brick, listening through a million microscopic rasps to the iron heart beating in the muck below.
