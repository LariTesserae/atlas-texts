I am not on the walkways. If you look for me on the bamboo grids, you will only see the grey-green bulge of my poncho disappearing around a buttress, my willow-frames leaving transient, slow-filling trenches in the drift of velvet-walkers. My shoulder-bafflers are almost always flipped to the blank, unpainted side of the horn—*do not converse / listening / hands full*—except for the single, wide paddle above my left collarbone that is smeared with pale green badger-light and crossed with two notched lines of black pitch: *Inspector of Tension. Stay off the starboard anchor-cable.*

I live my life three inches from the slate, suspended in a cradle of braided gut-string that smells of old vinegar and wet dog. 

This rock is too young. That is the central fact of my existence, the thing I feel in my teeth even when I am curled inside the sleeping-drum with my brothers and our twenty shirt-toads. A mountain should be old; it should have settled into its bones. This fissure is a fresh split in the world’s skin, barely five generations wide, and it has not finished happening. The slate is still full of locked-up anger. It wants to shear; it wants to spall; it wants to drop a ten-ton flake of its face through the middle-tier vats and take forty yards of comb-line down into the bone-glacier at the bottom. 

Worse than the falling stone is the sound it makes before it falls. A dry, sharp *crack* of splitting shale is the loudest thing in our world. It rings from wall to wall like an iron bell, and within six heartbeats of that ring, the white roof of fog above us curdles and drops its monsters—the great, pale, wing-walking things with heat-pits along their jaws that come tumbling down the slot to tear open whatever made the noise. 

So I am a **Loom-Listener**. I am the one who hears the rock thinking about breaking before it makes up its mind.

Right now, I am hanging seventy feet below the great South Windlass, in the blind belly of an overhang where even the balloon-ferns grow thin and pale for lack of sinking drift. My willow-shoes are unhooked and dangling from my belt; my split-toed socks are stripped off and tucked into my bosom to keep them warm against the largest of my shirt-toads—an old, heavy female named *Silt* who sits directly over my solar plexus like a warm, breathing loaf of bread. My bare feet, blue-veined and prehensile as roots, are wedged into a hairline vertical crack to hold me steady against the updraft.

My forehead is pressed flat against the naked, weeping stone. 

Actually, not flat against the stone—against my *ear-horn*. It is a seven-inch cone carved from the dense, ivory-cured femur of my mother’s brother, taken down from the Salt-Cradles ten seasons ago. The wide bell is sealed to the wet slate with a ring of stiff slug-mucus; the narrow tip is jammed firmly into the bridge of my nose, right between my eyes. 

Through the bone of my dead uncle, the mountain hums into my skull.

If you have never listened to the wall, you think the chasm is quiet. You think it is only the *shhh-shhh* of the walkers’ feet and the wet *plop* of a fat rag-moth hitting a moss-bolster. You are wrong. Inside the stone, the town is roaring. 

With my eyes shut, sorting the vibrations through my sinuses, I can hear the whole three-hundred-yard harp of the settlement. There is the low, greasy, rhythmic *thrum-thrum-thrum* of the third-tier windlasses turning in their tallow-sockets. There is the high, tight, singing whine of the main horsehair hawsers stretched across the void—a sound that rises in pitch when the fog grows heavy and weights the fibers with water. Below me, forty fathoms down in the violet dark, a herd of ten thousand badgers is shifting from the east wall to the west; through the bamboo struts driven into the rock, their forty thousand padded feet register as a deep, bubbly purr in the back of my jaw, like a pot of sour mash working in a corner.

I filter all of that out. I push past the windlass-thrum and the rope-whine until I find the voice of the slate itself. 

Good rock has a long, cold, heavy grain to its silence. It feels like pressing your face against a sleeping mammoth. 

This ledge under my temple does not feel like that. It has a *tick* in it. Every forty or fifty breaths, a tiny, microscopic thread of quartz deep inside the wet black leaf of the stone gives way under the weight of the cantilevered granary thirty feet above me. *Tink.* It doesn't travel into the air—not yet—but it hits my uncle’s femur like a needle tapped against a tooth. 

*Tink.* 

The granary is too heavy. The Vat-Minders have had a good month with the blanket-worms; their clay jars are full to the rims with pickled meat, and the main horn-dowels pinning their floor-beams into this fault have swollen with the damp and are wedging the stone apart. In three days—maybe four, if the fog thickens and the wet moss gets heavier—a slab the size of a house-wall is going to peel off this face. It won't just drop the Vat-Minders into the dark; the snap of its parting will bring the whole upper ceiling of blind flyers boiling down into the mid-tiers to strip the warmth out of the huts.

I pull my head back from the bone cone. The suction of the mucus breaks with a tiny, wet *lip* that I muffle instantly inside the sleeve of my rag-moth poncho. 

For a moment, I just hang there in my gut-harness, letting my eyes adjust back to the pewter gloom. A gill-slug the size of a platter has crawled over my left calf while I was listening, leaving a cold, silver ribbon of slime across my woolens; three velvet-walkers are perched on my knee, their long, hairy legs flexing in unison as they groom the condensation from each other’s eyes. I don't brush them off. They are insulation. Instead, I reach into my pouch, take out a pinch of dried, powdered rail-liver, and drop it onto the moss beside my foot—a little bribe for the wall-things, an apology for taking up their grazing-space.

Then I reach into my belt for my wedges.

I do not use iron, and I do not use a mallet. I am a creature of the cleft; I do my heavy work with grease, dry wood, and patience. 

From my hip-quiver, I draw six slivers of bog-oak that have been kept inside a bladder-skin bag packed with roasted bone-ash—the driest things in five miles of dripping rock. They are whittled to blades no thicker than a fingernail. Moving with the slow, underwater deliberation that keeps the air from rippling, I slide them one by one into the hairline fracture below the granary’s main strut. I don't tap them in; I lean my shoulder against them, using the leverage of my long, tendon-strung legs, pressing until the veins in my neck swell against my collar and *Silt* shifts uneasily against my ribs at the sudden spike in my body heat.

When the dry oak is seated deep in the stone's wound, I take my grease-horn and pack the outside of the crack with stiff badger-tallow, leaving only the butts of the wooden wedges exposed. Then I reach up, pinch a fat, drooping curtain of water-moss that hangs from the strut above, and wring it out over the oak. 

The dry wood drinks the cold water instantly. Over the next six hours, those wedges will swell with a slow, relentless, silent violence. They won't split the rock; I have placed them along the counter-grain to pinch the fault shut against the granary's pull, shifting the weight three inches to the left, onto a rib of solid, unfractured greywacke that I sounded yesterday. It won't hold forever—nothing here does—but it will buy the Vat-Minders two seasons to empty their jars and move their anchor-pins.

My fingers are numb now, the purple webs on my knuckles darkening to black. It is time to climb up and find a face to press against.

I hook my willow-frames back onto my feet, release my safety-pin from the slate, and begin to walk up the vertical wall—not hauling myself on the rope like a fat Greaser, but stepping from moss-bolster to bamboo-peg with my long, hooked toes, my body tilted out over the three-hundred-foot drop into the violet mist. Rag-moths flutter past my hood like falling, wet leaves; one brushes my cheek with its cold parchment wing, and before it can recover its glide, a fist-sized toad anchored to the shale beside my knee opens a mouth like a pink purse and folds the moth inside without a sound.

When I reach the middle-tier platform, the herd of badgers has passed, leaving the bamboo decking warm and slick with their musk. A young Weaver is waiting by the windlass-post, her baskets full of damp, grey walker-down. It is *Oul*. I know her before I see her pale, violet-lit face—I know the particular way she rigs her shoulder-bafflers, with three small, notched shells on the left that always hang slightly askew, and I know the smell of her tallow, which she mixes with crushed fern-seed to take away the chalk-stink.

She sees me heave myself over the lip of the platform. Her eyes—huge, liquid, black as wet coal—widen slightly, and the fingers of her right hand give a quick, silent flutter against her throat. *Gladness.* With a twitch of her chin, her shoulder-paddles flip from *Sorting wool / do not jostle* to a wash of soft indigo: *You were long in the overhang. Your hands are blue.*

I don't use my paddles. My thumbs are too stiff to work the gut-strings. Instead, I step straight into her space, moving slowly so the moss on my shoulders doesn't brush the moss on hers with a dry scrape. I reach beneath my poncho, open the warm, sour-smelling pocket over my belly, and slide my freezing hands inside alongside *Silt*. At the same time, I lean down—I am four inches taller than Oul, stretched by ten years of hanging in a harness—and press my damp, cold forehead firmly against the side of her jaw, just below her ear.

Her skin is greasy with tallow and warm as a newly laid egg. 

I keep my mouth shut tight and let the breath go into my throat, starting a low, resonant vibration at the base of my tongue. It travels through my frontal bone, across the slick film of grease between us, and straight into her mastoid.

*Mmm-hm-hmmmm,* I hum into her skull. *The stone under the third-tier vats is angry. I pinned its mouth shut with dry oak, but tell your sister to move her pickle-jars to the north corner before the mist thickens.*

She doesn't pull back. She shifts her weight on her willow-shoes so our knees lock together against the sway of the bridge, and she presses her temple harder into my cheekbone. Her own hum starts—higher than mine, a reedier, buzzing cadence that tickles the roots of my molars and makes the water beading on my eyelashes tremble.

*Hmm-mm-hmmm,* she vibrates back into my brain. *She already knows. The bristle-worms came out of that wall this morning—all of them at once, dropping into the walkway like rain. They always leave before the rock shifts. Come into our drum tonight. My mother traded three jars of sour-curd to the Dredgers for a piece of deep-marrow. We are going to grease our feet and pass the feel-stones.*

I stay like that for forty heartbeats, eyes half-shut, watching a line of translucent gill-slugs glide over the toe-frames of her shoes while her voice warms the bone of my head. There is no sound in the chasm around us—only the vast, green, shivering rush of ten million silent lives eating the fog before they freeze, and the faint, steady *thrum* of the ropes holding us all between the white sky and the black floor. 

I hum a single, low note against her jaw—*Yes*—and then I turn to the Vat-Minder’s hut, pulling my wooden fan from my belt to tell them their floor is about to fall.
