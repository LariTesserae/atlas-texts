Here, life does not run; it seeps. It does not call out; it listens. The absence of birdsong is not an emptiness, but a statement woven into the air itself. The air, so thick with water it is nearly a liquid, does not support the quick, frantic beat of wings. Instead, motion in the vertical spaces is a study in patience and silence.

The lords of the air are the Gheists. They are not hunters or chirpers, but harvesters of the atmosphere. A Gheist looks less like an animal and more like a living fragment of the sky itself. Its body is a flattened, asymmetrical lozenge of pale, membranous skin, stretched over a delicate, lattice-like bone structure. Its color is the uniform white-grey of the fog, and its skin is perpetually dewy, beading with moisture that it continuously absorbs. There are no discernible eyes, only dark, shallow pits that seem to drink light. It does not have a beak or teeth, but a wide, ventral slit that it can open to glide through the dense air, filtering out the untold billions of spores, algae, and microorganisms that live suspended in the atmospheric-water. To launch, a Gheist will crawl to the very edge of a basalt spire, anchor a single claw, and simply let go. It does not so much fly as it does *fall with intention*, its wide, leathery wings catching the imperceptible convection currents rising from the warm, wet rock. It will drift for hours, a silent, silent wraith, tracing a path between peaks before alighting with the soft, wet sound of a squeezed sponge, its inner reservoirs full. They are spectral, functionally immortal, dying only by a slow, graceful dissolution into the very elements they consume.

Supporting this aerial existence is the world of the hanging gardens, and its primary engineer is the Verdigris. The Verdigris is the creature of the vines. It is a slow, deliberate climber, built for a world without ground. Its body is a low-slung, six-limbed platform of muscle, covered in a mosaic of tough, pebbled hide in shades of black, grey, and the same electric green as the moss. Its feet are broad, splayed discs with thousands of microscopic, adhesive setae that allow it to cling to the sheer, wet obsidian with the ease of a snail on a leaf. A Verdigris does not walk; it flows. It spends its life traversing the thick, rope-like vines in a perpetual, slow-motion journey, its file-like rasping mouth methodically stripping the super-abundant moss and fern fronds. It is a living, mobile garden bed, its droppings rich with the nutrients of the stone and plants, falling into the great catch-plants below to be reabsorbed. They are solitary, their contact limited to rare, silent, tactile meetings on impossible-wide vines, a brief exchange of scent before they continue their separate, vertical migrations.

But where there is a slow and silent herbivore, there is a slower and more silent predator. The Obsidian Weave is terror in its most static form. From a distance, or even up close, it is indistinguishable from its lair. The Weave is an arthropod-like construct, a long body of chitinous plates that have the exact texture and color of the wet, fractured basalt. It does not have legs in the traditional sense, but dozens of jointed, needle-like limbs that it tucks into crevices, anchoring itself within the rock face or hollowed-out sections of the great vines. It is a master of ambush. When a Verdigris crawls within reach, the Weave does not lunge. It *unfurls*. Two of its forelimbs, prehensile and whip-thin, lash out with impossible speed and silence, wrapping around the Verdigris and impaling it with barbs that deliver a potent, paralytic neurotoxin. The Verdigris does not even have time to struggle. The Weave then retracts its prey, dislocating its jaw to swallow the creature whole. It may not eat again for another ten years. Its presence is only known by the strange, lattice-like patterns its legs leave on the rock, almost like mineral deposits, and by the sudden, unexplained absence of a Verdigris from its regular path.

And in the spaces between them all are the Chitterlings. Swarms of them. They are the world’s silent hum, its constant, unseen janitorial staff. A Chitterling is no larger than a human thumb, a glossy, multi-legged scavencer with a shell like obsidian. They are totally blind, navigating by sensing the faintest vibrations in the wet stone. They live in the micro-fractures, emerging in their thousands when a piece of plant matter falls or when an Obsidian Weave finishes its meal. They flow over the remains like black mercury, their mandibles working without sound, scraping and cleaning every last bit of organic matter until the rock is pristine again. They are the base of an invisible food web, and the food of nothing, for they are too numerous, too fast, and too hidden in their swarms within the stone’s skin.

Beneath it all, in the endless, windless water, is the true sovereign. There are no crashing waves, only the patient, infinite swell. The splash that sometimes echoes in the distance is not a breach for air, but the shifting of a continent. Down there lives the Slow Leviathan. It is less a creature and more a geological event that possesses life. Imagine a manta ray whose wingspan is measured in miles, whose flat, broad back is a self-sustaining ecosystem, a floating forest of symbiotic mosses and algae that has existed since the mountains broke the surface. It is a filter-feeder on a planetary scale. Its mouth, a cavern on its underbelly, opens slowly, cavernously, and it simply moves, a glacial pace across the mirror-surface, straining the rich upper layer of the global ocean. It has no brain as we understand it, but rather a distributed nervous system threaded through its immense body, thinking in geologic time. Its only movement is a slow, rhythmic undulation of its mile-long fins, a flexing that displaces enough water to create a single, heavy, delayed splash that can travel for miles across the flat sea. It knows no enemies, no urgency. It simply *is*, growing by imperceptible increments, following ancient currents, the silent, patient god in a world defined by silence and patience. It is the mirror given life, watching its own reflection in the water it has claimed.
