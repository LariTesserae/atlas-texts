Here, in the vertical vastness of the High Rime, the inhabitants are not so much distinct entities as they are extensions of the Stranglers themselves—parasitic flecks clinging to the hide of a sleeping god. To survive the crushing emptiness, they have evolved into something between a climber, a burrowing insect, and a breath held too long.

They are a people of the ledge and the hollow, known perhaps to no one but themselves. Their bodies are short and densely packed, built for conserve rather than display. There is no waste in their physiology; their limbs are ropy with ropy, high-density muscle, and their bones are porous yet light, filled with micro-structures of air to survive the pressure shifts of the vertical world.

Their skin is the most striking adaptation. It is not the soft dermis of lowland thinkers, but a thick, keratinized hide, gray and mottled with the textures of lichen and dead bark. This armor allows them to press directly against the spongy, calcified flank of a Strangler without losing their own body heat to the vacuum-chill of the air. Often, they are indistinguishable from the pillars they inhabit—until they move. When they do, it is with a jittering, insectile speed, a blur of sudden motion that belies their stillness.

Their faces are dominated by the need to breathe. The "metallic stinging" of the air has necessitated a radical evolution of the respiratory system. They possess large, flared nostrils and intricate, turbinated nasal passages that warm the freezing air before it enters their lungs. When they exhale, it creates a permanent plume of steam that clings to them like a second ghost. Their eyes are wide, black pools, adapted to the bruised purple gloom, possessing a tapetum lucidum that catches the faintest ghost-luminescence of the clouds below, making their gaze shine in the dark.

They do not walk. The concept of "walking" is absurd here. They move with a synergetic fluidity, using all four limbs with equal dexterity. Their hands and feet are hooked, the fingers elongated and tipped with calcified claws that can shear through the fibrous outer layers of the Stranglers or gouge into the ice-slick bark. They hook, pull, and swing, traversing the vertical architecture with the confidence of spiders.

Their society is defined by the terrible emptiness (0.1). They do not gather in villages, for there is no ground to hold one. Instead, they exist in "Clings"—small, kin-based clusters distributed across a single kilometer of pillar. The "built" environment is a triumph of desperation over physics. They do not construct outward; they excavate inward.

Using primitive tools made from the sharpened carapaces of the giant insects they hunt, they burrow into the "dead-wood" of the Stranglers—the spongy, calcified layers of ancient moss that form the outer crust. These burrows expand over generations into complex warrens, shallow caves honeycombing the pillar’s face. The entrances are disguised with woven mats of living vine and thorn to keep the heat in and the hunting banshees out.

Connecting these warrens are the "Veins," precarious bridges made from dried gut stretched taut between pillars, or braided vines harvested from the canopy. To traverse these is an act of faith; the Veins sway violently in the wind, humming with a low, thrumming vibration. The inhabitants cross them on all fours, their claws hooked into the fibers, moving low to the rope with a practiced, rhythmic sway. To fall is not just to die; it is to drift for hours through the void, an agonizing descent into the cloud membrane leagues below.

Culturally, they are obsessed with holding on. Their language, a series of clicks, whistles, and sharp inhalations, is designed to travel short distances without carrying too far—secrecy is survival. A loud voice is a death sentence in the High Rime; sound travels unimpeded for miles, betraying a location to the leathery, gliding predators that haunt the gulfs.

Their fires are rare, sacred, and highly controlled. They do not burn wood—wood is structure, life. Instead, they farm the "Bleed." They wound the Stranglers, carving shallow spirals into the bark to collect the thick, amber resin that oozes out. This resin is hyper-flammable and burns with a fierce, chemically bright blue flame that produces little smoke and intense heat. Fires are only lit in the deepest recesses of the hollows, shielded by sheets of polished ice or polished phytoplankton chitin, and they are never left unattended.

The diet of the High Rime is as harsh as the altitude. They are opportunistic omnivores, scraping the "air sweat" from the bark—drinking the mineral-rich, oxygenated water that runs in rivulets down the pillars. They hunt the schools of gliding creatures, setting nets woven from gut and spider-silk across the gaps between pillars to snare the unwary. But mostly, they rely on the "Carpet"—the layer of mosses, ferns, and fungi that grows in the crevices of the Stranglers. They cultivate this carefully, pruning it to encourage growth, eating the spongy, bitter tubers that hide beneath the surface.

Emotionally, they are a quiet, intense people, shaped by the indifference of their world. There is no room for expansive joy or explosive anger; such extremes waste energy and distract from the primary task of clinging. Their art is small: intricate carvings made from the beaks of dead banshees, or knots tied in gut-rope that record the history of a Cling’s lineage.

At "night," when the bruised light fades to absolute black, they retreat deep into the stone-wood of their homes. They press together for warmth, listening to the deep, groaning creak of the Strangler shifting in the wind and the popping of the freezing sap. In the dark, they can feel the air rippling, the viscous fluid of the world swimming past their anchored sanctuary. They sleep with one eye open, one hand hooked into the roots of the ceiling, acutely aware that they are merely temporary tenants in a cathedral that does not care if they fall.
