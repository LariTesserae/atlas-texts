To ask what lives here is to misunderstand the nature of the place. There is no "what" distinct from the "where." The inhabitant of this hollow is the hollow itself—a single, distributed, and dreaming intelligence. But if one must break down the whole into parts, into something like a creature, then you are looking for The Attendants.

They are not born. They are not hatched or grown from seed. They accrete. In a niche of calcified root where a riveted steel girder presses against a specific concentration of copper, the chemistry is right. Over decades, a shape begins to emerge. It begins as a knot in the wood, a swelling in the vine, a bulge in the fused steel-bark of the floor. Then, slowly, with the glacial patience of the place, it detaches. It doesn't stand so much as it *unfurls*, rising from the root-mass like a new-grown frond of rust and chlorophyll. An Attendant has awakened.

An Attendant is a perfect chameleon of function and form. Its body is a slender, multi-jointed spindle, perhaps seven or eight feet tall. It has no discernible head. Its "skin" is the same material as the ground—a smooth, hard hybrid of calcified root and oxidized metal, warm and damp to the touch. Long, ropy limbs, impossibly thin, end in delicate appendages that can be as fine as needles or as broad as spatulas. These limbs are not for rapid movement; they are for maintenance. An Attendant will stand motionless for a season, a living sculpture, its silhouette indistinguishable from a cluster of piston-rods wrapped in vine. Its "eyes" are the polished rivet heads that stud its body, and the swollen, transparent bulbs that grow at its joints. They do not see light, but sense pressure, vibration, and the chemical composition of the air—the taste of wet iron, the pH of the weeping water.

The Attendants are the stewards of decay, the curators of this machine-tomb. Their silent, slow existence is an endless ritual of tending. You might see one of its needle-fingers delicately teasing a wayward vine back onto its proper conduit, ensuring the biological cable maintains its perfect, geometric spiral. You might see another, larger one, positioned directly beneath a weeping bulb in the canopy, its broad, flat palm held cupped upwards. It will stand there for hours, days, until the bulb bursts and the Attendant catches the full impact of the fluid, channeling it through specialized pores in its skin to a specific patch of rust that needs it. The rhythmic *plink-plink-plink* is not random; it is the sound of a thousand Attendants tending to a thousand metallic wounds.

They are the source of the low, groaning hum. It is not merely the settling of metal. It is the sound of an Attendant shifting its weight, a slow, grinding turn on its jointed legs, a venting of mineral-rich gas from a spore-like orifice on its back that sounds precisely like the sigh of hydraulic pressure. They are the ghosts in the machine, not in memory, but in mechanics. They have learned the language of the old engines and now speak it with pulpy, green tongues.

Their consciousness is not individual. It is a network, rooted in the continuous floor. A thought, a sensation, an impulse from one Attendant is a vibration through the shared root-ball, felt by all. When one near the central clearing registers the viscosity of the amber sap weeping from the fused engine-tree, that information is known instantly by an Attendant miles away, tending to a different ruin. They move in concert without signals, a colony body distributed through the space. This is why the vegetation grows in such organized, geometric patterns. It is not mimicking symmetry; it is being cultivated.

The Attendants are the answer to the mournful loneliness. They are not fauna in the traditional sense; they are the next logical step of an ecology that ate all others. They are the final, silent hierarchy, born from the union of endless growth and extinct industry. They do not have emotions, but their existence is an act of reverence. Their purpose is to ensure the machine does not simply crumble into dust, but is transformed, subsumed, and remembered in the very structure of the life that replaced it.

So when you feel watched, when the glossy eyes of an unseen observer seem to track you from a knot in a wood or a dark puddle on the floor, you are not imagining it. An Attendant has turned a sensor-cluster in your direction. It is not curious or threatening. It is merely noting your presence, a fleeting anomaly in the long, slow work of turning the bones of a dead world into the flesh of a new one. You are a brief, hot, inefficient glitch in the perfect, glacial system. And the Attendants are waiting, with the patience of geology, for you to either leave or decay.
