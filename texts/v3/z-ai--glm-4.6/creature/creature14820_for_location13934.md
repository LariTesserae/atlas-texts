The people here are carved from the same material as their home. They are not so much born as they are stubbornly formed from the wet clay and cold iron of the hollow. You see it first in their skin, which has the dull, greyish-pink and tough texture of clay that has been worked, re-wetted, and worked again. It doesn't tan under the diffuse light, but seems to slowly absorb the mineral-rich air, taking on the colour of the stone they rise from.

They are a short, solid people, broad in the shoulder and hip. Their posture is a permanent, slight crouch, as if bracing against a blow or listening for a shift in the ground beneath them. They move with a deliberation that can look like slowness to an outsider, but it is in fact the economy of pure necessity. Every footfall is placed, tested, and committed to with the careful focus of a stonemason. To move recklessly here is not to trip, but to invite the ground to tear itself away. You almost never see them run.

Their faces hold the history of the place. Eyes are typically pale, washed-out colours—the blue of pale sky, the green of wispy lichen, the brown of stagnant water—adapted to pull every last mote of light from the heavy grey air. But it is their hands that tell the true story. The palms are broad and calloused, the knuckles permanently swollen. The fingerprints are worn down from a lifetime of handling cold, rough stone and slick, wet timber. Under their nails, the dirt is not black soil but a permanent stain of reddish-brown clay and dark grey silt. You will not find a single person in this hollow, from the youngest child to the oldest elder, whose fingers are not stained.

Their teeth are a testament to their diet and environment. They drink the water that weeps from the stone, rich in iron and minerals, and it stains their enamel. Most have a faint, reddish-brown tinge to their teeth, as if they are constantly chewing on rust. Their food is what the land begrudgingly offers. They are not farmers in the traditional sense but gleaners and cultivators of the margins. Their primary sustenance comes from vast, fungal gardens they coax to life in the dampest, darkest recesses beneath their homes, on pallets of damp wood and scavenged moss. These fungi are tough, chewy, and carry the same wet, earthen taste as the air itself. They supplement this with thorny, bitter roots that they pry from the mud, roots that other grazing animals have the sense to avoid. Meat is a profound rarity, perhaps the flesh of a blind, pale fish dredged from the deepest stagnant pools, a delicacy eaten with a silent reverence.

Society here is not a choice; it is a survival mechanism compacted into a philosophy. The density of their homes is matched by the density of their obligation to one another. The "Pact with the Landslide" is the unspoken and spoken law of their existence. When a section of wall gives way, when a timber groans and a shanty collapses into the scree beneath, there is no wailing or despair. There is a quiet, immediate, and communal response. The displaced are simply absorbed into the homes of their neighbours while the entire community works to reclaim the stone and timber and rebuild. They see it not as a random act of cruelty from the earth, but as the ground reclaiming a loan. To mourn its loss is foolish; to immediately put it back to use is wise.

This has created a culture of profound patience and pragmatism. Their language is quiet and economical. Loud noises are for danger— a sharp crack of stone, a sudden rush of water, the cry of the Rim-Walker returning from an expedition. Conversation is a low murmur, a rumble that blends with the ambient groan of the settlement. They have more words for the qualities of wet stone and the sounds of settling timber than they do for joy or love. Affection isn’t shown with grand gestures but with quiet, practical acts: sharpening a neighbour's tools, reinforcing a weak joint in their wall, or sharing a portion of one’s fire to dry their damp cloak.

The most revered people here are not warriors or chieftains, but artisans and engineers. The "Water Diviners" are the lifeblood of the community. They are not mystics but hydrologists, men and women who can read the flow of the weeping cliffs and predict where a new channel must be hacked into the stone floor to prevent the rot from taking hold in a home’s foundations. The "Stone-Shapers" are their priests, elders who know the precise angle of every fractures in the canyon walls and can choose which rock will bear a weight and which will crumble. The constant *scrape-scrape-scrape* you hear from time to time is the sound of a community-ritual: the sharpening of every essential tool, an act that affirms their readiness and their intent to endure.

Children are taught differently. They are not taught to run or sing loudly. They are taught to feel the subtle vibration in the soles of their feet that signals a shift in the scree. They learn to distinguish between the drip of water on stone, on wood, on mud. Their games are of balance and precision—walking the narrowest ledges, stacking small stones into the most precarious towers, learning the weight and texture of every part of their world. A child’s first rite of passage is not a hunt, but a successful journey to the rim and back, a test of their footing and their nerve against the raw, open sky. To them, the vast emptiness above is far more terrifying than the claustrophobic, weeping walls of their hollow.

They are a people who have made peace with unease. They do not hope for the land to become stable; they accept its instability as its fundamental nature and build their lives around the rhythm of repair. They find comfort not in permanence, but in the cyclical return to a state of readiness. They are clenched, resilient, and quiet. They are the damp, patient moss that refuses to be washed away, the stubborn fist that closes hard around a tool even as the earth grumbles beneath its feet. They hold their breath with the canyon, waiting for the next shift, ready to rebuild.
