# What Lives Here

The ferns live here. That must be said first, because they are the most obvious life, and because what else lives here is harder to name.

**The ferns.**

They are not true ferns. They lack the ancient patience of their surface cousins, those plants that have persisted since before flowers were invented. These are something newer and more anxious, though they do not move. Each one grows from a single crack in the ruptured floor, its root system descending into a wound in the rock that goes deeper than light can follow—down toward whatever thermal source feeds the heat and humidity. They drink from below. They do not drink the condensation that beads on their own waxy leaves; they let that water gather and fall, returning it. This is not generosity. This is indifference.

Their fronds stand erect, five feet or more, curled tight at the top like a fist that has never been taught to open. The wax on their surfaces is thick enough to thumbprint. In the faint phosphorescence that clings to the wettest stone, their green appears almost black, a green so saturated it seems to be hiding something darker at its center. They are spaced with a precision that feels deliberate, as if each one chose its location with territorial care—never close enough to touch a neighbor, never so far as to be invisible from the next. A congregation of solitaries. A crowd that does not acknowledge itself.

They do not unfurl. That is the thing about them that matters most. Fiddleheads are supposed to open; that is the whole narrative of a fern, the slow unclenching of compressed life into broad receptive surface. These have refused. Some have been standing long enough that their lowest fronds have begun to brown at the edges, curling inward rather than outward, tighter rather than looser. They are not growing toward anything. They are growing upward—that is all—and holding shut.

It is possible they are waiting. It is possible they are dying in a posture that looks like waiting.

---

**But something else lives here too.**

It is not an animal. It is not a plant. It is not a machine, though it has a relationship with the machine that is difficult to describe without the language of parasitism or worship.

In the center of the room, where the iron hulk juts from the floor at its awkward, regurgitated angle, there is a discoloration on the stone. It spreads outward from the base of the machine in a pattern that is not quite radial and not quite dendritic—something between a spill and a root system. It is rust-colored at the edges, where it is oldest, but nearer the machine it shifts to a deep, wet brown, and then to something almost organic: a dark, glistening amber that beads with moisture like a living surface.

This is not a stain. This is not a mineral deposit. Watch it long enough—which means watching it for hours, because its movement belongs to the geological timescale that governs everything here—and you will see that it changes. The amber patches migrate. They thicken in some places and thin in others. They climb the machine's corroded surfaces in increments too small for casual observation, following the copper piping upward in slow, branching trails that suggest a system of transport rather than simple decay.

What lives on the machine is a biofilm—though that clinical word does not capture what it actually is. It is a community. It is a metabolism.

The current that still hums in the machine's buried heart does not power the biofilm, exactly, but it orients it. The biofilm grows along the paths of greatest electromagnetic potential, its cell structures—whatever they are, bacterial or archaeal or something less categorized—arranging themselves in patterns that echo the machine's original circuitry. There is something in this slow, living corrosion that remembers what the machine was for. The rust weeps in streaks that follow the logical flow of the pistons, the rotational plane of the frozen gears. It is as if the biofilm is trying to complete the machine's function with organic materials, substituting its own wet processes for the dry mechanical ones that have seized up.

It is not succeeding. The machine is dying. But the biofilm does not know this, or does not care, because the current still flows, faintly, and where there is current there is the possibility of work, and where there is the possibility of work there is something to eat.

The biofilm is why the air tastes of wet iron and rich oxygen. It is releasing the one as it consumes the other. It is breathing, after its fashion—slowly, on a timescale that makes the dripping condensation seem frantic. The oxygen it gives off is what feeds the ferns, which stand in their scattered grid like air intakes for a system whose primary processor is the orange-amber mass that sheaths the dying engine.

They are all the same organism, possibly. The ferns and the biofilm. Connected through the cracks in the floor, through the root systems that descend into the dark and the corrosion trails that descend into the same dark. There is no way to know without excavating the stone, and the stone is still too new and too sharp to excavate with anything less than violence.

---

**And there is one more thing.**

It is not alive. But it behaves in a way that makes the distinction feel academic.

Deep in the tangle of the machine—in a chamber that was once a boiler or a turbine housing, now cracked open like an egg by the same rupture that tore the floor—there is a space where the current concentrates. The hum is audible there, not just in the teeth but in the chest, a vibration that synchronizes briefly with the heartbeat of anyone who stands close enough before falling out of phase and becoming just noise again.

In that space, the air moves. Not with the lazy drift of the thermal vents, but with a purposeful circulation, a slow churning that keeps the humidity from condensing on the machine's inner surfaces and instead pushes it outward, where it beads on the stone and drips. This is a mechanical process continuing without mechanical input—the architecture of the machine still shaping the flow of air even though its parts have frozen. The machine breathes too, or rather, it is breathed through. The current draws air across something warm at the very bottom of the apparatus, and the air rises, and it cools, and it falls, and it is drawn again.

This circulation is what maintains the greenhouse. This is why the vegetation is lush despite the sparse light. The machine—broken, buried, half-corroded into organic matter—is still doing the work it was built to do, or a shadow of that work, a ghost of climate control that keeps this cavern at a stable temperature and humidity, a sealed terrarium buried under the earth.

It is not alive. But it is keeping other things alive, and it is being kept alive by the things it keeps, and the loop is closed, and the distinction is academic.

---

**So: what lives here.**

A grid of unfurling ferns that will not open, drinking from deep thermal wounds and breathing oxygen they did not photosynthesize but received as gifts from something below.

A slow, amber corrosion that climbs the machine and remembers its functions and eats its current and sweats rust and oxygen.

A circulation of air driven by a dead engine's last electrical ghost, wrapping the room in a blanket of stable heat and wet that makes the whole system possible.

And nothing else. No insects. No birds. No rodents or reptiles or fungi or anything that moves on a timescale human beings find comfortable. The silence is not empty; it is full of processes too slow to hear. The dripping water is the fastest thing in the room, and it drips once every few seconds—a frantic pace compared to the growth of the biofilm, the reach of the ferns, the cooling of the stone.

If you stayed long enough—years, decades—you would watch the biofilm consume the last of the current and release its hold on the machine. You would watch the ferns lose their oxygen supply and tighten their curls further, some defense against starvation that their ancestors never needed. You would watch the circulation fail and the heat equalize and the condensation slow and stop. You would watch the cavern age, its raw walls finally settling into the patience of old stone.

But you would have to stay a very long time. The things that live here do not intend to leave. They do not intend anything. They simply persist, in this hot, dark, subterranean solarium, tending their dying engine in absolute silence, waiting for nothing, refusing to unfurl.
