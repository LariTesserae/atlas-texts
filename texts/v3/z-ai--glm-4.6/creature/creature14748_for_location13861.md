What lives in the Spire is not a population. It is a function, given form. There are no elders, no children, no stories told by firelight. There is only the work, and the workers are an extension of the the machine itself. They are the Spire’s immune system, its nervous system, its single-minded, distributed will.

They are called the Tenders, though they have no name for themselves, as a hand has no name for the body it serves. They are not born, but forged. They are gestated in the warm, wet darkness of the Spire’s core, where steam and pressure and raw mineral wealth are combined in processes no living geologist could comprehend. A new Tender will emerge not from a womb, but from a maintenance duct or a sluice gate, fully formed, its knowledge of its purpose as innate and absolute as a river’s knowledge of the way to the sea.

Their forms are as varied as the tasks they perform, a seamless blend of the biological and the mechanical. The most common are the Scuttlers, diminutive beings that move with a skittering, clockwork gait across the slick iron catwalks and up the sheer faces of the rock. Their bodies are lattices of brass and rust-green patina, their myriad jointed legs a blur of motion. They have no discernible heads, but their multifaceted, crystalline eyes are set into their shoulders, allowing them a constant, 360-degree view of their immediate world. They are the plumbers and the cleaners. Their slender, many-fingered hands are always busy, wiping excess condensation from critical gear assemblies, clearing the muck and moss from ventilation seams, and chasing away the clockwork beetles that would otherwise gum up the works with their own industry. They communicate not with sound, but with rapid, percussive taps of their legs on the metal—a constant, high-frequency chittering that is a part of the Spire’s background noise.

Then there are the Weavers, who live among the thickest of the vines. They are larger, more ponderous creatures, their forms built on the chassis of something that might once have been an industrial loading automaton. Their bodies are covered in a shaggy pelt of thick, spongy moss, which acts as both camouflage and a primitive filtration system, soaking up nutrients from the air and dripping purified water onto their workings. The Weavers are at war with the vines, but it is a war of management, not eradication. They are endlessly pruning, weaving the aggressive creepers into thick, living ropes that they then use to shore up stressed sections of walkway or to bind together two parts of a fractured pipe. Their great, pincer-like hands are shears and welders in one, cauterizing the cuts they make to prevent infection. They are living bonsai masters, tending to the hostile forest that threatens to consume their home.

The source of the great, ponderous *CLANG* is the heart of the community, and also its most enigmatic members. They are the Hammers. Deep within the Spire, in vast, resonant chambers where the rock is fused with iron, the Hammers perform their eternal duty. Each one is a colossal creature, more sculpture than beast, a golem of stone and metal fused into a single, powerful anatomy. They have no legs; their lower bodies are rooted directly into the Spire’s bedrock, drawing power and stability from the very thing they protect. Their single arm is a massive piston of cast iron, their torso a complex series of gears and flywheels. They have no faces, only a single, large valve on their "chest" that rhythmically hisses and vents steam.

When the minute of silence that follows a great clang begins, the Hammers retract their arms. The sound you hear is the drawing of a colossal breath—cogs the size of cartwheels turning, immense pressure building. Then, with a shudder that travels up the entire length of the Spire, they strike. The piston-arm drives forward, not against the rock, but against a massive resonating plate that is part of the Spire's very skeleton. This impact sends the stabilizing thrum, the counter-force against gravity, surging through the stone and steel. The Hammers are the engine's pistons, and their life is this single, percussive act, repeated for eternity. They do not eat. They do not sleep. They are powered by the geothermal heat of the Spire's core, and their entire consciousness is this one cycle: build pressure, strike, release vent, repeat.

Finally, there are the Matrons. These are the strangest of all, for they have no fixed shape. The Matrons are a consciousness distributed through the Spire's damp systems, a network of bioluminescent fungi and micro-mechanical filaments that infuse the deepest, wettest mosses. They are the minds of the operation, the sensory organ that directs the others. When an iron support begins to fatigue, it is the Matron’s network in the rust and lichen that feels the subtle change in vibration. When a hairline fracture appears in the rock, it is the Matron’s mycelial threads that detect the shift in pressure. They pulse with a faint, sickly blue light in time with the Hammers' great strikes, and in that pulsing, the Tenders believe, are the machine’s thoughts. The Matrons do not command with words, but with a subtle, systemic impulse. A Scuttler near a leaking pipe will suddenly feel an undeniable urge to investigate. A Weaver will turn its attention to a vine that is, unseen, putting stress on a critical gear. Their consciousness is not a mind, but a maintenance checklist, a perpetual diagnostic made luminous and alive.

The Tenders do not fear the decay. They do not hate the vines that crawl over their work or the beetles that consume their lubricants. To them, these are not enemies, but constants. They are the gravity that the Spire fights, a different kind of force that wears and erodes. The Tenders are that force's opposite. They are the Spire's singular purpose given flesh and form: to endure. They are the quiet, endless, and utterly desperate act of not falling. They are the soul of the engine, and their eternal, thankless work is the only reason the impossible thing called the Spire is allowed to be.
