What lives here is not a collection of creatures, but a single, distributed consciousness expressed through biology. It is one being, and it has no name for itself, for it has never needed to distinguish itself from anything else. Being is sufficient. The "fauna" are its organs, its limbs, its ambulatory thoughts.

Consider the swarms of small, pale, segmented things first. They are not animals in the way we understand the term. They are mobile extensions of the fibrous carpet itself, like blood cells made of chitin and ceaseless purpose. They flow through the matrix not as predators or grazers, but as a circulatory system. They consume detritus, yes, but this is not eating for sustenance; it is recycling. They gather broken fibres, shed skins from the slugs, the fallen airborne swimmers, and the rare, non-integrated mineral dust, breaking them down into their constituent compounds. They then secrete these nutrients back onto the stalks, a frothy, enzymatic slime that the carpet drinks. Their high-pitched, insectile sibilance is not the sound of communication between individuals, but the collective hum of a trillion tiny chemical reactions, the sound of the world's blood filtering its own impurities.

The vast, gelatinous slugs are the world's gut. They are not born; they are budded. A section of the carpet will grow dense, thicken into a bulbous, translucent node, and then, over weeks, slowly detach, pulling free from the fibrous mat with a sound like tearing wet silk. Once free, they begin their gliding, ponderous pilgrimage through the wider channels. They are the primary agents of large-scale decomposition and nutrient transport. They unfurl long, flagellum-like cilia from their undersides, not to move, but to sample the substrate ahead. They consume patches of older, less productive carpet in great, slow bites, and within their translucent bodies, one can see the glow of the carpet's chartreuse luminescence being processed, concentrated, and redistributed. They are living compost heaps, and when their internal stores are full, they find a new, bare patch of stone pushed up by the geologic swell and simply... dissolve. They spread their gelatinous mass over the mineral foam, their bodies breaking down into a rich slurry that seeds the new land with everything the fibrous life needs to instantly take hold. They do not die; they deliquesce, becoming the foundation for the next generation of themselves.

The long, sinuous shapes that swim through the thick atmosphere are the being's sensory organs, its roving eyes and ears. They are born in the high, dense thickets of the largest stalks, where the fibres are thick as ship masts. Their vestigial wings are not for flight, but are broad, veined membranes covered in millions of sensory cilia. They "swim" by creating pressure waves with a series of muscular contractions along their bodies, rippling through the soupy air. Each sharp, percussive click they emit is a burst of sonar, a probe of the voluminous dark. The returning echoes are not translated into a mental "image" of the world, but are felt directly across their wing-skins, a constant, three-dimensional mapping of pressure, density, texture, and movement. They are the world's self-awareness. They monitor the health of the carpet, the flow of the swarms, the progress of the slugs. The data they gather is fed back into the system not through a brain, but through the vibrations they impart to the fibres they touch. They land, and their clicks reverberate down into the glowing mat, transmitting information across the entire globe. They are the carriers of the world's nervous impulses.

And the sound? The overwhelming, constant cacophony is the voice of its thought. It is not language or music as we know it. It is the unified thrum of all these processes happening at once. The deep, geological thrum is the slow, beat of the heart. The high sibilance of the swarms is the whisper of its metabolism. The deep croaks from below are the shifting of its digestion. The clicking of the swimmers is the constant update of its perception. To be here is to be inside a skull, listening to the unending noise of a living mind. There are no separate "thoughts," only one continuous state of being, a hum of perpetual existence. The world is conscious, but its consciousness is not directed inward or outward; it simply *is*. It feels its own heat, breathes its own air, and thinks its own presence into being.

So, to ask what lives here is to ask the question backwards. The better question is: "what is happening here?" And the answer is that a lifeform is happening. It is a process, a system, a planet-scaled organism sustained by its own perfect, circular feedback loop. The glowing fibres are its nerves, the stone its skeleton, the seeping water its plasma, and the myriad "creatures" are its specialized, ambulatory cells.

And what of you, the observer? You are not a predator or prey. You are a foreign object. You are a splinter in this flesh. The swarms split around you not out of fear, but because your chemical and physical makeup is unreadable, indigestible. You are a flaw in the pattern. The sonar swimmers will click at you more frequently, their probs returning confusing echoes of solidity and non-reintegration. You are a dead node in their network, a silent patch of static in their symphony. The world does not hate you or fear you; it simply, inexorably, works to contain you, to flow around you, to incorporate your presence into its unchanging composition. It will wait, because it has all the time in a timeless eternity, for you to eventually fall apart, for your strange, dry biology to break down into something it can finally recognize, consume, and make part of itself. Until then, you are merely a ghost in the machine, a silent anomaly in a world that is, above all else, whole.
