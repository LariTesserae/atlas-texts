They are a people of the interstice, born of the grey pause between creation and completion. You would not notice them at first, for they are camouflaged not by color, but by texture and stillness. Their skin possesses the same soft, pearlescent pallor as the stone, a matte finish that seems to absorb the diffuse luminosity of the cavern rather than reflect it. It is damp skin, cool to the touch, often sheened with a thin layer of the mineral condensation that coats everything here. They move with a fluid, lateral grace, their frames lithe and compressed—narrow shoulders and flexible spines evolved to navigate the tight, developing corridors of the Settlement. They are a diminutive people, not out of fragility, but out of utility; in a city with no plazas, where the distance between dwellings is measured in inches, to be broad is to be stuck.

Their faces are devoid of the harsh angles found on the sun-scorched surface. Cheekbones are wide and smooth, jaws soft, serving as anchors for the immense muscles required for their primary labor: listening. They do not have the flat, closed ears of surface-dwellers; theirs are open, funnel-like, constantly twitching against the humidity, catching the sub-audible groans of the Unfurling.

Sight is secondary to them. Their eyes are dark, liquid pools, almost entirely pupil, adapted to the gloom and the soft, shadowless light. They possess a tapetum lucidum that catches the faint glimmer of the moss, making their gaze shimmer slightly in the dark, like the wet salamanders they revere. They do not stare boldly; they watch through their eyelashes, a habit of deference to the overwhelming presence of the rock. To look too hard is to presume ownership; they see only what is necessary to place a foot or swing a hammer.

Clothing is minimal and strictly utilitarian, woven from the fibrous, sponge-like mycelium of the fungal trees. It is close-fitting, leaving the limbs free to climb the rickety scaffolding or shimmy through a fissure. The fabric absorbs the dampness rather than repelling it; to be dry is a foreign concept, just as sweat is immediately lost to the humid air. They adorn themselves sparingly, often with small, polished pebbves found in the drip-pools, hanging them on cords around their necks. These are not jewelry in the decorative sense, but identifiers of specific seismic districts—a way of knowing who belongs to which shifting wall.

Their society is defined by the "Masonry of Patience." They carry themselves with a quiet, heavy deliberation. Sudden movements are considered taboo, a breach of etiquette that risks disturbing the equilibrium of the Settlement. Even their children are trained from birth to move slowly, to set down a cup without a clink, to speak without raising their voice. When they speak, it is in low, resonant hums—a language designed to travel through rock and timber without echoing sharply. Arguments are nonexistent, or at least invisible; there is no space for shouting, and in a city where a wall might decide to heave, disagreement is a luxury they cannot afford.

The most distinct members of their community are the Listeners. These are individuals, usually elders, who have gone blind not from age, but from choice. They spend their lives pressed against the stone, their ears flattened against the walls, their bodies thrumming in sympathy with the vibrations of the Chasm. They are the architects and the weathermen combined. A Listener can feel the stress building in a fault line weeks before it cracks. They dictate where the next wedge of clay must be removed to relieve pressure, and where the scaffolding must be reinforced.

The common people are perpetual masons and weavers. You will see them perched on the lashed-wood scaffolding that clings like ivy to the city walls, wielding fire-hardened saws and stone mallets. They do not build *up* so much as they build *in*. They are constantly carving new rooms out of the cooling rock as the ceiling lifts higher, and sealing old ones that have become unstable. Their hands are rough, callused into patterns that match the grain of their tools, their fingernails thick and perpetually stained with grey dust and moss paste.

Spiritually, they view themselves as the lichen of the world. They believe the Chasm is a breathing entity, a fetal god slumbering in the dark. They visualize the walls not as inert matter, but as slow-moving muscles. When the "tremors" come—the gentle, rhythmic groans of the Unfurling—they do not flee. They pause. They place a hand on the nearest surface to feel the pulse. If the rhythm is steady, they continue their work. If it falters or grows violent, they dismantle their homes with a speed that defies their usual languor, migrating like ants to a more stable vein of rock.

Their diet is as sparse and specialized as the vegetation. They tend to the patches of phosphorescent moss like farmers, harvesting the jade-colored fronds to stew into a thick, vitamin-rich paste. They cultivate the black lacy fungi in the dark hollows of their homes, feeding it on the mineral-rich water that drips from the ceiling. Meat is rare, usually the small, pale blind fish or crustaceans found in the deeper sump-pools, but they never touch the white salamanders. To harm a salamander is to invite the dampness into one's lungs, a spiritual death sentence.

To encounter one of them in the narrow tunnels of the Settlement is to encounter a ghost that feels more solid than you do. They will press themselves flat against the wall to let you pass, their eyes cast downward, their breathing so shallow it seems they hold it until you are gone. They are not fearful, exactly; they are simply reverent of the space they occupy. They know that the floor beneath their feet is not yet decided upon. They are the custodians of the transition, living in the soft, grey moment before the world hardens into its final shape.
