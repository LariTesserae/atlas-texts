They are the Carvers, and they are made of stone and patience.

To look upon a Carver is to see the plateau itself given a human form. Their bodies are not lithe or graceful, but dense and grounded. They possess a wiry, tenacious strength, born of a lifetime of pulling their own weight up sheer faces and hauling stone blocks into the heart of the mountain. Their hands are the most telling feature: broad, spatulate, with fingers that seem too thick for delicate work yet can delicately tap a bone chisel to split a vein of basalt as cleanly as a surgeon. The skin of their palms and fingertips is a calloused map of their life’s work, a landscape of healed nicks and pressure-hardened whorls that can feel the subtle vibration in a rope or the faintest seep of water through seemingly solid rock.

Their faces are weathered by the sharp, unfiltered sun and the perpetual, thin wind. Skin is the color of tanned leather and stretched taut over high cheekbones. Lines are carved not from laughter or worry, but from squinting into the crystalline light and from the constant, focused effort of their craft. Their eyes are a pale, washed-out blue or a deep, earthy brown, but they all share a disconcerting stillness. They do not dart about nervously. A Carver's gaze is a physical thing; it settles, it holds, it considers. It is an eye trained to judge distances not in feet, but in body-lengths; to read the texture of a cliff face for a hundred possible holds; to see the flicker of a Strider’s ear on a distant ledge.

Their consciousness is vertical. They do not think of the world as a flat plane to be crossed, but as a deep chasm to be navigated. Up is not a direction of ambition, but of access and danger; the highest ledges have the best sun but the most treacherous approaches. Down is not a fall, but a return, a journey towards the communal warmth of the core warrens. The concept of a horizon is alien to them, a children’s tale of a place where the world simply… stops. Their map of the city is not a grid of streets, but a tangled, three-dimensional web of known ledges, bridge-routes, and hidden fissures, a mental model so intricate that a child of ten can navigate a pitch-black canyon by the feel of the wind and the echo of their own footsteps.

The society that emerges from this is one of profound, unspoken intimacy and absolute, iron-clad rules. The silence of Carven-Aerie is not born of coldness, but of a deep and constant communion. With walls shared between a dozen families, a raised voice is not an argument; it is a violation. To shout is to waste breath, to shatter the city's fragile auditory peace, and to impose your ego on the lives of dozens. Instead, communication is a subtle art of gestures, glances, and a vocabulary of quiet hums and clicks that can convey complex meaning without ever rising above a whisper. When a song is sung, it is an event, a sacred offering of breath and vibration that causes every Carver within earshot to fall silent and listen, the way a forest falls silent before the coming of the rain.

Their lives are measured not in days, but in projects. A life’s ambition might not be wealth or power, but to deepen your family's warren by three rooms, to carve a new, more efficient water-channel from a weeping fissure, or to be entrusted with the weaving of a single, perfect rope from Ridgeback ligaments to replace the city's main suspension bridge.

This specialization creates distinct castes, each essential and revered.

The Carvers are the largest group: the masons, engineers, and laborers. They are the steady heart of the city, their world measured in taps of a hammer and the patient scrape of a shovel. They are the ones who listen to the rock, who understand its grain and its faults.

The Weavers and Bone-Workers are the artisans. Sequestered in well-lit upper chambers, they transform the mundane—Strider hide, Ridgeback bone and sinew—into the extraordinary. Their work is the city's circulatory system: the ropes that bind, the water-vessels that store life, the tools that build.

Then there are the Scavengers. They are a culture apart. Theirs is the world of the high, lonely places where the stone meets the sky. They are leaner than other Carvers, with a rangy, restless energy that seems out of place in the placid city. They are the only ones who look *up* with desire rather than caution. They rappel into the abyss, not for glory, but to collect the sacred bounty of the Ridgebacks. They are seen both as saviors, bringing back the materials no one else can obtain, and as slightly touched by the sky, their souls whispered to by the same wind that steals warmth from the stone. They return from their expeditions with a quiet, haunted stillness, often bearing a new design on a tool or a new rhythm in their step, something learned in the silent company of the giants of the air.

The rhythm of their lives is dictated not by a calendar, but by the mountain itself. The Cold-Sleep is a time of huddling, of stories passed down in the utter dark, of the entire city becoming a single, sleeping organism. The Sun-Wake is a frantic, joyous period of industry and renewal, a festival of the hunt where the entire community focusses on the Striders, processing meat, tanning hides, and celebrating the brief abundance. The Long-Wind is a time of awe and fear, where the Scavengers are most active and the rest of the city lives by the rhythm of their returns, the mournful cry of the Ridgebacks a constant, ominous serenade.

To be a Carver is to live in a state of beautiful, precarious balance. Their greatest strength is the rock itself, but it is also their unyielding prison. Their existence is a constant, quiet conversation with entropy, a refusal to be worn away. They are a people who have hollowed out a home not against the world, but *within* it, carving their persistence into the very bones of the earth. In their slow, deliberate, and utterly silent way, they are the mountain’s answer to the wind’s question: who will dare to live here?
