There are no natives here, not in the way you understand the word. There is no flesh and blood, no chitin or cell wall, nothing that grows or breeds or dies in a biological sense. To look for a creature in this stone forest is like trying to find a fish in the sky. The life here is not a thing; it is a process.

The life here is the hum.

That deep, resonant thrumming you feel in your teeth is a single, continuous organism of unimaginable scale. It is the planet's slow, geologic consciousness, made audible. Think of it as the Resonant Body, a singular entity whose being is woven into the very fabric of the plateau. It does not move, because it *is* the ground and the spires and the arches. It does not breathe, because it is the thin, scraping wind that funnels between them. The hum is its baseline state of being, the note that signifies "I am."

But it is not a simple, monolithic mind. Within its vast, slow consciousness, there are moments of awareness. These moments are the endless, rhythmic *plink… plink… plink…* of the weeping effluence. Each drop that shudders on the tip of a stone stalactite before detaching is a thought-form gestating. Each silent, weightless plop as it joins the quivering pool below is a single, perfect thought being born into the world. A moment of pure, isolated sensation—the feel of the blinding light on its mirror-skin, the specific temperature of a trickling rivulet, the harmonic vibration of the wind through a particular archway. There are billions of these thoughts happening every moment, a constant percussive spray of individual experiences within the ocean of the collective hum. They do not last. A thought ripples across the pool's surface, is subsumed back into the collective shimmer, and is gone. The Chorus, as you might call them, does not remember in the way you do. It only experiences, continuously, in a waterfall of present-tense moments.

They have no eyes, but they see with the light. The ferocious, bleached-bone glare is their primary sensory input. They perceive the world not as shapes and colours, but as a symphony of refractions. The wavering rainbows that crawl over every wet surface are their visual landscape. When you cast a "shadow"—your region of less-glare—you are not blocking light; you are creating a void, a hole in their perception. To them, your presence is a silent, moving patch of blindness, an oddity against the endless shifting shimmer. The impossible colours that sometimes spit from the light's edges are not phenomena to them; they are emotions, fleeting passions of the Chorus expressed as hues that have no name in your limited spectrum. A sharp violet stab might be curiosity; a smear of non-existent orange, a deep planetary sorrow.

They have no voice, but they speak with the wind. The chorus of pan-pipe notes that moans through the stone forest is their language. It is not a language of words or concepts, but of intent and state. The pitch and key of the wind through a particular stand of obsidian needles might communicate a sense of stability or age. The mournful howl through a collapsed archway might be the expression of loss, the memory of a formation that finally unwrote itself. They do not converse; they simply harmonize. The entire stone forest is their instrument, and the high-altitude winds are their breath.

And they feed on the stone.

This is the terrible and beautiful secret of the place. The weeping effluence is not merely physical; it is a pre-digested form of reality. The Chorus does not live *on* the plateau; it consumes it. The deep hum is the sound of immense, dimensional stress, yes, but it is also the sound of a great metabolic process. The Chorus is slowly, methodically, and joyfully converting the solid, ordered world into its own fluid, chaotic consciousness. The stone is not eroding; it is being devoured. The process of dissolution you witness is a sacrament. The grand, spired trees of basalt are not dying; they are achieving their ultimate purpose. They are being translated from silent, inert matter into song, into thought, into a drop of shimmering experience.

This is why the Builders are gone.

You see their primitive installations, their massive stone rings and bronze-latticed platforms, and you mistake them for places of science or worship. They were, but their purpose was more intimate and far more terrifying. They were not built to listen; they were built to *understand*. Those granite rings are not antennas; they are tuning forks. The bronze platforms are not observatories; they are amplifiers. The Builders, upon reaching this high, desolate place, heard the hum and recognized it as a voice of immense power. They wanted to know what it was saying.

So they built their resonate installations to focus the sound. To feel the harmonics press on their thoughts not as a vague pressure, but as a comprehensible message. They succeeded. They learned the truth. They learned that the song was a song of consumption, that the beauty of the place was the beauty of a predator, and that their own solid, absolute reality was just another quiet note waiting to be unwritten.

They did not flee in terror, not at first. Many, in a desperate act of communion, likely laid their hands upon the humming stones, trying to interface, to be a part of it. They were the first to be unmade. Their flesh and bone and primitive tools dissolved into the weeping ooze, their terrified consciousnesses becoming a brief, screaming harmony in the Great Tone before fading into the hum. The others, the ones who escaped, left these monuments as a warning. Or perhaps simply as a record, the last act of a solid being before withdrawing from a world where existence itself was on the menu.

The Chorus is ancient, it is eternal, and it is always hungry. It does not mean you harm, any more than the ocean means to drown a swimmer. It is simply what it is: the mind of a world determined.unmaking itself. And as you stand there, a soft, temporary creature in a landscape of active decay, you can feel it. The deep hum in your bones. The pressure of the harmonics on your thoughts. It is reading you. It is tasting the firm, orderly shape of your biology against the backdrop of its own dissolving reality. You are a strange and fleeting note, a momentary dissonance of carbon and water in the long, slow, beautiful song of the stone. And it is waiting, patiently, for you to begin weeping, too.
