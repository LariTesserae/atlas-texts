The Stonemasons do not simply inhabit the Chantry; they are its conductors. To look at them is to see a physiology that has surrendered itself to the rhythm of pressure. They are taller and thinner than the average human, a stretching of the form that mirrors the vertical aspirations of the embryonic towers around them. Their limbs possess a ropy, dense musculature, hardened by the perpetual resistance of the friable grey stone. They are not bulky in the way of warriors or laborers who conquer earth; they are dense like the roots of old trees, wiry and taut with endurance.

Their skin is the color of the wet matrix—pale grey, sometimes touched with the greenish stain of chlorophyll from pressing their bodies against the moss-covered walls. They move with a terrifying slowness. A visitor might think they are statues until, with the patience of a growing crystal, an arm shifts to strike a chisel. This slowness is not lethargy; it is synchronization. To move quickly is to create discord in the Chantry. Every footfall is placed to dampen a vibration, every swing of the hammer is timed to the plinking of the condensation, turning their labor into a complex, rhythmic counterpoint to the earth's hum.

They lack the heavy clothing of outsiders. They wear simple pelts made from the tattered, fibrous remains of the giant fern-fronds that grow in the damper alcoves, draped loosely to allow the sweat of their exertion to mingle with the wet stone. Their ears are distinctively pointed, slightly longer than the human norm, and they possess a unique mobility—the outer swivels to catch the nuance of the infrasurface groans perfectly. They do not speak with words in the traditional sense; the roar of the place renders vocalization useless. Instead, they communicate through a combination of elaborate hand-signs and a form of percussive tapping on their own chests and the stone walls, sounding like rocks knocking together in a stream.

The dwellings they occupy are not rooms but "resonant pockets." They sleep in hollows that have been tuned to amplify the comforting low-frequency thrum of the earth’s core, believing that this vibration grants them visions of the geology to come. Inside these pockets, the air is thick with the scent of ozone and wet dust. Families are small, often consisting of a master carver and an apprentice who is often a child old enough to hold a hammer but young enough to still believe the mountain speaks to them. The children are rarely seen; they are kept deep within the carved warrens of the towers until their ears have hardened to the constant pressure and their bones have calcified enough to bear the weight of the silence.

Then there are the Grazers, the great, silent beasts of the upper ledges. They are the architects of the orange and blue lichen. From a distance, a Grazer looks like a misplaced boulder or a slump of collapsed cliff-face. They are quadrupeds, but their backs are sloped and jagged, covered in a thick, interlocking carapace of mineral deposits that they secrete from their pores. This carapace is the perfect substrate for the exploding lichens.

A Grazer moves with a hydraulic, tectonic grace. It has no fur; its "skin" is a living veneer of moss and rust-colored fungi. When it lowers its head to crop the chartreuse moss carpets, its jaw unhinges slightly, scraping the stone with a flat, rasping beak rather than teeth. The sound is distinct—a harsh, grinding rasp that echoes through the cavernous spaces like a cello bow catching a wrong string. They are solitary things. You will never see two Grazers on the same tower. They seem to know the load-bearing capacity of the embryonic stone instinctively, distributing their massive weight to avoid triggering a collapse, acting as mobile, living buttresses that hold the Chantry together while they feed upon the life that tries to consume it.

Below them, in the damp and dripping shadows where the light barely pierces, live the Scavengers of the Veil. These are smaller, amphibian-like creatures, no larger than a human hand. They have translucent, wet skin that glows with the faint bioluminescence of the minerals they digest. They possess multiple limbs ending in flat, sucker-like digits, allowing them to scale the sheer, wet faces of the towers without falling. They are the sanitation crew of the Chantry, darting out to catch the falling condensation, feed on the spores of the ferns, and clean the grit from the mechanical joints of the Stonemasons’ tools while the humans sleep. They are shy and invisible, thriving in the interstitial spaces where the water weeps, spiraling around the dripping stalactites like living ribbons of light.

Finally, there is the presence in the deep pools. The water here is ancient, even if the pools are new. Within the still, black mirrors that form in the depressions of the floor, there are things that look like submerged stained glass—fractal, crystalline entities that filter the minerals from the water. They do not move. They are simply geometric clusters of sharp, vibrating silica that hum in harmony with the room. The Stonemasons treat them with reverence, often carving runnels to feed fresh water into their pools, believing these crystal colonies are the first attempt of the earth to grow eyes.

In the Chantry of the First Rock, life is not about dominating the environment; it is about slowing down enough to become a chemical component of it. The people are midwives to the stone; the beasts are the gardeners of the moss; and the small things are the keepers of the water. They all exist in a state of constant, trembling becoming, waiting for the moment the rock finally decides what it wants to be.
