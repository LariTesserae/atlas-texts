They are small, broad-bodied creatures, not in the sense of being stout or heavy, but in the way of low-rooted things—anchored and stable. Their stature is perfectly matched to the architecture of their world; standing upright, the tallest among them would barely brush the top of a meter-high doorway. Consequently, they do not stand often. Their natural posture is a crouched, rolling lope, a shift of weight from the balls of their feet to their knuckles, moving through the damp, fibrous corridors like a flowing eddy in the current of the air.

Their skin is a marvel of adaptation, textured to mirror the very walls they carve. It is thick and deeply rugose, a landscape of ridges and valleys colored in deep bruising purples, russet ochres, and the dark mottled green of shadowed moss. This rough surface is not dry; it glistens perpetually with a hydrophobic oil that beads the constant mist into shimmering droplets, keeping them warm despite the cool alpine chill. They smell of wet earth, crushed fern, and the faint, acrid tang of the shell-tools they wear at their belts.

Their faces are dominated by sensory organs adapted for the dim, amber twilight. They have large, dark eyes that lack the stark white sclera of a human; instead, the iris is so vast it swallows the eye, appearing like pools of liquid obsidian that catch the filtered red and yellow light of the canopy fronds. Below these, the nose is almost nonexistent, reduced to a pair of small slits, for the air here is too thick with moisture for heavy breathing. Instead, oxygen intake seems to be moderated by the loose, flaps of skin at their necks, which pulsate faintly with the resonant bass thrum of the ground.

The mouth is small, hidden beneath a heavy, expressive brow, but when opened, it reveals rows of tiny, needle-like teeth designed for shearing and scraping—not flesh, but fiber. They are grazers of the structure itself, supplementing their diet with the armored, many-legged harvesters and the gossamer-winged insects that blunder into the gloom. They move with a sonic awareness; their ears are tufted and mobile, swiveling independently to isolate the click of a beetle in the wall from the constant hiss of the water.

What is most striking about them is their integration with the architecture. They do not live *in* the hollows so much as they symbiose with them. Their hands are their primary instruments of evolution: dexterous, three-fingered things with opposable thumbs capable of immense pressure and gentle finesse. The fingertips are calloused into hardened pads, yet sensitive enough to detect the vibration of a deep-root tear before it becomes a structural failure.

You see an elder now, resting in a polished concavity near a weeping wall. The niche has been worn to a glass-like smoothness by the friction of thousands of bodies. The elder is scraping at the wall with a long, curved blade made from the iridescent shell of some colossal river-beast of the past. The movement is rhythmic—scrape, twist, lift. They are not fighting the plant; they are grooming it. The scraping removes the dead, grey outer layer of the substrate, stimulating the fresh, pale-green growth beneath. It is a negotiation: the creature creates a new alcovy for a birthing chamber, and the substrate receives the pruning it needs to grow inward and seal the old, decayed sections.

They communicate not through shouted words, which would be lost in the sonic riot, but through a complex language of touch, posture, and low-frequency hums. You see two younger ones working together on expanding a tunnel near the floor. They press their foreheads against the spongy wood-plants, feeling the thrum of the living mountain, and then produce a sub-vocal growl that vibrates through their chests and into the work. They are locating the "veins" of the water-mass to ensure they do not cut off the flow that feeds the room. Their clicks and chirrs are high and sharp, used only for close-range identification, like the birds they share the dark with.

Their clothing is minimal, constructed not from woven cloth but from the sheddings of their environment—capes of dried fronds loosely stitched with gut, harnesses for their tools made from the cured, leathery tubes of giant amphibian skins. To look at them is to see a creature perfectly evolved to be a cell within the lung of the mountain. They are serene in their busyness. They possess the patience of stone and the fluidity of water, understanding that their home is not a fortress to be defended, but a body to be cared for. They are the architects of the hollows, the listeners to the deep sighs, the carvers of the quiet dark.
