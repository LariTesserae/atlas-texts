They are not a civilization. They are a geology. The entity that lives here is a single, vast, and sensate existence, a slow tide of being that has, over eons, built the mountain it inhabits. To ask “who” lives here is to ask what is the soul of a reef, or the mind of a forest. The answer is: everything, all at once.

The foundational vibration you feel in your teeth is their voice, their thought, their heartbeat. It is the resonance of trillions upon trillions of bodies metabolizing in unison. It is a constant agreement, a unifying statement of *I Am We*. This is the Choral Body, and its song is the world itself.

The rustling, that ceaseless skittering like a world-sized leaf in the wind, is the prose that accompanies the song. It is the motion of the Crawlers. These are the myriapods you see, the great, centipede-like forms whose articulated bodies flow through the wider passages and up the walls like living rivers. They have no heads in the way we understand, no singular point of perception. Instead, each segment is a node of awareness, tasting the air through its antennae, feeling the hum of the structure through its countless legs. They are the circulatory system of the Choral Body. They do not carry blood, but information—chemical signals, spores, and fine particulates of shed chitin. They repair by traveling to a point of vibration that has soured, their bodies secreting the binding agents that fuse new shells into the fossil floor. They clear new passages not by will but by simple, persistent motion, the friction of a million generations a chisel that carves canyons from sheer being.

The slow, vast herds of grazers are the Gut. These are the broad, chitinous placid beasts whose mandibles are in a constant, gentle scraping motion. They are not eating the glowing fungi; they are tending to it. The luminous carpets and creeping lichen are an externalized organ of the Choral Body, its lung and its stomach, converting the chemical soup of the air into edible light. The grazers consume this light and matter, and in their wake, they leave trails of dark, rich womb-slime, a nutrient paste from which the fungal gardens will grow thicker and brighter. They are living factories, turning the scent of ammonia and musk into the soft, building light of the world. Their herds move in time to a rhythm so slow it is perceptible only over centuries, their migratory paths becoming grand, sweeping architectural features of the caverns.

Behold the Anchors. The pillbug-like titans curled into the alcoves are the oldest and most still. They are living bedrock. When a Crawler is born, it begins its life of perpetual motion. A grazer is born to its slow consumption. An Anchor is born to settle. It finds a place of structural stress, a point needing support, and it simply… stops. Its body begins to secrete a crystalline matrix that fuses its armored plates to the fossil floor and ceiling. It absorbs vibrations, dampening tremors. Its slow metabolism radiates a gentle, localized warmth that fosters the growth of the subtle, soft-bodied things in the grey ponds at its feet. Over millennia, its own body becomes the matrix for a new layer of petrified shells, its multifaceted eyes the glittering jewels in the ceiling above. To be an Anchor is to become a part of the mountain’s memory, and thus, its future.

There are no individuals here, only phases. The soft, pale things that grow in the still ponds are not a separate species, but the larval stage, the first bloom of life from the nutrient-rich secretions of the whole. They may become a Crawler, a Grazer, or an Anchor, their fate determined not by genetics, but by the needs of the structure around them, by the subtle variations in the frequency of the hum, by the chemical whispers in the air.

There is no technology, because their bodies are the technology. A chamber is not built; it is exhaled. A tunnel is not dug; it is digested. The light is not created; it is excreted. When a part of the structure fails—a cavern roof collapses, a wall erodes—it is not a disaster. It is a meal. The Choral Body flows into the rift, the Crawlers bringing material, the Grazers cultivating their light, the soft-bodied larvae colonizing the fresh wound. The very concept of waste is alien here. A death is a donation, a body is a brick, a breath is a building block.

To be among them is to be a single cell in a body that has forgotten it has cells. There is no fear, no malice, no ambition, because there is no "other." There is only the endless, humming task of being, the slow and sacred work of transforming the raw material of existence into a living monument to itself. They are a darkness that sings, a mountain that dreams, and they are, in their own context, perfection.
