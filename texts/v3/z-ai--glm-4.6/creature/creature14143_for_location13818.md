Here, in the City of Unmaking Rain, there are no citizens in the way that a biological census would recognize. If you were to stand on the slick black tongue of a roof and wait for a living thing to pass, you would Petrify into stone before you saw one. The inhabitants of this place are not distinct from the architecture; they are the architecture made animate, the slow will of the mountain given limbs.

They are the **Steward-Monoliths**.

At first, you would mistake them for the canyon walls themselves. They stand perfectly motionless, recessed into the vertical fissures between the massive blocks, their physiology mimicking the porous, lattice-like aggregate of the city stone. They are tall—impossibly tall—stretching fifty, sixty feet in height, yet so thin that from a distance they appear as nothing more than a shadow caught in the corner of two buildings. They are bipedal, possessing legs of dense, vitrified black mineral that taper down to blade-like edges, allowing them to slot into the narrowest V-gutters without disturbing the water’s flow.

Their bodies are devoid of heads. Where a skull would sit, there is only a smooth, convex slope of stone, polished to a mirror sheen by the constant condensation. They are sensorial in a way that makes no concession to eyes or ears. They "see" through vibration and pressure. They feel the stochastic percussion of the trillion dripping tears not as sound, but as a texture on their skin. They know the exact location of every blockage, every fracture in the masonry, and every fluctuation in the water’s purity, sensed through the soles of their feet and the porous lattice of their exoskeletons.

The builders may have been primitive in their tools, but they were sublime in their creations. The Stewards are the ultimate expression of "directed crystallogrowth," entities that grew rather than were assembled. Their shoulders are massive, jagged outcroppings of rock, reminiscent of the stone hammers used to build the city. Their arms are long, telescoping appendages that hang nearly to their ankles when at rest. These limbs are articulated not by muscle, but by hydraulics—the very water that the city weeps circulates through their internal channels, pressurizing to extend a limb, depressurizing to withdraw it.

They move with a terrifying silence. Because they possess no joints to creak and no lungs to heave, they slide. When a Steward decides to act—a decision that might take a century of consideration—it simply steps out of the wall. It glides over the wet stone, the water sheeting beneath its feet reducing friction to zero. If you were to touch it, it would feel as cold and unyielding as the grave, Thermodynamic equilibrium given hide.

There is no hierarchy among them, no society, no language of words. They operate on a singular, hive-logic shared through the stone itself. A clog forms in a collector channel three miles below; the vibration travels up the masonry, resonating through the city’s skeleton, and the nearest Steward detaches from its camouflage to correct it. It descends the vertical shafts using its bladelike feet as brakes, scraping the stone with a sound like a fingernail on a chalkboard amplified a thousand times—but so slowly that the sound is a low-frequency hum rather than a screech.

Their purpose is the maintenance of the Unmaking.

They are the farmers of the crystalline fur. You will see them standing waist-deep in a channel, their massive, hammer-hands moving with surprising delicacy. They do not prune the glass-like nets; they harvest them. They scrape the fragile, calcified structures from the stone, ingesting the powder into intake vents on their chests. They process the mineral deposits that the water leaves behind, preventing the city from choking on its own calcification. In return, the water flowing through them is purified, a symbiotic exchange where the Steward acts as a biological filter, ensuring the tears of the mountain remain unnervingly clear.

Occasionally, one witnesses a "settling." Occasionally, a massive block of the city drops by a fraction of a millimeter, causing that subsonic groan felt in the bones. This is often the work of the Stewards. Using their immense strength—derived from the hydraulic pressure of the entire city's water table—they are capable of levering a million-ton slab back into alignment. They are the living shims of the world, correcting the imperceptible sag of geology. To watch them work is to watch a mountain do pushups; it is slow, agonizing, and majestic.
