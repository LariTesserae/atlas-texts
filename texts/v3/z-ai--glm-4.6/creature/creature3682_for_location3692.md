They are a people carved from the same silence that governs the mountains. You do not see them so much as sense the interruption in the wind when they step from the shadow of a doorway. They are the Kholdi, and their existence is a long, slow exhalation against the backdrop of the eternal.

Physically, they are shaped by the unyielding nature of their world. They are broad-chested, their lungs evolved to process the thin, razor-cool air, their ribs seemingly too large for the contained space of their torsos. Their skin is the color of the wet moss—pale, bordering on translucency, with a faint, bluish undertone that speaks of slow blood and a lowered metabolic rate to survive the scarcity. They do not rush; rushing is a waste of heat, a biological affront to the conservation of energy. When a Kholdi walks, they place their feet with the deliberate precision of a mason setting a cornerstone, testing the ground not for instability, but for the reality that it will not shift beneath them.

Their faces are smooth, almost devoid of the sharp lines of expression found in lower altitudes. They do not frown in concentration or squint against the sun, because the light here is constant and the work does not vary. Their eyes are deep-set, dark pools that reflect the glassy striations of the rock, protected by heavy, ridged brows that seem to have evolved to shield against the vertical glare of the sun bouncing off the obsidian shards. To look into a Kholdi’s eyes is to look into a mine shaft; they carry the weight of the deep, unlit places of the earth.

They dress themselves in the permanence of the plateau. Their garments are not woven from fabrics that fray and decay, but are constructed from the cured, treated hides of the Skinslers. These leathers are tough, scarred, and nearly as enduring as the stone they walk upon. A tunic is not merely clothing; it is an heirloom that will be passed down, father to daughter, mother to son, for a thousand years. Because nothing rots, nothing smells of must. The Kholdi carry no scent of sweat or stale earth, only the clean, dry smell of cold leather and the sharp tang of the bronze clasps that fasten their garments. These clasps are artifacts of supreme importance, for metal is the only currency that matters in a city that never rebuilds.

Their society is a direct reflection of the Stillness. It is a culture of hyper-awareness regarding *position*. Because a dropped pot remains a dropped pot forever, lying in the street exactly where it fell a millennium ago, the Kholdi have developed an agonizing precision. They live in a crowded museum of their own history. To walk through the streets of Khold is to navigate a maze of ancient accidents—a rusted chisel left on a ledge three thousand years ago, a child’s toy resting in a gutter, a circle of ash from a long-extinguished fire. They do not clean these things away. To clean would be to erase time, and time is the only thing they possess. They step over these artifacts with the reverence of a pilgrim, their paths dictated by the debris of ancestors.

Their hands are their primary instruments of interaction with the unyielding world. The tools they use—bronze adzes, stone hammers, chisels of a metal so hard it rings like a bell—are never replaced. A Kholdi artisan does not "own" a chisel; they are merely its custodian. The handle of a hammer may be replaced, worn by the grip of thirty generations of hands, but the head remains, eternally sharp. Their arms are corded with muscle, not from lifting heavy loads, but from the repetitive, unchanging motion of carving stone that never dulls their blades. They are sculptors of the immutable, etching tiny details into a landscape that ignores them.

Socially, they are communal but detached. They do not gather in crowds to celebrate, for celebration implies a change in status. They gather to witness. The primary ritual of the Kholdi is the "Observing." At dusk, when the air begins to sweat and the condensation weeps down the glass faces of the mountains, they stand in the open courtyards, motionless, and watch the water trace the same paths it has traced since the beginning of time. It is a meditation on futility and endurance. They speak little, their language consisting of subtle hums and clicks that do not carry far in the thin air, preserving the quiet that the Stillness demands.

Their relationship with death is perhaps their most striking adaptation. They do not fear the end of life, for they see the cycle in the Skinslers and the Gleaners. When a Kholdi dies, they do not bury them in the earth, for the earth is too hard and too valuable to disturb. Instead, they practice "The Setting." The deceased are dressed in their ceremonial leathers, adorned with the family bronze, and carried to the high terraces overlooking the bone mosaics of the valley. There, they are arranged in seated positions, facing the rising sun, locked in a final, permanent posture. They are left to the cool air. They do not rot; they merely desiccate, becoming leather and bone statues that watch over the city. A grandmother, dead for five centuries, still sits on the balcony where she knitted, a permanent fixture of the household, her presence as solid and unmoving as the furniture.

In this way, the Kholdi are never truly gone. They accumulate. They overlap. A child in Khold grows up in a house crowded with the mummified remains of their great-grandparents, sitting at a table to eat next to an ancestor who died a thousand years ago. They are a people who live in the present only because they cannot escape the perfection of the past. They are the stewards of a frozen instant, forever holding their breath in a world that will never exhale.
