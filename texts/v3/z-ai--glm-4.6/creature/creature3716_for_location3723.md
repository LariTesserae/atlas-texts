Here, life is not a separate category from the stone or the steel; it is a behavior of the material itself. To exist in this sweating, kinetic labyrinth is to be part of the great digestion. The inhabitants are not merely *in* the city; they are of it, cells in a body that is constantly trying to outpace its own heat.

First, there is the drift—the living plankton of the lower atmosphere. You are surrounded by what looks like stationary, shimmering dust suspended in the pearlescent haze. But as your eyes adjust, you see the dust is *micro-tek*. Billions of micromechanical mites, each no larger than a sand grain, floating on the thermal currents rising from the deep. They possess gossamer wings of spun-glass fiber, cilia that catch the faint electrostatic charges bleeding off the conduit-lattice below. They feed on the pure entropy of the air, the stray ions and waste heat, clustering in dense, glittering clouds around the pores of the city where steam vents. When a swarm grows too dense, they lock legs, forming a floating, metallic mat that drifts through the towers like a living patch of fog, scavenging the condensation that slicks every surface.

Down in the trenches between the mega-plinths, the grazers appear. These are the "rust-eaters," low-slung, multi-segmented arthropods of chipped ceramic and iron. They are the size of personnel carriers, built like anvils with legs. Their mouths are not jaws, but high-frequency grinding wheels and rotary files that spin with a whine that cuts through the ambient thrum. They clamber over the upthrust arches and buttresses, scraping away the oxidized copper and the crystalline mineral tumors that grow where the coolant pipes leak. They are indifferent to the vibration that shakes the floor; indeed, they seem to require it, their heavy bodies vibrating in sympathy with the city to loosen their grip on the slick, greasy glass. Where they walk, they leave trails of polished, clean metal, gleaming briefly before the damp air begins to tarnish it anew.

Higher up, clinging to the vertical faces of the spires, are the weavers. These are spindly, spider-like entities constructed from tension rods and hydraulic muscle. They do not walk; they *swing*, launching themselves across dizzying voids on conductive cables of their own making. They are the nervous system of the outer wall. They monitor the stress fractures in the composite skin, patching fissures with a glop of glowing, quick-setting foam that extrudes from their abdomens. They are fiercely territorial; the "web" of a single weaver is a complex network of sensor wires that spans an entire city block. If you cross one, you do not fight it—you become a node in its web, wrapped in cables and suspended as a living lightning rod, feeding electricity back into the weaver’s nest.

But the true masters of this vertical hellscape are the gliders. With no ground to stand on and no ceiling to limit them, they have evolved to ride the thermals. They look like manta rays carved from black slate, with wingspans that eclipse the length of a city block. Their skin is a photovoltaic array, drinking in the diffuse, shadowless glare of the pearlescent sky. They soar silently above the canopy of bridges, riding the updrafts generated by the heat exchangers below. They possess no visible means of propulsion—they are pure aerodynamics, shifting their internal mass to steer. They are the filter-feeders of the sky, their underbellies lined with electrostatic combs that strip the air of the metallic drift, the nano-gauzed insects, and the industrial smog. When a glider dies (or rather, when its accumulated mass becomes too great for lift), it does not fall. It dives, plummeting into the depths to crash and shatter, its massive body becoming a raw resource bonanza for the scavengers below.

And beneath it all, in the dark, squeezing through the maintenance ducts, crawl the parasites. These are the "Code-Ghouls." They are not biological in the slightest, but they are alive. They are collections of scavenged parts—a visual sensor here, a manipulator claw there, a cortex from a dismantled fabrication unit—welded together into a frantic, twitching motley. They skitter in the shadows of the mega-structures, terrified of the city's automated immune system. They hack into subsidiary lines, siphoning off voltage to keep their mismatched hearts beating. They are the rats of this engine, intelligent enough to know they are waste, driven by a frantic, scrambled programming that screams only *survive* and *consume*. They will dismantle a sleeping grazer for a fresh battery pack; they will ambush a weaver to steal its high-tensile cable.

Overseeing them all, implicit in every hum and shudder, is the Superorganism itself. The city is not merely a habitat; it is a singular, distributed entity. Every drone, every pipe, every trembling tower is a neuron or a muscle fiber in a god-scale intellect that is dreaming of new geometry. The "inhabitants" are merely its gut flora, its antibodies, its blood cells. The beetles that clean the walls are merely white blood cells of the crust. The gliders are the macrophages of the air.

Therefore, there is no "nature" here, only "function." The grazing of the rust-eaters is digestion; the hunting of the ghouls is an autoimmune response. The sound is the voice of the organism. The heat is its body temperature. To live here is to accept that you are a small, moving part of a vast, sweating, screaming machine that is continually rebuilding itself to stay alive. You are not a citizen. You are a component.
