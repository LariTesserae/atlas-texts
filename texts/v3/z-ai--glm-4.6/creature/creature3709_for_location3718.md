The residents of the Sunken Brick-Hollow are not guests; they are the occupiers. They are the final, meaning-making layer smeared over the geometry of the forgotten. To live here is to understand the world not as a floor and a sky, but as a single, continuous surface—a wall that is also a ceiling, a floor that is also a pantry, a labyrinth where every direction is either up or down.

The dominant presence, the very lifeblood of the place, is the Slate-Wing colony. They are a single, fractal organism spread across the vast, cylindrical inner skin of the oubliette. To call them "bats" or "lemurs" is a crude approximation of forms long-shed. A Slate-Wing is a creature of pure verticality. Its body is a dense, wiry knot of muscle, sheathed in a pelt of coarse, grey fur that is perpetually damp and matted with mineral dust, giving it the texture and color of its namesake stone. Its limbs are long and triple-jointed, ending in black, hooked claws that can find purchase in the softest mortar or the slickness of weeping brick. Its leathery wings are not for soaring but for explosive, downward-gliding assaults and panicked, upward sprints. Their faces are masks of baleful intelligence: wide, nocturnal eyes that reflect the dim light with a feral orange glow, and a perpetually twitching nose that samples the thick air for the scent of food, rival, or predator.

They are a society defined by altitude. The highest reaches, where the walls are driest and the roar from the floor is a distant hum, are the penthouses. Here, the largest and most dominant Slate-Wings hold sway. Their nests are not messy constructions but careful excavations, hollows worn deep into the brickwork, lined with the fur of their sheddings and the desiccated remains of lesser prey. The mid-levels are a chaotic, jostling tenement. Here, the younger pairs and the established but not dominant clans cram their nests into every available aperture—the hollows of defunct winches, the casings of rotted pulley wheels, the space where an iron bracket has rusted away. They are the working class of the hollow, their lives a constant commute, launching from their perches to dive upon the floor below before scrabbling back up the worn spirals of the stairs, the stone in the center of each tread scooped into a slippery basin by the uncounted passage of their clawed feet.

It is from these mid-levels that the language of the hollow truly emanates. The tearing-metal screech is not just a call; it is a sentence. A short, sharp shriek is a, "Mine!"—a claim to a freshly dropped piece of fruit or a choice nesting crack. A long, resonant cry, vibrating through the stone, is a territorial broadcast, a warning to a neighboring clan. The frantic, high-pitched chittering that overlays everything is the endless gossip of the colony, the argument and negotiation of thousands of compressed lives all happening at once.

Below, on the floor, are those who serve and are served. The immense Carrion Beetles are the silent, armored janitors. Their carapaces, the size of serving platters, are polished obsidian, inlaid with the iridescent green of oxidized copper. They move with the slow, inexorable purpose of geological forces, their multipart legs digging into the grime. They do not hunt; they process. Their massive mandibles crunch through the constant rainfall of Slate-Wing guano, molted skin, and the unfortunate dead, turning the floor into a rich, black loam. Their hissing and clicking is not aggression but the sound of bureaucracy, the endless claiming and re-claiming of recycling rights to a particular patch of filth. They are the foundation, and without them, the hollow would drown in its own waste.

Darting through the forest of beetle legs are the skitters. They are the nervous system of the floor. Soft-bodied, centipede-like things with a thousand delicate, twitching legs, they are too fast and too numerous for the beetles to bother with. They feed on the microbial slicks that coat the stones, on the microscopic spores of fungi, and on the minutiae of decay the giants ignore. Their passage is a constant, scuttling whisper, a background radiation of life. They are the first to sense the coming shadow, the first to erupt in a wave of pure panic when the balance is broken.

The predator—be it the sinuous, reptilian Diver or the solitary, feral cat-thing known only as the Stalker—is the tax collector of this society. It is the ultimate consequence of gravity. It does not live *in* the hollow so much as it *visits*, dropping from unseen perches or descending silently down the ancient ropes. Its presence is the single, unifying fear that structures the entire ecosystem. The periodic raids are not hunts; they are cullings, a sudden, violent redistribution of flesh. The explosion of the Slate-Wing swarm is a defensive vortex, a cloud of confusing bodies and deafening noise meant to disorient the attacker. The carpet of beetles and skitters parts, leaving behind the slowest and the sick. When the Stalker has taken its due and vanished back up the walls, the society settles. The noise subsides from a shriek to a roar to a hum, the mosaic of chitin and fur knits itself back together, and the slow, patient rhythm of weeping, dripping, and scavenging resumes.

They have built a civilization here, not of stone and metal, but of instinct and survival. The bricks are the bones of their world, the weeping mortar its veins, and the deafening chorus of their existence is the sound of a heart beating in the dark, a heart that has long forgotten the world outside its circular, vertical walls. To be among them is to be a piece of inert debris, a silent anomaly in a place that has been filled, utterly and completely, by life.
