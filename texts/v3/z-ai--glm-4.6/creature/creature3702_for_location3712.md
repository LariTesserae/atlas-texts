To ask what lives here is to misunderstand the nature of the place, for the distinction between "habitat" and "inhabitant" has dissolved. There is no environment populated by creatures; there is only a single, vast, stratified creature, and you are walking inside its body. The "living things" of this hollow world are not species competing for survival, but specialized cells of a planetary consciousness, differentiated by function rather than form.

**The Drifters**

High above the spongy floor, in the humid vaults where the root-mass twists like geological strata, drift the thoughts of the world. These are the translucent, manta-like entities, vast and silent gliders that navigate the air as if it were water. They possess no mouths, no digestive tracts, and no apparent means of propulsion; they move by the sheer force of intention, riding the slow, convection currents of the heat rising from the depths.

Up close, they are less like animals and more like living lenses. Their bodies are membranes of pure, wet light, stretching twenty feet or more from tip to tip. Trailing behind them are long, filamentous tendrils—neural extensions that snag on the hanging vines and brush against the glowing ceiling. They are collectors of information. As they drift, they absorb the chemical signals of the roots, the subtle vibrations of the growing walls, and the moisture content of the air. They do not eat; they process reality itself, turning the raw data of the cavern into the complex, dreaming consciousness that dictates the patterns of growth. When a Drifter dies—which happens rarely, and without struggle—it does not rot. It simply dissipates, a heavy rain of luminous mist that saturates the moss below, fertilizing the next generation of thought with the nutrients of the old.

**The Stewards**

Down in the dense, thicketed lower levels, where the light is gold and the air is thick enough to taste, live the Stewards. If the Drifters are the mind, the Stewards are the immune system and the hands combined. To the unobservant eye, a Steward is merely a bizarre hillock or a tangle of fallen logs. But if you watch the fibrous substrate for long enough, the hillock uncoils.

They are constructed of the same material as the world itself: a chitinous, ceramic bark resembling jade, interwoven with muscle-fibers of dense moss. They move with a geological slowness. A single step might take an hour, a shudder of mass and displacement of air. They have no distinct heads, only clusters of sensory nodules that pulse with the same internal light as the stars above.

The Stewards are the architects of decay and renewal. They are the ones that, with infinite patience, dismantle the "built things" that have outlived their purpose—a bone-white dome here, a calcified spire there. They grip the structures with powerful, limb-like roots and simply... absorb them. They break down the crystallized cellulose and return it to the slurry from which all things grow. They are the grave-diggers and the midwives in one, ensuring that the "eternal emergence" remains a perfect equilibrium. They ignore you, these massive, multi-limbed things. You are too temporary, too irrelevant to the grand cycle to warrant their attention.

**The Weepers**

Smallest, and perhaps most numerous, are the Weepers. They are the mechanics of the water cycle. They are spindly, spider-like creatures that live entirely within the canopy of broad, shield-sails and fungal brackets. Their bodies are glass-like and fragile, designed to reflect the hot white light of the world.

The Weepers do not drink; they transport. They scuttle along the undersides of the massive leaves, their legs making no sound on the waxy surfaces. They gather the perpetual beads of condensation, the "sweat" of the planet, that form on every surface. Using Capillary tubes extending from their abdomens, they siphon the water and transport it to the drier, higher reaches of the hanging root-systems, deliberately wetting the filters and air-intakes of the world-flesh.

They are obsessive workers. If you watch a single patch of moss, you will see a dozen of them, appearing and vanishing into the dense shadows of the vegetation, a frantic, silent industry that maintains the humidity required for the great cellular creaking to continue. They are the white blood cells of the climate, ensuring that the dampness—the breath of the god—never falters.

**The Great Presence**

But ultimately, all these things—the thinking Drifters, the heavy Stewards, the frantic Weepers—are merely organelles. The primary entity that lives here is the space itself. The consciousness of the place is not localized in a brain; it is distributed through the tension in the vines, the bioluminescence of the moss, the hardness of the bone-white walls, and the rhythm of the dripping water.

It lives in the "emergence." When the ground pushes up a new spire of calcified lymph, the entity is asserting its will. When the heat thickens the air, the entity is breathing. It is a god that does not rule its subjects, but *is* them. It is the loneliest form of life possible, for it contains multitudes yet experiences no otherness. It dreams of itself, builds itself, eats itself, and rebuilds itself, forever trapped in a perfect, luminous, and unchanging moment of becoming. And you, the temporary guest, are nothing more than a fleeting spore, passing through its lungs—a mote of dust suspended in the light of its endless, single day.
