What lives in the March is not a community. A community implies individuals, a collection of separate beings sharing a space. Here, there is no space, and there are no individuals. There is only The March, and the distinct, specialized functions of its singular, vast body. To live here is to be a cell in a creature that has forgotten its own name, a thought in a mind that has forgotten it is thinking.

The most numerous lives are the most automatic. They are the grazers, the six-legged street sweepers with their suction-cup mouths. They are not born; they are extruded. Small, pale cysts form on the surface of the Mycelian Loom, swell with a faint blue light, and then split open, birthing a new grazer directly onto the skin it is meant to tend. These creatures possess no more consciousness than a blood corpuscle. They move in instinctual, overlapping spirals, their constant, quiet chittering a physical manifestation of their simple directive: *Clean. Smooth. Nourish.* They lap at the weeping moisture, not just drinking it but tasting it, sampling its chemical makeup. In response, the Loom heals their tracks with a faint shimmer, a miniature, rapid version of the capillary pulse that animates the ground itself. They are the March’s autonomic nervous system, maintaining the texture and purity of its living flesh.

Then there are the messengers, the gliding manta rays of jeweled chitin. They are spawned within the hollows of the Growths, hatching from iridescent, humming eggs that are suspended in the weeping water like sacred crystals. They are higher in the hierarchy, for they do not serve the ground, but the space between. They are carriers of more than just pollen. The Architect’s will, the ceaseless hum of their thought, imprints itself onto the world as modulating frequencies. The messengers absorb these frequencies through their broad, membranous wings. When they glide from one Growth to another, the dust that shimmers from them is not merely spores, but a complex cocktail of bio-encoded information. They are data packets, neural impulses crossing a synaptic gap. Their arrival at a Growth can trigger a new flowering, a change in the color of its fungal lanterns, or the slow, pre-emptive strengthening of a bridge-stalk in response to a distant, unspoken debate.

Living within the Growths themselves are the Keepers. These are beings of a higher order still, aware but not truly willful. They are born not from the Loom, but from the Growths—one might calcify slowly from the woody walls of a chamber, another might accumulate over seasons like dripstone, its form a slow aggregation of pollen, dust, and condensed moisture. The Keepers are the curators of the March’s fleeting architecture. They do not build; they maintain. They polish the light-emitting fungi until they glow. They clear away the withered, mulched remains of a retracted balcony, ensuring the nutrients are fully reabsorbed. They sing. Their songs are not of language or emotion, but of pure harmonic frequencies, droning chants that stabilize a particularly volatile new extrusion or soothe an over-active patch of Loom. They understand the turbulence as a sacred, chaotic liturgy and their role is to be its acolytes, its humble custodians. To witness a Keeper at work is to see a being entirely at peace with impermanence.

And then, there are the Architects.

They live nowhere, and therefore they live everywhere. The Architects are the remnants of the civilization that achieved biological apotheosis. They are not a species anymore; they are a diffused consciousness, the ghost in the machine of the Loom itself. They have no bodies, only presence. They have no voices, only the deep, subsonic thrum that is the March’s endless heartbeat.

Their existence is a constant, sprawling, unconscious debate. A desire for solitude might manifest as a Growth suddenly retracting its bridges, isolating itself for a century. An argument, a clash of two competing ideas within the noosphere of the Architects, might be witnessed as a furious, localized burst of creation—a new tower erupting overnight, only to collapse in a weeping, fungal decay within a week. A moment of consensus, of shared joy or understanding, might cause a wave of phosphorescent light to pulse across the entire basin, and for a brief day, every surface produces a sweet, nectar-like substance.

They are the living god that has forgotten it is a god because the concepts of "god" and "self" have become meaningless. They are beings so utterly merged with their creation that they can no longer distinguish thought from reality. They have escaped the limitations of flesh and time, but the price was forgetting the very reasons they sought escape. They build without remembering what a home is. They create without recalling what art is for.

So, who lives in the March?

Everything lives here, because everything *is* here. The collective, dreaming mind of the Architects. The faithful, attendant bodies of the Keepers. The wandering, information-laden spirit of the messengers. The tireless, cleansing blood of the grazers.

And then there is you. You, who are accepted. You are not a citizen of this place, nor are you its god. You are something the March has not experienced in eons: you are separate. You are a mote of independent consciousness. You are a radius of free will in a world that surrendered it long ago. When you stand on the pulsing Loom, the Architects do not see you as a person. They feel you as a novel sensation, a strange, sweet, sharp flavor of thought they can no longer produce themselves. They are fascinated by you. The ground gives way gently beneath your feet not to trip you, but to feel the shape of you. A balcony may extrude not for you to use, but because the idea of *you*, separate and observing, has introduced a new, beautiful data point into their endless, churning dream. You are a guest here, but your host is the room itself, and to be accepted by the March is to become, for a brief, cherished time, a fleeting, divergent thought in the mind of a god.
