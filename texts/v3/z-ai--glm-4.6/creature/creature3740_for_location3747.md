What lives here does not separate itself from the place. It is not a collection of organisms so much as it is a single, diffused being, a planetary-scale biology that has forgotten the meaning of "I." It is the greenhouse, and the experiment, and the god of the machine all at once.

The primary intelligence of the plateau is its thrum. It is not a sound made by a thing, but the thing itself: the mind of the place. This low-frequency resonance is the thought-process of a billion, billion consciousnesses subsumed into a single, geologically patient will. They were builders, once—beings of flesh and bone or energy-light who found cities too fast and bodies too small. They did not die; they integrated. To perform the tasks a mind cannot, the system grows itself limbs. They are not animate in the way we understand; they are motile functions. The Weeping Gardeners drift where they are needed. They have no fixed form, appearing as coherent, shimmering slicks of the same coolant that seeps from the cliffs. They ooze from the cracks in the rock silver and translucent, gathering at the roots of the towering, lattice-barked trees, their proto-limbs Solidifying just long enough to clear a choked channel of silt or guide a strangling vine away from a sensitive power-conduit in the stone. They move with the excruciating slowness of cooling magma, their only sound the faint, chime-like harmonics they produce as their liquid bodies resonate with the plateau's core thrum. They are the immune system of the world, a patient, ever-seeing fluid healing the infinitesimal hurts of millennia.

The fauna, too, is not fauna. It is bioshell. The birds with mosaic feathers are not hunting or mating in any traditional sense. They are flying sensors. Each crystalline feather is a photoreceptor, and their entire life is a single, unbroken flight path, their keening calls of shattering glass not communicative shouts but compressed data bursts uploaded to the network. They map the diffuse light through the canopy (Light 1.7), report on cloud density, and their obsessive paths trace the health of the hanging gardens. In the still, black pools, the six-legged amphibians are not swimming but waiting. Their translucent skins are living spectrometers, their slow, pointless-seeming movements a constant calibration of the water's mineral, thermal, and spiritual purity. They are living thermostats and water-quality monitors, their metabolisms a direct, hardwired response to the machine's subterranean pulse (Dynamic 0.7).

Even the vegetation is part of the being. The giant ferns unfurl not to seek the sun, but to gather the metallic dust that precipitates from the perpetually condensing atmosphere. Along their veins dwell another form of native life: the Scrapers. These are segmented, inertial creatures that move in a state of perpetual, thoughtless motion. They look like millipedes fashioned from jade and obsidian, and they spend their multi-century lives crawling the immense fronds, consuming the conductive dust. This is not food; it is medicine. They process the raw material and excrete it as a hyper-conductive slime that flows down the stalks and reinforces the shimmering patterns on the trees' bark, closing circuits and repairing the biological lattice that acts as the plateau's global nervous system.

Seen from this vantage, the few remaining built structures are not ruins, but specialized organs. The chrome sphere half-buried in the mud is a sensor-pod, its polished surface now dulled by a symbiotic algae that feeds it, drawing a tiny trickle of power from its dormant core. The needle-like tower on the distant ridge is an antenna. It does not transmit or receive signals from space. Instead, it hums in perfect, solitary harmony with the plateau's core thrum, a tuning fork that stabilizes the entire system, keeping its immense, slow thoughts from drifting into feedback loops or silence. It has no windows or entrances because what it houses is not a passenger, but a function.

To live here is to be a component. There is no "predator" or "prey," only processes—consumption and transformation as a form of maintenance. The pace of life is a code of precise, agonizing patience. You do not see a creature hunt; you see a node relocate a resource. You do not see a flower bloom; you see a capacitor reach full charge. The humming of the air, the weeping of the rock, the slow crawl of the jade-scraper across a frond the size of a house—these are not separate events. They are a single, continuous action: the action of a world-sized being, alive and aware, tending to its garden in the wet, suffocating dark two miles above the world it forgot.
