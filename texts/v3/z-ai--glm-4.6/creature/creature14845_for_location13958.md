The things that live here do not hear the Great Engine; they *are* the Great Engine. They exist in a state of perpetual synchronization, their heartbeats locked to the deep-vibrating drone of the piston, their metabolisms spurred by the waste heat of the city-block machine.

At the very bottom, in the microscopic crevices of the shale, are the **Oxidizers**. They are not truly insects, though they possess the segmented plating of beetles. They are crustaceans of the deep dark, evolved from freshwater scavengers that found their way into the sumps and vents millennia ago. They are colored a deep, bruised purple, nearly black, which allows them to blend into the rust shadows. Their pincers are oversized, not for combat, but for shearing. They feed on the metal itself. They rasp away at the slow-flaking iron of the conveyor belts, consuming the oxide as a source of minerals. You hear them before you see them—their clicking mandibles create a subtle, high-pitched chatter that weaves through the bass thrum of the machinery. When the Great Engine bucks—a shudder that travels through the floor—the Oixidizers freeze, instinctually flattening themselves against the cold steel, mimicking the stillness of the rock until the world stabilizes.

Clinging to the vertical faces of the coolant towers are the **Damp-Creepers**. These are the amphibian-like creatures alluded to in the faint, sweet smell of the air. They are gelatinous things, loosely resembling frogs or salamanders, but possessing six limbs that end in sucker-like pads. Their skin is translucent and wet, glowing with a faint bacterial patina that allows them to navigate the darker zones between the arc lamps. They are the silent assassins of the vertical spaces. They do not hunt by sight; they hunt by pressure. The sensitive pads of their feet can feel the vibration of a beetle walking on a pipe three meters away. They move in lurches, timing their lunges to coincide with the subsonic groan of the piston, masking their attack in the noise of the world. Their mouths are circular rings of teeth, designed to bore into the soft shells of the isopods and suck out the moisture inside.

Between the islands of light swarms the **Vapor-Moths**. They are beautiful and tragic, their wings spun from a silk as fine as glass dust. They are blind, their eyes devolved into pits, but their bodies are covered in sensory hairs that taste the humidity of the air. They live short, frantic lives fueled by the sugar-rich condensation that weeps from the pressurized pipes. They gather in great, spiraling columns around the failing arc lamps, drawn not by the light, but by the heat. To a Vapor-Moth, the hot glass of the lamp is a mate, a food source, and a sun all at once. They batter themselves against the bulbs in vast, rhythmic waves, the *thwack-thwack-thwack* of thousands of bodies impacting glass adding a staccato percussion to the Engine's drone. Those that do not die from the impact fall to the grate below, piling up in drifts that the Oxidizers eagerly scavenge.

Deeper in the shadows, where the rock is still jagged and the footing is treacherous, live the **Shale-Striders**. These are the titans of the Basin, creatures as large as draft horses, yet spindly and spider-like. They have evolved specifically to traverse the sharp, unstable ground that would slice a human boot to ribbons. Their legs are long, multi-jointed pillars of chitin, allowing them to stride over the crevasses and balance on the tips of the basalt shards. They are slow-moving grazers that use long, proboscis-like snouts to suck the mineral-rich slime from the rock faces. They act as the living umbilicals of the ecosystem, their massive bodies serving as mobile fortresses. Colonies of smaller, mite-like parasites live in the furrows of their shells grooming them, while Oxidizers follow in their wake to clean the waste they leave behind. The Striders communicate through aseries of low-frequency rumbles produced in their hollow abdomens, vibrations that travel through the rock so clearly they can "speak" to each other across the chasm without ever opening their mouths.

And finally, there is the **Weeping Mold**. It is not an animal, but it lives. It covers the abandoned barracks and the shattered windows of the control rooms, a thick, grey-blue wool that pulses with a slow, rhythmic luminescence. It is the physical manifestation of the "sweet, organic" smell. It digests the organic compounds left behind by the ancient workers—the leather of the chairs, the paper of the logs, the oilskin of the coats—and metabolizes it into a sugary excretion that feeds the insects. When a shadow moves across it, the mold flashes with bioluminescent panic, ripples of blue light racing across the wall to warn others in the gloom that something large is moving.

This is the population of the Sunken Basin. They are a civilization of rust-eaters and shadow-dwellers, packed shoulder to exoskeleton-shoulder in the warm, wet dark. They do not know the silence of the surface world. They only know the thrum. They are the mechanics of decay, the custodians of a machine that is slowly dying, ensuring that when the Great Engine finally takes its last breath and the lights flicker out for good, they will still be there in the dark, chewing on the iron, waiting for the next vibration.
