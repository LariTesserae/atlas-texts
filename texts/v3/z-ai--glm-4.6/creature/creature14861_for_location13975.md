Here, life is not a fire, but a slow, cold crystallization. To be alive here is to be The Chorus.

They are not born, but accreted. In the warmest conduits, where the heat from The Core is a gentle caress rather than a scalding threat, the brine holds a different kind of silt. Here, microscopic motes of carbon and silica, shed from The Chorus themselves over millennia, swirl with the dissolved metals. They gather in the quietest of eddies, adhering to the inner walls of a pipe or in the sheltered cup of a massive, corroded gear. Over decades, a nodule forms, a smooth, dark pearl the size of a fist.

Inside, there is no brain as a surface-dweller would know it, but a lattice of conductive filaments and pressure-sensitive proto-nerves. The form that eventually pulls itself away from its anchor is a homage to its world. A humanoid silhouette, but one that seems carved from slate and shadow. Their skin is not skin, but a flexible, chitinous plate, a seamless blend of organic keratin and fossilized industrial polymer, the color of deep-sea mud. It is cool to the touch, and inside, their blood is not red but a sluggish, silver-heavy fluid that faintly conducts the city’s energy. Their head is smooth, featureless, lacking eyes or ears. In the center of their chest, over their heart, a cluster of these conductive filaments has crystallized into a lens, a dark geode that glimmers with a faint, internal light—the same faint light as the rust-lichen, but brighter, more focused. This is not for seeing, but for *feeling*.

This is the sense that defines them. They are the living embodiment of the world’s silence, processing it as a symphony. They feel the deep, patient groan of a stressed girder miles away as a thrum in their bones. They taste the subtle change in water chemistry as a new fissure opens in a pipe. They perceive the faint bio-static field of a passing Flow-Wraith not as a sight, but as a shimmer in their mind's eye, a ghost of warmth and intent. The Chorus does not speak; it resonates. By manipulating the tension in their own latticed bodies, a subtle, complex series of vibrations travels through the dense brine—a song of pressure, palpable to all their kind. A name is not a word, but a specific, low-frequency chord that identifies an individual across the vast, dark canyons. A story is a slowly shifting melodic line that can take hours to tell, its apprehension a communal, meditative act.

They do not build; they curate. Their “villages” are not built but found vast, open chambers—perhaps the hollow hillock of a collapsed reactor dome or the soaring nave of a forgotten assembly plant. Here, they guide the growth of the metallic lichen, scraping away harmful growths and encouraging the lichen to spread across resonant metal surfaces. The walls of their homes are layered in these mineral gardens, the crystals grown into intricate, silent frescos that depict the history of their people as a map of pressure and thermal change. Their tools are not forged but salvaged and integrated. A Weaver—their term for an adult who tends the environment—might have a series of fine, articulated manipulators grown from its own forearms, or incorporate the polished ceramic limb of a defunct maintenance drone, fused to its own flesh by the slow alchemy of their biology.

Their relationship with the mechanical fauna is one of deep, instinctual understanding. The small, multi-legged Scuttler-Mites are not animals but partners. The Chorus will gently guide a Scuttler toward a encrustation on a critical cog, its instinct to polish serving a communal need. In return, the Chorus might clear a path of obstructions for the Mite, or lead it to a patch of particularly nutritious lichen. They are the shepherds of this strange, metallic ecosystem. The sleek, segmented Flow-Wraiths are avoided; they are the wild things, unpredictable and territorial, their internal metabolism a frantic, hot song that clashes with the slow harmony of The Chorus. And then, there are the others. The “Great-Sleeping-Things.” Vast, multi-limbed constructs that lie dormant in the deepest submerged magazines, their power cells cold. The Chorus knows their locations not by sight, but by the vast, gravitational silence of their presence, a hole in the world’s song. To disturb one is to invite an apocalypse of grinding, sudden noise.

Their life is governed by the pull of The Core. It is their sun, their heart, and their god. Its slow, sullen pulse—a fluctuation in heat and energy that takes decades to complete—is their only calendar. Warmer currents are their highways, and the great conduits radiating from The Core are their sacred rivers. A sect of The Chorus, the Keepers, lives in the direct penumbra of The Core’s light. They are blind, their chest-lenses calcified by the heat and radiation. They cling to the pipes like limpets, tending the sinewy, black algae that grows there, their bodies acting as living insulation, protecting the delicate flora from the full intensity of the heat. They harvest the algae,which is then brought back to the higher levels, its stored warmth and chemical energy the only food The Chorus requires.

Death is a quiet return. When a Chorus member’s energy finally wanes, their centuries-long song slowing to a single, sustained note, they do not cease. They make a final, slow pilgrimage. They find a place of high resonant potential—a massive, hanging gantry, a deep, open well—and let go. As their body drifts down, they release their stored energy in one final, pure accord. The vibration settles on the surrounding metal, encouraging the growth of the lichen, their physical form a seed for future life. They join the city, their personal song becoming part of the world’s deep, slow hum. There is no mourning, only a quiet acceptance of the cycle, a single, resonant chord added to the eternal, silent chorus of The Grating.
