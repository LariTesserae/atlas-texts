Life in the Drowned Range does not scuttle, swim, or dart. It creeps. It is a kingdom of cellulose and chlorophyll that has usurped the thrones of the predator, filling the vacuum left by the absence of flesh with a riotous, slow-motion violence. Here, the concept of "flora" has burned away the distinction between plant and animal, resulting in something... else.

**The Kelp-Colossi**
The dominant lords of this dark sphere are the Kelp-Colossi. They are not the swaying ribbons of the surface world; they are pillars, massive as pillars of a cathedral, composed of fibrous, woody muscle. In the twilight gloom, a single Colossus can loom for a hundred feet, its trunk as thick as a ancient oak, but dark and slick with the cold.

They possess a terrifying, blind intelligence. Rooted in the rock, they spend decades in a meditative stillness, filtering the minimal nutrients that drift down from the dead sun above. But they are not static. When the chemical tremors of a thermal vent shift, or when the deep currents change their subtle course, the Colossi move.

It is a geological movement. They tear their massive anchor-roots from the basalt with a sound like a cracking spine, sending up clouds of the velvet silt. Once free, they drift. They are graceful in their bulk, using long, vine-like tentacles to catch the heavy currents, migrating across the vast, empty voids between peaks.

Their "feeding" is the source of the region's latent horror. Where animals have eyes, the Kelp-Colossi have vibration-sensing nodes along their lengths. Where animals have mouths, they have trigger-cysts—clustering bundles of fronds that snap shut with the force of a steel trap when they detect the displacement of water. They do not hunt meat; there is no meat to hunt. They hunt "snow"—the organic detritus that falls from above—and they hunt each other.

A forest of Kelp-Colossi is not a community; it is a war of strangulation. You will see them entangled, their great trunks wrapped tight around one another in a slow, millennia-long embrace. One squeezes the life from another, crushing its fibers to steal its accumulated nutrients or simply to eliminate a rival for the current. The victim does not die quickly. It stands, gray and rotting, while the victor uses its carcass as a trellis to reach higher toward the violet gloom.

**The Globe-Algae**
Suspended between the titans are the flocks of Globe-Algae. These are the lanterns of the deep, massive spheres of translucent jelly-plant ranging from the size of a man’s torso to the bulk of a small house. They are tethered to the peaks by long, umbilical cables of tough, braided vine, anchoring them against the crushing pressure.

Inside their gelatinous skin, bioluminescent organs pulse with a rhythmic, greenish light. *Thrum-thrum... thrum-thrum.* This is not just decoration; it is a lure. The Globe-Algae are farmers of the current. Their light attracts clouds of thermophilic bacteria and microscopic organisms released by the vents. When a cloud grazes the skin of the Globe-Algae, the surface instantly absorbs the nutrients.

When the thermal vents erupt—the "weather" of the deep—the Globe-Algae go mad. The burst of heat and minerals causes them to flare into a blinding brilliance, a synchronized warning that turns the abyss into a strobe-lit highway. In this frenzy, they can detach their tethers, bobbing upward into the heat to gorge, before slowly reeling themselves back down to the cold stone as the water cools.

**The Silt-Stalkers**
Down in the velvet silt, where the water is heavy enough to crush a human lung, live the Silt-Stalkers. These are low, creeping organisms resembling a cross between a sea anemone and a patch of brambles. They wait. They are essentially stomachs embedded in the mud, surrounded by a ring of trigger-sensitive needles.

When the Kelp-Colossi drift overhead and shed their dead fronds—or when one falls in the slow war of the giants—the Silt-Stalkers are the first to know. They surge from the mud, writhing masses of thin, stinging threads that wrap around the falling debris. They secrete a concentrated acid that dissolves the tough plant matter into slurry, which they drink. They are the cleanup crew of the Drowned Range, stripping the fallen kings down to the bone-white fibers.

**The Masonry-Moss**
Perhaps the strangest life is what has grown on the ruins of the nameless builders. The primitive stone walls and sea-stacks are covered in a thick, gray shag—Masonry-Moss. This moss does not just eat the stone; it mimics it.

The moss grows in flat, geometric patterns, aligning itself with the mortar and the grain of the rock. Over centuries, it creates a perfect simulation of the wall, a living hide over the dead stone. It is entirely parasitic, slowly dissolving the primitive rock to feed its own structure. Eventually, the stone wall crumbles into dust, leaving behind a freestanding shape of pure plant-matter—a hollow echo of a building built by creatures who have long since turned to dust.

**The Chorus of the Roots**
There are no voices here. No cries of pain or territorial roars. But the life here makes noise. The hollow, porous basalt of the mountains acts as a resonator. The Kelp-Colossi, as they drag their massive roots across the rock floors, create a low-frequency thrumming that vibrates through the stone. The Globe-Algae, when they pulse with light, cause a subtle displacement of water that adds a high-pitched, watery hiss to the acoustic pressure.

Together, the ecology creates the "Song of the Green Deep"—a constant, unsettling symphony of creaking wood, groaning stone, and the hissing of millions of leaves hunting in the dark. It is the sound of a world that has forgotten what it means to be an animal, a symphony of hunger playing in the key of absolute zero.
