They arePale, glistening things, the color of bruised clouds. To look at them is to look at a biology that has abandoned the vanity of sight and the rigidity of bone. In the perpetual, flat twilight of the basin, eyes are useless organs, and so they have evolved into sealed, smooth ridges in the flesh, or deep, sunken pits covered by a nictitating membrane of thick pearl.

The dominant populace are the Grey-Skins. They possess the texture of wet velvet, their hides perpetually slick with the heavy condensation that drips from the obsidian eaves. They are humanoid only in the loosest sense—bipedal, but with a stoop that suggests their spines have adapted to the low, crushing ceilings of the stone labyrinth. Their limbs are disproportionately long, ending in digits that are not quite fingers: wide, flat pads tipped with small, suction-like spiracles. They do not grasp so much as they adhere. In a world of razor-sharp scoria and unstable, shifting glass-gravel, to fall is to die, and so they have become living climbing gear. They creep along the vertical faces of the basalt huts, clinging to the wet rock like slugs, their movements slow, deliberate, and fluid.

Their silence is physical; their mouths are small, slit-like openings designed more for the intake of moisture than for speech. But this does not mean they are quiet. The noise—that deafening, vibrating wall of sound—is born in their throats and chests. They possess a unique resonant sack, a distensible bladder of waxy skin that sits high on their chests, pulsating visibly. When they compress their diaphragms, the sack expels air through the narrow mouth slit, producing a sound like a wet saw grinding against bone. It is not a call for mating or a warning of predators; it is a metabolic necessity. In this pressurized, wet heat, their bodies are constantly buoyant with gas and fluid; the scream is how they vent the excess pressure of existence. To stop screaming is to burst.

Among them move the Scuttlers—the chitinous minority. Where the Grey-Skins are soft and yielding, the Scuttlers are articulated armor. They resemble an obscenity of isopods and crabs, their shells a polished, obsidian black that makes them nearly invisible against the wet ground. They have too many legs, each jointed and sharp, clicking rhythmically on the glass shards. They are the scavengers of the vertical city, scuttling over the huddled bodies of the Grey-Skins without malice, cleaning the algae and fungal mats that grow in the perpetual damp of the larger creatures' skin. The Grey-Skins tolerate them, too lethargic in the heat to swat them away. The Scuttlers eyeless, faceted heads are equipped with sensitive hairs that taste the vibration in the air, allowing them to navigate the claustrophobic alleys by reading the acoustic topography of the roar.

Then there are the Anchors—the bloated, gargantuan toads that occupy the doorways and the wider depressions in the stone floor. They are massive, mountains of grey flesh that appear immobile, their skin folding over itself in thick, wet wrinkles. They have been there so long that mossy patches of grey lichen have begun to grow on their backs, making them indistinguishable from the rock they rest upon. The Anchors do not scream. They merely vibrate. Their bloated bodies act as the bass speakers for the entire basin, a deep, thrumming hum that rattles the teeth of the other inhabitants. They are the bottom of the food chain and the top simultaneously; they filter the stagnant water from the puddles, swelling with liquid, and when they die, their bodies collapse into a nutrient slurry that feeds the next generation of Scuttlers and the fungal dampness that clings to every surface.

There is no violence here. The sharp edges of the world make aggression too risky—a slip could mean a fatal cut from the terrain. Instead, there is a suffocating intimacy. The creatures pack the narrow alleys shoulder-to-shoulder (or shell-to-shell), a living carpet of flesh and chitin. They sleep in piles, entangled with one another, the warmth of their bodies raising the local temperature to a feverish pitch. The young are born live, slippery and fully formed, slipping from the undersides of the adults to immediately attach themselves to the nearest wet wall.

Their society is not one of hierarchy, but of hydrology. They move in rhythms dictated by the dripping water. When a particularly heavy flow of condensation begins to stream down a wall, dozens of Grey-Skins will detach from their perches and shuffle toward it, lining up to press their open mouths against the rock, drinking the metallic-tasting runoff. They are fungi given flesh, processing the wet air and the sharp rock into more of themselves.

They are a speciesestival stasis, perfectly adapted to a prison of their own geology. They do not wonder about the sky, for it is always a flat, unchanging grey. They do not dream of escape, for the basin walls are too steep and the air too heavy. They simply exist—soft, wet, and screaming—clinging to the broken shards of the earth, metabolizing the humidity, and vibrating in harmony with the eternal, deafening song of the stone.
