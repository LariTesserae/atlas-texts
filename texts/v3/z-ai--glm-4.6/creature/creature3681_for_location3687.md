They are the Quiet Ones, a people carved from the same gloom as the cliffs they cling to. They do not stand so much as they adhere, moving through the vertical city with the cautious, fluid grace of spiders. Their bodies are elongated and ropy, wiry muscle packed onto light frames, an evolutionary response to the scarcity of horizontal space and the necessity of climbing. To fall here is to vanish into the obsidian deep, and so their hands and feet are their primary means of survival—digits long and prehensile, tipped with flattened, tough nails that can hook into the crags of the glassy stone or grip the slick, braided-vine ropes of the bridges.

Their skin is a palette of the twilight: shades of charcoal, deep algae-green, and the bruised purple of shadows under water. It is textured, thick, and leathery to protect against the sharp, unweathered rock, covered in a fine, perceptible down that traps the moisture of the air, keeping them permanently damp. This dampness is essential; the air here is thick enough to coat the throat, and they seem to drink it in as much as they breathe it, their broad, flat noses equipped with filtering tufts to strain the heavy spore-clouds that drift up from the water.

Their eyes are their most striking feature—vast, dark pools that possess no whites, adapted entirely to the pearly, luminescent gloom. They see gradient rather than color, detecting the subtle shifts in the vibration of the air and the pressure of the deep water. They do not blink often; when they do, a nictitating membrane sweeps sideways, a clear, third eyelid that keeps their vision perpetually clear of the mist.

Socially, they are hive-like, bound by the terrifying reality of their architecture. Privacy is a foreign concept; homes are open-sided pods stacked one atop the other, so that a family in the lower tiers is constantly privy to the conversations and movements of those above. This has bred a culture of profound, communal consideration. They speak in the "hushed tones" dictated by their environment, a language composed less of distinct syllables and more of melodic, low-frequency purrs and clicks. These sounds travel poorly through the thick air, ensuring conversations remain intimate, shared only between those who are touching or nearly touching.

They are a people of the Swell. Because the deep leviathans displace water with enough force to rock the entire archipelago, the inhabitants have developed an entirelyvestibular sense of balance. They do not stumble when the timber catwalks heave violently; they flow with it, bending their knees, shifting their weight in a synchronized dance that ripples through a crowd like wind through wheat. Children learn to walk on the swaying ropes before they can speak, their inner ears calibrated to the chaotic rhythm of the High Flood.

Their technology, born of bone and obsidian, reflects their relationship with the deep. They are hunters, but not of the sky. They fish the abyss below, lowering long, kelp-lined traps that descend thousands of feet into the dark. The tools they forge are singular and deadly—harpoons tipped with the jagged teeth of the monsters they hunt, needles carved from the hollow bones of avian-like creatures that occasionally perish against the spires. They possess no metallurgy, yet they have mastered the tensile strength of organic matter, weaving knots that hold as tight as welds.

Clothing is minimal and strictly functional, consisting mostly of skins and woven reeds, but adornment is significant. They wear the "hum"—small, carved bones and hollow stones that hang from their necks and wrists, clattering softly against their chests when the deep vibration runs through the city. To wear a heavy necklace of deep-water ivory is a status symbol, a sign that one has descended on the lines and returned.

Perhaps most importantly, they are a people in perpetual transit. Because the water rises slowly over centuries and the rock beneath them is volatile, they are constantly dismantling the lowest levels of the city to feed the highest. It is a slow, upward migration. A person born in the dark, wet shadows of the base may die in the windy, misty peaks, having lived their entire life within a vertical mile of their birthplace, yet amidst a constantly shifting geography. They understand that nothing is permanent—not the spires, which crack and shed shards like glass, not the bridges, which rot and fray, and not the water, which endlessly seeks to reclaim them.

They are a suspension of biology, held in the amber of the humid air, waiting for nothing, simply existing in the quiet, resonant exhale of the planet.
