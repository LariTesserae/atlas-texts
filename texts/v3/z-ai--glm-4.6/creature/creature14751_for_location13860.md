If you were to search this green, sweating amphitheater for the scurry of claws or the beat of wings, you would find only thejh hiss of water and the patience of stone. The declaration that no animals live here is true, if one defines "animal" by the frantic metrics of blood and bone, of speed and hunger. But life is not always swift. Sometimes, life is a slower geology.

Here, the inhabitants are the **Polished**.

They are few—far fewer even than the giant ferns that tower over the basin. To the untrained eye, they are indistinguishable from the very ground itself. They begins as low, rounded hummocks, dark and grey-green, resting at the edges of the miniature rivulets that trace the floor. They are roughly the size of a shield, or a large tortoise shell, but their shape is irregular, like a clay mound that has been pressed by a thumb but never fired.

Their flesh is not soft, but it is not precisely stone, either. It is a densely packed, fibrous material, smooth and cool to the touch, possessing a texture like wet slate that has been rubbed with oil for a thousand years. They have no limbs, no distinct head, no eyes to pierce the heavy veil of the humid air. They are simply masses of awareness and appetite, anchored to the world by their own weight.

Yet, they are not static.

Movement here is an act of supreme leisure. A Polished does not crawl; it flows. By contracting the dense fibers of its underside, it creates a slow, rippling motion that inches it forward across the sweating floor. It moves perhaps an inch in an hour, a pace that makes the growing of the giant ferns seem frantic by comparison. It leaves no trail of slime, only a temporary disruption in the bead of water it traverses, a brief shimmer on the stone before the condensation reforms to cover its passage.

They are the shepherds of the water’s path. While the stone weeps endlessly, the Polished navigate the gradients of moisture, inexorably drawn to the places where the water gathers deepest. They are the filters of the engine. Their underbellies are lined with millions of microscopic, cilia-like filters that drink the water as they pass over it. They do not consume the liquid itself, for the basin needs water. Instead, they strain it. They pull from the flow the microscopic detritus, the mineral dust that might otherwise clog the pores of the stone, and the rare, drifting spores released by the giants. They metabolize this cleanliness, purifying the cycle that sustains them.

Their existence is one of profound, deafening solitude. They do not call to one another, for sound is an intruder here. They communicate, if they communicate at all, through the stone itself—vibrations so low and deep they travel through the rock rather than the air, a subsonic hum that registers only in the roots of the ferns and the basalt floor.

Occasionally, inevitably, the paths of two Polished will converge. Because they move so slowly, this convergence is an event that takes days of slow approach. There is no aggression, no territorial posturing. They do not possess the biology for violence. When they meet, they simply edge against one another. They might pause for a week, pressed side-by-side, perhaps exchanging a chemical signature through their damp skins, a silent acknowledgement of the other's presence in the vast machine. Then, with equal slowness, they will slip around one another, continuing their separate, aimless drifts around the bowl.

They are the silent witnesses to the valley's breath. One might find a Polished resting against the bulbous, sponge-roots of a pale tree, waiting for the condensation to drip from the waxy leaves above. When the drop falls—*plink*, a heavy sound in the cathedral quiet—the creature ripples slightly, absorbing the impact, aligning itself to catch the next one.

They age with the landscape. Over centuries, their outer layers harden and calcify, colors deepening from a wet grey-green to a near-black obsidian. Eventually, they do not die so much as they stop moving. They petrify, becoming permanent fixtures of the floor, indistinguishable boulders that serve as new obstacles for the rivulets to wind around. In this way, the inhabitants become the place itself, their bodies added to the walls, their stillness contributing to the eternal, perfect cradle of the bowl. They live, they drift, they cleanse, and finally, they settle into the stone, becoming the foundation for the next slow, green breath.
