At first, you see nothing moving. In a place defined by stillness, life must be indistinguishable from the stone, a deception of texture rather than a disruption of form. But if you watch the sheen of the oily scum coating a specific column, you might notice the reflection shifting slightly, independent of the light. Then, a shadow detaches itself from the darkness between two stone bulges, and the occupant of this damp hollow reveals itself.

They call nothing to mind so much as a spill of heavy velvet, draped over the curves of the basalt. They are soft-bodied creatures, lacking the rigid skeletons of the world above, shaped instead by the relentless pressure of the humid air and the walls that enclose them. Their skin is the color of wet ash—pale gray mottled with the faint, bruised purples and rusts of the mineral scum they consume. This skin is loose, hanging in loose, draping folds that allow them to glide effortlessly over the polished, slick stone. They possess no distinct limbs; their movement is a function of hydrostatic pressure, rippling beneath the heavy gray dermis like a muscle under a blanket, allowing them to flow with the sluggish viscosity of treacle around the tight corners of the labyrinth.

They are not large. An adult could comfortably fit within a carved stone basin, curling into a doughnut-like shape to sleep. When they stretch, measuring perhaps four feet from their anterior to their trailing end, they look like a living shadow, a blot of grease against the rock.

There is no face in the human sense. The anterior end tapers slightly, forming a blunt, singular head that is mostly taken up by a  frill of sensory tissue. This frill, a delicate ruffle of translucent flesh, pulses rhythmically, tasting the thick air. They are eyeless, for the diffuse gray light offers nothing to see. Instead, they navigate through a highly developed sense of proprioception and touch, feeling the minute vibrations of the stone, and chemoreception, sampling the air for the metallic tang of the lithophilic slime.

Their method of feeding is the primary movement in this static world. They wrap the front of their bodies around a patch of the oily scum, their skin secreting a mild solvent that loosens the microbial grip on the rock. Then, through a microscopic, sandpaper-like texture on their undersides, they rasp the stone clean, ingesting the slurry of minerals and primitive life. It is a silent, slow process. A single feeding session can last days, the creature remaining motionless in a tight embrace with the column, indistinguishable from a lichen patch until it slowly slides away, leaving behind a patch of pristine, wet black basalt that gimmers in the gloom.

They are solitary, distributed sparsely through the forest of stone. The territory of one is defined by the reach of the dripping water and the density of the scum. They are not aggressive; there is enough slime for all, and the energy to fight would be calorically wasteful. However, they are acutely aware of one another. When two meet in a narrow corridor, they pause. The frills on their heads flare outward, tasting the chemical signature of the other. They press their flanks together—not in affection, but in communication, a transfer of information about water sources and cleared stone. Then, with a fluid ripple, they slip past each other and continue their endless patrol.

Curiously, they seem to venerate the equilibrium of their home. The ancient channels carved by the vanished three-fingered architects are not ignored by these simple grazers. They will often align their bodies with these runnels, allowing the condensation to flow over their skin, drinking the pure water through their porous hides. They have been seen resting in the larger hand-carved basins, floating motionless in the centimeter-deep water, perfectly buoyant, their gray forms blending with the dark底 until they are invisible.

They are the inheritors of a finished story. They do not build; they do not transform. They simply maintain. By grazing the stone, they prevent the scum from choking off the condensation points, ensuring the water continues to flow into the pans. In a way, they are the biological component of the machine, living enzymes preserving the stasis. They seem immortal in their patience; you cannot tell if the creature you are watching is ten years old or a thousand. They simply exist—drifting, grazing, tasting the heavy air—waiting for nothing, needing nothing, perfectly attuned to the rhythm of the plinking water and the next drop, and the next.
