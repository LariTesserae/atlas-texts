Here, on the high, smooth back of the world, life does not compete; it consolidates. The beings that inhabit this ridge are not a collection of individuals so much as a single, distributed organism that has absorbed its environment. They are the Waterless People, though they have no such name for themselves, just as a lung does not name the air. They are simply… what lives here.

Their bodies are adaptations to pressure. The atmospheric pressure, low and thin, is kind to their short, compact frames, their barrel chests designed to pull what little oxygen there is from the limestone-tasting air. Their skin is the same brown as the cracked earth between the stone tiles, tough and opaque, a barrier that sweats little but weeps a cool, proprietary oil that swells the fibers of their linen wrappings, creating a microclimate of dust-repellent dampness against their skin. They are not beautiful in the way of lowlanders. Their features are broad and soft, their eyes large and dark, pupils perpetually dilated to drink in the meager, golden light.

Their faces are their most expressive feature, but not with smiles or frowns. They communicate with a subtle, nearly imperceptible twitch of cheek muscles, a slow flutter of eyelids, a precise angle of the head. It is a language designed for a world where a shout is an act of futility.

The defining sense here is not sight or sound, but vibration. The constant, thrumming drone of the chitinous fauna is not just noise; it is the medium in which they exist. Their feet are bare, calloused pads of horn and skin, and they feel the world through them. They can discern the skittering of a single beetle twenty paces away in the Needle-Thicket, not through the sound it makes, but through the faint scratches it leaves on the stone’s resonant surface. Their spoken language is a form of low-frequency hum, a complex series of resonant notes produced deep in the chest. These vibratic phrases travel through the stone and the dense-packed spines of the thicket more efficiently than through the dusty air, allowing them to "speak" over considerable distances without raising their voice. A conversation between two gardeners is a silent exchange of glances and a low, thrumming hum that makes the dust on their bodies dance.

They are the masters of the chitinous world. They do not fear the teeming, hard-shelled masses; they cultivate them. The Needle-Thickets are not just building material, but pasture. The people, known among their limited conceptualizations as "Myr-midons," meaning "tend-of-the-thicket," manage the forest's growth with geological patience. They will cut a swath through the thicket using wedges of polished stone and sinew-wrapped mauls, and the new growth, while taking a century to mature, will provide a tender, nutrient-rich bark for the Wrack-Beetles. In turn, these beetles become their primary source of protein and fat.

The harvest is a slow, holy affair. They don't hunt the beetles; they collect them. At the cyclical peak of the beetle population, they lay down wide, fresh-mudded traps. The beetles, drawn to the moisture, become mired. The Myr-midons then move through the traps, selecting the largest specimens with a gentle prod. A quick, precise twist of a stone blade at the neck-seam ends the beetle’s life. Nothing is wasted. The carapaces are pried clean and used for everything: for scooping algae paste, for roofing a new scoop-home, for weaving into articulated armor that protects from the thorns and the mandibles of rival insects. The chitinous legs are ground into a fine powder that, when mixed with water, creates a tough, quick-setting grout. The wriggling young are kept in clay-lined troughs, fed on mashed lichen, and harvested as needed. It is agriculture, but of a profoundly slow and alien kind.

Their water is life, memory, and ritual. The vast nets of woven spider-silk are their most sacred technology. Hung between the highest rock pillars, they are invisible in the golden gloom until the morning. As the temperature fluctuates, a microscopic fog rolls through the ridge. The nets, coated with a paste of crushed, hydrophilic moss, catch this dew. It trickles down the guide-fibers into channels carved in the rock, flowing into cisterns deep within the mountain. The "Dew-Tenders" who maintain the nets are the priestly caste of this world. Their hands are stained green with moss, and their knowledge of wind currents and thermal shifts is absolute. To spill a cup of this water is not just a loss of resource; it is a violation against the community's soul.

Living amidst such biomass, they have their own gardeners, but they do not grow flowers or fruit. They cultivate algea and lichen in shallow stone trays. The "Grit-Gardens" are dampened with a single measure of precious water each day, then dusted. They use fans of woven leaves to whip the golden pollen-laden haze down onto the trays. The vibrant, grey-green and rust-orange life that blooms is their soft food, their only sweetness. It is eaten as a paste, a living slurry of minerals and air, tasting faintly of stone and sunlight.

Yet, they are not the only thinkers here. Nestled within the human settlement is another species, a true parasite of the parasite. These are the "Husk-Menders," small, pale, millipede-like creatures that live in symbiosis with the Myr-midons. They are kept in small, carved wooden boxes brought from the body of a petrified tree found in a lower cave—the only wood on the ridge. Each morning, a person will open the box to their chest, and a dozen of the Husk-Menders will crawl out. They move with an unnerving delicacy across the skin, feeding on dead cells and parasites, their myriad legs creating a pleasant, tingling sensation—a waking massage that cleans the body without the need for water, which would be profane. In return, the people provide them with shelter, a safe place from the predatory mandibles of the wilder insect world, and a steady diet of their own shed skin.

And beyond the warm, chittering embrace of the settlement, there are the things they fear. The open salt pans are haunted by the "Glass-Wyrms," creatures of living crystal and salt that burrow through the mineral tiles. They are blind, hunting by sensing the vibrations of a careless step. Their strike is silent and absolute; they erupt from the ground as a cluster of razor-sharp crystalline shards that envelop and digest their prey, leaving behind only a lingering, faint crystalline hum in the stone.

But the greatest terror is silence itself. The wind, for all its howling, is merely a bass note. The true music of the ridge is the eternal insect drone. On rare occasions, for reasons they do not understand, the sound stops. The hum ceases. The clicking halts. In that sudden and impossible silence, the Myr-midons freeze. It is in these moments that the true predators of the High Ridge emerge. Pale, silent hunters that mimic the texture of the thicket, their limbs like twigs, their bodies no thicker than a human arm. They move with a speed that defies the world's slowness, flowing through the branches with a dry rustle that is indistinguishable from the wind. They are drawn to the silence as much as they are drawn to movement. To be loud is to be ignored. To be silent is to be noticed.

So the people of the ridge endure. They are born into the hum, they live their lives in its resonant field, and when they die, their bodies are not buried but carried to the deepest part of the thicket, laid upon a platform of woven beetle carapaces, and left. The beetles come first, then the softer-bodied scavengers, and finally the microbes that live in the dust. In a week, there is nothing left but a faint, dark stain on the stone and a memory held in the thrumming song of the place. They are the final shape of life for a final, desiccated world, a people of stone and bone, of dust and drone, listening to the slow, beautiful grinding down of time itself.
