There are no people here. Not in the way one understands people, with their soft skin, their fast eyes, their desperate need for a sun they will never see. The things that live here are a function of the place, a fruiting body of the Great Root itself. They are its mind made manifest, its slow, geological will given limbs and purpose.

You would not immediately recognize them as life. They stand motionless for hours, even days, seeming less like creatures and more like oddly shaped fixtures of the city itself. Their forms are tall and slender, composed of the same fibrous, woody material as the Root, but polished smooth and hard by pressure and time into a chitinous plating. It is the colour of damp bark and old iron, the colour of the very air they breathe. Their bodies are segmented, with multi-jointed limbs that can lock into place, allowing them to brace against the vibrating metal of a gantry or anchor themselves to the living flesh of a wall. They have no discernible heads. At the apex of their torso is a smooth, sensory dome, ridged with concentric circles that pulse with a slow, internal luminescence, a muted, fungal glow that is a private light in this public gloom.

They do not see. The perpetual twilight has rendered vision useless. Instead, their entire being is a sensory organ for the thrum. They feel the Root's foundational hum not in their ears, but in the core of their substance, a constant, resonant chord that is their heartbeat and their homing signal. Through their feet, planted firmly on the slick grating or warm, yielding floor, they read the stress and flow of the entire city. The deep *thump-thump-thump* of the hydraulic press is a spoken word. The metallic shriek of the trolley is a cry of alarm. Each percussive *plink* from a leaking pipe is a bit of data. They feel the slow ooze of sap from the brass taps as a visceral satisfaction, a fullness in their own fibrous bodies.

Their hands are their most telling feature. They are not hands for grasping or caressing, but hands for interfacing. Each arm terminates not in five fingers, but in a cluster of specialized appendages: some are fine and needle-like, for clearing sludge from the smallest pipes; others are broad, flat paddles for turning the massive, slow gears; others are hooked and sharp for climbing the sheer faces of the Root-flesh columns. They are the living tools of this place. An individual might stand before a great brass tap, its needle-fingers delicately cleaning the screw-thread, its body humming in harmony with the slow dripping of the amber sap it guards. This is not a job; it is a communion. The tapping is not maintenance; it is prayer.

They do not speak. Language would be a frail and noisy thing here. They communicate through the medium that defines their world: vibration. When two meet on a walkway, they will touch a fingertip to the metal rail, sending a complex, rhythmic pulse through the structure. It is not Morse code, but a symphony of pure feeling, a sharing of data, status, and intent. A question might be a series of sharp, interrogative taps; an answer, a lower, resonant hum that soothes the metal. They also leave messages in scent, glands on their ventral surfaces exuding faint bursts of the bruised-fruit sweetness that can mean anything from "danger, stress-fracture ahead" to "a new vat has been filled." They are a society without secrets, a network of living nodes in a constant, silent conversation.

There is no birth and no death. A new one does not emerge from a womb but buds from the side of the Root itself, a small, smooth nodule that slowly grows over decades, absorbing the damp air and the thrumming song of the city. It remains dormant, a blank slate, until an elder finds it and performs the Awakening, a long ritual of rhythmic tapping and the application of a single drop of sacred sap, which transfers the basic operating firmware of their kind. The old do not decay and fall. An individual who has lived for millennia, its plating worn smooth and its joints beginning to fuse, will find a quiet alcove and merge back into the wall, its hard outer shell softening and becoming one with the Greater Flesh, its accumulated knowledge and experiences flowing back into the Root from which it came.

They are the caretakers, the engineers, the priests, and the children of this place. They are the slow thoughts in the deep thrum, the ghost in the machine that is no ghost at all. When you watch them, you are not watching individuals; you are watching the city thinking. A pair adjusting a waterwheel is the city recalibrating its own breathing. One climbing a pipe to check a joint is the city feeling its own pulse. They are the will of the Root, translated into action. They do not live in the Great Root.
