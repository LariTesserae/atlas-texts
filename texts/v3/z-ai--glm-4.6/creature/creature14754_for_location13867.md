To exist here is to be shaped by the squeeze; the skeleton of the inhabitant is not one rigid bone, but a hundred overlapping plates of calcified cartilage that slide over one another like the tectonic shifting of the city itself.

You do not see them in schools. The density of the vegetation prohibits the frantic scattering of a flock. Instead, you see The Flow.

They are elongated, serpentine in the way that a cable is serpentine, lacking the distinct tapering of a tail or the bulbous head of a fish. They possess a uniformity of shape—a muscular, blunt-ended cylinder of muscle and plate, perhaps the length of a man’s arm, perhaps twice that. Their skin is their most defining feature: it is a scavenger’s armor. Over years of dragging their bellies through the rust and the sharp edges of shattered industrial glass, their hides have accumulated a layer of detritus. Metal filings becomes embedded in their mucus, hardening into a coarse, sandpaper-like hide that matches the color of the decay: a bruised reddish-brown, mottled with the pale greys of calcified scars. They are camouflaged not by color, but by texture; they look exactly like a stray pipe or a fallen beam that has begun to crumble.

They move with a terrifying economy of motion. There is no wasted energy here. In the warm, oxygen-rich thickness of the water, they do not swim so much as *displace*. They grip the vegetation with multiple pairs of limbs, though "limbs" is a generous word. They are manipulators—hooked, chitinous appendages that fold flat against the body when not in use, protruding only to catch a frond of rubbery kelp or a jagged outcropping of iron to pull themselves forward. They hunch. They contract. They slide. It is the motion of a maggot inside a ripe fruit, unseen and intimate.

Their faces—if the sensory cluster at the leading end of their bodies can be called a face—are terrifyingly utility-driven. They have no eyes. The bruised-purple light is too weak for vision to matter. Instead, they navigate by pressure and taste. Their "nose" is a ring of pulsing pits, sensitive to the minute vibrations of the water. They can feel the thrum of the distant heartbeat-engine through their own bellies, aligning their own sluggish metabolism to its rhythm. When the great pump cycles, they pause, receding into their shells to wait out the seismic wave.

But it is the mouth that reveals their true nature in this hybrid world. It is not a jaw of teeth, but a beak. A heavy, industrial-looking rasp, like the head of a tunnel-boring drill shrunk to a horrific scale. It is black, pitted, and immensely strong.

Because these creatures are eaters of the interface. They do not hunt the kelp, nor do they hunt each other. They sustain themselves on the marriage of the organic and the mechanical that defines this place. They rasp at the places where the vines have pierced the pipes. They consume the "rust-bark"—the oxidized metal that flakes off the great gears in scales as soft as potato chips, ingesting the iron for the blood and the moss that grows upon it for sustenance. They are the grazers of decay, the biological mechanism that speeds the erosion of the machine into the forest. Their waste, in turn, is a rich, oily sludge that feeds the luminous bacteria, closing the loop in this sunless, sealed jar.

Yet, they are not alone, and they are not the highest form of life here.

Occasionally, amidst the forest of manipulators and the rust-colored eels, one might see the color White.

It is a pale, sickly white, the color of something that has never known the sun. It belongs to the Elder. The observer.

The Elder appears superficially similar to its kin—it possesses the same segmented armor, the same hooked limbs, the same draping of camouflage—but it is massive. It is swollen to the thickness of a cathedral pillar, an aggregation of years and gluttonous consumption. It no longer flows through the small gaps; it has claimed a clearing. It sits in the center of a subtle depression in the "floor," a clearing that it likely scraped clean itself over a century.

Where the smaller creatures are brown and rust-red, the Elder is covered in a thick, fibrous coat of pale algae, making it look like a marble statue that has been left in the damp for a thousand years. It is a stationary god in a world of motion. It does not need to move. The current brings the detritus to it; the smaller creatures, in their cleaning of the machinery, dislodge flakes of metal nutrient that drift down to the Elder’s waiting maw.

The Elder is the Weaver. While the smaller creatures merely graze the interface, the Elder *maintains* it. Its longer, more delicate tendrils are constantly at work, weaving the living vines through the spokes of the rusted gears. It tethers the drifting kelp to the heavy iron anchors. It knits the city together, ensuring that the machine never fully collapses into the mud, and that the forest never chokes itself out. It builds the lattice that creates the friction that feeds its children.

It is intelligent. You can see it in the way it manipulates a loose glass dial, spinning it with a single hooked limb before letting it drop, seemingly for the sheer pleasure of watching the bubbles rise. It feels you long before you become aware of it. It senses the change in pressure your presence creates, the disturbance in the heavy water.

When the thrum of the deep engine begins—the low, tooth-vibrating hum that marks the passage of an hour—the Elder reacts. It does not hide. It unfurls a frill of bioluminescent skin along its flank, a pattern of dim, intricate geometrical shapes that flash in sequence. It is not a warning. It is a prayer. It is a signal synced perfectly to the machine’s beat, a biological acknowledgment of the mechanical titan that keeps the water warm.

In this place, silence is not empty. It is filled with the slow, methodical rasp of a thousand beaks eating the city, and the rhythmic, patient weaving of the White one, binding the ruin together, leaf by iron leaf. They are not infestations. They are the citizens of the deep, warm dark, living inside the belly of a corpse that refuses to die, keeping the heart beating by feeding on its ribs.
