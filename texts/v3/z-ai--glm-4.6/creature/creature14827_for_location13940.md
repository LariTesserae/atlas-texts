To live in the Resonant City is to be a consequence of a sound. The people here, the Damp-Hands, are not a species that colonized this place; they are the place, given form. They are the human embodiment of the Great Hum, a biology scoured and shaped by the ceaseless, foundational thrum.

Their bodies give the first clue. They are stocky and dense, their skeletons heavier than a low-lander’s, the bones themselves subtly porous, like volcanic rock. They are living resonator chambers. The Hum doesn't merely travel *through* them; it becomes a part of their metabolic hum. Their flesh seems to absorb the vibration, cancelling it out through a complex internal damping you can feel if you press a hand to their back—a deceptive stillness that masks the maelstrom within. Their skin is their truest organ of perception. It is thick, leathery, and perpetually damp, beaded with the same seepage that weeps from the plateau stone, but beneath this weathered exterior is a lattice of microscopic nerve endings sensitive not to touch in the conventional sense, but to pressure waves and their frequency. A Damp-Hand does not just feel the cold metal of a railing; they feel its note, its mood, its structural integrity in a complex chord of sympathetic vibrations.

Their faces are adaptations. Ears are small, sealed over by a tough membrane to prevent the deafening pressure from tearing the delicate inner workings. Instead, they have broad, flat cheekbones and refined jawlines that tremble with the Hum, turning the entire skull into a sounding board. Their eyes are their most vulnerable feature. The constant vibration of the ocular fluid has, over generations, evolved a unique set of anchoring muscles, giving them a perpetually wide, unblinking stare. To see a Damp-Hand close their eyes for more than a moment is a signal of extreme trust or deep meditative focus, as they are voluntarily severing a primary link to their world.

Society is stratified not by wealth or lineage, but by sensitivity to the Hum.

At the pinnacle are the Harmonists. They are the priests and master engineers of the City, though the two roles are indistinguishable. They are individuals born with an almost pathological sensitivity to the frequencies, able to discern infinitesimal dissonances within the planet-wide thrum. A Harmonist can place a bare palm on a gantry strut miles from the Engine and, from the texture of the vibration alone, tell you which bearing inside the piston-assembly is wearing smooth, which section of rock is channeling water too freely, and whether the next seasonal shift will be one of release or compression. They do not speak in the harmonic dead-zones; they are rarely seen in them. Their communication is a complex, refined form of the hand-to-rail listening, a "tongue" of pressures, resonant chimes struck from specially tempered alloys, and the precise tactile feedback of their tools. They interpret the Hum as a divine language, and their duty is to translate it into the actions of maintenance. They are the only ones who dare to theorize about the Engine's purpose, their philosophies esoteric treatises on modulation, attenuation, and the Great Silence that is said to await them all if the Hum ever fails.

Below them are the Scuttlers, the bulk of the population. These are the acrobats and laborers who swarm the City’s superstructure like ants on a clock. They are born with less sensitivity, duller to the Hum's nuances, but physically robust and fearless in the dizzying, three-dimensional maze. Their world is one of constant, focused work: replacing a corroded rivet, scraping a meter-thick accretion of mineral salts from a conduit, tightening a flange that has begun to "whine" at a variable frequency. They communicate in a terse, practical sign language of pulls, pushes, and taps, their conversations more akin to a plumber tightening a pipe than a person sharing a thought. They live and die by the Harmonists' directives, finding meaning not in the grand scheme, but in the personal satisfaction of a well-tuned resonator or a perfectly silenced joint.

At the margins are the Weep-Collectors, or Alchemists. They tend to the water, filtering the perpetually weeping moisture through vast, slow-moving charcoal and sand beds housed in the City's lower levels. They study the mineral accretions, grinding them into powders used for everything from antiseptic poultices to the paste Scuttlers use to seal their gloves. They are the custodians of the City's byproducts, the quiet chemists who understand that the Hum's grand work is also one of dissolution and reformation. Theirs is the most tactile knowledge, the closest to the ancient alchemy of turning stone into sustenance. They know the metallic reeds that feed on vibration can be rendered into a bland but nourishing paste, and that the blind, scuttling mole-claws are a source of noisome protein when they can be caught.

Life for a Damp-Hand is a study in quietude. The loudest thing in their world is the overwhelming, non-audible Hum. True silence is the ultimate sacrament, the most blissful state imaginable. They seek out the harmonic dead-zones not for conversation, but for sanctuary. In these strange pockets of stillness, where conflicting pressure waves cancel each other out, a Damp-Hand can lie down and, for the first time, hear only the frantic, shallow beating of their own heart—a sound so alien and un-hymned it is both terrifying and ecstatic. Here, in the false silence, they sleep, they love, and they dream of a world without a note.

The children are born into the vibration, their tiny bodies quick to adapt. A child's first game is not of tag, but of "resonance," placing small, carved stones on different surfaces to see which one hums the most sympathetically. Their education is an apprenticeship in attenuation. They learn to walk with a gait that settles their weight into the stride of a gantry, to hold a tool so its own vibration does not interfere with their reading of the machine, to know the difference between the healthy thrum of the City and the death-rattle of a failing section.

Ultimately, the Damp-Hands are a people defined by their servitude. They know, with a certainty that bypasses faith, that they are not the masters of their world, nor even its inhabitants. They are its components. They are living attenuators, biological dampeners, and custodial nerves. They do not fear their own death, for they see it as just one more part wearing out, one more frequency fading into the Great Hum. They will be replaced, the part will be swapped, and the single, slow, world-defining note will continue to play across the Weeping Plateau, unchanged and eternal.
