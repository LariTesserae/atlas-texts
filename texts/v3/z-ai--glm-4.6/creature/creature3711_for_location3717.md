To see a citizen of this city is to first missee them as part of the architecture. They stand as still as the structures they tend, their forms echoing the polished bone texture of the pylons and the deep green of the canopy. There is no sudden movement, no casual glance. Their stillness is the absolute stillness of a thing that is exactly where it belongs, performing its function with the patience of a growing tree.

They are bipedal, a choice of form that suggests a common ancestor with the life that evolved under open skies, but every aspect of their physiology has been refined by the humid, dim, and resonant basin. Their skin is not skin but a smooth, hard rind, porous like bark, yet as lustrous as untreated marble veined with chlorophyll-green. This rind is their primary interface. It does not protect from a harsh world; it *drinks* the world in. Through its microscopic pores, they absorb the warm, wet air, drawing moisture and nutrients directly from the living medium they inhabit. They do not eat; they breathe their food.

Their feet are not feet but wide, fleshy pads that split into dozens of fine, root-like tendrils. When a citizen settles into a position—to work, to commune, to rest—these tendrils deliquesce into the springy, mossy ground. They anchor into the city's single, contiguous substrate, tapping directly into its slow, deep hum. It is how they stand, and it is how they listen. The vibration of the steam-vents, the press of the pistons, the rush of water through the vine-pipes—these are not mere sounds to them, but direct neural inputs. They have no eyes. The filtered, bottle-trapped glow of the canopy offers no detail worth seeing, and this world is understood through other senses. In place of a head, they possess a delicate, complex structure of interwoven fronds, which slowly unfurl and retract like a sea anemone. These are not merely antennae; they are a hyper-evolved olfactory and respiratory organ. They taste the air, discerning the chemical language of the fungi groves—the bloom, the blight, the need for spore or shade—and they measure the exact moisture content and saturation of the pearlescent condensation that weeps from every surface. You will see them turn this "head" not to look, but to better taste a change in the breeze.

Their means of communication are as integrated as their senses. They do not speak. Instead, they modulate their own bioluminescence, their rind pulsing with soft, slow-moving light in patterns of emerald, amber, and sapphire. These signals, nuanced and complex, convey simple commands and status updates across visual distances. For deeper communion, for memory and planning, they use the ground itself. Rooted, they send and receive bio-electrical impulses through the city's floor, a network as sophisticated as any nervous system. A complex thought is not a solitary event in one mind, but a resonance that spreads through the local root-web, felt and considered by all who are connected. It is a society of overlapping, localized consciousnesses.

Their arms and hands are their most animal feature, yet still utterly botanical. Their limbs are slender and surprisingly strong, like boughs that can flex without breaking. Their hands are not five-fingered but are graceful, six-digited arrangements, with two opposable digits and four long, nimble tendrils. The tips of these tendrils can alter their consistency at will, softening to gently sponge lichen from a transport-chute or hardening into a sharp, chitinous point to graft a new Chloro-silk thread into a growing wall. They are the city's immune system, its gardeners, its engineers. One citizen might spend a lifetime pruning the excess growth of a single spiraling tower, ensuring the bioluminescent windows remain unobstructed. Another might serve as a living valve, rooting over a major steam-pipe to sense pressure and subtly altering its own physiology to regulate flow. They are the machine's cogs that are also its mechanics.

Their lifecycle is one with the city’s. There is no birth as we know it. New citizens bud from the flanks of mature, "nodal" individuals, emerging as small, silent copies that remain rooted to their parent until their rind hardens and their fronds unfurl. They learn not through instruction, but through osmosis, their developing consciousness integrated into the root-web's collective memory. There is no death, only a final state of stasis. An ancient individual, having served for millennia, will find a quiet cul-de-sac, root itself deeply, and begin a process of decanting. Their rind fuses with the city's structure, their internal biology breaking down to become nutrient-rich loam or hardening into the unique, scarlet, fungus-like ceramic that reinforces the walls. They become a part of the very architecture they tended, a physical monument in the slow, evolving history of the grove.

To wait for an arrival in a plaza is to watch nothing change, until suddenly you are no longer alone. A citizen has simply been there, rooted and listening, perhaps for hours. They are not visitors in this place; they are its will, its slow, deliberate action, and its endless, patient self-perfection. They are the heart that beats with the chuffing of the pistons, and the mind that dreams in the resonant hum of the deep, living dark.
