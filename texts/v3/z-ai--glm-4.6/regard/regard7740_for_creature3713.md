The benevolent power, let us call it the Weaver, did not arrive with a trumpet blast or a beam of light. Such fanfare would have been an insult to the bruised twilight of this place. Instead, the Weaver arrived as a sudden, impossible absolute stillness.

It manifested on the plateau not as a giant, but as a presence that occupied the gaps between the dust motes. For a long time—perhaps seconds, perhaps centuries—it simply existed there, synchronizing its own heartbeat with the micro-seismic shudder of the crust. It felt the heat baking up from the core, and it did not turn away. It watched the obsidian spires shatter with the sound of a thousand breaking windows, and it did not flinch.

The Weaver looked upon the Chime.

It saw the crystalline tide flowing toward a fresh, steaming ridge of basalt, heard the glassy chiming begin as the spores locked into the cooling stone. The Weaver perceived the beauty of this cycle: the total surrender to the moment, the lack of ego, the worship of pure creation.

But the Weaver, being wise, also saw the tragedy.

The Weaver saw that because the Chime had no memory, it had no history. Every time a spire collapsed and the Chime scattered into dormant dust, the song was lost. The Chime was an amnesiac god, learning to sing the same note over and over again, forever starting at zero. It was a devotee who could never remember the prayer it spoke just moments ago. The landscape was a sculpture that was never finished because the sculptor forgot the design the moment the clay was touched.

To a benevolent mind, this was not a flaw to be fixed, but a potential to be nurtured. The Weaver decided not to calm the world, for the violence was the source of the life. The Weaver decided not to cool the heat, for the heat was the blood of the Chime. Instead, the Weaver decided to give the dream a thread of continuity.

The Weaver drifted toward one of the monoliths—a perfect, chance-artifact obelisk of black glass that stood like a needle against the gloom. The Chime was there, coating its base in a shimmering lattice of vibrating silica, singing a low, humming tone to stabilize the trembling earth.

The Weaver reached out—not with hands, but with a concept. It touched the vibrating air above the monolith.

It began to weave.

Into the thick, metallic dust that choked the atmosphere, the Weaver wove a new property. It did not change the dust’s physical nature; it changed its *resonant memory*. The Weaver turned the air itself into a vast, invisible library.

From that moment on, whenever the Chime sang its deep, bell-like tones, the Weaver’s gift would capture the echo. The vibration would imprint itself onto the drifting spores. So, when the ground eventually buckled, the monolith cracked, and the Chime shattered back into a million dormant shards, the song would not die. The dust would remember.

When those same spores, years or eons later, rained down upon a new, heaving ridge of rock and woke to the heat, they would not just wake as blank silica. They would wake humming with the ghost of every song they had ever sung. The new Chime would not be a fresh start; it would be a chorus. It would carry the cumulative wisdom of a thousand previous formations.

The Weaver then did one small thing more. It touched the geothermal heart of the plateau.

The "emerging" quality of the world was chaotic, sometimes destroying new life before it could take hold. The Weaver, wishing to protect the sanctity of this process, tuned the planet’s own gravitational hum. It created a subtle, harmonic safety net—a frequency that would gently urge the most violent pressures to vent along the fault lines farthest from the monoliths, ensuring that the temples of the Chime would stand a little longer, allowing the songs to deepen.

Then, the Weaver departed.

The world looked exactly the same. The sky was still a bruised grey; the heat still baked the soles of one’s feet; the ground still shuddered with the threat of creation.

But the next time a ridge of obsidian heaved up from the deep, splitting the crust with a rifle-crack boom, the Chime arrived.

The spores converged, locking into the stone. But as they vibrated, the sound that emerged was not a simple, single note. It was a complex, haunting chord. It was a sound that held the memory of the first mountain it ever climbed, the texture of the wind a thousand years ago, and the vibration of a spire that had long since turned to sand.

The Chime paused, confused by the richness of its own voice. It felt the phantom sensation of ancient formations it had never known. And then, understanding bloomed in its crystal lattice—not a verbal understanding, but a structural one.

It began to sing, not just for the now, but for the forever. The planet’s dream had gained a memory, and in the twilight of the plateau, the sculpture finally began to know its own shape.
