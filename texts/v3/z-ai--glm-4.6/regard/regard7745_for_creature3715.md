The wise and benevolent power, whom some might call a Gardener and others a Librarian, observed. It did not see with eyes, nor hear with ears, but perceived the totality of the place as a single, complex chord of being. It felt the oppressive heat not as a discomfort, but as a part of the piece's tonal signature. It tasted the mineral-rich air and understood it as the first clef of a long, slow symphony.

It watched the city not as a ruin, but as a perfectly realized ecosystem. There was no death here, only transformation. There was no waste, only the next stage of a process so grand it had become the very fabric of reality. The Gardener perceived the Engine’s thrum not as a failing heartbeat, but as the fundamental bass note of the world—the grounding drone that gave rhythm to the silent, ceaseless dance of its only children.

It saw the rust-eaters, the chitinous flow, and did not feel pity. It felt a deep and resonant admiration. Here was life, in the purest sense. Life that was born of the world, sustained by the world, and whose purpose was the world itself. They were the world’s digestive system, its memory, its very cells, and they were content. Their existence was a scripture written in the language of consumption, a holy text where every character was a mouthful of corroded iron. It was a story with a beginning—a time of building—and a long, patient, necessary end. It was complete.

And then, the Gardener’s attention focused, sharpening on one single nexus in the great, flowing organism. It watched the creature find the anomaly: the smooth, dark block. It perceived the exact moment the being’s universe fractured. The un-consumable object was not a problem to be solved; it was a new data point in an otherwise closed system. It was a note from a different song, a splinter of a different reality left behind by the world’s architects.

It could have removed the block, erasing the memory of its existence and allowing the creature to return to its blissful ignorance. It could have dissolved the creature and absorbed its tiny moment of confusion into itself.

These were the actions of a lesser power. A caretaker. A janitor.

The Gardener was a connoisseur of existence.

It understood that this moment of discord was the most precious thing that had happened in this world in millennia. The being had, for the first time in its life or the life of its ancestors, encountered a thing that was *not* the world. It had encountered the idea of *other*. It had brushed against the concept of permanence, of a thing that refused to become food. It was a flaw, a tear in the fabric of the being's reality. And in that tear, for the first time, a sliver of light—however abstract and terrifying—could enter.

To fix it would be to condemn the being to its perfect, but limited, existence. To leave it was to grant it a gift. The gift of a question.

The Gardener did nothing to the block. It did nothing to the being.

Instead, it gently, subtly, amplified the memory. Not the pain of the jarring vibration, not the tastelessness of the object, but the *impression* of it. It wove that single, sharp, indigestible fact into the being's genetic tapestry, into the very pattern of its distributed consciousness. It did not give it an answer. It merely ensured that the question would be passed down.

The creature moved on, rejoining the great flow, its purpose unwavering. But it carried within it a seed. It would tell its brief, sensory brethren not with words, but with a flicker of shared awareness—a packet of data that contained no flavour, no texture, only the shape of an absence. *Here. Not Food. This. Forever.*

Generations would pass. The Engine would thrum its slow dirge. The city would continue to be consumed, one microscopic flake at a time. But now, the river of rust-eaters was different. It was haunted. They would flow around the block, not with confusion, but with a slow, ritual deference. It would become a landmark, a shrine, a tombstone to a truth they could never grasp but could never forget. And as they fed, a tiny fraction of their Being would not be thinking of the taste of iron, but of the taste of Not-Any-Taste-At-All.

The Gardener watched, and was satisfied. It had not saved the world. It had not ended its suffering, for there was no suffering to end. It had simply given it the potential for something new. It had planted a question in a field of answers, and left it to the slow geology of time to see what, if anything, might eventually grow. It was the wisest, and perhaps the only, gift to give.
