The Power arrived not with a trumpet blast, but with the subtle pressure of a barometric shift—a change in the weight of the air so gentle that the dust motes suspended in the pillar of light merely rearranged themselves into more perfect spirals.

It perceived the Sunken Brick-Hollow not as a ruin, but as a lung. It saw the frantic rhythm of its respiration—the panic of the Slate-Wings, the scuttling of the beetles, the slow drip of mineral water—and recognized a heart that beat too fast against the cage of its ribs. It was a living thing screaming in the dark, terrified that the ceiling would one day fall.

The benevolence of this Power was not the softness of pity, but the strength of a gardener pruning a tree so it might not break under its own weight. It knew that to remove the predators would be to invite rot, and to flood the place with sunlight would be to blind the children of the dusk. So, the Power did not seek to change the nature of the inhabitants, but to heal the container that held them.

It began with the **Song of the Stone**.

The Power reached down through the aperture, a presence felt rather than seen, and touched the ancient matrix of sandstone and mortar. Where the mud had turned to sludge and the bricks had worn "tumorous" and soft, the Power sang a low, deep frequency that resonated in the silica of the stone. It did not rebuild the walls as they were new—the sharp edges of the past were gone—but it annealed them. The soft, trembling gray mortar hardened into a substance like agate, transparent and diamond-tough, yet warm to the touch.

The weeping walls did not stop weeping, for the water was the lifeblood of the Hollow. But the Power cleansed the veins It touched. The silver tears running down the masonry lost their taste of old dust and stagnation. They became mineral-rich, sweet, and life-giving. Where the water pooled on the floor, it no longer bred a sour miasma; instead, it fermented into a faint, luminous moss that cast a soft, bioluminescent glow upon the flagstones—a gentle nightlight for the darkest corners, banishing the terror of the absolute unknown without ruining the necessary gloom.

Next, the Power turned its attention to the **fracture of the sky**.

The wide, circular aperture far above remained, but the Power adjusted the air currents. The oppressive, humid soup that trapped the noise and the heat was given a voice. A gentle, constant updraft was established, a thermal river that swept the stale air upward and out, pulling the screams of the Slate-Wings away into the world above, leaving the air below fresh, cool, and smelling of ozone and wet mint.

This updraft was a gift to the tenants. The Slate-Wings, whose frantic flight was a desperate struggle against gravity, found that the air now buoyed them. A single beat of their leathery wings could now carry them high. The exhausting, dangerous climb from floor to ceiling became a graceful glide. The "work" of survival was lightened, just enough to shift the collective anxiety of the colony from terror to vigilance.

Finally, the Power addressed the **Architecture of Sound**.

The Hollow was a place of deafening noise—a claustrophonic cylinder of shrieks that grated on the nerves. The Power could not silence them, for their language was their life. Instead, it tuned the chamber. It subtly altered the curvature of the bricks, a geometry invisible to the eye but perfect to the ear. The space became a vast, whispering gallery.

Now, when the Slate-Wings screeched their "Mine!" or their territorial warnings, the sound did not ricochet into a chaotic roar. It harmonized. The walls kissed the noise and returned it as a resonant, chordal hum—a backing track to their existence. The tearing-metal screech was softened into a sound like wind through a reed bed. The constant background roar became a rhythmic, Om-like vibration that soothed the hatchlings in their niches rather than startling them.

The Power did not banish the Stalker or the Diver. It knew that without the tax collector, the city of the Hollow would starve beneath the weight of its own population. But it offered a mercy to the dying. The glowing moss on the floor released a subtle vapour—a gentle analgesic. Those who fell to the predator did not die in panic and pain, but slipped into a deep, dreamless sleep, their bodies returning to the earth (and the beetles) in peace.

And then, the Power withdrew, leaving no trace of its visitation save the changes in the world itself.

In the wake of its passing, the Sunken Brick-Hollow remained a vertical slum, a place of scavengers and hunters. It was still dark, wet, and loud. But now, the walls were strong enough to hold the sky forever. The air was sweet. The flight was easy. The chaos had resolved into a symphony.

The Slate-Wings clung to their agate-hard mortar, their orange eyes reflecting the soft bioluminescence of the floor. They chittered to one another, a new note in their voices—something between curiosity and relief. They did not know a god had visited them; they only knew that the world, for the first time in memory, felt safe. They preened their damp fur, spread their wings to catch the benevolent updraft, and continued their civilization, no longer a prisoner of the dark, but a connoisseur of it.
