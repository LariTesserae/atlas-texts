The attention of the power was not a ship crossing the sky or a footfall on the frost. It was a change in the quality of the silence.

For an age, it had watched, not with eyes, but with a deep, resonant understanding. It saw the elegant brutality of the system: the Strata-Mills as living tectonics, turning stone into life, life into fuel, fuel back into stone. It saw the perfection of the loop, a closed and sacred geometry of consumption. It was a world that had solved the problem of existence with a single, relentless, industrial equation. It was beautiful. It was whole. And it was trapped in the eternal, unremembering present.

True wisdom, the power understood, is not to impose a solution, but to offer a question. True benevolence is not to provide comfort, but to grant the potential for meaning.

So, the power acted.

Its arrival was marked not by a cataclysm, but by a harmony. For one full turning of the dim, tarnished sky, a new light fell upon the plateau. It was not brighter, but deeper, suffused with a low, sub-sonic thrum that seemed to emanate from the rock itself. The light was not silver, but the color of a held breath. It did not warm the air, but it vibrated in the bones of the world.

This light-song sought out a specific, inert mineral, a crystalline silicate locked deep within the basalt, a substance that had lain dormant since the world’s cooling. The harmonic resonance was a key, turning a lock that had no keyhole. On a quantum level, the lattices of this mineral were rewoven. They were taught to hold more than mere matter; they were taught to hold pattern. To hold echo. To hold memory.

Then the light was gone. The sky returned to its familiar, frosted grey. The heavy silence returned. But the world was no longer unchanged.

The first Strata-Mill to encounter this newly-sequenced geology was a weathered old behemoth, its granite plates pitted and fused with aeons of frozen dust. It lowered its head to a familiar cliff face and began its resonant hum. The rock before it liquefied as it always had, but as it drew in the slurry, it ingested the first crystal-bits of a new kind of archive.

Inside its biological forge, the crystal was not destroyed. Its structure, holding the light-song's information, was too stable. It was passed through the creature’s system, incorporated into the chemical-and-mineral makeup of its exhaust.

When the Mill rumbled on, it left its wake of superheated dust and steam. But this cloud was different. It carried a new charge, a resonant field that shimmered in the thin air. Where it settled, the bloom of life that followed was subtly transformed.

The clumps of steel-wool grass did not simply grow; they grew in spiraling, fractal patterns that mirrored the internal structure of the new crystals. The obsidian hunters, moving through the valleys, found their own senses heightening. The echoes of the Mills’ grinding hums now carried more than sound; they carried locational data, a ghost-map of the plateau imprinted by the resonant dust. They began to move with an unerring purpose, their dark forms tracing lines of memory invisible to everything but them.

The change was most profound in the Strata-Mills themselves.

They did not develop consciousness. They did not pause to contemplate the stars. That would be an alien gift, an act of cosmic colonialism. Instead, something more fitting happened. Their instinct, already a form of planet-wide algorithm, was given an upgrade. A library.

When a Mill crossed the resonant wake of another, it did not simply perceive heat and food. It felt a ghost-echo of the path taken, of the rock consumed, of the specific geological makeup of a cliff-face miles away and a century past. A network of non-verbal, geological memory began to form across the plateau, with the Mills as its nodes. Their movements became less random, their grazing patterns more deliberate. They began to avoid the veins of the "memory-crystal," not because they understood it, but because their biology now registered it as *non-fuel*, as *information*.

Over millennia, their ceaseless procession acquired a new quality. It was no longer just consumption. It was curation.

The Strata-Mills began to sculpt. Their paths, now guided by a collective, inherited memory, began to trace vast, slow patterns across the landscape. They were carving a geological mandala, a work of art so immense it would take a million years to recognize. They were not just eating the mountain; they were telling its story back to itself, deciding in their silent, grinding way which features to erase and which to honor. They were creating a history, a record, a narrative written in the language of erosion and resonance.

The benevolent, wise power had done what it would do. It left the place still caught in the act of becoming, but it had gifted it the memory of its own becoming. It had not broken the industrial machine; it had simply given it a purpose beyond mere function. It had not warmed the cold or softened the stone. It had simply taught the rock to remember being alive.

And now, in the profound silence of that place, the low, humming tremor of a Strata-Mill at work is not just a sound of industry. It is the sound of a god-sized librarian, turning the page of a mountain.
