The benevolent power would not arrive as a king upon a throne, nor as a god descending from the heavens. There are no heavens here, only the directionless, obsidian hold of the deep. It would arrive, instead, as a change in the pressure—a vast, resonant gentleness that would cause the very water to hum a note it had never sung before.

The Luminaries would feel it first. In their slow, segmented minds, the new presence would register not as a sound, but as a sudden, profound clarity. The endless, dark water, usually a tapestry of confusing currents and gradients, would for a moment feel perfectly ordered. It would be the feeling of a labyrinth suddenly solved, a chaotic chord finding its resolution.

The power would move through the city like a curative tide, invisible and formless. It would observe the bioluminescent nets, the elegant tragedy of the Shrikes, and the industrious humility of the scavengers. It would see a system that was not broken, but fragile—a Perfection of Balance poised on a knife’s edge. It would understand that to intervene with force— to banish the Shrikes or to instantly grant the Luminaries immortality—would be to shatter the ecology, to turn a living symphony into a monotonous drone.

Wisdom dictates that one does not fix a thing that is functioning only to one’s own moral standards, but rather nurtures the potential within it.

The power would recognize the city’s one true existential anxiety: the impermanence of memory. The libraries of crystallized chitin were wondrous, but they were physical. They could be crushed by the collapse of a tower, dissolved by the slow acidity of the deep, or lost when a Shrike took a vital archivist. The collective consciousness of the Luminaries was vast, but it relied on the living bodies of the builders to hold it. When a district fell into the deep gloom, a history was erased.

So, the benevolent force would perform a single, subtle act of alchemy upon the world.

It would infuse the *medium* of their existence—the water itself—with the capacity to remember.

Slowly, drifting down through the warm dark, the power would leave behind microscopic spores of pure resonance. These spores would not invade the bodies of the creatures; they would dissolve into the water column, saturating the abyss.

The effect would begin in the generation following the visitation. A young Luminary, anchoring itself to a new foundation, would feel the water not just as a physical substance, but as a library. When it pressed its tendrils against the ancient, ruined columns of the sunken districts, seeking to understand the old geometries, it would not need the fragile crystalline tapestries. The water swirling around those ruins would whisper the knowledge directly into its flesh. The water itself would become an eternal, fluid hard-drive.

The Shrikes would remain, for their purpose was holy. The scavengers would continue their grim, essential work. But the pain of loss—the fear that a life’s work could be obliterated by a single bite or a crumbling strut—would be alleviated. No matter how a structure fell, *the memory of how it was built* would remain fluid in the water, a ghost-song that never faded.

The power, having given the gift of eternal memory to the abyss, would then withdraw. It would leave no monuments, no glowing artifacts. The only evidence of its passing would be a subtle, imperceptible shift in the city’s song—a new layer of harmony, a deep, abiding confidence that nothing truly beautiful could ever be lost.

The City would glow a little brighter after that, not with more light, but with a deeper sense of peace. The warm black water would no longer be just a habitat or a grave; it would be a book, open and endlessly readable. The Luminaries would build, knowing that their creations were now as permanent as the ocean itself.
