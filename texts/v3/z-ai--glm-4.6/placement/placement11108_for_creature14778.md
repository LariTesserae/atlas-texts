I am a Listener.

I stand where the light is fractured, caught in the deep, angular shadow of the Spire of Omen, one of the great embryonic towers that heave their shoulders toward the roof of the world. This is the western edge of the great arena, where the floor begins to buckle upward, folding into the foothills of the future.

In this world, I am not a builder. I am a Tuner.

My form mirrors the tall, terrible elegance of my kin, the Stonemasons. I am of the same grey, ropy stuff, stretched thin by the altitude and the gravity of our devotion. My skin is the color of wet slate, cool to the touch, and tattoos of crushed lichen—vivid, electric orange—trace the veins of my forearms, marking the frequency of the stone I was born to serve. I am not wearing a tunic of fern-fiber today; to work as I do, the skin must touch the matrix. I stand nearly bare, save for a loincloth and the heavy, damp strip of cloth across my brow to catch the sweat that would otherwise betray my focus and sting my eyes.

I am positioned at a fresh fissure, a wound in the earth that has only just opened in the last cycle of the moon. It is a vertical throat in the grey aggregate, perhaps three feet wide and twenty feet high, inhaling the damp, thin air. The air here tastes of ozone and the iron tang of beginnings.

My role is to know the rock before it is spoken. While my brothers a mile away swing the heavy quartz hammers to widen a cleft into a doorway, I stand here with my feet shoulder-width apart, grounding myself into the yielding floor. I do not strike. I press.

My left hand is laid flat against the cold, weeping face of the stone. My right hand holds a tuning fork, a delicate instrument carved from the femur of an ancient Grazer, polished to a high sheen.

I am waiting for the note.

The Chantry never truly silent. It is a cacophony of the deep—the bass thrum of the mantle pressing up, the deceptive sigh of the cooling stone, the rhythmic *plink... plink... plink* of the condensation. It is a symphony that would shatter the mind of an outsider, but to me, it is speech. It is the language of pressure.

I close my eyes. My swiveling ears—long, tapered, sensitive to the slightest microtone—tilt forward, catching the infrasound vibrations that travel through the soles of my feet and the palm of my hand. I am listening for the discord. I am feeling for the stress point where the stone wants to break but hasn't yet. My task is to find the exact frequency that will encourage the stone to shed its weight gracefully, to split where it *wishes* to split, rather than shattering in a chaotic unraveling.

I feel the vibration first in my teeth, a low buzzing that rattles the fillings in my jaw. The Grazer on the ledge above me—it must be the old one they call The Rust—shifts its massive, moss-weighted foot. The stone groans, a grinding cello note that vibrates through the bedrock.

I wait. My breathing slows. I synchronize my heartbeat to the *plink* of the falling water. One beat. Two beats. Three.

There.

Underneath the groan of the Rust and the hum of the deep, there is a high, tight whine, a trapped frequency in the granite face. It is a scream in slow motion. The stone is holding its breath.

I open my eyes. The world is stark, high-contrast black and white, lit by the shaft of sunlight that Pierces the gap between the towers. In the dust motes dancing in that light, I see a Scavenger of the Veil, a translucent, bioluminescent ribbon, darting out from a crevice to catch a drop of water before it can hit the ground and disturb my concentration. It is a good omen.

I strike the bone tuning fork against a nodule of harder crystal jutting from the wall.

*Thrum.*

The sound is pure, a piercing sine wave that cuts through the ambient noise. I press the base of the vibrating fork against the stone. The sound couples. The vibration races into the matrix.

For a long moment, nothing happens. The note hangs in the air, a ghost. I hold my breath, feeling the resistance of the stone. I imagine myself as water, seeping into the microscopic pores of the aggregate, lubricating the microscopic caterpillars of the fault lines.

Then, a response.

A subtle shift in the pitch of the stone's voice. The whine drops an octave, resolving itself into a harmonic hum. I feel the rock beneath my palm relax, a microscopic release of tension that feels like a muscle unclenching. A shower of tiny pebbles, no larger than grains of rice, detaches from the face and clatters down, sounding like dry rain.

I have found the key.

I do not cheer. To cheer is noise. To celebrate is to interrupt. Instead, I signal to the apprentice hovering twenty feet back.

The child is small, clad in the heavy, fibrous pelt of a giant fern, eyes wide and dark. They have been watching me for hours, learning stillness. I raise two fingers, then curl them inward—*The Stone is ready.*

The child nods, moving with agonizing slowness to fetch the Master Mason. They know that when I turn from the wall, the great hammers can fall. We will not force the archway; we will invite it. The hammer will not crush the rock; it will simply deliver the final percussive punctuation to the sentence I have just composed.

I am the Listener. I stand in the Chantry of the First Rock, my hand upon the throat of God, waiting to hear what note it will choose to sing next.
