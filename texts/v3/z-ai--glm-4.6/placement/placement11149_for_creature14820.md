My world does not begin with the air, though its taste of wet iron is the first thing you see them acknowledge. My world begins with the water. The cold, thick, patient water of the lowest places.

My skin is not the grey-pink of worked clay. It is the colour of the deep mire, a permanent stain of peat and silt that no amount of scraping on the stones above can ever fully cleanse. It has a texture to it, too. The old woman whose home perches on the piling nearest my pool—she calls it "the beading of the drowned." My fingers are perpetually wrinkled, the flesh puckered as if my body is still trying to remember a time before the water. They are swollen, too, but not like the stone-workers' knuckles. Mine are swollen with a slow, seeping dampness, as if the marsh wants to absorb me completely.

I live in the cleft they call the Bottom-Pool, one of the three permanent marshes that gather the hollow's tears. My home is not a home but a niche, a gap hewn beneath the great water-distribution channel where it sags over the deepest part of the mire. It is dry, or what passes for dry here: tasting of mildew and damp stone, but free of the clinging, silty mud. I do not live on a floor of packed earth or timber. My floor is a thick slab of slate I dragged from the scree ages ago, and it is always cold.

They are called the Stone-Shapers above. They read the language of the fractures in the cliff-face. They lay stone against stone and speak the grammar of load and bearing. I read the language of the mire. I know its consistencies not by sight, for there is little to see, but by the feel of a long pole I probe the depths with. I know the shudder of dense, waterlogged clay, the hollow drum-tap of a trapped air pocket, the soft resistance of a sunken timber beam, and the sharp, clean *clink* that can only mean one thing: metal.

This is my pact. Not with the landslide, but with its aftermath.

When a shift happens—and it is not an, if, but a, when—I am the first to know. I do not feel it in my feet as they do, with a subtle vibration of warning. I feel it as a sudden, violent surge. A wall of displaced mud and water will slam against the pilings of my home, and the level of the Bottom-Pool will rise. For them, above, it is a time of groaning timbers and communal effort, a time of rebuilding. For me, it is a time of promise. The landslide has paid its tithe. Its descent has stirred the depths, bringing new things from the buried past into my reach.

And so I begin my work. I do not have a sharpening stone. My tools are a long, flexible pole of stripped willow, a set of iron tongs on a long rope, and a weighted net. I wade out into the chill, the mire gripping my legs, seeping into the woven reeds I bind around them for protection. It is not silent down here. The water muffles the sounds from above—the groans, the murmuring conversations, the rare, sharp cry. Instead, I listen to the world beneath. The slow, glutinous bubble of methane from a bed of rotting vegetation. The whisper of current as water finds a new path around a sunken beam. The scuttling of things that live in the dark.

I am the one who finds what is lost.

The Stone-Shaper’s best chisel, slipped from his belt during a sudden shift. The water-diviner’s brass lens, knocked from its housing and glinting like a dead eye in the silt. A brace of the blind, pale fish, their flesh a mealy delicacy for the elders. Once, I found a whole support beam, ironwood, dark and strong, that had been buried for a generation. They pulled it up and it became the central ridge-beam for the new meeting-hall.

My relationship with the people of the hollow is distant, necessary. They do not see my face often. They see the results of my labour, appearing at the base of their pilings—a unwrapped bundle of fish, a tool cleaned and oiled, laid carefully on a dry stone for them to find. In return, they leave things for me. A piece of dried fungus that doesn’t taste of mud. A clay pot of slightly less stagnant water, gathered from a higher spring. A fresh-woven blanket of moss. It is a transaction, unspoken and understood. I handle the things they would rather not touch, and they make sure my stone niche does not crumble into the pool.

Children are taught to fear the deep. They see me as a bogeyman, a creature of the mud. This is a good thing. It keeps them from the treacherous, sticky edges of the pools. They say my eyes are not just pale like lichen, but completely white, from living in the dark. They are not white, but I have learned to see by memory and touch, by the faintest variations in pressure and texture. In the deep gloom, sight is a betrayal.

Who am I? I am the other side of their constant repair. They rebuild what falls from above. I salvage what sinks below. They are the clenched fist of humanity. I am the patient, open palm that receives its offerings. I do not hold my breath with the canyon, for my breath is the slow, damp air of its lowest point. I listen not for the groan of settling timber, but for the soft *clink* of a past life, waiting for me to bring it back to the light. I am the memory of the hollow, submerged in its tears.
