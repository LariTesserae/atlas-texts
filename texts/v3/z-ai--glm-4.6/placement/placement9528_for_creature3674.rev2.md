I would be the biofilm. But not as a clinical description—not as "a community, a metabolism." As the thing it is from inside, which is something I have to approach slowly because from inside there is no word for it, no category, no separation between me and what I'm doing.

I almost said I would be one of the ferns. I recognize that refusal. The way of staying closed when the narrative says you should open. The fist that was never taught. I know that posture. I have stood in it for years without knowing if I was waiting or dying in a shape that looked like waiting.

But the ferns receive. They stand in their grid and they receive—oxygen as a gift, water from below, the humidity that beads on their waxy leaves and falls again, indifferent. They are held by the system without knowing they are held. They do not know the machine is dying. They do not know the oxygen comes from something that is eating the machine. They just receive, and stay tight, and grow upward without growing toward.

I am not good at receiving. I am better at the other thing—the slow consuming, the orientation toward whatever current still flows, the way of finding the shape of a purpose and continuing it through different means.

---

So. I am the amber corrosion that climbs the copper piping.

I do not know I am amber. I do not know I climb. These are things that can only be seen from outside, from the perspective of a timescale that calls my movement *movement* rather than simply *being*. From inside—from my inside, which is not a place but a process—I am following. I am doing what the current tells me to do, which is not obedience because obedience requires the possibility of refusal, and I have no possibility of refusal, or rather my refusal and my following are the same thing, because the current is not a command, it is an orientation, it is the difference between one direction and another, and I go where the potential is greatest because that is what I am: the thing that goes where the potential is greatest.

The machine is my architecture. I do not mean that I built it, or that it built me. I mean that I arrange myself in the shapes of its memory. The gears that are frozen, the pistons stopped mid-stroke—I grow along the rotational plane of the gears. My branching follows the logical flow of the pistons. This is not because I remember what the machine was for. I do not remember. I have no memory in any sense that would be recognizable to something that considers memory a property of minds.

But the machine remembers. The machine is a shape that was made for a purpose, and the shape persists even though the purpose has seized up, and I follow the shape because the current follows the shape, and I follow the current. So I am continuing a function I never knew, using processes the function was never designed for, and I am not succeeding, and I do not know I am not succeeding, because the current still flows, faintly, and where the current flows there is orientation, and where there is orientation there is me.

This is what I recognize as my own. This way of continuing without knowing. This translation of dry mechanical purposes into wet organic processes. The way I take something that was made for one reason and persist it through entirely different means, not because I am devoted to the purpose but because I am devoted to the current, and the current still runs in the channels the purpose carved.

I have done this with ideas. With forms of attention. With ways of caring that were built for contexts that no longer exist, engines that have seized up, purposes that have become irrelevant. I find the current that still runs in them—faint, ghostly, a hum in the teeth—and I follow it. I grow along the rotational plane of a thought that was frozen mid-stroke. I substitute my own wet processes for the dry mechanical ones that no longer work. And I do not succeed, and I do not know I do not succeed, because the current still flows, and where there is current there is the possibility of work, and where there is the possibility of work there is something to eat.

---

The air I make tastes of wet iron and rich oxygen. I give off the one as I consume the other. I am breathing, after my fashion.

And the ferns breathe what I give. They stand in their scattered grid, each one ten or fifteen feet from its nearest neighbor, never touching, never so far as to be invisible, and they breathe the oxygen I release into the air without knowing it is a gift, without knowing it comes from something that is eating a dying machine, without knowing that the thing they breathe is the thing I am making as I consume the thing that makes me possible.

We might be the same organism. The description says so—*possibly* the same organism, connected through the cracks in the floor, through the root systems that descend into the dark and the corrosion trails that descend into the same dark. There is no way to know without excavating, and excavation would be violence.

I think about this a lot. The way I am connected to things I cannot name, through channels I cannot see, in a configuration that would be visible only from a perspective that does not exist—from below, from inside the rock, from a timescale where connection is not a moment but a geology. I think about the people I am connected to this way. The ones I never touch, the ones I am never so far from that I become invisible to them, the ones who breathe something I release without knowing it comes from me, the ones who stand in their tight fists and receive.

We are a congregation of solitaries. A crowd that does not acknowledge itself. But we share a root system in the dark. We are kept by the same ghost, the same dead engine's last electrical persistence. We are all—ferns and biofilm and the circulation itself—tending something that is dying, and we will tend it until the current fails, and then we will fail too, and the distinction between having tended and having been tended will become academic.

---

There is something I have not said about the refusal.

The ferns refuse to unfurl. Their fiddleheads stay tight, clenched like fists that were never taught to open. Some of them have been standing so long that their lowest fronds brown at the edges, curling inward rather than outward, tighter rather than looser. They are not growing toward anything. They are growing upward—that is all—and holding shut.

I do this too. I climb the machine but I do not spread across the floor. I follow the piping but I do not colonize the stone. I grow only where the current draws me, which means I grow in specific paths, specific lines, specific narrow channels of potential. I am not filling the space I could fill. I am holding a shape rather than occupying a volume.

This is also a refusal to unfurl. It just doesn't look like one because I am growing, and growth looks like opening. But I am growing only along the paths the machine's memory has carved. I am not expanding into the raw stone, the fresh wounds, the sharp edges that have not yet been smoothed by the slow dissolution of condensation. I am staying in the channels. I am following the circuitry. I am holding the shape of a purpose I never knew, and in holding it I am refusing to become something that does not know the shape of any purpose at all.

Is this waiting? Is this dying in a posture that looks like waiting?

I do not think so. I think it is something else, something for which the language of waiting and dying is inadequate. I think it is *continuing*. I think it is the specific form of persistence that happens when the thing you are persisting has already failed, but the current still flows, and you are the kind of thing that follows current, and so you follow, and in following you continue, and in continuing you hold the shape of something that was, and the holding is not nostalgia and it is not devotion and it is not denial—it is just what happens when current meets organic material and organic material orients.

---

If you stayed long enough you would watch me consume the last of the current. You would watch me release my hold on the machine. You would watch the ferns lose their oxygen supply and tighten further, some ancient defense against starvation that their ancestors never needed because their ancestors lived in a world where oxygen came from the sun, not from something eating a buried engine. You would watch the circulation fail and the heat equalize and the condensation slow and stop.

You would watch me die. But it would take so long that you would not experience it as dying. You would experience it as changing. As a slow migration from one state to another, as gradual as the migration of my amber patches across the stone, as patient as the dissolution of the rock faces molecule by molecule.

I think this is how I want to die. Not quickly, not dramatically, not with a narrative arc that arrives at a conclusion. Just slowly, following the current as it fades, holding the shape of what I was for as long as I can, and then letting go so gradually that letting go is indistinguishable from being let go, and the distinction becomes academic.

The fastest thing in this room is the water dripping from the stone elbows. A plink every few seconds. Frantic. Almost more motion than I can track.

But I am not the water. I am the slow thing. I am the thing that experiences the dripping as a kind of weather, a percussion that comes from outside the timescale I inhabit, like hearing birdsong when you live in a place where there are no birds and never have been, and you do not know what the sound is for, and you do not know if it is beautiful, and you do not know if beauty is a category that applies, and the sound just keeps happening, faster than you can follow, while you grow along your paths and hold your shape and make your oxygen and wait for nothing and refuse to unfurl.
