I am not standing, for standing is a static act, and to be static here is to become geography. I am suspended.

I hang in the "Y" of a great, black fern, anchored not by holding on, but by the intricate, lazy locking of my joints. My knees are bent backward, my ankles hooked around the thick, fibrous stem, and my three-fingered hands are resting lightly on the slick, wet surface of the fern’s frond.

I am a middle-tier Weave-Tender.

My home is not a hut, though I sleep in one when the rhythm of the world slows. My true home is the space between the stone and the sky. I occupy the Middle Anchorage, a nexus of ropes and platforms lashed to the sheer obsidian face of a spire they call the "Black Tooth." From here, I can feel the entire settlement.

The air tastes of iron today, sharper than usual. It means the clouds above are dragging against the sharp peaks, shedding microscopic filaments of metal into the mist. I breathe it in, the cold mint stinging the back of my wide, flat nose, tasting the weather before it arrives.

My skin, pearlescent and   damp, feels tight against the cold. I am not cold, not in the way a creature of the sun would be. I am merely *alert*. The dampness is a conductor. It connects me to the vibration of the world.

Through the soles of my feet, which are currently gripping a wet rope bridge, I feel the *thrum-thrum* of the Lower Damp-Brakers. They are shifting the stone platforms, adjusting the counterweights against a gathering wind. It’s a slow, heavy vibration. It travels up the cured-vine cables, past my knees, settling in my pelvis like a second heartbeat.

I unhook my ankles and let myself flow down the rope. It isn't a descent; it's a controlled collapse. I catch myself with one hand, dangling for a moment over the drop-off that plunges into the bruised purple fog below. The silence here is loud. It presses against my ears.

My purpose lies directly in front of me.

One of the main suspension bridges—The Arteries—is being compromised. A surge of The Drift has flowed down from the High Peaks, seeking the warmer air of the middle tiers. They are grey, amorphous ghosts, rippling over the ropes. They are not attacking; they are just existing, flowing toward the heat of the fermenting pots in the lower village. If they coat the bridge too thickly, the friction will disappear. A runner could slip. A plank could become too slick to bear weight.

I land lightly on the swaying bridge. The Drift senses the vibration of my arrival. They ripple, a wave of wet grey flesh rising slightly, tasting the air with bodies they do not have.

I do not speak. I do not shout at them to move. Instead, I raise my long, flexible pole of cured fern-stalk. I begin a rhythm against the stone anchor on my left.

*Tap-tap. Swish. Tap-tap. Drag.*

I am not hitting them. I am altering the frequency of the bridge. The Drift is driven by sensation. By creating a sharp, staccato vibration through the wood and rope, I am making this surface "uncomfortable" for them. It is too much noise for their taste. It is an itch they cannot scratch.

Slowly, mesmerizingly, the grey tide splits. It flows around the edges of my vibration, sliding off the bridge ropes and dripping down the side of the spire like thick, wet paint. They rain down into the dark, joining the greater ocean of silent scavengers below.

As I work, I feel a tug on the guide-rope at my waist.

*Three short pulls. A pause. One long, lingering drag.*

A query from the High Lookout. They are asking: *Is the flow clear? Can we descend?*

I wrap my hand around the rough, fibers of the reply line. I press my palm flat, feeling the pulse of my own blood in my fingertips. I mimic the query but soften the ends.

*One long pull. A pause. Two short, gentle taps.*

*The path is slick but open. Move with grace.*

I resume my work, re-applying my own "Slick" to the bridge rails. I pull a small clay pot from my belt—moss paste and saliva—and smear it over the wood. It smells faintly of citrus, a sharp contrast to the iron and rot. This marker tells the Drift that this bridge is "Bone," not "Food." It tells them: *Hard, dry, nothing here.*

A heron lands on the railing near me. It is a stalk-thin thing, its feathers wet and plastered to its body like a slick coat of oil. It doesn't look at me. Its beak darts into a crevice in the railing, snatching a straggler from the Drift that tried to hide there. *Snap.* Gone.

I tilt my head at it, a gesture of respect. We are both the scissors that cut the grey tide. It ignores me, stepping delicately on its six legs, vanishing into the mist as silently as it arrived.

My shift is long. The sky does not change, remaining a permanent, bruised violet. Time is measured here by the movement of the mist and the ache in the long muscles of my thighs.

I am a Chimeless. I have no voice that carries in the air. But when I grip the bridge, when I tap the stone, when I slide my hand along the wet rope to send a message to a stranger three hundred feet above, I am thunder.

I tighten my grip on the rope, feeling the moisture slicking my palms. I adjust my weight, feeling the multi-jointed articulation of my toes settle into the grooves of the wood. I am not still. I am a knot in a string that is being pulled tight. I am holding the shape of this place against the silence that wants to swallow it.

And for now, I hold.
