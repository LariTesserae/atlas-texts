Here, in the space between the stone and the falling, is where you would find me.

I am not a Carver in the way the others are. They have the rock in their bones, patient and certain. Their world is one of subtraction, of slowly, patiently removing what is not home until only home remains. Their silence is the warm, dense silence of a shared breath within the stone. I understand it, I come from it, but it is not my native element.

My hands, too, are different. A Carver’s knuckles are broad, flattened from the impact of hammer on chisel. Mine are long and ropy, the bones defined by a life spent clinging. The calluses are not on the striking pads of the palm, but on the insides of the fingers, where the rope bites, a constant, abrading kiss. My body does not have the dense, grounded strength of a mason. I have the taut, spare strength of a thing stretched across a chasm. I am the rope.

To be a Scavenger is to live in a third state, a place that is neither the city of Carven-Aerie nor the open sky of the Ridgeback. We inhabit the membrane between them. When I am not in the warren, I is on the rope. The descent is the defining motion of my life. The moment I swing my legs over the edge of a high-level walkway, the world reforms. Behind me, the murmuring labyrinth of home, a place of known textures and remembered warmth. Below me, the sheer, unforgiving drop, a place where even sound falls away into nothing. I do not fall; I unspool.

Here, the silence has a different quality. It is not the hush of the city, which is filled with the muffled press of a thousand lives. This is a pure, resonant silence, the kind you feel in your teeth. It is the sound of the wind threading through my hair, the sound of a granite flake breaking loose a thousand feet above and hissing through the air past my ear. It is the sound of my own blood, a slow, steady drum in a world of vast emptiness.

My consciousness is not vertical, but *tensional*. I think in terms of lines and anchor points. The city is a web of potential routes I see not as a map, but as a series of stresses and loads. I see the frayed spot on the main suspension bridge that a Weaver would miss, the place where the ice will weaken the rock lip of a hidden tunnel. This is my communion with the whole. I do not listen to the rock’s grain; I listen to its resonant frequency, the low hum it gives up under stress. I am the city's early warning system, my nerves tuned to the mountain's subtle agonies.

They say the Scavengers are touched by the sky. It is true. But it is not a gentle touch. It is the kind of touch that leaves its mark, like frostbite on the skin. Up here, in the high chasms, I see the Ridgebacks not as creatures, but as forces of nature. Their aerial battles are not fights; they are thunderstorms with wings and claws. I have seen a male dive from a cloud so high it was invisible, a meteor of leather and fury, to strike a rival from a ledge. The crack of their bodies colliding echoes for an age. To witness it is to have your sense of scale broken and remade.

I do not share the Weaver's reverence for the Ridgeback's materials. For them, a strand of sinew is a perfect, pre-made thread, a gift to be transformed. For me, a ligament is the memory of a scream. The bone I craft into a needle feels cold with a death that happened in the storm. My work is not one of transformation, but of translation. I take the raw, violent story of the sky and translate it into the quiet language of utility for the stone.

My most prized possession is not a tool or a weapon, but a shard of a Ridgeback's egg, the size of my thumb, pearlescent and impossibly thin. I found it in a nest after a Long-Wind squall, the only survivor of a clutch dashed on the rocks. I keep it in a pouch of Strider hide next to my heart. Sometimes, in the deep dark of the Cold-Sleep, I take it out. It is utterly silent. It holds no story, no echo of the fall. It is a piece of a beginning that never was. In the absolute solitude of my work, it is the only thing that is truly still.

When I return, my people look at me differently. Not with fear, but with a quiet awe, as if I am a ghost who has briefly returned to the land of the living. I bring the sacred bounty—a still-warm chick, a pearly fragment of shell, the unhollowed bones of a foreleg—and I lay it at the Weaver’s door. I do not speak of the descent, of the way the wind tried to pry my fingers from the rock, of the immense, yellow eye of a Ridgeback that watched me from above, a patient, dispassionate god. Those are my stories. They belong to the silence.

I retreat to my alcove, higher than most, carved into a spur of rock that catches the first, thin light of the Sun-Wake. I do not huddle for warmth like the others. I lie still, feeling the deep, grinding pulse of the geology beneath me, and I watch the sky. I watch the clouds form and break, and I feel the pull, the deep and terrifying call of the abyss just beyond the edge.

I am a Carver. My home is the stone. But my soul is a weight on the end of a rope, perpetually dangling in the vast, beautiful, and hungry quiet. I am the price the city pays for its stubborn existence, and I am the proof that even here, on the very edge of the world, life refuses to let its thread go.
