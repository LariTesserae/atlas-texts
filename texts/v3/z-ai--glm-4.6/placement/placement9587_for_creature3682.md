I am Ihrun.

My breath is the first thing I know in the cool, sharp air. It is not a gasp for life, but a slow, deliberate intake, a recognition of the thinness that is our due. My skin, the colour of damp, shadowed moss, feels the deep, resonant chill of the stone that forms my niche. Before I even open my eyes, I know where I am. I know because the world has not moved.

I do not open my eyes to the light. I open them to my father.

He sits on the carved ledge just outside my doorway, his back to the vast, unmoving plateau. My father is Set. Has been for ninety-seven years. He sits as he always did when he was alive, his posture of patient observation now made permanent by the cold, dry air. His leathers are the same dark, scarred ones he wore, his bronze clasp at his throat holding the neck closed. The air has turned his skin to tough, greyish parchment, drawn tight over the scaffold of his bones. He is not a corpse to be mourned; he is fixture, a landmark. He is my morning star. On his lap rests his Palette of Memory, a tablet of obsidian so covered in the fine, white scratches of his life's work that it looks like a captured piece of frost. It is my inheritance and my reference.

My own body is a conversation with the Stillness. My chest is broad, a bellows evolved to pull what little oxygen there is from the high air. My movements are not my own; they belong to the path I will walk. I rise, and every joint is a slow, creaking hinge. I do not stretch; stretching is the wish of a body for change. My body has learned to accept its shape, its permanent seat. I pull my own leathers from their hook, the hide cool and stiff against my skin. The air smells of them, and of the stone, and of nothing else.

I take my staff, a length of petrified wood polished smooth by the grip of my father’s hand, and then his father’s, and now mine. I step out of my niche, not looking down at the ground. There is no need. I know its every permanent imperfection. Here is the glaze-slick shard of a pot dropped by a cook-servant a thousand years ago, its colour still a defiant, vibrant blue. I place my left foot just so, to the right of it. Here is the hairline fracture in the stone where a block of bronze fell from a higher terrace, its impact preserved for eternity. My right foot finds the familiar unbroken space beside it. To walk in Khold is to perform a memorized dance of avoidance, a pilgrimage through the accumulated accidents of forever. I walk not on the ground, but in the spaces between the past.

My work takes me out onto the plateaus, to the high terraces that overlook the vast, geometric valleys. I am a Reader of the Bone-Mosaics and Keeper of the Living Paths. My purpose is to watch the only things that are permitted to change: the Skinslers. They are the breath of the static world.

I walk for hours. The only sounds are the sigh of the wind through the impossible, knife-edge peaks and the soft chuckle of the water in its unchanging channels. I am heading for the Spur of Unmaking, a place where a great horn of rock juts out over the main migration path of the southern herd. It is from here my father watched, and his father before him. My destination is as fixed as the stars.

I arrive, the wind pressing my body against the unyielding stone of the Spur. I do not look for the herd. I listen. The air carries the soft, six-legged rhythm of their movement, a sound like distant, soft-padded drums. And then I see them, a river of dark grey fur flowing across the grey stones, their bodies the colour of the world they inhabit. They move with the same certainty I do, their hooves finding the paths worn a million times over.

My staff has a series of notches cut into it. I use it as a sighting tool, a simple abacus. I hold it at arm's length, my deep-set eyes, the colour of shadowed basalt, narrowing against the glare. I don't count individuals. That would be madness. I count the patterns. I count the ratios. For every ten breeding females, there must be a certain number of young. For every hundred males, a certain number of elders. I watch the flow. A new calf, stumbling on its six legs, still slick with the birthing fluid that will never truly dry, but will simply be worn away over the next week. Its mother nudges it along. A symbol is made in my mind. Life.

I watch until the sun begins its slow descent, until the light turns the valley from stark black and grey to a deep, melancholic purple. My body knows the light. It knows that soon, the Sweating will begin. I look down, lower, to the flat expanse below. There is a new shape on the bone-mosaic. A Skinsler, an old male by the colour of its worn fur, has fallen. It is not a sad sight. It is a completion. Even as I watch, I see them: the Gleaners. Their white fur is the only stark contrast to the stone, a moving scar of pure white. They move with the patient, unhurried economy of their function. No sound, no drama. They simply begin, attended by the silent witness of the mountains. Another symbol is made in my mind. Death. Balance.

Only after the Gleaners have finished their work and the last sliver of sun is gone do I turn from the ledge. I pull my own Palette from the leather satchel at my side. It is a dark, glassy piece of the mountain itself, polished to a faint, cold gleam. On it, there are thousands of fine white scratches, my life's work. It looks empty compared to my father's.

I take a sliver of sharpened bone from a pouch. I do not carve the stone; I can't. That would be altering the world. I *score* it. I make a mark. A short, vertical scratch for the new life I witnessed. A single, horizontal line for the death. I do not rush. The sound is a faint, breathy *skritch*, the only new addition to the sound of the world today. I close my eyes for a moment, feeling the vibration of the mark through the bone, through my hand, up my arm. I am not writing. I am amending the present onto the permanence of the past.

I walk back to Khold as the air begins to weep, the condensation tracing its eternal paths down the glassy rock walls. I enter the wide, empty courtyards where other Kholdi have already gathered for the Observing. We stand apart, each of us a solitary island, and we watch the water. We do not hum or click. We are simply present, a collective witness to the one process that will repeat tomorrow exactly as it did today and a thousand years ago.

Back in my niche, I slump against the wall. My father sits, immutable. I look from his Palette to mine, from his dense web of scratches to my own delicate, beginning skein. I am not a scholar or a priest. I am a memory-keeper for a world that cannot forget. I am the one who watches the pendulum swing in a room where time has stopped.

I place my Palette on the stone floor beside my sleeping mat, close to my father's feet. Tomorrow, I will wake. I will breathe the thin, sharp air. I will step over the blue-shard pot. I will walk to the Spur of Unmaking. And I will watch. It is all I am. It is everything.
