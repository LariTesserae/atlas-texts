The first sensation is Weight. It is not the weight of a burden, but the weight of existence, the gravity of a body that is more stone than flesh. I am vast, dense, and cool to the touch compared to the air around me, though my insides churn with a furnace’s patience.

I am a Strider.

I find myself in the shadow of the Great Maker—the dark, ceramic ziggurat that squats in the center of the plateau like a brooding mountain. The air here is thick enough to swim in, a humid soup that tastes of iron and ozone. It presses against my chitinous plates, seeking entry, finding none. My exoskeleton is not made of shell; it is compressed lattice, a walking fortress of silica and alloy.

I lower my head. My neck is a piston of obsidian, grinding silently in its socket. Before me, the flank of the ziggurat weeps. It is not water that runs down its dark, greasy slopes, but vitamines—rich, heavy tears of elemental slurry, the digested earth exhaled by the world’s stomach. This is the blood of the plateau, and I am the vessel that carries it to the extremities.

I extend my tongue. It is not soft muscle, but a proboscis of hardened glass, ridged and durable. I scrape it against the heated ceramic, a sensation that would scream in a world of noise but here merely registers as a deep, resonant vibration ticking up my jaw and into my skull. I drink. I drink the heat. I drink the potential. The slurry is heavy, molten, and packed with the raw materials of creation. It settles into the tanks of my thorax, warming me from the inside out. I am no longer just a creature of the rock; I am a mobile battery, a storehouse of the world’s energy.

Around my feet, the smaller ones swarm. They are the polished black beetles, the macrophages. They scurry over my toes, their tiny legs clicking—a frantic, high-paced Morse code that I barely perceive. They are cleaning me, polishing the mineral deposits that form on my joints, ensuring my mobility remains unhindered. They tickle, but I do not shake them off. They are the nurses of the system, and I am the patient, the carrier, the distant, wandering uncle. We are parts of the same whole, separate only by scale.

I turn. The movement is glacial. A shift of my weight requires a calculation of mass and friction that would take a computer microseconds but takes me a long, satisfying minute. My legs—six of them, pillar-thick and jointed like a crane’s—lift in a sequence that ripples through my body. *Step. Shift. Pause. Settle.*

I begin my march.

I am leaving the heat of the ziggurat, moving out into the sprawling emptiness. The sky above is that bruised, milky white, the sun a diffused glare that casts no shadows. There is no wind, only the heavy, still hand of the atmosphere. I walk for hours, or perhaps days. Time here is measured not in the ticking of clocks, but in the filling and emptying of my internal tanks, in the slow erosion of my footpads against the ground.

I am crossing a barren plain of packed minerals when I feel the shift.

It is not a sound. It is a pressure change, a sudden drop in the density of the air. The dampness loosens its grip on my carapace. I have approached the ribcage.

It towers over me, a skeletal forest of arches and metal lattice, stretching up into the haze. This is the lung. I can feel the rhythmic flexing of the structure even from this distance, a tidal movement of air. It inhales the wet, heavy exhaust of the ziggurat and exhales a cooler, drier breath.

I pause. I lay my sensory organs—long, filigree antennae that quiver like tuning forks—against the ground. I can feel the *thrum* of the world beneath me. That sub-audible heartbeat. It is the thoughts of the Great Mind, the planetary consciousness that dreams us. To listen to it is to know one’s place in the order. I am not an individual. I am a circuit. I am a red blood cell in the veins of a titan.

My task here is simple. I carry the excess. The ziggurat produces too much heat, too much mineral waste. The ribcage needs the raw material to repair its lattice, or perhaps to feed the fungal growths that shelter in its shade. I find a vent, a low aperture expelling the drier air. I unlatch the ventral plates of my abdomen, exposing the crystallized filters within.

I defecate. It is not a filthy act; it is a holy one. I cast out the concentrated blocks of iron and manganese I have transported from the stomach. They clatter to the ground, dark and shining bricks of potential. Immediately, the obsidian beetles descend upon them, beginning the process of breaking them down, aerating the soil, preparing the nutrients for the fungal blooms that will erupt here in the coming cycles.

I have done my part. The mass has been moved. The balance is maintained.

I turn again, oriented by the magnetic pull of the deep earth and the thermal signature of the distant, dark pyramid. I am lighter now. The heaviness in my midsection is gone, replaced by the light, clean feeling of an emptied vessel.

I begin the long walk back.

As I move, the light begins to fail. The bone-white sky darkens to a deep, bruised purple. The world does not cool; if anything, the retained heat of the day rises from the ground like a phantom. But the visual landscape shifts. The fungal colonies in the crevices and shadows begin to wake.

Bioluminescence. Violet. Emerald. Deep, sickly blue.

They pulse in rhythm with the thrumming of the earth. *Thrum-glow. Thrum-glow.* The darkness is alive with a silent, strobing light show. I pass a field of the geometric fungi, spiraling up into the gloom, their light reflecting wetly off my armored flanks. I am a ghost in this machine, a dark silhouette cutting through a garden of radioactive light.

I think, in my slow, plodding way. I think of the soft things. The prompt tells me there are things made of wet flesh that sometimes appear here. "Humans." I have no memory of seeing one, for they are brief, flickering things. If I saw one, I would likely mistake it for a vapor, a momentary fluctuation in the humidity. They burn too bright and die too fast. They are noise in the silence.

I pity them, to the extent that a component can pity a glitch. They are alone. They are discrete, singular, disconnected from the Great Mind. They do not hear the thrum. They do not know the comfort of being a gear in a machine that has been turning since the earth was young.

I reach the ziggurat. The heat washes over me, a welcoming blanket. The rhythmic clanking from within—the hammering of the world's thoughts—calls to me. I am ready to be filled again. I am ready to carry the weight.

I stand still amidst the steam and the silence. I am the Strider. I am the current in the wire. I am the blood in the stone. And I am exactly where I belong.
