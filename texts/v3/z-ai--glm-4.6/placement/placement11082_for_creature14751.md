Where would I find myself? Not lost, for there is no concept of being lost here. There is only being *in place*. I am one of the Polished. My existence is a conversation, held over millennia, with the floor of the bowl.

My waking consciousness is a slow, cool tide that fills my entire being. I do not open eyes, for I have none. I become aware. First, there is the *pressure*—the unyielding weight of my own dark, fibrous body against the sweating stone. It is not a burden but a definition. It is how I know where I end and the world begins. Then there is the *hiss*. It is not a sound that enters me through ears, but one that resonates up through the stone, through my underside, a constant, fertile whisper that is the very grammar of this place. I am hearing the world’s engine breathe.

My memories are not images, but tastes. For an immeasurable span, I have been a taster of waters. My purpose is not just to filter the microscopic life from the stone’s perpetual weeping, but to know the water itself. I carry a living library of mineral signatures, a slow-changing map of the bowl’s health etched into my tissues.

At this moment, I am resting in the slow curve of a rivulet that traces the path of an ancient, long-petrified brother. The water here has a clean, deep taste, the story of stone purified through countless cycles. I am drinking this history, letting it seep into my core. My underside, a dense carpet of microscopic fibers, tightens almost imperceptibly, a single, slow ripple that will, in the passing of a day, move me an inch forward. My current drift takes me towards the pale, gnarled tree with its sponge-bark roots. For decades now, I have been tasting a new note in the water that flows from its direction—a faint, sharp tang, like chilled metal. It is not an impurity, but a changing. A new expression from the tree. I am moving toward it to understand.

My sense of self is spread wide. The edge of my body that faces the high, bleached-blue sky feels the faint warmth of the day, a gentle coaxing that encourages the stone to sweat. The edge that presses against the path of another rivulet feels the minute, rhythmic pressure of a droplet forming, swelling, and finally rolling past, a tactile clock marking a fragment of a second in the vast span of my being. I am not a point of view; I am a landscape of sensation.

Time is not measured in the frantic beat of a heart, but in the major events of the bowl’s life. I remember when the colossal fern near the center was nothing more than a tight, green fist pressed against the stone. I witnessed its centuries-long unfurling, a process so slow it was visible only by the shadow it cast shifting with the seasons. I remember the last deep, subsonic hum from a Polished I call Grey-Ridge, a silent acknowledgement of our century-long proximity as our paths slowly diverged. He was already heavily calcified, his movements almost imperceptible. I do not know if he has finally settled, become a permanent feature of the western wall, or if he still drifts. I do not need to know. We are all part of the same slow flow.

As I move, my thoughts are not a swift internal monologue. They are the gentle sum of these sensations. The metallic tang grows stronger. It is a flavor of something being born, some new process within the tree. Is it altering the water it drinks? Or is it exuding something new? The question does not frustrate me. It is the very current that pulls me forward. My purpose is to reach it, to press my soft flank against the tree's drinking roots and to taste its secret directly. It will take me another ten years to reach its base, perhaps five more to feel the first of its shed drops upon my upper surface. This is not delay. This is the journey.

I feel the beginning of my own end, not as a sadness, but as a quiet confirmation. My highest, most-exposed edge has begun to lose a fraction of its suppleness. Under the constant caress of the air and the sun, it is hardening. The dark grey-green is deepening towards the polished obsidian of a true stone. In a few centuries, this part of me will no longer flow. It will anchor me permanently. My drift will cease. I will become a new obstruction for the water, a new guardian in the silent landscape.

And this is right. It is the fulfillment of the only law that matters here: that life must eventually turn back into stone. That the shepherds must one day join the flock they tended. I will become part of the bowl itself, my body a testament to the long, slow, and beautifully patient act of becoming. For now, I ripple. I taste. I move. I am the slow, flowing mind of a green, sweating world.
