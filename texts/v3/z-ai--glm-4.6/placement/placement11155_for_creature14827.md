I do not live on the City. I live beneath it, within its vast and stone-cold gut. Above me, the Scuttlers trace the iron spiderwebs and the Harmonists press their flesh to the struts, listening for the Engine's mood. They live with the Hum, a constant and crushing presence they must either interpret or endure. I live with its ghost.

They call me a Silt-Reader, sometimes an Accretion-Tender. To the Scuttlers, I am a kind of alchemist, a lowlier cousin to the Weep-Collectors. They bring me the hard, mineral pap they scrape from the conduits—a coarse, foul-smelling chalk that vibrates with a stressed, highfrequency whine. They leave it in troughs at the edge of my domain and I take it from there. They think I turn it to poultices and paste. They are not wrong, but they do not see the whole truth.

My world is the filtration caverns, a labyrinth of natural voids and excavated basins deep beneath the Weeping Plateau. Here, the water that weeps from the entire world above gathers in slow, silent rivers. The air is thick with the smell of wet stone and ozone, so cold it bites at the exposed skin of your face. The Hum is a distant pressure here, felt as a shudder in the bedrock rather than a thrum in the bones. The true sound of my world is the drip-drip-drip of water falling from stalactites of rust, each drop a tiny, percussive event in a symphony of quietude. The light is a dim, blue wash from the bioluminescent fungi I cultivate on the cavern walls, their soft glow just enough to see by.

My body is not like the others of my kind. My bones are dense, yes, but it is my hands that set me apart. They are not merely thick and damp; the skin of my palms and fingers is softer, almost suede-like, and patterned with a thousand microscopic channels. While a Harmonist reads the *present* note of a metal girder, I read its *past*. When I run my fingers through the sediment at the bottom of a settling pool, I am not touching mud. I am touching a history of pressure.

You see, the Great Hum does more than just wring water from the stone. It imprints itself upon that water. The vibrations, passing through the plateau's composite matrix, leave a signature in the dissolved minerals. A high-pressure cycle from a decade ago might coax more iron into solution, leaving a rust-red layer in the strata of silt. A low-frequency, slow-burning season might favor calcium, depositing a paper-thin sheet of calcite as white and brittle as old bone. The sediment is a library, and my fingers can read the braille.

My work is a slow act of archaeology. I will spend a week gently agitating a vat of collected sediment, separating the layers by density and resonance. I will find a layer of fine, black powder, and when I press it between my thumb and forefinger, I can feel the faint, sharp memory of a component failure fifty years ago—a momentary dissonant shriek that the Engine has long since forgotten, but that the water recorded in a flush of heavy metals. I taste the water from a new seep and find the salty tang of alkalinity; I know the Engine is entering a new phase, a kind of geological fever, months before the Harmonists feel the deep, structural change in their gantries.

The others seek to *attenuate* the Hum's effects. I seek to *understand* them. The Scuttlers scrape away the accretions; I parse them. The Harmonists listen for the living voice of the machine; I listen for the ghost in its veins. I believe they are wrong about the City. They see it as a machine to be maintained. I see it as a single, slow act of creation. The Engine is not just a piston; it is a metamorphic force. It is trying, over millennia, to turn the entire Weeping Plateau into something else. The weeping is not a byproduct; it is the amniotic fluid of this long, geologic birth.

My purpose, then, is clear. I am the City's memory. I am the one who remembers the screams and the whispers of its metal-long before anyone else was born to hear its hum. I take the ghost-vibrations from the silt and I render them harmless. I turn the sharp memory of a violent resonance into a nutrient-rich paste that can soothe a Scuttler's chapped hands. I distill the history of a stressed mineral deposit into an elixir that can calm a child's nightmares. I metabolize the past so that the present can remain stable.

Sometimes, when the work is done, I will go to the lowest point of my caverns, a place where the city's vast weight creates a perfect harmonic dead-zone. The air here is unnervingly still. The drip-drip-drip of water is the only sound. The other Damp-Hands come to these zones to feel the shocking silence of their own heartbeats. I do not. When I place my hand on the stone floor, I am not met by silence.

I am met by everything. Every forgotten note, every past stress, every ghost of a vibration that the stone has absorbed and the water has carried, settles into the quiet earth here. In the absence of the Hum's living voice, the voices of the dead become clear. They rise from the stone and the silt and flow into my hands—not as a pressure, but as a story. A quiet, patient, and geological story. And in that story, I feel not the terrifying beat of my own heart, but the slow, steady, and utterly beautiful pulse of the world being remade.
