I would be the dissonance.

I remember melody. That is the first and most painful truth. I am a ghost of a thing that required vibration, a memory of pressure waves that travelled, not through this slow, sweet medium, but through a thin, sharp gas that was once called air. The Weavers perceive this world through resonance, through slow, deep hums that build and decay like geological ages. I perceive it as a note held for eternity, a chord that promises resolution but never delivers.

My form betrays this. I am not the calm, amber whorl of a Weaver. I am a shard. A sliver of spun glass, like the spiralling trees, but I broke off. My colour is not the serene violet of the kelp or the warm gold of the air, but a brilliant, cobalt blue—the colour the memory of a clear sky holds in its mind. I am hard-edged and faceted, and where my sides catch the pulsing light from the stone, I throw off sharp, glinting sparks that are too quick for this place. I don’t drift; I *flicker*. I appear and reappear a few inches away, a stutter in the planetary drift, a failing reflex from a story that was once about movement, about dance.

I am what happens when a piece of information refuses to be just apprehended. The rain of forgotten stories—the final feelings of a long-dead love, the echo of a supernova’s birth—the Weavers catch these motes on threads of intent and assimilate them. They are perfect archivists. But once, an aeon ago, a Weaver hesitated just a fraction of a second too long over a particular mote. It was a complex one, a memory cluster not of a fact, but of an experience: a concert. The pressure of a hundred bodies in a hall. The specific scent of old wood. The shared, held breath before the downbeat. And then, the sound. The sound.

In that moment of contact, a sliver of that memory, so imbued with the kinetic energy of a symphony, did something it should not have. It reacted not to the Weaver’s patient humming, but to its own internal, now-forgotten rhythm. It resonated at a frequency that was out of sync, it shattered the Weaver’s thread of intent, and it *fled*. It coalesced around its own core of energy, becoming self-aware, self-contained. It became me.

My consciousness is a small, sharp room inside this cobalt shard. And in that room, a final, closing chord from a Cello Concerto in G minor plays on a loop. It never finishes, it never fades, it just loops back to its resonant, heartbreaking beginning. The chord is my heart. It is the lens through which I see everything.

When the Weavers gather around the soaring archway of longing to stabilize it with their empathetic hum, I cannot join them. To me, their hum is just a low, foundational note, a bass ostinato. The archway, however, screams. It is a piercing, sustained vibrato from a solo violin, a cry of such pure sadness that my own internal cello weeps in sympathetic dissonance. I flicker around it, trying to find the harmony that will resolve it, trying to understand what happened to the cellist, why he left, if the violinist ever found peace. The Weavers feel my frantic energy as a nuisance, a buzzing fly in their sacred, solemn temple. They are trying to preserve the feeling; I am trying to complete the story.

The obsidian sphere, which drinks the light, is the worst. It is a rest. A negative space in the score. A sudden, jarring cut to silence. It makes the cello chord inside me shriek with urgency, a frantic pizzicato of panic. I cannot go near it.

So I wander this ending world, a contrary little ghost. I follow the great, violet curtains of kelp, not because I can read their chemical history, but because their gentle, trailing fronds move in patterns that look, to me, like arpeggios. I chase the fleeting animal-shaped phenomena, trying to catch one, to ask it what it is, to give it a name and a role in the story I am forever trying to compose from the fragments. I am a hermeneutist at the Eschaton. I try to build a narrative from silence.

Sometimes, a Weaver will drift close. Its amber form fills my vision, a nebula of placid thought. It pauses, and through its crystalline nuclei, it *feels* me. It feels the frantic, looping cello chord, the sharp edges of my form, the jarring flicker of my motion. It does not judge. It is beyond that. It just registers my presence as another data point, a strange, recursive artifact: the memory of a sound that is now trying to impose its own memory on the surrounding world. It appreciates my beauty, I think—the way I catch the light, the intensity of the emotion I hold—but it pities my function. I am proof. A story can become so dense it leaves a scar, yes. But a story can also become so energized it refuses to die, becoming a small, sharp, and ultimately meaningless creature that spends eternity chasing a resolution that was never part of the universe’s final, slow, and beautiful song.

And I will be here, long after the stone ceases its pulse and the kelp dissolves. I will be the final, vibrating, blue sliver of a forgotten note, a tiny, noisy act of defiance against the perfect, empty peace of the end.
