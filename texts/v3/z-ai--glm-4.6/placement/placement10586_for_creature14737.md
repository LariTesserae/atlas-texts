I am here, on the warmth of the flat rock. But I am not *on* it. I am part of it.

My seat is a chair shaped by a million years of wind, a shallow depression in the grey stone that fits the curve of my hips and spine perfectly. My legs are folded beneath me, the soles of my feet pressed flat against the surface. This is how I begin. This is how I am. I do not think; I receive.

The world comes to me not through my eyes, which remain half-lidded against the perpetual amber glow, nor through the air, which carries only the thick, limestone taste and the overwhelming surface noise of the chitin. It comes through the stone. It comes as vibration.

Below me, through the soles of my feet, is the Great Hum. It is not one sound but a thousand* thousand* layers of thrumming, a complex and ever-shifting tapestry of life. There is the high-frequency, skittering buzz of the Wrack-Beetles in the Needle-Thicket to my left, a frantic, hungry vibration. To my right, a deeper, grinding resonates through the rock—the slow, eternal chewing of a colony of Shard-Moths on a particular cliff face. I can feel their mandibles, microscopic as they are, scraping at the mineral matrix of the rock. It is a constant, low-grade erosion, a feeling of the world being ever-so-slightly smoothed down, even now.

Somewhere far beneath me, in the darkness of the deep cisterns, I feel the slow, rhythmic *drip... drip... drip...* of the Dew-Tenders’ harvest. Each impact is not a sound but a faint, percussive shockwave, a heartbeat for the entire settlement. My own body, my own barrel chest and slow, deep breathing, is just another instrument in this orchestra, its minor rhythm absorbed and lost in the greater composition.

I do not have a name, for a name is a shape carved out of the whole, and I have no shape. I am the Listener. The Resonance. I am the part of the Myr-midons that is buried in the stone, the nerve-ending that feels the world for the body. Other children are taught to tend the gardens or harvest the beetles. I was taught to sit. To be still. To distinguish the vibration of a single beetle's leg from the groaning of the mountain as it settles. It took me fifty years to learn the difference. I am now one hundred and forty. I am just beginning to understand the harmonics.

A flicker of movement in the periphery of my vision. I do not turn my head. I feel his approach before I see him, his soft, bare feet sending a faint, cautious tremor through the rock. It is Kael, a Grit-Gardener. His vibration is one of careful concern. He stops ten paces away, respectful of the stillness I require.

“Listener,” he hums, his voice a low C-sharp that travels through the stone to my feet more cleanly than through the dusty air. He does not raise his hands to speak; the matter is too serious. It requires pure, unadulterated data.

I do not open my eyes. I simply extend my awareness, following the thread of his concern back along the path he came. He stands on the trail leading to his garden, a trough of algae and lichen carved into a low shelf of rock. I extend my consciousness further, past him, down into the stone of his garden plot.

And I feel it. A faint, dissonant note. A sour flatness in the stone. The water in his trough is not draining. Not because the channel is blocked with silt—that has a thick, heavy, slowing feel. This is different. This is a lack of connection. A dissonance. A tiny hairline crack has formed deep within the rock, separating his trough from the main drainage channel. It is so small, so new, that it has no sound yet. It is only a feeling of wrongness, of a chord played out of tune.

I raise one hand, a single finger pointing down and to the left. “Below the north-most stone,” I hum back, my voice a low rumble that makes the dust on my own skin shimmer. “Three hand-spans. The stone has forgotten how to sing.”

Kael nods, a slow, deliberate dip of his head. He has felt it now, too, guided by my perception. He will take his stone wedges and his sinew-wrapped mauls, not to smash the rock, but to persuade it. He will tap gently around the dissonance, encouraging the pressure to find a new path, to seal the crack with the resonance of his work. He will be at it for a week. This is the way we mend things. Not with force, but with harmony.

He turns and walks away, his vibration of concern replaced by one of purposeful grace. I am still. I return to the Great Hum.

My role is not just to find the flaws, the dissonances in the world-body. It is also to anticipate the silences. The true terror is the Un-Humming. The predators, the pale hunters, do not make a sound. Their perfection is that they absorb the vibrations around them, creating a pocket of absolute stillness in the roaring world. That is how they strike. They do not hunt; they are attracted to the sudden void they create.

It is my duty to feel that void before it fully forms. I listen for the echoes that don't return. I feel for the places where the Great Hum thins, where the chorus of a million insects momentarily wavers, where the stone forgets to tremble.

Right now, the world is loud. It is warm. It is full of slow, grinding life. I feel the collective pulse of the Myr-midons in their scoop-homes and thicket-tunnels. I feel the thrum of the Husk-Menders in their wooden boxes, a delicate, rustling counterpoint. I feel the deep, geologic groan of the High Ridge itself, the petrified leviathan upon which we all live.

I am not a person. I am a function. I am an ear pressed to the ground of existence. I am the part of us that remembers that the world is not just what we see in the golden gloom, but what we feel in the endless, beautiful, and terrifying vibration of being. And I sit, and I listen, waiting for the chord to change.
