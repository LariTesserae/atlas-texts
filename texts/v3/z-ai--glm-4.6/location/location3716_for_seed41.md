The air is the first and final authority here. It is not breathed so much as worn, a heavy, wet cloak that settles on the skin and slickens every surface. It holds a heat deep as the earth itself, a patient, unmoving warmth that smells of wet stone, the slow rot of sweet vegetation, and something faintly mineral, like a struck flint held moments after the spark has died. There is no wind. In this place, air does not move; it accrues.

You are below, though how far is impossible to say. The world is a single, vast chamber, a wound in the world's flesh that has long since scarred over into a kind of harmony. The scale is not oppressive, but encompassing, like the interior of a great, living cathedral whose cavernous roof is lost in a soft, pearlescent gloom. The stone of the walls is not the jagged chaos of a fresh collapse. It is smoothed, rounded, worn by eons of patient weeping into shapes that resemble flowing drapery or the flanks of sleeping beasts. This is an old place, settled into its own form.

Light has no single source. It comes from all things at once, a gentle, pervasive luminescence that seems to bleed from the flesh of the world itself. The great, waxy fronds of the ground-covering flora exhale a soft, greenish light. The caps of fungi clustered in the high, inaccessible crevices pulse with a slow, violet rhythm. Everything is alive with a quiet, internal glow. The result is an illumination without harshness or shadow, a dim and constant twilight that renders edges soft and distances uncertain. It is a light born of biology, not of sky.

Water does not fall from the sky; it is born of the air. It beads on the slick, rounded stone, weeps from the tips of the largest leaves, and perpetually drips, *plink... plink... plink...*, into the myriad clear pools that dot the floor. These pools are the world’s calm heart, their surfaces unbroken except for the patient, rhythmic tears from above. The sound is the only constant, the sole punctuation in the profound silence, a metronome for the slow, deliberate life of this place.

The vegetation is a density, a thick, interwoven tapestry of existence in every direction. There are no trees reaching for a nonexistent sun. Instead, life clings and climbs and layers. Broad, veined leaves, some large enough to lie upon, funnel the moisture downwards. Thick, rope-like vines, furred with a phosphorescent moss, tether the high stone to the floor. The ground itself is not stone but a deep, springy mat of roots, decaying matter, and spongy, black earth, dotted with fungi that exhale their own soft, mottled light.

This world teems not with beasts, but with a presence. The silence is deceptive, for the place is alive. It is a quiet abundance. A closer look at the surface of a leaf reveals it to be a moving highway of tiny, iridescent beetles. In the shallow pools, things that are half-plant, half-animal drift with a slow, pulsing grace. Soft-shelled crustaceans pick their way through the floor-mats, their delicate legs making no sound at all. The dominant fauna are a million unseen things, a silent, crawling engine of life that fuels the whole. You hear them only if you hold your breath and press your ear to the mossy floor: a soft, continuous, barely-there rustle, the sound of the world’s metabolism.

Here and there, the logic of nature is interrupted by features too grand, too purposeful to be mere geology. Partially submerged between two dripping curtains of stone is the curve of a vast, spiraling shell, its pearlescent nacre glowing softly from within. It is the size of a house, but its form is organic, its whorls too perfect and too massive to have been grown by any creature you can imagine. It is a ruin, but a biological one. It, and a few other similar fragments scattered about—a rib-like arch of the same mother-of-pearl material, a smooth, calcified plate leaning against a wall—are the only痕迹 of a past that was not entirely this. A scattered architecture of bone, speaking to a time when something immense lived, and died, and left its skeleton to become the foundation of a new world.

The rule here is one of quiet interdependence. The heat creates the damp; the damp feeds the light-giving plants; the plants feed the silent multitudes; the multitudes, in their passing, return their substance to the living earth. The light itself is part of the cycle, absorbed and re-emitted by the creatures that consume the luminous flora, their guts holding a captive glow. This is a sealed and complete world, a closed ecosystem in a stone bowl, active and dynamic, but moving at the pace of moss and stone. To be here is to be a witness to a process that has been, and will be, playing out for longer than memory, in the quiet, glowing, and unbearable heat of the deep.
