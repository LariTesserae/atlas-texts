The pressure is the first truth. It is not a weight but an absence, the negation of all other sensation, a universal hug from a directionless ocean. The cold is the second: a patient, pervasive chill that has leached all haste from the universe, leaving only the slow, geological metabolism of the truly deep. Here, in the high submerged ranges, the water is so pure and still it feels less like a liquid and more like a lens for viewing eternity.

There is no ground, not in any biological sense. The world is a floor. It stretches for miles, a vast, high plateau on what must once have been a continental shelf. But it is not stone. It is smoothed beyond the possibility of natural erosion, a flawless, dark, glassy surface with the deep, interior luster of obsidian. It bears no scars, no sediment, no fossil. It simply *is*. To run a hand—or its equivalent—across it is to feel a substance that is absolutely uniform, a material so old and settled it has surrendered all texture to the unimaginable ages it has endured. This is the "no-natural-substrate," a foundation so deeply built it has become the only nature left.

Rising from this black glass sea-floor are the features that give the landscape its meaning. They are the "mountains" of this high-elevation world. Colossal, perfectly hexagonal towers that climb up towards a ceiling of water so far above it may as well be the void. They too are built, the same seamless, dark material, but they are not inert. Their surfaces are veined with faint, dead lattices of what might have once been light or power. They are the ruins of a architecture so vast it was mistaken for geology, and so ancient the distinction has ceased to matter. This is a world where everything is made, where the last natural atom was paved over eons ago.

There is no sun. The only light is alive. Vast, attenuated creatures, like sheets of living aurora, drift through the depths. They are the Wraiths, the main body of the world’s fauna. They are miles long, tissue-thin, and move with the implacable slowness of continental plates. Their bodies pulse with a soft, shifting, internal luminescence—nebulae of pale blue, green, and violet. They do not seem to hunt or eat; they simply wander, their biolumes casting sweeping, silent searchlights across the spires and the glassy plain. Their passage is the primary event, the slow calendar of this world. To see a Wraith turn and expose its softer, under-lit belly is to witness a dawn that lasts for a week.

The soundscape is a study in almost-silence. The crushing pressure damps all noise. For long, unmeasurable stretches, there is only the theoretical sound of your own existence, a thought in the quiet. Then, a Wraith will shift its immense form, and a sound will travel—not a roar, but a deep, resonant *hummmm*, a cello note scaled to the size of a mountain, felt more in the bones than heard with the ears. It is the sound of a planet sighing in its sleep.

But the silence is a lie. It is the static background against which the true, chaotic nature of the world plays out. This is the turbulence. Life here is not the Wraiths. Life is the Skitterers.

When a Wraith's light washes over a spire, it reveals the true dynamic. The surface is not smooth. It is teeming. Swarming.Covering every inch of the ancient towers are crystalline, insectoid creatures, each no larger than a fingernail. They are the abundant but sparse fauna, sparse in their distribution across the vast emptiness, but abundant in their localized multitudes. They are the planet's immune system, or perhaps its stomach. Their geometric legs make tiny, ceaseless, flicking motions, and their mandibles, harder than diamond, are always engaged.

The Skitterers are eating the world.

They scrape and chew at the towers, breaking down the eons-old material into glittering, microscopic dust. This is the erosion: not water on rock, but life on artifact. Their ceaseless, frantic activity against the backdrop of infinite slowness and stillness is the region’s core turbulence. A close listen—and one must be close—in the brief moments of Wraith-light reveals not silence, but a constant, high-frequency *ticking*, the sound of a billion microscopic chisels. It is the sound of a city being unhoused, one grain at a time. They create no waste. The dust they create—the powdered essence of the built world—is the food for a second, even smaller order of life, translucent amoeboid things that drift in the clouds created by the Skitterers' work.

There are no rules of biology here, only rules of chemistry. No plant life can gain a purchase, because the ground itself is consumable. The ecology is a simple, brutal, and beautiful equation: the great dead towers are transformed by the frantic living Skitterers into sustenance for the drifting micro-fauna, who are in turn occasionally filtered from the water by the immensely slow, placid Wraiths as they pass.

It is a world that is utterly alone and utterly crowded. A tomb that is being constantly devoured. A place of absolute silence and the frantic screams of a billion mouths you cannot hear. As the last light from a passing Wraith fades, the Skitterers on the spire are plunged back into darkness, but they do not stop. In the absolute cold and the immense pressure, their work continues, patient and relentless, erasing a masterpiece of creation with the only tools available: life, and time. It is a world that has died, and is only now, in its ruin, discovering how to live.
