The first thing is the sound. It is not a noise, not a din, but a physical presence, a pressure against the skin and a vibration in the bones. It is a solid wall of life, a cacophony so dense it becomes a single, roaring entity. It is the shriek of a thousand soaring things, the percussive bang of bodies colliding in the air, the chittering and guttural calls that form its bass notes, and an impossibly high-frequency thrumming that feels like the pain of a tooth. To be silent here would be to be deaf. To speak is to shout into a maelstrom that simply absorbs you.

You are standing on a surface that is not stone, not soil, but the ancient, ossified substance of a plant. It is fibrous and incredibly tough, like woody coral that has petrified over eons. The ground beneath your feet is a single, continuous mass, but it does not stretch flat. It rises into colossal, fluted pillars that push up into the sky like the trunks of petrified gods. These are the Pillars. They are the world. There is no other ground.

The air is warm and perpetually damp, carrying the smell of wet rot and blooming pollen. The sky is a distant, bright ceiling far, far above, but the Pillars are so tall their summits are lost in a constant, roiling mist of condensed cloud. Water does not fall from the sky as rain, not exactly. Instead, the immense surfaces of the Pillars act as condensers. A fine sheen of moisture perpetually slicks their sides, and everywhere, droplets gather and fall, a slow, silent, endless rain that plinks into the unseen abysses between the structures. The vegetation is total, yet the space is empty. The Pillars themselves are the plants, the final form of a world’s flora, grown to mountainous scale and then settling into a slow, geological decay. Clinging to their sides are living things—a network of thick, ropey vines, curtains of moss so deep they have their own gravity, and great, lung-like lichens that pulse with a faint, moist light in the gloom between the day and night cycles.

The scale is impossible to judge. A Pillar that seems a hundred paces away could be the size of a small city, the sense of distance destroyed by the sheer verticality. The gaps between them are vast canyons of empty air, misty and terrifying. To fall from one of the narrow ledges is not to hit the ground, but to disappear into the sound, into the frenetic cloud.

And this is where the fauna resides. The overwhelming life, the source of the deafening sound, is an ecosystem of the air. It is a vertical world. There are creatures with vast, membranous wings that ride the updrafts between the Pillars, their shadows flickering across the mists. There are things that leap and glide, spreading flaps of skin to travel hundreds of body-lengths in a single bound. There are swarms of smaller, insect-like beings that move in glittering, chaotic clouds, and things that prey upon them, diving through the clouds like feathered arrows. The sound is their conversation, their hunting cries, their territorial warnings, their mating songs—a three-dimensional symphony of death and desire.

Faint upon the surface of the immense Pillars are the marks of other tenants. Primitive, scattered structures. Not buildings, but modifications. A perfectly smooth, circular basin carved two dozen feet across, perpetually filling with condensation. A series of hand-and-toe-holds chiseled into a vertical face, leading up to a hollowed-out chamber. A weathered platform woven from the same vines that grow on the Pillar’s surface, its ropes tied with knots of breathtaking complexity. These are the artifacts of a people who did not try to conquer this place, but to live within its geometry. Their tools were simple—stone flints, sharpened bone, woven fiber. Their presence is a whisper against the planet-scale roar.

The erosion here is ancient, a process so slow it is nearly static. The Pillars are not growing; they are eroding. Fine dust, the powdered remains of this ancient plant-matter, settles in the crevices. Great, vertical fissures scar their sides where, over millennia, the structure has begun to give way to its own impossible weight and the endless, patient attrition of the damp wind. The world is in its final form, a colossal skeleton colonized by a vibrant, deafening explosion of life. It is a paradox: a landscape at the absolute end of its geological life, yet vibrating with more biological activity than perhaps anywhere else. To be here is to stand on the corpse of a world while it screams with the full-throated sound of its inheritors.
