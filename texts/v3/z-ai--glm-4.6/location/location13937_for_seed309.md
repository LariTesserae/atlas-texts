***

The first thing you learn is the sound. Or rather, you learn that you cannot listen to it. You can only let it happen to you. To try and pick out a single source from the chorus is to go mad; it’s a solid architectural feature of the place, as much a wall as the stone that encloses you. It is a deep, organ-grinder thrum, overlaid with a billion-fingered, chittering static, punctuated by the percussive *clack-hiss* of machinery and the slow, resonant *drip… drip…* of water falling from a ceiling you cannot see.

The air is the next lesson. It’s warm and thick enough to chew, tasting of damp iron, sweet decay, and the sharp, clean bite of ozone. You breathe it in and it coats your lungs, a humid film that is both nourishing and cloying. This is the breath of the Heartwood.

We exist below the skin of the world, in a single, vast cavern—something between a geode and a collapsed lung. The scale is cathedralic, but defined. From the central fungal platforms of the settlement, you can just make out the distant, curved walls, shimmering with a faint, mineral luminescence, and the root-like buttresses that vanish into a gloom so complete it seems a solid substance. Here and there, a harsher, more artificial light cuts through the dimness: the actinic blue-white of an arc lamp, the angry orange glow of a furnace, the steady chemical green of a status flare. These are the industrial constellations of this world.

There is no soil. The ground is a single, spongy substrate, a dark, resilient mesh of mycelium and compressed dendritic matter. It gives underfoot like a worn leather saddle. Across its expanse, the growth is sparse. You can walk for a hundred meters in a straight line, your boots making soft, sucking sounds in the damp matting. But the emptiness is an illusion.

It is a vertical world. `Vegetation` is not a coating; it is the structure. Sprouting from the mycelial floor are the Pillars. These are the true occupants of the cavern, colossal, slow-moving trees that have been trained, grafted, and coaxed over millennia into the shapes of buttresses, bridges, and arches. They are not wood in the way you know it, but a layered, living composite of bark, extruded ceramic, and woven fiber. Their bark is etched with glowing patterns of lichen, and their hollow cores hum with the circulation of sap under immense pressure. The settlement—our town—is built across the widest of these colossal platforms, broad, flat-topped fungi that have been encouraged to grow in a network, their gills venting warm, spicy steam into the cavern's air. The "buildings" are not constructed so much as pruned into being, their walls polished wood, their roofs woven from water-shedding fronds lit from within by bioluminescent moss.

This is where `urban` meets `total vegetation`. We are not separate from the Pillars; we are their symbiotes, their gardeners, their nervous system. And our `tech` is the proof. Look up. Clinging to the sides of the Pillars like barnacles on a leviathan are the factories. They are not brick and steel, but chitinous pods and silken domes, pulsing with a faint internal light. Their function is inseparable from the ecosystem. Vast, slow-slung presses harvest the tough outer bark of the Pillars, grinding it into a nutrient slurry that is fed back into the mycelial floor. Great, suspended looms, manned by buzzing insect-like drones, weave the Pillars' own sap-fibers into the cables and sheets we use for everything from clothing to sail-cloth. The powerful engines, whose thrum forms the bass note of the cavern's symphony, do not burn coal. They are distilleries, vast copper-and-glass apparatuses that ferment the sugary nectar of the high-canopy flowers, turning it into a volatile, glowing fuel that powers the pumps and lights the workshops. The `water` we need is not collected in wells but gathered in vast, condensation-capturing nets strung between the Pillars, where the warm, damp air precipitates a constant, fine rain that trickles down channels carved directly into the living wood.

The life here, the `fauna`, is the true chaos within the order. It is overwhelming. The air itself is thick with life: clouds of translucent, winged shards that drift on the updrafts, their carapaces humming like tuning forks; spindle-spiders that cast conductive nets between the factory-pods, their threads glinting with captured electricity; darting, fanged manta-like flyers that navigate by the industrial lights, their calls like tearing silk. On the ground, herds of many-legged beasts, their bodies plated with scavenged ceramic, graze on the tougher fungi, their movements a slow, grinding tide. They are not wild, not entirely. We are their shepherds and their butchers. Their chitin and sinew are other raw materials. We do not dominate them; we are a niche in their overwhelming ecology, the predator, the parasite, the fellow symbiote.

The `erosion` you see is not one of decay but of integration. Nothing is lost. The waste from the composters feeds the floor. The ash from the furnaces is mixed into the slurry to harden the new growth on the Pillars. The shed carapaces of the grazing herds are carted away to the looms, where they are spun into armor or tool handles. Everything is a conduit, a trade. The sound is the sound of this constant, frantic exchange.

And it is all active, in a state of constant, dynamic motion. The light from the arc lamps flickers as power surges through the spider-nets. The ground trembles as a new section on a distant Pillar is brought online, its harvesters kicking to life with a shuddering groan. A migratory swarm of the sliver-flyers will pass through, their sheer numbers blotting out the dim bioluminescence for an hour, their swarm-song a deafening wave that forces the work to halt, everyone huddling under ceramic-sheltered eaves until the tide passes. This is the weather of this world: biological and mechanical cycles intertwined.

The strangest thing, the true `weirdness` that still makes the old-timers pause, is the grafting. The newest factory, the one designed to produce the refined crystals needed for the focusing lamps, is not being built. It is being *grown*. A scaffold of living wood was coaxed into a complex lattice years ago. Now, a specialized species of grazer is brought in. It feeds on a specific fungus, and its digestive tract secretes a mineral-rich paste. The beasts are encouraged to crawl over the scaffold, seeping this paste onto the wood. Over months, the paste catalyzes the wood, transforming it into a crude, living ceramic. Workers, protected by thick, leathery suits, come and guide the process, scraping away excesses, directing the flow. The factory is, in essence, being pooped into existence by a living machine, a process halfway between sculpture and digestion.

To stand in the Heartwood is to stand in the engine room of a god, the gut of a world. It is loud, it is dim, it is damp and alive with a relentless, crawling, flying, growing, grinding, building, dying, and recomposing force. It is a place that has found its balance not in stillness, but in a perpetual, roaring, and beautiful form of consumption. You don't just live here; you are processed. You are a part of the hum.
