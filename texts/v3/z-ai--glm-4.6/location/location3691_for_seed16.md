There is no sun here. Not a true one. Light is a secondary quality, a consequence of heat, a sullen, red-glow seeping up from the floor of the world and a faint, cool phosphorescence emanating from the very air. To stand here is to be at the bottom of a bowl, a deep and immense concavity scooped from the flesh of the planet. This is the Chime-Deep.

The floor is a mosaic of cooled, black glass and jagged slag, cracked by the planet's slow breath. From these cracks, the world's voice begins: a constant, low-frequency hum that you feel in your bones more than you hear with your ears. This is the foundational note. Superimposed upon it are the sharps and flats of the place—percussive hisses as jets of superheated, mineral-laden gas erupt from fissures, screeching like a thousand kettles boiling at once. These plumes of vapor, dense and smelling of hot metal and ozone, rise into the dimness before cooling and dissipating, adding their chemical mass to the choked atmosphere.

This is the water of this world, not a liquid but a hot, greedy vapor. And where there is sustenance, there is life. But not life as we know it. The "lushness" of the Chime-Deep is a garden of glass and crystal. Towering stalks, as tall as primordial trees, grow from the rock. They are not green but a deep, bruised purple, their surfaces faceted and shimmering, diffracting the ruddy glow into a thousand miniature rainbows. They rise in silent, competing forests, their roots sunk deep into the thermal veins below.

Clustering around their bases and carpeting the less volatile ground are thick, fibrous mats of a paler, almost white crystalline moss. When the hot vapors pass over it, it glitters with an internal, cold light, a bioluminescence born of chemical conversion, not photosynthesis. It is a slow, patient form of devouring. The air itself is so saturated with the raw stuff of life that these crystal-growths can simply breathe it in. There is a strange, terrible beauty to it, a landscape of hard, sharp, and delicate things, all thriving in suffocating heat.

And woven through this mineral jungle is the city.

The Chime-Deep was once, or perhaps still is, called Khos-Adun. It is not built so much as it is carved. Immense, tiered structures are hewn directly from the caldera walls, their balconies and buttresses projecting into the hollow space. Bridges of stone, impossibly thin and graceful, span chasms that vent the planet's fire. Great plazas, paved with obsidian-chip mosaics, are now buckled and cracked by the slow, stubborn pressure of the crystal-moss growing between the stones.

The architecture is primitive, but on a monolithic scale. It speaks of a people with infinite time and stone tools, of hands that could persuade basalt into lacework. Yet it is empty. The density of the place is an illusion. For all its sprawling urbanity, there is a profound and echoing hollowness. The spaces between the towers and the carven cliffs are vast, the silence between the geothermal shrieks deep and resonant. It is a ghost city, but one that is not decaying. It has reached an equilibrium.

The crystal flora is the new occupant and maintainer. The purple stalks grow in great columns inside temple halls, their tips brushing against ceilings a hundred feet up. The glowing moss carpets the thoroughfares, its slow expansion holding the cracked paving stones in a tight, living embrace. The city is not ruins; it is a fossil being slowly repurposed, its architecture now the trellis for another kind of growth, entirely alien.

And so it is loud. The name "Chime-Deep" is no metaphor. The primary sound, the one that gives the place its character, is the music of the city itself. The crystals are sensitive to temperature, expanding as the vents hiss and contracting when the vapors drift past. This constant micro-motion creates an endless, shifting symphony. A forest of purple stalks nearby plays a low, organ-like drone. The fibrous mats on the plazas emit a gentle, glassy tinkling, like a thousand wind chimes stirred by a gentle, hot breeze. And from the towering structures themselves, where great, plate-like crystals have colonized the stonework, come deep, resonant gongs and clear, bell-like tones that hang in the hot, dead air for long moments.

It is a world without animals, without birdsong or insect chatter, without water's soft babble. Its soundscapes are geological and mineral. It is an active place, a dynamic system. The planet's heart beats, the vents breathe, the crystals grow and chime their slow song of existence. To be here is to be inside a machine of stone and glass, running on heat and chemistry, a grand and terrible instrument playing a music older and more patient than any life that has ever walked. The rules are simple: endure heat, breathe the vapor, grow slowly or not at all, and listen to the endless, evolving chime of a world without water, but full of life.
