There is no ground, only floor. It’s a fine distinction, but one the place insists upon. The pressure is immense and absolute, a weight that has been here for longer than mountains, a cold that feels less like a temperature and more like a fundamental quality of the substance you are suspended within: the water. It is not turbulent, not flowing in any direction a creature could use, but a still, medium-thick ocean that fills a space so vast it has long forgotten the concept of a surface.

The floor is made of stone. Great, tessellated tiles, each one the size of a city plaza, are fitted together with a precision that feels impossible for their immense scale. They are not clean. Eons of immersion have worn their surfaces to a slippery, rounded smoothness, but the edges where they meet remain sharp, defining a grid that stretches into a darkness where light has never been. This is the substrate. The entire seabed, for a distance that defies measurement, is a single, continuous structure of megalithic masonry. It was laid down by hands that knew only simple tools: levers, ropes, chisels of harder stone. It is a work of primitive technology taken to a planetary, insane conclusion. A ziggurat built to be the very bed of an ocean.

From the seams between these titanic stones, the "vegetation" grows.

It is not lush in the way of a jungle, vibrant and quick. It is lush in the way of accumulation, of density. It is a forest of slow, patient life. Primary among these are the great stalks. They rise from the cracks in the floor like slender, silent towers, some reaching hundreds of feet towards a roof of black water. They are not green, but the deep, bruised purple of a twilight sky, or a matte black that drinks any stray luminescence. They do not move in the current, for there is none. They are utterly still.

Along their length grow fronds, not like leaves, but like the ribs of a fan, delicate and intricate. These fronds are the source of the only light. A soft, internal bioluminescence pulses through them in veins of cool, ethereal blue and phosphorescent white. The light is not bright; it is the gentle glow of a dying ember, enough to silhouette the world but never to truly see it. It reveals more forest. Clinging to the great stalks and spreading across the stone floor are colonies of smaller, fungus-like growths, their caps forming layered shelves in shades of deep indigo and rust. They too hold a faint, greenish light, making the floor a murky, star-dusted plain.

The density is overwhelming. The space between stalks is often only a body's width, a maze you must navigate without disturbing anything. The fronds brush past with the feather-light touch of ancient tissue. The water is thick with spores and microscopic creatures that are not fauna, but merely motes in this grand, static ecology. They are the medium, not the message. There is no fish, no crustacean, no swimming things. The silence is not the absence of sound, but the presence of a profound quiet.

What you hear is the world's slow process. There is a faint, eternal susurrus as water is drawn through the gill-like structures of the fronds. Occasionally, from the deep, there is a sound that is not a creak but a groan, the geologic sigh of the vast constructed floor settling by a fraction of a millimeter under a weight it was never truly meant to bear for uncounted millennia. A single tile might tilt, an event so slow it would take a century to complete, sending a microscopic cascade of silt and dislodged spores drifting horizontally in the still water.

The rules here are simple and absolute. Change is glacial. Life is a form of erosion, a patient, methodical dismantling. The roots of the great stalks, finer than wire, work their way into the mortar between the stones, seeking trace minerals. Their metabolism is a form of chemosynthesis, drawing energy not from a sun that does not exist, but from the slow chemical breakdown of the structure itself. This entire world—the water, the cold, the lightless dark, the intricate living "lushness"—is a single, vast organism of decay. It is a tomb that has become a garden.

To be here is to understand time not as a passage, but as a pressure. You are an intruder from a world of frantic flux, a place where things are born and die, where weather happens, where the very ground is subject to upheaval. Here, everything has already happened. The builders are a myth. The ambition is a forgotten secret encoded in a continent of stone. The only process left is the slow, silent, and total digestion of a world that was made, by a world that grew. The light you see is the glow of its stomach turning.
