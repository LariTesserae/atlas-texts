This is a place that has not yet decided if it is mountain or plateau. It is a landscape caught in the act of becoming, a high-altitude world of raw geology stretched to a regional scale. Elevation is the defining law here; the land is so high it scrapes the underbelly of a thin, cold atmosphere. The air, starved of warmth, holds a perpetual, watery dimness, as if viewed through frosted glass. The light, when it comes, is not a bright, assertive thing but a diffuse, luminescent gruel that leaks weakly from a sky the color of tarnished silver.

This world is constantly wet. But the water does not flow in rivers or rest in lakes. It condenses directly out of the thin air, a perpetual sweating of the atmosphere themselves. This is the “weeping rocks” landscape given its full, terrible expression. Immense, un-eroded faces of fresh basalt and granite, sharp as broken glass, run with uncountable threads of water. The moisture is so pure and the cold so immediate that these crystalline tears freeze as they fall, building delicate, translucent curtains of verglas that shatter like spun sugar at the slightest vibration. The ground is a treacherous mirror of slick ice and frozen mud, crunching underfoot with the sound of shattered frost.

The scale is open and profoundly empty. Vast, U-shaped valleys, too young for glaciers to have truly carved them but showing the first signs of the immense pressures involved, separate mesas and buttes that look like they were cleaved from the earth’s crust only yesterday. There is a hollowness to the space, a sense of immense volume with very little to fill it. The vegetation that does exist is sparse, tenacious, and lives on borrowed time. Patches of what looks like black mold and tough, liver-colored lichens cling to the lee sides of rocks where the ice-wind is less brutal. Here and there, a clump of something like steel-wool grass manages a brittle existence, its roots digging into hairline cracks in the stone, thriving on the thinnest patina of nutrient film.

The sound is mostly quiet, a silence that is not peaceful but heavy, filled with the pressure of the void and the low-frequency presence of the place itself. The dominant noise is the sound of water freezing—a faint, constant ticking and cracking, the glacial chime of a billion microscopic structures forming and collapsing. But the quiet is regularly broken.

This landscape, despite being utterly untouched by constructed hands, is industrial.

The fauna is abundant, and it *is* the industry. The dominant life forms are the Strata-Mills, creatures so large and slow they are mistaken for geological features until they move. Imagine an armadillo the size of a warehouse, its carapace not made of keratin but of interlocking plates of living granite, veined with quartz and feldspar. They move on dozens of thick, piston-like legs, progressing with a glacial, rhythmic deliberation across the high plateaus.

They are the engines of this world. Their purpose is digestion. A Strata-Mill will lower its head, a maw of unimaginable grinding power, to the raw, un-eroded rock face. It does not bite. It resonates. A deep, sub-sonic hum fills the air, a vibration you feel in your bones more than you hear with your ears. The rock in front of it, subjected to this focused sonic energy, liquefies into a mineral slurry. The creature then feeds, drawing the slurry into its immense internal forge.

This is the industrial process. The sound of a Strata-Mill at work is the sound of a foundry: a low, grinding rumble punctuated by the hiss of high-pressure exhaust. They do not excrete waste; they broadcast fertilizer. From vents along their flanks, they expel a hot, chemically rich gas and a fine, grey-black dust—superheated silicates and mineral salts. This cloud of life-giving industry billows out and settles, coating the surrounding rock. Where it lands, the sparse black mold and steel-wool grass explode in vibrant color, turning patches of the oyster-grey landscape vibrant emerald and deep ochre for a few weeks before the cold reclaims them. This is the dynamic heartbeat of the ecosystem. The Strata-Mills create the only conditions where life can flourish.

Swarming around the massive grazers are smaller, more numerous creatures: the Scavengers. Skittering, beetle-like things the size of dogs, their carapaces a mix of chitin and recycled metal, they clean the Mills, picking parasites from the rock-plates and lapping at the nutrient-rich drips. In turn, they are preyed upon by sleek, solitary hunters that look like weasels sculpted from obsidian, moving with terrifying speed through the shadowed valleys, their dark forms almost invisible against the raw rock.

The rules of this world are simple and harsh. Geology is food. The largest creatures are not predators but processors, living factories that run on stone. They are the agent of change that the nascent erosion has not yet had time to be. Life is a brief, bright fire fueled by the exhaust of a grander, slower engine. There is no past here, only the sharp-edged present of a world being forged and the tireless, biological machinery that is forging it. You stand on a mountain peak that has not yet existed for a million years, watch a living rock-grinder eat a cliff face under a dim sky, and breathe air that tastes of cold stone and the hot, metallic breath of creation itself.
