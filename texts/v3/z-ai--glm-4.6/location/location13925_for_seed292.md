The world is water. Not a world *with* oceans, but a world that *is* the ocean. There is no surface, no thin membrane of air. Perhaps there once was, an aeon ago, but if so, its memory is now ground into the silt of the abyssal plains. Here, "up" is a direction relative to the floor, not a destination. You are always submerged, always under the immense, placid weight of a global sea.

The temperature is cool, a persistent, bracing chill that seeps into bone and metal alike. It is a cold that clarifies, slowing the processes of decay yet lending a profound energy to the living things that have adapted to it. The water itself is dense, packed with microscopic life, dissolved minerals, and a constant, fine haze of silt stirred by the planet's restless pulse. Visibility is a matter of relativity; you might see for dozens of meters in a rare, clear patch, or be blinded by the living, breathing fog of a productive current.

Light does not come from a sun. At least, not directly. High, high above, beyond any conceivable navigation, is a perpetual, pearlescent twilight. It is a faint, diffuse luminescence, perhaps the result of pressure on exotic gases or the decay of some primordial energy, and it provides a baseline illumination of soft grey and violet. The true colors of this world, however, come from within. The vegetation is lush, and it is the primary source of light. Forests of towering, crystalline anemones—some the size of redwoods—pulse with slow, rhythmic light. Vast, veiny mats of phosphorescent algae cling to every surface, their glow shifting from teal to rose in response to the currents. The light is not static; it is a living, breathing thing, a silent, bioluminescent symphony that paints the deep in cascading waves of color.

The terrain is a paradox of extreme elevation and ancient erosion. There are no jagged, young peaks here. The landscape is a testament to unimaginable age, smoothed and shaped by the relentless flow of water. Yet the "erosion" is not a gentle wearing-down; it is the very source of the world's turbulence. Imagine a riverbed, but scaled to a planetary level and filled with fluid as heavy as lead entire mountain ranges exist as submerged seamounts, their broad slopes not covered in soil but in a living carpet of glowing flora and anchored fauna. Between them are canyons so vast they swallow horizons, their floors a kilometre below their rims. These are the Great Rifts, and through them, the world’s true power flows.

The dynamic of this place is one of constant, chaotic turbulence. The currents are its weather, its tectonics, its lifeblood. They are not predictable streams but titanic, shifting forces. There is the "Deep Flow," a slow-moving, planet-straddling river of icy water that circulates in the abyssal plains. There are the "Rift Gales," sudden, violent upwellings that can tear a creature from its anchorage and carry it kilometers in minutes. There are the "Whispering Tides," resonant harmonic frequencies that vibrate through stone and crystal.

This turbulence creates the sound. The world is loud. There is no silence. The dominant noise is a low, gut-deep hum, the sound of quintillions of tonnes of water moving through the canyons and over the plains. It is the foundational note of the world's existence. Overlaid on this is a cacophony of sharper sounds: the grinding sound of a Rift Gale scouring a canyon wall, a sound like a mountain being dragged across the seafloor; the sharp, percussive reports of gas-filled bladders in certain fauna, used for communication or as a defense mechanism; the high-frequency chittering of countless small organisms; the resonant chime of crystal-structures being struck by the current. To be here is to feel the sound as much as hear it, a constant vibration that presses in from all sides.

Life is abundant and densely packed. Ecological niches are defined not by geography, but by hydrology. On the "windward" slopes of the seamounts, where currents are strongest, are thickets of six-armed, starfish-like creatures the size of huts, their arms locked to the rock, filter-feeding the nutrient-rich flow. In the calmer "leeward" eddies, schools of eel-like, quasi-sentient creatures with bioluminescent patterning on their flanks dance in complex, shimmering formations. Giant, slow-moving grazers, resembling armored isopods the size of whales, pick their way across the glowing plains, their immense legs making soft, thudding sounds against the stone floor. The ecosystem is a cascade of energy, all of it driven by the turbulent flow.

Amid this raw, primal life are the scattered hints of something else. The built remnants are almost invisible, swallowed by the rampant ecology. They are industrial. A single, massive pipeline, sheathed in rust and thick with centuries of crystalline growth, snakes down the side of a canyon, its origin and destination lost to the deep. In a rare, stable plateau, a rusted gantry crane leans at an impossible angle, a fossil of brute-force engineering. These are not ruins to be explored; they are tomstones. They speak of a civilization that once tried to impose its will on this world, perhaps before the final, permanent flood. Now, they are simply more features on the landscape, anchor points for anemones and nesting grounds for skittering, metal-eating fauna.

To exist here is to accept a fundamental truth: you are a temporary observer to an ancient process. The landscape is ancient, its basic forms settled into a final state, but that state is perpetual transformation. A canyon carved today by a Rift Gale will begin its slow fill of silt tomorrow, even as another current starts the work of carving a new path elsewhere. The world is in its end-of-life, yet that end is a dynamic, roaring, and beautiful state of being, a final, turbulent equilibrium that has lasted for eons and will last for eons more. You are just a brief, warm spark moving through its overwhelming, cold, and shining depths.
