There is no sky. There is only a roof, so inconceivably high that its existence is known only by the way sound booms back and forth in the vast, cold air, and by the faint, grid-like shadows that sometimes fall from unseen sources. This is the Sump, the Under-Tundral, the Geofront. It is a place built.

The ground underfoot is not earth, but a deck of articulated, bolted-down metal plates, each the size of a city square. They thrum with a constant, sub-audible vibration that travels up through the soles of your boots and settles in your bones. This is the world's first and only terrain: sharp-edged, industrial, immaculately machined, and young. There are no weathered stones here, no smoothed-over contours. Every edge is a clean, ninety-degree angle or a precise geometric bevel, still bearing the faint, parallel-scored marks of the fab-lathe that birthed it.

Water is everywhere, but it is not the water of rain or river. It is an exhalation from the world itself. A perpetual, biting fog, thick and cold, drifts in slow, tenebrous rivers across the metal deck. The air is heavy with moisture, so saturated it feels like breathing a chill soup. From towering, monolithic structures that rise into the gloom—the Cryo-Pylons—a constant weeping of condensation trickles down their fluted metal sides. This water collects in the seams of the deck plates or pools in vast, shallow, perfectly rectangular basins cut into the floor, their surfaces like black, oily glass reflecting the dim light above. The temperature is always just a few degrees above freezing, a cold that is wet and invasive, a cold that feels actively hostile to warmth.

The sound is the true master of this place. It is a cacophony so huge it becomes a symphony. The dominant note is a deep-structural groan, the sound of a continent-spanning machine settling under its own immense weight. Layered over this is the high, piercing whine of untold millions of gallons of coolant being pumped through unseen conduits, a sound that sets your teeth on edge. Periodically, a rhythmic *CLANG… CLANG… CLANG…* echoes from an unknown sector, the staccato beat of some titanic press or hammer. Shrieking metal-on-metal, the hiss of decompressing vapour, the drone of fans the size of windmills—all of it merges into a constant, roaring pressure. It is so loud that silence is an abstract memory; even human thought feels like it has to shout to be heard.

They flicker erratically, their light feeble and sickly, casting long, dancing shadows that make the sparse features of the landscape seem to move and writhe. When a light array fails, a section of the world is plunged into a profound stygian darkness, a pocket of absolute void that the ambient fog can only soften, never illuminate. Patches of faint, greenish bioluminescence sometimes cling to the warmer surfaces of the machinery—the only "vegetation" this world can support. It is a strange, crystalline moss that seems to feed not on sunlight, but on the waste heat and electrical hum of its mechanical host. It is sparse, a lonely dusting of emerald on a canvas of gunmetal grey and rust-orange.

Life is a rumor. The true fauna of this place are not flesh and blood, but machine. Occasionally, you will see them: spider-like maintenance drones, long since gone feral, moving with jerky, unpredictable purpose across the deck plates, their optical sensors glowing like angry red coals. They scavenge broken parts, tear at loose wiring, or simply patrol their allotted territories with a mindless, terrifying determination. Rarer still are the things that are neither fully machine nor fully animal—amalgams of scavenged metal and consuming fungal-coral that grow slowly around leaking power cells, their silicon nerves twitching in time with the machine's pulse. They are rare, solitary, and best left un-disturbed.

The rules here are simple and absolute. The world is the engine. The landscape is the factory floor. The weather is the rhythm of industry: a high-pressure coolant burst is a flash flood; a system-wide temperature spike causes the fog to boil away into a temporary, sweltering mist; a power surge turns the dim lights into a blinding, momentary glare. To move is to navigate by sound, following the hum of a functioning conduit or fleeing the approach of a grinding, clanking automaton. To live is to exist as a glitch, a brief anomaly in the vast, relentless logic of a world that was built, not grown. Every surface is a tool, every sound a process, every drop of water a byproduct of a function so far beyond your understanding it may as well be the will of a god. This is the Sump. And it is active.
