The air up here is a cool, thin broth, and you breathe it so deep you feel it in your teeth. It carries the perpetual scent of damp petrichor and a low, sterile hum, like the echo from a forgotten power station. You are on a high plateau, so vast it erases the horizon with its own gentle curvature. It is not rock underfoot, though. It has the stillness and colour of bare, wind-scoured stone, but it yields slightly, like ancient, compressed peat. This is the floor of the world.

This place is a single, continent-spanning lifeform that has reached a terminal, primordial state. Call it a lichen, if you must, or an organism so beyond that word that it is a mere echo of the truth. Over uncountable eons, it has consumed all but itself, digesting soil, bedrock, and mountains, leaving behind this woody, cellulose-and-chlorophyll rind. Its surface is ridged and corded like petrified muscle tissue, scored with deep, dry channels that wind like creeks between fossilized forests of its own dead matter. The "erosion" here is not of rock by wind, but of life by time; it has settled into its final, flattest, most ancient form. Nothing new grows here, not in the way you understand it. The age is so profound that it has become a quality of the light, a cool, weary patience.

And yet, it is not truly empty. Punctuating the vast, open emptiness at sparse, almost decorative intervals, are the structures.

They rise from the living ground like crystalline growths, but they are made of the same material, woven and thickened into monumental, architectural forms. They are towers, arches, and ziggurats of densely-packed, woody matter, their surfaces smoothed to a high polish by the same immeasurable age. Some are hollow, their interiors revealing intricate latticeworks of what look like vertebrae and tendons, all woven from the same fibrous stuff. This is the city, but it was never built. It was instructed to grow. These are the "built" environments, the "urban" core, an infrastructure of living tissue, an orderly imposition of geometry onto a biological sheet that stretches for a thousand kilometres. They are the lungs, the kidneys, the factories of this great being.

This is where the water comes in. The plateau, for all its dampness, is a desert. The moisture isn't in pools or rivers; it is in the air, a condensate that perpetually clinging to everything, a fine, cool sheen on every surface. The giant, scattered "buildings" are condensers. Their wide, flat tops gather the mist. Their internal, twisting channels and chambers breathe in the humid air. Slowly, with a glacial, deeply-etched rhythm that defines this place, they process it. You can feel it more than hear it: a subsonic thrumming that vibrates up through the soles of your feet, a constant, loud resonance that is the sound of a planet-scale organ at work. This is the industrial landscape. There are no pistons of steel, but massive valves of sphincter-muscle that squeeze droplets of purified water down impermeable stems, dumping them into the deep, dry channels below. The clanking and groaning is the sound of cellulose frameworks contracting, of millennia of tension being released in a slow, mechanical shudder.

The sound is the first thing that breaks you. It is a loud, pervasive presence, a background hum that has texture and weight. It is the sound the weirdness makes. There is almost no fauna. What animal could survive on a world that is also its sole predator? The organism's strange metabolism and the psychic pressure of its humming make this a sterile, silent place for life as we know it. Occasionally, you might find the impossible: a bird with feathers of cracked porcelain, perched frozen on a spire, its eyes like dull mercury. It hasn't moved in a century. Or you might see the fossilized outline of some great beast, pressed into the "ground" like a leaf in a book, its form perfectly preserved in woven cellulose. They are not inhabitants; they are footnotes in an organism's memory, its one-time dialogue with a life it has since outlived and absorbed.

To be here is to be inside a machine that is also a god. The rules are simple. Keep moving. The stillness invites the world's shifting logic to settle on you. Do not drink from the channels until the water has run clear; before that, it is still being processed, full of chemical truths and temporal paradoxes. The sound is your constant companion. It is loud enough to drown out your own thoughts, and eventually, it feels like it is thinking you. This is the city of the final plant. An industrial park at the end of geologic time. An open plain where the ground is breathing, and you are a mote of dust in its ancient, humming, and ever-so-slightly broken throat.
