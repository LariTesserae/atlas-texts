The air here tastes like chalk and old dust, dry enough to crack the lips, but the temperature is a gentle, pervasive warmth, like the inside of a pocket recently emptied of a stone. It is a quiet place, a small place—a single, vast room rather than an open landscape, though the ceiling is lost in a soft, ubiquitous haze of normal, white daylight.

The ground is not earth. It is constructed. Beneath your feet lies a tessellated pavement of baked clay bricks, each one the size of a dinner plate, perfectly fitted and sharp-edged. There is no mortar, only the friction of geometry. This is a youthfully formed world; the erosion score is near zero, meaning nothing has been rounded off by time. The corners of the room where the floor meets the walls are knife-sharp ridges. The steps leading up to low, dais-like platforms are pristine, their edges catching the light with a harsh, fresh definition. It feels as though the masons laid the last tile only moments ago and then simply vanished, leaving the mortar to flash-dry in the heat.

There is no water. The score is abyssal. Not a drop, not a hint of humidity, no condensation on the clay. Yet, the parameter for vegetation is lush.

This is the paradox that defines the Bower of Dry Silk.

Because there is no rain to weigh them down, the "plants" here do not droop. They are architectural. Imagine a forest constructed entirely of woven, dried materials—papyrus, stripped bark, horsehair, and coiled sinew. They rise from the cracks in the brickwork in dense, spiraling columns. They are "lush" in the sense of saturation; the volume is packed tight with these growths. There is no empty space between the trunks; it is a wall offiber, a thicket of braided organic cables.

They are not green, save for perhaps a faded, desaturated olive where a dye hasn't fully faded. Mostly, they are the colors of dried wheat, rusted iron, and bone. The leaves are not soft pads of liquid-filled cells, but stiff, papery cut-outs, millions of them layered in a fractal density that creates a sound-deadening buffer. When the wind moves through this room—and there is a slow, patient draft that circulates endlessly—the sound is not the rustle of nature, but the dry, whispered friction of a million pages turning in a library.

The technology that built this, or that maintains it, is primitive. You see no servos, no glass, no polymer. The construction is joinery and knot-work. The "trees" are lashed together with rawhide cords. Strange, ceramic urns hang from the "branches" on woven ropes, acting as counterweights or perhaps fruit. The builders are gone, but their handiwork remains, crude yet effective.

And it is inhabited.

The fauna are abundant, small, and perfectly adapted to a world of fiber and dust. They are the "Shepherds of the Knot." They resemble stick insects, but composed of the same dried material as the forest—chitinous and pale. They move with agonizing slowness. The dynamic of this world is slow; a deviation takes hours to resolve. A Shepherd might spend an entire day climbing a single papery frond, its many legs testing the tensile strength of the fibers. They do not eat; they appear to "prune." With mandibles like snips, they sever a stray thread here, tie a new knot there. They are maintaining the structural integrity of the simulation, laboring under the rules of a reality that is fraying at the edges.

The "Weirdness" here is high, a reality breakdown hovering near the critical threshold.

Because it is so dense, and the geometry is so sharp, and the water is so absent, the world struggles to hold its shape. If you look closely at the woven vines, you can see that the fibers do not always loop around solid objects. Sometimes, a vine weaves through a shadow, knotting itself around a patch of sunlight that has fallen on the floor. The "urDs<L0>" system is glitching in a beautiful, terrifying way.

In the corner, near where the sharp angle of the wall meets the floor, a knot of roots has tangled with the empty air. The air there has become solid, crystallized into a brittle, glass-like substance that suffocates the root. The Shepherds are swarming it, hundreds of them, slowly gnawing the air away to free the plant.

Walking here is difficult. The vegetation is dense, forcing you to push through walls of dry, woven resistance. But as you push, you feel a resistance that isn't physical. You feel a drag on your *existence*. Because the world is so dry—so lacking in the fluidity of water—it has latched onto the concept of "flow" and applied it to "time" and "space." To walk forward is to walk against the gradient of history.

You step over a ceramic urn. It lies shattered, but the shards are held suspended in mid-air, connected by a net of vibrating spider-silk. The break is fresh—young erosion, sharp edges. The Shards haven't decided to fall yet. Gravity here is a suggestion, voted on by the collective weave of the plants.

If you stand still long enough, you might feel the dry warmth begin to desiccate you, not draining your fluids, but your *definition*. The parameters are stripping you down. Without water to blur the lines, your edges become too sharp. You begin to look like a drawing.

This is the Dry Bower. A room without a roof, a forest without rain, a garden that is slowly weaving itself into a knot so tight it will eventually sever the space it occupies, leaving nothing but a clean cut in the void.
