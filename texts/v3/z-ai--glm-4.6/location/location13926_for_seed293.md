You do not enter the March; you are accepted by it.

The first sensation is the give underfoot. There is no stone, no packed earth, no certainty of geology. The ground is a vast, continuous suture of living material. It’s a cushion, a mattress of midnight blues and deep greens, shot through with veins of phosphorescent capillaries that pulse with a slow, internal light. This is the floor of the world here, a single, organismic substrate the Sages called the Mycelian Loom, though everyone who lives its reality just calls it the March. It is ancient beyond reckoning, a final, settled form of life having consumed all inorganic competition. This is its basin, its last, comfortable sigh.

The air is warm and wet, a visible breath. It tastes of loam, of sweet decay, and the faint, electric tang of ozone. Water doesn't just fall in rain here; it condenses directly from the saturated atmosphere, weeping from every surface in thick, syrupy drops. The March is perpetually slick, beaded with moisture that runs in rivulets along the intricate, folded topology of the ground. The sound is a constant, layered presence: a deep subsonic thrumming, like the lowest note of a cello held for an eternity, pierced by the clean, percussive plink-drip-plink of water falling from impossible heights, and all of it underscored by a mindless, loud chittering from the life that teems within it.

The vegetation *is* the substrate. It is total. Yet, despite this, the space feels sparse, almost empty. This is because the Loom, for all its richness, is a vast, flat, horizontal presence. The true features, the things that define space and draw the eye, are few and far between. Sprawled across the basin are the Growths.

These are not trees or buildings, but both and neither. They cathedralize the landscape. each one is a unique, sparsely-placed edifice of woody matter and vascular tissue, grown to some urbane, half-remembered blueprint. One might be a buttressed tower of petrified bark, its hollow interior a spiraling staircase lit by fungal lanterns. Another is a flattened, sprawling canopy, its underside a warren of smooth-walled chambers and exposed roots that function as bridges and walkways. These structures are the city, yet there is no plan, no grid. They rise from the Loom like slow thoughts, separated by acres of the quiet, living floor.

And this is the wonder and the terror of the March: it is not static. It is turbulent. It is a hyperactive ecology in a state of constant, chaotic flux. A perfectly serviceable bridge of woven fibers you crossed yesterday may, by this afternoon, have retracted into the main Growth, its surface budding with strange, iridescent flowers before being reabsorbed. A new balcony might extrude from a tower over a week, provide a perch for the local avifauna for a season, and then wither and slough off, crumbling into nutrient-rich mulch that the Loom eagerly drinks. The design is not a plan, but a process. It is a Solaris-like storm of biological creation and destruction, beautiful and meaningless in its sheer, relentless activity.

The fauna is abundant and utterly symbiotic. Skittering, six-legged grazers with mouths like plumber's suction cups move across the Loom, feeding on the weeping moisture and leaving behind neat, swirling patterns that heal within minutes. They are the city’s street sweepers. Winged things, like manta rays of jeweled chitin, glide between the towering Growths, pollinating them with a dust that glitters and hums. They are the messengers. And then there are the Architects, the remnants of whatever civilization seeded this place. They are rarely seen, but their presence is felt in the turbulence itself. They are no longer bodies that build, but minds that *will*. They are the consciousness encoded into the nanite-infused Loom, the ones who chose to stop shaping their world and instead *become* its nervous system. The loud, constant hum is the sound of their ceaseless thought, their debates and their dreams manifested as the budding and with- ering of a wall, the growth of a path, the sudden blooming of a light.

To stand in the March is to stand on the skin of a living god that has forgotten it is a god. You are a pleasant, transient itch on its hide. The air you breathe is its exhalation. The ground beneath you is its flesh. There is no past, only the deep memory of its final form, and no future, only the chaotic, churning perpetual now. Step carefully, for the ground itself may decide to become a staircase, and the sky may decide to blossom.
