This is the Chantry of the First Rock.

The first thing you register is not the sight, but the sound—the resonant, percussive landscape. It is a space made of noise. The air itself seems to vibrate with a low, constant hum, the sound of immense pressure bearing down from above and pushing up from below. It’s a bass note you feel in your teeth and in the hollow of your chest. Woven through this drone are the higher-pitched sounds of becoming: the sigh of air escaping a newly formed fissure, the whisper-thin skitter of pebbles and scree sliding down a fresh face of stone, and the ceaseless, rhythmic plink… plink… plink… of condensation falling from heights you cannot see into pools that are only just beginning to form. The whole place is an instrument, and the geology is its music.

You stand on a floor that is not yet fully stone. It is a vast, grey composite, un-eroded and embryonic. Modern geology has no word for it; it is a friable, almost clay-like aggregate of minerals and volcanic glass, still warm to the touch from the slow exertions of the world below. It feels ancient, but it is younger than you are. This is the foundation of a high plateau, a nascent summit still coalescing from the planet's mantle. It has not yet known the violence of weather, the grinding passage of glaciers. It has only known this slow, patient upward press. The terrain is simple, a single expanse bounded by things that are not yet mountains but aspire to be—colossal, buttress-like formations of the same semi-soft material, reaching like supplicant arms toward the cool, thin air.

The water is everywhere and nowhere in particular. There are no rivers, no streams. The moisture is simply part of the high, cool atmosphere, constantly condensing on the raw stone. The entire landscape glistens with a patina of wetness. Water weeps from the nascent cliffs in a thousand steady tears, darkening the grey stone to black, polishing it here, stippling it there. This dampness is the catalyst for the only abundant life here: a lushness of primary conquest. Every surface that can hold water is a tapestry of low-lying ecology. Radiating chartreuse mosses creep across the ground in thick, plush carpets. Delicate, feathery ferns unfurl from every tiny ledge and crevice, their fronds tipped with crystalline droplets. Lichens, in explosive oranges and metallic blues, bloom across the stone like mineral rust. There is no soil, only the raw material of the earth itself, feeding this first generation of green.

The scale is intimate yet vast, cathedral-like. You are standing in a single, room-like arena miles across, but the soaring, incomplete columns that flank it give the space a verticality that dwarfs the human form. There are no trees. The presence is defined by the towers, which are part of the same continuous geological upwelling as the ground beneath your feet. They are mountains in miniature, embryonic peaks whose bases you can walk to and touch. They are not distinct features, but rhythmic nodes in the landscape, spaced far enough apart that the space between them feels vast and empty. This is the density: a few colossal presences defining a world of hollow, echoing air.

And it is in the shadow and shelter of these embryonic towers that you see the urban element. This is not a city of cobbled streets and tiled roofs. This is a settlement that is a continuation of the landscape itself. Dwellings are not built, but carved. Niches and alcoves are painstakingly hollowed out of the yielding stone by hand. Low, sinuous walls are shaped to define communal spaces, their surfaces still bearing the raised ripples of the mason's primitive tools. The people here, the Stonemasons, are as rare as the fauna of the world outside their domain. They move with a slow, deliberate grace, their bodies lean and hard from their work. They are not masters of the rock; they are its midwives. Their technology is primitive: hammers and chisels fashioned from nodules of a harder, crystalline stone they find within the softer matrix. Their work is not fast construction, but a responsive ritual. They listen to the hum, follow the settling cracks, and gently guide the emergence.

They are carving the Chantry a form. A cleft is widened to channel a weeping rock-face into a proper waterfall. A bulge in a tower is slowly hollowed into a resonant chamber to amplify the world's hum. A path is worn and then chiseled to connect one carved dwelling to another. The "urban" aspect is this organized, networked response to the geology. They are not imposing a design, but revealing the one they believe is embedded in the stone itself. This is the world’s central rule: the land is becoming, and their purpose is to assist and give it voice.

The light, high in the sky and sharp, cuts through the thin air, creating harsh contrasts. The brilliant white of the sky, the deep black of the wet stone, the vivid greens and oranges of the life—all of it is rendered in stark clarity. But because it is a single, bounded space, the light is also architectural. Beams of sunlight fall through the gaps between the raw towers, illuminating columns of mist and dust, turning the entire space into a nave of shifting, ethereal light.

The fauna may be rare, but there is a presence. Sometimes, on a ledge high above, you see a creature that seems made of the landscape itself—a slow-moving grazer composed of moss and stone, munching contemplatively on a patch of lichen. But mostly, the life is human and stunted. The Stonemasons live and die here, generation upon generation, their lives marked by the slow completion of a single archway, the deepening of a single chamber. Their world is loud, but it is a slow, patient, changing loudness. It is the sound of the planet thinking out loud, and they, its humble acolytes, provide the punctuation.
