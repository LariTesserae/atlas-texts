There is a light here that does not come from a sun.

It is a constant, pervasive brilliance, a hot, white-gold wash that fills everything with an unflickering, photographic clarity. The heat is a companion to this light, a thick, wet blanket of air that feels solid enough to lean against. This is the first and last weather; dawn is a myth, and dusk an impossibility. Time is measured not by the movement of celestial bodies, but by the slow, inexorable seep of sweat from the world itself.

You are below the surface, though the scale of it defies the word "cavern." This is not a hollow in the rock, but a world*made* of hollow. The space is vast, a regional-scale void, a valley scooped from the guts of the earth. So high is its ceiling that it is lost in a luminous haze, a vaulted sky of tangled root-mass as thick and knotted as geological strata. These roots, and the lesser ones that descend from them like the cables of a forgotten god, pulse with the same internal light as the rest of the world. They are the stars of this earth.

There is no ground. To step is to press down into a yielding, fibrous substrate. The surface is a continuous, living carpet, a woven mat of mosseses so dense they have compressed into a springy, soil-like tissue. Interlaced through this are vines with the girth of pythons, their bark a ceramic, jade-green, and wide, fleshy leaves the size of shield-sails that shimmer with a deep, photosynthetic energy. Colors are not just green. The light illuminates veins of indigo and orange in the ground-tissue, flushes of magenta in fungal shelves that cling like balconies to descending buttresses of root, and the pure, stark white of new growth, always pushing, always emerging.

This is the world's central rule: total vegetation. The walls of the chasm are not stone but a kind of geologicalized plant-matter, a slow-flowing, ancient tissue of fused cellulose and calcified deposits that gleams with moisture. Water, too, is a part of the living cycle. There are no rivers, no rain. The heat from the biological light causes a constant, gentle condensation on every surface, a perpetual sweat that gathers in beads and traces. The dominant sound is the patient, rhythmic *plink… plink… plink* of these droplets falling from leaf-tips and root-hairs to hit the spongy floor with a soft, wet sigh. Beyond this, there is the sound of growth itself. It is not a rushing noise, but a deep, cellular creaking, the sound of tonnes of living fiber slowly shifting, settling, reinforcing. It is the sound of geology happening on a biological timescale.

The world is dense, a saturated three-dimensional tapestry. Vines drape over fungal brackets that grow upon the broad trunks of plummeting roots, which themselves are coated in glowing mosses. To try and walk in a straight line is impossible; one must climb, duck, and weave through the living architecture. Yet, for all this density, it is not a world of creatures. Fauna is rare, a whispered exception. You might glimpse something, once. A thing like a manta ray made of translucent membrane, silently gliding through the humid air, trailing longer, more delicate filaments that snag on the vegetation before it pulls free. It does not seem to eat, or breathe, but simply *is*, a temporary thought of the ecosystem, solitary and strange.

Scattered throughout the vastness are the built things, but they are not made by hands. They are eruptions of the living world itself—perfectly hemispherical domes of polished, bone-white cellulose, or tall, spire-like structures of calcified plant lymph that stand like graves in a city that is still being born. They have no doors, no windows, no purpose obvious to an outsider. They are simply the world's way of practicing form, a sketch of architecture designed and built by a single, distributed, planetary consciousness. They are artifacts without an artificer.

This is the great paradox, the core of the place's weirdness. The landscape is both embryonic and eternal. The parameter reads "emerging," that it is pre-formation, raw. And it is. New growth is everywhere, a bright white against the green and jade, a constant push into the present moment. Yet, it is also frozen in a perfect, never-changing equilibrium. The "emergence" is not a process that will one day end, not a construction site that will become a finished city. The emergence *is* the finished state. It is a perpetual moment of creation, stretched across infinity. For every new push of growth, an old part of the world decays with an equal, imperceptible slowness, becoming the fresh, damp substrate for what is to come. The form of the world remains the same, a constant and unchanging landscape, while the material of it is born, lives, and dies in a slow, silent, and continuous cycle.

You are a temporary guest within the anatomy of a god. You breathe its air, sweat from its pores, stand on its living flesh, and bathe in the light of its cells. It is a place of profound, luminous, and utterly changeless loneliness. And the only sound that really matters is the quiet, endless drip of water, counting out the seconds of a day that will never end.
