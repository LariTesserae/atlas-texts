The first thing you understand is the sound. It is not a noise that assaults the ears from a single direction, but rather a pressure that exists everywhere at once, a physical presence in the air. It is the foundational hum of the world, a low, resonant growl like a colossal bow drawn across the string of the planet’s core. Woven through this drone are higher frequencies: the delicate, crystalline tinkle of a trillion tiny bells, the slow groaning of stressed stone, the whispering chime of glass striking glass. It is a cacophony that has achieved a kind of harmony, a permanent and ever-shifting symphony that drowns out thought and forces you to feel it instead.

You stand in the Chime Fields, a broad, low basin cradled by rounded, sleeping hills that are old, soft, and silent. Here in the basin, the world is young and sharp. The ground is not soil but a dense, interlocking lattice of mineral growth. It is a forest of stone, a thicket of geology. There are no trees, no leaves, no blades of grass. The vegetation parameter reads zero, and the world complies, offering no mercy of green. Instead, shapes that echo life fill every space. Towering spires of basalt, as densely packed as a crowd of people, claw at a sky you cannot see. Between them, blades of obsidian, some as thin and sharp as razors, others as broad as a sail, jut from the ground at acute, impossible angles. There are sprawling, fractal fronds of what looks like frozen lightning, their intricate patterns glinting with a damp, internal light. The ground beneath your feet is a jumble of these forms, a floor of broken needles and shattered crystals that crunch softly with every step, a sound immediately swallowed by the greater chorus.

The air is cool and saturated, heavy with the promise of a rain that never truly falls. This is the expression of the landscape’s constant thirst. A fine, perpetual mist drifts between the mineral spires, not a fog that obscures, but one that clarifies, catching the dim light and making every surface glisten. Condensation is the lifeblood here. Water molecules, drawn from the heavy atmosphere, bead and cascade down the flanks of every rock formation. This is what they call the weeping. Trails of dark moisture stain the faces of the spires, tracing slow paths down their edges. The drops that fall from the highest points hit the formations below with an almost inaudible *pat*, but their impact sends a shudder through the crystalline structures, a vibration that adds a new note to the ambient chorus. The world is drinking itself, and the sound it makes is the sound of its own slow, geological digestion.

There is no sun, only a uniform, pearlescent gloom. The light source is diffuse, a silver-grey contamination that seems to emanate from the mist itself. It casts no shadows; it only reveals depth, creating an effect of infinite regression. Looking into the forest is like looking into a fractal, each spire and blade reflected and duplicated in the wet sheen of its neighbors until the landscape dissolves into an abstract pattern of light and stone. The dimness is a mercy; in a bright glare, this place would be overwhelmingly sharp and blinding.

 life here is not biological, but lithic. The mineral structures are the flora and fauna, the ecosystem itself. They are geo-organisms. They grow, but their growth is measured in millennia, the accretion of new crystalline layers so slow it is imperceptible. They metabolize, but their food is atmospheric water and resonant energy. The sound is their breath. Each formation has its own signature tone, determined by its composition, its shape, and the flow of water through its internal matrix. When the wind curls through the densest thickets, it plays the spires like a panpipe, creating mournful, sliding scales. The slow grind of tectonic stress alters a formation’s pitch by a microtone over centuries. This is the dynamic of the place: a glacial, patient, musical evolution.

Fauna is correspondingly rare. The only creatures witnessed are the Chime-Eaters. They are living things, but so perfectly adapted to the mineral world they are almost impossible to distinguish from it. They are quadrupeds with facets instead of fur, their bodies seemingly carved from smoked quartz. They move with a slow, deliberate grace, their six-jointed legs so delicate they seem to float. They do not eat the stone. Instead, they press their broad, flat heads against resonant spires and seem to listen, drawing energy from the vibrations. When they feed, the specific tone of that spire diminishes, becoming muted and dull, only to slowly return to strength after the creature moves on. To see one is to see a pause in the music.

Scattered through the endless rows of sonic life are the marks of something else. Not a civilization, not a settlement, but the ghost of an attempt. Every few kilometres you find a single, standing structure. They are always the same: a ten-meter-tall slab of a different, utterly dead stone. It is a pitted, dull grey material that does not weep or chime. It is unresponsive, inert. A single, deep notch is carved into its top, like a sight for a bow. These Monoliths, for lack of a better word, are anchors of silence in the sonic sea. They offer no explanation, only a question. Who or what built them, and why did they use a material that cannot participate in the world’s only true process?

To stand here is to be an anomaly. You are a warm, soft, silent thing in a cold, hard, loud world. The water that beads on your own skin and the slow thud of your own heart are the only personal rhythms you have, feeble disruptions in the grand, humming, geological fugue. The rules here are simple and absolute: water flows, stone sings, and time is measured by the changing of a chord. The landscape is a slow-motion symphony, and you are just a brief, dissonant note.
