The ground is not stone. It is a grid of interlocking bronze plates, each the size of a plaza, and between them, seams as wide as a canyon. Down there, in the seams, is where the damp lives.

It seeps. It doesn't rain, not in any way you’d recognize. The cool, thin air carries a perpetual, invisible mist, a chill so constant it feels like a property of the light itself. This moisture, heavy and patient, condenses on the vast, flat surfaces of the bronze. It beads, then flows, seeking the seams in the metal geology. The sound it makes is not a roar, but the faintest, most intimate whisper—a soft, sustained *hiss* as a million microscopic rivers find their channels and drain away into the darkness below. To feel for water is to touch a surface and find it perpetually slick, filmed with a residue that is neither oil nor dew.

There is no natural soil, but life has found its analogue. A sparse, crusty lichen, the colour of oxidized copper, clings to the plates where the seepage is constant. It is the only vegetation. It doesn't grow towards the light—a pervasive, sourceless brilliance that makes the air itself look white—but it follows the faintest chemical trails left by the water, sprawling in concentric, rusty stains. In the places where the lichen is thickest, a faint, internal phosphorescence gathers, a soft blue-green glow that is the only break in the sterile, metallic palette.

The landscape is high, open, and immense. You stand on one bronze mesa and look out across a regional valley that is not a valley at all, but a single, impossibly vast floor. In the distance, where mountains should be, there are structures. They are tiered ziggurats of riveted iron, silent and sheer, their edges so sharp they could have been machined yesterday. This is the hallmark of youth: a world without the softening touch of erosion, where every angle is a decisive, final statement.

Yet this sharpness is a lie. The largest of these iron mountains turns. Or it will. It is a gear of a scale that defeats perception. Its motion is slower than glaciers, measured in centuries, its revolution a geological event. A plateau that was unbreachable a thousand years ago might, in another thousand, present a shallow incline. A pathway you walked this morning is being ground into dust by a mechanism that will not complete its thought before your species is a memory. The world is young in its features, but ancient in its processes.

Everything is built. The air in your lungs, the light in your eyes—it all feels generated, part of a colossal, self-contained system. This density is not one of clutter, but of function. The space between the bronze floor and the distant, vaulted brightness of the "sky" is layered with the invisible infrastructure of the place. Silent conduits float like phantom rivers, power fields hum without sound, and the air itself feels thick, saturated with the machine's silent, industrious presence. This is an industrial landscape, but the factories are the size of continents and the engines have the patience of stars.

And it is abundant with life. The quiet is populated. Skittering things patrol the seams, multi-legged constructs of wire and hardened chitin. Their bodies are jointed metal, their movements fluid and unnervingly silent. They are the janitors of this world, scraping away the coppery lichen, clearing blockages, consuming the particulate waste that is the only detritus here. Larger, ponderous creatures, like segmented fortresses on dozens of legs, make their slow circuits across the open plains. They are so vast they take hours to pass, their footfalls producing not a thud but a deep, resonant *thrumb* felt through the soles of your boots and the plates of the floor. They do not eat. They are recharged by the ambient power of the place, sustained by the same fields that enforce the silence.

This is the reality of it: the breakdown of the expected. A silent industrial world. Abundant life in a place with no food chain. A landscape of impossible mass and glacial speed, set in a permanent, brilliant, and unchanging day. The rules are different here. Gravity feels like a suggestion, sometimes weaker on a wide-open plate, stronger near the base of an iron tower. Looking too long at the distant horizon can induce a sense of vertigo, as the parallel lines of the seams cease to be parallel and the mind struggles to reconcile the scale with the density.

The quiet is the most profound rule. It is not an absence of sound, but an active suppression. It is a blanket woven from technology so vast it has become an environmental law. To shout is to feel the very air resist, the sound swallowed whole before it can travel a few meters. The only enduring sound is the one you don't hear, but feel: the low, foundational thrum of the Great Machine, the slow, patient heartbeat of the world itself. It is the sound of a gear beginning its turn, a process that will not be complete for ten thousand years, and it is the only promise of change in a place that is simultaneously new, ancient, and terrifyingly alive.
