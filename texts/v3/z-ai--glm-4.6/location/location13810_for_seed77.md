The horizon is a line of pure heat, a shimmer that cooks the air and reduces the world to a hazy, opalescent glaze. There is no true beginning or end to it, only an endless, low-lying plain that sprawls to a curvature you can almost feel. This is a world of scale so vast that the sky seems to press down, not like a roof, but like the surface of a deeper, more silent ocean.

The ground underfoot is not soil. It is a seamless, tessellated pavement of hexagonal tiles, each the size of a small house, fitting together without mortar or gap. The material is a milky, translucent silica, like fossilized resin, and it’s cool to the touch despite the oppressive air. Walking on it, your feet make no sound, only a soft,鞋ado-like scuff. You must step carefully; in places, the hexagons give way to abrupt, geometric declivities, perfect cubes or pyramids centuries deep, their angled sides still as sharp and unweathered as the day they were cleaved. The erosion here is not a process of wearing away, but of incomplete assembly. This place is not old; it is embryonic.

Rising from this geometric plain are the presences. They are nothing like mountains, which are accretions of chaos and time. These are structures, and they obey a different, more deliberate logic. Colossal, slender arches of a bone-white, porous material span distances of miles, their tops so high they seem to scratch the washed-out turquoise of the sky. They are not solid; they are more like immense, fossilized skeletons, shot through with a tracery of darker channels and voids. Farther off, a forest of single, vast spires stands in perfect formation, each one a tapered monolith of the same bony substance, their surfaces ridged and fluted like a colossal seashell. They are spaced with mathematical precision, creating a sense of profound, rhythmic emptiness.

There is no animal life, and because of this, the world is held in a state of near-silence. The dominant sound is your own breathing. But it is not a true silence. If you stand still for an hour, as the heat climbs to its zenith, you begin to hear the sound of the place. It is a faint, high-pitched *tinking*, like a million tiny glass hammers striking anvils. It is the sound of the crystalline ground expanding in the heat, settling infinitesimally against its neighbors. Occasionally, a deeper, resonant *groan* will roll across the plain from one of the great arches, the sound of a vast structure shifting under its own impossible weight.

The moisture here is a promise, not a presence. During the day, the heat bakes the dampness from the air, leaving it bone-dry and hot enough to warp vision. But as the sun—normal, white, and merciless—begins to dip below the horizon, a change occurs. The bone-white arches and spires, cooler than the ground they stand on, begin to perform their silent function. A sheen of dew, impossibly thick, begins to bead on their porous surfaces. It runs down the fluted ridges and channels, not in chaotic rivulets, but in guided, purposeful streams, tracing the dark veins in the bone-stone.

This is the riverine heart of the world. As full night falls, the great structures slowly weep, each one shedding a silent, constant cataract of water that flows perfectly down their sides and pools at their bases. Here, on the margins of the geometric plain, is where life exists. It is a sparse, dark ecology. Clinging to the base of the arches, fed by the nightly runoff, are thick, moisture-black mats of a primitive, velvet-like moss. They do not grow on the stone; they seem almost fused to it, pulsing with a slow, metabolic rhythm. From their edges emerge single, crystalline shoots that look like spun sugar, catching the faint starlight and glittering with a cold, internal fire. They do not photosynthesize in the daytime; they seem to inhale the precious water at night, becoming firm and taut, and exhale a thin, cool mist by dawn.

This is the rule of this world: it was built, wholly and utterly, but not by hands. It is a total made-environment, a construct of biology so advanced it has become geology. There are no devices because the builders *were* the technology. They created the ground, erected the arches, and designed the nightly condensation cycle as a planetary-scale hydroponic system. Then, they vanished. Perhaps they transformed into their creation, or perhaps they departed, leaving their world-sized garden to run its automated course.

What remains is a mechanism on a continental scale. The active process is not a chaotic ecosystem, but the slow, relentless, and unhurried execution of a pre-written program. The dynamic is the planet's breath, a single, slow inhalation of water at dusk and a long, hot exhalation of mist at dawn. To be here is to stand inside a beautiful, incomprehensible machine that has been running for millennia without its operators, its purpose lost to time. It is a place of perfect function and profound loneliness, a testament to a creation so complete it has outlived its creator.
