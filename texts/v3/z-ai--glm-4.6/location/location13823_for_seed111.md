It is not a landscape so much as a single feature. A spire. A wound in the skin of the world that has not yet scarred over.

From the outside, it is a flaw of impossible geometry. It rises from a flat, stony plain that itself is barely touched by life—a sparse and tenacious black lichen that clings like frost to the rocks. The spire erupts from this monotone as a single, colossal column of a material that is neither stone nor metal. It has the deep, lustrous black of volcanic glass, but a conchoidal fracture reveals an interior of layered, semi-translucent crystal, like a geode cracked open to the cosmos. It is young; the slopes are not smoothed by wind or rain, but are a chaos of razor-sharp edges, fractures that look only seconds old, and facets that gleam with a cold, internal light even in the gloom. This is the extremity of its elevation, a lonely needle piercing a perpetually overcast sky. The scale of the plain is vast, but the scale of the place *is* the spire—a single, dominant, overwhelming presence.

Climbing it would be suicide. The only way in is through a flaw, a great, diagonal cleft that runs deep into its flank, as if the titan that grew it shuddered just once before freezing in place. To step inside is to leave the world.

The air inside is immediately, strikingly different. It is cool, carrying a mineral smell like a battery terminal after a storm, and it is heavy with dampness. This is the source of the spire’s moisture: a constant, patient condensation. The immense, cold surface of the inner crystal sweats, and countless beads of water form, race along tiny, almost invisible grooves, and fall. The sound of this slow process is a constant, whispering counterpoint to the primary noise of the place.

And the noise is overwhelming. It is not the roar of a beast or the rush of wind, but a cacophony on a vast, small scale. It is the sound of a billion tiny hammers. A clicking, clattering, scraping, chiming, and tapping that echoes up the seemingly infinite vertical shaft. This is the sound of the fauna.

There is no other life. The vegetation is almost non-existent—pale, phosphorescent mosses that cling to the wettest patches where the trickling water gathers, looking like forgotten patches of starlight on the floor of the chasm. But the walls… the walls seethe with creatures.

They are the crystalline vermin of this place, the geode-kin. They are born of the slow, fracturing growth of the spire itself. Their bodies are segmented, armored in carapaces of the same black, glassy material as the structure they inhabit, but thinner, allowing a dim, internal bioluminescence to leak through the joints. They have too many legs, each ending in a single, sharp hook of crystalline keratin. They flow up and down the vertical surfaces in glittering rivers, a density of life so intense the walls themselves seem to be moving. The sound is their voices and their passage—the scrape of their limbs on the crystal, the tick-tick-tick of their mandibles as they farm the nutrient-rich condensation and the powdered crystal that flakes from the walls. They are primitive tool-users; some can be seen levering up larger flakes with found shards to build small, temporary nests in the larger crevices, or to tap at particularly resonant parts of the wall.

The light is their own, a strange and living darkness. They glow with a cold, blue-white light, and when disturbed, a cascade of strobing flashes will run up a section of the wall, a silent wave of panic that illuminates the sheer, impossible scale of the space for a breathtaking second. You see then that you are inside a hollow world, a single, immense geological instrument. The shaft rises up into darkness so profound it seems to swallow the faint light of the creatures, and drops down into an equally black pit. It is a place of absolute verticality. There is no "here" and "there," only "up" and "down."

The dynamic of this place is a study in contrasts. The life within it is frantic, a constant, loud, chaotic churn. The geode-kin are born, swarm, and die in rapid cycles, their generations like the cresting and falling of a tide. But the structure they inhabit is glacially, impossibly slow. It is still growing. You cannot see it, cannot feel it, but over millennia, new facets are forming deep within the crystal, new layers being laid down. The entire spire might be a single, patient organism, and the creatures within it are merely its fevered nervous system, its crawling, ticking thoughts. The slow drip of water, the patient creep of the pale moss, are the pulse.

To be here is to be inside a pocket of physics that is not quite our own. It is to be surrounded by a density of strange life that defies the sparsity of the world outside, to be deafened by a billion tiny voices in a space defined by profound silence, to feel the cool damp of a young mountain’s sweat while standing in what feels like the clockwork guts of a dead star. The only rule is the vertical. Down is where the water pools and the moss glows brightest. Up is where the air thins, the cold deepens, and the crystal light of the geode-kin finally winks out, one by one, as they fall.
