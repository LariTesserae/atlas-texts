The world is water. It is the default state, the all-encompassing medium. There is no surface to break, no sky to see, only the immense, patient pressure of the deep.

This is a high place, yet it is submerged. A sunken mountain range, its peaks forming the jagged roof of a vast, self-contained chasm. The scale is not of continents, but of a grand cathedral—a single, bounded feature in the world's anatomy. This is the Geode of Rust. Its floor and towering walls are not worn smooth by eons, but are raw, young, and unforgivably sharp. Sheer cliffs of obsidian and basalt, erupted from the seabed only yesterday in geologic time, jut into the cold, clear water. There is no silt, no sand, only the fine grit of fresh, pulverized rock and the perpetual slow drizzle of mineral dust from above.

The temperature is cool, a constant, pervasive chill that seeps into everything. It is a cold that feels clean, antiseptic, unmarred by the decay of organic life, because there is none. The vegetation parameter is a null set. No algae coats the glassy rock, no kelp waves in the currents. There is no green. The color palette is stark: the jet-black of the volcanic rock, the oxidated reds and oranges of metal, and the cold, electric blue of the light.

The light is the first true strangeness. It is a normal, steady illumination, yet it comes from no sun. The entire chasm is filled with a diffuse, ambient glow, as if the water itself were a weak light-emitting solution. This is the legacy of the tech.

It is an industrial world, but nothing has been built *here*. The "tech" is a graveyard of fallen giants. Colossal, impossible machines lie half-buried in the sharp rock on the chasm floor and crammed into its side canyons. They are wrecks of industry, the rusted, corroded hulls of engines the size of hills and factories that have been shattered on impact. They are not structures; they are landscape, geological features as much as the basalt cliffs. Their purpose is long forgotten, their names erased by corrosion.

Yet they are not dead. This is the source of the light and the ever-present sound.

The sound is loud, but it is a loudness of immense, slow force. A deep, fundamental thrumming vibrates through the water, a sixty-cycle hum that feels more like a constant pressure than a noise. It is the sound of vast, unknown power sources still ticking away within the machine-carcasses, a geological-level process of energy conversion. Interwoven with this industrial drone is the sound of life—the fauna.

The fauna is abundant and dense. The water is thick with them, a living, moving soup. But they are not fish. They are creatures carved for this strange world. Most are small, ranging from the size of a fingernail to that of a lobster. They possess multi-limbed, crystalline carapaces that shimmer with metallic iridescence, allowing them to blend against the rust and rock. They are the Scuttlers. They have no mouths, but rather complex grinding and filtering apparatuses that they use to scrape mineral condensate and energy-bearing micro-particles from the surface of the dead machines. They are scavengers of waste, living on the constant, slow bleed of energy from the industrial bones.

The Scuttlers are a living, moving carpet across the machine-wrecks. Their collective clicking and chittering form a high-pitched, continuous static that overlays the deep hum. The larger creatures are stranger still. There are vast, slow-moving beasts, the Leviathans, whose biological forms have so completely merged with a machine component that it is impossible to tell where the flesh ends and the metal begins. One might be a living being wrapped around the slowly turning core of a reactor, its nervous system wired directly into the machine's dwindling power output. Another might be a filter-feeder that has incorporated a shattered intake fan into its own maw, using the motionless blades to sieve the water more effectively. Many of these creatures carry pieces of salvage—glowing diodes from a control panel, lengths of cable that trail behind them like electrical tentacles, small servo-motors grafted onto their limbs. The light is a combination of the machine-core's ambient glow and the bioluminescence of these creatures, many of which have evolved to mimic the cold, blue-white light of the technology they depend on.

The world's rules are simple and alien. The food chain starts with mineral dust and waste heat. Ecosystem ecology is based on proximity to a power source. Life is defined not by cellular biology alone, but by a seamless integration of the mechanical and the organic. The "weirdness" is the fundamental truth: this is a mechanically-mediated ecosystem, a post-industrial environment that, without any builders, has continued to industrialize biologically.

The overall dynamic is slow. The Leviathans move with the patience of continents. The slow drizzle of rock from the ceiling is a geologic clock. The energy bleed from the machine-cores is decreasing, but on a timescale of millennia. The Scuttlers live and die in frantic, swarming bursts, but the overall picture is one of profound, glacial stability. This is a world in the middle of a change so vast it can barely be perceived—a slow, quiet, roaring conversion of mechanical entropy into biological order, unfolding within a single, sharp-edged, sunken cathedral in the dark.
