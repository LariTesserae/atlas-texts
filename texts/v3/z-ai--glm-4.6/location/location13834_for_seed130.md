There is no sky here. There is only a ceiling, so high and lost in the oppressive dark that it might as well be infinity, but it is a ceiling nonetheless. You know this because of the sound, and because of the air. The sound is the first and final reality. It is a constant, pressure-filled presence that you feel in your bones more than you hear with your ears: a deep, resonant hum that seems to emanate from the very ground beneath you, punctuated by groans of such vast and slow tectonic stress that they register as seismic events. On top of this foundational bass is a cacophony—the distant, rhythmic *CLANG* of something impossibly massive striking metal, the high-pitched, soul-grinding shriek of metal under unimaginable torsional stress, the ceaseless, trillion-gallon rush of deep, subterranean currents.

The air is cool and heavy, a clammy, perpetually damp atmosphere that carries the metallic taste of ozone and some colder, stranger mineral. It is so saturated with moisture that condensation is a permanent feature, a weeping fog that clings to every surface and slicks the ground underfoot. Breathing is a conscious effort, each inhalation thick and wet.

You are standing on a floor made of riveted plates of some bluish-gray metal, each one the size of a city block. The seams between them are deep, canyons filled with black, oily water that reflects neither light nor hope. This is the land: a low, sprawling plain of pitted, eroded industrial substrate. There are no natural stones. The only boulders are colossal, rust-stressed bolts or worn-down gear-teeth the size of hills, their original purpose lost to the deeps of time. The erosion parameter here is not a natural process of wind and rain, but the slow, grinding decay of a machine whose complexity defies comprehension. The land has settled into its final, ancient form, but that form is one of organized chaos, a skeleton of some dead god slumped into geologic stability.

The "vegetation" is almost an afterthought, so sparse is its presence. It does not grow from soil but clings directly to the metal. In the etched pits and corrosion channels, you find it: a strange, symbiotic ecology of mineral and moisture. What looks like a patch of black moss reveals itself on closer inspection to be a crystalline growth, a dense forest of tiny, interlocking metallic filaments. They drink the condensation weeping from the substrate and, in a process that is utterly alien, metabolize its trace elements and ambient energy. At night, or what passes for it, these patches emit a faint, sickly green phosphorescence, the only native light. Here and there, a shelf-like "fungus" will have colonized a vertical surface, a wide, thin plate of oxidized copper that shimmers with iridescent film. It is all lifeless, a passive, inorganic corrosion that mimics the forms of biology.

Water is ever-present. It is not rain, but a constant percolation from above. The ceiling, somewhere in the darkness, weeps. Great, slow drops, fat with dissolved minerals, fall with the unhurried pace of geological time, exploding into silent, oily crowns on the metal plains. In the lower elevations—the depressions and troughs between the vast, machine-like foothills—this condensation has gathered into shallow, stagnant lakes. The water is black and viscous, and on its still surface, rafts of the crystalline moss drift like miniature continents.

There is no animal life. No birds, no insects, no skittering things in the dark. The only movement is the movement of the machine itself, and that movement is measured in centuries. A colossal support structure, a girder thicker than a mountain range, will shift with a sound like the world cracking, settling a fraction of a millimeter under its own impossible load. A distant valve, one the size of a cavern, will groan open, releasing a torrent of pressurized fluid that echoes for days. The "dynamic" nature of this world is the slow, agonizing process of its own decay, a mechanical metabolism that continues to pump and thrum in its final, senile cycle.

This is a world that is totally built, an environment with no natural substrate. Every "mountain" is a housing for a forgotten engine. Every "valley" is a maintenance access shaft fallen into ruin. Every "river" is a coolant channel, now clogged with the sediment of ages. The scale is open and regional; you could walk for weeks and never see a change in the fundamental nature of your surroundings, only variations in the scale and arrangement of the derelict machinery.

The world’s weirdness is subtle and profound. It is in the non-Euclidean angles of the structures, which seem to fold space in ways that make the head ache. It is in the mathematics of the soundscape, which, if you could filter it, you would find has patterns, a language spoken over millennia that no one is left to hear. The rule here is simple: you are inside. You are a bacterium on a fossilized circuit board, a ghost haunting the tomb of a machine. The deafening sound is the machine's ghost, its breathing, its dreams. And you are small, and wet, and cold, and trapped in a cage the size of a world.
