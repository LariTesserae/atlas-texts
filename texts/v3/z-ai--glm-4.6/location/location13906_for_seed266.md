The sky is a hard, thin blue, the color of a sapphire held up to the sun. There are no clouds. There has likely never been a cloud here, not in any way a creature of soft flesh and wet lungs would recognize. Down below, or perhaps, far below, is the Floor, a great flat expanse of cracked, pale mineral that shimmers with heat haze. But one does not live on the Floor. One lives *above* it.

This is the world of the Pillars.

They stretch from the Floor like the petrified bones of forgotten gods. They are not stone in the way a mountain is stone, but stone in the way a fallen tree becomes stone. Their surfaces show a faint, ghostly grain, the memory of vascular bundles and bark seamlessly intergrown with crystalline matrices. Some are colossal, kilometers in diameter at their base, tapering to impossible heights that scratch the stratosphere. Others are slender, needle-sharp spires of obsidian-like basalt. They are the last remains of a flora that drank a different sea and grew toward a different sun, and now they are the only land.

The heat is a constant presence, a warm, dry pressure on the skin. It’s not a killing heat, but a patient one that slowly leaches moisture from any unsealed surface. The air is thin and tastes of ozone and fine, abrasive dust. It is an engineered air, cycled and recycled in immense sealed habitats, but even then, the mineral tang of the world gets in.

You are on a transit-tram, a small, magnetic sled moving along a thick, bundled cable called a Line. The Line stretches for hundreds of kilometers between two of the great Pillars, sagging in a gentle, immense catenary against the sky. For hours, there is only the silent, humming glide of the tram, the vast, empty air on all sides, and the distant Floor.

The city is not a place, but a network. It is called the Weave. It consists of the Habs—sprawling, multi-level habitats built directly onto and into the flanks of the Pillars—and the Lines that connect them. From a distance, a Hab is a glittering geometric ulcer on the side of a brown-grey monolith, crisscrossed with service elevators and gantries. Brightly lit windows patchwork its surface, and from its base, the great industrial Roots descend. Built things are sparse, immense, and isolated. The distance between two neighboring Habs on the same Pillar can be a vertical journey of an hour by service lift.

The sound is a study in silence and vastness. The dominant, ever-present noise is the wind. It’s not a howling or a roaring, but a constant, high-pitched, sifting hiss. It is the sound of eons of erosion, the sound that has rounded the sharp edges of the Pillars into their current, settled, weary forms. This wind carries the dust: microscopic shards of silica, the powdered remains of ancient forests.

Every so often, the silence is broken. From the industrial Roots far below, a sound will travel up the stone—a deep, resonant *BONG*, like a cosmic bell being struck. This is the sound of a Rock-Hammer, a chemical-thermal charge used to fracture a portion of the Pillar for extraction. Then, for a long time, the only sound will be the slow, grinding rumble of the excavator crawlers, a tremor you feel more in your teeth than you hear with your ears.

The industry is the reason for the Weave. The Pillars are not just geography; they are resource. They are composed of a unique mineral, a fossilized silica-cellulose complex that, when subjected to intense electromagnetic and catalytic processes, yields three things: breathable oxygen locked in its crystalline lattice, pure carbon for graphening and construction, and a tremendous amount of usable energy. The Habs are anchored to great processing plants, the Foundries, which are the true hearts of the Weave. They glow with a soft, internal light at night, their chimneys breathing not smoke, but shimmering heat-haze and ionized air.

And there is life.

It is not life as you know it. There are no birds in the sky, no insects in the dust. But the fauna is abundant in its own, scattered way. These are the Geods. They are creatures of crystal and slow energy. You might see one clinging to the side of a Pillar, a thing like a flattened, angular obsidian sculpture, perhaps two meters across. It will be perfectly still, drinking in the sunlight. Only the slow, almost imperceptible crawl of its ventral Grippers—gecko-like pads of retractable mineral fibers—betrays that it is alive. Others, called Glass-wings, fly, but their flight is a thing of glides and sudden, silent bursts of acceleration, their translucent, fractal wings acting as solar sails and atmospheric gyroscopes. They are drawn to the energy fields of the Lines and the Foundries, sometimes congregating in the thousands in the shimmering air above a plant, creating silent, glittering cyclones of light. They neither eat nor excrete in any recognizable way; they simply are. They are an evolution of the mineral world, a life that solved the problem of existence by becoming one with the stone and sun.

The world operates on a glacial timescale. A single Hab may take a century to grow, carve out its new chambers, and lay its next set of anchor cables. The Rock-Hammers inch their way down a Pillar face, a geological process measured in human generations. The Geods live for millennia. The wind, the erosion, the slow warming and cooling of the stone—these are the true political forces of this world.

Standing on an open-air platform of a high-level Hab, looking out across the immense void between Yourself and the next distant pillar, you understand the rules. You are a node in a fragile, vast machine. Your life is dependent on the Line, the Foundry, the silent mining of the dead. The sky is empty and the Floor is a graveyard. But here, strung between the two, in the thin, engineered air, a strange and quiet fever of activity persists. It is the ongoing, slow-motion struggle against the immense,patient, and beautiful stillness of the world itself. The sound is mostly silence, and the pace is mostly stone. But when the Hammer falls, you can feel it in your bones. It is the sound of a city eating a mountain, to take one more breath.
