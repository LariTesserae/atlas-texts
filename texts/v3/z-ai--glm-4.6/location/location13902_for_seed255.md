Here, there is no ground. There is only the Floor.

The Floor is damp and cool to the touch, a dull, dark grey stone that seems to drink the light. It is not a single, uniform surface but a dense, layered mosaic of flagstones, the edges of each chipped and wobbly, grouted with a gritty, mineral paste that glistens with perpetual condensation. To follow a line on the Floor is to trace the generations of its creation. Here, the stones are large and relatively smooth, their edges worn by countless abrasions. There, a new section has been laid, the stones crude and freshly hewn, still shedding a fine, chalky dust that quickly turns to mud in the thin film of moisture.

This dampness is the defining quality of the air. It is not the heavy damp of a swamp, but the thin, crisp damp of a deep cellar, an atmospheric humidity that settles on every surface and in every lung. It carries no scent of soil or decay, only the clean, mineral smell of wet stone, of cold iron, of ozone after a silent, unseen spark. Constant, soft, percussive sounds accompany it: the sigh of air through a thousand unseen fissures, the patient *plink… plink… plink…* of water gathering in minuscule channels on the underside of a beam and falling, finally, into one of the ever-present, dark puddles that never, ever evaporate.

Above and all around, there is no sky. This place is a basin, a low, wide valley carved not by wind or water, but by hands. The scale is deceptive. From any one spot, the space seems vast, open, but this is an illusion created by the oppressive dark. True sight is limited to a dozen meters, the rest lost in a gloom so deep it feels like a physical substance. The "open" space is actually filled with a forest of pillars, columns, and arches so dense they stand shoulder-to-shoulder, forming a continuous, impenetrable wall only broken by the narrow, cobbled thoroughfares. They are not uniform. Some are as thick as a great tree, others tapering to fragile needles. They are canted and bent, their surfaces warty with protruding nodules of spire-stone, their texture porous and complex, like petrified bone or some alien, leached coral. They do not feel built; they feel grown, or excavated, as if the world is a solid block of this strange material from which all existence has been painstakingly hollowed out.

Because everything is made. There is no natural substrate. The Floor is laid, the pillars are raised, the ceilings—distant and unseen, choked with dangling, root-like structures—are vaulted. Yet the technology is achingly primitive. There are no seamless joints, no perfect geometric repetitions. Everywhere are the marks of the Maul and Wedge. The universe is constructed by hand, from simple tools. You can see the fracture lines where huge blocks were split, the crude, hand-wrought iron brackets that support a failing lintel, the immense, carved gears of a forgotten mechanism that stand half-finished, their wooden teeth still showing the slash of a simple adze.

The central paradox is the erosion. Or, rather, the lack of it. There is no weathering, no rounding of edges, no erosion in the geological sense. Instead, the world is in a state of perpetual emergence. It is embryonic, under construction. Always. New pillars, white and softer than the ancient, stained ones, can be seen swelling upwards from the Floor, pushing their way through the flagstones with an impossibly slow, geological violence. New passageways are being chipped out of the "living" rock. The world is not finished. It is a construction site of continental scale, caught in a single, terrifyingly long moment of raw creation. The sound that underscores everything is the proof of this: the Groan. It is a low, deep, resonant thrum that you feel in your teeth more than you hear with your ears. It is the sound of a structure this immense settling, of a billion tons of stone shifting infinitesimally, of new, crystalline fibers forming deep within a pillar as it hardens over centuries.

Light is a commodity, not a constant. The general darkness is broken by two sources. The first is the Glimmer: a faint, internal bioluminescence that suffuses some of the newer growths. In the deepest chasms between pillars, you can see a cool, blue-green phosphorescence emanating from the raw stone, a faint and ghostly light that reveals shapes without defining them. It is the light of life, or something like it.

The second source is rarer and more active: the Flare. The fauna of this place are rare, but they are not absent. They are skittish, chittering things, clad in glossy, black chitin that clicks and rustles as they move through the high, narrow passages. They are not conventionally alive, but a kind of mineral-analogue, sustained by the same slow chemistry that builds the world. Some use the Glimmer to see, but others manufacture their own unholy light. Cinderscales they are sometimes called, for they drag behind them a phosphorescent moss cultivated in a shell on their back, leaving a brilliant, sputtering orange trail that illuminates the architecture for a frantic moment before guttering out. They are the dynamic heart of the ecosystem, predators, or perhaps just engineers, tending to the strange, spectral flora that clings to the upper arches, pruning the weak growths, dragging away the rubble.

The sounds of their passage—the sharp, chittering clicks, the scrape of carapace on stone, the tapping of their specialized limbs—join the Groan, the plinking water, and the distant, rhythmic *thump… thump…* of another construction crew, far away, hammering on a new section of Floor. The noise is loud, a constant symphony of a working, active place. It is a world defined by its noise and its touch, a place that must be felt and heard to be known.

To be here is to exist inside a single, colossal act of craftsmanship. It is to be an ant on a cathedral that will never be consecrated, a forge that will never cool, a tomb that has not yet found its corpse. The rules are simple: the world is raw, the work is endless, and the damp, echoing dark is the only constant. You are a witness to a process of creation so vast and slow it feels, to a fleeting life, like the deepest kind of decay—the eternal, unfinished moment before the world is ever truly born.
