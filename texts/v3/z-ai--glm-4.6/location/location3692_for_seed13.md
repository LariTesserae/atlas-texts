The air is thin and cool, carrying the clean, sharp scent of recently split stone and wet moss. Here, at the top of the world, the mountains are not old, rounded things softened by eons of wind and rain. They are newborn. Colossal shards of a dark, glassy rock thrust violently toward the sky, their flanks bearing the raw, geometric striations of their violent birth. There are no screes of talus at their bases, no worn valleys between their peaks. The landscape is a frozen sculpture of cataclysm, arrested within the first breath of its existence.

This is the rule of the world, an edict known here as the Stillness. Time moves for the living, but for the canvas upon which they live, it has stopped. Erosion is a forgotten, theoretical concept. A raindrop will strike a peak and trace the same path down a microscopic channel it has carved for a thousand years, making it no deeper. The wind keens a constant, mournful note through the jagged passes, but it cannot sand a single edge into softness. The world exists in a state of perfect, immutable equilibrium.

Moisture here is not a matter of weather, but of a constant, patient process the inhabitants call the Sweating. The cool, high-altitude air condenses on the immense, cold faces of the rock every single night, gathering into beads that trace the same tiny, permanent tributaries. These veins of water coalesce into larger, unchanging streams that flow in fixed, glass-smooth channels carved to their exact depth. In these damp fissures and along the constant watercourses, life has found a foothold. The vegetation is paradoxical: intensely lush, yet sparse. Great, fern-like fronds, dark verdant green, unfurl from cracks in the stone, their massive roots gripping the unyielding rock. They grow to a certain size and then simply stop, entering a long, perpetual adulthood. A fallen frond does not rot; it lies on the ground, slowly losing its color to a pale, papery beige, but never truly decaying, becoming a permanent feature of the forest floor.

The fauna is abundant in number, not in concentration. Herds of lean, six-legged beasts called Skinslers roam the wide, open plateaus, their fur the color of the dark stone. They migrate along the same ancient paths, cropping the tough, unchanging grasses. Their lives and deaths strike a perfect, synchronous balance. A Skinsler that falls is not left to decompose. Within a day, it is found by the patient, white-furred Gleaners, scavengers whose presence ensures no biomass is wasted. The bones, however, are never taken. They lie where they fell, gleaming, polished by the endless, gentle wind, creating silent, white mosaics across the valley floor, a permanent census of every life lived.

It is in this setting that the city of Khold was built. It is not a city that dominates the landscape, but one that defers to it. Built is the word, for Khold is an urban sprawl on a primitive, monumental scale. The structures are low and sprawling, carved directly from the un-erodible bedrock or shaped from colossal blocks joined with bronze fittings that will never tarnish. The streets are wide, unnaturally empty, laid out to navigate the sharp, immovable protrusions of the new mountains rather than to conquer them. There are no towering spires, only vast, single-story edifices and open courtyards. The architecture is a conversation with the permanent, a series of accommodations to a world that will not yield.

The technology is one of hands and simple tools. Metals are rare and precious, mined from veins that do not replenish; a bronze chisel is a treasure, passed down through generations, its edge as sharp as the day it was forged because it cannot wear. Wood is harvested from the mummified remains of ancients trees that fell in whatever event created the Stillness, the material petrified but workable with immense effort. Life in Khold is one of maintenance, not innovation. They maintain the water channels that will never change their course. They build onto structures that will never decay. Their culture is one of deep memory and immense patience, for the past is never truly buried. The broken pot from a failed meal a thousand years ago still lies in the street where it was dropped, its glaze undimmed.

And above it all, the sound is mostly quiet. It is a quiet of profound stability. There is no crash of rockslide, no rustle of shedding leaves, no buzz of decay. Only the high, thin whistle of the wind through the sharp peaks, the endless, soft chuckle of the water in its fixed channels, and the distant, echoing calls of the abundant, scattered fauna, living out their cyclical lives against a backdrop that will never, ever change. It is a landscape at the very beginning of its history, forever.
