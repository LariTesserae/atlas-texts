The air here is thick enough to drink. It hangs heavy, warm, and humid, carrying the scent of wet limestone and blooming algae. This is the High Flood, a vast, tepid sea suspended thousands of feet above the world below, though no one here has ever seen the bottom. The water is not rushing; it is a still, immense sheet of obsidian-dark fluid, broken only by the jagged, waking teeth of the earth.

The terrain is in the act of being born. Erosion has not yet touched these places; the geography is raw, embryonic. Great spires of dark, glassy rock punch upward through the surface, their edges sharper than knives, their faces slick and vertical. They emerge as if the liquid itself were crystallizing into stone—violent, sudden thrusts of geology that haven’t had the time to weather into grace. It is a landscape of accidents, a frozen explosion of peaks and shards poking through a mirror.

Perched precariously atop these nascent towers and strung between them on vast, creaking catwalks of woven bamboo and driftwood is the city. It is dense, urban, and claustrophobic, a primitive metropolis of thatch, bone, and unpolished timber. The architecture is determined by the lack of flat ground; homes are stacked vertically, clinging to the cliff faces like barnacles, suspended over the abyss by ropes made of braided vines. Bridges—hundreds of them—lace the peaks together, creating a tangled web of walkways that quiver with the footsteps of the inhabitants.

The technology is simple, bound by the scarcity of metal. Tools are fashioned from obsidian, shell, and the bone of whatever leviathans lurk in the depths. Fires are kept in clay bowls suspended from the eaves, their smoke curling lazily upward to merge with the low, heavy ceiling of mist.

The light is dim, a perpetual twilight. There is no sun to speak of, only a diffuse, pearly luminescence that seems to emanate from the water itself or the thick cloud layer that blankets the high elevation. Colors are muted—mossy greens, charcoal grays, the dull brown of drying reeds. The lack of sharp light makes the water look like an extension of the air, a seamless medium that swallows the horizon.

Vegetation is lush, greedy, and everywhere it can find a purchase. Where the rock is too steep to build, ferns the size of ships unfurl their fronds, thick mosses carpet the vertical cliffs, and creepers hang down in long, silent curtains, brushing the surface of the water far below. The plants grow fast here, fueled by the heat and the moisture, reclaiming the walkways and the roofs of the huts if they are not constantly hacked back.

Fauna is rare, but what exists is strange. Occasionally, a massive, shadowed shape will break the surface of the water miles away, displacing enough liquid to send a slow, rhythmic swell rolling through the archipelago, making the entire city sway gently. The skies are mostly empty of birds; instead, small, translucent creatures with too many wings drift through the humid air, clinging to the damp walls of the buildings, glowing faintly like dying embers.

The dominant sound is not the roar of waves, but a deep, resonant hum—a vibration that travels up from the deep water and through the stone pilings of the city. It is a sound felt in the teeth more than heard by the ears. Beyond that, there is the quiet creak of stressed timber, the soft slap of water against the stilts, and the murmur of human voices speaking in hushed tones, as if afraid to wake the sleeping earth.

Time moves slowly here. The "emerging" erosion means the mountains grow, but they grow on a geologic timescale that mocks the frantic scratching of human life. The water level rises and falls across centuries, gradually swallowing the lowest towers while new ones erupt in the distance. The city is constantly rebuilding itself, migrating upward, always balancing on the fine line between the solid, sharp rock and the warm, enveloping deep.

It is a place of suspension—literally suspended between water and sky, and metaphorically suspended between the violent act of creation and the slow, patient decay that has yet to come. It feels less like a place that *is*, and more like a place that is *happening*, a slow, wet, exhale of the planet.
