The light here is violent, yet it has no source.

We are beneath the crust, in a hollow the size of a cathedral nave, yet the air is flooded with a blinding, studio-white luminescence. There is no sun, no bulb, no magma glow. The brightness seems to bleed directly out of the stone itself, a passive, aggressive radiation from the mineral-rich veins that web the ceiling. To look up is to look into a snowblindness that forces the eyes shut. It is a place of perpetual, high-noon exposure, hidden away from the sky.

The silence is heavy, weighted by the humidity. The air is warm (1.6), a tropical bath held within a stone jar. It smells of wet ozone and crushed celery. It is damp (0.7), but not wet; the walls do not weep, they perspire. A fine, slick sheen of condensation coats every surface, making the rock glisten like oily skin. The moisture gathers into singular, heavy droplets that hang from the stalactites for minutes, swelling with agonizing slowness (1.1) before they finally detach. The sound of a drop hitting the floor is a sharp, singular *clock-tick* that echoes for too long, the only marker of time in this place.

The geology is settled (1.9). This is not a jagged, young cave system. The walls have been smoothed by eons of unseen currents into flowing, parabolic curves. There are no sharp edges, only rounded bulges and sweeping depressions, giving the space the feeling of being the inside of a giant, hollowed bone. The floor is a vast, flat pan of compacted silt, cracked in a tessellated pottery pattern where it has dried and re-wetted a thousand times.

Here, the scale (0.6) plays tricks on the eye. The room is not infinite—perhaps a hundred yards across—but the lack of features makes it feel taller and wider than it is. It is aggressively empty (0.0). The distance between objects is vast. You could stand in the center and shout, and the voice would die in the humidity before reaching the walls.

And yet, there is life.

In the vast, open expanse of the silt floor, the fauna (1.7) persists. They are sparse (1.4 vegetation?), no—the vegetation is the sparest element of all. There is no green, no canopy. The flora here is reduced to solitary, science-fictional oddities: thick, trunk-like stalks that stand like isolated obelisks, their surfaces photosynthetic and translucent, glowing faintly pink against the white glare of the room. They do not cluster; they stand with mathematical precision, fifty yards apart, as if placed by an architect.

Between these lonely towers graze the "Menders."

They are the dominant presence, strange (1.7) and abundant in their solitary way. They resemble a cross between a glass mantis and a weathered tortoise, standing perhaps waist-high to a human. Their shells are translucent, revealing the slow, churning workings of their internal organs. They move with glacial indifference, their six legs stepping delicately over the dried mud cracks.

They are masters of primitive technology (1.4). There are no walls here, no cities, but the Menders carry tools. Each creature clutches a smooth river stone in a single, pincer-like claw, worn perfectly round by years of grip. They use these stones to tap at the earth, listening to the vibrations, or to gently scrape the nutrient-rich algae from the pillars of vegetation. It is a tool-using culture stripped of all complexity—no fire, no wheels, no homes—just the rock, the claw, and the slow, rhythmic *tap-tap-tap* that occasionally punctuates the humid silence.

The ecology is one of patience. There is no rushing here. The dynamic of the world (1.1) is akin to the movement of tectonic plates. The Menders live for centuries. They mate once a decade, a silent, touching of antennae that takes hours. They do not flee; they do not fight. When one dies, it simply stops moving, its shell turning opaque and white, eventually crumbling into the silt to feed the next lonely stalk.

It is a room of intense brightness and profound isolation. A pocket world where the air is thick enough to taste, the light is bright enough to blind, and the rules are written in the language of stone and slowness. There is a sense that if you were to turn your back on the room for a year, nothing would have changed when you looked again, except perhaps the position of a single drop of water, finally ready to fall.
