The air is the first thing you notice. It’s not thin, as one might expect at this extreme elevation, but heavy and wet, like breathing in cold tea. It clings to the lungs, each breath a chore, and brings with it the smell of wet stone, ozone, and something vaguely mineral, like the tang of a battery terminal. There is no sky, not in the way of blue and open sun. There is only a ceiling: a perpetual, pearlescent stratum of cloud that presses down on the world, diffusing the light into a shadowless, weary grey. The sun is a myth, a brighter patch of glow behind the pearl that you can stare directly at without pain.

This is the Roof, the High Moil, a plateau so ancient it has forgotten the violence of its own birth. The elevation is extreme, but the rock is not jagged. Eons of patient, slow erosion have worn the mountain ranges down into a surreal landscape of softly rounded hills and vast, shallow basins, all swaddled in a silence so profound it feels like a pressure. The stone everywhere is dark, a deep basaltic grey, and polished smooth by uncountable aeons of wind and water. In places, it dips into natural stone bowls, some the size of a carriage, others wide as lakes, all filled to the brim with still, black water. There are no rivers, no streams; the water does not flow here, it simply *is*, collected from the clouds that snag on the plateau’s edge and weep their moisture into every crevice.

A thick, velvet carpet of moss covers everything, glowing with a faint, internal phosphorescence in the gloom. It grows over the hills, seeps into the stone basins, and softens the edges of the world. Rising from this moss carpet are thick, leathery ferns the color of rust, and low-lying shrubs with leaves like polished jade. The vegetation is lush, but horizontal, hugging the ground as if afraid of the empty air above. It is a jungle pressed flat.

Into this dim, damp, and ancient world, the city was built. It is not so much placed upon the land as grown out of it. They call it Harken Foundry, and it is dense and urban. Structures, hewn from the same dark stone as the plateau, are packed shoulder-to-shoulder in the valleys and braced against the sides of the rounded hills. There are no soaring skyscrapers; the architecture is compressed, layered, and defensive. Narrow, dripping alleyways twist between buildings slick with moss and mineral stains. Pipes, thick as a man’s waist, wrapped in tarred cloth and beaded with condensation, writhe along the buildings like metallic vines. They connect everything in a web of industry.

This is no quiet town of artisans. Harken Foundry is industrial, its purpose a strange and subtle one. The city does not mine the ancient rock; it tunes it. Beneath the city, and out in the uninhabited moss-fields, are the foundations of the Foundry’s purpose: resonator towers. These are not buildings of steel but immense, single-piece structures of the native basalt, shaped into colossal tuning forks that stand half-buried in the moss. They are driven not by fire, but by the wind. Wind-scoops, vast, silent turbines carved into the hillsides, channel the constant high-altitude breezes into the bases of the resonators, setting them into a state of perpetual, ultra-low-frequency vibration. The hum is mostly quiet, felt more in the bones than heard with the ears, a constant thrumming that seems to emanate from the planet itself. This vibration, this industrialized geophysics, is used to refine or transmute something, to create a source of energy or a material that cannot be made elsewhere. The specifics are a secret of the Foundry’s engineers.

The world is active with this slow, powerful process. The resonators thrum, the wind-scoops turn with barely-audible whooshes, and deep within the Foundry, great pistons the size of houses pump in glacial, rhythmic cycles. Steam, thick and white, plumes from occasional vents, only to be swallowed by the pressing cloud layer. The dynamic motion of the city is a counterpoint to the deep geological stillness.

Fauna is rare, which makes its presence all the more unsettling. The low-frequency thrum has driven away most birds and mammals. The only things that thrive are strange, adapted creatures. Lichen-mites, pale and translucent, graze on the glowing moss. Sometimes, in the deep alleys, you might see a Gutter-crawl, a skein of sinew and chitin that moves like a liquid along the pipes, feeding on the mineral condensation. The silence is their domain, punctuated only by the drip, drip of water into a stone basin, the distant, metallic groan of a resonator shifting its tone, or the hiss of steam from a relief valve. The most common sound is your own footsteps, the crunch of wet gravel and the squelch of moss under your boots.

It is a place of contradictions: ancient rock pierced by industrial age; lush life in a grey, dim world; immense scale compressed into dense, claustrophobic spaces; a profound quiet that underlies a constant, powerful activity. To stand on a mossy hill overlooking Harken Foundry is to feel the deep, patient thrum of the ancient earth being played like an instrument, while the clouds weep onto a city that is slowly being consumed by the very green it was built to dominate. It is a landscape at the end of its geologic life, and at the peak of a strange, new one.
