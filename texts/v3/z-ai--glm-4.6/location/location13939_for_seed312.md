The world does not end in a horizon here; it ends in a wall. Not a single wall, but a thousand, a vast, continental rampart of granite and basalt thrown up to the thin, cold sky. This is the Aethelgard Plateau, a place of such sheer scale that it generates its own weather, sweeping clouds into its jagged teeth and spitting them back as fine, crystalline snow. The scale is not horizontal but vertical, an immensity of up and down.

The mountains are young, unnervingly so. There are no gentle, rolling slopes here, no foothills to ease the transition. The rock faces are as sharp as a freshly struck flake, sheer cliffs that fall away into bottomless chasms. Erosion has not yet had the time to soften the edges; it is a process currently dominated by the slow, grinding fracture of ice and the patient cleaving of wind. The valleys are not lush and rounded, but deep, angular clefts, geological scars still raw from the earth’s violent throes of uplift.

The cold is a constant presence. It is the air itself, thin enough to make you light-headed yet heavy enough to press a deep, persistent chill into your bones. The sun, when it shines, is a brilliant, merciless disk that offers light but little honest warmth. Its light is different here—normal in spectrum, but sharper, cleaner, stripping shadows of their softness and making every edge crisp. Except for the few hours around midday, the land lives in a state of diffused, cool luminosity, the light reflecting endlessly between snow-dusted peaks and galleries of cloud.

Water is the great paradox. The plateau is high enough that most of the world's weather happens far below, yet an intermittent dampness defines life. It does not fall in nurturing rains but seeps from the rock itself. Percolating through the strata, the ancient meltwater of forgotten glaciers emerges as a constant weeping from the cliff faces. This is the source of all life here. Icicles, thick as a man’s arm, pipe from overhangs, marking the的位置 of unseen fissures. In quiet moments, you can hear the slow, rhythmic *drip… drip… drip* echoing in the canyons, the patient clock of the world. The plants that cling to existence are a testament to this moisture. Sparse, yes, but tenacious. Dark, rubbery lichens paint the rock in ochre and rust. Mosses form thick, spongy cushions in the perpetual seeps, their green a shock of vibrant defiance. In rare, sheltered niches where soil has had eons to gather, stunted, windswept trees twist themselves into gnarled, bonsai-like sculptures, their every branch pointing away from the prevailing wind.

This overwhelming, raw landscape is not empty. It is dense with life, and most profoundly, dense with the presence of people.

This is the city of Carven-Aerie.

It is not built so much as it is liberated from the stone. From a distance, it is nearly invisible, a pattern of subtle discoloration and shadow that confuses the eye. Look closer, and the scale reveals itself. The entire face of one of the plateau’s central mountains, a monolith named the Needle Spire, is a settlement. It is a city of staggering verticality, a termite colony carved from a mountain’s corpse. The people here—known, with simple pragmatism, as the Carvers—possess a primitive tech that is breathtakingly sophisticated in its application. Their tools are stone hammers, bone chisels, ropes and levers. With these, they have hewn a civilization into the rockface.

Habitations are not free-standing structures but deep, multi-room warrens, excavated with generations of painstaking labor. Doorways are heavy, stone circle-seals that can be rolled into place to block the biting wind. The city is layered. The city is dense, a saturate press of presences. The walkways are narrow, sometimes no more than a shoulder’s width, forcing a constant, polite acknowledgement of others. The buildings are packed together, sharing walls, roofs, and foundations. From within a home, you can hear the muffled sounds of your neighbours: the scrape of a stone knife, the low murmur of conversation, the cry of a child. The city is not loud, however. The prevailing sound is a hush. There is no metal-on-metal clangour, no roar of engines. It is the quiet of stone on stone, of footsteps on packed earth, of the wind that funnels through the canyon corridors—a low, mournful hum that is the city’s background music. It is a place where a shout feels like a transgression, and a song is a sacred, rare gift.

The ecology of the plateau is what makes this all possible, centered on two key lifeforms. The first is the Strider, a great, shaggy, six-limbed goat-beast the size of a small van. They are herbivores of incredible hardiness, navigating near-vertical cliffs with an unnerving, insect-like grace. They are the primary source of meat, hide, and bone for the Carvers. Their milk, curdled and fermented into a sharp, hardy cheese, is a dietary staple.

The second, more magnificent, is the Ridgeback. These are the masters of the sky, vast, leathery-winged creatures with bodies like a wolf’s and wingspans that can block out the sun when they pass overhead. They nest on the highest, most inaccessible ledges. The Carvers do not hunt them directly. Instead, a revered and dangerous caste of the called the Scavengers, will rappel down into the nesting chasms to collect fallen chicks, unhatched eggs, or the carcasses of adults who die in the ferocious aerial battles for territory. The ridgeback’s hide is too tough for clothing, but its ligaments make the finest ropes, its hollow, nigh-unbreakable bones are used for tools, and its eggshells are fashioned into water vessels. The relationship is one of profound respect and profound danger.

The slow dynamic of this world governs all things. A Carver does not think in terms of days or weeks, but in the time it takes to hollow a new room or to raise a child to be able to safely navigate the bridges. Seasons are not gradual fades but violent, sudden shifts: the deep, months-long freeze of the Cold-Sleep, when the Carvers retreat into their deepest rooms, sustained by stored food; the brief, frantic bloom of the Sun-Wake, when the Striders descend to the alpine meadows to give birth, and the city focuses all its energy on the hunt; the Long-Wind, when the sky is dominated by the migratory flocks of Ridgebacks and the Scavengers earn their dangerous keep.

The rules of the world are not written laws, but unbreakable commands of physics and ecology. Thou shalt not waste water. Thou shalt not break a bridge. Thou shalt not hunt a nursing Striker or touch a living Ridgeback’s nest. These are not moral codes; they are the simple conditions of continued existence. It is a society built on an absolute foundation of communal responsibility and individual bravery.

To stand on a high-level walkway in Carven-Airie is to occupy a unique position in the universe. To one side, the warm, entrancing, and utterly silent labyrinth of stone—home. To the other, the vast, cold, and thunderous quiet of the plateau—the everything else. The wind carries the scent of dust, lichen, and the far-off, sharp tang of a carcass far below. You feel the slow, grinding pulse of the geology beneath your feet and see the timeless dance of the clouds above. It is not a place that welcomes life, but one that life, in its most stubborn and concentrated form, has refused to leave. It is a city that is both a fortress against the world and, in its immense and patient solitude, a kind of beautiful tomb.
