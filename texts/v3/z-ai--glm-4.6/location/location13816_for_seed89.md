The world up here is a bone. An ancient, sun-bleached plateau of stone that the winds have planed down for millennia, leaving it smooth and vast. The air is thin and sharp, smelling of ozone and distant frost. This is the world of the raptors and the sky, an empty throne room of a god that long since departed. But the plateau is not uniform. It is cleaved by a wound.

This is not a grand, sweeping canyon. It is a fissure, a crack in the world's foundation, narrow as a cathedral is long. From the edge, it looks like a simple, geometric shadow cut into the stone. To approach it is to walk from the high, wind-scoured table into a sudden, profound silence. The sky vanishes above, replaced by a thin line of dusty blue. The stone underfoot changes from the pale, lichen-dusted surface of the plateau to something darker, slick, and cool. The temperature, already warm despite the altitude, becomes humid and stagnant. The air grows heavy.

And then comes the sound.

It begins as a low hum, a vibration felt in the teeth and bones more than heard with the ears. As you descend, following weirdly smooth, downward-curving ramps of stone that feel less like natural formations and more like worn pathway, the hum resolves. It is not one sound, but a million. It is the drone of an engine idling forever, the clatter of a thousand looms worked by frantic, invisible hands, the rustle of dry leaves amplified to the roar of a wildfire. It is an overwhelming, layered, deafening tide of life that fills the narrow space to bursting. This is the sound of the Chasm.

The light that reaches the floor is a mere suggestion, a deep and somber twilight. The only illumination comes from the walls themselves. The stone is so dark as to be nearly black, but it is not bare. It is coated.

The Chasm is not empty. It is dense. Every vertical surface, from a dozen feet below the rim to the unseen floor a thousand feet down, is alive. It is a living tapestry, a single, writhing organism composed of uncountable billions of smaller ones. They are the cliff-weavers, the silent rulers of this vertical empire.

They are things of chitin and short, dense fur, shaped like a gecko crossed with a marmot, six-limbed and absurdly flat. Their limbs end in pads of microscopic, hook-like hairs that allow them to cling to the sheerest rock, to move with the unconcerned ease of a creature for which "down" is a choice. They are layered atop one another, packed so tightly that the stone beneath is almost completely obscured. Chitinous plates click and scrape against one another in a constant, dry hiss. Their pelts, in shades of dark grey and mottled brown, create the illusion of a moving, breathing rock face. The sparse vegetation is limited to the tough, black molds and algae that thrive on their constant waste and the water that condenses on their bodies. It is an ecosystem built on a single species.

Their primitive technology is one of alteration. Generations of weavers have scraped certain patches of rock clean with their claws, creating smoother, more hospitable nesting ledges. They have dropped small, dark pebbles into crevices, perhaps to wedge them open or change their acoustics. They have built nothing, but they *have* made their home more home-like, through the slow, patient work of eons.

The overwhelming sound is their communication—a constant, high-frequency chittering and clicking that carries information, warnings, and social status through the darkness. It is the language of the hive. The weirdness is their light. In the deep gloom, they glow. Faint, phosphorescent patterns of ghostly green and cool blue trace the veins beneath their chitin, shifting and pulsing in slow waves. It is a language of light, creating an ever-changing map of constellations on the canyon walls. A lone weaver's light might pulse a nervous, fast blue when it is disturbed, which will ripple outwards in a wave of agitation through the bodies pressed against it, a silent and visual echo to the rising tide of clicks and whirs.

The water here is the Chasm’s breath. The warm, humid air from the world below snakes its way up the fissure and meets the cold stone of the upper walls. Condensation forms constantly, tracing invisible paths down the rock before beading and falling. The floor of the Chasm, unseen, must be a mire of damp stone, Shed fur, and organic sludge, water dripping from the sky of bodies in a perpetual, gentle rain. This is the only source of moisture, a patient and reliable process that defines life in this dark pocket.

To stand here is to be a foreign element in a system that predates you and will outlast you by ages. The geological dynamic of the place is one of supreme slowness. The stone walls are settled, in their final form, eroding by imperceptible grains. The grand cycles are measured in the time it takes for a single drip of water to wear a new pocket in the stone. But the life within this stone is frantic, loud, and claustrophobically dense. The Chasm is a paradox: a place of absolute stillness and overwhelming noise, of eternal darkness pierced by living light, of a single creature that has, in its sheer numbers, become a landscape unto itself. It is a world in a crack.
