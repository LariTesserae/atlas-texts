The air is thick enough to drink. It is not mist and not fog, but a humid, mineral breath that hangs heavy in the lungs, tasting of hot ozone and wet iron. The heat is a constant, oppressive presence, a weight rather than a glow, radiating not from a sky but from the ground beneath your feet. There is no sun, no moon, no celestial bodies. Only a dim, sourceless twilight, a glistering gloom that lends a soft, pearlescent sheen to everything.

You stand on a vast, low-lying plain that extends to every horizon, though the horizons are blurred by the heat and the watery air. The ground is not earth. It is a sprawling, continuous substrate of a dark, glassy material, like cooled obsidian slag. It is not flat, but rippled with immense, slow-moving curves and swells, as if the entire crust were a sheet of black, petrified honey being kneaded by a god-size hand. It is new. Unfinished. The erosion here is not one of decay, but of assembly. This is a world in the act of its own creation.

Across this endless, dark floor, the only things that break the monotony are the scaffolds. They rise like skeletal trees, miles apart, their spindly forms reaching hundreds of feet into the dim sky. They are not made of wood or metal, but of the same glassy, black substance as the ground, though here it has been coerced into geometric, crystalline forms. They are fractal and impossibly precise, branching into lattices that glow with the same soft, directionless light that fills the air. They do not seem to support anything; they simply are, like forgotten radio towers in an ocean of slag. This is the extent of the "vegetation"—a forest of silent, inert, illuminated structures.

Everything is wet, but it has not rained. The moisture weeps from the world itself. From every seam, every fissure in the glassy ground, from every joint in the crystalline scaffolds, water beads and flows. It is not pure water, but a thin, viscous fluid that feels slick and warm to the touch. It collects in innumerable shallow pools and marshy depressions, their surfaces trembling with the constant, subsonic vibration of the land. Weeping stones. A sweating world.

The most profound and unsettling feature is the quiet. It is a silence so dense it feels like a vacuum. The landscape is *turbulent*, but its violence is silent. In the distance, you will see it happen: a new swell of ground will begin to rise, not with a groan of grinding rock but with a smooth, silent flow, like wax in a lava lamp. A thousand-foot-long weal will push upward from the crust, the material at its apex glowing cherry-red for a moment before cooling back to black. As it forms, a network of fine cracks spiders across its surface, and from them, fresh sheets of the warm, oily water weep out, steaming as they meet the humid air.

There are no animals. No birds, no insects, no cries in the distance. The only fauna is the process itself. There are builders here, but they have no bodies you can perceive. Their "technology" is primitive in its execution but vast in its effect: it is the act of focused will, of percussive, rhythmic thought. They are hammering reality into shape. The landscape is their anvil, the glassy substrate their ore, and the dim, silent, chaotic shift and flow of the ground is the endless, eternal strike of their hammer. This place is a forge, but its fires are internal, its metal is the fabric of spacetime, and its blacksmiths are principles, or entities, whose simple tools—intention, geometry, and repetition—are powerful enough to unmake and remake a world on a continental scale.

To be here is to stand on the skin of a thing that is becoming itself, a raw, embryonic artifact of impossible scope. It is a place with no past but an eternal, violent present. The rules that bind other worlds—biology, geology, the gentle passage of time—are moot. This is a place of pure, quiet, and ceaseless construction. A reality whose foundations are still being laid, one silent, world-shaping hammer blow at a time.
