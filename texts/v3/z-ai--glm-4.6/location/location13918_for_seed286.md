Here is the world that emerges from those parameters.

There is no land, not in the way one understands it.

You are in water, up to your neck, but the sky is not above you—it is miles away, a thin, distant blue sheet fringed with the white crests of cloud formations that will never touch you. The air here is thin and cool, carrying the clean, sharp scent of ozone and distant ice. This is an ocean suspended at an altitude where mountains should be, on a plateau that has forgotten the rest of the planet. They call it the High Sea, though there is nothing lower to measure it against.

The temperature is a constant, pervasive cool—not a biting chill, but a clammy, all-encompassing cold that seeps into your bones and makes the air feel like a cold stone pressed to your cheek. The light is normal, a familiar sun-and-sky cycle, but it feels sterilized as it descends through the clear, still atmosphere, striking the surface of the water with a skeletal purity before being diffused into the depths below.

To call the water a sea is almost a misnomer. To descend is to not find a floor of rock or sand, but a substrate of living matter. This is the truth of the High Sea: it is not a body of water resting on a continent, but a single, continent-scale organism that holds the water upon its vast, vegetative back. There is no distinction between landscape and life. The ground is a plant.

This entity has no name in any human tongue, for no human has ever built a language to describe it. One might call it the Mangal-colossus. Its ‘surface’ is a complex, interlocking matrix of fronds, stalks, and broad, leathery leaves the size of small nations, all growing from a shared, planet-spanning root system. The water pools in the colossal, waxy valleys between these photosynthetic continents, which rise from the depths like the flanges of an impossible engine. Erosion is a meaningless concept here; time has not worn the Mangal down, but has simply been the medium in which it perfected its form. It is ancient, settled, existing in a state of absolute equilibrium with itself.

And it is loud. The sound is not a roar or a crash of waves, for there are no shores to break upon. The sound is a deep, resonant thrum that vibrates through the water, through the soles of your feet, into your very marrow. It is the sound of a world-sized metabolism. It is the hum of the Mangal-colossus’s industrial biology. Its circulatory system is a planet-scale hydraulics network, pumping nutrient fluids through veins as wide as rivers. The steady, rhythmic pulsing you feel is the slow, powerful beat of its heart, a chamber of living muscle and hypertrophied wood deep beneath the sea.

This is its technology. The Mangal has evolved beyond mere survival into a state of perpetual, self-regulating industry. Certain patches of its surface are studded with crystalline structures that hum with a barely audible, high-frequency whine, slowly exhaling plumes of pure oxygen into the thin air—biological refineries. Great, stalk-like organs, capped with mineral deposits like industrial smokestacks, continuously filter the water of impurities, the waste crystallizing and falling away to be slowly reincorporated into the substrate. The entire system is electrified, a living grid. Veins of metallic, conductive sap carry bio-electric charges across its whole body, creating a low-level, permanent electromagnetic field that makes the hair on your arms stand on end. There is no built structure, yet this is a world of factories, engines, and mass production. It simply grew that way.

Life exists here, but sparsely, as if the sheer scale of the engineer-organism leaves little room for anything else. The fauna are strange and highly specialized. Flat, manta-like creatures glide silently through the deep channels between the Mangal’s fronds, their skin patterned with circuit-like motifs that seem to absorb the ambient bio-electricity. Schools of small, silver fish with turbine-shaped fins dart through the water, not disturbing it so much as flowing with it, feeding on the algal blooms that are the Mangal’s equivalent of industrial runoff. They do not compete aggressively; their populations have been stable for millennia, precisely balanced with the energy output of their host-planet.

This is the core of its strangeness and the rule of this world: nothing ever changes. The High Sea is a frozen equilibrium. The Mangal does not grow, nor does it decay. Its internal processes are a perfect, closed loop. The climate is static. The fauna are born and die in numbers that have been constant since the last ice age on the world below. It is a machine of life that has reached its final, ideal state and now simply runs, forever. The loud hum you hear is the sound of a system operating at 100% efficiency, a chord that has been held for eons and will be held for eons more. It is a world that is not dead, but has completed the process of living. It is a landscape that has achieved its ultimate form and now exists, vast and submerged, cool and industrial, a continent of living, breathing, humming tech, frozen in a timeless and perfect state.
