The first thing you understand is the sound. It is not a single noise but a condition, like humidity or pressure. It is a constant, layered presence that you feel in your bones as much as you hear with your ears. There is the low, deep thrum that seems to come from the ground itself—a slow, ponderous resonance, the sigh of an immense living thing sleeping. Over this, the hiss and gurgle of a million trickles of water, a polyrhythmic percussion of drips and weeps. And woven through it all, a high, dense tapestry of clicks, chirrups, skittering legs, and the whir of countless wings. It is the sound of wet, teeming life in a state of perpetual, quiet riot.

You are standing not on soil or stone, but upon something that is both. The ground is a dense, fibrous mass, dark ochre and russet in color, spongy underfoot but resilient, like aged wood or compressed cork. It gives slightly with each step, then holds firm. This is the substrate. It is a single, continuous organism, a vast, interwoven mat of sphagnum, anemone-root, and a hundred other nameless bryophytes and creepers that have fused over millennia into a unified tissue. The ground *is* the plant. It fills the small, high-altitude amphitheater you occupy, flowing up the steep sides to meet the mist, a seamless, organic carpet. In places, it has bulged and knotted itself into pillars, buttresses, and sweeping arches that hold the sky.

Here and there, this mass is not left to its own devices. It has been shaped. The “urban” nature of the place is subtle, a partnership rather than an imposition. Dwellings are not built upon the surface but hollowed out from within. Simple, rounded openings, no more than a meter high, lead into warm, dry chambers carved from the living substrate. The tool marks are visible: the faint, spiraling scrape of a long-bladed shell used to pare the fibrous walls, the smoothed, almost polished concavity of a sleeping niche where generations of bodies have rested. This is primitive tech in its purest form—the art of coaxing space from a willing host, of nudging a living thing into a form that also serves life. The construction is ongoing and constant; you can see fresh, pale-green scrapings near a new doorway where a family is expanding their chamber.

The water is the architect of this world. At this elevation, the air is always cool and heavy with moisture. Wind, forced up the mountainside, loses its moisture as it presses into this sheltered dell. It condenses on every surface, not as sharp rain but as a perpetual, gentle weeping. The plant-substrate is a sponge, drinking the mist from the air and the seepage from the rock face it clings to. It cannot hold it all. Water emerges from the walls in constant, clear tears, tracing intricate patterns down the fibrous columns before being absorbed again by the floor. In places, the water has worked with the plant to create hollow channels, slick and dark, that gurgle with a steady flow, carrying the excess down into the unseen heart of the mass.

Light is a precious, filtered commodity. The sun, when it breaks through the high clouds, does not strike the floor in harsh beams. It is caught, scattered, and softened by the canopy of translucent, fan-like fronds that grow from the upper surfaces of the structure. These fronds, veined with delicate patterns of red and yellow, act as a colossal, living stained-glass window. The light that reaches the ground is therefore dappled, warm in hue but cool in intensity, casting the entire space in a perpetual, gentle twilight of amber and sage green.

And then there is the fauna. Overwhelming is the only word. This is not a land of large beasts; the scale is too constrained, the architecture too intricate. Instead, the space is saturated with smaller life. Gossamer-winged insects with bodies like polished driftwood swarm in the air, their wings chiming softly as they collide. Ample, slow-moving amphibians with waxy, patterned skins cling to the weeping walls, their croaks adding a guttural bass to the soundscape. Armoured, many-legged things harvest algae from the damp floors, their shells clicking together like a thousand castanets. Birds, small and swift, with needle-like beaks, dart through the upper fronds, their sharp calls piercing the thrum. The turbulence is this life in motion: a constant, chaotic bustle, a hyperactive ecology where every surface is occupied, every niche filled. It is not violent, but it is ceaseless.

This is a place that has existed in its own settled state for an age. The erosion of the underlying rock is long-established, the form of the basin final. The plant-substrate has reached equilibrium with the water and the light. The people who live here—the hollow-carvers—are as much a part of the ecosystem as the birds and the amphibians. They do not seek to dominate or change the fundamental nature of the place. Their urbanism is an act of belonging, a domestication through integration. They live within the body of the world, listening to its deep breath, shaping its hollows, and sheltering under its weeping, living walls. The sound is the sound of their home.
