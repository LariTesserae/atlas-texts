You don't enter this place so much as you are submerged in it. The first sensation is not sight or smell, but sound. It is not a noise, not a simple cacophony; it is a solid, palpable wall of vibration. It’s the pressure you feel before a thunderclap, but the thunderclap never arrives. It is a constant, humming, shrieking, chittering, rustling, clicking *presence* that presses against your skin, vibrates the bones of your skull, and makes the air feel thick and heavy, like water. To breathe is to inhale the noise. This is the first and only law of this space: it is loud beyond all comprehension.

When your eyes adjust, or perhaps when your mind simply surrenders to a new way of seeing, you perceive the source. You are in a cavern, but a cathedral is a closer analogy. The scale is immense, yet it feels contained, a single, bounded space. The floor beneath your feet is not stone or soil, but a spongy, resilient mass of living matter, deep green and threaded with pulsing, violet vessels. It yields slightly under your weight, like dense moss grown over millennia. This is the soil. This is the bedrock.

From this floor, colossal pillars of the same material, glistening with a constant sheen of condensation, rise towards a vaulted ceiling lost in a soft, sourceless glow. The walls are not separate from the pillars; they are simply where the pillars have sprawled and fused, forming a continuous, living enclosure that encircles the entire space. This is the vegetation. It is not growing *in* the environment; it *is* the environment, a seamless, total, plant-as-substrate. There is no distinction between a wall, a column, and a floor. It is one contiguous, architectural organism, settled into its form for an age, its great hollowed-out space as ancient and eroded as any canyon.

And between these great living structures… nothing.

The density is a vast, hollow zero. The interior volume is a near-void, an immense emptiness that makes the sound all the more impossible. The acoustic properties should be those of a whispering gallery, but instead of amplifying a single sound, the space magnifies trillions. It is an empty arena filled with a deafening audience of the world itself.

The warmth is cloying, tropical, and perpetually damp. A fine, ceaseless condensation weeps from every surface, tracing delicate, ever-changing rivers down the colossal walls. Pearly droplets form on the edges of leaves the size of your torso and fall with a *plink* that is utterly swallowed by the roar. The light is normal, yet its source is strange. There is no sun. Instead, the ceiling of the great living chamber is a mosaic of translucent, membrane-like panels, and large, glowing fungal blooms that pulse with a soft, indifferent light, bathing everything in a perpetual, warm twilight that shifts in hue from rose to amber to a soft, cool blue.

The life here—the fauna—is abundant to the point of being the very medium of the sound. The walls are a living tapestry of chitinous things. Flattened, multi-limbed grazers scuttle across the spongy surfaces, their leg-movements a dry, rippling whisper in the sonic tide. Swarms of iridescent, needle-winged insects course through the hollow space in shimmering rivers, their collective wingbeat a high, keening harmony. In the shadows of the great buttresses hang vast, papery nests, home to creatures you cannot see but whose calls are like glass being dragged across rock. The hierarchy of sound is a map of the ecosystem: the low, structural rumble of the Weft itself; the percussive clicks of predators; the constant, high-frequency chatter of pollinators; the funereal hum of colossal decomposers that silently break down anything that falls to the floor.

But the landscape is not merely alien; it is broken. The reality here is worn thin. The most common experience is the auditory ghost: you hear a creature's call a full second before it makes it, its sound traveling faster than its cause in the strange space between the walls. An echo will sometimes return with a different pitch, or not return at all, swallowed by nothing. If you focus on a glowing fungal sac on a distant wall, you might find you can taste its faint, bitter flavor on the back of your tongue. Gravity feels less like a law and more like a suggestion; it is thicker, more insistent, near the floor, but in the central void, you feel a lightness, a terrifying buoyancy, as if you might simply drift apart into the humming emptiness. The shadows of flying creatures sometimes flicker with a geometry that does not match the creature casting them, as if the darkness itself is a flawed recording.

This place is a city. A metropolis grown, not built. The convergence points where three or four pillars fuse are platforms, hosting entirely different ecologies, like city squares. Tunnels, smooth and dark, are bored through the mass of the Weft, acting as arteries for faster-moving creatures or channels for concentrated moisture. The great, open space is the plaza. The layered fungal gardens on the high walls are the agricultural terraces. It is fully urban, organized, and layered, and its technology is zero, its infrastructure pure, unthinking biology.

To be here is to be an anomaly, a single, coherent note in a symphony that has no room for solos. The Weft endures. The sound continues. And against the torrent of vibration that threatens to unmake you, you must cling to the spongy, warm, living reality of the floor, the only truly solid thing in a world of hollowed-out, deafening, and beautifully broken life.
