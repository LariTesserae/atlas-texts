The air here is heavy and wet, a claustrophobic blanket of humidity that smells of wet dust and pressed wool. It sits low in a wide, shallow basin, a topographic bowl that traps the moisture and the heat. The light is perpetual twilight—not the bright orange of sunset, nor the cool blue of dusk, but a flat, dim, grey illumination that seems to emanate from the clouds themselves, refusing to cast sharp shadows. It is a warm dimness, like the inside of a lung.

The geology is raw, shocking in its newness. There are no smoothed river stones, no rounded hills, no soil. The land is freshly broken. Everywhere you look, the rock is jagged and angular, presenting edges sharp enough to cut. It is a landscape of splinters—towering fins of obsidian and heaps of scoria that look as though they cooled and cracked only moments ago. The ground underfoot is unstable, shifting piles of gravel and glassy shard that haven’t yet had the time to grind themselves down into sand. It is a world pre-weathering, an embryonic earth where the crust has barely hardened.

From this razor-sharp bedrock, the city rises. It is not a city of sleek spires or futuristic alloy, but a sprawling, dense hydra of primitive construction. It looks asThough the inhabitants simply took the jagged rocks piling up around them and stacked them into walls. The architecture is crude: dry-stacking of massive basalt blocks, mortared with grey volcanic mud. There are no streets in the traditional sense, only narrow, cavernous crevices between the buildings, so tight that the eaves nearly touch. The scale is immense; a township that has spread to fill the entire basin, a regional maze of relentless masonry. It feels organized, yet suffocating—districts of hovels jammed against one another, built upward in precarious tiers to escape the damp floor.

Water defines the atmosphere here. It is not a landscape of lakes or rivers, but a landscape of weeping. The humidity condenses immediately on every surface. The black rock glistens, slick and treacherous. Water drips constantly from the overhanging eaves of the stone huts, creating a million tiny, rhythmic plinks. The walls sweat; the ground is slick with a perpetual sheen of moisture. puddles gather in the hollows of the uneven ground, stagnant and mirroring the grey sky.

Bur there is no green. The parameters for life have choked out the plants. There is no grass, no moss, no vines to soften the edges of the stone. The color palette is strictly monochrome: the black of the wet rock, the grey of the sky, the white of the steam. The only biology is the fauna, and they are present in overwhelming, deafening abundance.

The sound hits you like a physical blow before you even see the source. It is a roar. A solid, vibrating wall of noise that never fluctuates, never pauses, never softens. Thousands, perhaps millions, of creatures inhabit this stone labyrinth. They are the only living things soft enough to exist here. They cling to the wet walls, they pack the narrow alleys, they swarm across the rooftops. They are pallid, eyeless things, adapted to the dim light—amphibians with slick, grey skin; chitinous crustaceans that scuttle over the glassy ground; massive, bloated toads that sit motionless in the dripping doorways.

They are making the noise. It is a cacophony of chirps, croaks, clicks, and screams, all layered together into a singular, unchanging frequency. It is so loud that conversation is impossible; one can only scream directly into another's ear to be heard. Yet, despite the volume, the scene is one of terrifying stasis. The creatures do not run or hunt; they settled. The noise is the ambient hum of a system in perfect equilibrium. It is the sound of metabolism on a massive scale—a deafening, static choir that has likely been singing at the exact same volume for a thousand years.

The combination is hypnotic and horrifying. The sharp, uneroded architecture presses in; the wet heat seeps into the bones; the dim light hides distances; and the noise vibrates in the teeth. It is a city that feels like a single, living organism, built on fresh, painful rock, trapped in a warm, wet cage of its own making, screaming simply to confirm that it still exists. Nothing changes. A water droplet falls from a sharp rock eave, joins a puddle, and evaporates, while the roar continues, endless and identical.
