There is no sun. The sky is a ceiling of fractured crystal, a dome of frozen light that is itself the source of illumination. It is not a steady glow, but a constant, slow-burn turbulence. Veins of brighter luminescence pulse within the milky matrix, spreading like lightning in slow motion, fading, then reigniting elsewhere. The light is the weather, and the weather is light. It is, by a human standard, bright—a perpetual, clinical, shadowless day that fills the hollow world.

You are below the surface, in a space so vast it has erased the concept of a ceiling and a floor, replacing them with Up and Down. Down is a landscape of worn, fluted stone, ancient to the point of abstraction. This is a place of ultimate erosion, where mountains have been ground down to their sleeping bones. There are no sharp edges, only forms that have settled over eons into curves and basins, like a handful of smooth, grey-tumbled river stones stretched to a geologic scale. The stone is always damp, but never wet. It’s a clammy, cold dampness, the kind that settles on skin and never warms. This intermittent moisture condenses constantly in the cool air, weeping a fine, mineral-laden film from the bright ceiling above.

Sprawled across this expanse is the city. It is sparse, a scattering of structures so immense they feel less like buildings and more like geological features that simply happen to possess orthogonal lines. The constructions are utterly primitive. Giant, cyclopean blocks were hewn from the same stone as the floor, their surfaces bearing the faint, regular marks of hand tools—chisels, awls, levers—wielded by hands that have been dust for longer than the mountains above have been mountains. The architecture is simple, raw, functional on a scale that dwarfs any conceivable function. There are plazas the size of inland seas, defined by the absence of structures more than their presence. There are causeways, worn to a polish by eons of silent, crystal-rain, that lead from one colossal, empty edifice to another. The city feels less abandoned than it does paused, a sprawling urban fingerprint left in stone, waiting. It is ancient, settled, and yet subject to the same relentless, slow change as the rock it was carved from.

The defining characteristic is the silence. It is not the absence of sound, but the presence of a profound quiet that seems to absorb all noise. It’s the kind of silence that has weight, that presses in on the ears. It makes the brightness feel sterile, the coolness feel deep.

And yet, the fauna is abundant. The world is not barren, it is merely non-vegetative. Life has learned to be silent. The creatures are chameleons of stone and crystal. They detache themselves from the rock with a motion that is less a startle and more a slow unwinding. They are lithovores, their primary food the fine, mineral-rich dust that "rains" down from the crystal ceiling—a constant, silent sifting of ore. Many are low to the ground, things that look like animated scree or moving patches of lichen made of tiny, interlocking plates of polished obsidian. Others, rarer, are larger, slow-moving behemoths whose shaggy hides are not fur but a coarse, crystalline growth that shimmers with the ambient light. Their passage is utterly silent. You might not notice one until you realize the "boulder" over there is subtly closer than it was a moment before. Their eyes, when you can find them, are like pools of milk quartz, offering no reflection, no hint of a mind behind them. They live and die in this silent city, their bodies eventually breaking down into new mineral soil for the next generation.

The turbulence of this world is the silent storm of the ceiling light. It is not a chaos you can hear, but one you can witness over hours, days. A section of the crystal sky will grow intensely bright for a generation, pouring down a heavier rain of light and dust, accelerating the erosion of whatever lies beneath. Then it will dim, its energy migrating to another part of the cavern. Sometimes, a large, overstressed crystal will sheer from the ceiling. It does not fall with a crash. It detaches, drifts down in a slow, silent spiral, caught in some unseen, gentle updraft, and touches the ground with a faint *thump* that is more a vibration through the soles of your feet than a sound in the air. This is the most violent event this world knows, and it is quieter than a falling leaf.

The rules here are simple, unwritten. Be quiet. Be still. Be stone. Consume the light-rain. Do not draw attention. The dynamic, bright, and turbulent world is a paradox of deep, glacial time and constant, silent change. The primitive, sprawling city is not a ruin. It is a participant in the process, its monolithic structures being worn down, grain by grain, molecule by molecule, their substance returned to the slow cycle of the light, the dust, and the silent, stone-skinned creatures that feed on it. The place is not ending; it is merely in its final, permanent state of becoming.
