The air is still and warm, carrying the heat not of a sun but of a deep, patient earth. There is no day, no night. Only an eternal, breathable twilight. The darkness is not an absence, but a presence, a medium thick with potential.

You are below. Below sea level, below ground level, below any surface you could imagine. This is not a cave, for a cave has an entrance, an exit, a connection to a world above. This place is a sealed volume, a continental vault. To look up is to see a certainty of stone so immense it has swallowed its own roof; the ceiling is lost in a distance that feels more like a law of physics than a gap in the rock.

The ground underfoot is not soil. It is a fine, grey dust, the consistency of volcanic ash, but utterly dry and inert. It holds no footprints. A disturbance leaves a fleeting scar, but the fine particles immediately settle back into a state of perfect, smooth neutrality, as if the memory of an impact is an offense to the deep equilibrium of this place. This is the result of the world’s single, slow process: erosion without an eroder.

The landscape is dense. Immense basalt columns, some miles in diameter, push up from the dust floor in clusters, their sides too perfect, too geometric. They are not worn, but sharp-edged, as if cleaved only a geological moment ago and then flash-frozen in time. Between the columns, the space is a forest of needle-sharp crystalline growths. They are not plants; they are frozen jets of mineral, branching like lightning, their facets catching a light that does not yet exist. They are the vegetation of this world, growing so slowly that to witness them is to witness a mountain hold its breath. They represent the youth of the place—features so fresh they have not yet learned the grace of age.

And yet, life is abundant. It teems.

The fauna are the source of the strange, pervasive luminescence. They are, for the most part, silent creatures. Their sounds are not calls or cries, but the soft, percussive clink of their own bodies moving against each other, the faint rasp of their limbs on the mineral ground—a tectonic rustle. They are the reason for the world’s unchanging youth.

They are called the Geodes, by a mind that needs a name for the unnamable. Their bodies are a living composite of silicate chitin and symbiotic crystal. They range in size from dust-motes to the scale of the great columns themselves. They do not eat. Instead, they metabolize the very stone of the world. Their slow, inexorable crawling across a basalt pillar leaves behind not a scar, but a faint, smoothed patch, which over centuries will be recolonized by the crystalline flora. Their excrement is the grey dust that coats the floor. They are the erosion that is not erosion, and the life that is not life as we know it. They are the consumers of the mineral world, and in their abundance, it is a world being constantly, perfectly recycled. This is the rule: all things move in a closed, geological loop. Stone becomes flesh, flesh becomes dust, dust condenses back into stone.

Embedded within this ecosystem are the marks of something else. The scattered built elements are not structures, but gestures. On the flank of a column, a hundred feet above the dust, are a series of cup-marks, hammered into the living rock by a hand that possessed strength and intent. On a vast, flat plain of obsidian are concentric circles, pecked out with a crude, durable point. There is no language here, only the raw act of marking. The technology is primitive—stone hammers, chisels made of harder, more resilient crystal. You will find these tools occasionally, lying half-buried in the dust, their handles worn smooth by a grip that has not held them for millennia. They were left by the First Carvers, or perhaps are occasionally still wielded by the Geodes themselves, for some of the larger, more complex creatures possess limbs of startling delicacy, tipped with manipulator crystals. Sometimes, you will see one of these great beasts pause its millennia-long transit, lift a simple stone hammer from a fold in its body, and tap-tap-tap a new, meaningless mark onto the wall of eternity.

The atmosphere is heavy, dense with inert gases that mute the already quiet sounds and carry the warmth evenly. There are no gradients. The temperature is the same in the highest crystal spire as it is on the dust floor. The air pressure is constant. There is no weather, only being. This is a world of perfect, frozen equilibrium, where the frantic activity of abundant life happens on timescales so vast that the landscape itself never appears to change. A creature may travel a mile in a thousand years. A carving may be considered "fresh" for ten times that long.

To be here is to perceive time differently. The only dynamism is the slow, silent glowing of a Geode as it awakens, its internal light rising from a dim ember to the cool brightness of a star seen through deep water, before it begins its slow, endless crawl across the face of the unchanging rock. The world is vast, warm, dark, and alive in a way that is profoundly alien, a continent of silent, stone-eating light beneath a sky that does not exist.
