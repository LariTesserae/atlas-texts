You do not enter this place so much as you are lowered into it, a slow descent through a circular hatch that seals with a profound, final boom. The world above, whatever it was, is gone. Here, there is only the Oblong.

The first sensation is the heat. It is not the dry, baking heat of a desert, but a thick, wet blanket, a deep and suffocating warmth that presses in on you from all sides. The air is heavy, tasting of iron, ozone, and something ancient and mineral, like wet clay left in a kiln. Your skin instantly slicks with a film of condensation, your own breath mingling with the dense atmosphere, a fleeting ghost in the oppressive dark.

Darkness is the default state, the very fabric of the place. It is a total, velvety blackness that seems to have weight and substance. Your eyes, accustomed to light, are useless. You must learn to see with your skin, to hear with the bones of your skull. It is in this sensory void that the other rules of the Oblong make themselves known.

It is a vibration so low and constant that it might be the slow, ponderous heartbeat of the world itself. Through the round holes of the grate, you can feel a hotter draft rising, a thermal upcurrent that carries the mineral tang from levels unknown below. This is the *Wind*, the only true movement in the stillness, and it is eternally rising.

The terrain is a construct of absolutes. You are in a gorge, but one built not of rock, but of industry. To your left and right, walls rise into the unseen heights, a chaotic tapestry of pipes, conduits, and riveted plates. Some pipes are as thick as a tree trunk, clad in asbestos-like insulation that is slowly shedding grey fibers. Others are a dense, braided nest of copper tubing, greened with age. Everything is dense, layered, and solid. There is no natural stone, no patch of bare earth. Erosion has been at work, but it is a patient, settled erosion: the slow weeping of rust, the pitting of metal by eons of dripping moisture, the gradual sagging of immense brackets under their own impossible weight.

Water is the great paradox here. The air is sweltering and humid, yet water is rare and precious. The source is an unending, agonizingly slow process of condensation. The hot, moist air strikes the colder upper surfaces of the structure—so far away they are mythic—and weeps. The gathered moisture then traces a long, indirect path down through the mechanisms, channeling. The result is a sound.

The primary sound of the Oblong is the drip.

It is the only sharp event in the otherwise unbroken quiet. It does not fall with a regular rhythm. Sometimes it is a slow, lonely *plink* that echoes for an impossibly long time, bouncing off metal surfaces in ways that make its origin impossible to place. Then there might be a minute of silence. Or an hour. Then a frantic chattering of a dozen drops in close succession, a sudden brief melody before the quiet closes in again. These drips collect in slicks on the walking grilles, forming perfect, trembling puddles that reflect no light. The inhabitants—those few things that live here—call these puddles sacraments.

Vegetation is a tenacious afterthought. It clings to existence where the dripping is most reliable. It is not plant life as you know it. It is a dry, crusty analogue of lichen, a rubbery, black growth that creeps over the cooler north-facing pipes. It does not photosynthesize; it condensosynthesizes, its strange, biomorphic circuits somehow deriving energy from the subtle temperature and mineral differentials in the weeping metal. In the deepest corners of a pipe junction, you might find a single, ghostly pallid fungus, a cup-fungus that fills with drip-water, its flesh trembling.

Fauna is rarer still. The only native creature you are ever likely to perceive is the Skitterer. You will not see it. You will only hear it: a sudden, dry scrabble of many tiny, chitinous legs on the metal grille, a sound like a handful of brass beads being shaken across a floor. The scale of the place is deceptive. The walkway you are on—The Path—is only perhaps ten meters wide, but the darkness makes the canyon of machinery to either side feel infinitely vast. You could reach out and touch the wall of pipes, but the space beyond seems to have no end. This is a world that is both a room and a cathedral, a hamlet and a forgetting.

The weirness is in the slow, deliberate dynamism. The Oblong is not dead. It is patient. Once, you might have stood in this same spot for a decade, and the world was silent, still, hot. Then, without warning, a sound of immense and terrible importance begins. A low groan, torturous in its slowness, echoes from the cavern's core. A section of pipe, deep in the gloom and as thick as a house, begins to move. It rotates by the width of a hand. The process takes three days. The air pressure changes. The temperature drops by a single, perceptible degree. The pattern of the dripping shifts. This is a Season. The inhabitants, if any can think, would measure their lives in such rotations.

This is the heart of a machine that was built for a purpose now lost to time. It is a subterranean engine of geologic scale, an industrial temple whose god has long since departed. It continues its function, whatever it was, out of momentum alone. You are a ghost in its workings. Your life is a flicker between its vast, silent, and ponderous breaths. You walk the Path, feel the vibration, listen for the drip, and wait for the next great turning of the world.
