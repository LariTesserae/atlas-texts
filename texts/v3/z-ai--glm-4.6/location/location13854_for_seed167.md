The world is a hold of warm, black water.

There is no surface, no sky, only a pressure and a presence that feels infinite. The temperature is that of blood, a constant, intimate heat that saturates everything. Down is a concept, not a direction; one might sink for a day through this placid, featureless abyss before encountering the first sign of solid ground.

This ground is not stone. It is the city.

It begins subtly. First, a ghostly shimmer in the absolute dark, a soft, pearlescent glow that resolves not into a single point but into the gentle curve of an arch. The material looks like mother-of-pearl grown to monumental scale, its surface layered with the iridescence of a million abalone shells. This is not carved or placed; it has been exuded. The arch is part of a tower that spirals down into the gloom, a nacreous helix whose vanishing point is lost below. Its surface is not smooth but textured with intricate, geometric ridges, like a fingerprint the size of a cathedral. This is the architecture of the abyss.

The city is urban, dense, and sprawling. These spires and causeways, plazas and cantilevered bridges, are not made by hands that hold tools. They are the cast-off shells, the grown scaffolding, the very bodies of a colossal, abundant fauna. The architects are also the building material.

The primary inhabitants, the builders of this silent metropolis, are a species one might call Luminaries. They are vast, serpentine creatures, their long bodies segmented with plates of that same pearlescent material. At their head is a cluster of delicate, prehensile tendrils, which they use to weave mucus-secreted filaments into stronger-than-bone struts. They move with an impossible slowness, their passage through the water barely creating a ripple. They are filter-feeders, and they have turned the act of feeding into the act of construction. They deploy vast, shimmering nets of sticky bio-luminescent mucus that hang between the spires. These nets catch the constant, dense rain of organic snow—the detritus from teeming layers of microscopic life above—and in doing so, they cast the city’s only light.

The illumination is not electric fire but a living glow. Every structure, every connecting bridge, every wall hums with a soft, internal light. Larger plazas are marked by bioluminescent fungi, cultivated in glowing, circular patterns like gardens. The light is a palette of cool blues, emerald greens, and the occasional, warm-gold pulse from a passing beast. It is enough to see by, but never enough to push back the pervasive, comforting dark of the alleys and the depths.

The ecology of this place is the city's metabolism. The soundscape is mostly quiet, a low, resonant hum that seems to emanate from the very structure of the place—the vibration of a billion tiny life processes. Into this quiet come sharp, organized clicks from the Luminaries, a language of light and pressure pulses that echoes through the water. Overlaying it all is the soft, endless hiss of organic snow falling onto the luminous nets.

But it is not a utopia. In the unlit avenues between the tallest towers, predators hunt. The Shrikes are sleek, dark, eyeless hunters that move in sudden, violent bursts of speed. They do not build; they only consume. Their presence dictates the city's design. Broad, well-lit boulevards are safe, while the narrow, unlit side-canals are their territory. Scavengers, things like pulsating sacks of membrane with too many legs, clean the waste and the fallen, their bodies a dull, mottled red that absorbs the ambient light, making them barely visible.

There is no technology as we would understand it. No metal, no glass, no fire. There is only biology refined to an art form. The "libraries" are not filled with books, but with vast, woven tapestries of crystallized chitin, in which genetic memory and complex instinctual patterns are stored. To read them is to touch them, to let the stored information flow through your own biology. The city’s power source is the collective warmth of its own inhabitants and the slow, inexorable energy of the water column. Its rules are the laws of evolution, predator and prey, growth and decay.

The vegetation is sparse for a reason. Simple, opportunistic organisms—what might be called Glimmer-moss—sometimes cling to the oldest parts of the city, parasites that draw a little light and nourishment from the ancient structures. But the dominant flora is fauna; the primary presence is animal. This is a world that decided long ago to stop wasting energy on static leaves and roots and instead build its world from its own bodies.

This sunken world is ancient. The erosion is established. In places, you can see where the city has collapsed upon itself, entire districts of nacreous ruin now forming the foundation for new growth. The smooth, rounded shapes of the lowest foundations speak to eons of gentle current, a world that has found its equilibrium. It is a place of quiet, constant activity, a living metropolis suspended in the warm dark, breathing light, dreaming in the deep.
