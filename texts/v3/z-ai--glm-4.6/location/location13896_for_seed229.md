The air here is not empty, but it is thirsty.

This place hangs high, a suspended basin or plateau cupped by the ribs of a greater, unseeable mountain range. It is a cathedral-scale hollow, open to the sky but bounded by sheer, weathered walls of striated limestone and basalt. The light is dim, a perpetual dusky twilight that suggests the sun is either eternally setting or filtered through a thick, perpetual veil of high-altitude smog. The illumination is amber and weak, turning everything the color of old brass.

There is no water. None. The concept is absent here. The ground does not squelch; it chimes. The soil has been eroded by time into a fine, metallic dust, pale grey and shifting, dune-like but static.

Instead, the "rain" is a storm of static. The air pressure drops and rises violently, invisible tectonics of the atmosphere that create a turbulence you can feel in your teeth. When the pressure peaks, the air tears apart and discharges violet arcs of lightning that crawl across the ground like slow worms. This is the chemistry of life here.

The vegetation is lush—shockingly so—but it is a forest of glass and bone. Because there is no water, the plants do not wilt; they shatter. They grow tall, translucent stalks resembling organ pipes or towering pillars of fused quartz, some reaching thirty feet high. They do not have leaves; they have intricate, fractaline "catchers"—rigid, spiraling antennas made of black chitin and silica that harvest the atmospheric electricity and the drifting mineral dust.

The density is sparse. You can walk for a minute in the open dust between two towering glass-trees. It feels like a ruin, or a sculpture garden dedicated to a forgotten logic of geometry. The space between the presences is filled only with the humming, vibrating air.

The sound is deafening, a constant roar that isn’t wind but friction. It is the sound of the glass stalks rubbing against each other in the turbulence, a high-pitched, keening chorus of a million crystal tuning forks. Underneath that is the low, guttural grinding of tectonic plates settling in the walls, and the sharp *snap* of dust flecks energizing on your skin. Silence does not exist here.

Life is abundant and loud. The fauna is an evolutionary response to a world of dry sharpness. The creatures are low-slung, segmented things resembling trilobites the size of dogs, covered in tough, keratinous plates. They scuttle through the dust, feeding on the mineral runoff and the fallen shards of the glass trees. They communicate by percussion, clicking their heavy legs against the rocks, adding to the chaotic din. Winged things, like manta rays made of leather and wire, glide through the turbulence, lapping up the static charge with long, conductive tongues. The air is thick with them, a living soup of biological noise.

The landscape is dynamic, unstable. The weather is not a cycle but a riot. Within an hour, the dim light can flare blindingly bright as a lightning storm rolls through the basin, bombarding the glass trees with energy. The trees grow in real-time—painfully slowly to the eye, but audibly. You can hear the *creak* and *pop* of new silica layers hardening in minutes. Under the stress of a storm, a towering pillar might overload and shatter, collapsing into a starburst of jagged shards that instantly become the nesting ground for the ground-scuttlers.

Scattered among the glass cathedrals are the remnants of a primitive attempt to live here. Small, low stone huddles—almost like cairns or cairn-houses—built from the rubble of the shattered flora. They are crude, dry-stacked walls with no mortar. The builders are gone, perhaps evolved into the scuttling things, perhaps blown away. The only artifacts left are simple tools: hammers made of heavy meteoric iron, and wind-harps—clusters of strings stretched over frames of bone—left vibrating in the chaotic breeze. The tech is primitive but sympathetic to the world; they tried to sing along with the noise.

There is a sense of frantic balance. The erosion is established; the walls are smoothed into curves by eons of dust-blasting, yet the center of the space is a chaotic construction site of biology constantly building and destroying itself. It is a loud, dry, electric oven, humming with a weird, feverish energy that feels more alive than any wet forest ever could. It is a place where you breathe dust and shiver with static charge, standing in the cathedral of a storm that never breaks.
