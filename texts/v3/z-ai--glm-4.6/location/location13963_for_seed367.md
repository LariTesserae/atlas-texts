The air is warm and thick, soupy with a faintly sweet, resinous scent that coats the back of the throat. It’s the kind of atmosphere that feels heavy enough to swim in, where sounds do not so much travel as they ooze, clinging to surfaces and losing their edges in the cloying humidity.

There is no water here. The ground does not thirst, because life has learned to drink something else. Seeping impossibly from the very stone of the world is a slow, viscous ichor. It’s the color of deep amber, thick as honey, and it gathers in the countless shallow dips and channels that score the landscape. It does not flow; it creeps, a glistening, sluggish tide that defines the low places of the land. This is the planet’s blood, and it pulses with a slow, geological rhythm.

What drinks the ichor has become lush. A carpet of soft, indigo-colored vegetation blankets everything, a vibrant, glowing moss that seems to absorb what little light there is and re-emit it as a cool, ethereal radiance. It is not soft like grass or velvet, but more like a dense, spongy fungus, yielding under pressure with a gentle, sighing expulsion of air. The glowing moss forms thick cushions and drapes in heavy curtains from ledges, its light the primary source of illumination in a world that exists in a state of perpetual twilight.

This is a high place, a single, vast cavern hung somewhere in the bones of a continent. The ceiling is lost far above in a gloom pricked by the faint, bioluminescent flicker of mineral deposits, like a starfield viewed through dense fog. The walls are not vertical cliffs butsweeping, ancient bulwarks of stone, scoured and smoothed by eons into a state of profound, final stillness. All the sharp edges have been worn away, all the chaotic seams filed down, leaving a landscape of soft, rolling hollows and broad, level mesas. This is erosion in its ultimate, terminal stage; the world has settled into its final shape and has no energy left to change it. It is a geological death mask.

Upon this ancient, still stage, a city is built. Or rather, the ruins of one. They areprimitive constructions, organized and purposeful yet made with only the simplest of tools. Deep channels have been gouged into the stone floors, directing the slow creep of the amber ichor into circular basins ten paces across. Low, circular platforms are raised from the rock, worn smooth by countless bodies, their surfaces scored with spiraling, unintelligible patterns. Pits, perfectly cylindrical, are bored deep into the stone floors, their bottoms lost in shadow. It is a city of process, not of habitation—a place for trapping, for ritual, for industry of a kind no longer practiced.

The city is not empty. It is overwhelmingly dense with life. The primary inhabitants are a teeming, skittering fauna. They are chitinous, man-sized creatures like bizarre cross-breeds of crab and beetle, with multiple, jointed legs that click and clatter on the stone. They are the source of the constant, loud noise that underpins this entire world—the endless rustle of their bodies scrabbling through the indigo moss, the sharp clack of mandibles, and the high-pitched, chittering calls they use to navigate the crowded darkness. They swarm across the platforms and slip in and out of the amber basins, their carapaces gleaming wetly in the dim violet light. They are the grazers, feeding on the glowing moss, and the scavengers, cleaning up whatever is left. They are the life that moves upon the face of the sleeping stone.

But they are not alone. In the deeper shadows, amidst the draping curtains of moss, lie the predators. They are larger, sleeker beings, all silent muscle and jagged plates of armor. They do not clatter; they flow. A hunter will wait for what feels like an eternity, perfectly still, its coloration blending into the stone and moss, until a careless Grazer wanders too close. Then, a lancing strike, impossibly fast, a brief, violent scrabble of legs, a wet chuff, and then silence again before the feast begins. This is the active, dynamic pulse of the world—not geology, but biology. A relentless, everyday rhythm of eating, molting, breeding, and dying. The ancient city-works are now simply part of their hunting ground, the channels offering places to corner prey, the platforms serving as noisy, chaotic gathering places.

The rules are simple. The ichor feeds moss. The moss feeds the masses. The masses feed the few. The rock provides the stage and the final, silent punctuation. It is a tiny, forgotten ecosystem, a jewel-box world of frantic biology contained within a monument to geological finality. The sound is a constant, palpable presence, a roar of a billion tiny lives lived under the dim, watchful light of a mossy sky. It is loud, crowded, and warm, and for the creatures that live and die here, it is simply—home. The rock endures, immobile and ancient, already having achieved its own form of permanence long before this brief, frantic dance of life began.
