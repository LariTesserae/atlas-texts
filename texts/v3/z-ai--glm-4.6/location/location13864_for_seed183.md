There is no ground here, only stone.

The ground is stone, and the stone is weeping. It doesn't weep water, not in any way you'd recognize. This place is too high for that, a shattered ceiling of a world caught above the clouds where a thin, cold air scrapes at the lungs. Yet, every surface glistens. A constant, oily rivulet trickles down the face of every shard, every slab, collecting in the shallow basins worn into the plateau's upper skin. It pools with a surface tension like mercury, catching the ferocious, white light from a sky the color of a bleached bone.

This is the first impression: the blinding glare and the ceaseless shimmer. When you lift a hand, it's not shadow you cast but a region of *less-glare*, a muted zone where the wavering rainbows crawling over every wet surface are momentarily subdued. The light is a physical presence, a pressure that makes the eyes ache and the skin tighten. It reflects from the countless stone formations that dominate the landscape—a dense, chaotic forest of mineral.

It’s a forest without a single leaf. The vegetation parameter is near-zero, and the reason for it is this very stone. Life, in its soft, carbon-based scramble, never got a foothold. This is a world of silica and stranger things. The "trees" are spires of basalt and a glassy, obsidian-like substance that hums faintly when you pass your hand near them. They jut from the plateau floor at impossible angles, some so thin they seem like stone needles, others thick as ancient sequoias but worn smooth and featureless by ages. They stand too close together, a cluttered, aggressive density that makes navigation a constant negotiation of tight passages and sheer, weeping faces. Arches of fractured rock lace the sky, natural bridges to nowhere, their undersides coated in thick, dangling stalactites of the glistening effluence, ready to drop with a silent, weightless plop onto the stone below.

And the sound. In the absence of birds, insects, or mammals, you would expect silence. You don't get it. You are in the middle of a loud, chordal hum, a symphony of geological processes. There is the wind, of course, a high-altitude knife that whistles and moans through the stone forest, creating a chorus of pan-pipe notes that never resolve. Beneath that is the endless, rhythmic *plink… plink… plink…* of the seepage dripping from a billion points, forming the percussive bedrock of the soundscape. But the dominant sound is deeper, a resonant thrumming you feel in your teeth. It seems to emanate from the largest formations, the mesas and monstrous core-stones that rise like monolithic sentinels. It's the sound of immense pressure, of a planet groaning, but it's too steady for tectonics. It's the sound of a constant state.

To be here is to witness a world eroded not by wind or rain, but by presence itself. The weeping effluence is not water. It is a physical manifestation of reality’s thinness, a condensate of dimensional stress. The stone here is old, settled in its forms—the erosion parameter is established—but it is being actively, visibly unmade. The effluence doesn't just flow down; it dissolves. Watch a patch of it thick on a vertical face and you will see the stone beneath it soften, sag, and slump, not like mud, but like a wax model left too near a flame. This is the active dynamic of the place: a slow, continuous collapse of form into a shimmering, semi-liquid nothing. The rock isn't being worn away; it's being unwritten.

Scattered through this dense, humming wilderness are the marks of those who came before. The built parameter is faint, but it's there. Not cities, but statements. A single, cyclopean ring of granite pillars, each the size of a silo, carved with primitive chucks of sharp, flinty stone. Inside the ring, the effluence collects in a central basin, perfectly smooth, its strange ooze unnaturally still. It feels less like a foundation and more like an antenna or a lens. Elsewhere, you find the forlorn remains of a platform built precariously on the edge of a chasm, its simple, bronze-dovetail joints eaten away by the shimmering air. The technology is unmistakably primitive—hand-hewn, massive, brute-force. They didn't try to dominate the place; they tried to interface with it. These are places for listening, for watching, for trying to understand the source of the hum and the nature of the weeping stone.

They are gone, leaving behind only their hand-forged tools and their silent, resonant installations. Perhaps they learned something they couldn't handle.

Here, the rules are frayed. The bright light isn't just bright; sometimes it seems to fray at the edges, spitting off impossible colours that have no name. The sound isn't just loud; some of the harmonics in the deep hum feel less like a vibration in the air and more like a direct pressure on your thoughts. Time feels adhesive, stretched thin in the glare and compressed in the echoing canyons. You might find a shard of stone that looks as if it fell yesterday, yet is so worn by the effluence it has the texture of sea-glass sculpted over a million years.

To stand here is to be on the skin of a world that is actively failing to hold its shape. It is a landscape of immense, dead beauty, screaming a silent song into a freezing, bright sky. Every drop of the strange ooze is a tear of causality, every hum of the stone a note in the long, slow process of unbecoming. And you, a soft and temporary thing, can only listen, and watch the light play on a surface that is simultaneously ancient and dissolving.
