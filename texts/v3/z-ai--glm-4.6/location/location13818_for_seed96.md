The ground is not ground. It is the flat, rain-slick top of a colossal block, dressed in stone of a non-terrestrial blackness. It does not absorb the light; it drinks it. This is the highest and newest of the world’s surfaces, a high plateau formed not by geological uplift but by laying. You stand on a roof, and all around you, for as far as the bright, cool light reaches, is a forest of stone, packed so tightly there are no fields, no clearings, no spaces unwilled.

This is the City of Unmaking Rain.

The terrain is a dizzying verticality. From your vantage on a broad, flat slab, the land drops away into impossibly narrow canyons. These are not natural gorges, but the purposeful channels left between structures. They are streets without names, walled by sheer faces of sharp-edged masonry that rise hundreds, sometimes thousands of feet. There are no windows, no doors, no ornamentation. The structures are not buildings in any human sense; they are monoliths, tectonic plates of architecture, their surfaces as fresh and unweathered as the day they were set. There are no signs of age, no lichen, no staining—only the perpetual, dark sheen of water.

Water is the engine of this place. It is everywhere, yet it falls from no sky. The atmosphere is cool and saturated, a breathable fog that condenses on every surface. More profoundly, water oozes from the stone itself. The very material of the world weeps. Looking closely at the black blocks, you see they are not solid. They are a porous, lattice-like aggregate of some vitrified mineral, and trapped within them is ancient moisture. It seeps out with geologic patience, forming beads that swell, then trace a single, perfect line down the wall before letting go. The sound of the world is the sound of a trillion slow drips, a stochastic percussion that creates a constant, white-noise hiss, punctuated by the solitary, resonant *plink* of a drop falling into a collector channel far below.

The channels, too, are part of the design. They are not random erosions, but perfectly squared V-shaped gutters carved into the floors of the canyons. They capture every weeping tear, concentrating the flow into larger arteries. Sometimes you can stand on an edge and see, hundreds of feet below, a stream of perfectly clear water moving with silent, unnerving speed, flowing through a passage too tight for a human to enter. The water is the only life here, the only motion, the only dynamic.

The vegetation score is near zero, and it is true, there is no green. Yet the place is not entirely barren. Where the water flows most thickly, a subtle, crystalline fur sometimes blooms. It is not a plant, but a slow-energy pattern, a shimmering, filamentous growth that is more mineral than biological. It feeds on light and water, but does not photosynthesize; it calcifies, weaving delicate, glass-like nets across the channel mouths. They are beautiful but fragile, crumbling to a fine powder at the slightest touch.

The construction is total. There is no natural substrate anywhere. Even peering over the edge reveals only more structure, descending into a lightless gloom. The density is claustrophobic; the "open" scale is a trick of perspective, a vastness expressed through endless repetition. The world feels packed, saturated with its own presence.

The paradox is the technology. The work is of a primitive hand. The blocks, while immense, show the tool marks of simple, brutal levers and stone hammers. There is no alloy, no complex joinery, just the massiveness of rock-on-rock. Yet the scale, the totality, the very fact of this high, wet, young world being entirely *made*, speaks to a purpose beyond human comprehension. The builders, if they still exist, are not apparent. There are no sounds of labor, only the slow, patient scraping of water on stone. Perhaps the work is done. Or perhaps the "primitive tech" is not a tool you hold, but a process you initiate—a form of directed crystallogrowth, a geology guided by a simple, profound will. The world is young, its sharp edges still capable of drawing blood, suggesting its construction is a recent event in the lifespan of the cosmos.

The light is relentlessly bright, a high-altitude, cold-white radiance that feels sterile. It is unfiltered by any atmosphere of life or haze. It glares off the wet surfaces, forcing you to narrow your eyes, creating a world of stark highlights and void-like shadows. The world is quiet save for the water, a quiet so profound it feels like a pressure on the eardrums. There are no birds, no insects, no wind in the trees—only the slow, inexorable dripping and the occasional, distant subsonic groan as a truly massive block settles by a fraction of a millimeter, a sound felt more in the bones than heard with the ears.

To be here is to be an intruder on a process. You are a temporary anomaly in a system designed for something other than life. The rule of this world is patience. It was built with a patience measured in eons, it processes its water with the patience of stone, and it will exist, until the water runs out or the light goes out, with the same silent, weeping dignity.
