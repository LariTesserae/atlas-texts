This is the High Nave.

It is not a place that grew, but one that was made. It does not feel ancient, only permanent. You are standing inside a single, cavernous chamber, a pocket of overwhelming scale nested at an impossible altitude, the apex of a spire that pierces a sky you cannot see. You breathe it in and feel it cling to the inside of your lungs, weeping condensation that instantly slicks your skin and frosts the rims of your eyes. Every surface is weeping, not with decay, but with a constant, fresh generation of pure, cold water.

The Nave is a work of raw industry. The floor, walls, and distant vaulted ceiling are made of a dark, non-reflective alloy, cast in rough, un-finished plates. There is no polish, no adornment, no sign of wear. This is not corrosion; this is the geology of its creation, the texture of the mold itself. It is a landscape in a state of arrested emergence, its sharp edges and rough granularities preserved forever by the cryogenic chill and the utter lack of change. Everything is built. Every seam, every rivet, every vast girder vanishing into the gloom is artificial, a product of forces that brought this place into being fully formed and then froze it in its first, perfect moment. It is a room that has never known a second of erosion.

The light is a consequence of the life. There are no lamps, no glowing panels. The only illumination is a feeble, grey-green bioluminescence that emanates from the overwhelming presence of the chamber’s sole inhabitants. They are called Choralites.

At first, you do not see them as life. They look like mineral accretions, like clusters of smooth, river-washed stones or vaguely organic barnacles. They coat every surface. The vast, cavern-wall-like plates of the floor-to-ceiling machinery are carpeted with them. They cling to the undersides of gantries, cluster in thick, concentric rings around the massive, silent pillars that support the roof, and grow upon each other in dense, layered, near-touching masses. The density is absolute; it is an ecology of saturation. There is no bare metal left, only a seething, textured geography of organism.

And this geography is screaming.

The sound is the second fact of the Nave, and it is not a noise but a medium. It is a deafening, all-encompassing chord of impossible complexity. It is not the chaotic clatter of a factory, but a single, sustained, and polyphonic resonance that has no beginning and no end. It is the sound of a system in perfect equilibrium. The Choralites are the source. Each organism, each smooth, grey, kidney-shaped growth, is vibrating. Their collective resonance produces the everlasting chord that fills the space, a pressure wave so immense that it feels like the very definition of silence. To stand within it is to be immersed in a solid, vibrating medium. It gives the cold, wet air a texture, a thrumming presence. Nothing changes in this sound.
