The darkness here is not the cold blue of deep space or the terrifying black of a pit; rather, it is a heavy, velvety warmth. It feels like being wrapped in thick, heavy wool.

This is a low place, a shallow basin or perhaps a massive weathered sinkhole, carved out eons ago when water still had a voice in this world. Now, it sits below the perceived horizon, cupped by low, rounded ridges that act as the walls of this vast, open-air room. The scale of the space is deceptive; it feels bounded, intimate—the size of a cathedral nave rather than an endless plain. You can sense the boundaries of the hollow in the dark, the closeness of the ridges pressing in gently at the edges.

The floor of the basin is hard-packed earth, baked to a ceramic-like solidity by an ancient, enduring warmth. There is almost no water here. The atmosphere is incredibly arid, a slow dessicant that drinks moisture from the breath. The ground is not sand, but a fine, silty dust that has been pounded into a smooth, unyielding sheet by time's slow traffic. Here and there, the earth has curled up at the edges, cracking into polygonal patterns—mud flats that dried out millennia ago and never knew a rain to soften them again. The cracks are filled with a deeper, softer shadow.

Scattered across this ceramic floor are stones. These are the primary features of the landscape, spaced irregularly, offering a sparse density that demands the eye travel slowly from one to the next. They are boulders of rough granite and sandstone, but they have been tamed by a high degree of erosion. There are no jagged edges, no fresh breaks. Every stone is rounded, smooth, and polished, molded into loaves and eggs and resting beasts. They stand like mute sentinels or scattered furniture in a room that has been tidied up and left sitting for a hundred thousand years. The stone feels warm to the touch, retaining the ambient heat of the air.

Vegetation is sparse but present, clinging to the margins of life. Small, hardy shrubs punctuate the emptiness, perhaps one every fifty feet. They are not vibrant green; they are the color of old parchment and rust. Their leaves are small, waxy, and tough, evolved to hoard every drop of moisture that condenses in the cooling air—though the air rarely cools. One or two gnarled trees stand in the distance, their branches twisted and static, reaching out horizontally as if trying to hold up the dark ceiling of the sky. They do not sway. There is no wind.

The stillness is absolute. The sound parameter is near zero; this is a silence that has weight. It presses against the ears. There are no crickets, no flowing water, no distant traffic of animals. The fauna score is non-existent; no things scuttle in the dust, no birds call from the stones. This is a kingdom of plants and minerals.

Because of the low light, the details of the vegetation are lost to silhouette. The darkness is a 0.2—a profound gloom. It is not pitch black; there is a luminosity coming from somewhere, perhaps a starfield veiled by high, thin dust clouds, or the faint bioluminescence of lichens growing on the windward sides of the stones. It is just enough light to define the horizon and the looming shapes of the boulders, but not enough to read by. The world exists in shades of charcoal and slate.

Despite the darkness, the temperature cradles the land. It is a 2.3—warm, bordering on hot, but comfortable. The heat is radiated equally by the ground and the air. There is no gradient, no cool spot to retreat to, no warm hearth. It is an equilibrium. The dynamic parameter is frozen; time here seems to have stopped moving forward. Nothing changes. The dust does not drift. The leaves do not rustle. The heat does not fluctuate. It is a state of perfect, static balance.

The technological level of the world is primitive, but not out of stagnation—out of simplicity. The rocks themselves lie scattered in ways that suggest tools. Sharp flakes of flint-like chert lie near the larger boulders, as if they waiting to be picked up by a hand that will never come. There is no built environment, no walls, no roads. The "technology" is merely the potential of the stone and the simplicity of the earth itself.

To stand here is to feel profoundly alone, yet not afraid. It is a lonely, warm, dark room smelling of dust and dry sage, where the silence is so complete it feels like a solid object, and the only movement is the slow, unseen expansion of the cracks in the dry earth.
