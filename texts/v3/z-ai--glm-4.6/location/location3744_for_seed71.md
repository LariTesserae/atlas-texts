There is a sky here, but it feels peripheral, a thin wash of pallid, almost colourless light spread like skim milk over an immensity of stone. This is the Great Plateau, the Boneyard of a forgotten continent, a landscape so vast it creates its own weather—not of clouds and rain, but of pressure and silence. The elevation is absolute; the air is thin, sharp, and so cold it feels like a solid sheet pressing against the skin. It carries no scent, no pollen, no dust of life, only the sterile, mineral tang of bedrock that has been exposed to the void for eons.

The land itself is the primary fact. The erosion is ancient, primordial. The mountains that once clawed at the sky have been worn down to nubs, their sharp peaks ground away by a patience so profound it is a force of nature in itself. What remains is a vast, rolling plain of exposed stone, cracked like old pottery, its surface smoothed in immense swaths and yet fractured into intricate canyons and hollows on a human scale. It is a world that has settled into its final form, ossified, geologically exhausted. It is not dead, merely complete.

The cold is a constant, but so is the dampness. There is no rain, no falling water. Instead, the world sweats. As the pale sun makes its transit, a thin, slick film of condensation weeps from the stones, collecting in runnels and pooling in the countless depressions that pockmark the plateau. This water is unnaturally pure and still, so cold it is on the verge of freezing, capturing the weak light in perfect, tremulous mirrors. It is this intermittent moisture that supports the impossible phenomenon that defines this place: the vegetation.

It is not vegetation as life understands it. It is a lushness of crystal. From the cracks and crevices of the stone, from the very edges of the water-pools, erupt fronds and fans and branches of a transparent, silica-like material. They grow with a slowness that makes geology seem frantic, their forms impossibly intricate, frond-like structures of spun glass branching into sub-atomic filigrees. They are “lush” in their density and coverage, creating forests of glittering, silent foliage that shatter the light into a billion rainbows. They do not photosynthesize; they refract. They drink the condensation, and in some unknown process, they use the cold to grow, their delicate forms rimed with perpetually refreezing moisture, giving them the appearance of being carved from ice. They are utterly silent, utterly still, their only dynamism the imperceptible, century-by-century addition of another crystalline leaf.

Across this immense, sparsely populated vastness, there are signs of construction. They are the urban remnants of a people who must have been as hard and enduring as the stone they worked. Their settlements are not cities, but lonely, widely spaced monoliths. Single, massive towers, or low, sprawling compounds of cyclopean blocks, quarried from the plateau itself. The technology is primitive; the stone is dressed with hammer and chisel, the marks crude and brutal against the billion-year-old smoothness of the landscape. There are no arches, no delicate detailing, only a stark, functional geometry. These structures are not placed to command a view, but to align with something unseen. A tower will point not at a peak, but at a specific, empty point in the sky. A wall will run for hundreds of kilometres only to curve deliberately and end before a chasm. They are astronomical charts, ritualistic lines, or desperate attempts to map a world that defies mapping.

The sound is mostly quiet, a profound silence that is the loudest thing here. The wind, when it comes, is a low, mournful whistle that dies as quickly as it rises. There is no birdsong, no insect hum. But it is not silent. The dynamic quality of this place is rooted in its deep weirdness, the pervasive reality breakdown that permeates everything like a low-level radiation.

This is the rule of the world: stability is an illusion. The silence can be broken not by a sound, but by a visual schism. Watch a distant crystal-forest for centuries, and one day you might see a "tree" of glass suddenly replicate itself, its twin form shimmering into existence for a heartbeat before vanishing. Sometimes, as you walk, the gravity beneath your foot will stutter, a fraction of a second of weightlessness that sends your heart into your throat. The crystalline flora pulses with a light that has no source, a slow, internal luminescence that washes out the weak sun. The most unnerving phenomenon is the Whispering, a “sound” that is felt, not heard—a vibration in the bone, a shift in the corner of the eye—that presages a change. A straight line between two monoliths will, for a day, bend into a gentle arc. A pool of condensation will suddenly freeze solid with a soundless *thump* of kinetic energy, only to melt again in seconds.

Fauna is absent because life, as a fragile, chaotic process, cannot gain a foothold here. The only dynamism is the slow, silent, crystalline growth and the constant, low-level decay of the laws of physics. The builders of the monoliths must have understood this. Their primitive tools were an act of defiance, an attempt to carve permanence into a world of fluid reality. They are long gone, leaving behind a civilization of quiet stone, facing down an eternity of slow, glittering, and silent chaos. To be here is to stand on a world at the very end of its story, where the only things left to grow are the fractures in reality itself.
