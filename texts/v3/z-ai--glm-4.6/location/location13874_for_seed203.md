This place has no ground.

To speak of “ground” is to assume a floor, a base, a thing to stand upon that is fundamentally separate from the air. Here, there is only the Great Below, a vast, hollow space that is not a cavern, for a cavern has walls. The scale is vaster than any mountain range, vaster than the sea, a continental drop in the guts of the world where a sun has never shone. Yet, it is not dark.

It is blindingly bright.

The light does not come from a point. It comes from everything. The entirety of this volume, for millions of cubic miles, is suffused with a lattice of living plant. It is one single, continuous organism, a Photosynthetic Super-structure, the Photophyte. Imagine a crystalline tree growing in all directions at once, its branches as fine as glass fibres, its trunk as wide as a chasm. Now, imagine that the wood of this tree is transparent, and the very flesh of its being glows with a cold, blue-white light.

This is the world. The air you breathe is threaded with these glowing filaments, so dense in places they form a shimmering, solid-seeming surface, so sparse in others that they are only a barely perceptible haze against the distant, chaotic glow. You do not stand on the ground below you; you stand upon a relatively dense matting of these filaments, a temporary platform woven by the organism’s own chaotic growth. Beneath it, the void continues, the same lattice glowing all the way down into an unfathomable, dazzling abyss.

The temperature is a profound, cutting cold. The light gives no heat. It is a cold fire, a stellar process stripped of warmth, leaving only illumination. This is the engine of the dampness. The frigid air, moving in vast, slow tides created by the sheer pressure of this enclosed space, condenses the infinitesimal moisture present in the void onto the surface of the Photophyte's fibres. A constant, silent rain of pure, cold water falls everywhere—a mist so fine it coats everything in a shimmering film before droplets grow too heavy and drift down, down through the glowing lattice, to be caught on another layer a mile below. There are no rivers, only this endless, vertical misting.

The sound is nearly absolute. The predominant sensation is a deep, resonant quiet, an acoustic hush that feels almost like a physical pressure. The growth of the lattice is silent. The light makes no noise. The only sounds are rare and immense: the distant, groaning chime of a billion fibres suddenly shifting under their own colossal weight, a sound like a cathedral-sized bell being struck with a continent; or the faint, papery whisper of a new island of the lattice crystallizing out of the void, a process that might take minutes, silent and visible for a hundred miles.

The ecosystem’s turbulence is a thing of light and structure. The Photophyte is young. The entire space it has colonized is a fresh geological fissure, a recent wound in the world. It is growing with explosive, chaotic energy. Bridges of light-fibre spin across voids, connecting floating continents of the plant. Entire sections of the “floor” beneath you might thin out over a day, their fibres retracting into the greater mass, leaving a platform of precarious emptiness. New, massive spires jut upward like frozen lightning, sharp and young. Standing still is to be part of a silent, violent creation. To stand in one place for too long is to risk being overgrown, woven into the very fabric of the world as the organism continues its relentless, blind expansion.

Life here is an afterthought, a fragile niche carved in the margins of a god.
Fauna is rare, mostly gliders or floaters that navigate the empty channels between the densest growths. They are translucent, winged things that feed on the condensed moisture or smaller organisms living in the film of water on the fibres. They are seen only as fleeting shadows crossing a distant, glowing wall.

The built environment is even more scattered. Remnants of a primitive tool-using species, the Weavers, are occasionally found. They do not carve or mine; they persuade. Using tools of sharpened bone and hardened resin, they coax the fibres of the Photophyte to grow in specific directions, weaving them into small, temporary shelters and platforms. These settlements are never permanent. The Weavers are nomadic by necessity, always migrating ahead of the lattice's chaotic overgrowth or the slow dissolution of their foundations. A single, beautifully-woven nest might be found, deserted, hanging in the void, its fibres already beginning to thin and glow with the same impersonal light as the rest of the world, evidence of a brief, tenacious existence soon to be reabsorbed.

The rules are simple and absolute. There is no solid ground, only living matter of varying density. There is no sun, only the light of life itself. It is always cold, always wet, always bright, and always quiet, except for the Earth-shaking transformation that is the world’s natural state. To be here is to be a ghost in the machine of a living universe, a transient observer in a silent, turbulent storm of light that is eternally creating itself.
