The first sense to assert itself is not sight, but pressure. It is a deep, sub-aural thrum that vibrates up from the soles of your feet through the bone of your skull, a fundamental chord pressed into the air itself. It is the sound of the place, a deafening, all-consuming presence that is less a noise and more the medium through which all other things must pass. It is the groan of stressed alloy, the sigh of impossibly large turbines turning at a geologically patient pace, and the constant, percussive hiss of a million condensed droplets of water striking hot metal.

You are inside a chamber, yet the scale is all wrong for that word. It is cathedralesc. The space is dominated by a vast, central emptiness, a vertical shaft that drops into unseen gloom and rises toward a ceiling hazed with a diffuse, greyish light. To call it a shaft gives the wrong impression; it is a void, a pocket of nothing held within something. The air is thick and warm, like a greenhouse left to simmer under a sunless sky, and it carries a complex odor: the sharp tang of ozone, the clean scent of hot iron, and beneath it all, the rich, loamy smell of wet earth and decaying leaves.

There is no natural ground. Every surface is built. The floor you stand on is a wide, circular catwalk of riveted iron plates, worn smooth in places and pitted with a strange form of bronze-age rot in others. Through the gaps in the grating, you can see more levels receding into the gloom below, each identical, a series of concentric rings suspended in space. The walls are not walls but the interior of a single, colossal machine. A latticework of gargantuan girders, pipes thick enough to drive a vehicle through, and colossal piston-rods, slick with condensation, which move with such slowness their motion is only perceptible if you force yourself to look away and back again.

This is where the lushness resides. The "vegetation" parameter is not a carpet of moss but a riot of vertical life. From the dizzying heights above, thick, rope-like vines, some as thick as a man's waist, descend into the void, their ends lost in the deepening shadows. They are covered in broad, waxy leaves, deep green and perpetually dripping. Other, stranger growths cling directly to the machinery: vast, bracket fungi the color of rust and verdigris that fan out from heat-exchange pipes, their mycelial networks burrowing into the seams of the metal. From these fungi, phosphorescent spores drift down like a slow, silent snow, the only organic softness in the hard-edged world. These organisms are not encroaching on the machine; they are part of it. Their roots tap into the coolant pipes, their leaves feed on the humid air, their symbiosis is the engine's purpose.

The occasional rain is not from clouds, but from above. At random, slow intervals, panels in the high ceiling will groan open, and a deluge of warm, clean water will sluice down through the superstructure, drenching the vines and washing dust and mineral deposits from the machinery. It is the machine sweating, or breathing. The water gathers in troughs and runs along channels carved into the girders, disappearing into the mechanism's guts.

The fauna are just as specialized. You don't see large animals in the traditional sense. The abundance is in the smaller things. Clockwork beetles with intricately patterned steel carapaces scuttle along the pipes, scraping away lichen with mandibles of brass. Their movements make tiny, precise clicks that are completely swallowed by the greater hum. Swarms of what look like dragonflies, but with wings of crystalline glass, navigate the central void, their flight paths tracing the invisible currents of warm air rising from the depths. They are the machine's diagnostics, their shimmering patterns indicating air quality and flow. Most unnerving are the Echo-Moths, creatures with fuzzy, sound-absorbent wings that flutter in specific, slow, mesmerizing patterns, their tiny movements creating silent pockets in the overwhelming roar, a momentary, baffling relief.

The entire place feels ancient. The erosion is not of wind and stone, but of time and function. Every surface is stained with long vertical streaks of rust from the constant dripping, a patina of age that has settled over the industrial violence. There are no sharp, manufactured edges left; everything has been rounded and softened by millennia of operation. This is its "in-its-own-state," a system that has long ago achieved its equilibrium.

The rule of this world is simple and absolute: symbiosis with the mechanism. The plants feed on its heat and waste; the insects maintain its surfaces; the machine, in turn, cultivates its ecosystem. It is a single, unified entity, an artificial god nurturing a tiny, impossible garden in the most extreme of places—a self-contained, manufactured biome at the top of the world, humming its slow, deafening song to no one but itself. And you, a visitor, are a foreign protein, an echo in the thrumming heart of a thing that was never built for you.
