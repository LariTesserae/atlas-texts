The first sense to return is not sight, but a feeling in the teeth—a vibration, a low, guttural hum that seems to rise up from the soles of the feet and resonate in the bones. It is a sound so total that it quickly ceases to be a sound at all and becomes merely a property of the air, like its temperature or its thickness. This is the hum of life, but on a scale that drowns out thought. Layered over this foundational frequency is a constant, high-pitched rustling, like dry leaves skittering across pavement, magnified a million times. It is the sound of countless legs on countless surfaces.

The air itself is a warm, wet blanket. To breathe is to inhale a thick soup of humidity, scented with damp earth, the sharp tang of ammonia, and the deep, fungal musk of a world that has never known the sun. It condenses on the skin in a fine, slick film, and droplets of the same moisture pear on every surface, gathering weight before letting go with a soft, constant *plink* that is lost in the overwhelming chorus.

When the eyes adjust, they do not adjust to a lack of light, but to a new kind of it. There is no sun, no moon, no single point of illumination. The light comes from the world itself. It is a weak, greenish-blue phosphorescence that seeps from the spongy, black carpets that coat the ground and climbs in intricate, branching patterns up the sides of the cavernous space. These are not plants; they are closer to fungi, or perhaps a kind of lichen, their glow barely strong enough to outline the sheer, impossible walls that disappear into a gloomy, vertical distance.

You are standing on a floor that is not rock. To touch it is to feel the texture of a thing made, not born. It is hard and unyielding, like stone, yet under the fingertips, you can discern the ghost shapes of its components: the whorl of a spiraling shell, the geometric plate of a barnacle-like organism, the delicate lattice of an insect's wing, all of it fused together by a patient, geological process into a smooth, seamless whole. This is the "ground," the "bedrock"—a limestone-analogue built from the compacted, petrified bodies of whatever lives here. It is a fossil floor, and a hundred yards above, the ceiling is another, studded with the glittering, multifaceted eyes of what looks like crystallized flies.

This place is built. Every surface, every precipice, every grand, vaulting chamber is a construction. But it is a construction without a builder, or rather, a builder that is everything you see. The overwhelming fauna is the architect. In the gloom, shapes move. They are the source of the rustling sound. Glimpsed only as shifting shadows against the bioluminescent walls, they are skittering, flowing things. Myriapods the length of a man, their countless legs a blur of silent motion against the noise of their brethren. Chitinous grazers that move in vast, slow herds, their mandibles scraping the glowing fungus from the walls, leaving behind polished, dark trails. Armored things like pillbugs the size of rhinos, curled into impassive spheres in the deeper alcoves.

There is no water as rivers or lakes, only this endless, patient dampness. The warmth of the place—remarkable at this height—is metabolic. It is the combined body heat of trillions of creatures, radiating out from the core of this immense, living structure. This heat causes the moisture they exhale to condense on the cooler, outer layers of the Reef, perpetuating the damp, misty environment. The water trickles down in rivulets, pooling in the shallow depressions on the fossil floor, creating still, grey ponds where more things, soft-bodied and pale, grow and feed.

To ascend is to move upward through the layers, to climb into a higher density of existence. The "mountain" is not made of igneous rock thrust up by tectonic forces, but of biologic accretion, grown over millennia. The air grows thicker, warmer, the humming vibration more intense. The sheer-sided canyons you climb through are not weathered by wind and rain, but hollowed out by the passage of generations, their walls smoothed by the constant friction of bodies.

There are no tools, no technology, no sign of a mind that plans. The construction is instinctual, a process as natural and as slow as the growth of a coral reef. The architecture follows the paths of least resistance and the dictates of symbiosis. A chamber forms where a creature dies and its body becomes the anchor for new growth. A tunnel is worn by the daily migration of a specific species. The entire landscape is a record of this life, etched in bone, shell, and chitin.

It is a place of overwhelming presence, a closed-loop ecosystem of staggering, deafening complexity. It feels ancient, settled into its state, its dynamics of change playing out over epochs. It is not a weird place. Within its own context, it is the only place, a normal, stable, and self-perpetuating world built not *for* life, but *of* life itself, a mountain that breathes, a darkness that sings.
