The sky is a high, pale, and indifferent white, a sheet of stretched linen that diffuses the light into a cool, shadowless evenness. There is no sun, only a gradual, imperceptible brightening and dimming that marks the day. From where you stand, the land unfurls in every direction to a horizon so distant it’s warped by the sheer scale of the place, the curve of the world visible as a gentle, hazy dip. This is the Great Plateau of Aethelburg, a continent still being born.

The ground underfoot is not soil. It is rock, but rock that feels unfinished. In places, it's a smooth, dark, glassy stone, polished to a mirror sheen that reflects the featureless sky. In others, it fractures into sharp, newly-made shards, like a slate roof shattered in a slow-motion accident. There are no rounded edges, no smooth river stones, no beaches worn by a million years of tides. This is geology in its infancy, raw and sharp-edged. The highest ridges hum with a faint, almost subliminal warmth, a vestigial heat from their violent extrusion from the planet's crust.

This is a wet world. The cold is a constant, a damp chill that seeps through any fabric and settles deep in the bones. It never freezes, not quite, but it holds a persistent, clinging humidity. Water is everywhere, yet there are no rivers or rain. The air is thick enough to taste with mineral sweetness. From every fissure in the new rock, water weeps, slowly and continuously, creating slick, film-like puddles that pool in depressions too shallow to logically hold them. These pools are impossibly clear, quivering lenses that show the un-weathered stone bottom in perfect detail. The sound of this place is the sound of that seepage: a billion, billion tiny drips and hisses, a constant, gentle whisper that merges with the vast, atmospheric hush into a single, all-encompassing quiet.

If the rock is the canvas and the water the ink, then the city is the press.

It is not a city of buildings, not truly. It is a single, continuous structure that snakes across the plateau for hundreds of kilometres in a line that is almost perfectly straight. It might be called a wall, if walls were a kilometre high and tended to grow in both directions. Its surface is a dark, non-reflective crystalline material that seems to drink the light. There are no windows, no doors in the conventional sense, no discernible street-level. Instead, it is a monument of layered, functional construction: vast, buttressed sections give way to elegantly arched bridges that span canyons so new they have not yet accumulated a single drop of windblown dust. Intricate latticeworks of spires and gantries climb its flanks, housing devices whose purpose is utterly alien. This is urbanism expressed not as a place to live, but as a machine to *be*. It is the source. The city is the engine that is forging the plateau, extruding new land from its forward edge, its resonant hum the only evidence of the titanic forces it marshals. It is a piece of infrastructure so advanced it is indistinguishable from a mountain range.

Vegetation is a stubborn afterthought. Where the city's geological field is weakest, at the very periphery of its influence, life clings on. It is sparse and low-lying. Mats of dark, velvety moss coat the damper rock faces in thick, intricate patterns. Tough, black lichens carve crystalline sigils into the stone. The only "flowers" are frost-laced clusters of mineral filaments that tremble in the rare, gentle breezes. They do not grow from soil; they seem to precipitate directly from the wet, mineral-rich air, a solidification of the environment itself.

But there is fauna, and it is abundant. They are not creatures of flesh and blood, which would struggle in this plant-poor, wet cold. They are creatures of glass and light. The most common are the Grazers, swan-sized beings with long, slender legs of polished chrome and bodies of faceted, semi-transparent crystal. Their "wings" are not for flying but for navigative balance, vast, delicate sails of iridescent film that catch the ambient energy of the city. They move in silent, gliding herds across the open spaces between the city's vast sections, their multifaceted eyes glowing softly. They do not eat the moss. They lower their heads to the weeping rock and lap at the mineral-rich water, their bodies slowly, visibly brightening as they absorb something from the seepage. They are a living part of the system, distributors of energy, mobile components in a planetary-scale process. Higher up, winged predators made of what looks like tangled fiber-optic cables hover and dive, their attacks silent and decisive, a flash of impossible speed and a brief, glittering shower of crystal shards.

This is a dynamic world, but its dynamics are glacial. The city expands, ceaselessly, its forward foundation extruding a fresh strip of continent at a rate perceptible only over decades. The Grazers follow its wake, colonizing the new land. The water weeps, the moss spreads, a slow, silent, and deliberate ecosystem unfolds. There are no seasons, only the constant, silent pulse of the world-making machine. To be here is to be an observer to a process so vast it nearly negates your own existence, watching a world being written, one line of wet, cold rock at a time.
