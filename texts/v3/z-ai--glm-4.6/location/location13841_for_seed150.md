There is no ground here, not in the way you understand it. There is only the present surface of The Lung, a pate of spongy, dark green fibres that yields slightly underfoot, a surface so saturated with cold water that your first step brings a faint, wet sigh from the membrane below. The air is the first shock: it has the biting, metallic chill of a high-altitude precipice, yet it is thick enough to drink, heavy with the scent of wet loam and the clean, ozonic tang of oxygen so dense it tastes sharp on the tongue. Every breath plumes visibly, a brief ghost in the gloom.

You are inside something. That is the immediate, overriding truth of the place. The scale is that of a cathedral, but a cathedral grown, not quarried. The architecture is biological, manifest. Where there should be buttresses, massive ribs of a woody, near-chitinous material arc overhead, their surfaces ridged and sharp-edged, almost crystalline in their geometry. These are the “young” features, “fresh” expressions of a growth still in the process of defining itself. They form a series of interconnected domes and vaulted corridors, all woven from the same singular, living substance. The "city" is not built upon a substrate; the city *is* the substrate. The density here is absolute—there is no empty space. The walls, the floor, the ceiling are a continuous mass of deep, bruised-coloured plant matter, swaddled in a skin of softer, moss-like velvet. Light barely penetrates. Above, the sky is a theoretical ceiling, but here, under this self-made canopy, it is a perpetual, pearlescent twilight. The only luminescence comes from colonies of algae that trace the architectural lines in silent, pulsing green veins, a slow, biological traffic system of faint light.

Water is everywhere, yet never free. There are no streams, no pools, no rivers. The entire city weeps. From the joints where structural ribs meet the membrane walls, from the tips of hanging filamentous growths that resemble stalactites of green silk, a slow and constant process of condensation occurs. The frigid, saturated atmosphere surrenders its moisture, drawing it out in thick, gelatinous drops that gather on every surface. Each bead swells, catches what little light there is, and then lets go, falling with a distinct, echoing *plink* onto the yielding floor. The sound is the only consistent noise in the otherwise silent landscape. It drips and plinks in a complex, arrhythmic percussion that is the dynamic heartbeat of the place. This is the active ecology: a perpetual cycle of atmospheric water condensing on and within the plant-mass, then percolating slowly down through the fibrous layers to be drawn up and wept out again.

This is a world without tools, without heat, without the intrusion of anything inorganic. The only technology is biology itself. The rules are simple and absolute: you are either part of the Lung, or you are a transient within it. The rare fauna reflects this. They are not creatures of fur and feather, but things that have adapted to graze the urban surface. You might see the only native animal, a six-legged arthropod the size of a large dog, its carapace seamless and shimmering like an oil-slick. It moves with unnerving quiet, its legs tipped not with claws but with soft, sucker-like pads. It does not walk on the Lung; it adheres to it, its mouthparts methodically scraping at the velveted skin, consuming the dew-moistened algae. It is a part of the city’s slow metabolism, a mobile janitor. They are solitary and rare, and their presence is a testament to how few ecological niches remain unfilled by the plant-life itself.

To touch a wall is to feel a faint, subaudible hum, a vibration of life so immense it is felt more than heard. It is the groan of slow growth, the creak of cellulose under the strain of its own architecture, the thrumming pulse of nutrient transport somewhere deep within the mass. The sharp features are an illusion of permanence; this place is constantly renewing itself, shedding outer layers while growing new ribs within. The space you occupy was not formed, but emerged. The gloom is not a lack of light, but a feature of the organism, shading its inner workings from a cold, high-altitude sun it no longer needs. This is not a place you conquer; it is a place you are permitted. You walk through its corridors, listen to its weeping, feel its cold, living breath, and you understand that you are nothing more than a temporary cell in the body of a strange, lonely god.
