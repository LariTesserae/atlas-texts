The air is its first statement. It has no smell of water, of dust, of decay. It is cool and inert on the tongue, carrying only the faint, clean tang of ozone and the ghost of hot metal. It is a breath that has been used a million times before, filtered and cycled, stripped of all personality save the fact of its own perfect, lifeless dryness. This is the air of a sealed place.

There is no ground to speak of, not in any conventional sense. What you stand on is a grated platform of sintered iron composites, its surface sharp-edged and new. Below, the platform doesn’t end; it simply opens into a gulf. Looking down is to stare into a bottomless well of indigo gloom, pierced at random intervals by the distant, angry glint of white-hot furnaces or the momentary, blinding flash of arc-welding. The chasm seems to fold back on itself into impossible darkness.

To look up is to see the same. The hang of the space is not a ceiling, but the same vertical void, a mirror of the depths. Far, far above, so high that perspective dissolves, other platforms and structures hang like motes of dust caught in a sunbeam—if there were a sun. The sense is one of extreme elevation, of being a speck clinging to a wall that stretches upward forever. This is not a place so much as a place’s *interior*, a pocket of impossible scale.

The sound is a low, pervasive thrum that resonates in the bones. It is the heartbeat of vast, unseen machinery. And over this, a constant, dry whisper like sleet on a tin roof. It is the sound of a million, million legs on a million metal surfaces.

They are the Klik. They have no flesh; they are all exoskeleton and articulated joint, built from the same dull-grey alloys and ceramics as the world they inhabit. Their bodies are pragmatic arrangements of pincers, multi-faceted sensor-arrays, and clamping limbs. They do not wear clothes or carry tools; they *are* the tools. Some are small, skittering like ants in the conduits that run like vine-thick cables along the walls. Others are immense, brahminic creatures the size of ground-cars, their movements ponderous and hydraulic as they haul massive, unrecognizable components along the central void on suspended rails.

This is the city. Not a collection of buildings, but a single, continuous structure. Factories are not separate edifices but bulges in the "flesh" of the wall, theirclangour and heat radiating outwards through perforated vents. Living quarters are not homes but crystalline growths of modular cells fused to the gantries. There are no open squares, only grooved conveyer-belts and concave plazas that serve as collection or distribution points for raw materials. The environment is dense to the point of saturation; there is no empty space that is not already claimed by a pipe, a conduit, a walkway, or a nest of Klik.

The light is dim and functional. It is not illumination in the human sense, but the ambient spill of the world’s own processes. Glowing filaments run in neat racetrack patterns down the walls, pulsing with a slow, cool light. The flora is absent, replaced by this technological photosynthesis. The only flashes of brilliance are the violent, localized flares of industrial action—a jet of plasma used for cutting a girder, the incandescent cascade of molten slag being poured from a crucible high above.

The turbulence here is not of weather, but of activity. A section of the wall a hundred meters away, previously stable for as long as memory serves, suddenly shudders. A great, pincer-armed Klik the size of a siege engine attaches itself to the structure. With a series of thunderous reports, it begins to systematically dismantle the perfectly sound gantries, snapping support beams and feeding them into its own internal furnace. Molten metal drips into collection channels below. In its wake, smaller Klik swarm, laying down fresh, sharply-edged flooring, weaving new conduits from spools of glowing filament. The city is in a constant state of digestion and regrowth, ensuring no part of it ever grows old, no surface ever softens. It is a geological process of metal, an ecosystem of pure mechanics.

Its weirdness lies in the subtle, symbiotic seam between creature and construction. You will see a Klik approach a humming engine housing. Its armored thorax will split open, revealing not organs, but a complex array of gyroscopic sensors andactal couplers. It will slot itself directly into the machine, its limbs locking into place, its multifaceted eyes darkening as it becomes a temporary, living valve or capacitor. The hum of the engine will pitch and stabilize. There is no operator; only an integration.

This is a hidden place. The Klik have no concept of a planet or a sky. For them, the universe is the shaft, the chasm, the Confluence. They are the citizens of a single, industrial-purpose organ embedded within a far vaster and unimaginable body they call the Lattice. They are both its abundant fauna and its own immune system, their ceaseless, turbulent activity the very definition of its life. It is a small room, a pocket-scale environment, that is also a mountain. It is young and ancient, quiet and roaring, all at once.
