There is no sky. The concept of a sky is a story told elsewhere, a myth of blue and distance. Here, above, is only a perfect, featureless black. It is a canopy so absolute that the immense dark structures of this place do not fade into a horizon, they simply terminate, their upper edges dissolving into a lightless void. The ground far below is no different. This is a place suspended between two unending blacks.

You are standing on the Terminus Plateau, the final, eroded nub of a mountain range that has been ground to its root-stone over a span of time that makes the birth of stars feel like a recent event. The wind is a memory. The air is still, inert, and profoundly, unnervingly dry. It is not empty, but filled with a thin, stable gas that carries no scent and holds no moisture. To breathe it is to inhale warmth— a steady, ambient heat that radiates not from a sun but from the deep bones of the plateau itself, a geological residual that has long since lost its source but not its warmth. It is a temperature that is neither comfortable nor uncomfortable, merely constant.

The silence is the first and most lasting law. It is not the silence of absence, but the silence of completion, a universe that has shouted itself out. It is a pressure in the ears. Yet, within it, there are exceptions. A very deep, almost sub-audible hum, felt more in the soles of the feet than heard, is the planet's sole, continuous sound. It is the resonance of zero, the frequency of a system at perfect equilibrium. Very rarely, from an indeterminate distance, comes a single, resonant *CLANG*—a sound like a bell forged from a neutron star, struck once and allowed to ring down across eons. The sound is so full of age and space it feels physically heavy.

In the profound dark, the "buildings" are not seen so much as they are perceived as negations of the void. They are monolithic, colossal structures of a dark, non-reflective metal, spaced kilometers apart across the vast, flat expanse of the plateau. They are not a city in any known sense; they are an urban blueprint stretched to the breaking point. There are refineries shaped like inverted roots, their pipes and conduits thick as subway tunnels, burrowing into the ground. There are forge-halls the size of stadiums, their immense doors sealed shut. There are silo towers that climb into the unseeable heights, their surfaces smooth and featureless. The overall effect is one of immense power and perfect desolation.

There is no vegetation. No lichen, no moss, no fossil of a leaf. Life, as a chemical process based on water and carbon, is an alien concept here. The closest thing to growth is a strange form of mineral crystallization that patterns every metal surface. These are not corrosion or decay, but a stable, final-state accretion. In some places, this crystalline "scale" has a phosphorescent quality, emitting a faint, cold, blue-green light. It is the only consistent illumination. These patterns do not glow with warmth, but with a sterile, informational light, like circuits on a dead motherboard. They trace diagrams and pathways across the flanks of the great refineries, hinting at a logic that is no longer active but frozen in place.

This is an industrial necropolis, but it is not rusting, not collapsing into ruin. It is preserved. The "ancient erosion" parameter refers not to the rock, but to possibility. Any dynamic process that could change this place ran its course and finished. The last energy was spent, the final chemical reaction occurred, and everything settled into a state of such deep stability that further change is almost thermodynamically impossible. It is a world held in a state of suspended animation.

The fauna are equally rare and strange. They are called Kine. They are not biological, nor are they simple machines. They are manifestations—semi-ethereal glitches in the profound stillness, humanoid figures made of faint, shifting light and low-level static. They appear without warning, gliding silently along the causeways or phasing through the sealed walls of a forge-hall. They never interact with anything. They perform no discernible task. They are echoes, phantom limbs of the immense, automated workforce that once operated these engines. They are the ghosts of process, repeating a path worn into the fabric of spacetime itself by countless repetitions over an unimaginable duration. To watch one is to watch a three-dimensional recording of an action that has no beginning or end. They are the ultimate expression of the "never changing" rule: life-like motion without life, without purpose, without change.

This is the rule of the world: stasis is god. Water, should it be introduced from some impossible off-world source, would not nourish but flash-vaporize, perhaps explosively, a sudden violent anomaly in a system that has forgotten violence. To start one of the great engines would be an act of supreme cosmic vandalism, disrupting a quietus that has lasted longer than the Earth has existed. The landscape is not waiting for anything. It has arrived. This warm, dark, silent, and ancient industrial plateau is its own final form—a machine at the end of its function, a city whose inhabitants are the faint ghosts of their own labor, a world whose ecology is the slow, beautiful, and terrifying crystallization of absolute zero.
