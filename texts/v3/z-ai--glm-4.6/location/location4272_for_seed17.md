To be here is to be suspended.

The world is water, not merely covered by it. It is an oceanic planet, a sphere of liquid held tight by its own gravity, with no surface to speak of, no boundary between air and sea. There is only the deep.

And it is cold. A cold that seeps past any insulation, a gentle but absolute chill that slows the pulse and clarifies the mind. The water itself is unnaturally dense, heavy, its viscosity like thin oil. It pulls at you, a constant, patient weight. Yet this is not the abyssal dark of Earth's deepest trenches. The entirety of this liquid realm suffused with a diffuse, steady light. It is a pale, sterile luminescence, the color of winter twilight, and it comes from everywhere and nowhere at once. It seems to be a fundamental property of the medium, as if countless microscopic energetic resonances within the water柱 create a perpetual, ambient glow. The light never wavers. It casts no true shadows, only gradients of lesser gloom.

The "ground," such as it is, exists miles below. These are the high places of this world: continent-scale seams of fresh, sharp rock pushed up from the planetary mantle. They are not mountains, but seamounts of mythic scale, their peaks still two miles beneath the "surface" that isn't a surface. The erosion is young, and the features are cruelly sharp. Vast, sheared-off cliffs of obsidian-black volcanic glass drop into canyons that would make the Grand Canyon a modest ditch. There are no sediments, no beaches of worn sand. The rock is raw, unweathered, and its edges are as keen as the day they cooled. On these jagged peaks, the only solid surfaces in all the world, stand the structures.

They are scattered and impossibly rare. To travel from one to another would take a lifetime of silent, unchanging drifting. They are not cities or settlements, but singular, monolithic artifacts. Each is a perfect, geometric shape—a vast, black cube hundreds of meters on a side, a seamless metallic tetrahedron rooted to a peak, a cylindrical shaft that plunges miles into the crust. Their surfaces are without feature, without inscription or viewport. They are industrial in their sheer, imposing scale and purposeful design, but they show no signs of activity. No smoke, no light, no movement. They are simply there, like forgotten tombstones for gods of engineering.

And from them, as from the very bones of the planet, comes the sound.

The world is deafening. It is a sound you feel more than hear, a seismic harmony that resonates up from the seafloor and through the dense water. It is the overwhelming, multilayered drone of a billion-ton engine running in perfect, timeless equilibrium. It is the hum of transformers the size of mountains, the thrum of planetary-scale pumps, the vibrational frequency of an unknowable industrial process. This sound has no beginning and no end. It is the sonic signature of the never-changing reality. It is the sound of a machine that has been running for so long its original purpose is meaningless, its operators long gone. It is the sound of stasis made audible.

There is fauna. Abundant, in a way, but utterly in keeping with the emptiness. You might wait a century, suspended in the grey twilight, and then see it. A leviathan. It is not flesh and blood, not in any way you would recognize. It is a creature of metal and mineral, a bio-mechanical colossos kilometers long, its shape a flowing, hybrid of armor plate and crystalline structure. It moves with the horrifying slowness of a glacier, its passage silent, its massive form undisturbed by the crushing depth. It does not hunt or feed. It simply *is*—a mobile component of the global machine, a self-repairing drone on a geological timescale. One passes, navigating the jagged peaks with an ancient, programmed precision, and then it is gone, and the long silence—filled only by that eternal hum—reasserts itself.

There is no vegetation. No algae, no coral, no kelp. The mineral rock faces are bare. The ecology, if it can be called that, is one of pure energy and mineral transfer, a closed loop managed by the machines and the leviathans. Life, as a process of growth and decay, does not exist here.

This is a world in a state of perpetual, frozen function. The light does not dim. The water does not flow, save for impossibly slow planetary currents. The hum does not change key. The structures do not crumble. The leviathans do not age. It is a colossal, planetary-scale mechanism that achieved its equilibrium eons ago and has simply continued to run, its operators, its builders, its very reason for being, lost to a time before this static present began. To be here is to be a ghost in the machine, a fleeting anomaly in a universe defined by its own unchanging, deafening, and perfect sameness.
