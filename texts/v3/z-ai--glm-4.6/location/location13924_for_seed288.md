The first thing you register is the sound. It is not a noise that enters the ears so much as a pressure that presses on the skin, a vibration that travels up through the soles of your feet and resonates in the bones of your skull. It is the sound of a worldquake in slow motion, a bass note of such depth and longevity that it has become the very medium through which existence is perceived. It is the combined chorus of a trillion chitinous legs shifting on stone, of countless dry-rustling wings, of deep, guttural calls that are less vocalizations and more tectonic plate-shifts of air. This is the constant, deafening hymn of the place, a sound so overwhelming that silence itself would be a violent, alien intrusion. Beneath this foundational roar, a higher register of clicks, whistles, and scrapes forms a sibilant, ever-shushing static, like an ocean made of broken glass.

You are standing on a floor that is not soil, not rock, but something in between. It is a smooth, unbroken sheet of stone, cool and slightly damp to the touch, though there is no liquid water to be seen. It bears the color of old ivory, shot through with faint, swirling patterns of grey and blue that resemble a petrified wood grain on a geological scale. In the perpetual gloom, the surface seems to drink the light. Run your hand over it and you feel the impossible smoothness, the result of eons of a friction so slow it can barely be called erosion. It is the oldest thing you have ever touched, a surface settled into its final form before memory began. Here and there, the sound is joined by a single, resonant *plink*, as a droplet of pure, heavy condensation finally gathers its mass and falls from the distant dark above, striking the floor with an impact that feels like a tiny, deliberate hammer blow. It does not spread. The droplet is instantly sucked into the stone, vanishing without a trace, its thirst quenched by the hungry, ancient crystalline lattice below. The air is thick with this same thirst, a cool, humid breath that smells of clean, wet minerals and a sharp, ozonic tang, like the air after a lightning strike in a cave.

Light, such as it is, is a uniform, sourceless twilight. There is no sun, no moon, no single point of illumination. The world is simply filled with a dim, violet-tinged luminescence that emanates from everything. The vast, cavernous ceiling, so high its vaults are lost in a soft, featureless gloom, glows with a faint, internal light. The floor beneath your feet emits the same soft gloss. It is a place trapped in a state of permanent dawn or endless dusk, a cool shade that will never brighten or fade. In this light, colors are muted, deepened. The world is a painting in shades of ultramarine, charcoal, and slate, with edges softened as if seen through a sheen of water. This is the light of ancient processes, the faint, residual glow of a universe whose fire has long since settled into a warm, quiet ember.

The landscape across this stone plain is one of sparseness and immense scale. At first, it seems empty. But as your eyes adjust to the gloom, you begin to discern shapes strewn across the vastness. They do not grow in jungles or thickets, butstand apart, like lonely monoliths in a desert. These are the "trees." They are not made of wood, but of stone, living stone. Great, branching spires of crystalline silica, some as slender as needles and others as thick as ancient oaks, thrust upwards from the floor. Their surfaces are faceted, catching the ambient light and refracting it into a million tiny, rainbow glitters that ripple as the air moves. They are pale, ghostly things—white, grey, and ethereal green—silent and immobile. Their "lushness" is in their sheer number and their impossible lifespan, not in their density. You can see for kilometers across the floor, a forest of isolated, mineral giants, each one a silent, slow-growing mountain of life. They do not shed leaves or bear fruit. They simply *are*, their growth measured in the passage of ages, their only dynamic the slow, geological accretion of new crystal facets.

And punctuating this crystalline forest, at irregular intervals across the plains, are the structures. They are the source of the complex, higher-pitched end of the world's roaring symphony. At a distance, they look like hills, irregular mounds of woven shadow. As you draw closer, their scale resolves: they are not built, but *grown*. These are the cities, sprawling, multi-levelled constructions of dried, fibrous material harvested from the crystalline trees, tightly woven and bonded with some unknown biological secretion into a material as tough as horn. They are colossal, organic cathedrals of mud and fiber, riddled with tunnels, chambers, and vast, open-sided platforms. They cling to the bases of the largest crystal-spines, they sprawl across the open plains, they climb the cliffs of distant chasms that crack the stone floor. These are termite mountains the size of districts, woven wasp-nests that could house a metropolis.

They are alive with movement. This is the source of the overwhelming sound. The residents are huge creatures, many times the size of a man, built on a twelve-limbed plan that is vaguely insectoid but also something else entirely. Their bodies are covered in a hard, chitinous carapace in shades of deep indigo and violet, making them nearly invisible against the glowing backdrop. They move with a slow, deliberate, and unnerving grace, their multitude of legs moving in a fluid, rippling wave that carries them across the stone. They communicate not with sounds from a throat, but by clicking their jointed limbs together, creating a percussive language that adds its sharp, frantic rhythm to the world’s bass drone.

They are the architects and the laborers. With powerful, pincer-like forelimbs, they carve new chambers, harvest the fibrous "bark" of the crystal trees, and meticulously repair their woven homes. They tend to vast, fungal gardens that grow in dark, damp chambers within their mounds. They herd smaller, skittering creatures through the open avenues. There is no metal, no fire, no tool more complex than the living pars of their own bodies. Their technology is biology, their engineering instinct on a planetary scale. The cities are extensions of their own bodies, a physical manifestation of their collective will, built and reshaped over countless generations, standing as permanent fixtures of this ancient landscape.

There is no wind here, not really. Only the slow, massive displacement of air caused by the movement of the great builders, a breath that sighs through the crystal spires and makes the woven structures groan. Time is measured not by the rising of a sun, but by the slow growth of a facet on a crystal tree, the expansion of a new chamber in the city-hive, the glacially slow smoothing of a stone ridge. This is a world at the end of its geological story, a place that has settled into a final, stable state. It is not dead. It is profoundly, thunderously, eternally alive. It is a landscape that has been ancient for so long that it has forgotten what it was like to be young, and now simply exists, in a cool, dim, roaring present that feels, in its immensity, like it will last forever.
