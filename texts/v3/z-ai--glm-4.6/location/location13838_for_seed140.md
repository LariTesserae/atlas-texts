Here, there is no sun. The concept of a star is an archived myth. Light has no history here, only pressure. The first thing you learn is that you do not see this place; you feel it in your bones.

The air itself is the medium of existence. It is heavy with latent heat and water, a perpetual sigh that feels like the breath of a slumbering titan. It’s warm enough that bare skin feels no chill, only a constant, supple dampness. Every surface is slick to the touch, beaded with moisture that never truly falls as rain, but rather precipitates from the atmosphere itself, weeping from any available edge in slow,_routes of glistening condensation.

You stand upon the Plate. To call it ground is a misnomer; it is a surface of impossible smoothness, a stone floor that stretches for a distance measured in cathedral-spans, not horizons. Its edges are lost in a gloom so profound it feels like a physical presence. This is the high place, but the air is not thin. It is dense, saturated, and gentle. The Plate is an ancient ruin, its sharp corners and original purpose worn away by eons of a process so slow it can only be called patience. It is the final form of a mountain, ground down not by wind or ice, but by something far more inexorable: time itself.

The vegetation does not cover the Plate in a carpet, but erupts from it in isolated, violent riots of life. These are clustered around the few landmarks that interrupt the vast flatness: the scattered remains of structures. They are not buildings in any recognizable sense, but monoliths, torii, and ramps of the same dark, weeping stone as the Plate, their geometry suggestive but eroded into ambiguity. From these, a lushness pours. Fleshy, phosphorescent fungi cling in vertical shelves, their caps emitting a soft, internal glow that is irrelevant to sight and everything to texture. Vines as thick as a man’s waist, braided from what looks like black glass and chitin, writhe up the sides of the monoliths, ending in broad, fan-like leaves that do not photosynthesize, but audiosynthesize. They are tuned like vast, organic instruments.

This is woven with a million higher tones: the percussive clicking and droning of countless insects hidden in the fungal forests; the long, mournful notes drawn from the broad, resonant leaves as they tremble in the sonic currents; and the calls of the larger fauna.

The fauna are the reason for the emptiness and the abundance. They are not creatures of flesh and blood, not entirely. They are the Echo-drakes, vast and ethereal, their bodies composed of slowly swirling vapor and shimmering energy fields, held in a cohesive shape by the very sound they produce. They swim through the air of the plateau in slow, majestic arcs, whales of a twilight ocean. You don't see them; you infer their presence by the shifting, doppler-effect of their songs. Their call is a deep, resonant bell-tone that passes through you, vibrating your sternum and loosening your teeth. Their abundance is a fact of the soundscape—the air is thick with their passing, their overlapping songs creating the dense tapestry of sound that is this world’s light. Their physical density, however, is almost zero. You could walk through the space where one is and feel only a change in pressure and temperature, a sublime, terrifying closeness.
