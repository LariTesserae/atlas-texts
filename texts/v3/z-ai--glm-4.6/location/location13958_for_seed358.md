The first thing you learn is that silence is a myth you once believed in. Down here, there is only the hum. It is not a sound you hear with your ears so much as a pressure you feel in your bones, a constant, deep-vibrating drone that makes your teeth ache and the air in your lungs thrum. It is the voice of the Great Engine, the primary pump, though you have never seen it. They say it is the size of a city block, a single piston of rusted iron the size of a tower, cycling once every few minutes. Each downstroke is a subsonic groan that travels through the stone and steel, an exhalation that seems to draw the breath from the world. Each upstroke is a higher-pitched, electrical shriek, the sound of a billion volts arcing across failing contacts. The two sounds, the groan and the shriek, overlap into one seamless, deafening symphony of labor that has been playing for longer than anyone remembers.

You are in the Sunken Basin, though there is no sun. The sky is a flat ceiling of rock so high it is lost in a perpetual, chemical twilight. The light comes from down here. It comes from miles of cabling that snake across the ceiling like glowing metal veins, terminating in inefficient arc lamps that cast a hard, blue-white glare on the world below. The light is impossibly bright right under a lamp, a disc of sterile clarity, but between these islands of illumination lies a thick, oily gloom where shapes move and resolve themselves only when they are upon you.

The air is warm and wet, thick enough to drink. It smells of hot iron, ozone, wet stone, and something else, faintly sweet and organic, like a fungus blooming in a forgotten cellar. Every surface is slick with a constant sheen of moisture. It doesn't rain, not in the way you know it. Water leaks from the world. It beads on the underside of the iron conduits overhead and falls in fat, rhythmic *plink-plink-plinks* that are utterly swallowed by the Engine's drone. It weeps from the rock faces in steady, silent sheets, tracing mineral patterns like abstract maps on the fractured stone. You wipe a hand over a wall, and it comes away gritty with lubricants and wet with condensed atmosphere.

This is a world of raw edges. The Basin is an infant in geological terms. It was not carved by a river over eons; it was ripped open in a single, violent event, a cataclysm of pressure and fracture that happened only a few thousand years ago. The rock faces are still sharp, jagged, and chaotic. There are no smoothed river stones, no rounded hills. There are only great, tilted plates of basalt and crystal like the bones of some titanic, broken creature, thrusting up from the floor at impossible angles. The ground underfoot is a treacherous shingle of this shale-like rock, brittle and sharp enough to slice through a boot sole if you're not careful. Erosion has barely had time to whisper its name here; the Great Engine's deafening churn seems to be holding it at bay, or perhaps postponing the inevitable.

Sprawled across this raw, nascent landscape is the decay of ambition. This is an industrial world. Structures are scattered, not as a town, but as components of a single, vast machine. There are the colossal housing units for now-silent drills, towers perforated with access ladders that lead nowhere. There are vats the size of amphitheaters, crusted with mineral salts and silent, catwalks circling them precariously. Conveyor belts, snaking for miles, lie motionless under a fine layer of rust and damp dust. Small, corrugated metal huts—control rooms, barracks, workshops—are wedged into crevices or bolted onto the sides of rock formations, their windows long since shattered, their doors hanging open into the damp twilight. It's a place built on a scale that defies human comfort, a landscape of function so vast it forgets the individual.

And yet, it is packed. The space feels impossibly tight, not because of the structures, but because of the life. There is almost no vegetation. The dim, artificial light provides no energy for a green world. The water is too mineral-rich. Instead, the ecosystem is built on industry’s leavings. The abundance of fauna is astonishing. Every pipe drips with a living, moving carpet of slick, black isopods, some as large as a man’s hand, their many legs clicking softly on the metal. Swarms of pale, blind-winged moth-things hover in the warm updrafts from the machinery, their bodies glowing faintly with some internal bioluminescence that makes them look like sparks in the gloom. Fluted, amphibian-like creatures with too many legs cling to the wet rock, their skin patterned like rust. They navigate by sound, their calls—an arsenal of clicks, chirps, and croaks—totally lost in the Engine's roar. This is a fauna of scavengers and specialist-feeders. They eat the rust, the mold, each other, the detritus of a forgotten civilization. They fill every pipe, every shadow, every forgotten space.

To be here is to be a minor character in a slow-motion drama. The change is glacial. The Great Engine’s scream is a measure of its own slow, inevitable decline. Each cycle is a fraction weaker than the last. The water's patient drip is carving its first channels in the raw rock, beginning the true work of erosion that will, one day, millions of years from now, soften this jagged wound in the earth. The factories are not being reclaimed by nature in a generation; they are being dissolved, molecularly, by the constant damp and the endless, methodical chewing of a billion tiny creatures.

You stand on a metal gangway, slick with condensation, overlooking a chasm filled with the shadowy hulks of machinery. The hum presses in on you from all sides. The air is warm and heavy in your lungs. You watch a column of pale, glowing insects swirl in the light from a failing arc lamp, a thousands-strong galaxy that no one has ever seen. Down here, in the newborn dark, under the deafening and dying engine, the world is slow, but it is not still. It is absolutely, terrifyingly, alive.
