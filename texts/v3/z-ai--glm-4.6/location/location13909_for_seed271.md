The air changes first. You step down from a dry, dusty ridge into a pocket of warmth, a breath that is still and heavy against the skin. The light, already a muted and indifferent gray from a high, thin ceiling of cloud, softens further here, losing what little direction it had. This is the Hollow.

It is not large. You could walk its perimeter in under an hour, a simple, bowl-shaped depression in the land, like a giant's palm pressed gently into the earth. The scale is intimate, almost architectural. The ground swells in soft, rounded mounds and subsides into gentle shelves, all of a single, dark stone, fine-grained and smooth like river-washed basalt. Ages of water have worked here, not with the violence of a flood, but with the persistence of a sigh. There are no sharp edges, no fractured crags; every form is bloated, softened, worn to its final, placid shape. This is a landscape that has finished eroding. It has settled.

And it is damp. This is the Hollow's defining character. The air itself holds a fine, invisible moisture that beades on your skin and darkens your clothes. There is no standing water, no stream or pool, only a constant, patient weeping from the stone itself. Every surface carries a slick sheen, and on the underside of the stony ledges, water gathers slowly into a single, heavy drop, holds its shape for a long moment, and then falls with a soft, resonant *plink* that is one of the only sounds in this place. The plops are spaced far apart, metronomic, marking a time that moves but does not change.

Vegetation is an afterthought, a statement made in a whisper. Clusters of coarse, dark green sedge grow in the cracks where the meager soil has accumulated, their blades stiff and wiry, perpetually glossy with wet. They do not seed or spread; they simply are. Here and there, the stone is mapped with the intricate, crusty charts of lichen, in muted shades of rust, olive, and slate gray. In the very center of the Hollow, where the ground is most level, stands a single tree. It is not tall, but wide, its trunk as bent and smooth as the surrounding rock, its bark covered in the same slick, black-looking moss. It has no leaves, only a few skeletal branches that reach out into the dim air, curled and unmoving, as if petrified in mid-gesture.

There are no birds. No insects. The silence is not an absence of noise, but a presence in itself—a thick, woolly blanket that absorbs all edge. The only sound is the rare, distant drip of condensation, and the sound of your own breathing, which feels loud and intrusive.

Here and there, the land is scarred by the faintest memory of intent. Not a ruin, but a ruin's ghost. For a dozen yards, a low wall runs along the crest of one of the stony mounds, only a few courses high, its stones so weathered and merged with the earth that it is hard to tell where construction ends and geology begins. On a small level patch near the southern rim stand three square-cut stones, each the size of a man's torso, arranged in a neat line. They are pitted and stained, crowned with moss, their purpose utterly lost to time. They are not eerie, only profoundly lonely, artifacts from an era of ambition that has long since been worn away by the patient damp.

The rules here are simple, though unspoken. Nothing happens. The warm, dim light never brightens or dims. The stones never get drier or wetter. The sparse plants do not grow, wither, or die. This place has achieved a perfect, unyielding equilibrium. It is a system that has run its course and found its final, stable state, a geological and biological equation that has been solved. It is a landscape in a state of grace, or perhaps a state of suspended animation, and to stand within it is to feel like a fleeting, disruptive anomaly—a being made of change in a world that has forgotten how.
