The first thing you feel is the cold. It’s a patient, skeletal cold that has long ago given up on heat and now simply exists as a state of being. It doesn't bite or sting; it settles, a lens of frigid air through which everything else is viewed. It rides a humidity that you can’t see but can taste on the back of your tongue and feel collecting in the fibers of your clothes—a fine, perpetual dampness that is the region’s only true intimacy with water. The air itself feels thick enough to catch in your throat, cold and wet.

You are standing in a basin, a low place where the cold air pools. The ground underfoot is not stone or soil, not yet. It’s a dark, glassy substance, smooth and unfeatured, like obsidian before it’s been fully cooled. It is fractured into tessellated polygons, the wide, black plates separated by thin, hairline cracks that emit a faint, internal luminescence—the source of the place’s strangely normal, ambient daylight. This crepuscular glow is constant, painting the landscape in shades of grey and muted, bruised purples.

You look up, and the world becomes a contradiction.

This is a city, or the frantic dream of one. Structures of impossible density crowd the basin, a small, contained space only a few hundred meters across. They are towers and archways, winding ramps and what appear to be multi-leveled walkways, all packed together so tightly that they form a single, continuous mass, like a petrified forest of architecture. The scale is cathedral-like, but the boundaries are visible; the sheer cliff walls of the valley, made of the same dark, glassy material as the ground, enclose the burgeoning metropolis. Yet the city is not dead stone. It is being born.

This is the emerging erosion of the place. It is not a process of decay, but of accretion. Raw reality congeals here. From the inky, glassy floor, the substance of the city grows. You can see it, if you are still. Here and there, a thin, glossy spire oozes upward from a fissure, its growth so glacially slow you would mistake it for a trick of the eye. The surfaces of the "buildings" are not finished. They weep a viscous, black ichor that hardens moments later into new strata, adding layers of terraces or buttresses. The overall impression is of a vast, three-dimensional schematic being rendered in the world’s most stubborn, most physical material. The "urban" is a stage of geology; the "construction" is an embryonic process of world-forming.

The technology behind it is beyond comprehension. It isn't machinery in any recognizable sense. It is a law of this specific locale, a fundamental principle that dictates matter arrange itself into a metropolitan pattern. The underlying physics are bent, forcing the raw substrate—this unformed, glassy earth—to follow a blueprint. The faint light from the cracks is the only sign of the immense energy involved, the slow, silent hum of a god-compiler working through lines of existential code. The reality breakdown is not chaotic here; it is meticulous, organized, and terrifyingly deliberate.

Life here is an afterthought, a footnote. Sparse vegetation clings to the lower levels of the still-forming architecture—a tough, black moss that glitters with condensed moisture, feeding not on sunlight, but on the faint, sub-audible thrum of the city’s gestation. Fauna is rarer still. You might, over hours, witness the only native creature: a silent, scuttling thing made of jointed crystal, like an insect carved from quartz. It moves with insectoid purpose across the surfaces, scraping away imperfections, eating the still-soft ichor, its presence a tiny, living cog in the grand, inorganic machine. It makes no sound.

The silence is the most profound presence. The dense, layered structures swallow noise. The only sounds are the ones your own presence creates—the crunch of your feet on the tessellated floor, the dampened thud of your heartbeat. And the whisper from the cracks. Not a wind, but a slow exhalation of cold, damp air that smells of ozone and deep, wet stone. Occasionally, a single drop of condensation, swollen and heavy, will fall from a high archway and hit the glassy floor with a sharp, clean *tink* that echoes for an unnervingly long time.

To be here is to be an impermanent feature in a landscape that endeavors for permanence on its own terms. The ramp you climbed an hour ago may now be a series of deep steps, or it may have sealed over entirely, a new, smooth face staring blankly back. The dynamics are slow but absolute. You do not inhabit this place; you are a temporary impurity within it, an error in the grand design that will be eventually eroded away, not by wind or water, but by the patient, relentless, and utterly alien logic of urban creation. You are inside the womb of a city made of geology, and you are not meant to survive its birth.
