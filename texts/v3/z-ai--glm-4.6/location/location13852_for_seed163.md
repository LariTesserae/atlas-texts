The light here is a physical blow. It strikes the plateau with the intensity of a high-altitude star, reflecting off a world that is permanently, aggressively wet. There is no dust to soften the glare; every surface is slick—polished stone, saturated moss, sheets of standing water—and all of it conspires to throw the sunlight back into your eyes. It creates a landscape of blinding whites and searing greens, a place where you must squint even to look down at your boots.

You are standing on a high tableland, a geologic shelf suspended in the cool, thin air. The temperature is bracing, a persistent chill that settles in the marrow, kept from freezing by the sheer thermal mass of the water swirling through the environment.

The ground beneath you feels strange—spongy, yet unyielding. This is the "Total Vegetation" taken to its literal extreme. There is no distinct soil layer, only a hyper-dense, interwoven matrix of root systems, mosses, and creeping mats that have cemented themselves into a unified, living substrate. You are walking on the skin of a single, planet-sized organism. It is seamless, an endless green-grey carpet that stretches to the horizon, absorbing the sharp geology beneath it.

For the geology here is raw. The erosion score is low, meaning the land is embryonic. The rock formations that do pierce the vegetative skin are jagged, violent shards of granite and basalt. They look as if the earth cracked open only yesterday, leaving behind fresh, un-weathered scars. These cliffs and ridges possess knife-edge sharpness; time has not yet had the chance to round them off or soften their aggression. From these raw wounds, the water weeps. It does not flow in lazy rivers here; it seeps directly out of the stone face, coating every cliff in a shimmering, wet veneer, dripping perpetually into the thirsty root-mat below.

Despite the total coverage of flora, the place feels empty. The density of presence is sparse. There are no dense thickets, no claustrophobic groves. Instead, the vegetation acts as architecture: immense, solitary columns that rise like biological skyscrapers, spaced miles apart. These might be trees, or perhaps something closer to coral, tall and ribbed, their canopies high enough to form a separate ceiling of mist. Between these giants lies the open, quiet expanse of the living floor, creating a sense of immense, lonely scale. It is a town-sized emptiness, a valley of distinct features separated by rolling, wet miles.

And it is loud. The sound parameter is deafening, yet the source is not biological. There are no birds to sing over this roar. The noise is industrial, a constant, grinding thrum that vibrates in your chest. It comes from the "Scattered Built" elements—massive, brutalist structures that sit on the plateau like rusted beetles.

These are the industrial scars: pumping stations, filtration plants, and vast, turbine-filled halls. They are not cities, but singular, functional monoliths. Huge pipes, some as wide as a house, erupt from the raw rock cliffs, channeling the weeping water into massive, electrified turbines that scream with a high-pitched whine. Massive conveyor belts, scaled for giants, crawl slowly across the landscape, carrying harvested biomass or refined minerals from the open pits to processing silos. The machinery is old, clanking, and relentless. It creates a wall of sound—a hydraulic roar mixed with the grinding of metal on metal—that renders silence impossible.

The fauna is virtually non-existent, driven away by the noise and the industrial extraction of the water table. You might see tiny, metallic beetles or flightless birds near the heat vents of the machines, but for the most part, this is a sterile, mechanical garden.
