The tunnel is eight meters long and just wide enough for one. You enter through a sealed hatch, the kind with a hand-wheel instead of a handle, and immediately the world contracts to this narrow corridor of glazed tile, every surface green and dark and wet.

Water moves here constantly, has always moved here. A channel runs through the center of the floor—a shallow gutter carved into the concrete, no wider than a hand's span, carrying a thin current of warm water that never stops. The water is silted and faintly sweet, carrying the mineral smell of old pipes and something organic underneath, something alive in the warmth. It moves with quiet purpose, the way water does when it has been moving for a very long time and has nowhere else to be.

The sound is enormous. In a corridor this narrow, the gurgle of the channel becomes a roar, and layered over it are the smaller sounds—the drip and trickle of water finding its way down the walls, the faint hum of a pump somewhere beyond the visible length, the wet settling of things shifting. The tiles amplify everything, every drop ricocheting until the corridor seems to breathe with noise, a constant wall of sound that presses against you from every direction.

Moisture collects on every surface. The warm air of the underground space condenses against the tiles, making them slick, making the channel's edges glisten, making the air itself feel thick. In places where the flow is strongest, a pale mist hangs above the water. Your skin feels damp the moment you step inside.

Light comes from fixtures set low in the ceiling at long intervals—small industrial lamps with clouded glass covers, warm yellow, steady, not bright. They illuminate the tunnel in sections, and between them the corridor falls into deeper shade, so that the full length only becomes clear as you move through it. Normal light. Enough to see by, not enough to feel exposed in. The kind of light that has been here long enough to become part of the place.

The tiles cover every surface—walls, floor, ceiling—small squares of glazed ceramic in a deep green that has darkened over the centuries. They were made to be impervious, to channel water without absorbing it, to last. And they have. But time works differently here: the water has found every seam, every edge, and worked its way through. The grout is black with mineral deposits. The tiles at the channel's edge are worn smooth, almost polished, where water has moved over them for longer than memory. In places, whole tiles have slipped or cracked, and in those gaps—moss. Ferns. Something with thin trailing stems and small pale leaves that has threaded itself out from the joints and hangs over the channel's edge, drinking from the air.

Vegetation is present but not abundant. These are not plants growing in soil—they cannot be, there is no soil here, everything is tile and concrete, every surface made. They grow from the cracks, their roots anchored in the grout, drawing sustenance from the water and the slow accumulation of organic matter that has settled in the joints over generations. The ferns are the most vivid thing in the tunnel: dark green, heavy with moisture, their fronds catching the light from below and holding it. When a drop of water falls from a frond into the channel, the sound is small and clear and perfectly ordinary.

Fauna is rare. A water beetle moves through the channel, unhurried. In the deeper shade between fixtures, something skitters across the tiles and is still. The tunnel is not empty but it is sparse, life distributed across the space the way stones are distributed across a flat seabed—present, spaced far apart, each one the only thing in its small territory.

Nothing changes. The channel carries the same amount of water it carried a century ago. The ferns grow and die and grow again in the same patches, their roots holding the same seams, their fronds reaching the same distance over the channel's edge. The pump hums. The tiles hold. This is not maintenance—it is equilibrium. The tunnel has reached the state it will be in forever: the end of its life, the oldest stable state possible, where nothing breaks because there is nothing left to break, where nothing changes because change has already finished its work. An ancient thing, settled into its final form.

The scale is intimate. A few meters across, a few dozen meters long, contained—pocket-sized, you could close your hand around it. The density is empty. Vast space between each presence: between the ferns, between the beetles, between the drips of water from ceiling to floor. The tunnel is mostly air, mostly silence between the sounds, mostly darkness between the fixtures. But the sound fills it. The moisture fills it. The sense of presence—of a world running on its own, with no need for witness—fills it.

It is warm and wet and completely made. Every surface here was designed. Every material was engineered. Nothing is natural—not the tile, not the channel, not the water that runs through it, fed from somewhere deep in the city's infrastructure and moving toward wherever it goes next. The tunnel is a made thing, built to serve a purpose no one remembers, in a system so old that the people who designed it have been gone for longer than most civilizations last. But it still runs. The channel still carries water. The ferns still grow. The system holds.

And in this small sealed world, none of it needs you. The water flows. The tiles wear smooth. The ferns drink from the air. The tunnel breathes, wet and warm and ancient, all of it at once.
