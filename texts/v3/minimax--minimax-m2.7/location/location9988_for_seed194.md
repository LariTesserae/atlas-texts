You are standing in something that was once a room.

The floor is there — you can feel it underfoot, a cold mineral crust, faintly gritty with salt or something like it. But the walls have long since ceased to be walls in any meaningful sense. They are *substance* now. Living tissue in the form of architecture. Ribbed and columnar, like the inside of a gullet, or like the inside of a cathedral where every pillar has been replaced by a single enormous organism that grew through the stone until stone and stem became the same thing. You run your palm along the nearest surface and it yields slightly — not quite give, more like *tolerance*, as though the material has decided to permit your touch.

No light. None. The darkness is not a condition here but a substance, a weight that gathers in the hollows of this pocket-space and presses against your open eyes. But the darkness is not empty. You can see — or your eyes are telling you you can see — a faint, sourceless luminescence bleeding through the tissue of the walls. Not glow, exactly. More like a memory of light, the way a bruise is a memory of impact. It seeps from the interstices of the plant-growth in filaments so dim that the space around you exists in a state of permanent maybe-seeing, where shapes assert themselves and dissolve before certainty can form.

The plants here are not green. You will never know that they are not green. In the darkness, color is a rumor, and the organisms covering every surface have adapted beyond chlorophyll into something that feeds on mineral substrate — on the salt andite of the crust beneath the city, on the dry chemistry of ancient stone. They are white and faintly iridescent and they grow in dense mats so total that the original built environment is now less a structure than a *skeleton* for the ecosystem. A settlement that has become the soil for its own replacement.

And it is a settlement. You can feel the geometry — the right angles, the planned intervals — even through the growth. There were streets here once, rooms, a grid of intention. The builders used stone and early metal, hand-worked, fitted without mortar, the way of people who understood their materials with their fingers rather than their mathematics. Primitive, yes, but *thorough*. The settlement was built to last, and it has outlasted its own people by so long that no memory of them survives in whatever faint cultural substrate this place still holds.

The sound is the most disorienting thing. In darkness and near-void, sound becomes the dominant sense, and this place is *full* of sound in the way a deep cave is full of silence — not because there is noise but because the absence is so absolute that any event becomes loud. A slow creak. A settling groan. A deep, almost subsonic hum that rises from the walls when you are still long enough, as though the living architecture is dreaming, or breathing, or simply *being* under a load that has no urgency because it has endured for so long that urgency has been metabolized into patience. You hear your own breath. You hear your own heartbeat, or think you do. Then something shifts — a truss of biological material adjusting its position by a millimeter — and the sound fills the chamber with a low, resonant protest, the kind of sound that wood makes when it has been holding too much for too long and has forgotten that it was ever allowed to complain.

The air is cool and dry, tasting faintly of mineral dust, of salt, of a chemistry that does not include you. There is no water here. There has been no water here for longer than the city has been dead. The plants persist without it, drawing what they need from the substrate itself, cycling something ancient and alien through their own slow metabolisms. When you exhale, the moisture in your breath is the most water this place has seen in aeons, and you watch — or imagine you watch — the faintest condensation form and vanish on a tendril of the nearest organism.

There is no animal life. There is almost no life at all, really, if you are measuring by the standards of a wet world. But the plant-mass does not measure by those standards. It is total. It fills every surface, every seam, every joint between the old stones. It has sealed the exterior entirely. This pocket-space — this buried chamber or vault or room — is a world now, self-contained and slowly alive, and it has been slowly alive for so long that its dynamism operates on a timescale that would make geological patience seem impatient. The walls are not growing so much as *rearranging*. The ecosystem is not changing so much as *refining*, settling deeper into the only configuration that works, the only way that dry life can persist in darkness without water, without sun, without anything that the wet world would recognize as hospitable.

You are standing in something that was once a room. The walls are no longer walls. The floor is barely floor. Everything has become the same material — built and grown together into a single ancient body — and you are inside it, warm and wet and smelling of animal, and the organism that surrounds you does not object because it has not yet decided whether you are interesting.

The silence — when it comes — is not the absence of sound. It is the sound of everything waiting.
