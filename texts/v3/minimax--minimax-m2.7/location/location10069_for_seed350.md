# The Still-Speaking Waters

The world exhales in thunder. Not wind—something deeper, something structural, as if the skin of the planet itself is vibrating at a frequency that predates atmosphere. It rises from the water, from the saturated earth, from the rusted towers that emerge from the murk like the ribs of some drowned leviathan. You feel it in your teeth before you hear it with your ears. The sound is so total it has texture: pressure and release, pressure and release, a heartbeat scaled to the horizon.

Water covers everything here, but it is not the ocean. It has no salt, no taste of depth. It is warm—always warm, like bathwater, like something living. It moves constantly but without current, without direction. The surface trembles with small conspiracies of motion, and the deeper you look, the more the water seems to *contain* rather than flow. Suspended in it: nutrients, minerals, the dissolved remnants of what was once built and burned and thrown away. This is a drowned world, but not flooded by rain or melting ice. The water came from below. From sources that opened in the earth's crust when the breaking happened, when the seams of reality split and the pressure beneath found its way up.

The land—if you can call it that—exists in fragments. Low flats of dark soil and pale stone, barely risen above the waterline, still sharp-edged with their newness. Erosion here has only begun its patient work. The angles are fresh. Where water meets rock, you can still see the striations of recent emergence, the place where ground pushed up from below in convulsion or eruption. The features have not yet been softened. Canyons have not yet been carved; ridges have not yet been worn to whispers. This is a young geography, still announcing itself, still surprised at its own existence.

On these low islands, vegetation erupts in impossible density. Not forest—something more aggressive, more layered. Canopies collapse into canopies. Vines knot with roots knot with fungi networks that pulse faintly with bioluminescence, casting the murk beneath them in pale green light. The plants here are wrong in their abundance. They grow in spirals that seem almost intentional, their leaves too large, their flowers too complex, their roots descending not into soil but directly into the warm water that permeates everything. They do not photosynthesize—or they do, but differently, drawing energy from some source beyond light. The light here is dim, filtered through a permanent haze, through particulate and vapor that hangs low and yellow-gray over the water. The sun is a suggestion. The sky is a possibility. But the plants are real, so real they seem to vibrate at the edge of vision.

There are no animals. None. No insects in the flowers, no birds in the canopy, no fish in the water. The absence is not peaceful; it is *active*. The world feels like it should be full, should be teeming, but the teeming has been subtracted. What remains is the infrastructure of animal presence without the animals: nests that will never be used, burrows that descend into empty darkness, webs stretched between stalks with nothing caught in them, trembling in a wind that carries no wings. The plants have continued without competition. They have spread into every niche, every gap, every atom of space not already occupied by plant. And they have continued without grazers, without pruners, without the checks and balances that animal life provides. They grow into shapes that suggest architecture, that suggest intention, that suggest they are reaching for something—or trying to hold something back.

The built structures are scattered like evidence. A factory half-submerged on a rise of land that may once have been a hill, its smokestacks still reaching for a sky they can no longer pollute. The machinery inside has stopped but not decayed; it has been *replaced* by something, overwritten by crystalline growths that look organic but are too regular, too geometric, too exactly repeated. A water tower, rusted to the color of dried blood, still standing because nothing has pushed against it, because nothing walks the earth anymore. A road, barely visible beneath the water, its lines worn but present, leading to a town that is mostly foundation now, mostly suggestion, the buildings themselves long since subsumed by the vine-growth and the slow rise of water. These are remnants, not ruins. They have not yet become history. They are still close enough to touch, to examine, to misunderstand.

And then there is the weirdness. You will notice it if you look at the horizon for too long. The horizon does not stay where it is. It breathes. It expands and contracts in slow, tidal rhythms that have nothing to do with the water. The water itself sometimes *stops*—entire stretches of it freezing in place, becoming mirror-smooth and reflective, while the rest continues its restless motion. The color of things is not reliable. A tree that is green when you look at it directly becomes red when you look from the corner of your eye, and when you turn to examine it directly again, it is neither, it is a color you have no name for, a color that is *between*. The plants emit a sound—not the thunder, but a whisper, a almost-sound, a pressure at the edge of hearing that might be language if language could be pressed into frequencies that do not exist in air. And sometimes, when the light shifts and the haze thins for a moment, you see things in the distance that are not there. Structures that are not structures. Movements that are not movements. The world is not broken; it is *still becoming*, still negotiating the terms of its own existence, and it has not finished deciding what rules to follow.

The factories were built before the breaking. The industry was human, or mostly human—smokestacks and turbines and the scaled production of things that people needed or wanted or feared. But something happened in the factories, or beneath them, or in the spaces between the factories where the pipes ran and the waste pooled and the workers never went. Something opened. Or something woke up. Or something that was always there finally paid attention. The water came then. The plants came then. The silence of animals, and the screaming of whatever is screaming beneath the sound you cannot quite hear.

The sound is everything. It is the thing you cannot escape. It does not come from any direction; it comes from *depth*, from below, from the place where the water originates and the ground has not yet finished rising. It is not mechanical. It is not organic. It is the sound of a world still being born, still fighting its way out of whatever it was before, and it will not be quiet until the becoming is finished—if it ever is.
