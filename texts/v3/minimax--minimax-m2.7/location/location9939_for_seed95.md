# The Plateau of Quiet Life

The plateau is ancient beyond reckoning. Once it might have been mountains, but the mountains are gone now, worn down by water and time into broad, undulating tableland that rolls to every horizon. What remains is the skeleton of stone, mostly buried under shallow soil, exposed here and there in low ridges of dark rock that feel more geological fault than natural formation. The plateau has settled into itself. It will not change much in the next several million years. It has reached its final shape, and the shape is patience.

The ground is iron-red in the dry seasons, soft ochre when the moisture comes. Patches of thin soil hold sparse grass—tussocks that grow in loose clusters, leaving bare earth between, not quite dead but never lush. Occasional thorn-scrub breaks the monotony, low and wind-twisted, their leaves small and waxy against moisture loss. These are not hardy survivors; they are simply what remains when everything more demanding has been worn away. The plateau does not permit excess.

Water comes as fog more often than rain, rolling in from the lower lands in pale curtains that wet the grass and pool in shallow depressions. The depressions are everywhere—shallow bowls worn into the ancient rock, holding water for hours or days after moisture arrives before it soaks away. Some are perfectly circular. Most are not. You learn not to look too closely at their edges.

The plateau is warm year-round, though the nights bring a coolness that lingers into mid-morning. By midday the air is thick and still. The warmth breeds life in quantities that seem impossible given the sparseness of the vegetation. Creatures of every scale have claimed this plateau, and their presence is felt in layers—a dense ecology that has had time to fill every available niche, every gap between tussock and stone, every hollow where water gathers.

Insects move through the grass in waves. Reptiles sun themselves on dark rocks, their bodies flattened by generations of slow selection into shapes that maximize surface area for heat. Birds pass overhead in enormous murmurations, their coordinated movements drawing patterns across the sky that repeat, shift, repeat again. Herds of hoofed animals—antelopes, perhaps, or creatures that occupy the same ecological space without being quite antelopes—move across the plateau in slow, patient streams, their bodies dark against the red earth.

And there are other things. The plateau is strange, and the strangeness lives in the fauna. Creatures that move in patterns slightly wrong—too synchronized, too deliberate. Shapes that are almost familiar but whose proportions shift when you look away. Herds that seem to flow around invisible obstacles, that pulse with a rhythm that has nothing to do with wind or weather. They do not speak or call or cry out; the plateau is mostly quiet, broken only by the rustle of grass in wind and the soft, pad-pad-pad of hooves or feet in the distance.

This is not a silent place. It is a quiet one. The sound that dominates is the low hum of life at scale, of movement and respiration and hunger and death happening everywhere at once but slowly, so slowly, in the patient rhythms of a landscape that has finished becoming what it is.

A few structures dot the plateau—stone walls half-buried in soil, the remains of small buildings that have been returning to earth for longer than recorded time. They are not ruins in the dramatic sense. They are faint marks, artifacts that have become part of the landscape. Whoever built them is long gone. Their purpose is unguessable. They do not dominate the view; they blend into it, their lines softened by millennia of wind and rain and the slow creep of roots.

The plateau does not feel empty. It feels full—overfull, saturated with presence, with the weight of everything that lives and dies and lives again in its ancient soil. But it does not feel alive in the way of a forest or a jungle. It feels like a held breath, like a place that has settled into its final form and will wait, slowly, through epochs, until even its strange fauna has faded and the stone has worn down to sand and the sand has blown away and there is nothing left but the idea of a place that once was high.
