The courtyard—if courtyard is even the right word for it; the hollow feels more accurate—occupies a space no larger than a cupped pair of hands. It might be the gap between three collapsed walls, or a natural pocket in stone that settlement builders centuries ago recognized and enclosed with fitted blocks. The blocks are still there, beneath. You can see them where the moss peels back, where roots have levered great slabs aside and left the edges sharp, the joints fresh-split. Young erosion. Recent collapse.

Water here is not present as rain or stream but as persistence: moisture that beads on every surface and never quite dries, that condenses in the perpetual cool and settles into the porous stone, feeding the slow digestion of the structure. The walls weep. The floor is damp to the touch even through the living carpet. Somewhere beneath the roots there is surely still water pooled, or running, but it is hidden; the surface has been entirely overtaken.

Everything grows. Not just in the sense of plant presence but in the sense of *substrate*: the courtyard is substrate. The three walls, whatever remained of the original construction, have become frameworks for vertical forests. Each block is an opportunity. Each crack, a channel. What was once a window slit has become a green well, choked with fronds that reach up toward the faintest suggestion of light and then stop, curled, patient, waiting. The moss is total—a gray-green velvet over every surface that isn't root. The roots themselves are architectural: thick ropes of pale growth that have woven into lattices, that have cracked and crept and now hold the hollow's geometry in a botanical grip. Where a corner has fallen inward, the plants have flowed into the gap and built new terraces. The whole space feels like the inside of a lung, or a throat, or the kind of underwater cave where light doesn't reach but life still gathers.

It is cold here. Not freezing—water doesn't quite freeze—but cold enough that breath mists, cold enough that the air has weight, cold enough that condensation gathers on the undersides of fronds and drips in irregular rhythms. The cold is the kind that settles in stone, that stays. It might be elevation, or it might be the courtyard's relationship to larger systems: a thermal pocket, a cold sink, a place where warmth simply doesn't accumulate.

The built presence is here but subsumed. Under the moss, under the roots, you can still read the walls' intentions: the fitted blocks, the deliberate coursing, the signs of measurement and tool. This was constructed. Organized. This hollow was *made*—someone laid those stones with purpose, with hands, with early iron tools or bone-handled ones. The settlement this belonged to had reached some density, some organization, enough to coordinate labor. Primitive, yes, but deliberate. And now the construction has become a skeleton, a base layer in a new kind of architecture that the plants are building and the animals are using.

The animals are the strangest part.

They are not visible, not exactly. In the darkness—with that near-total darkness, light only a suggestion, a memory of brightness from some crack or gap far above—you sense them more than see them. The movement. The weight of presence. Something small and many-legged crosses the back of your hand: bristly, quick, gone before you can focus. Something in the roots shifts. In the upper fronds, a faint stirring: the place where birds or something like birds have nested, a woven mass of stems and droppings and bodies. The sound parameter is mostly quiet, but not silent: a dry rustle, a soft click, the faint drip of condensation, and beneath it all a patient, breathing hum that might be metabolism, might be the sound of many small things alive and moving and not yet ready to be still.

Overwhelming fauna at this scale means the courtyard is *full* of them. They are the dominant voice. They have adapted to the darkness, to the damp, to the cold. They are perhaps eyeless, or nearly so. They are perhaps not quite any species you could name. The strange parameter sits here: this is a strange ecology. The life here has been left alone long enough to become its own thing, to develop its own patterns, to fill every niche the courtyard offers with something that fits and lives and moves. The fauna doesn't dominate through size but through *saturation*—every surface with something on it, something moving, something breeding.

And yet the density is sparse, or balanced with emptiness. The courtyard feels intimate, *spacious* in its smallness. The plants fill, but not without gaps. The animals move through, but the eye can rest. There's air between the presences. Not crowded, not jammed—a micro-jungle that still breathes, that has not collapsed into its own density.

The dynamic parameter pulls everything into motion: this is not a static place. Water moves. Plants grow visibly, slowly, nudging stones. Animals go about their cycles. Things die here, too, and decay feeds the growth that follows. The dark is active with small life. The cold is a process: condensing, evaporating, cycling. The walls are being slowly, slowly unmade by root and moisture and time, and the plants are building something new in their place, and the animals live in that building, and the whole system is in motion toward something that isn't equilibrium but process.

You could stand here. You could hold this courtyard in your mind. It would fit.

It is already inside something larger, or something larger is inside it.

You breathe the damp air. Something flickers at the edge of your vision and is gone.

The walls, the moss, the cold, the motion: all of it is waiting. All of it is alive.
