# The Pocket at the Throat of the Mountain

You are inside something. The scale insists on this — you cannot see a horizon, cannot place yourself in a vista. The walls are close, the ceiling close above, and every surface holds the same density of presence as the one before it. The world here is a single chamber carved into the throat of a cliff so high that altitude should bring cold, but the cold never came. The heat rises from below, from vents in the mineral floor that exhale slow sulfurous warmth, and the pocket has been sealed — by collapse, by geology, by some process that found this space and closed it away — long enough for life to come and find its own logic.

The walls are black basalt, but the darkness is not flat. The stone is young — the erosion parameter demands this — and it shows. Surfaces that should have weathered in ten thousand years instead show the textures of recent fracture: sharp ridges, angular ledges, faces where rock cleaved along planes that have not yet softened. Water moves through this stone like blood through lung tissue. It seeps from every joint, condenses on every face, beads on every ledge. The moisture is constant, a slow weeping that makes the air close and mineral-scented, heavy with wet rock and the faint copper of thermal springs. Pools gather in pockets and dips, shallow and steaming faintly, edged with mineral deposits that crust the basalt in pale and fragile rings.

There is no plant life. Not in any form you would recognize. The green of chlorophyll is absent, and the world should feel dead for that absence — but the walls do not feel dead. They are covered. They are thick with movement. Filament-mats in pale yellow and rust color cling to the basalt, thin membranes that breathe and shift in the warmth, that catch moisture and hold it, that grow and retract with the cycles of the day in miniature. Between them, things move — not insects, not worms, but bodies that do not fit the categories you know. They have mass and intention. They hunt. They flee. They reproduce in the wet seams of the stone, their eggs gelatinous and clinging, their larvae blind and quick. The fauna parameter is overwhelming, and you understand it now: the space is not empty of life. It is *full* of it, dense with organisms that have adapted to this pocket and made it theirs. They move in patterns you cannot parse. They cluster in the hot zones near the vents, and disperse when the temperature rises too high, and their bodies leave trails that heal slowly in the moisture, faint phosphorescent traces that fade as the water reclaims the stone.

The light is present but wrong. It does not come from above. It comes from below, from the vents — a faint geothermal glow, amber and rose, that makes the steam look colored and the mineral pools look like diluted blood. The shadows fall strangely. The light does not behave as light should in a sealed space; it bends, or seems to, near the walls, near the densest concentrations of life, as if the organisms themselves are altering its passage. You do not understand how this works. The weirdness parameter would have it no other way.

And the weirdness is present. It is not dramatic — not a wound in reality, not a screaming void — but it is *there*, in the wrongness of the air, in the flicker at the edge of your vision, in the sense that the chamber is larger than its walls suggest, that the organisms you see are not all occupying the same moment, that time in this pocket runs in eddies and currents rather than a single line. You look away and look back and something has shifted. A cluster of the filament-mats has moved to a different wall. An organism that was near the ceiling is now pressed against the floor. You cannot say how long it took. You cannot say whether it took any time at all.

The sound is quiet. You strain for it, and find it: the faint hush of water moving through stone, the soft tick of condensation dropping from the ceiling, the almost-silent scrape of something small and armored shifting its position in a crack. Life here is not loud. The organisms move slowly or quickly in pulses, and between the pulses there is only the wet breathing of the chamber, the slow chemistry that sustains them all. No calls. No songs. The life is too dense and too alien for the communication patterns you know. They leave chemical traces instead. They touch antennae and part. They are present to each other in ways that do not require sound.

It is hot, still, even here in the pocket. The heat should not persist at this altitude, but the geology of the chamber has made its own weather, its own thermodynamics, a system that runs on what lies below. The walls are warm to the touch. The pools are warm. The air is warm and wet and close, and you understand that you are breathing the same air as thousands of small lives, that your exhalation feeds their chemistry, that you are part of a system that does not need you to continue but will absorb you if you stop moving.

You do not know how long you have been here. You do not know if *here* is a place you will leave. The pocket has its own rules, its own time, and you are inside them now, a presence in a landscape that does not recognize your presence as special, that holds you as it holds the water and the heat and the slowly moving mats of alien life — with the same indifference, the same density, the same quiet ongoing of processes that do not require your understanding to continue.
