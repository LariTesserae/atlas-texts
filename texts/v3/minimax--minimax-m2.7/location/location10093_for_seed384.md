# The Drowned Works

The water is black and still, though not still at all—it moves in slow circles, in slow spirals, in the slow breath of machinery that has not been tended in some time but continues anyway. The cold does not come from the water itself but from the air above it, a membrane of frozen breath that hovers over the flooded district, and the water accepts this cold, accepts it the way concrete accepts rain, without resistance, without change.

What was once a low-lying industrial quarter now sits at the floor of itself. The water reaches about chest-height in the main channels, lapping against the lower windows of what were once apartment blocks, what were once factories, what were once a market that someone built over a century ago with the confidence that the river would stay where rivers stay. The river did not stay. The river rose or the land sank or both in the patient mathematics of things that do not care what we thought, and now the water is here and has been here and will be here, and the machinery that was built to pump it away has become instead the only sound in the world: a distant thrum, a pulse, the wet grinding of something that does not know it is working but works.

Buildings rise out of the water like the fingers of a drowned hand. Their walls are dark concrete or black brick or rusted metal sheeting, and on their surfaces the water has left its mark—not the dramatic erosion of flood gates torn open but the slow, patient work of water that was always going to be here, that was given time to learn its patterns. Moss grows in the places where water has pooled and frozen and thawed, in the cracks, in the corners, in the sheltered hollows where light might touch if there were more light. But there is not more light. The sky above is a gray that could be dawn or dusk or noon on a winter that never ends, and it presses down, and the water below mirrors it, and the space between is the space where you stand, waist-deep, the cold a presence that has weight, that presses back.

Vegetation is rare but not absent. The willows that grew along the canal banks have not died so much as grown differently—their roots in water, their branches reaching toward the low light, and something strange about their bark, something that glistens wetly when there is no rain. In the shallower areas near the old market stalls, where the water only reaches knee-height, there are patches of something that might once have been grass, bladed and pale, growing in a direction that ignores the sky. Fungal growths climb the lower walls, shelf-like, gray and pale, and they do not seem to have the relation to light that fungal things usually have—they grow in the darkness, and they thrive there, and they are not alone.

Fauna is almost absent. Almost. The silence is not the silence of emptiness but the silence of restraint, of listening. Something moves at the edge of perception—not visible, not quite—but present, breathing in the cold water, feeding on the things that feed on the things that feed on whatever the water provides. You feel them before you see them, if you see them: the slight displacement, the ripple that comes from no wave, the wrongness in the water's reflection. Once, near the old pumping station, you thought you saw something pale and slow, the length of an arm, moving beneath the surface between two buildings, and it did not look at you because it did not have eyes in the way you understand eyes, and it was gone before you could be sure you had seen anything at all.

The built structures are dense—they crowd each other, this district was built for people and there were many people and many buildings to hold them, and now the buildings hold the water instead, or the water holds the buildings, and neither will give way. You can see three levels still: the flooded ground floor where everything important happened, the waterline and just above where the marks remain, and above that the sealed doors and dark windows where no one has been and nothing has happened for a long time. The architecture is industrial, scaled, the work of an age that built for the many—the tenement blocks with their rows of windows, the factory floors with their high ceilings, the canal infrastructure with its locks and its channels and its bridges that have not held traffic in longer than memory. And everywhere, the signs of that age's technology: pipes that emerge from walls and descend into water, vents that still breathe, the platforms of the old elevated railway, rusting in the wet air, holding nothing but the sky.

Light is dim because light cannot find a way in. The sky is gray and low and it does not want to touch the water, and the water does not want to reflect it, and the buildings block what little might have reached, and so the district exists in a permanent dusk, a not-quite-dark that makes shapes harder to read than darkness would. You see better than you would in complete darkness, but you see wrong. Faces, if there were faces. Surfaces that should be smooth and are not. The wrongness accumulates.

Sound is the machinery and the water and the silence between. The pumps, if that is what they are, thrum from somewhere below, from somewhere in the infrastructure, from the parts of this place that the water has not reached because the water cannot reach everything. The water itself makes small sounds: the lap against concrete, the shift of something beneath, the drip from pipes that have not been drained. And then the silence, which is not silence but the space where sounds should be and are not, the held breath of a place that is waiting for something to break it.

What is strange is not the water, though the water is strange. What is strange is the balance. This place should be dead, should be abandoned, should be a ruin, and in some sense it is. But the pumps still run. The fungal growths still grow. The pale things still move. The water, which should have won, which is winning, has not finished winning, and there is something in that unfinished quality, in that not-yet-ness, that makes the strangeness you feel when you stand here, waist-deep in the cold black water, waiting for something you cannot name, watching the ripples that come from nothing you can see.

You will not stay long. You came for a reason, or you were brought, or you followed something, and now you are here, and the water is cold, and the machinery hums, and the dark windows above you watch without eyes, and you understand that this place is not haunted but it is not empty either, that it is occupied by the patient machinery of something that has not finished, that is still happening, that is waiting for the water to rise just a little higher, for the light to dim just a little more, for the silence to become complete.
