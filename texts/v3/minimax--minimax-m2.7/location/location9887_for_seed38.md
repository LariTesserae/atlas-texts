# The Weeping Plateau

In the oldest place in the world, the land has forgotten what it once was.

The plateau rises eight thousand feet above the coastal shallows, but you would not call it a mountain. Mountains are events, interruptions—here, the surface has been worn so long and so thoroughly that the bones of the continent show through as gentle ridges, as smooth domes of ancient stone half-consumed by the green that now covers everything. This is a land at the end of erosion, a landscape that has settled into its final posture and then stayed there for longer than many planets take to form. The slopes are patient. The valleys are shallow. Everything curves.

And it rains, or rather, it weeps. The distinction matters here.

Water does not fall so much as it emerges. The atmosphere is so saturated with warmth and moisture drawn up from the lowland seas that every surface weeps. Rock weeps. Wood weeps. The vast mats of root and vine and leaf weep from their margins, trailing slow beads of condensation that merge into rivulets and disappear into the living ground. The air has weight. You taste it with every breath—not rain, not fog, but a persistent dampness that clings to the tongue and settles into the lungs like something almost alive.

The vegetation has won.

It won so long ago that the word "vegetation" feels insufficient. What covers this plateau is less a forest than a totality—a living membrane stretched across every surface, descending into every crack, climbing over every obstruction. The original stone is still there, somewhere, beneath twenty or thirty feet of root-mass in some places, but it has become substrate rather than substance. The ground is not ground; it is a conversation between organisms so intertwined that no botanist could separate them. Root meets root meets fungal thread meets the pale body of some vast decomposing trunk, all of it fused into a single mass that breathes and pulses and grows with the patience of geology.

There are no clearings. There are densities.

The canopy—though the word fails, for there are canopies above canopies, a stratification of growth accumulated over epochs—rises to two hundred feet and more, layered so thoroughly that the light below is green-gold and thin. Sunlight reaches the lower levels only as theory, only as memory. What grows here has adapted to near-darkness, or has abandoned light entirely in favor of the slow chemistry of decomposition and symbiosis. Bioluminescent fungi mark the paths through the understory—dim blue-green pulses that flicker when disturbed, that seem almost to be thinking. The plateau has its own gentle pulses of life-light, its own rhythms that have nothing to do with day and night.

In the highest reaches, where the plateau's ancient surface approaches the clouds, the weeping is most constant. These weeping cliffs are the plateau's most striking feature—long faces of stone from which water seeps in an endless slow exudation, feeding rivers that are less rivers than capillary systems, thin threads of flow that wander through the root-mats and vanish into the mass. The water here does not carve; the vegetation prevents it. The water is absorbed as quickly as it emerges, fed into the vast circulatory system of the living land. The plateau drinks its own rain.

Fauna moves through this density in slow procession. You hear them before you see them—hooves or pads or the dry whisper of scaled bodies moving through the understory. The creatures of the Weeping Plateau have evolved for low-light vision, for dense cover, for a world where every surface offers purchase and every direction offers obstruction. They are patient creatures. They have to be. The ecosystem operates on timescales that render individual lives almost meaningless—a particular tree may live three thousand years; a particular fungus may span a square mile and never be born or die in any conventional sense. The fauna moves through this permanence like guests through a museum, brief presences in a space built for durations they cannot comprehend.

The quiet is the loudest thing about the plateau.

Not silence—the canopy-dwellers call, and the waters speak, and sometimes a sound rises from the deep vegetable mass that might be voice or might be wind or might be the slow creak of a root finding its way through stone. But the sounds are sparse and far between, separated by long silences that feel intentional. The plateau does not announce itself. It endures.

Here and there, half-consumed by the green, you find evidence of builders.

Not technology—the tech-level is zero, has always been zero—but something else. Walls of fitted stone, their purpose forgotten, their surfaces colonized so thoroughly that they seem to grow from the hillside rather than stand against it. A flight of steps ascending to a platform now indistinguishable from the living ground. A circle of posts that might have been a gathering place, or might have been a boundary marker, or might have been nothing at all. The builders left no record. The plateau has absorbed their works the way it absorbs water, the way it absorbs light, making them part of the slow accumulation of its being. You could spend a lifetime searching for all the traces and find only hints—a corner of worked stone where the moss grows slightly differently, a curve in the land that feels too deliberate to be natural.

The plateau does not resist meaning. It simply renders it irrelevant.

At night—if night is even the right word for the dimming that passes for darkness here—the bioluminescence strengthens. The fungi pulse. The weeping cliffs continue their slow exudation into the warm air. The fauna moves through its ancient patterns, hunting and nesting and dying and being absorbed back into the mass that will feed the next growth, the next slow accumulation of life upon life upon life.

This is the Weeping Plateau. It was here before anything you would recognize as ecosystem, and it will be here long after. The rain falls upward sometimes, in strange updrafts that carry mist into the upper atmosphere. The roots descend toward the molten core of the continent, seeking warmth, seeking purchase, seeking anything that will let them grow a little further, a little longer, into the next epoch.

The land has forgotten what it once was.

Perhaps it never knew. Perhaps this is what land feels like, when you give it enough time—enough warmth, enough water, enough sheer patient duration—and let it become what it was always going to become.

It becomes everything.
