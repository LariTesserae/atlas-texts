# The Iron Plateau

The plateau stretches to every horizon, a vast table of oxidized stone and compacted mineral that hums with faint geothermal warmth. No water has touched this place in any form that living things would recognize—no rivers, no rain, no dew. The air is dry as old bone, and carries a faint acrid sweetness that is not quite chemical, not quite organic, something in between that the breath learns not to notice. It is warm here at the peaks, though the thinness of the atmosphere at this elevation would make the cold unbearable on any world with thicker air. The warmth rises from deep places; from vents and fissures and the slow pressed heat of deep geological time. The plateau is ancient, settled into its forms across epochs, the wind and the slow creep of thermal stress having rounded every edge, polished every surface into smooth undulations like frozen waves or the skin of some resting creature.

The scale of it is difficult to hold. From the edge of the great shelf, the land drops away into canyon systems that carve down toward depths unseen, a geography of parallel wounds that could swallow mountain ranges from older worlds. The settlements cling to the rims and the ridgelines—the only places flat enough for construction—clusters of squared architecture carved directly into the rock face: habitation blocks, processing facilities, the angular geometries of industry rising in tiers like fossilized beehives. Between them, pipelines and cable-trays follow the contours, and distant shapes move along them with the slow deliberation of things that do not hurry.

There is no vegetation here, but there is life. Abundant life, if you know how to look. The fauna of the iron plateau does not graze or hunt; it *weathers*. It emerges from the rock in forms that are half-mineral, half-something else—crystalline growths that drift in the thermal currents, colonial structures that unfold along fault lines in patterns too regular for geology, too irregular for machines. They move, slowly, across the surface of the plateau in drifts and colonies, their presence announced by the subtle hum that runs through the stone on still mornings, a vibration that is felt more than heard. Where they gather, the rock takes on a darker patina, as if stained by centuries of shadow. They do not eat. They do not drink. They consume heat and rarefaction and the faint electrical gradients that run through the plateau's deeper layers, and they leave behind a residue that the settlements' smelters find useful.

The fauna is abundant but sparse—scattered in clusters across the vastness, communities separated by distances that a slower traveler might cross in days. They have their own rhythms, their own territories, and the settlements have learned to leave certain formations unmolested, certain ridgelines uncrossed, as one might respect the borders of a neighboring nation whose language one does not speak. In the deep canyons below the plateau's edge, where the temperature climbs from geothermal pressure and the air thickens enough to taste, the fauna grows denser, stranger—forms that move in clouds and spirals, patterns that hold and disperse and reform in ways that seem almost purposeful.

The industrial hum is everywhere if you listen for it—the low throb of generators, the hiss of processes that do not require water because they require nothing from the hydration paradigm at all. Pyrometallurgical chambers. Electrostatic separators. The faint high tone of systems that have no human-equivalent name. But the plateau's own sounds are quieter. Wind moves through the canyon systems with a voice like distant water, a ghost-sound that the atmosphere carries from places that do not exist on this world. And beneath that, the deep hum, the slow pulse of the planet itself, patient as tectonics, patient as erosion, patient as the slow war of heat and stone that shaped this place before the first settlement carved its first foundation and will continue long after the last light is extinguished.

It is not silent here. But the silences are long. The sounds that do exist are unhurried, ancient in their cadences, as if the plateau itself is a creature that breathes once per century and has no need to speak.
