# The Breathing Terraces

You stand at the edge of something too large to see whole, and your eyes keep sliding off it.

The terraces fall away from you in vast, curving tiers — enormous steps carved into the high plateau's flank, each one a dozen meters high, each one stretching left and right until they vanish into atmospheric haze. They were made. You know this the way you know the weight of old wood, the way your hands understand stone: these walls, these channels, these precisely-angled slopes were placed here by something that measured and planned and intended. The cut stone fits together with the patience of ten thousand hands, and the joints have grown soft with age, softened further by the moss that has claimed every surface. Not claimed — *become*. The moss is not separate from the stone now. It is the skin the stone has grown.

Water moves here constantly, invisibly. It seeps upward through the ancient masonry, condensation forming in the cracks and crevices, the terraces weeping slow moisture down their long faces. The air is thick with it — not rain, not fog, but something between, a permanent dampness that makes your skin slick and your breath taste green. Rain must have once been channeled here deliberately, you realize. The builders understood water's hunger, its need to descend, and they gave it channels cut with the precision of ritual. Now those channels are wet all the time. Water is always moving through this place, feeding the thick living mass that fills every tier, every hollow, every upright surface.

And the vegetation — it has become the architecture. What once were fields or growing-beds are now platforms of dense, intertwined root-matter, soil and plant and decay inseparable. The walls that were laid in straight lines now bow slightly, softened by the pressure of growth. The carved channels that once held water's flow are now themselves growing, their stone lips extended by centuries of root-hair, and the water moves through living tubes now, water wrapped in membrane, water inside something alive. The plants are so total that you have to look for the built beneath them, have to trace the logic of the terraces' spacing to understand what was made versus what grew.

The creatures are here in abundance but they are quiet. They move through the tiers with a patience that feels ancient, creatures built for wet dark places, for the permanent twilight that filters through the canopy-mass overhead. The canopy — if it can be called that — is not organized like a forest. There is no ground beneath it and no sky above it. There is only the living mass, a kilometer-thick mat of growth that spans the terraces from edge to edge, filtering the light into greenish dimness, the quality of a room whose windows face a garden. Sunlight comes through as rumor, as memory.

Your voice makes almost no sound here. It disappears into the wet air, absorbed by the massive living surfaces. You could shout and it would last only a moment before the terraces swallow it. There is a deep quiet beneath the small sounds — the drip of water from moss-heavy stone, the soft creak of a root adjusting its grip, the distant movement of something large moving through a tier you cannot see. The quiet is the kind that makes you hear your own pulse.

The terraces descend and descend, tier after tier, and you cannot see the bottom. The scale is wrong for what your mind expects. It should be possible to perceive the whole thing from here, but it keeps slipping away from you, the bottom lost in haze, the edges lost in growth, the top lost in the slow fade of altitude-thinning air. It is large enough to have weather of its own. You can feel it: the way moisture gathers differently in different tiers, the way the air moves in slow columns upward from the warm living mass, the way a rain might fall on the upper terraces while the lower ones stay dry, this place large enough for its own systems.

What the builders wanted this for, you cannot imagine. It is too large for crops, too large for habitation, too large for any practical purpose you understand. Perhaps they were building something that grew toward its purpose slowly, something that needed centuries to fill in, that needed a place this vast and this saturated to become what it is now. Perhaps the terraces were always meant to be colonized, were always the scaffolding for a living world, and the making and the growing were one project from the beginning.

You climb down one tier and then another, and the moss reaches for you as you descend, the soft growth pressing against your legs, and the stone beneath it is warm even in the shade, warm the way living things are warm. The water drips from above into your hair, and each drop feels like a small, quiet language. You descend further and the scale keeps revealing itself — another tier below, and another, and the whole thing breathes around you, slow and wet and immense, built by hands that knew they would not live to see it finished, built by hands that wanted it anyway, built and grown and now breathing in the high thin air where the light is dim and the water weeps and the quiet is total.
