The basin exists like a wound in the high ground—raised enough to catch faint light at the rim, but dropping quickly into shadow. Heat rises from below, warping the air so that the far walls seem to breathe, though nothing moves. The smell is mineral and old: iron, sulfur, something sharper that has no name. It coats the tongue.

Every surface is covered. The floor, the walls, the low ceiling that arches overhead—all dense with life that is not quite life. The organisms are soft and translucent where they press against each other, some pale as bone, others deep amber or the color of rust. They grow in stacked layers, each form depending on the last, so that removing any single presence would collapse a dozen others. They are the size of hands, of teeth, of closed fists. They fit together with the precision of masonry. In the wetter seams of stone, they bloat and overlap, filling every gap until the rock beneath is lost entirely. Where it is drier, they thin to films, to nets of tissue barely holding form.

The floor is not stone. It is salt—or something the locals call salt, though it tastes of nothing. It has been walked, or crawled, or eroded by movement so long that the surface is glass-smooth in places, dark with oils that the things above leave behind. Walking here is difficult, not because of elevation but because the footing shifts: the organisms yield slightly underfoot, a slow compression, before slowly expanding back to fill the space.

Small structures rise from the carpet. They are not built but grown, or deposited, or excreted—the distinction is not clear. They look like coral, or like frozen froth, or like something that was once soft and is now calcified. They are the color of burnt sugar and smell faintly of copper. They are scattered, not placed. The largest reaches waist-height and curves inward, hollow. Inside, the walls are lined with the densest cluster of organisms, and the heat here is strongest. You do not know if this is shelter or trap.

The fauna does not move. Or rather, it moves so slowly that stillness is the better word. Over hours, the amber forms shift slightly, pressing into new configurations. The pale ones near the ceiling swell and contract in a rhythm that might be respiration. There is no sound except the faint creak of the crust under shifting weight, and the hiss of heat from below, and sometimes—a low, subsonic thrum that seems to come from everywhere at once. You feel it in the chest rather than hear it.

The darkness is not complete. Near the ceiling, where the organisms thin to transparent layers, a faint glow pulses: amber, amber, amber, then a pause, then amber again. It casts no light. The illumination is internal, self-referential, visible only to eyes that have adjusted to the dark—and to whatever senses the organisms themselves are using to orient and grow. The heat, the darkness, the density—this is not a place that was meant to be seen. It is meant to be endured, or inhabited, by things that do not distinguish between the two.

There are no predators here because there is nothing outside. The ecosystem is closed, circular, complete in itself. The organisms eat heat. They grow toward each other. When one dies, or is crushed, or simply reaches the limits of its form, it becomes substrate for the next. The floor is littered with the remnants of old growth: desiccated husks, mineral traces, patterns in the salt that might be body-shapes pressed into the ground. But even these are not empty for long. New forms emerge in the hollows, smaller, faster, filling every gap before it can cool.

The air tastes different the longer you stay. Sharper. The heat seems to penetrate rather than touch, as if the body's response to temperature has been overridden. The organisms do not acknowledge you. They do not flee, do not approach, do not orient. They simply continue their slow pulse in the dark, and the dark continues around them, and the heat continues to rise from below, and nothing here knows that you are watching—or, if it does, it has no capacity left to care.
