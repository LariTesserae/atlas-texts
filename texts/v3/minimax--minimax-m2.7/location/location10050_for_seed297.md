The basin has no name because no one who has seen it remains in any state to speak one.

It stretches across the lowland in a shallow bowl, ringed by distant ridges that seem farther than they are. The floor is smooth black glass—volcanic, but not cooled from any eruption anyone would recognize. The rock fractured in patterns too perfect to be natural, long hexagonal plates fitting together across miles like a frozen skin, and the edges are sharp. Young. Unworn. The basin was *made*, or unmade, or simply decided to be, and that decision happened recently enough that nothing has yet had time to smooth its angles down.

Between the plates, plants grow.

This is the impossible part, and it cannot be said too simply: there is no water here. The air is warm—pleasantly, evenly warm, with no variation between what passes for morning and what passes for night—and it smells of ozone and metal and something faintly sweet, like burnt sugar, that might be the plants themselves. The atmosphere is thick with suspended particulate: motes of mineral dust, chemical compounds that drift and never settle. Light moves strangely through it, diffusing into a perpetual soft brightness that seems to come from everywhere at once.

The plants do not photosynthesize. They do something else, something that current physics would describe as "impossible" and that here simply *is*. They drink light. They metabolize the ambient radiation directly, feeding on frequencies the sun produces and that drift in from sources too distant to name. Or they breathe the air itself, pulling compounds from the dense atmosphere, exhaling something that glitters faintly when it catches the light. Their chemistry is not carbon. It may not even be chemistry as you understand it. The cells—if they are cells—are structured around lattices of grown crystal, growing not from the ground but *into* it, anchoring themselves through the glass by force of simple existence.

They are dense. That is the word that fits best, though it does not carry the weight of the experience: the basin floor is packed with them, stalk and frond and canopy and vine, rising to varying heights, overlapping, crowding, their geometries interlocked like the bones of a vast ribcage. They are not trees. They are not coral. They are organisms designed—*engineered*—for this world and this world alone, their forms fractal and recursive, spiraling inward toward centers that seem to pull the eye. Their surfaces shift between matte and mirror depending on the angle, and their colors—silver and deep violet and a red so dark it is nearly black—are not quite accurate to their actual wavelengths. They are *dense*, and they fill the space, and they are quiet. They do not rustle. They do not creak. The only sound is a faint hum that might be the ground vibrating, or might be them thinking, or might be nothing at all.

And the space is wrong.

This is the reality breakdown, and it is everywhere. Walk toward the ridge and you will arrive somewhere else. Stand at the basin's edge and count the steps to the nearest plant—a spiral thing, two stories tall, its branches crystalline and iridescent—and you will find that the number changes depending on how you count. Stand at the edge of its shadow and you will discover that you are not in shadow. The light falls one way; the illumination falls another. Two geometries occupy the same ground, superimposed, and both are true.

This is not magic. It is engineering. Something built this place—or grew it, or dreamed it, or calculated it into existence—using principles that our physics has not yet found names for. The tech level is *beyond* modern: not a matter of better transistors or fusion reactors, but of folded dimensions, of causality as a design parameter, of space itself as something to be arranged. The plants are the tech. The glass is the tech. The wrongness is the architecture.

It is stable, though. That is what makes it bearable, and what makes it terrible. The distortions do not shift. The distances do not change. The geometry does not heal or worsen. It is locked, frozen, a permanent instability—equilibrium in the sense of a held breath, a world that has stopped changing because it has already become exactly what it intends to be.

Fauna are rare: you might see one, once, moving between the stalks with the slow deliberate patience of a thing that has no predators and no need for haste. It is not animal. It might be another kind of plant, or a symbiote, or a tool left behind by whoever—or whatever—designed this place. It does not acknowledge you. It moves on, and the silence closes behind it like water.

The basin holds. The plants drink light. The glass holds. The wrongness holds, a stable fracture in the shape of a world, and you can walk through it forever without ever arriving anywhere you did not already stand.
