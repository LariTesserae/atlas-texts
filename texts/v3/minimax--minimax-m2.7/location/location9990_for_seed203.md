# The Vast Below

The cavern — if cavern is even the right word — extends past the horizon in every direction. Not a single hollow but a world of hollows: cathedral spaces connected by arterial corridors, the whole of it stretching far enough that it has developed its own weather. Cold drafts move through the main chambers with a purpose, carrying moisture that condenses on surfaces and pools in the low places, forming dark mirrors that reflect the light from above — or what serves as above in a place without sky.

The brightness comes from everything. The walls are alive. Not metaphorically: the stone itself is saturated with bioluminescent plant matter, fine filaments of fungal light threaded through the rock so that it glows from within, a pale blue-green radiance that never fades and never flickers. The illumination is total, sourceless, omnipresent. There are no shadows in the traditional sense. Instead, darkness is the absence — the void spaces between the glowing surfaces, the empty pockets where the plant-growth hasn't reached or has been scraped away.

Vast emptiness alternates with total occupation. The growing things fill some volumes completely: massive stalks rise from floor to ceiling, their surfaces colonized by smaller organisms, those colonized by smaller still, a hierarchy of plant-life building architectures of living material. In other volumes, there is nothing — just cold air and the faint mineral smell of deep stone, the silence broken only by the occasional drip of collected condensation. The scale is disorienting. Your footsteps echo in ways that suggest spaces larger than cities, but the details are intimate: you could touch both walls of a corridor if you stretched, though the corridor might run for days before opening into another chamber.

The erosion is young, which is strange for a subterranean world. The features here are fresh — sharp-edged where the plant-growth hasn't yet softened them, angular junctions where passages meet, geometries that haven't yet been weathered into curves. This place may be old, but the surfaces are new. Something keeps exposing raw stone. The turbulence — the dynamic parameter — manifests not as wind or water but as change. The plant-life pulses. Stalks that were knee-high yesterday are waist-high today. New pools appear in depressions that were dry. The bioluminescence breathes in slow waves, brightening and dimming in patterns that feel almost like respiration.

In the empty spaces, you find traces of habitation: small stone structures that might be shelters or shrines or something your categories can't accommodate. Primitive tools — hand-held, manually operated, worn smooth by use — lie half-buried in the growth. Faint marks on walls, not quite writing. A single carved figure, humanoid but elongated, sitting with what might be a tool or might be a weapon across its lap. The beings who made these things are gone now, or gone mostly. Their artifacts are scattered and few, as if they lived lightly or as if the world keeps overtaking them, growth covering what is left behind.

The fauna is rare but present. Small creatures move through the undergrowth — translucent things with too many limbs, eyes tuned to the perpetual glow. They watch you from a distance and do not approach. The cold keeps them sluggish, the bright light keeps them careful. You are the strangest thing in this place, and they know it.

It is quiet here most of the time. The quiet of deep places, the kind that makes you hear your own circulation. But it is not silent: occasional sounds drift from deeper in — a crack of shifting stone, a splash from an unseen pool, the faint creak of biological material growing. The turbulence is there, under the surface. The place is alive in the way that ecosystems are alive, changing moment by moment, and you are inside it, and the light is inside you, and the cold is in your bones, and the vastness is in your mind, and you are small, and the plants keep growing, and somewhere far away something that might be rain is falling in a chamber you will never reach.
