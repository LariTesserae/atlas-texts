The ravine does not breathe.

It is the first thing you notice, after the wet and the dark and the press of too many living things. Everything is still. The water that feeds this place seeps from fractures in the stone overhead in a slow, constant murmur — not dripping, exactly, but exhaling — and it has been exhaling for so long that the sound has worn smooth, has become indistinguishable from silence. The moisture does not move through the air so much as it saturates it, each breath a small intake of cold fog. Moisture beads on every surface: on the close-packed ferns that crowd the narrow floor, on the moss that thickens the walls into a single living face, on the lichens that climb past where the light gives out into a second, lighterless kingdom. Water lines the stones in thin films. Water pools in the hollows of ancient erosion, in basins carved so deep and so long that the water in them has taken on a mineral smoothness, dark and still as poured iron. The air tastes of stone and of something older than stone — the taste of water that has been here since the world was younger.

This is not a place of gradients. Nothing shifts. The temperature holds at the same cool throughout, neither cooler nor warmer in the deep places or near the seeps. The light — a faint, sourceless gray, as if the darkness itself gives off a minimal warmth of color — does not change from one hour to the next, because here there are no hours. What light reaches this place reaches it through some accident of geometry, some high cleft in the ravine wall that admits a pale thread of sky, and the thread never brightens or dims, never angles differently, as if the sun above has been locked in place along with everything else. You wait for the light to move. It does not move. The seep does not pulse or surge. The ferns do not sway. The beetles that cover the wet stones in a dense, iridescent layer — there are thousands of them, their shells catching the faint gray light in oily greens and blacks — are still. Not dormant, not dead, but held. Frozen in some moment of perfection that the world outside forgot how to reach.

The animals are everywhere. They are the most disorienting part, because their numbers belong to a warmer, brighter place. Flies that should be seasonal are present in small clouds that drift without purpose. Things with too many legs — pale, eyeless things that fit into the cracks between the water-smoothed stones like fingers into a glove — press against one another in dense clusters. Frogs, small and dark and unendingly numerous, cling to every surface the moisture touches. The ground is not quite ground. It is a shifting of bodies. When you look closely, you cannot tell where one organism ends and another begins. They are packed so tightly that the ravine floor appears to breathe, a slow undulation of packed life. But even this breathing is still — or perhaps it is happening so slowly that you cannot perceive it, so slowly that in an hour it moves less than your heartbeat, so slowly that a year from now, the same frog will be in the same crack, and the same beetle will be pressed against the same stone, and the water will still be rising from the same fracture in the same slow exhale, and the air will still be the same cold weight on your lungs.

This is what it means when they say the land is in balance. Not alive in the way you know, not changing, not struggling. A perfect equilibrium, reached so long ago that no force remains to disturb it. The water does not cut new channels because the channels it has cut are exactly right. The vegetation does not spread because the space it fills is exactly the right amount of space. The animals do not migrate because there is nowhere to go and nothing to want. They simply are, in their enormous numbers, held in a stillness that is not the stillness of death but the stillness of completion — the held breath after a long sentence has ended, the moment of perfect, unbearable stasis when nothing further can be added or taken away.

You are standing in it. Your breath fogs in the cold wet air. A drop of water runs down the back of your neck from a ledge overhead, where something pale and many-legged is pressed against the stone, motionless. You watch it. It does not move. You wait for it to move, and in the waiting you begin to understand that you are the only thing in this ravine that still believes in time.
