# The Underbelly

The cavern does not echo so much as hum. At this depth, sound travels strangely—absorbed by damp stone in some passages, amplified in others into something that presses against the skin like low atmospheric pressure. The dominant texture here is wet mineral: walls slick with moisture that beads and runs in thin channels, pooling in the low places where the floor dips into basins carved by water that no longer flows this way.

You are standing in one of the broad chambers, though "standing" feels provisional—the ground is uneven, broken by collapse rubble and flowstone ledges that rise knee-high and higher. The ceiling vaults upward into darkness, disappearing where the faint glow from phosphorescent crusts on the walls can no longer reach. You cannot see the full extent of this space. The parameters say it is open, regional, town-sized, and you believe them: when you drop a stone into one of the deeper shafts that pockmark the floor, the sound of its landing comes back from distances that suggest you are standing in a hollow the size of a valley.

Water is present but not dominant. It comes through—trickling down formations that haven't yet grown into full stalactites, just wet fingers of stone pointing groundward. It gathers in pools along the chamber's margins, where the rock dips and seals against clay seams. The pools are dark and still, mirror-smooth except where condensation drips from above, creating ripples that spread and cancel and spread again in endless small negotiations. The moisture in the air is enough to make your skin feel tight, your breath feel thick. Condensation beads on every surface, on the smooth grey rock and the rougher patches where erosion still works at the raw stone.

Young erosion. This is important. The walls are not yet softened. Edges are sharp where passages branch, where water has cut through recently enough that the angles haven't yet begun to round. The stone has a raw, almost quarry-like quality—fractured faces catch the dim bioluminescent light in ways that polished flowstone would not. Minerals precipitate in crude patterns, not yet the delicate filigree of ancient caves. The system is new enough that the water still makes its decisions, still cuts new channels when it can find them, still hasn't been locked into paths worn deep by ten thousand years of patience.

Sparse vegetation clings to the wettest zones. Moss in pale green felt, growing in patches that follow the water's logic—thickest where the trickles persist, thinning to isolated tufts at the margins. Ferns sprout from cracks where enough moisture has collected, their fronds pale and delicate, adapted to this lightless world. Lichen marks the higher, drier surfaces in crusts of grey and rust. Between these islands of growth, the rock is bare, offering no purchase to roots.

And the fauna.

The fauna is what you notice first, then what you stop noticing entirely, then what you cannot stop thinking about. There is no moment of arrival here, no threshold where the ecosystem begins. It is simply everywhere: skittering, pulsing, writhing, hanging. Beetle-shapes in iridescent black move across the walls in loose herds, hundreds of them, their leg-sounds a soft susurrus of contact with wet stone. Cave crickets cluster near the pools, their long antennae testing the air, their bodies pale and translucent as if the dark has bleached them from the inside. Millipedes in oily reds and browns navigate the rubble in slow processionals, each body segment lifting and settling in the wave-motion that lets them move without legs interfering with each other.

The spiders are worse. They are always worse. Webs lace the corners where walls meet ceiling, geometric and purposeful, their strands catching the dim light into near-invisibility. The spiders themselves are small but not small enough, and they do not flee when you pass close to their snares. They wait.

In the water, eyeless fish move in sudden silver flashes, quick as thoughts. They have no use for eyes in a place of no light; their lateral line system maps pressure and vibration, lets them feel you coming from meters away. They scatter when you lean close to a pool, then reform their slow circuits once you withdraw. Salamanders rest in the deepest damp, their skin glistening, their limbs splayed as if they've melted into the stone.

And smaller things: springtails in impossible numbers, living in the substrate, leaping in faint white arcs when disturbed. Worms pushing through the wet sediment. Flies that have forgotten the sun, their wings iridescent and useless.

Primitive signs exist here. A fire ring in one alcove, the stones blackened but old, the ash long since compacted into the cave floor. A hand-axe of simple stone wedged into a crack, left behind or lost by someone who moved through this place before you. Rope-marks on a column where something was once tied. Not built, not made into—only passed through, only used and discarded. The cave does not care about their presence. It has absorbed them, or will.

Light is present but insufficient. Bioluminescent fungi grow in patches on the walls, emitting a blue-green glow that is more atmospheric than illuminating. It is enough to see by, barely, to navigate the immediate space. Beyond a few meters, darkness reasserts itself. You become aware of how much you are relying on sound, on the texture of the air on your skin, on the slow adjustments of your eyes.

Sound here is layered. The fauna creates a constant baseline: leg-noises and wing-noises and the faint high cricket-chirrs that serve as communication in a world without sight. The water provides punctuation—drip, drip, the irregular percussion of condensation returning to itself. Sometimes a trickle from deeper in the system, audible as a texture more than a noise, a wet rushing that says *there is still water moving somewhere in the dark*. The cave itself creaks and settles in its own slow geology, popping and groaning in ways that are not alarming but are also not comforting.

But what strikes you most—after hours, after the initial overwhelm fades to something you can hold—is the stillness. The dynamic is 0.1. The ecosystem has reached equilibrium and does not move from it. The water drips where it drips. The beetles follow paths worn into generations of behavior. The pools maintain their depth. The temperature never varies; the moisture never intensifies or retreats. This is not stasis as failure but stasis as success: every process feeding every other process, the whole vast system in perfect balance, requiring nothing, changing nothing, existing as it has existed and as it will continue to exist.

You are the variable that does not belong. The cave absorbs your presence, your heat, your sound. Within hours—or days, or however long you stay—the ecosystem will adjust to include whatever you leave behind. Your waste. Your cells, shed in the dry air. Within weeks, your gear will begin to be incorporated: fabric fiber colonized by springtails, metal surfaces filmed with bacterial mats.

You are the weirdness this place does not have, passing through the normalcy it cannot escape.
