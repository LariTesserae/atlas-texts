# The Weeping Plateau

The plateau should have been flat by now. Thirty million years of rain and frost had worn the mountains down to nothing — had worn everything down, past hills, past ridges, past the very memory of peaks. What remains is a vast cold highland where the word *flat* has lost its meaning. The ground is smooth as skin, smooth as water, but it isn't smooth — it breathes, it pulses, it rises and falls by inches over centuries. Erosion at this stage becomes something else: not the wearing away of stone but the settling of all things into their final arrangement. The plateau is old the way equations are old. It is done becoming.

And yet everything is alive.

Water weeps from the rocks — but there are no rocks anymore, not in any way that matters. The stone has been so consumed by time and by the patient, total work of root and mycelium that what remains is neither. You could call ititeite: a substance that wasite once, worn smooth and alive, weeping. Water condenses on its surface in the cold air and trickles down in a thousand thin threads, pooling in the shallowest dips, finding channels that wind like veins across the living ground. The air is perpetually saturated. Fog clings to everything, thins, reforms. It is not quite rain. It is not quite breath. It is the atmosphere perpetually on the edge of weeping itself.

Vegetation covers the plateau, but *cover* is the wrong word. The plants here grow in defiance of the cold, of the altitude, of everything that should forbid them. They grow because something in this ancient place has learned to sustain them — water from weeping stone, nutrients from the slow digestion ofite, warmth from processes deeper than sun. They grow in forms that don't belong to any taxonomy you know. Towers of spiral growth. Flat planes that spread across the ground and then rise, suddenly, into complex fractals. Things that might be trees, if you squint, if you allow the word *tree* to expand past breaking. They are all connected. Their roots braid through theite, through each other, through the shallow water — a single organism pretending to be ten thousand.

Between the plants, space. Not emptiness — the air is dense with mist and spore and the faint smell of copper and ozone — but space nonetheless. The plateau is abundant and sparse at once. Fauna moves through the fog in bursts of motion and then stillness: pale creatures with too many legs, things that might be birds if their wings didn't fold into geometries, the slow passage of larger shapes whose forms dissolve at the edge of sight. They are adapted to cold, to wet, to the weird slant of light. They are adapted to the noise.

Because there is noise. The plants groan — not in wind, but in themselves, the slow creak of growth, the settling of weight. Water sings in channels you can't see. The fog carries sound in ways that feel deliberate, as if the atmosphere is learning to speak. And beneath it all, a sound that isn't organic: a low industrial hum, rhythmic, patient, coming from everywhere and nowhere. The plateau is not just alive. It is *running*.

You can't see the machines. You can only see what they've become. A spur ofite rises from the ground ahead, covered in the same pale plant-growth, symmetric in a way that vegetation rarely is. It hums when you approach. Its surface is warm. A few hundred meters away, the ground dips into a shallow basin where the mist is thicker, and shapes move in the thick of it — structures, half-buried, fused with root andite, their original purpose long since absorbed into the plateau's metabolism. Old industry. Ancient purpose. The machines continue because something below the ground continues, and the plateau has grown around them like a body around a splinter, making them part of itself.

Light here is dim and unreliable. The fog filters everything to a pale gray luminance, soft as moth wings. But when the mist thins — when a pocket of warmer air rises, or the wind shifts, or the plateau decides — light falls in sharp columns through gaps in the growth, illuminating the wet surfaces, the moving fauna, the strange angles of plants and machines alike. Then the light bends. Not refracts — *bends*. A column falls across a patch of ground and curves, follows the surface, rises again toward a sky you can't see. The angle is wrong. You look away, look back, and the angle is different. The plateau doesn't care about the rules. It was old when rules were young.

Storms come without warning. They are not weather — they are the plateau exhaling. Rain that falls sideways, that pools in midair, that runs uphill before falling. Lightning that doesn't flash but *pulses*, a slow brightening that lingers, that illuminates a world you didn't know was there and then dims, leaving afterimage. Between storms, periods of false calm where the fog thickens and everything slows and the only sound is water weeping fromite, everywhere, endlessly, as if the plateau has been crying for so long it has forgotten how to stop.

You walk. You have walked for hours, or minutes, or no time at all. The ground pulses beneath your feet — not a heartbeat, but something slower, something geological, the slow respiration of a thing that measures existence in millennia. The plants tower above you, their forms uncertain in the fog. You hear something moving to your left, large, deliberate. To your right, the hum of machinery, patient and sure. Ahead, the ground rises slightly — a ripple in the ancient flatness — and on its crest, a shape that is either a very large plant or a very old building, its edges soft with growth, its surfaces weeping.

The plateau extends in all directions, vast beyond reckoning, and you are not alone in it. You are not the only thing that moves here. But you are the only thing that remembers *flat*, that remembers *still*, that still expects the world to obey. Here, everything runs, everything grows, everything weeps. The machinery beneath continues its ancient work. The water finds new channels. The plants reach toward a light that bends. And the plateau breathes, breathes, breathes — the final form of something that was once a mountain, now nothing but the oldest surface in the world, covered in life, saturated with process, holding itself together through sheer industrial patience.

You are very small here. That is fine. The plateau has always been this big, and you will pass through it like weather.
