# The Sunken Dark

The water here is not water so much as it is weight itself, pressing in from every direction with the patience of deep time. You are below what once was surface, below what once was shore, in a place where the land surrendered so completely to the sea that no memory of standing anywhere remains. The bottom—if "bottom" means anything in a world without floor—lies miles beneath, and the currents move through it like argument, like unresolved tension, never still, never the same current twice.

This ocean is old. Older than the mountains that once pressed it down. The water itself tastes mineral in a way that speaks of eons, of stone worn to salt, of coastlines erased so thoroughly that geologists who could reach this place would find nothing to date, no stratum to anchor their timelines. The erosion here achieved its final form so long ago that the landscape settled into something like peace: vast, featureless except where ancient current carved shallow troughs now soft at their edges, patient as a sleeping thing. No cliffs. No falls. Only the long slope downward into darkness.

The cold comes not from season but from depth, from pressure, from the absence of any light to warm. It is a cold that finds the spaces between your ribs and stays. The water temperature and your body temperature have agreed to disagree, and you are losing.

What little light exists comes in shifts. At the surface—far above, unreachable—storms pass and light leaks down in diffuse gray pulses that fade before they reach you. Between storms, nothing. The darkness is not total but it is thorough, a blue-black that makes distance meaningless since you cannot see the edges of anything, cannot tell if the shadow ahead is ten meters or ten kilometers. Your eyes strain toward uselessness.

Vegetation here is not abundant. Where current catches on some ancient prominence—a ridge no higher than a child's hand, eroded nearly to nothing—there cling dark mats of something that might once have been kelp or might be a cousin to it, pale and stretched, surviving on less than should be possible. Rare enough that you have not seen it in days of swimming. Rare enough that when you do, you stop and look, confirming that something still grows here, still persists.

Fauna arrives in the same spirit: rarely, without ceremony. A shape passes at the edge of your vision's reach and is gone. Something with bioluminescence, a flicker of pale blue-green, appears once and vanishes into water too dark to track. The fish, if they are fish, have learned that visibility is danger. They stay deep, stay shadowed, stay away from each other and from you. You are alone in the way that only deep water can make you alone.

The packed density is not crowding in the way that a city crowds, with bodies and noise. It is the density of pressure, of water that has no room left for anything else, that has squeezed out the air from every space and filled those spaces itself, completely, all the way to the edges of the possible. There is no gap here. There is no pocket of less-pressure, no bubble of different medium. Only water, everywhere, all of it, pressing in.

In this world, there are no structures. Nothing built. Only what was here before the water rose, worn now to suggestions. Perhaps there were cities once where you swim, their foundations sand-blasted into nothing, their stories dissolved. Perhaps there were forests, plains, a civilization that looked up at the same stars you cannot see through all this water. Now there is only the eroded bed, the endless dark, and the sound of your own breathing echoing back at you, quiet as it is.

You move through the water with hands and patience, no tools beyond what you carry lashed to your body, no technology beyond your own muscles and the will that drives them. Primitive is the word, yes. You are a primitive thing here, early and simple, a pattern of carbon and water yourself, no different from the sparse life around you except for the stubbornness that keeps you swimming.

And the dynamic—the turbulence—makes liars of all the other numbers. This place is not still. It is not settled despite its ancient age. Currents pull from three directions at once, wrestling with each other, and the wrestling creates chaos: sudden surges that push you sideways, pockets of relative stillness that collapse without warning, cold water that rises from below and warm water that descends from above, fighting in the space around you. The ocean here has not finished becoming. It is still in argument with itself, still carving, still mixing, still very much alive with the kind of energy that erodes mountains, that takes millennia to notice and then one day leaves nothing standing.

You swim through argument. You breathe through turbulence. You are a visitor in a place that does not remember visitors, that would not notice you if you stopped, if you let the current take you, if you became just one more presence in the packed dark. But you do not stop. You swim. The water holds you and the darkness hides you and somewhere far above, beyond reach, beyond imagining, a storm is breaking light against the surface of a world you cannot see.
