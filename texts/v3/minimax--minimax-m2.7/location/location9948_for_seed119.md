The cistern was not made for water. Water made it necessary.

A vast enclosure of fitted stone, hand-dressed but leaving the hammer-marks — three hundred hands' breadths across and half that in height, a space like the inside of a ship's hull turned cathedral-side. The builders worked with chisels and wedges, with wooden pegs driven into cracks, with copper ladles to pour their lime and ash mortar. Their tech is legible everywhere: the tool-grooves, the worn grips of carrying-slots, the way each block sits against its neighbor with the patience of things fitted by eye. Nothing here grew. Nothing here was ever earth. The floor is stone; the walls are stone; the vaulted ceiling is stone, and where it is not stone, it is the blackened timber of scaffolding long rotted into the structure — fallen joists wedged between arches, a scaffold-axle still standing in a corner like a scarecrow.

And yet the water is everywhere.

It seeps from the joints. It pools in the low places between flagstones. A channel was carved into the floor to drain this enclosure, but the drains are silted, the channel broken, and so water collects — a thin, cold sheet that laps at the edges of things, knee-deep in the middle and spreading. It smells of mineral and age: the particular chill of underground places where water has been moving through stone for centuries. Mist hangs in the upper space, beading on the cold rock, and in the dim light — a weak gray filtering from a single shaft-cut in the western wall — the droplets catch like something almost luminous. The atmosphere is perpetually damp, perpetually post-rain, though no rain has fallen here in living memory. The weeping stones weep. The condensation knows its own cycles.

On the walls, in the angles where stone meets stone, something lives.

The vegetation is sparse but present: a lichen that grows in pale concentric rings, faintly phosphorescent at its edges; a crust of moss in the wetter cracks, a sourceless green that seems to glow from within. Here and there a wisp of something fibrous — a root-tendril, a single blade of sedge — pushes through a crack in the floor, drawing its thin life from the standing water. But plants do not dominate here. They are exceptions.

Fauna dominates.

The cistern is full of things that move in the half-light. The sound is constant: a layered hum, a clicking, a wet rustling. The walls are furred with beetles — black-shelled, slow, the size of thumbnails — and in the spaces between the stones, something nested and many-legged shifts with the sound of a slow breath. Flies hang in the mist like motes of dust. Midges spiral in columns above the standing water. In the dark corners, away from the shaft of gray light, things wait — things with too many legs, things that drink from the water's thin surface and leave wakes like calligraphy.

At night — or rather, when the light fails — the sound changes. Something with a voice clicks in the depths. Something that is not a frog and does not breathe air nevertheless makes a sound like one, a wet, resonant call that travels the length of the cistern and bounces from the vaulted ceiling. Something else answers. The water moves.

The built substrate is everywhere, total, inarguable: no natural ground, no earth, no living rock. The floor is laid; the walls are cut; the ceiling is vaulted and buttressed from within by a system of arches that the builders devised to hold back the weight of the hill above. No substrate is natural, and the substrate is all there is. But the erosional processes have been at work for centuries: the mortar is receding; the joints are widening; the flagstones have tilted, their edges worn soft by water and by the slow passage of whatever creatures cross them in the dark. The place is in balance — not static, but settled into its own rhythms, its own state. The water does not flood. The stones do not fall. Everything that was built has found its accommodation with everything that has come to live there.

The scale is a room, a chamber, a single bounded space. The density is near-void — you could cross the cistern in minutes, and what you would find is almost nothing: the water, the stones, the life, the pale light, the gray dark. But the sound fills the space as if it were packed solid. The hum of insects, the wet call of unseen things, the clicking that comes from somewhere deep in the walls — these fill the air with a pressure that is less like noise and more like presence. The space is empty of human things. It is full of everything else.

The weirdness is subtle but present: a wrongness that does not announce itself. The beetles move too slowly. The mist moves against the light. The carved channel in the floor, the one meant to drain, bends in a way that makes no hydraulic sense — it rises, briefly, before falling again, as if the water here has its own current, its own intention. And in the northwest corner, there is a figure.

Not a creature. Not a tool. A figure.

It is made of the same fitted stone as the walls, but it was set into them — a low relief, a face carved into the stone and worn smooth by centuries of seepage, its features blurred to an ambiguity that might be sorrow or hunger or nothing at all. Water beads in the hollows of its eyes. In the evenings, when the light fails, and the sounds rise from the depths of the cistern, the figure is the only thing that does not move. And yet — it was built to be here, like everything else. It was made by hands that held chisels and wedges, that worked with patience and with aim. It was made to be looked at, perhaps, or to watch.

The water moves.
The beetles shift.
The figure waits.
