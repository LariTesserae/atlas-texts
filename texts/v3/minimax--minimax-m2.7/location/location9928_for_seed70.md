Water does not behave correctly here.

It falls from the plateau's edge in wide curtains that hang suspended in the warm air, catching light before they catch ground. It gathers in basins that have no visible bottom — lower a stone into the clearest pool and watch it drift into darkness you cannot fathom the depth of. It runs in thin sheets across the young basalt, too clear, carrying a mineral taste that makes the back of the throat feel strange, and in the slow channels between mossy rock it moves with the patience of something that has not yet decided where to go.

The plateau rose recently. You can tell by the rock — still sharp at its ridges, its clefts unweathered, its faces bright where water has not yet worn them down. From this height, the water that falls from the southern rim must travel far before it finds the deep pools of the basin floor, and it takes its time. It seeps. It gathers in the hollows of the new stone, fills them, tops them, begins again. The air is warm and wet and smells of wet rock, of growing things, of the particular sweetness that moss releases when rain has been falling long enough that everything — rock, root, the deep pads of broad leaves — is equally wet.

Vegetation covers every surface that can hold it and several that cannot. Mosses carpet the stone in layers of close-pressed green, deepening in the crevices where water pools after rain. Ferns crowd the margins of the deeper channels, their fronds dark and veined, heavy with water that makes each leaf droop slightly toward the earth. Where soil has accumulated in pockets — against the base of rock faces, in the hollows of the young terrain — broad-leafed trees rise in dense stands, their roots interlaced with stone, their canopies closing overhead to form a second skin of green and filtered light. The vegetation is so dense that one form grows upon another: epiphytes cluster in the crooks of branches, their roots gripping bark, their leaves drawing moisture from air. Moss grows on the surface of moss. Ferns sprout from the decomposing mass of their own dead fronds. Everything leans against everything else, and the air between the trunks is close and wet, thick with the breath of growing things.

Light enters in fragments — shafted through the canopy, reflected bright from the surfaces of pools, turned amber where it passes through water-filed leaves. It makes the green of the moss glow from within, and at the edges of the pools it turns the water silver and gold. But the light is wrong in some small way that accumulates as you move through the basin. It falls at angles that seem plausible but do not quite match the position of the sky. It pools in places that should be shadow. It catches the edges of things and makes the rest of them fall away into a depth that seems greater than the space should allow. The basin is large enough — open enough — that the eye cannot find its far limit. The rock and water and green extend in every direction, layered, the nearest forms sharp and saturated, the distant ones blue-grey and soft, and the horizon, if there is one, is always somewhere past where you thought it was. The scale of the place is disorienting not because it is vast — it is not a plain, not an ocean — but because it is continuous. There is no moment when the landscape changes character. It is all this: dense, wet, young, warm, and large. It could be a single room if the room were large enough to swallow you.

The silence is not silence. Water is never absent. It falls in the high channels above the basin rim, it runs in the shallow sheets across the plateau surface, it pools and seeps and trickles through the moss that lines every rock face and roots through every soil pocket. But the air does not carry sound the way air should. Each noise exists alone — a drop of water striking a pool, a leaf releasing its load of rain, the creak of a branch bearing the weight of epiphytes. Between each sound, the silence is total, as if the wet growth absorbs all echo and reverb, or as if the air here is made of something other than gas, something that holds sound only long enough to deliver it before letting it fall away into nothing.

Fauna is rare but present. A bird crosses the canopy gap high above, its call answered from somewhere in the green — one answer, or many, or the same single voice returning from every direction at once. Fish hold themselves in the still water, too bright, too still, their colors not quite right, their position held without effort. In the deep moss, something small moves and is still.

There is something strange about this place beyond the strangeness of water that will not settle and air that will not carry sound and vegetation that grows so thick it becomes its own ecosystem, layered, self-sustaining, a world within the green. The strangeness is in the feeling that this plateau is impossibly new. The rock is sharp. The water is still learning its channels. The soil is just beginning to form, its first dark inches a gift from moss that has barely begun to decompose. Everything here is raw. Everything is young. The world has not yet decided what it is, and the not-yet-settled quality of it — the fact that all the features are still being carved, still being worn, still being grown — makes the place feel charged, awake, more itself than a landscape that has had time to forget its own becoming. The basin is not haunted. Nothing lives here that should not live here. But it is a place that has not finished being born, and the excess of water and the abundance of green and the quiet that is not quite silence are the symptoms of a world still in the act of finding out what it is.
