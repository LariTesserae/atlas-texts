The basin holds water. Not as a flood—water here isn't an event but a condition, a saturation that has worked its way into everything. The concrete cracked years ago, and through those fractures, moisture rises and falls with the heat, pooling in the low places where the ground dips toward the city's center. Every surface glistens. Every wall is slick with condensation that never quite dries, beading and running in patterns that depend on the angle of metal, the texture of brick, the chemical residue of whatever industry last occupied these blocks.

The city was built to last, and it does, in its way. Brick and steel, the bones of it, still stand in long rows along streets that were once organized for commerce and transit. But the organization has gone out of them. The buildings stand like teeth in a jaw that no longer bites. Their windows are empty frames or gaps where glass once was. Their interiors hum with residual heat—from buried pipes, from thermal exchangers still carrying heat from some central source that nobody tends anymore—and that warmth keeps the water from stagnating, keeps it moving, evaporating and condensing in endless small cycles across the face of the place.

There is no vegetation. You would think that means a dead landscape, but it doesn't. What it means is that the food web runs on other things: the slow digestion of wood by fungi that thrive in the wet, the breakdown of organic waste that accumulates in the drainage channels, the bodies of the dead themselves—fauna and whatever else has fallen and been consumed by the crowd of creatures that persist here. Rats in the foundations, feeding on detritus and each other. Flies breeding in the wet seams of concrete. Cats—feral, wary, thick-furred for reasons of their own—hunting by the drainage grates at dusk. Birds on the rooftops, watching. Insects in the walls, in the floors, in every gap where moisture collects and something edible pools.

The heat is the other constant. It comes from below as much as from above, rising through the ground, through the infrastructure, through the seams of a city designed to generate and distribute warmth. The basin's low elevation traps it. The water traps it. On still days the heat presses down like a weight; on days when something moves the air, the humidity shifts and reforms, a vaporous second skin that makes the buildings seem to breathe.

Sound is present but subdued. The hiss of steam at pressure points. The drip and trickle of condensation finding its way through old architecture. The distant mechanical pulse of something still running in the deep infrastructure—engines, maybe, or turbines, a heartbeat at the threshold of hearing. The fauna moves quietly: footsteps on wet concrete, the scrape of claws, the occasional call that carries brief and sharp through the heavy air.

The light comes muted, filtered through haze that might be humidity or might be residue, the ghost of processes that once put smoke and steam into the atmosphere. The sun, when it appears, is a pale smear behind the perpetual murk. The streets glow with reflected warmth where water lies thin over stone, creating puddles that seem almost to steam in the heat.

This is a place that was made for purpose, and then emptied of it, and then filled again with something that lives in the margins of that absence. The fauna moves through it with the ease of long habitation. The infrastructure still functions, if barely, if wrongly, if in ways that serve no economy but keep the moisture and the heat alive in the bones of the city. It is not a dead place. It is a place that has gone on living in the dark, wet, hot, and dense, fed by what remains and by what breeds in the remainder, a world that persists on the dregs of its own metabolism.
