The plateau sits above the world in a way that makes the world feel like a rumor. It is a place of stone and silence, of light that never quite arrives and wind that never quite stops. The altitude has stripped away everything soft, everything unnecessary. What remains is architecture: the slow architecture of erosion, patient over epochs, carving the dark rock into fins and shelves and faces that catch the pale light at strange angles.

There is water here, but not as water. It exists as hoarfrost in the morning, as rime that coats the north faces of stones, as condensation that beads on exposed surfaces and vanishes by midmorning. Occasionally, in the deepest folds of the plateau, you might find a frozen trickle—a stream arrested mid-flow, its surface glazed with ice that will not melt for months. The air holds moisture like a held breath, not quite precipitate, not quite dry. It settles on the skin, on the tongue, carries the faint mineral taste of dissolution.

Sparse vegetation clings to the sheltered crevices. Not grass but something harder: lichens the color of old rust, freeze-dried moss that crunches underfoot, the occasional twisted shrub whose branches grow horizontal, all its energy bent away from the prevailing wind. These plants exist in a state of near-dormancy, alive but barely, patient in a way that life elsewhere cannot afford to be. Between these pockets of life, the ground is gravel and bare stone, scree and flat rock extending to the limits of vision.

The temperature is the temperature of endurance. Not the killing cold of true extremity, but the cold that makes presence itself an act of negotiation. It finds the gaps in clothing, the places where heat escapes from the body, and it settles there. At night it deepens; in the small hours before dawn, it seems to achieve a quality of finality, as if cold itself has rules and consequences.

But here is the thing about this place, the thing that makes it strange: the world does not behave as it should. The light falls at wrong angles, or perhaps the angles are right but the mind cannot accept them. Shadows point toward the wrong sources. The horizon, when you try to look at it, slides away from the gaze—it is present, then it is not, then it is somewhere else. Sound carries strangely; a rockfall echoes from a direction where no cliff exists. The wind sings in frequencies that seem to carry meaning, though no meaning can be found.

They might be shelters, or markers, or the remains of something whose purpose has been forgotten by both the world and the stones themselves. They sit in isolated positions, each one alone, as if proximity to others was precisely what needed to be avoided. Around them, barely visible, are lines scratched into the rock: not writing, not images, but something else. Intentional marks whose intent has eroded along with everything else.

The plateau extends past the immediate in a way that the mind resists. There is always more of it—another ridge, another fold, another expanse of gravel that looks exactly like the last. The eye searches for landmarks and finds only the infinite patience of erosion, which makes everything and distinguishes nothing. Scale becomes uncertain. A boulder becomes a hill becomes a mountain becomes a trick of the light. The emptiness is not the emptiness of void but the emptiness of subtraction; the plateau has had everything taken from it until only the skeleton remains.

The sound is the most insistent feature. The wind does not gust but flows, a constant river of air moving over and around and through the stone. It finds frequencies in the rock formations and makes them speak—a low, continuous tone that vibrates in the chest, underlaid by higher whistles and the occasional deep groan from somewhere beyond the visible. When the wind stops—and it does stop, rarely, for seconds at a time—the silence that follows is so complete it has texture, weight, a presence that presses against the ears.

Yet the plateau is stable. The erosion that shaped it happened long ago; what remains is in equilibrium with itself. The frost heaves the stones slightly each winter, and each summer they settle back. The wind moves the gravel by millimeters per year. Change is measured in geological time, not human time. The plateau does not remember the things that built the scattered structures; it does not forget them either. It simply continues.

At night—and the nights here are long, the sun a brief visitor that skirts the southern horizon and leaves—the light comes from the stars and a moon that seems too large, too present. The darkness is not complete; there is always a faint luminescence to the sky, a glow that might be atmospheric or might be something else. In this light, the distortions intensify. The eye sees movement in still places. The ear hears footsteps that stop when you stop. The plateau offers no hospitality, but it offers no hostility either. It simply is, in a way that exceeds the capacity of being to contain it.

The fauna are rare and reluctant: a bird that passes through without landing, a mammal-like shape glimpsed once in a distant ravine and never seen again. Whatever passes through here does so with purpose, with haste, without lingering. There is nothing here to linger for.

And yet. The plateau has been walked. The structures have been built. The marks have been made. Someone, or something, found meaning here once, or found a reason to mark this place as significant. The plateau holds these traces the way it holds the frost—not quite part of itself, but not entirely separate either. They have become part of the landscape in the way that erosion has become part of the stone.

You walk across it, if you walk across it, and the ground is solid and the air is thin and the wind finds the gaps in everything you are. The plateau does not resist; it simply continues. It does not care about your presence; it does not not care. It is beyond the categories that care implies. It is the place beneath the place, the world before the world, the stone that remains when everything else has been taken away. It is cold and empty and strange and real, and it will be here long after the strangeness has been forgotten, long after the memory of cold has been erased, a wound in the world that has healed over into something that does not quite fit.
