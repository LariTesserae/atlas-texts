# The Condensation City of Veth Karan

In the upper atmosphere of this place, where the pressure drops and the heat holds like a thick hand, water still finds its way into the world. It arrives not as rain but as condensation—night mist that clings to every surface, morning dew that beads on stone and trickles down through ancient channels carved by centuries of this patient weeping. The rocks themselves weep here. Basalt and obsidian formations, black and glassy smooth from millennia of thermal expansion and contraction, weeping moisture from their pores like grief made mineral. The air smells of wet stone and hot metal, of something sulfurous and alive beneath the surface.

The elevation matters. Not mountain-high, but high enough that the land stretches flat and open in every direction, a vast plateau cut through with erosion channels—shallow valleys that have been falling toward the same center for longer than memory. From any rise, you can see the horizon's shimmer, heat bending light into liquid shapes. The scale is regional: this isn't a single city but a network, a sprawl of infrastructure and habitation spread across the basin floor and climbing the plateau's edges. Distances that would be walked in other worlds are here traveled by rail, by automated cart, by the slow mechanical pulse of this place's industrial heart.

What strikes you first is the emptiness. Veth Karan is built—or rather, *has been* built—across a vast emptiness that dwarfs its structures. Between any two points of construction lie kilometers of bare volcanic rock, cracked and dark, where nothing grows because nothing can. The vegetation parameter is honest: there is almost no plant life here. What passes for biology on this plateau is simpler and stranger—microbial mats in the condensation channels, pale fungal threads feeding on moisture itself in the deep places, a kind of life so minimal it barely registers. The silence between sounds is immense. You can hear the hum of distant machinery, the tick of cooling metal, the faint drip and trickle of water finding its way down through the city, but the spaces between these sounds are vast as deserts.

The city rises from this emptiness like a fever dream. Industrial-era construction, its bones exposed: steel frames blackened by decades of heat, concrete bunkers with ventilation stacks that breathe steam and waste heat into the dim air, rail lines running between structures with the religious regularity of arteries. The infrastructure is dense where the infrastructure exists, and then it stops, and then there is only the bare rock and the heat shimmer and the patient silence. The people who built this place understood density the way other cultures understand distance—they clustered their work and habitation into nodes, connected by lines of movement, separated by voids that made the nodes possible. To walk from one structure to another is to cross emptiness that could swallow a small village whole.

Light here is dim and diffuse, filtered through atmospheric moisture that never fully clears. The sun is present but muted, a pale disk behind perpetual haze. At night, the darkness is absolute beyond the pools of amber light that mark inhabited zones—the glow of incandescent filaments, the red pulse of cooling forges, the occasional bright flare of some industrial process. The darkness is not empty, exactly, but it is vast, and the sounds that emerge from it are few and strange: the creak of metal expanding in heat, the distant lowing of some rare beast adapted to this place, the hiss of condensation hitting hot stone.

Fauna is rare by design. The creatures that survive here are the ones that found a niche in the condensation economy: small things that drink from the weeping rocks, larger things that eat the small things. Occasionally, at the edges of the map, you see movement—a shape crossing the flats between structures, gone before you can name it. The ecosystem is barely an ecosystem; it's more like a rumor of one, an echo of biological process in a world that has moved on to stranger experiments.

And there is a strangeness here, a wrongness that accumulates the longer you stay. The water should evaporate in this heat, and yet it persists, drawn upward by processes the city's founders understood better than their descendants do. The weeping never stops. The condensation forms in the hot air like memory, like something trying to remember what the world was before it became this. The machines that extract moisture from the air, that pipe it through the city's channels, that feed the rare fountains and bathing houses—they run constantly, their engines' rhythm a heartbeat underlying everything. But sometimes, late at night, you hear the water moving through channels that have no names on any map, feel it passing beneath your feet, and you wonder if the city is extracting moisture from the stone or if the stone is exhaling it, slowly, over centuries, and the city has simply learned to listen.

The dynamic is active but slow. Weather patterns cycle through haze and clearer moments. The condensation varies—denser at dawn, thinning through the heat of day, returning with the temperature drop at dusk. The mechanical processes continue, the rare creatures move through their territories, the water moves through its channels. Nothing is static, but the motion is patient, geological, the pace of a world that has been here longer than it will admit and intends to outlast whatever comes next.
