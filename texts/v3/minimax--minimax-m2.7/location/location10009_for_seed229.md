# The Basin of Resin and Bone

This is a place that should not breathe, and yet it breathes.

The basin cups itself in a high hollow of the world's back, a caldera long since dead—or sleeping, at least, its fires banked deep in the root-rock below. Walls of dark basalt rise around a floor some three kilometers across, their faces veined with white mineral threads that catch what little light falls here. The light arrives thin and amber-tinted, filtering through a haze of floating spores that never quite settles. At midday you might mistake it for dust. At dusk it glows.

There is no water. The last of it left epochs ago, or perhaps it was never water at all—whatever solvent once moved through the stone has long since been bound up in crystalline lattices, absorbed into the cells of things that grew without it, evaporated into a memory of humidity the air can no longer hold. The ground crunches underfoot with a sound like old teeth. Salt-bones erupt from the flats in shapes that might be formations, might be the fossilized architecture of creatures, might be something that was both and neither. You learn not to ask which.

And yet it is *lush*. This is the thing you cannot account for, no matter how many times you see it.

The vegetation grows in forms the word barely fits. Resin-trees, the settlers called them, though they are not trees: they are thickets of amber-like extrusion, rising in clusters from cracks in the basalt, their surfaces tacky and warm to the touch. They drink what they drink—condensation from the deep, perhaps, or the metabolic exudate of the substrate itself—and they grow, slowly, in pulses you can hear at night, a creak and swell like a body turning in sleep. Between them sprawl mats of porous stone-vegetation, pale grey and faintly breathing, their surfaces patterned with fractal pores through which faint moisture moves in chains of micro-condensation. They are warm to the skin. They smell of hot pitch and something older, something mineral that the nose wants to call *food* even though no human tongue could find purchase on it.

The fauna does not notice the absence of water. It is *everywhere*.

The bone-beetles come first: carapaced things the length of your thumb, their bodies a patchwork ofite anditeite, that make a sound like rain on a distant roof as they swarm across the flats in murmurations of thousands. They drink from the resin-trees, lapping at the tacky surfaces where the trees weep their slow secretion. They breed in the salt-bones, their eggs laid in the shade of the mineral overhangs where the heat cannot reach. When a flock rises—startled by a shadow, or simply because the hour has turned—it fills the air with the click of wing-cases and the rustle of chitin, and you stand very still and let them pass, because they are beautiful and because they are the only weather this place has.

There are other things. Crawling shapes that fold themselves flat against the stone, drinking through membranes pressed to the cooling mineral flows. Pairs of luminous organs that watch from the resin-thickets, belonging to creatures that never show themselves entire. Vines that move when you are not looking, that have not moved when you look again, that have definitely moved when you look a third time. The basin is dense with life, but the life is secretive, adapted to a world where attention means death.

The settlers' marks are faint and far between.

You find them in the sheltered angles of the wall-rock: handholds carved deep into the stone, their grips worn smooth by generations of palms. A circle of stackedite-ite blocks, low and tumbled, that might have been a shelter or might have been a boundary. Scorch-marks on a flat-faced boulder, where someone once lit a fire that did not need water to catch. The tool-marks are simple: scratches, gouges, the primitive vocabulary of stone against stone. Whoever lived here understood the local materials. Theiteite-ite blocks fit together with a precision that speaks of long study, though the means were only hands and patience.

No one lives here now. Or if they do, they are not the kind who leave marks.

The basin is loud.

This is what you notice first, and what you cannot stop noticing: the sound. It rises from everywhere at once, or from nowhere, or from the walls themselves—the creak of resin-trees growing, the tick and scratch of bone-beetles on stone, the distant hum that might be the deep geology settling or might be the collective breath of the stone-vegetation mats. When the bone-beetle swarms rise in the evening, the air fills with a sound like paper tearing, like wind through a canyon, like the ocean if the ocean were made of chitin and memory. It is not unpleasant. It is not anything. It is the basin speaking to itself.

And the basin is not still.

The turbulence is subtle, at first—you might call it peace, the kind of peace that a cathedral has, where nothing moves but nothing is settled. But look longer. Watch the resin-trees pulse in their slow growth. Watch the mats of stone-vegetation shift and resettle, redistributing their weight. Watch the bone-beetles move in their great slow waves across the flats, their paths curving around obstacles you cannot see, as if they are reading a map written in something older than sight. The basin is alive in the way that weather is alive: not a thing that changes, but a process that *is* change. Creation and destruction happening simultaneously, at every scale, a million tiny deaths feeding a million tiny births, the whole basin a single metabolism turning slowly in the heat.

You came here looking for something. A reason, perhaps. A pattern in the stone. An answer to the question the basin keeps not asking: how does life persist where nothing should grow?

You will not find that answer here. But you will find something else.

You will find the resin-trees weeping their slow amber tears at dusk. You will find the bone-beetles swirling in patterns that almost mean something. You will find the handholds worn smooth by hands that understood what it means to hold on in a place that offers nothing to hold onto. You will find the sound, the warm dim light, the salt-bones rising like questions from the flat dark ground.

You will find a place that was never meant to exist, and that exists anyway, fiercely, with everything it has.

And you will understand, finally, that this is what life does. It does not ask for permission. It does not wait for conditions. It simply *continues*—in the cracks, in the margins, in the basin that cups itself in the world's back and breathes with a breath it should not have, and does not explain itself, and does not stop.
