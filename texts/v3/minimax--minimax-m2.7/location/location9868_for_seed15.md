# The Reservoir Chamber

The chamber breathes.

Water moves through it everywhere—not as flood, not as ocean, but as a patient patient presence, a saturation that has found its way into every joint and crack since the day the last mason descended and sealed the door behind him. It seeps from the chiseled joints in the stone walls. It pools in low depressions in the floor, which was once meant to be dry. It traces dark lines down faces of white limestone that were never meant to be faces at all, merely load-bearing surfaces, merely the walls of a room built to hold something other than water.

Light comes from the stones themselves.

Not fire, not torch, not the filtered gleam of the world's surface. The limestone—touched, perhaps, by some mineral impurity in its burning, or by some early treatment the masons applied with their crude brushes—emits a continuous pale luminescence. It is not sunlight. It does not warm. But it is bright, consistent, and casts no shadows worth speaking of. The chamber is evenly lit from within, from the walls, from the floor, from the single raised platform at its center where something once stood. If you needed to see to work, you could work here. The masons did.

There is no vegetation. But.

In the corners where water pools and evaporates, pools and evaporates, a faint white crust collects on the stone. At the very edges of the waterline—where it rises and falls, where there is rhythm here, a tide of sorts, a cycle connected to some larger system of pressure and passage—you might find the barest green. Algae, perhaps. Or something that was algae, once, before this environment taught it to be something else. It clings to the limestone in threads so faint they might be imagination. They are not imagination. They are the only life.

The room is small. Perhaps eight paces by eight. Perhaps less. The ceiling—if that word applies to a surface carved downward from the earth rather than built upward—arches perhaps two and a half times a human height above the floor. It was designed as an intimate space. A room for work, or rest, or ritual, of a kind practiced by people who had not yet learned to make machines but had learned to make architecture, to quarry stone, to cut and fit and join. Their chisel marks remain, sharp as the day they fell from the chisel. The erosion here is fresh: not the weathering of centuries, but the younger erosion of water, finding its way into the first cracks, beginning its patient work on the first day it was let in.

And it was let in. That much is clear. The chamber was built to be sealed. The door—if there was a door—is gone now, dissolved or removed or simply lost to time. But the frame remains, a dressed archway leading to darkness, and beyond the darkness: more darkness, and the sound of water moving in a channel, a controlled flow, a system. This chamber was once part of a network. It still is.

The sounds are the room's most vivid feature. Water drips from the vaulted ceiling in a rhythm that never quite repeats—more drops now, less later, variations in pressure, in the accumulation and release of moisture through stone. The pool in the floor's depression makes its own sound: a low lapping, a wet whisper against limestone, as the water rises and falls in its cycle. And beneath it all, audible through the walls, a continuous murmur: water moving through channels, through channels, through channels, somewhere deeper, somewhere else, connected to whatever the people who built this place needed water to be connected to.

There is no animal life. No insects in the corners. No rats in the walls. Whatever lives in the world beyond this room has not yet found its way in—or has found it and cannot survive. The chamber is not sterile, not exactly, but it is sparse. It holds only what it needs, which is to say: the water, the light, the stone, the slow work of time.

And the platform.

It sits in the center of the room, raised perhaps a third of a meter above the floor, its surface smooth where everything else is rough-cut. Here the erosion has been slowest. Here the water touches last. On its surface: nothing. A ring of darker stone, where something stood—or sat—or was placed—and cast no shadow in the luminous light because the light comes from everywhere and casts no shadows because the light is that kind of light. Water does not pool on the platform; it runs off, into the channels cut into its surface, channels that lead to the edge, channels that lead to the floor, channels that lead to the drainage system that was designed for dryness but now moves water instead of air.

The chamber was built. This is certain. Every stone was placed. Every joint was filled. The archway was dressed, and the floor was leveled, and the platform was finished to a smoothness that took time and took care and took tools held in hands that knew what they were doing even if they did not know what they were making, or perhaps especially if they did.

And now the water has come, and the chamber breathes, and the light glows, and the erosion—young as it is—continues its patient work, and the small green things at the waterline persist, and there is no one here to see any of it, and there has been no one here for a very long time, and the water does not care.
