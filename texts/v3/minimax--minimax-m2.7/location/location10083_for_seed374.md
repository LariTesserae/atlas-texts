# The Dripping Wastes

The city—if it can be called that—rises from ground that is still learning its shape. Basalt andite crusts the surface in great hexagonal plates, cracked along seams that haven't yet learned to widen, edges still sharp enough to split skin. No water has worn them smooth. No time has softened the angles. The stone here is newborn, geologically speaking: raw, unfinished, erupting or cooling or simply *being* in a way that hasn't accumulated the patience of erosion.

And yet it is wet.

Water seeps up between the plates, welling from somewhere deep and pressurized, or perhaps condensing from atmosphere thick with humidity. The surface stays damp constantly—a moisture that doesn't evaporate in the warm air but rather *persists*, beading on every mineral surface until the entire ground glimmers like something alive. Some outcroppings weep steadily, thin threads of water tracing down their faces to pool in the low places. The air tastes of mineral salt and something older, something that might once have been called alive.

Inhabited. That much is certain.

The settlement sprawls across the low basalt plain in a density that feels almost intentional, every gap filled with structure or presence. Walls of fitted stone, timber, packed mud, woven reed. The architecture is not crude so much as *simple*—these people have hand-tools, simple blades, manual techniques passed down without metal or machinery—but they build with persistence and purpose. Rooms pile against rooms. Streets become tunnels. The density is such that the buildings seem to grow from each other rather than stand alone, and from above the settlement would read as a single continuous mass, carved and channeled but unified.

Between the structures, between the cracks, between every near-touching surface—the fauna.

They are everywhere. Not the fauna of a forest or grassland, which moves and grazes and hides in clearings. This fauna is *dense*: layered in the vertical space of walls, present in every low hollow, crawling across the weeping stone in quantities that make the ground seem to shift. They are not large—not the megafauna of open savanna—but they are many. Insects of strange configuration, molusks that have adapted to damp air, translucent creatures that cluster in the shade of overhangs, skittering shapes that move in coordinated patterns across the wet basalt. The settlement's inhabitants have learned to build around them, with them, perhaps for them. The fauna is not a resource or a pest but simply a fact of existence, like the moisture, like the warmth.

And the sound.

The sound is the first thing you notice, the thing that precedes sight, the thing that makes you stop at the settlement's edge and wonder if you can proceed. It is not wind or weather or the dramatic sound of catastrophe. It is the accumulation of small things: the drip of weeping rock into pooling water, the skitter and click of countless creatures moving across mineral surfaces, the low hum of things that do not move but do not cease. The fauna calls and scrapes and pulses. Water trickles and beads and falls. The built structures—timber, stone, reed—settle and creak in the humid air. Layer upon layer of sound, dense in the way the presences are dense, near-touching in the way the structures are near-touching, so that you cannot isolate one source from another but rather stand inside a single continuous noise that is the voice of the place itself.

It does not change.

You wait for it to shift, for the rhythm to vary, for one source to rise or fall. It does not. The fauna continues its endless skittering. The weeping rocks drip at their steady rate. The settlement settles into itself with equal persistence. The warm wet air holds its humidity like a held breath. This is not a silence that precedes change; this is equilibrium itself, a world so balanced in its wetness and warmth and density that nothing has yet found a reason to shift. The city was built this way. The fauna bred this way. The water seeped and the stone cooled and everything remained, and remained, and remained.

You enter, and the sound closes behind you like water.
