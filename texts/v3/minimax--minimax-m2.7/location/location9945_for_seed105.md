# The Drowned Plateau of Kel Suarath

The water here does not ask permission. It has not asked for a very long time.

What was once a highland valley—rolling hills, perhaps, or the shoulders of an ancient mountain range—now lies beneath perhaps forty feet of dark water, though the floor rises and falls enough that some promontories breach the surface as bare stone islands. The water is cold, and it moves. Not in waves exactly, but in a slow churning that never ceases, a regional current that runs through the entire flooded territory like a sluggish circulatory system. Where it presses through gaps in the submerged ridgelines, it grinds against stone with a deep, resonant sound—low and constant, like a throat song from the earth itself. The locals, if there were any locals left to call them that, had a word for this sound. The word doesn't translate. It meant something like *the breathing of the old place*.

The light comes from below as often as above. The water carries a faint luminescence—bioluminescent algae, perhaps, or mineral deposits stirred up from the floor by current and current's slow friction with stone. At night the entire plateau glows from within, a blue-green murky glow that turns the submerged world into something between cavern and sea. During the brief hours of true darkness, the glow intensifies where water moves fastest, tracing the underwater channels in lines of cold fire.

Vegetation grows. This is the strange thing, the thing that sits uneasily in the mind: vegetation grows here where nothing else can, or will, or perhaps *chooses* not to. Pale forests rise from the flooded floor—trees with black bark and leaves that hang like torn cloth, their crowns reaching upward toward a light they will never see. They grow thick in places, forests going down and down until the dark takes them, and they grow in the shallows too, the submerged meadows where long fronds sway in the slow current. Ferns cling to the few surfaces that break the waterline. Moss covers the stone of the islands, thick and gray and damp. But nothing moves through these plants. Nothing stirs among them.

The islands themselves are few and scattered. Each one is a knob of dark rock, smoothed by time and water, perhaps fifty yards across at most, barely large enough to stand on. On some of them stand the remains of structures—round houses with thick walls, built of stacked stone, their roofs long collapsed. They are half-submerged on their waterward sides. They are not ruins exactly; they are more like things that were built to be slowly consumed, slowly returned to the water. The stone is fitted with a primitive precision, hand-cut and hand-placed, the work of patient labor. There are no metal implements among the remains, only worn wooden pegs and rope made from something fibrous, now swollen and dark with water.

The erosion here is deep and patient. Stone has been worn smooth at the waterline, crevices widened, sharp edges softened. The process has taken centuries at minimum. The plateau in its current drowned state is not young.

But it is not empty.

The water is alive with motion. Currents shift and interlock; pressure differentials cause small whirlpools to form and collapse; where the floor rises near the surface, water boils upward in white confusion. The sound is enormous and very specific—it is the sound of a place that is actively, continuously doing something. Of processes at work. Of a world in the middle of being itself.

And everywhere, in the forests below and along the current-swept channels, the plants move. Not from animal life—there is no animal life, not a fish, not an insect, not a grub. But the plants move. The fronds bend and sway as though stirred by breath. The pale trees flex in currents they should not be able to feel. There is a rhythm to it, a slow pulse, as though the water itself is alive, or the plants are breathing in concert, or something beneath the floor is dreaming and its dreaming stirs the water and the water stirs the plants and the plants make sounds like whispers in the drowning dark.

The islands are safe enough to stand on, but standing on them for long is uncomfortable. The structures are uninhabitable. The light is poor. The sound grinds on. Nothing eats anything here; nothing hunts. The ecosystem—if it can be called an ecosystem—is frozen at the level of pure production, pure growth, with no consumption to balance it. Everything is growing, always, everywhere, and there is no one to stop it, no one to thin it, no one to return what falls to the floor.

The water is rising, or the islands are sinking, or both. The margins of the islands are increasingly soft, increasingly returned. In a thousand years, in ten thousand, there may be no stone above the water at all. Just the drowned plateau, the dark forests, the slow grinding current, and the sound of the breathing of the old place, forever, until the water takes everything, until nothing is left but the green-glowing dark and the movement that is not movement and the silence that is not silence, the silence of a world where nothing lives and everything grows and the sound comes from the stone.
