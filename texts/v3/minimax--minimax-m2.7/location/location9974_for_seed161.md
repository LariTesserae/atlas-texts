# The Plateau of Worn Bones

At this altitude, the air is a thin, dry thing that slips past your lips without satisfying them. You breathe, but the breath never quite fills. The plateau rises from the distant lowlands like a broken tooth — not dramatic, not jagged, but *old*. So old that the peaks which once stood here have been ground down to wide, shallow domes of stone, their edges softened like river-polished glass. The highest points barely clear the clouds that gather below, and you understand looking up that you are standing on something that was once a mountain. Now it is merely a whisper of one, a plateau worn to its last honest shape.

The surface is stone — pale, mineral-heavy, porous in places where ancient water once seeped through and left behind trace caverns that never collapsed. It is not flat. It undulates in gentle swells and shallow basins, the geometry of long erosion, the way water and wind and ice have cooperated across epochs to make everything *smooth*. The corners of cliffs are rounded. The edges of the stone fields are beveled. Nothing here is sharp except where recent weather or the hands of the settlement have broken through the ancient patina.

The settlement clings to the plateau's southern face, in the lee of a ridge that blocks the worst of the wind. Here the plateau drops into a shallow ravine — or perhaps it is simply the way the old settlement was built, walls becoming cliffs becoming foundations becoming streets. The structures are stone, dry-stacked, fitted together with mud and the patience of generations. They are not tall. They do not need to be. The plateau offers no lumber, and timber is hauled up from below at great effort, so the buildings are low and compact, hugging the ground, their roofs flat so the wind slides over without purchase. Some are very old, their walls worn to half their original thickness by the constant abrasion of dust-laden wind. Others are newer, built over ruins, built into ruins, the stones of earlier houses used again by later hands.

There is moisture here, barely. The plateau catches fog — not rain, never rain, but the marine layer that rolls up from the unseen distant sea, thickening at this elevation into a clammy, persistent mist that beads on every surface and drips into stone channels cut for that purpose. The channels run between buildings, between fields of stacked rock that form the small terraced gardens where the people grow their food. The water is not enough. It is never enough. But the plateau has taught its people to be satisfied with very little, and the gardens are dense, the plants waxy-leafed, deep-rooted, engineered by generations of selection for drought and altitude.

Vegetation clusters where water gathers — in the ravines, in the crevices, along the channels, in the courtyards of the settlement where the old walls trap moisture against their feet. There are trees here, sparse and bent, their wood dense and dark, grown slowly over decades. There are bushes with leaves like horn, thick and slippery. There are vines that climb the stone walls and bloom briefly in the pre-dawn hours when the fog is thickest, their flowers pale and fleeting, already wilting by the time the sun crests the eastern ridge. The plateau is not barren, but it is not generous. Life is present, insistent, adapted — but always on the edge of something.

The light here is diluted. The altitude strips away the atmosphere's bulk, and the sun is fierce when it shines, but the plateau is often shrouded in the marine layer that rises from below, or in thin clouds that form at this exact height, or in the dust that the wind carries up from the lowlands. When the light comes, it is sharp and flat, casting shadows that are too crisp, colors that are too saturated. When the light is absent, the plateau is dim, blue-grey, the stone darkening to near-black, the settlement windows glowing faint with the warmth of fires that are always burning, for warmth and for light and for the cooking of food from the sparse gardens. The people here do not know darkness the way lowlanders do. They know *dimness*, a permanent greyness that is neither night nor day, a condition of the plateau itself.

The fauna is abundant — paradoxically, inexplicably, abundantly present. The plateau supports a species of small rodent that has learned to drink from the stone channels, its fur thick and grey to match the stone. Birds come — hawks and smaller things, nesting in the crevices of the old cliffs, their droppings feeding the plants that cling to the rocks. Insects are everywhere, the kind that thrive in dry air, their bodies narrow and dark, their hum a constant undercurrent to the louder wind. At night, something larger moves through the settlement — a cat-like creature, or a fox, or something between, something that has learned to live on scraps and rodents and the eggs of the cliff-nesting birds. The people do not hunt it. It is part of the plateau. They tolerate each other.

The sound of the plateau is the wind. Not screaming, not destructive, but *present* — a low, constant roar that changes pitch with the hour and the season, that speaks in the stone channels and the half-dead vines and the low walls of the gardens. When the wind dies, the silence is not silence; it is the sound of the settlement, of voices calling between buildings, of stone tools on stone, of the creak of old timber. The people here speak loudly, not rudely but necessarily, because the wind has trained them to, because the altitude makes them breathless and they have learned to say what they need to say before they run out of air.

The settlement is organized, old, layered. There are neighborhoods that were built in one era and neighborhoods that were built in another, their stones different, their layouts reflecting different needs, different technologies, different philosophies of how to build on a plateau that offers no room for expansion. The newest structures are the most carefully built, because the people know now how to work with the wind, how to shape stone to deflect it, how to dig channels to capture what water falls. The oldest structures are half-buried, their upper walls worn away, their foundations now the floors of new buildings, the archaeology of the plateau layered beneath your feet.

The plateau is slow. The wind changes slowly. The fog comes and goes with a patience that is not seasonal but tidal, rising and falling across months and years in a cycle too long for any one generation to perceive. The settlement does not grow. It does not shrink. It maintains itself, repairs itself, replaces itself stone by stone, generation by generation. The erosion that shaped the plateau has nearly finished its work; the stone here is stable now, ancient, settled into its final form. And the settlement has done the same — not frozen, but *slow*, changing at the pace of geology, patient in the way that the plateau itself is patient.

This is a place that should not be. The altitude is extreme, the water nearly absent, the wind constant, the light diluted. And yet the plateau holds its people, holds its plants, holds its small and stubborn ecosystem of adapted things. The stone is smooth and old and will be smooth and old long after the last building has been worn away. The fog will still rise. The channels will still fill. Something will still grow. The plateau does not care about survival; it simply *is*, and has been, and will be — patient, ancient, enough.
