# The Resin Plateau

The ground is not ground. It is a skin — a living membrane of compressed root-matter and fossilized bark stretched across the continental upland like the hide of some ancient and slumbering thing. Press a boot to it and it gives slightly, exhaling a thin vapor that smells of camphor and hot mineral. The surface is cool beneath the sun, always cool, even when the light falls bright and absolute across the plateau and the shadows shorten to nothing. The air here is thin at this elevation — almost three kilometers above whatever lies below — and it does not hold heat well. The warmth of the pale sun barely registers against the skin. Cool in the way that a river stone is cool, or a cave mouth, or a mouth that has been breathing slowly for a long time.

What grows here does not grow *on* the ground. The ground *is* growth — ancient, settled, dense with age. The organisms that compose it have been here so long that they have become geological. Their root networks run dozens of meters deep and interlock in strata that predate any reckoning of time. They have been weathered, compressed, saturated with their own resins until the plateau's surface is essentially a single continuous organism — not a forest, not a jungle, but a *field* of singular, continental life. Individual presences still rise above it, colossal, but they are set wide — separated by hundreds of meters, sometimes kilometers, of the root-skin. The density parameter is low, despite the vegetation's totality: what fills the space is not clumped but spread, distributed across the vastness like stones in a dry riverbed, each one immense and singular and leaving clear space around it.

The air smells like a factory that has never been built. It is the smell of chemistry happening without vessels: oxidation of saps, volatile compounds released from wounds in the bark-skin, the faint acidity of resins curing in the thin cold air. The plateau runs on something other than water. Water exists here only as trace humidity, as vapor bound in plant tissue, as occasional condensation that beads on resin-slick surfaces and vanishes before it can bead for long. The chemistry of this place is hydrocarbon-based — the plants have shifted over eons into something closer to petroleum than plasma. Their blood is tar. Their tears are amber. When they move, and they are always moving, slow peristaltic contractions of vascular tissue visible from a distance as broad ripples across the root-skin, the sound is the sound of something uncountable dragging itself forward through a landscape it has made by being alive in it.

The creatures are everywhere — abundant, though few are obvious. They are not animals in any sense that would be recognizable. They are motile growth: ambulatory tumors, ambulatory roots, colonies of specialized tissue that have evolved locomotion over millions of years. They range from the size of a dog to the size of a house. They move slowly, mostly. Their motion is the dominant sound of the plateau — a low, continuous, pervasive groaning, the creak of resin-heavy tissue bending under its own weight, punctuated by sharper sounds: the snap of a tendril retracting, the wet crack of a seed-pod opening and releasing its payload into the bright, thin, noisy air. Occasionally, larger specimens shift their mass and the sound carries for dozens of kilometers, a groan that crosses the plateau like a foghorn, answered moments later by another from the far horizon.

Weather exists here, but it is weather of a singular kind. The canopy — such as it is, these vast scattered organisms whose canopies rise and merge at altitude without ever quite touching below — generates its own systems. Thermal columns rise from the broad dark surface of the largest growths, carrying vapor, spores, resin-particles, and the creatures that live in the upper branches. These columns form and dissolve within hours, their cumulus-equivalents building and crumbling, painting the bright sky with slow grey calligraphy. Lightning arcs between two canopy-tops during the most turbulent cycles — not water-based storms but charge-differentials caused by the movement of chemical particles through the air. The plateau has weather of its own physics, its own rules, its own atmospherics born entirely from the metabolism of its living ground.

The light is bright here, and it is a particular light. It comes from above, from a sun that is not obstructed but is *filtered* — the resin-vapor in the air, the spore-clouds, the chemical haze that hangs perpetually over the plateau gives the light a amber-golden quality, soft-edged, omnipresent, casting no sharp shadows. The brightness is constant. The dark comes not from night — though the sun does set — but from the moving canopies overhead, which shift and rotate on slow hinges of vine and bark, opening and closing the light like eyelids.

What silence there is exists only in the small spaces — the hollows in the root-skin where a depression has been worn or grown into the living surface, or the lee of a great trunk where the sound is slightly less. But the base state is sound: the layered, full-spectrum sound of a place that is alive in every cubic meter of its volume. The groaning of slow-motion movement. The clicking and ticking of vascular pumps. The high thin whine of air moving through hollow structures in the smaller creatures. The vast low hum of the ground itself, the root-skin, the continental organism, the sound a planet makes when it is still growing.

Nothing is built here. Nothing has been made. The forms are entirely organic, entirely evolved, and yet they have the quality of architecture — arches of fused root, columns of heartwood, buttresses of bark that serve structural purpose and look like cathedrals. The difference is that no intelligence designed them, and yet they *work* as well as any cathedral, as well as any engineered structure, because this plateau has had ten million years to solve every structural problem, and the solutions are all made of tar and amber and heartwood and living root, and they are magnificent, and they are everywhere, and they are moving.
