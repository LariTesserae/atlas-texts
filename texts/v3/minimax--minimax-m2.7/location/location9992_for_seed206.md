# The Hollow Interior

You are inside something. Not inside a building — inside a *thing*, a presence, a space that is entirely organic and entirely itself.

The walls are green. Not painted green, not adorned green — the walls *are* green, and the floor is green, and the ceiling, such as it is, drapes down in green veils that sometimes brush your shoulders. The surface underfoot is spongy, yielding slightly with each step, warm to the touch as though the world itself runs with a mild fever. You cannot tell where root ends and stone begins because there is no stone here — only the pale, fibrous body of the plant that comprises this place, its surfaces weathered to a soft matte by years of condensation and slow, patient growth.

The moisture is ambient, pervasive. It settles on every surface as a fine mist, gathers in shallow pools in the low places where the living-floor has cupped itself, beads on the undersides of broad leaves that arch overhead like the vaulted ceiling of some enormous cathedral. The air tastes green — vegetal, mineral, alive. You breathe it in and it feels like drinking something. Water is not absent, then, but neither is it present as a thing in itself. It is a quality of the air, a condition of existence here. The weeping surfaces — those places where the plant-matter exudes moisture — are the only sounds of water, soft drips that mark time in an environment that otherwise seems to exist outside of time.

Because time, here, does not seem to pass. Or rather: time passes, but nothing changes. The moisture is the same moisture, the warmth the same warmth, the light the same pale green-gold filtering down through the photosynthetic canopy above. The low elevation ensures that nothing drains away — everything stays, accumulates, reaches an equilibrium and holds it. The centuries have done their work and found their answer. This pocket of world is complete.

Fauna persist here, but sparsely, as islands of motion in a green sea. Something moves in the upper reaches, climbing the broad midribs of leaves that could serve as highways. Something else clings to a weeping surface, drinking. Their sounds — small, deliberate, unhurried — compose the ambient noise of the place, a low murmur of life conducted in the usual languages of hunger and thirst and rest. The sounds do not layer or build; they simply occur, discrete, separated by silence.

There is a sound beneath the sound. A bass thrum, almost subliminal, that you feel more than hear — a vibration that seems to originate from deep within the plant-body itself, the slow mechanical process of living at this scale. It is not mechanical in the way of gears or pistons, but neither is it merely organic. The metabolism of the place operates with the patience and inevitability of industry, a process that has been running since before memory and will continue long after. It is the sound of an enormous thing being alive.

This is a pocket, a contained room, an interior. The scale is intimate even as the presence is total — you can reach out and touch the walls on both sides if you spread your arms, can trace the grain of the surface with your fingers. But the walls are not small. They are only close. You exist inside the body of a single organism, a single mind of chlorophyll and cellulose and slow, wet growth, and the scale of the thing is revealed only in the patience of its processes and the depth of its stillness.

You will leave this place changed, if leaving is possible. The moisture will have settled into your lungs, the warmth into your skin. You will carry the low hum with you for days.
