# The Rustfield Uplands

The sun is a physical weight here, pressing the plateau into submission. Heat rises from the cracked mineral flats in slow shimmers, warping the air into layers that fold and unfold upon themselves. The light falls harsh and direct at this altitude, throwing no shadows worth mentioning—just flat illuminations across a terrain that has no interest in depth.

The stone here is old. Not old in the way of mountains, which carry their age gracefully. This stone is old the way a scar is old—evidence of a wound that never healed. Erosion has carved channels through the rock face, smooth-walled arroyos and chimney-like spires that speak of water that once was, or perhaps water that will never be. The washes fill with fine ochre dust that rises in small cyclones when the afternoon thermals kick up, which is often. Everything is rust-colored here, or the grey of mineralite, or the deep burnt umber of oxide deposits. The palette of a world that used all its water cooling something, or cooling down from something.

There is no vegetation. There was never vegetation, or the last of it died so long ago that its carbon signature has been absorbed back into the stone. What clings to the shaded sides of rock formations is something else—pale fibrous mats, pale tendrils that might be fungal, might be something that predates the fungal kingdom entirely. It does not grow toward light. It grows toward heat.

And it moves.

The fauna of the Rustfield Uplands defies every rule you might carry with you from elsewhere. Dense, teeming, *overwhelming*—the air here is never empty, even when your eyes find nothing to track. Flights of angular shapes wheel against the haze at the plateau's edge. Things with too many legs climb the spires. Things with no legs at all pulse through the arroyos like living lava, their bodies adapted to metabolize heat directly, to extract water from the mineral lattice itself. They are not beautiful. They are not even coherent by some measures. The combination of high weirdness and high fauna means that here, in this place, the dividing line between organism and environment has been *renegotiated*. The creatures are as much made of the stone as of themselves. Some of them may not be entirely one thing or another.

And then there is the noise.

The machinery is here. No builders, no tenders, no purpose visible—but the machines persist. Industrial machinery half-buried in the flats, their geometries suggesting smelting, distillation, processes for a chemistry that has nothing to do with water or plants. The structures are vast, corroded husks, their smokestacks still exhaling slow plumes of amber vapor that catch the light like something between smoke and suspension. The sound they make is a deep industrial drone, punctuated by the hollow percussion of metal cooling, of processes cycling, of things that grind and hiss and breathe without lungs. The sound carries across the empty kilometers with a presence that should be impossible for a place this sparsely populated.

But the emptiness is not what it seems. Your sense of density adjusts. The volume between presences is vast, yes—but the presences themselves are dense in a way that resists counting. You are not alone in the Rustfield Uplands. You have never been alone. The plateau is occupied by something so thoroughly that your initial impression of void was simply your pattern-recognition failing to find a pattern it could name.

The weirdness is not an add-on. It is the substrate.

In the Rustfield Uplands, time does not pass. Time *settles*. The slow dynamic of this world means that change happens across durations that make the machinery look hasty. Rockforms shift over centuries. The creature populations expand and contract on cycles longer than civilizations. The amber vapor hangs in the upper atmosphere, thinning and thickening like the breathing of something too large to perceive. The light shifts from harsh white to something amber-tinted as the vapor layer thickens, then back again. Nothing fast happens here.

But the machinery keeps running. The creatures keep climbing. The dust keeps rising. And if you stay long enough—if you stop waiting for the world to make sense—you begin to notice the things that move at the edge of your vision, the shapes that are not quite shapes, the way the shadows fall at angles that don't correspond to the sun's position.

You begin to notice that the rust is not just oxide.

It moves.

Here, in the hot high emptiness, on a plateau that should not be able to support life but supports it in quantities that defy comprehension, the rules have been replaced with older rules. The machines are waiting for something. The creatures are the inheritance. The stone is remembering that it was once something else.

You are being observed. Not by anything with eyes. By the aggregate of presences, the weight of accumulated life, the machinery that has learned to count. You are being assessed in terms that would require new vocabulary to describe.

The dust settles.

The drone continues.

You stay.
