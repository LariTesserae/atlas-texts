The stones remember when they were mountains. That was long ago—before the long patience of wind and rain and the slow mineral dreaming of centuries upon centuries had worn them down to wide grey shoulders, to rounded ridges that rise and fall across the immensity like the breathing of something too large to see whole. The elevation here is not sharp. It is the elevation of a place that has settled into its final shape, that has chosen, after unfathomable time, to stop changing. The erosion is so complete that the land itself seems exhausted, worn down to a gentle agreement with gravity.

This is a vast place. Not merely large, but so large that weather forms in its lungs and travels its surface as a thing with weight and duration. Clouds pool in the basins between the ancient rounded ridges, and rain falls in one valley while another remains dry, and the rain is not always rain—the moisture here seems to remember other forms, condenses in patterns that don't quite follow the physics of simpler worlds.

The cold is constant but not violent. It is the cold of deep time, of stone that has forgotten warmth, of air that hangs still and damp without quite freezing. Moisture collects on surfaces. It beads on the grey stone and stands in pools in the shallow depressions and runs in thin threads down the faces of the old weathered rock, and it does not flow from anywhere or to anywhere—it appears and vanishes according to its own logic, according to pressures and consistencies that shift when observation is not fixed upon them.

The vegetation should not be this abundant. The ground is mostly bare stone and thin soil that has taken ten thousand years to accumulate, and yet clusters and forests of strange plants rise from it, plant-things with dark leaves and pale root-systems that grip the ancient rock as if they remember their purpose even if observers can no longer determine what that purpose is. They grow in patterns that suggest intention but resist interpretation—the lushness exists, undeniable, thick and dense in places, but the pattern of it is not quite the pattern of natural selection. It is as if the plants were placed here by something, arranged according to a plan that was conceived before the erosion had finished its work.

There are cities. They emerge from the pale stone and the strange vegetation like growths, like the land remembering that it was once capable of architecture. The buildings are low and heavy, constructed from the stone itself, fitted together with a precision that suggests tools but not metal—the tech level is primitive, hand-work, manual craft, but patient and accumulated over generations so that the walls are seamless, the roads worn smooth by feet that have traveled them for longer than memory. The buildings are not crowded. They are spaced according to some principle of density that treats emptiness as a resource, as necessary as the stone. Between the structures, the ancient rounded landscape continues, and within the structures, the ancient landscape continues, and it is sometimes difficult to tell where the city ends and the land begins.

No animals move here. The silence is not the silence of potential sound but the silence of absence. The plants sway, but nothing moves among them to notice the swaying. The water collects, but nothing drinks from it. The cities stand, but no feet walk their roads—or if feet do walk them, the walkers leave no tracks, make no sound, cast no shadows that quite correspond to what shadow should be. The quiet is so complete that the mind begins to hear things: distant frequencies just below hearing, the suggestion of a voice in a room when the room is empty, the sound of something breathing when the lungs have long stopped.

And the weirdness is very high.

The edges of things do not hold. Where the water pools, the reflection sometimes shows a sky that is not the sky above. Where the plants grow, their roots disappear into stone that has no bottom. Where the buildings meet the ground, the angle of intersection is not quite stable—it shifts when viewed from different positions, as if the walls are leaning inward or the ground is tilting, though measurement confirms that both are level. The light falls as light should fall, but the shadows it creates lean in unexpected directions, point toward corners where no corner exists, and sometimes, in the deep places between the sparse settlements, the light itself seems to hesitate, to stutter, to arrive a moment before it was emitted.

The buildings are occupied. This is certain. Something moves behind the seamless walls. Something tends the strange plants, or perhaps the strange plants tend something in return. Something walks the worn roads and opens doors that hang in the air and looks out through windows that do not quite match the interior geometry. But the something does not quite interact with the visitors. It goes about its business in parallel, in a state of near-contact that never achieves the friction of contact. And sometimes, when the eye moves too quickly or the mind relaxes its hold on the visible, the something and the visitors are revealed to be made of the same substance, to be aspects of the same presence that has not yet decided what it is.

This is a place at the edge of consensus. The cold and the vastness and the ancient erosion have worn away whatever kept reality coherent, have worn away to the place where things are still being worn away, where the fabric of what-is has thinned to translucence. The cities were built by whoever was here before, or by whoever will come after, or by the land itself remembering a future it has not yet experienced. The plants grew because growth is possible here, in the damp and the cold, because life is stubborn and strange and will grow almost anywhere, will grow even in the spaces between the real and the unreal. The water appears because water is patient and will seep through any boundary, will find its level in any world, will pool in the low places of any reality that will hold it.

The silence is not empty. It is full of the sound of something very large, very old, very patient, breathing just below the threshold of audibility. The city-builders knew this sound. They built their low heavy structures to withstand it, fitted their stones so precisely because precision was necessary, because the world needed to be held together with intention when the natural forces had given up. The plants grow toward the sound, their roots drinking from the deep places where the sound originates, their leaves catching the light that stutters, their dark vegetation feeding on whatever the weirdness provides.

It is cold here, and vast, and ancient, and wrong. But it is also settled, in its way. It has achieved a balance that is not quite stable but that has lasted long enough to be called home, to be called a place, to be mapped and measured and walked by visitors who cannot quite remember leaving and cannot quite remember arriving. The roads lead somewhere. The doors open onto rooms. The water can be drunk, though it tastes like a question. The plants can be touched, though they feel like a memory of touch. The cities can be entered, though the cities do not quite agree on how many stories they contain, on which direction the stairs go, on whether the windows look out or in.

This is a landscape that has survived its own strangeness. It persists. It endures. The weather forms and reforms above it. The moisture condenses and evaporates according to rules that are only approximately the rules of simpler worlds. The vegetation is lush despite the cold, and the settlements are urban despite the primitive tools, and the sound is mostly quiet despite the breathing of whatever lives beneath the stone, beneath the roots, beneath the worn smooth roads that lead to places that have no names in any language but that are nonetheless destinations, nonetheless places where something is waiting, has always been waiting, will always be waiting for visitors who almost arrive, who almost see, who almost remember what they came here to find.
