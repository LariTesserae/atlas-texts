# The City That Weeps

You arrive, or perhaps you have always been here — it is difficult to say, because the land offers no reference for arrival. No road winds inward to meet you. No gate stands open. You simply find yourself in a place that has been exactly this way for longer than memory, and will be exactly this way long after.

The city is built into the flank of an immense volcanic tableland, and it climbs — terrace upon terrace of fitted stone rising toward a sky that hangs low and gray and swollen with moisture it cannot release. The elevation is staggering. You can feel it in the pressure behind your eyes, in the way each breath requires a fraction more effort than it should. Far below, the land drops away into cloud, and above, the cliffs continue upward into mist. You are somewhere in the middle of something vast, and the middle is all there is.

Water is the first fact of the place. Not rain — the sky has never moved to release anything, not in any time that matters. The water comes from the stone itself, pressed out through millennia of volcanic rock like juice from a pulp. The cliff faces weep. Every surface in the city weeps. Moisture beads on granite and runs in threads down walls that have been smoothed by this same unending seepage until they shine like dark glass. In the lower terraces, water pools in courtyards between buildings, forming shallow, still sheets that reflect the overcast sky without ripple or wind-ripple or any disturbance whatsoever, because the air has never moved. The water simply sits, warm to the touch, opaque with a faint mineral white, like broth or like milk. It seeps up from below, too — from the infrastructure beneath the streets, the old channels and cisterns that no longer serve any purpose but still function, still carry water upward through the city's bones by pressure and capillary action, feeding pools that have no drain and no need for one, because nothing evaporates and nothing diminishes. The humidity is total. Your clothes do not dry. Your skin does not cool. The moisture does not condense into rain. It simply remains, a saturation so complete it feels like a second atmosphere, thick and warm and somehow visible, a pale haze that blurs the farther terraces into one another until the city becomes a vertical dream.

Lush is the wrong word for the vegetation, because *lush* implies growth, implies reaching, implies the passage of seasons. Here, the green does not grow so much as it *occupies*. It is everywhere, and it has always been everywhere, and it will never be anywhere else. Ferns sprout from every crack in the stonework, their fronds uncurling into shapes that suggest they have been uncurling for centuries and will never finish. Moss coats every horizontal surface in thick, velvety cushions of deep emerald and gray-green, climbing the walls in slow tide-marks, covering the roofs in a single continuous pelt. Epiphytes hang from every overhang — orchids with pale, waxy blooms that do not open or close with any rhythm, because there is no rhythm; air plants with rosettes of silver-green leaves that catch the light and hold it, their roots gripping the stone with a patience that suggests purpose but admits no purpose you can name. Vines run along every ledge, thick as wrists, their leaves broad and dark and faintly luminescent in the gray light. The vegetation does not smell green. It smells warm and wet and mineral, like wet stone in a greenhouse, like something alive but alien to the concept of life as you understood it before you came here.

The built environment is dense. The city was not planned for beauty; it was planned for water management, and the architecture follows the logic of water with a completeness that borders on obsession. Streets are narrow, barely more than channels, and they are carved with shallow drainage gutters that carry trickling water toward collection basins. Buildings press against one another with no alley between them, their walls shared, their shadows merged. Every roof pitches inward, channeling rainwater — or, here, channeling the condensation that never becomes rain — toward cisterns that are now simply open pools in courtyards or interior rooms, still lined with the same mosaic tile in blue and white and ochre, still fed by the same gravity-fed system from springs higher on the cliff. The stone is dark volcanic rock, fitted with a precision that admits no gap wider than a finger, and the joints have been sealed over centuries of seepage with mineral deposits that gleam like amber in the flat light. Windows are few and high, slotted for light and airflow that never comes. Doorways are low, requiring you to duck, as though the city's builders expected everything to remain inside and nothing to pass through.

And the silence is not silence. The sound is deafening. It is the sound of water — everywhere, from every direction, layered and contradictory. A vast, low, continuous murmur rises from the cliffs behind the city, the sound of water moving through rock in channels you cannot see, a sound like a river heard from very far away but also very close, a sound with no single source and no single direction. Against this, the city adds its own counterpoint: the drip and trickle of water emerging from every wall, every joint, every crack, each drop falling into a pool or a gutter or a puddle with a note that is almost musical but slightly wrong, slightly flat, as though the water here is denser than it should be. The sound accumulates. The drips overlap and blur into a wet, roaring whisper that fills the streets and the plazas and the interiors of buildings, that presses against your ears with a physical weight. You shout, and your voice is swallowed instantly, not by absorption but by replacement, the water-sound filling every gap your words leave. It is not unpleasant. It is not anything. It is simply what the place is, the way color is what light is. You cannot have the city without the sound, because the sound is the city's breathing, and the city breathes without stopping.

But here is the strangeness, the thing that makes your thoughts slow and catch and begin to slide sideways: nothing moves. You understood, from the sound, that the water was active — that water was falling, running, dripping, moving. But it is not. The water in the pools is still. The water in the channels is still. The water emerging from the walls is caught in the instant of emergence, beaded and trembling, a bead of water on a stone surface that hangs motionless, and when it falls, it falls so slowly that you can watch its descent across what would be a meter of air, and this fall takes minutes, and when it arrives at the pool below, the surface does not break, does not ripple, receives it the way a solid receives an impression. There is no wind. There are no birds. There are no insects. The orchids are not pollinated because there is nothing to pollinate them, and yet they bloom, their pale flowers open to a light that does not change because the sky does not change, because time here has reached equilibrium and stopped caring about motion.

The city has been abandoned by anything that could leave. The buildings stand furnished. You can see, through low windows, rooms with beds and tables and ceramics on shelves, with water-stained books on tables, with woven textiles rotting gently on wooden frames, with food left in bowls that has dried to something that is not mold and not dust but some third thing, some substance that has stopped midway through becoming anything at all. Stairs climb between terraces in long, worn flights, and at their landings, you find marketplaces — stalls of stone, their counters covered in the same mineral white as the pools, and behind the counters, goods: baskets of fruit that are not rotted and not fresh but simply *present*, preserved by the saturation in a way that admits no decay and no growth, fruit that is a fact about fruit rather than fruit itself. Textiles hang in doorways and flutter not at all. A loom stands in a workshop, its threads still threaded, its shuttle in place, its work a fabric that has neither grown nor stopped because it was never in time to begin with.

You walk, and the city lets you walk, and the walk is the same at every street because the street is the same at every point, not literally identical but *functionally* identical, as though the city's function has replaced its variation, as though every corridor is the same corridor, every courtyard the same courtyard, every pool of still white water the same pool in which you see your own reflection moving when nothing else moves, your reflection moving with a fluidity that seems, by contrast, violent, obscene, a violation of the place's deep and patient stillness.

And the weirdness — this is hard to hold in language. The geometry is correct. The walls are vertical. The pools are horizontal. The stairs rise and do not curve back on themselves. And yet there is a quality to the space that makes certainty feel aggressive, that makes you question whether you are moving through the city or the city is moving through you, that makes the light — the flat, even, directionless light that falls from nowhere and illuminates everything equally without casting a shadow worth mentioning — seem less like illumination and more like a *description* of illumination, a memory of what light was before it remembered itself.

You sit by a pool. The water is warm. The sound of the city's water fills you. You wait for the light to change, for the sky to shift, for the water to ripple. Nothing changes. Nothing will change. You are in the place where water comes from stone and plants occupy everything and the sound is deafening and nothing moves, and the place does not care that you are there, and it will not care when you are gone, because gone and here are not different distances from anything in a place that has settled into its own equilibrium so completely that it has become the equilibrium, the only thing left, the wet and silent and terrible and beautiful fact of a city that was built for water and has become water's dream of itself.
