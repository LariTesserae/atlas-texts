The plateau exists before weather. It has its own.

The air here carries more moisture than altitude should permit — a permanent haze that clings to the flat-topped massifs and their sharp, uneroded faces. Water seeps from every joint in the dark basalt: weeping faces on cliff walls, thin sheets sliding over ledges, pooling in the fresh-sharp gullies that haven't yet softened. The moisture condenses on cold surfaces, beads on engineered stone, gathers in channels cut by nobody's hand but running as though they had always run. Frost forms at night and vanishes by midmorning, leaving the rock slick and the engineered walkways gleaming. The air smells of ice and metal.

Vegetation clings where water meets stone — not forest but lush in the way of high places: dense mosses in every hollow, hardy sedges in the shallow soils that have had no time to thicken, strange creeping plants with silver leaves that catch the thin light. Nothing here has reached tree-scale. Everything is ground-hugging, mat-forming, adapted to proximity with ice. The green is startling against the dark rock and the grey sky. It grows without grazing. Nothing eats it.

The installations are few and enormous. You see them from a hundred kilometers: vast flat-roofed complexes of black glass and grey steel, their smokestacks trailing white plumes that dissolve upward into the haze. Between them: nothing. The plateau's interior is empty in the way that outer space is empty — presences separated by distances that make the presence of each one feel like a hallucination. A smelting complex here. A water-processing facility there, its holding tanks gleaming in ranks like the architecture of a city that never learned to be dense. Pipelines thread between them, above ground, uninsulated, ice forming on their outer shells where the steam inside meets the cold. They run for hundreds of kilometers in straight lines across the flat stone, connecting nothing to everything.

The machinery hums. Not in pulses — in a continuous tone, a low register that sits in the bones of your chest cavity, audible in the silence between the wind gusts. Steam vents somewhere beneath the nearest complex hiss in harmonics with it. The wind moves in long, stable arcs: a gradient system that never changes, because the plateau is too vast and the air too still for fronts to form and dissolve. The weather here is a fixed condition. It has always been the same: cold, wet, still, dim with haze and the light of a sun that seems perpetually low.

Nothing stirs. The moss grows — imperceptibly, by geological standards, by industrial standards not at all, but it grows. The water runs. The machinery turns. The tone persists. The haze thickens and thins by turns that are neither day nor season but some deeper rhythm, the plateau breathing moisture from a deep aquifer or an ancient sea beneath the stone. The rock faces weep. The pipelines freeze and thaw and freeze again. Nothing changes.

You can walk for a day in any direction and find nothing built. In every direction the same fresh-sharp landscape, the same weep-water, the same silver-leaved moss, the same low perpetual hum. The installations are fixed points — the way stars are fixed points, the way mountains are fixed points, except these burn fuel and exhale steam and sit in arrangements that are not geological but are not random either. They form a pattern. You cannot see it from the inside.

At night: stars, visible through the haze. The machinery tone drops lower, almost below hearing, until you feel it more than know it — a vibration in the stone beneath your feet, in the steel beneath your palms if you touch a railing, in the cold wet air that will not move. The complexes go dark except for a few amber lights at their margins. The weep-water keeps running. The frost forms, and in the morning will vanish, and the water will run, and the plants will hold their green in the cold, and nothing anywhere will be other than it is.
