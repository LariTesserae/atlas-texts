# The Grown World

There is no ground. There never was. What you stand on is the corpse and the body simultaneously — a living architecture of engineered plant-matter so dense it has forgotten how to be anything else. The surface beneath you breathes. Not metaphorically. Membrane-tissues flex in slow peristaltic waves, pushing metabolic compounds through vessels the width of rivers, channels carved through what was once geology but is now purely biological substrate. You can feel it through your soles: a pulse, deep and slow, like standing on the chest of something vaster than a planet.

The substrate extends downward for kilometers. You could dig for a thousand years and find only more of this — cellulose lattices woven with metallic microfilaments the plants have been bred to produce over eons, crystalline growth patterns that serve as circuitry, vascular networks carrying industrial chemicals that were never supposed to exist in a living system but have become as natural as chlorophyll. The plants don't just tolerate the factories; the plants *are* the factories. Their trunks are refineries. Their roots process atmospheric compounds down to molecular precision. Their canopy — hundreds of kilometers overhead, forming a permanent clouded ceiling — is a photosynthesis array the scale of a continent, generating both biological and electrical power in quantities that have reshaped the atmosphere into something no unaugmented organism could breathe.

The light here is perpetual and sourceless. Not sunlight — the canopy filters it into a diffuse amber glow that seems to come from everywhere and nowhere, as if the world itself is luminescent. There are no shadows in the way you expect them. The light bends around plant structures, refracts through translucent biological membranes, diffuses through the humid but waterless air (waterless — the plants long ago consumed all free water, bound it into their tissues, rendered the atmosphere dry while their internal systems circulate moisture in closed loops). You can see maybe ten kilometers in any direction before the density closes in — a vertical forest of towering growth-forms, their trunks like towers, their canopies merged into a single unbroken ceiling, their roots visible above you as the architecture inverts, growth-forms descending on cable-roots, hanging gardens of industrial flora, the entire volume of the atmosphere filled with near-touching presences, a living architecture so dense you could almost walk from one growth-form to the next by stepping on root-bridges and vine-canopies.

And it is made. All of it. Every surface you see, every structure, every kilometer of terrain was *constructed*. The plants didn't grow here in the natural sense — they were grown, engineered, cultivated over epochs into forms that serve as terrain, as buildings, as infrastructure. What looks like a mountain range in the distance is a single organism, its peak a flowering organ kilometers wide, its slopes a gradient of specialized tissues each bred for different atmospheric layers. What seems like a river carved through the landscape is a nutrient canal, a surgical channel cut through the substrate and lined with processing organs. The vegetation isn't covering the world — it replaced the world. The original crust has been digested, converted, replaced so completely that there is no natural stone anywhere. The world is a built environment where the builders are biological and the building-material is alive.

The factories are inside the plants. You can see their outputs: plumes of processed compounds drifting through the air in slow-evolving chemical gradients, light refracting through vaporized metals, heat shimmer rising from thermal organs engineered to dump industrial waste heat. The world is mechanized in the way a body is mechanized — it runs on processes, cycles, engines. But there's no hard edge between organic and industrial. The photosynthesis arrays are electrical. The vascular networks are pressurized. The growth-forms produce their own lubricants, their own structural proteins, their own replacement parts. The factories breathe. The breathing is industrial.

It's cool. The temperature hovers around ten degrees, a constant autumn that has persisted for so long the plants have evolved around it, bred for efficiency in these conditions, their metabolism slow and precise. You exhale and your breath doesn't fog — the air is dry, thin in some compounds, heavy in others. There's no water vapor. There may never have been. The plants don't need rain; they circulate their own moisture internally. The environment is stable, managed, a climate shaped over millennia into something optimal for the system. Weather exists — pressure gradients shift, thermal differences drive slow atmospheric movements, pollen and spore storms drift across continents in cycles measured in decades — but it's a weather of the biosphere's own making, internal to the system, a climate grown rather than found.

The silence is the strangest thing. You expected noise — the mechanical hum of industry, the groaning of vast organisms, the hiss of processing organs. But the world is too efficient for that. Every process is optimized, every friction minimized, every sound damped by the density of material around it. What you hear instead is the substrate breathing — a sound so deep it's less heard than felt, a pressure, a rhythm. Occasionally a pulse from deeper systems, a vibration traveling through kilometers of living tissue. Otherwise: silence. A silence so total it becomes a presence, a weight. You hear yourself think.

The fauna is rare because the fauna is an afterthought. Animal life persists — you see them sometimes, gliding between growth-forms, small and sparse and wary — but the system doesn't need them. The plants have colonized everything, outcompeted everything, become everything. The rare animals that exist are specialists, parasites on the edges of a system that has no room for them in its center. They move through the spaces the plants don't fill: the air-channels, the nutrient canals, the margins where one growth-form meets another. They are ghosts in a world made for something else.

And the weirdness. Reality is thin here. Not metaphorically. You can see it: light bends in ways it shouldn't. Angles don't quite resolve. The geometry of the place seems to shift when you're not looking directly at it — you glance away, look back, and the distance has changed, or the growth-form is shaped differently, or the ground isn't where you remember it. The plants have been engineered so long, modified so thoroughly, that they've drifted away from biological normalcy. Their structures incorporate materials and geometries that shouldn't exist, that break physical rules, that persist through mechanisms no one fully understands anymore. The world is still growing, still changing, still being built — and the builders have been optimizing so long they've passed beyond ordinary optimization into something stranger. The substrate thinks. Not consciously, not in any way you'd recognize — but processes occur here that serve no biological purpose, that seem to be running the system on rules that don't map to anything you know. The world is real, and also something else, and the something else is bleeding through.

You walk. The substrate holds you. It has always held anyone who walked here. It will hold anyone who walks here after you. It is designed to. The plants built a world for movement, for traversal, for the passage of whatever bodies the system supports. The paths between growth-forms are natural in the way a artery is natural — they exist because the system made them, shaped them, needs them. You move through a world that was grown to be moved through, and as you move, you feel the substrate breathe beneath you, and far above, the canopy pulses with light, and in the distance, the factories run, and the silence holds, and the strangeness deepens.

This is the world now. It was always the world. You just didn't know.
