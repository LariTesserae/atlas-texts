# The Plateau of Still Water

The stone is new. Not in the way of freshly quarried blocks laid into wall or foundation, but geological new—the rock has not yet learned to be smooth. Sharp faces of dark basalt break the surface where the earth shrugged itself into being, angular and unworn. The plateau sits high enough that the wind finds it without obstruction, sliding across the open ground with a persistence that has not carved the stone into patience, not yet. There is no patience here. Only the slow arithmetic of erosion that has just begun its long accounting.

The city climbs and sprawls across this young plateau in a way that speaks of deliberate spacing, of choosing. Stone buildings rise in clusters, separated by stretches of ground where thick moss holds moisture like a secret. The structures themselves are built by hand and hammer, mortared joints hand-troweled, roof beams hewn from timber that grows in the sheltered valleys below. You can see the labor in them. You can see the tool marks. The settlement knows this: that it is built from what the land gives, and gives nothing back that the land did not already contain.

Water gathers here in the way water always has, since the plateau first broke through whatever it broke through. Small basins of compacted stone hold moisture that does not evaporate as quickly as it should, fed by springs that issue from the young rock's cracks with a steadiness that has not varied since the first measurement was taken—or if there was no first measurement, then since the first memory. The basins are not lakes. They are not ponds. They are places where the ground says *stay*, where water rests and moss thickens and certain hardy plants grow with the particular lushness of species that have learned to call this altitude home. Between these wet places, the plateau is merely cool and sparse, the stones protruding through skin of lichen like knuckles through thin leather.

It is a lush place, but the lushness is specific and localized. Ferns crowd the north faces of walls where the wind cannot reach. Moss climbs the stone to a height of two hands and then stops, as if obeying a mark. Small flowers bloom in the basin-marshes—blue and silver, closing their petals at night with the same timing they used a thousand years ago, with the timing they will use a thousand years hence. The air smells of stone and moisture and the particular green of plants that have accepted what they are.

The animals do not migrate. This is the fact that becomes strange only when you stay long enough to notice it: the birds that are present in summer are present in winter, not because they are the same individuals but because their kind has never known to do otherwise. Insects emerge in their season and die in their season and are replaced by the next kind in the next season, and the sequence has no variance. Small creatures move through the city with the confidence of animals for whom the world has always been exactly this shape. They do not startle. They do not adapt. They simply are, in the way that the stone simply is, in the way that the water simply is.

Sound here is an event, not a constant. The wind makes its sound. The water in the basins makes its sound—a small, continuous speaking that does not rise or fall with rainfall or drought because there has never been drought, because the balance was found so early that it has never needed to shift. Footsteps on stone make sound. Voices make sound. But between these things, the silence holds, and the silence is not empty so much as full of a patience that does not know it is waiting.

You could walk the plateau for days and find the same moss growing to the same height, the same water at the same level, the same birds in the same places doing the same things at the same hours. There is no growing used up here. No season that depletes or renews. The flowers that bloom in spring bloom when the stone reaches the temperature that triggers blooming, which is always now, which is always this moment that the plateau has been in since the moment it began.

This is not a dead place. The evidence of life is everywhere—in the plants and animals, in the city built by human hands, in the smoke rising from chimneys, in the small sounds of craft and labor. But it is a place where life has finished its negotiation with the land and the terms have been settled, and the settlement holds. The plateau does not change. The water does not change. The creatures who live here do not change, not because they cannot but because the equilibrium does not require them to, and so they simply continue, in the way that water continues to gather in the basins, in the way that moss continues to climb the walls, in the way that the wind continues to slide across the young stone with its sound that is no sound until it meets something that can hear.
