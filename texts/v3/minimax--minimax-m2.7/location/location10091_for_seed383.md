This erosion is patient and mineral, a smoothing that took epochs, as if the world itself spent eons rubbing down every sharp thing until even the concept of corners began to dissolve. The walls curve into each other. The floor flows. Every surface has the texture of a river stone held too long in a palm.

Moisture moves through this place without rain — it seeps in from stone above, condenses in the deep cool, gathers in pools that seem to appear and vanish according to logic no longer remembered. The pools are dark and still and perfectly still, and then gone. The damp never fully leaves but never accumulates either. It is the damp of breath on a cold glass, of fog in a basement, of the moment before rain when the air forgets itself.

There is no plant life. Nothing green, nothing rooted, nothing that photosynthesizes or competes for light in the way of living things. Instead there are fungi — vast mats of them, pale and phosphorescent, growing in sheets across the stone where moisture collects. They glow with a cold blue-white light, the way certain deep-sea things glow, and they are bright enough to see by, which is perhaps why the parameter reads as bright even underground. The fungi are not vegetation. They are something simpler and older, and they pulse faintly when touched, and they taste like nothing because nothing has tried to eat them in longer than the erosion took to smooth the stone.

The creatures here are abundant but sparse — a contradiction the landscape holds without effort. They move through the fungal light in slow, strange ways. Many have no eyes. Many more have too many. They communicate in pressures and chemical trails, in gestures that predate language, and the settlement built into this place does the same. The city — and it is a city, settlement-scale, organized enough to have districts and shared infrastructure — was carved by hands that worked with simple tools, pre-metal, antler and obsidian and the patient scraping of stone on stone. The architecture is worn now too, smoothed by the same ancient erosion that flattened the bedrock, so the handholds and footholds that once guided movement have become suggestions, mere impressions in the curve of what was once a doorway.

The people who live here are adapted in ways that would be called strange by any surface standard. They are cool to the touch. They do not shiver. Their eyes are large and dark and gather the fungal light with an efficiency that borders on predatory, and they move through the tunnels with the same patient unhurried calm as the water, as the erosion, as the slow settling of a world into its final form.

But it has not settled. The turbulence is there if you know where to look — the way the tunnels seem to shift in memory, the way buildings that should connect open instead into chambers that weren't there before, the way the water that appears and vanishes has never been mapped and cannot be predicted. Something is always being built here. Something is always being unmade. The people have learned not to leave things they cannot carry, not to finish what cannot be abandoned, to live in the in-between of construction and ruin as if it were the only mode available.

And it is. It has been for so long that the distinction between the built world and the carved world no longer exists, and the people themselves have become part of the erosion — slow, patient, smoothing the sharp things, forgetting the corners, until even memory begins to curve.
