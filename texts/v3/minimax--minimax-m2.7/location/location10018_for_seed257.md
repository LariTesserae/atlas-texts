The water is warm. Not quite body-temperature, but close enough that it feels alive — a warmth that comes from depth, from pressure, from the accumulated heat of a world that has had time to settle into itself.

You stand in a space that was built. You know this because the angles are wrong for anything that grew. Walls rise in geometric planes, surfaces cut and fitted with a precision that speaks of hands and tools, of stone worked before metal, of rope and leverage and the slow patience of early craft. But the builders are gone, and what they made has spent longer underwater than it ever spent in air. The walls are smooth now — not from the water alone but from the water's passengers, the endless procession of small bodies that have clung and grazed and died and beengetter over generations beyond counting. The surfaces are layered. Life on life on the memory of life. The stone remembers being shaped but cannot show it; it has become a palimpsest of erosion and growth, unreadable except in the broadest strokes.

The space is small — cathedral-scaled, but a cathedral designed for a congregation of fish. Vaulted ceilings arch overhead, their underside colonized by clusters of things that drift and pulse with the faintest bioluminescence. The light is not light, not truly. It is an almost-nothing, a rumor of brightness that reveals only that darkness goes deeper. You can see shapes in it: the slow arc of a ray's wing, the sudden dart of something tubular and silver, the patient crawl of creatures whose forms you have no names for. They are everywhere. They are near-touching. They move in rhythms so slow you cannot perceive individual motions — only the sense that the space is alive, that it breathes with a respiration measured in centuries.

The water enters from below, rises through channels carved for drainage or irrigation by hands that understood only the surface world. It arrives warm, carrying the salinity of a sea that has been here so long it has forgotten it was ever anything else. Where it pools in the carved basins and channels, the creatures cluster thicker. The walls are slick with them — films and tufts and clusters, creatures that attach and creatures that drift, the layered ecology of a place that has had time to pack itself full of every possibility of life.

No sound, or almost none. The water is too dense, too still, for the turbulence that makes noise. What reaches you is subsonic — pressure more than sound, the slow pulse of the tide that does not come from any shore. Your own breathing, underwater, is a whisper of bubbles that the dark swallows instantly.

The builders who made this place worked with simple tools. Their carvings are crude by later standards — a temple or a granary or a way-station, built to last a human lifetime and forgotten for a hundred thousand. The erosion here is total. Nothing retains its original edge. Corners have softened. Surfaces have become organic. The space reads now as cave or hollow, as something that grew rather than something that was made. Only the angles give it away — the straight lines that water cannot invent, the right angles that growth cannot quite mimic.

But the angles are worn. The walls are soft with life. The space is so dense with creatures that you cannot see the stone beneath them, only the faint suggestion of what lies beneath through the pressure of bodies against bodies, the density of presence so high that the water itself seems crowded.

You are the only thing in this space that is not native to it. Even your thoughts feel like intrusions, brief and sharp where everything else is slow and patient and old. The creatures do not flee from you — they have no concept of you, have never known anything that moved this fast or breathed this wrong. They drift past your light like debris, like sediment, like part of the landscape.

You are the only thing that changes.

And you are changing so slowly that you might not notice.
