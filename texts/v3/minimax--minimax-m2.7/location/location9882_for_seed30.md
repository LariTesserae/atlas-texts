The plateau breathes.

Not metaphorically — the surface itself exhales, a slow tidal pulse that moves through soft sedimentary stone barely hardened from its geological infancy. The rock here is new in a way that resists comprehension: layers that should have taken millions of years to form exist in strata days old, or hours, or still being compressed from whatever process birthed this place. Your feet sink slightly with each step, leaving prints in material that holds them like wet clay, then slowly refills as you lift your weight. The erosion index reads nearly zero because erosion hasn't started yet — this landscape hasn't finished being made.

Water comes from everywhere and nowhere. It condenses on the stone surfaces in fine films that bead and merge, running down vertical faces in thin sheets that glisten in the dim light. The air is saturated without being rain; a persistent mist hangs knee-high across the plateau, diffusing what little illumination reaches here from a sun that seems perpetually filtered through something vast and particulate. Pools gather in the shallow dips of this small bounded terrain — a place maybe two hundred meters across, edged by cliffs that drop into cloud below — but the water doesn't sit still. It moves. It cycles. Ponds appear and drain. Springs open and seal. The moisture is a system in motion, and the rock, still soft enough to be worked by such small forces, records the paths.

Sparse is the wrong word for the vegetation. *Attempted* fits better. Thin mats of something green-grey grow in the crevices, clinging to what little purchase the stone offers. They photosynthesize at angles, leaves turned to catch the diffuse light, and they are wet constantly — not from rain but from the ambient saturation that feeds them through surfaces, through the air itself. Between the growth patches, the stone is bare and strange, patterned with crystalline formations that might be mineral and might be something else, their geometry too clean for natural formation at this early stage.

The fauna announces itself first by sound.

Calls that don't resolve into familiar shapes. Something between a tone and a voice, pitched low, sustained, overlapping with others until the plateau has a constant harmonic texture — not unpleasant, but strange, like hearing a choir sing in a language that hasn't been invented yet. Creatures move through the mist: elongated forms that fold and unfold as they navigate, their surfaces catching the dim light with an iridescence that suggests chitin, or scales, or something engineered to manipulate optics. They hunt, or graze, or simply move — the dynamic parameter demands that multiple processes run simultaneously, and the plateau delivers. Something chases something else through the taller growth. Something else sings. Something burrows. The population density is high for this small space, and the creatures don't avoid you so much as they navigate around you, their sensory apparatus cataloging your presence and filing it as irrelevant.

Built structures exist here, but barely. Three or four small geometries rise from the plateau surface — a ring, a column, a flat platform — and they read as *placed*, not grown. Their surfaces are too smooth, their angles too precise for the soft stone around them, and they bear marks that might be writing or might be wear patterns. One of them is partially buried; another seems to have sunk slightly, as if the rock beneath it is still compacting. They don't glow. They don't hum. They simply exist, artifacts of something that was here, or is still here, or exists partially across some dimension the tech parameter demands. The scale of the tech is beyond modern — nanoscale, cosmic, engineering current physics doesn't fully describe — and so the structures might be anything: tools, markers, seeds, doors that open onto conditions humans haven't encountered.

The light does something strange here. It falls in sheets, and the sheets have weight. You can see them move through the mist, visible beams of something more than photons, something that carries information or charge or life. When a creature passes through a beam, its iridescent surface flares briefly, patterns rippling across its body as if data streams through it. The light is dim — not darkness, but twilight, the quality of illumination just before stars become necessary — and the beams give it structure, make the dimness itself a medium.

The plateau is bounded. You can see its edges where the cliffs drop away into cloud, and beyond the cloud there is only suggestion: that this place floats, or hovers, or is one feature among many, part of a larger system whose logic remains hidden. The air above you moves in patterns, thin streamers of something that might be vapor or might be exhaust or might be the exhalation of processes you lack the vocabulary to name. The temperature is warm but not oppressive — a sustained warmth that seems to come from below, from the stone itself, as if the geological processes here still carry their original heat.

You could stand in the center of this plateau and see everything it contains. The pools, the growth, the structures, the fauna in motion, the light in sheets, the soft stone underfoot still remembering its formation. It's a room. A cathedral. A single feature in something larger that chose, for reasons unknown, to build here, now, in this particular configuration of moisture and warmth and emerging geology and beyond-modern tech that hums just at the edge of perception. The plateau breathes. The mist cycles. The creatures sing their non-language. And you are here, in a place that hasn't finished becoming itself, surrounded by abundance that shouldn't be possible on such bare stone.

Something engineered this. Something is still engineering it. And for now, in this bounded small space where everything is sparse except the strangeness and the life, you are part of the process.
