The first thing you notice is the weight of the air. It carries heat in the way water carries temperature—presses against you, soaks in before you feel it as heat. The moisture doesn't mist or fog so much as exist as a permanent condition, a saturation that makes everything feel recently wiped, like a mouth before speech.

You stand on what you might call ground, if ground were something shaped rather than grown. It is laid, not formed. Blocks of dark basalt fitted together across the plain with the patient imprecision of something built by hand, adjusted stone by stone across generations until the imperfections averaged out. The joins between blocks weep. Water emerges from the seams in thin threads, not flowing so much as appearing, as though the stone itself is exhaling. These threads join, split, rejoin, and eventually disappear into low places where the surface dips—a depression here, a basin there, where water pools in stillness. No plants. Not even the memory of plants. Just mineral surfaces: basalt and something likeite, something likebsite, a dozen variations of dark stone that the eye stops trying to categorize after the first hour.

The scale is the second thing that breaks your understanding.

The plain extends in every direction, and you realize you cannot judge distance here. What appears close—a wall rising perhaps a hundred meters ahead—is further than it should be. What appears far—a dark mass at the horizon that might be mountains—is closer. The world seems to be operating at a slight angular inconsistency, a mismatch between your eyes and whatever geometry this place follows. You learn to stop trying to trust the proportion between near and far, between tall and low. You move through space that was built for beings who had already intuited this mismatch, who laid their roads not in straight lines but in curves that compensated for the world's local warping.

The construction is total. You have been walking for what feels like days, and you have not found a single surface that is not shaped, not fitted, not clearly the product of intention. The walls that rise in places—vast slabs set vertically, stretching higher than any human architecture should reach—bear tool marks. Grooves where something like a chisel dragged across the surface. Hollows where hands gripped while the stone was being set. The tech is primitive: this is hand labor, simple tools, manual craft sustained across timescales that have no meaning here. But the scale. The scale is incomprehensible. This plain is a continent. These walls are the bones of something larger than any single project, assembled by generation after generation of builders who understood that their tools would not change, only their numbers, only their time.

The heat rises from the stone itself, from the water, from the thick atmosphere. It is not the dry heat of a desert but the humid heat of something processing, metabolizing without life. Everything feels damp. Your clothing, your skin, the surfaces of everything you touch. The water emerges, gathers, evaporates, returns as condensation on overhead surfaces—the great flat expanses of roof that stretch above you in places, the undersides of bridges that cross the deeper channels, the inner surfaces of structures you cannot see the tops of. Water weeps. It is the one process the built world cannot control, the one element that persists despite the total construction: the weeping. Not leaking, not dripping in the way of disrepair, but appearing. Seeping out of everything.

The turbulence makes itself known not through sound but through change.

The geometry shifts. You will look away from a structure and when you look back, the proportions will have altered—not dramatically, not in ways you can precisely describe, but in the way a dreamer knows a dream room has changed while they weren't watching. The horizon breathes. A wall that was straight when you began walking is curved when you reach it. The low places where water pools have moved, not flowed but relocated, as though the surface rearranged itself while you weren't tracking it. This is not decay. This is not ruin. The construction is still happening, still being built and unbuilt in the same moment. You are inside something that was designed to be unstable, to be made and unmade continuously, a constructed world that treats its own geometry as negotiable.

There is light, but it does not behave. It comes from above—filtered through the thick atmosphere, through the haze of water vapor, through the gaps in the vast ceilings that shelter the interior spaces—and it pools in ways light should not pool. Shadows fall at angles that don't match the sources. The dim quality of the light is not merely low brightness but something more fundamental: an attenuation, a light that has already traveled too far to carry clarity. You see shapes more than you see surfaces. You perceive depth more than you see form. The world is more impression than image.

The silence is not empty but held. The sounds that exist are small: the thin music of water emerging and gathering, the creak of stone settling into its own weight, the occasional sound of something shifting that might be the world breathing or might be your hearing miscalibrating. No voices. No movement of fauna or bodies through space. You are perhaps the only presence here, or perhaps the only presence that is still enough to be counted. The silence holds. It doesn't wait for sound so much as contain it at low concentration, like air from which the oxygen has slowly been removed.

And beneath everything, the wet heat, the raw emergence, the built world still coming into itself. The erosion is 0.2—nothing has yet worn this place down because it has not yet finished being made. The world is new. The world is still becoming. But the world is wrong—not broken, not failed, but operating according to rules that do not match the rules you carried here with you. The turbulence is not chaos but a different order, one that was planned for beings who had already abandoned the expectations of natural geology, who built a continent with hand tools because they had learned to work at the scale of their patience rather than the scale of their technology.

You walk. The ground weeps. The geometry shifts. The heat presses in.

You begin to understand that this place was not built for habitation. It was built for the act of building.
