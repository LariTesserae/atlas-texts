The plateau receives water like a patient owed a debt. Not rainfall so much as seepage — moisture climbing through porous limestone from some deep source, emerging as a slow weeping through the rock face wherever pressure finds a seam. Walls dampen. Streets glisten in the morning. Pools gather in low places, not quite ponds, not quite puddles — something between, permanent and shallow, their surfaces still in the early hours before the plateau wind stirs. The water table here is close enough to taste in the air: a mineral coolness, the smell of wet stone, moisture gathering on every surface that doesn't drain quickly enough.

Sparse vegetation follows where the water allows. Thin grasses with bluish stems. Low shrubs whose leaves are thick and waxy, holding what they get. Small trees — ash and something like it — bent by the wind that moves through from the plateau's interior, where the elevation pulls weather across this vast land like breath drawn slowly through a mouth. The vegetation doesn't carpet the ground; it dots it, punctuates the stone and packed earth, clusters around the water-sites and thins between them into bare places where the ground is too dry or too packed to hold roots.

This coolness is structural to the place. The elevation keeps temperatures low even in what passes for summer, and the moisture in the air cools everything further through evaporation. The stone holds that chill, conducting it upward through the soles of your feet, through the soles of buildings. In the higher districts the air has a bite to it, a clean cold that the body adjusts to but never quite loses awareness of. Weather moves across the plateau in slow systems — not dramatic, not stormy, but persistent, a climate rather than weather, a mode the sky enters and inhabits for seasons at a time.

And the plateau is vast. You can stand at its edge and see the ground fall away for kilometers, and the ground beyond is still this place — still the same stone, the same slow water, the same sparse grass bowing in the wind. The built environment stretches as far as that horizon, dense and continuous, broken only by the marshy basins that collect the seepage and the bare rock ridges that break through the soil. The city — or what has become one, grown over centuries from a cluster of settlements into something town-scale, then city-scale, then plateau-scale — occupies this immensity like a stain that has spread until it reached the edges. Districts blur into districts. Walls connect to walls. Streets climb and descend with the stone beneath them, following the terrain's long-settled contours rather than imposing new ones on it.

The terrain is old. Not dramatic old — no sharp peaks or raw clefts. The erosion here has been at work long enough to smooth the angles, round the ridges, give the land the quality of something that has finished becoming what it is. The cliffs are weathered into shelves and overhangs. The slopes are gentle where they can be, abrupt where stone demands it, but even the abrupt places have been weathered back, their faces softened by water and frost and the slow work of decades. This is land in equilibrium — still active, still moving, but moving the way a slow river moves: visibly, measurably, but without urgency.

The built structures layer over that settled terrain like sediment. Stone construction everywhere — fitted blocks, mortared walls, terraced platforms carved into the slope, towers rising from ridge-tops as though the stone simply continued building upward. The construction is dense: buildings touch, share walls, crowd the streets until the space between them is more wall than air. Overlapping construction periods show everywhere — newer structures on top of older foundations, older walls incorporated into newer buildings, repairs and additions and entirely new districts built over whatever was there before. This is a place that has been continuously occupied for a long time, and every generation left its layer. The base floors are ancient. The upper stories are newer. But the stone is all the same kind of stone, cut from the plateau, shaped by hand, laid by hand, and the effect is of a built environment that feels grown rather than built — as natural a feature of the plateau as the rock ridges, as inevitable as the water.

No devices. Nothing mechanical. The labor is human and animal — teams of people and draft animals moving stone, carrying water, hauling materials up the terraces and across the wide streets. Wheels turn on axles, levers lift loads, pulleys raise stones — but these are simple machines, wooden and rope-and-hemp, the kind that don't require anything but strength and geometry. The buildings have no mechanisms, no powered systems, nothing that operates without a body attending to it. Light is daylight. Heat is fire and the slow warmth stored in thick stone walls. Water moves through channels and pipes made of clay and stone, gravity-fed from the higher basins, tended by people who open and close gates, clear blockages, repair breaks. The infrastructure is real, functional, elaborate in its simplicity — but it is human-maintained, human-scaled, human-powered from end to end.

The plateau is full of people. Dense, layered, many presences per volume. Streets that are more people than space during the hours of movement, walls that carry sound and silence in alternation, windows and balconies and roof-terraces crowded with lives. The density is not chaotic — it is organized, the organization of a place that has had to fit its inhabitants into a finite space for long enough to develop habits, rules, structures — but it is dense nonetheless. The city breathes close. Bodies, voices, animals, goods, all moving through a built environment that was designed to hold them and has been redesigned and expanded to hold more, and still is full.

But when the movement stops — in the deep night, in the early morning before the city wakes — the silence holds. Not true silence. Water still seeps. Wind still moves across the plateau, finding its way through the streets and over the walls. Somewhere, always, someone is speaking or building or drawing water. But the sound is so faint relative to the scale of the place that it registers as quiet — a murmur at the bottom of a vast stillness. The plateau's silence is the silence of a world that is mostly not you. The built environment is still. The water moves without hurry. The sky, when visible from the narrow gaps between buildings, is large and indifferent and very close, because the elevation has thinned the air and made the sky a deeper blue and the stars, when they come, a brighter scatter.

The animal life is rare but not absent. Birds on the wet cliff faces. Small creatures in the marsh-basins — things with narrow heads and long tails, adapted to the brackish water that collects in the low places. Insects in season. Occasionally, a larger animal moves through the edge districts, or is kept by people — a draft animal, a dog, something brought in or grown there over long enough time to belong. The fauna of the plateau is the fauna of a settled place: the creatures that survive alongside dense human presence, which is to say fewer of them, and quieter, and always on terms negotiated over generations.

The activity is what holds the place in motion. Water rises and falls with the seasons, the marsh-basins swelling and shrinking, the seepage running stronger after the long rains and tapering in the dry months. The people move through their cycles — daily, seasonal, generational — their patterns layered over the landscape's own cycles so thoroughly that the two are difficult to separate. Weather moves across the plateau in systems that have names and that people plan around. Erosion continues its patient work. The city itself is a process — buildings rising and falling, districts expanding, water channels rerouted, terraces cut deeper or filled in. The plateau is active, multiple processes underway at once, all of them slow, most of them old, none of them finished. And at the center of it all, the water — still seeping up through the stone, still weeping, still feeding the basins and the vegetation and the wells and the walls that absorb it like the plateau absorbs everything: slowly, permanently, as though the moisture has always been here and the city simply grew to match it.
