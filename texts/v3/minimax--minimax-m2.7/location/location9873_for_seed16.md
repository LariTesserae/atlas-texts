The basin is easy to miss from above. It is not a canyon, not a cliff, not a dramatic tear in the land — just a shallow, nearly circular depression in the low, flat country, perhaps a hundred meters across. The rim is low enough to step over without noticing, and if you came here without knowing what to look for, you would pass over it without registering the wrongness. The lip of the basin is mineralite: a compacted, faintly iridescent stone that catches the sun and throws it back in a thin, sourceless gleam, as though the ground itself has a skin.

It is when you step down — not far, a matter of two or three meters — that the world changes. The walls rise around you, and the sky above narrows to a ragged opening perhaps thirty meters across. You are standing in a pocket. The air here is not just warmer. It is *thicker* — air that holds heat the way a kiln holds heat, saturated with the faint, sharp smell of mineralite oxidation, of iron and sulfur and something else you cannot name. Your skin prickles. The stone beneath your feet is dark rust-red, almost chocolate in color where it is fresh, fading to a deep burnt umber where it has been exposed longest. It is warm. Not hot to the point of pain — not yet — but warm in a way that stone should not be warm unless something beneath it is burning.

There are streets. This is the first thing that registers as genuinely strange, once the heat and the dimness have registered as background: this is not open ground. Channels have been cut into the basin floor, not deep, a meter at most, but regular. They run parallel and perpendicular to each other in a loose grid, and along their margins the stone has been dressed and squared and carved with simple tools. Pick-marks are still visible under the oxidation — blunt, repetitive, the work of hands and levers and patience. The grid is not precise; the angles drift and the streets widen and narrow as though they grew rather than were designed. But there are streets, and at their intersections there are wider spaces — shallow plazas where the stone has been smoothed by foot-traffic, worn to a faint polish, and around the edges of these spaces chambers open into the basin walls: doorways and corridors cut into the rock, rough-arched, their lintels marked with the same pick-marks, their floors scored with shallow channels that might once have carried water but now carry nothing but dust.

The city was built from the basin's own body. That is the logic of it. Theite — the local name for the mineralite substrate, though names matter less than the fact of it — is a porous, hydrated ore threaded with iron compounds and trace sulfides. It can be cut with stone tools. It holds moisture in its crystal structure, moisture that releases slowly when the ore is compressed or heated. And the basin floor is *always* heated: deep beneath the basin, in strata that may or may not be natural, iron and sulfide are undergoing a slow, continuous oxidation. There is no flame. There is no smoke. But the substrate sweats warmth, and has been sweating it for as long as the basin has existed, and the people who lived here — whoever they were, and they left no records, no carvings, no names, only the tool-marks and the grid — built their city into this warmth because it gave them something the surrounding country could not: life.

The vegetation grows in theite. Not in soil — there is no soil — but directly from cracks and seams in the stone, roots threading through the porous ore to reach the moisture locked in its crystal matrix. The plants are grey-green, fibrous, their leaves narrow and waxy to minimize transpiration. They grow in clusters along the streets, rising from the channels where old water-courses dried centuries ago, and they grow along the walls of the chambers, in the corners where the pick-marks left rough pockets that catch moisture and hold it. Their roots are pale, almost white, and when you pull one free the stone it came from is dark and damp, weeping moisture that evaporates within seconds in the heat. The plants are not lush in the way of a jungle or a meadow. They are medium-lush — substantial enough that you notice them, sparse enough that the basin does not feel alive. A middle ground. A place where plant life exists but does not dominate. There is no other vegetation in the surrounding country for kilometers: this is an island, and the island is the basin.

The heat varies through the day. In the early hours it is merely warm — uncomfortable if you are not accustomed to it, but survivable. By midday the basin floor radiates a dry, pressing heat that makes the air shimmer above the channels. You can see it: the distortion rises from the stone in slow waves, and the light from the opening above, which is already dim, filtered through the haze of mineral dust that hangs permanently in the basin, bends and splits and recombines, so that shadows fall in directions that do not quite make sense. The bloom is most visible at midday. Efflorescence: delicate mineral crystals that emerge from theite surface in response to heat, blooming in fern-like patterns along walls and channel-margins, white and pale yellow and rust-red, fine as frost, growing visibly over days. You can watch a new bloom form if you sit still long enough — a patch of stone that was smooth becomes matte, then dusty, then crystalline, over hours. The crystals are brittle; they crumble at a touch, releasing a fine mineral dust that catches in the throat and stings the eyes. The dust settles on every surface, lines the channels, powders the leaves of the grey-green plants until they look grey as well, blurring the boundary between stone and growth.

The fauna parameter is zero, and the basin honors this. There are no insects. No birds. No rats in the granary-chambers — and there are granaries, small rooms cut into the basin walls with channels still deep enough to have held grain, though the channels are dry and coated in bloom and the rooms are empty. No bones in the corners, no webs in the doorways, no sound of life. The absence is not peaceful. It is *charged*. A place this warm, this dense with growing things and growing crystals, should have something crawling in it. Should have bees in the grey-green flowers, if nothing else. The lack is a pressure, a negative space that makes every other sound louder.

The sound is the first thing that strikes you as genuinely wrong, if you arrive without knowing. The basin amplifies. Footsteps in the channels produce a deep, resonant hum that rises from the stone and surrounds you, so that you cannot tell if the sound is your own or the basin's. Voices — if you speak, if you call out — are swallowed and spit back, sometimes doubled, sometimes pitched differently, as the thermal layers above the basin floor refract and scatter the sound. The acoustics of the chambers are stranger still: a word spoken at the mouth of a corridor will echo not once but several times, the echoes arriving at intervals that seem to lengthen and shorten unpredictably, as though the stone is thinking about what to do with the sound before it lets it go. And beneath all of this, a high thin noise, almost subaudible, like tinnitus filtered through heat — a tone that might be the oxidation process singing at the molecular level, or might be nothing more than nerve-cells misfiring in the sustained warmth. You hear it in the quiet moments. There are no quiet moments for long.

The city is dynamic — not with biological dynamism, not with the motion of animals and weather and growth cycles, but with the dynamism of chemistry and heat and mineral time. The oxidation below is slow but constant; the substrate is warming, degree by degree, over centuries. The bloom grows and recedes, new patches forming where heat concentrates, old patches fading to powder. The stone expands and contracts with the day-night cycle — audible at night, when the walls cool and theite contracts, a deep, groaning sound that rises from deep in the basin, from chambers you cannot see, as though something vast and stone is breathing in its sleep. The grey-green plants are the only things that seem still, and even they are not truly still — their leaves orient through the day, tracking the patch of sky above with a slow, imperceptible motion, the way a sunflower tracks the sun. The patch of sky is not large. The orienting is subtle. But if you sit in one place for a full day and watch the plants, you begin to notice it, and once you notice it, you cannot stop noticing it.

There is no water. The people who built this place must have understood theite's moisture-holding properties — must have built systems, pressing-chambers or heating-plates, to extract water from the stone they lived inside. There are rooms whose floors are too smooth, too worn, too channeled with shallow grooves in a pattern that serves no drainage purpose you can identify. There are channels that run along the base of walls and lead nowhere. These are the remnants of a water economy — a way of living in stone and extracting life from it — but whatever systems they built have been abandoned so long that the stone has grown over them, sealed them, incorporated them into the basin's own slow chemistry. You can see where moisture still weeps from theite in the deepest chambers, where the oxidation runs warmest: dark stains on the walls that might be rust or might be water-marks, lingering evidence that the stone is still giving up its moisture, still sustaining the grey-green plants, still keeping this pocket of life alive in the basin of a dead world.
