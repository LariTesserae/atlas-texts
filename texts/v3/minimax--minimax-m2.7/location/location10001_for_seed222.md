The plateau has no name in any language still spoken here. It rises a thousand meters above the river basin below, a vast table of pale stone that extends past the horizon in every direction. From the air it would look like something left behind — a surface still there after the land around it dissolved. From ground level, it is simply where you are. The stone is cream and rose-gray, soft enough to scratch with a thumbnail in places, hard enough elsewhere to have held its shape against wind and rain for longer than memory. Water carved it once, deeply, then stopped. The plateau remembers its rivers in the shapes of the channels — shallow, broad, braided like old fingers — and in the occasional spring that seeps up through porous rock at the base of these ancient beds, enough to keep a patch of soil dark and alive. These oases are small, maybe fifty meters across, and they are spaced irregularly across the stone. Between them, the ground is bare rock with a fine net of desert-varnish streaks, or thin soil where a rock outcrop gathered enough dust to hold it. Sparse vegetation clusters there — low thorny shrubs, yellow-flowered and tough, and the occasional tree with a wide crown and roots that go down farther than anyone has measured. The trees stand alone. The pattern is that nothing stands together. Animals keep this spacing too, not by choice but by the mathematics of resources: a territory stretches for kilometers, and each creature knows the distances between the water-patches the way you know the rooms in your home.

The temperature never drops. Not in the way of deep equatorial heat, where the world radiates warmth like a forge, but a constant, generous warmth that permeates — warm stone underfoot, warm air against skin, a warmth that makes the plateau feel inhabited even when nothing moves. The light is excessive. The sky is a washed-out blue for most of the day, and the sun presses directly down with an intensity that bleaches color from the rock and sharpens every edge into crisp shadow. At noon the glare is blinding; at dawn and dusk the stone catches it and turns the color of ember. The air is thin at this elevation — breathing is easy but effort is felt — and the clarity makes distance feel compressed, so that a ridgeline a hundred kilometers away looks close enough to walk to before dark.

Fauna is abundant in the way that life is abundant on a hostile plain: not densely packed but everywhere in motion once you know how to look. Herds of the long-legged grazers move between the water-patches in loose arcs, their silhouettes sharp against the stone. Predators — low, wide-bodied, built for endurance rather than the ambush — follow at the distances that matter. Insects hover in the thermal columns above the scrub patches. Vultures hold still on air. The plateau is alive with the slow business of eating and being eaten, and the silence is broken — not by noise, but by the occasional sound of it: a low bark, a scrape of hoof on stone, the dry rattle of disturbed gravel as something moves at the edge of hearing. Most of the time the plateau is quiet. The quiet is the thing that unifies it.

Scattered structures break the open ground. Not ruins, not exactly — or not only ruins. Some are single standing pillars of dark glass set into shallow depressions in the rock, their surfaces faintly iridescent, humming at a frequency just below hearing. Some are low walls that follow no consistent geometry, barely higher than a hand, built from local stone but fitted together with a precision that no human hand has achieved here. One sits beside a spring: a basin carved from a single piece of something that is not quite stone, not quite metal, its inner surface patterned with lines too fine to see with the naked eye until light hits them at the right angle. They are not decayed. They are not being reclaimed. They are simply present, as though they arrived and decided to stay. In the evening, when the light drops to a low amber, some of them emit a faint glow — not bright enough to see by, but enough to notice, to hold your gaze. Enough to feel like a signal.

The plateau is not stable. It is actively eroding — slowly, the way established landscapes erode, the way stone decides over millennia to become sand. The channels fill with fine material between the wet seasons. The springs shift their positions by meters over decades. Dust moves in the wind. On certain mornings, a thin haze hangs over the eastern section — not pollution, not mist, something else — and animals avoid it, circling wide. In those zones, the light bends. A rock casts a shadow that doesn't point the right direction. The air smells faintly of ozone and something older, like the memory of rain that hasn't fallen yet. The strange elements are not dramatic. They are persistent — small wrongnesses that accumulate, that make the plateau feel less like a place discovered and more like a place maintained.

Night comes fast at this latitude. The warmth holds for an hour after sunset, then falls away just enough to matter. The stars are numerous and white. Somewhere in the distance — the direction is hard to determine — a low sound pulses: not mechanical, not animal, something between a hum and a breath, rhythmic and unhurried. It may be coming from beneath the stone. It may be coming from the structures. It may be coming from the animals, though no one has seen them make it. The plateau does not offer answers. It offers presence, and a quality of attention, as though the land itself is watching.
