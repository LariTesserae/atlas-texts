The cavity opens beneath you like a wound in the earth that has long since learned to heal. A sinkhole — not dramatic, not a chasm, but a broad pale throat of exposed limestone dropping away at a gentle-enough angle that you could walk down if you weren't careful. Forty meters across. Sixty deep, maybe seventy, though the floor vanishes into a soft murk at the edges where the light doesn't quite reach and the pooled water forgets its own edges.

This is what a bright underground place looks like.

It isn't daylight, not really. Or not only. The illumination comes from above — the sinkhole's mouth catches the sky, and the sun, when it passes, sends a pale column down that paints the far wall in a rotating theater of pale gold and white. But the brightness is also coming from *here*, from the walls, the floor, the quiet accumulation of things that glow. Bioluminescent fungi in pale greens and ghost blues colonize the damper seams of rock, clustering in the crevices where moisture gathers and slow-evaporates. They are not aggressive lights. They do not compete with the sun. They simply persist — a dim, living lamp left burning by something patient.

The walls of the sinkhole are terraced by long erosion into shelves and ridges, each one a different degree of damp. At the top, where the opening meets the air, the limestone is dry and warm to the touch, bleached pale by sun and time. Go down ten meters and the stone darkens. Twenty meters and it is cool, slick, colonized. Ferns root in the thin soil that has gathered in the ledges — not many, but enough. A few twisted shrubs push out from horizontal seams, their leaves thick and waxy against the humidity. The sparse vegetation is enough. It is not a garden. It is a holdout.

At the bottom, the water.

It seeps more than pools — a slow exudation from the rock, fed by rain that falls somewhere above and finds its way down through the limestone over years, decades, centuries. The pool is shallow and tea-dark from tannins, perhaps four meters across, barely a pond. Its surface is still. Mosquitoes skate it. Somewhere in the wet stone, something with too many legs lives and dies and feeds something else.

Fauna arrives. Birds from the surface world — doves, mostly, and a drab olive thing you don't name — perch on the upper ledges and descend to drink. They leave quickly. The sinkhole is not hospitable; it is merely tolerable, and they know it. But other things stay. Moths have evolved here, or colonized here, their wings patterned in pale grey and green that matches the lichen on the walls. They rest during the bright hours and rise at dusk, circling the fungal lights like congregants at a dim altar. Insects fill the lower air with a faint, layered hum that rises and falls without pattern — not quite a sound, not quite silence.

The floor is soft. Not sand, not mud — a loam of decomposed lichen and leaf litter and the accumulated fine dust of ten thousand dry days mixed with the moisture of ten thousand damp nights. Your feet sink slightly. The erosion here is geological, patient, complete — the sinkhole has been a sinkhole long enough that it has become *itself*, shaped into its own equilibrium, and nothing about it suggests change is coming. The walls will continue their slow retreat. The pool will continue its slow expansion or contraction. The ferns will continue their slow conquest of new stone. It is all happening on a timescale that makes urgency irrelevant.

You stand at the bottom of a hole in the ground that is lit like a room. The air is warm and damp and close, and it smells of mineral and green growing things and something faintly sulfuric from deep in the rock, a buried memory of older heat. The brightness above you moves as the sun moves. The brightness at your feet glows without moving at all. Between them, the air is thick with small life and quiet.

There are no structures. No tools lying near the pool, no ropes hanging from the upper ledges, no carvings in the soft stone. No one has been here — or if they have, they left nothing, because there is nothing to leave that would outlast the erosion. The sinkhole does not invite and does not refuse. It simply continues, beneath the surface of the world, lit from two directions at once, growing its ferns and hosting its moths and pooling its dark water in a patience that predates every living thing that has ever descended into it.
