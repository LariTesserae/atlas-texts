The gorge is young. You can see this immediately in the angles—nothing here has learned to be round yet. The walls rise in vertical planes of dark granite, their surfaces still holding the memory of whatever tore them apart: fresh fracture lines, razor-sharp ridges where one slab was ripped away from another, crevices that look more like wounds than geology. The rock is black and glassy in places, and where it isn't glassy it's raw, pale stone the color of bone. The cold has kept it this way. At this elevation, the air is thin and cool—not freezing, but cool in the way that makes your lungs work harder, that settles into the spaces between your ribs and stays. Wind moves through the upper reaches of the gorge, thin and high, but down here the air is still, held in the stone like something cupped in a hand.

There is no water. The gorge is dry the way a bone is dry—mineral, inert, offering nothing. The rock holds no moisture. You could press your palm to the granite and feel it draw warmth from your skin with an almost hungry efficiency. In the cracks and crevices, there are white crystalline deposits, faint as frost, the only trace of anything that might once have been liquid, now long since sublimated or consumed. The locals say the gorge drinks what falls into it. Rain, snowmelt, condensation—whatever the sky offers, the stone takes, and keeps.

And nothing grows. There are no plants, no fungi, no lichen. The walls are bare and clean as surgical steel, offering no purchase, no foothold, no hospitality to biology of any kind. But the locals have found other ways. Along the settlement's edge, where the rock has been cut and fitted with metal shelving, there are rows of sealed growing pods—industrial-looking, matte gray, each one backlit with the violet-pink glow of hydroponic arrays. Inside, green things grow: engineered crops, dense nutrient solutions cycling through hidden plumbing, plants that have been redesigned for this place—shorter root systems, thicker leaves, optimized for the artificial light and mineral-free nutrient baths that are their only connection to anything biological. The pods hum with aeration pumps. Their light casts colored shadows on the bare stone. Beyond them, the settlement continues: platforms of welded steel bolted directly into the granite, walkways and housing units and storage bins and machinery, all of it built against the rock or hanging from it, layered in a vertical urbanism that climbs and descends along the gorge's narrow axis. There are so many structures that they press against each other, near-touching, stacked and staggered, occupying every usable surface. A person walking from one end of the settlement to the other would pass through a corridor of human presence so dense it feels like walking through the rooms of a single enormous building.

The light is dim. Every surface is matte—the stone drinks light, the metal reflects it only when struck directly. The settlement's illumination comes from strips of phosphor tape along the walkways, from the violet glow of the growing pods, from a few high-mounted work lights that track slowly across the walls like searchlights on a prison. Between these, the gorge lives in a permanent state of twilight, a blue-gray half-darkness in which distances are hard to read and edges blur. Your eyes adjust, eventually, and you begin to see the layers: the raw rock behind and around everything, the metal framework bolted to it, the infrastructure of pipes and conduits that run along every surface, and then the lived structures—housing, workshops, storage, the places where people eat and sleep and work. All of it is industrial. The sound gives it away: the low, constant thrum of generators, the rhythmic pulse of air-circulation fans, the metallic clatter of something being moved or welded or repaired. The generators are everywhere—diesel and electric, old and new, some belching faint exhaust into the thin air, others running clean and closed-loop. The settlement is powered by a patchwork grid that somebody, or some group of somebodies, has assembled from whatever was available and has been maintaining, jury-rigging, expanding, and losing to the rock for as long as anyone can remember. There are factories here. Not large ones—the space doesn't allow it—but functional ones, workshops where raw materials are processed and parts are fabricated and tools are sharpened and repaired. The settlement makes what it needs, or close enough to needing. The industrial light is different from the phosphor light: warmer, more orange, more intermittent, spilling from doorways and open bays in rectangles that cut the dimness like wounds.

Fauna is abundant, which is to say: there are people here, and there are the things people brought. Cats, mostly—adapted, some of them, with thicker coats and wider pupils, living in the warm machinery spaces. Rats, certainly, though you might not see them. A few dogs, kept for specific work. The people themselves are the most present fauna: dozens of them,densely packed into a space that was never meant to hold this many, going about the tasks of keeping themselves alive. They move through the settlement's corridors and platforms with the ease of long familiarity, knowing which handholds to use, which panels to duck under, which floor plates shift when stepped on and which are solid. They have learned the settlement the way a body learns its own limbs. In the housing units—cramped, multi-level, stacked like cells in a honeycomb—you can hear voices, fragments of music, the clatter of dishes, the sound of children. In the workshops, you can hear the sharper sounds: the grind of grinding wheels, the hiss of plasma cutters, the rhythmic clanging of metal on metal. In the corridors, you hear footsteps, breathing, the soft sound of bodies moving close together.

The gorge is turbulent. This is the hardest parameter to hold, the one that refuses to settle. The settlement is not at rest. The rock is not at rest. The infrastructure is not at rest. You feel it in the way the walls flex under your hands—subtle, intermittent, the granite contracting and expanding with temperature changes that follow no simple pattern. You feel it in the way a walkway sags slightly toward one end, or in the way a pipe will suddenly rattle in its bracket for a few seconds and then stop, for no reason you can determine. The gorge is alive with small, unexplained movements. The locals have learned to read them. A faint tremor means the mountain is shifting—too much weight on one platform, too much vibration from the generators, a stress fracture propagating somewhere in the deep rock. A change in the pitch of the ventilation fans means a blockage forming in the ductwork, or a section of tunnel that is slowly, imperceptibly narrowing. A shift in the color of the phosphor lighting—and this is the strangest part, the part that doesn't fit easily into any mechanical explanation—means the growth pods need attention, or the solar collectors on the gorge's upper rim are being obscured by dust or cloud or something else, or the power grid is redistributing load in a pattern that somebody, somewhere, understands.

There is no water, but there is condensation. It gathers in the lowest points of the settlement—the undersides of platforms, the corners of corridors, the bases of walls where metal meets stone. The settlement has learned to collect it: channels carved into the metal flooring, absorbent material pressed into joints, small mechanical dehumidifiers that run constantly, their waste water fed into a recycling system that is itself jury-rigged, layered, maintained by people who understand it in the way you understand a language you learned as a child—intuitively, imperfectly, without access to the underlying grammar. The water tastes of metal. It has to.

The locals are unusual, though not in any single, describable way. They are adapted to this place the way all living things are adapted to their environment: by being shaped by it, and by shaping it in turn. Their language has words for things that have no names elsewhere. Their gestures include signals for conditions you'd never encounter in a wet world—signals for tremor, for blockage, for the particular quality of light that means a pod is failing. They eat food grown in the pods and processed in the workshops: dense, nutrient-optimized, slightly metallic in aftertaste. They drink the water they collect, and they know exactly how much each person is allowed. They sleep in shifts because the space is too small for everyone to sleep at once, and because the turbines need tending around the clock, and because the gorge, for all its stillness, does not invite long, unbroken rest. When they are not working, they are talking, or listening, or teaching their children the particular vocabulary of a dry world.

The gorge is young, and so, in a sense, is the settlement. The rock is still sharp. The infrastructure is still being built. Every year, the settlement grows—another platform bolted to the wall, another section of walkway, another pod line, another generator. And every year, something fails: a section of wall that can no longer bear weight, a duct that collapses, a platform that tears free from its moorings and must be rebuilt from scratch. The locals call this the rhythm of the gorge. They speak of it the way farmers speak of seasons—the mountain builds, and the mountain takes. Creation and destruction, not in alternation but in constant, overlapping presence, the way a river is both building its delta and eroding it at the same moment.

You could stand in the settlement's central corridor—this narrow, metal-lined channel that runs the length of the gorge floor, flanked by housing and workshops and growing pods, lit by phosphor strips that hum faintly when you touch them—and you could hear all the sounds of this place at once: the generators, the fans, the voices, the clatter, the breathing of the many bodies packed into this small space. And you could feel the cool air against your skin, and smell the mineral dust and the faint metallic sweetness of the hydroponic systems, and see the raw black rock rising on either side, sharp and bare and offering nothing. And you could understand that this place is not hospitable, not comfortable, not sustainable by any measure that applies to a world with water and air and soil. And you could understand that it is alive—densely, turbulently, urgently alive—and that the people and the cats and the machines and the rock are all, in their different ways, engaged in the same act: the act of continuing.
