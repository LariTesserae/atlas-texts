# The Plateau of Carved Stone

The wind here is patient. It has nothing to push against for kilometers in every direction, so it moves slowly across the plateau like a hand pressing a map flat, smoothing everything beneath it into a long, quiet exhalation. The cold is not sharp — it is *heavy*. It settles into the rock and stays. Frost forms in hairline cracks during the night, and in the morning the stone looks like it has been drawn on with a pale fingertip — white veins threading through black basalt, widening a little each morning, each year. This is a young geology. The plateau was here once, deep and buried, and something beneath it is pushing. The edges of it are fresh. Ridgelines break like teeth through the thin soil, raw and angular, not yet softened by the centuries of freeze and thaw that round such formations elsewhere. Where the rock is exposed it fractures along clean geometric planes, and the morning light catches faces of it at different angles so that a single ridge can seem to be made of several different materials — dark grey, near-black, sometimes a faint green where a mineral seam runs through it.

Between the ridges, in the lower places, moisture gathers. Not in rivers — the plateau is too vast for rivers; there are no gradients strong enough to gather the water into channels that last. Instead there are pools. They are shallow, often no deeper than a hand's length, and they form wherever a depression in the basalt catches snowmelt or the condensation that forms when the day's thin air cools against the stone at dusk. The pools are dark and still. They reflect the sky with no distortion, so they look less like water than like pieces of sky that have been pressed into the ground. Around their edges the vegetation grows — sparse, but present, and strangely specific. Thick moss in pale grey-green, low and dense, spreading in tight circles where the ground stays damp. Small ferns with fronds curled tight against the cold. Woody shrubs with small waxy leaves, their branches bent by the weight of frost crystals that form each night and persist well into the late morning. The vegetation does not carpet the plateau. It *punctuates* it — islands of green in a dark sea of stone, following the moisture the way breath follows the cracks in a window.

The settlements are dense. The plateau should not feel hospitable, and yet people have carved into it here, and here, and here — thousands of them spread across a terrain that stretches beyond the ability of the eye to measure. The architecture is stone. Always stone. Where other cultures might use wood or earth, this one has only the basalt and the frost, and so they have become masons of the rock. Towns climb the sides of ridges in tiered terraces, each building cut into the cliff face with hand tools, its front wall built outward from the rock in fitted courses of the same dark stone. Stairways are cut directly into the stone — broad, low steps, worn smooth at the centers where generations of feet have polished the rock to a dull grey shine. Between the buildings, narrow lanes twist along the contours of the land, and everywhere there are channels carved into the stone to carry water from the higher terraces down to the lower ones. The people who built this understood the moisture. They built systems for it — stone gutters, collection basins, cisterns cut deep into the rock where water collects and stays liquid even when the surface is white with frost. The tech is primitive by the standards of lowland cities: no metal beyond simple copper tools, no wheels, no complex machinery. But the stonework itself is sophisticated in its patience — each terrace fitted to the one below, each channel angled to catch the maximum runoff, each dwelling positioned to use the rock's thermal mass to stay warmer than the open air. They have been at this for a very long time.

The fauna is abundant, and this is the strangest thing. On a plateau this cold, this dry, this sparse — the number of living things seems wrong. They are here in densities that feel almost tropical, except that everything that moves is adapted to the cold and moves slowly, deliberately, as if each motion costs something. Large herd animals — heavy-bodied, thick-skinned, their breath pluming white in the still air — move between the ridge pools in long columns that take an hour to pass. They are not hunted here. The people of the plateau have, over many generations, developed a relationship with them: the animals are drawn to the settlements' water systems, and the people divert a small portion of their own water to keep the pools near the town edges full for the herds. The herds do not come close to the buildings, but they are never far. Smaller creatures are everywhere — small warm-bodied things with dense fur that nest in the stone's crevices, emerging at midday to move along the base of the ridges in quick, darting lines. Birds, too, though few of them sing. They ride the slow wind between the ridgelines, their shadows passing over the stone with the regularity of a clock's hand.

The plateau at dusk is the quietest thing. The wind settles. The frost begins. The herd animals stand motionless among the rocks, their breath the only steam in the cold air, and the stone channels glint faintly as the moisture in them begins to freeze and seal. From the terraces, the low orange light of oil lamps glows in the stone doorways, warm and small, and below them the pools hold the last of the day's light like a secret, like a gift — still, dark, and full of sky.
