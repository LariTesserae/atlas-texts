The moss-that-is-not-moss closes over your feet and seals there, a seamless membrane across the world. Press down and it yields not like soil but like something *monitoring* the pressure — distributing your weight across a span that may be a meter wide or a kilometer, you cannot determine which, because the mat stretches in every direction to a horizon that wavers in moisture and heat-haze and is — you realize slowly, with the dread that accompanies scale — not a horizon at all. It is a transition zone. Everything here is one thing touching another thing. There is no distance between presences, no gap in the weave. You look up and there is more of it: layered canopies in gradients of green and dark green and the blue-green of water-storage tissue, each frond and blade and runner pressed so close to its neighbors that they have fused. Not in the organic way of grafted roots, but with the precision of components engineered to fit.

Water is everywhere, though you cannot see a source. It seeps upward through the mat — not from below but *into* the mat from the air itself, condensed and corralled, drawn through channels that are perhaps vascular and perhaps something more deliberate: a capillary system that the mat's warmth, its sheer metabolic mass, keeps perpetually active. In the low places the mat sags into hollows, and in those hollows water pools in silence — black, still, surface-tension-skin taut as a drumhead. Fog sits in these pools like something sleeping. It sits in the air above them too, a pale particulate suspended in the space between frond-rows so narrow that you can barely pass. The air here is not breathable in the way you are accustomed to. It is saturated, cool, tasting of mineral warmth from below and metallic cold from above, and your lungs accept it only because they have no alternative. Moisture condenses on your forearms, in the creases of your knuckles, in the corners of your eyes. You are weeping and you cannot stop and you are not certain it is tears.

The terrain beneath the mat — if there is a terrain — is not visible and cannot be touched. You probe with a stick and the mat swallows it, drawing it down into a density that yields only after forty centimeters and then with reluctance. Whatever the geology is doing down there, it is *young*. The rock is still emerging, still pressing upward, still finding its shape. You notice this because in places the mat has split — not torn, but *parted* — along seams where the ground has shifted, and in those cracks you see raw, dark stone, unweathered, the sharp edges of its crystals still intact. No one has eroded this rock. It has not been rained on long enough, not streamed over, not worn smooth. It is raw geology, embryonic, barely arrived — which makes its burial beneath a living carpet of continental extent all the more strange. The plants are not growing *on* the land. The land is growing *through* the plants. And the plants are winning.

Light arrives in shafts, in wedges, in pale columns that descend through the canopy gaps and illuminate the mist and the wet surfaces and then are gone. The light is dim not for lack of sun — the sun is present, diffused — but for lack of *clearing*. There is no bare surface anywhere to catch it, no rock or sand or still water. Every photon that enters this world finds a leaf or a film of moisture or the damp underside of a frond within centimeters and is absorbed or reflected or scattered back into the maze. You move through a world that is lit from within, faintly, greenly, the way a deep ocean trench is lit: enough to see by, not enough to feel the sun.

And the sound — the sound is the one thing you cannot acclimate to.

It is not one sound but many, layered so densely that they do not separate into components. You hear, or feel, the slow exhalation of the mat as it cycles moisture — a low, subsonic pulse, half a minute between breaths, each exhale a collective moan in a register just below language. You hear the wet sounds, too: the tick of condensation forming on surfaces, the drip and slide of water finding its way through the mat's channels, the faint rushing of underground streams whose courses you will never see. Between these lower sounds, a higher register hums — a sustained, sourceless tone that is not mechanical and not organic and does not repeat. It sits in your molars, in the bones of your wrists, in the base of your skull. It is the sound of a thing that is very large and very alive and that does not know you are there, or that knows and does not care. Sometimes the hum shifts — a quarter-tone, a phase difference — and for a moment you feel the vast organism adjust, as a sleeper shifts under a fly's landing, and you stand very still.

There is something under the surface. Not an animal — there are no animals, not one, not even the small ones, not even things with shells or wings or carapaces. The system's 0.5 rating means there is a ghost of fauna, a theoretical absence: the ghosts of creatures that might have lived here once, or might live here in a future that hasn't arrived, or might live on a world adjacent to this one where this parameter sits at a different value. You cannot see them. You cannot hear them. You only notice the absence — a slot in the ecosystem, a function not filled — and the strangeness of a place that is so thoroughly, obsessively alive in every other dimension that the missing life becomes its own presence. No insects. No birdsong. No scraping of mandibles, no dragging of bodies, no eggs. The mat grows and the water cycles and the stone rises and the sound fills the air and nothing *eats*. The system is not a web. It is a single node. A vast, unprecedented, solitary node, feeding on light and water and whatever cycles through its own engineered geometry.

That word — *engineered* — keeps arriving in your mind, unbidden. You did not bring it here. But the mat insists on it. The growth is too regular in places. The fronds align in formations that suggest intent: spirals, fractals, geometries that recur across kilometers with a precision that cannot be coincidence. Water collects in the low places in patterns that are not random. The channels through which moisture travels are distributed with an evenness that speaks of design, of balancing — load-bearing mathematics, infrastructure for a system that has no engineers. And yet there is no structure. No concrete, no metal, no fiber-optic, no grid. The built rating is 0.3: *none*. Whatever intelligence shaped this world did not build *on* it. It built *it*. Every frond is the building. Every root-channel is the wire. The infrastructure *is* the living thing.

You press your hand flat against the mat and feel it pulse — a faint warmth, a slow rhythm, the metabolism of something continent-sized, and you think: this world was made to grow, and it grew, and it is growing still, and the stone beneath it is too young to argue.

The fog shifts. A column of light descends through a gap you hadn't noticed, and for a moment you see the full depth of it — the layers, the tiers, the staggering density of living tissue stacked from the raw stone to the uppermost canopy, all of it continuous, all of it knitted into a single mat that has no seams, no borders, no edges. It is not a forest. It is not a biome. It is a single plant that is also a continent that is also an ecosystem that is also, perhaps, a sentence in a language you do not yet read, growing from the bones of a world that has not yet finished being born.

The sound does not stop. The wet does not dry. The dark, raw stone beneath does not stop rising.

And you stand on the skin of something very large and very old, and you cannot tell if you are on its surface or inside it.
