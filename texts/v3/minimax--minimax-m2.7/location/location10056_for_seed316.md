# The Primordial Plain

It is an ocean that has forgotten how to be water.

The surface stretches in every direction with the flat, glassy patience of something that has worn down every obstacle across incomprehensible time. No waves break here—or rather, waves break constantly, but in patterns that follow no wind, no moon, no logic. The water moves because movement is the only thing it has ever known. What erosion could not flatten across those countless epochs, turbulence has continued working: the primordial ocean churns at depths and breadths that constitute their own physics, their own thermodynamics, their own weather systems. Weather forms above these waters, feeds upon them, dissolves back into them. The sky that hangs above this expanse is perpetually bruised, not with clouds so much as with *presence*—a saturated atmosphere that seems less to carry oxygen than to filter something older, something that the first lungs to ever draw breath found already waiting.

There is no land. There is no horizon as such—just a meeting of water and water-sky that the eye refuses to resolve into lines. The light that reaches here is sourceless, diffuse, warm in the way that deep-body warmth is warm: not from any sun you could point to, but radiating from the water itself, or from the dense and teeming invisible presences that the water carries. It is bright enough to see by, dim enough to sleep in. The glow seems to come from *within* things rather than striking them from outside.

The surface is not quite a surface.

It takes time to understand this. At first, you see water—thick, warm, darkly luminous. But then there is too much of it. The eye slides. The horizon does not behave. And the longer you look, the more you begin to see that the water is not distinct from the air, not distinct from the light, not distinct from the heat and the pressure and the *meaning* that suffuses this place. They are one continuous substance, only differentiated by the temporary coagulation of local density. What you call water is simply where the primordial medium has momentarily gathered into something you can touch. What you call air is simply where it has not.

And within this substance, things exist.

They are not creatures in any sense the word has earned elsewhere. They are presences, densities, local concentrations of the universal wetness that have persisted long enough to develop something like pattern, something like direction. Some of them move—lumbering through the middle distances like shadows shifting, like memories of bodies. You do not see them clearly; they are the wrongness of the light, the way your peripheral vision fills with motion that vanishes when you turn. Others are stationary—or rather, their station is their motion: they have been here so long that they have become architecture. Calcified structures of thickened reality rise from the depths, festooned with pale, sparse vegetation that clings to the wet stone without quite seeming alive. These growths are not plants as you know them. They are more like crystallized intentions. They photosynthesize something that is not quite light.

The sounds are everything. They are the first thing you perceive, before sight, before the wet heat presses against your skin. A deep, subsonic thrumming underlies everything—not heard so much as felt, resonating in the bones of the chest. Above this, a wet roaring: the ocean's turbulence made audible, the endless churning of a surface that never stills because stillness would mean an end, and this place has not reached its end. It is too saturated for endings. Over that, a higher register: clicks, whistles, pulses, the language of the presences in their incomprehensible communion. The sound has texture here. It has weight. It fills the space between all things until there is no space, until you are as full of sound as the water is full of everything else.

The surface beneath—if surface is still the word—has been worn to a smoothness that suggests not sand but something more fundamental. The erosion here is ancient beyond measure: the primordial ocean has worn down every ridge, every shelf, every underwater mountain until the floor (if there is a floor) is a single perfect plane of infinite softness, yielding to everything that touches it and rising to receive everything that departs. This is the end-state of erosion, the final form of a world that has been shaped by its own contents long past the point of memory. Nothing can wear it further. Nothing can build upon it. It simply *is*, in the way that existence is, without cause or direction.

And yet it is not still.

It is the most turbulent place you have ever stood. The turbulence is not chaotic—it follows rules, but the rules are not yours. Currents swirl in geometries that suggest thought. Columns of warmer water rise and fall in rhythms that suggest breath. The entire vast expanse breathes, pulses, shifts in the manner of something that is alive at a scale where the distinction between organism and environment has not yet been invented. Weather forms overhead—clouds of water-vapor that carry within them the same teeming density as the sea below, lightning that illuminates not the sky but the *substance* between water and air, a third state of matter that exists only here.

The built things—those faint marks, those scattered structures—are not human. They are not from any life you could name. They are calcified formations that have persisted so long they have developed something like intention: a pillar here, a ring of ancient stone there, half-submerged arches where the ocean laps against surfaces that have been wet for longer than geology can measure. They were never built. They grew. They accreted. They are what happens when density concentrates long enough to become purpose.

There is no technology here because there is no need for it. The medium provides. The turbulence sustains. What would a machine do that the water does not already do better, stranger, more truly? The presences that move through this world do not need to build tools because they are themselves tools of something larger, instruments of a process that began before process had a name.

You could drown here. Not in water—in *being*. The saturation is so complete that every breath draws in not just air but the totality of the place: its warmth, its pressure, its ancient turbulence, its presences, its alien meaning. Your lungs fill with a world. Your blood begins to match its rhythm. You are being absorbed, not destroyed but *incorporated*, and some part of you—if you stay long enough, if you do not flee back to the thin, dry world you came from—begins to understand that drowning is not an ending here. It is an arrival.

The fauna is rare because it does not need to be common. A few shapes move in the far distances, too large or too old or too singular to bother with numbers. The vegetation is sparse because growth here is a different substance, not better or worse but *other*, and what little of it exists clings to the ancient stones like lichen to bone, patient and slow and somehow alive in a way that makes your heart ache with recognition.

This is the place before places. The substrate from which all surfaces were cut. It still exists—you are standing in it now, or swimming, or sinking, or simply *being* held within it. The world you knew was built on top of this, but it never stopped. It is still here, still wet, still warm, still churning at the bottom of everything, older than erosion, larger than scale, more dense than anything has a right to be.

And it is calling you deeper.
