The ground is made.

Not carved from bedrock, not laid over earth — *made*, whole, a single material surface that extends from wall to wall like the inside of a ceramic bowl. You can see where it was tooled: faint striations in the compacted mineral substrate, concentric rings and scored patterns where hands pressed or dragged implements across the surface. Primitive technology — this was built by people with hand-tools, with patient manual labor, over what must have been generations. Yet the scale suggests a cathedral built by a civilization of giants, or a room built for someone who is not quite human. The scale is small but *total*, enclosed, a space you could walk the perimeter of in an hour, and everything — every surface, every raised edge, every channel and hollow — is manufactured, shaped, placed.

Lush vegetation grows here, which should be impossible. The substrate is dry mineral, a salt-impregnated composite that holds no moisture, and the air is parched despite the warmth. But the plants don't grow from the ground. They emerge from seams in the made-surface — narrow fissures that weep humidity in the night cycle, condensation forming in channels too fine to see, feeding root systems that thread through the engineered substrate like veins through wax. The vegetation is dense, crowded, layered — fronds and tendrils and broad waxy leaves pressing close, near-touching, so thick in places you must push through rather than walk. It's warm, persistently warm, the kind of warmth that comes from below as much as above, and the plants have adapted by drawing from whatever deep moisture the makers built into their creation.

The fauna is abundant and alien. Lizards with iridescent carapaces that click against the made-ground when they run. Colonies of something like beetles that nest in the raised textures, their shells clicking in slow rhythms. Flocks of small creatures that move in murmurations, their shadows flickering across the dense canopy. They fit this place perfectly — too perfectly. As if they were designed alongside it. As if the makers built their world for these creatures, or bred them for these surfaces, over long slow generations.

And the weirdness. Something is wrong with the physics here, a gentle wrongness that accumulates at the edges of perception. The space feels deeper than it should — you can walk the perimeter in an hour, but it takes longer, and you're never quite sure the distances are constant. The light falls from above but seems to originate from everywhere, sourceless, a diffuse luminance that has no angle and casts no true shadow. When you look directly at the edges where the made-ground meets the made-walls, you can almost see them shift — not moving, but *unsettled*, as if the material hasn't finished deciding what shape it is. The geometry is close to Euclidean but not quite, like a theorem that holds true everywhere except at the corners.

Mostly quiet. Occasional sounds — the clicking of fauna, the creak of a tendril pushing through a seam — but the silences are long, and when they fall, you become aware of a low hum that might be the substrate itself, or the foundations beneath it, or nothing at all.

It's a made thing. A constructed micro-world, sealed and self-contained, built by hands using simple tools to create a living space for creatures and plants in a place that should support nothing. It breathes slowly. It settles, expands, contracts — imperceptible cycles measured in years or centuries. Whatever it was meant to be, whatever it was built to become, it is still becoming. The process has not ended. The makers are long gone, but their handiwork continues.
