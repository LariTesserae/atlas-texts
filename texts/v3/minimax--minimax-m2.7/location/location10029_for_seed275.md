# The Rootwork

You are below the world here, though "below" loses meaning in the Rootwork. The floor, the walls, the ceiling—they are the same thing, a single organism that grows and shifts and breathes through you like a lung. Plant matter, dense and woven, forms the substrate of everything. Not soil. Not stone. The roots have become architecture: walls of bundled filaments pressed into load-bearing mats, archways of lignified tissue thick as your arm, floors of spongy mycelium that give slightly underfoot. The vegetation is not growing *in* this place. It *is* this place.

But it is dry. Bone-dry. The air holds no moisture, carries no damp, and your tongue feels thick and strange in your mouth. The plants here have done something impossible—they have extracted everything, every molecule of water from the mineral substrate around them, and locked it into their cells in chains of crystallized sugar that taste of nothing at all. When you touch the walls, the filaments are warm, almost alive, but they do not yield moisture to your skin. You cannot drink from them. There is no sap, no sapling, no rain. Only the dry architecture of growth itself.

Light comes from everywhere and nowhere—a diffuse amber glow that saturates the woven space without casting shadows. It comes from the plant tissue itself, bioluminescent nodules embedded in the root-walls like knots in wood, pulsing faintly in slow rhythms you cannot parse. It is enough light to see by, not enough to warm by. The temperature sits in that thin zone just below comfortable, the cool of a cathedral, the cool of breath on stone. Your hands go cold. Your breath mists faintly.

You are in a town, or you are in the ribs of something larger than a town. The scale resists easy reading. Corridors branch and repeat, widening into chambers that might be neighborhoods or might be organs. Built structures poke through the living walls—fractured pipes trailing dry vapor, platforms of rusted metal bolted to root-mat, abandoned machinery that no longer moves but still carries its heat-signature like a held breath. The infrastructure was never fully dismantled; it simply stopped being necessary and the plants grew over it, through it, despite it. Now the two are inseparable. You walk on root-floors that hum faintly with residual electricity from buried conduits. You duck under vine-struts that are also cable-runs.

Fauna move here in the half-dark: pale arthropods the length of your finger, skittering in the creases between root-mats; slow-moving slug-like things that graze the bioluminescent nodules, their trails leaving faint phosphorescence; distant sounds of larger things, not seen, whose movements make the floor shiver. Abundant life, but life in a minor key—life adapted to the dry and the dim, life that has learned to drink from the sugar-chains and the static.

The geology is newborn. Where the root-mats pull away from the walls, you see raw mineral underneath—soft, chalky, still uncompressed. This space is being made even as you move through it, the plants exuding acids that slowly digest the stone into substrate, building new levels of themselves above the old. The erosion is intentional, controlled, a digestive process. You are walking through a stomach that has not yet finished forming.

Sound is minimal: the creak of growth, the faint static-hum of abandoned power, the wet-slide of something moving three chambers over, your own breathing. Mostly quiet. Mostly still. But the Rootwork is active, always active—growth and decay, uptake and storage, the patient chemistry of plants that do not need water doing the work that water once did. It is the most alien thing about this place: the silence between processes, the stillness that is not static, the sense that everything is happening too slowly for you to see.

You came here through a breach in the surface, a crack in the dry field above. The farmers on the plateau above do not know what lies beneath their feet. They plant their crops and wait for rain that does not come, and beneath them, the Rootwork grows toward them, patient and dry and endlessly hungry for stone.
