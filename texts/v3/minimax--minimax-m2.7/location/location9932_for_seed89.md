# The Lapsed Bowl

This is a place that exists between.

High on a basalt plateau—ancient, eroded nearly to nothing over epochs—a fissure opened centuries ago and has since softened into something almost architectural: a shallow bowl, perhaps two hundred meters across, its rim low and broken, its interior plunging steeply toward a floor that receives no sun. Warm water rises here, geothermal, mineral-rich, and the bowl holds it like a cupped palm: condensation beads on every surface, water seeps through cracks in the dark rock, shallow pools gather in pocked depressions and linger, then vanish, then return. The warmth never quite leaves. It rises from below and from the metabolism of ten thousand small bodies.

The darkness is not absolute. A grey luminescence hovers at the rim, enough to distinguish the shapes of things but not their colors. Within the bowl proper, the light fails entirely. Life here has either abandoned vision or never required it. Instead: heat-sensors, vibration-sense, chemical trails, the old senses that predate sight. You would need to imagine your way in with your hands.

The vegetation is almost absent—only the thinnest pale filaments of fungus threading across wet stone, the ghostly root-tips of something that reached down from above and gave up halfway. This is not a place of plants. It is a place of bodies.

And there are so many bodies.

Insects crowd the walls in layers: pale beetles pressed together in papery masses where moisture seeps slowest; long-legged things that hang motionless for hours then vanish sideways in a flicker; spiracles and mandibles, antennae tasting the air that is warm and close and thick with the exhalations of others. Spiders have built their webs across the bowl's interior in overlapping generations, each web reusing silk from the last, a palimpsest of architecture, and the spiders themselves are present in numbers that make the air seem to thicken. In the pools—clear, still, shallow—blind segmented things move in patterns that serve purposes you cannot guess. Somewhere in the deeper dark, a sound rises: the slow exhalation of a geothermal vent, or something breathing in a frequency you feel before you hear.

The sound is everywhere. The dripping of water in the upper bowl, each drop falling into its own small echo. The shuffle and click of insects repositioning. A higher, almost musical tone that might be the collective vibration of wings. Beneath all of it, a low hum—the sound of heat and pressure doing their patient work in the rock below. It is not comfortable noise. It is the sound of a system so dense with life that the space itself seems to breathe.

The walls are smooth where water has run and stopped, run and stopped, over centuries. The erosion here is not dramatic—no canyons, no dramatic cuts—but it is *total*. Every edge has been softened. The bowl was a wound once; it has become a shape, a form, something that holds itself together. This is a landscape that has achieved its own balance, its own logic, and it does not require light to function.

There are no structures here. No made things. But the silk is architecture. The pooled water is infrastructure. The geothermal vents are the heart of a system that has no need for anything human, or even vertebrate, to persist. Creatures here are small, simple, overwhelming in number. They live and die and are replaced, their bodies becoming the matter that new bodies use. The bowl is full of them—not crowded, not chaotic, but *saturated*, each thing in its place in an order so old it precedes the concept of order.

This is what a warm, dark, high place holds when no one builds there, when nothing reaches down from above. It becomes itself: a closed loop of moisture and heat and small life, patient and slow, older than the plateau that surrounds it, waiting.
