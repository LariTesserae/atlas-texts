A decommissioned airlock at 4,200 meters, sealed shut for decades.

Water enters through the outer hull joints, weeping through micro-fractures in the concrete where the permafrost table shifts against the foundation each winter. A weep hole drains the cavity between the inner and outer walls, but it has long since blocked with sediment, and water pools in the gap instead, saturating the cavity, seeping inward. The chamber's own infrastructure contributes: pipes cooled by the altitude and by residual thermal regulation systems weep at every threaded joint and compression fitting. The cold junction where the outer wall meets the buried rock face condenses moisture in a slow, constant film. Every surface is wet. Water drips from overhead conduits at irregular intervals, pooling in the lowest corners, sheeting across the floor in slow, shallow sheets that never fully evaporate because there is nowhere for the air to go.

The scale is wrong for what the space contains. The chamber itself is no larger than a freight container — narrow, low-ceilinged, barely room for two people standing — yet every cubic meter of it is dense with infrastructure. Pipes run in parallel bundles along the ceiling, branching into sub-branches, feeding valves and仪表 and junction boxes and cable trays, each one a surface where condensation gathers and drips. The walls are sheathed in paneling, but moisture has swollen the seams, and between the panels, in the gaps, water stands in thin vertical pools. The emergency lighting — sealed amber fixtures, originally 25 watts each, reduced by decades of decay to a dull persistent glow — struggles to illuminate through the moisture and the insect casings that coat every lens. The light that does get through is thick, amber, slow, and it reflects off the wet metal in ways that make each droplet carry a faint point of light, so that the surfaces seem to breathe with tiny scattered stars.

The insects are fungus gnats. Not wild ones. These were bred for a closed-loop pollination experiment in the facility's agricultural wing, decades ago, before the wing was decommissioned and the entire level sealed. Some escaped into the ventilation system. They found the airlock. They stayed. The conditions are ideal: no predators, consistent moisture, a stable temperature around three degrees Celsius, and organic detritus drifting in from the sealed environment above — skin cells, hair, textile microfibers, the slow shed of an empty facility. The gnats have persisted for nearly thirty years. Their population is enormous. Densities reach several thousand per cubic meter in the densest sections of the chamber. They cluster on every surface — walls, pipes, junction boxes, the backs of light fixtures — forming a living patina three or four insects deep in places, so that the underlying infrastructure is glimpsed rather than seen, a world seen through a membrane of bodies. They are small, two to three millimeters each, dark-bodied and long-legged, and they hold still in the cold with a collective patience that makes the stillness feel deliberate.

The sound is the first thing you feel. It is not the sound of insects. It is a tone — a sustained, continuous mid-frequency hum between 400 and 600 hertz, so uniform it has texture, like the buzz of a power line heard through a wall. Underneath it, higher, a chorus of competing chirps: males advertising territory from the surfaces, each one a narrow frequency peak, dozens of them slightly offset from one another, creating a shifting, wavering overlay. And beneath that, the irregular percussion of water — drips from overhead pipes striking pools, drops from the swollen wall panels, the faint splash and spread of thin sheets moving across the floor at the lowest point. The three sounds layer without resolving. They do not harmonize. They do not compete. They simply coexist, filling the small space with a sound pressure that feels physical, as if the air itself has been given mass and texture, as if the sound is not in the chamber but is the chamber, as if the metal walls have been tuned by the congregation inside until they hum in sympathy and the whole room becomes a single instrument at maximum resonance.

Temperature sits just above freezing — perhaps three degrees, no more. The air is cold enough that bare skin loses sensation in seconds, cold enough that breath is visible, cold enough that the moisture on every surface feels not wet but *frozen in its wetness*, as if the water has been caught mid-drip and left there. The cold is not dramatic. It is quiet. It is the cold of altitude and sealed machinery and decades of residual thermal equilibrium. The insects are adapted to it. Their bodies move more slowly than a temperate species would. They hold to the pipes, the junction boxes, the backs of fixtures — wherever the residual heat of electrical systems raises the surface temperature by a degree or two above ambient. Those microclimates are the warm places. The insects cluster there, a living topology of preference mapped across every surface of the chamber.

Geologically, the space is a nothing. There is granite here — the chamber was carved from the mountain's bedrock during construction, and the raw rock face is visible in places where the paneling has swollen and fallen away. It is gray, fine-grained, streaked with white quartz seams that catch the amber light. But it is not landscape. It is substrate that was never meant to be seen, interrupted now by infrastructure, by condensation, by the insect bodies pressed against it. The erosion parameter does not apply. Nothing moves here. Nothing grinds, nothing weathers, nothing deposits. In a sealed environment, in stable cold, with no airflow and no particulate introduction, surfaces do not change. There should be decades of dust. There is none. There should be mineral deposits from the evaporating moisture, white bloom on the metal surfaces. There is none. Everything is exactly as it was the day the chamber was sealed, except for the insects, and except for the water.

And that is the strangeness of it. Not the insects, not the moisture, not the cold. The *stasis*. The moisture falls and falls and the drops land in the same places and the insect distribution on each surface is constant, not because the insects are motionless but because their motion is a closed loop — they hatch, they cluster in the warm places, they die, they are replaced. The sound stays at the same frequency. The amber light stays at the same dimness. The temperature holds at three degrees. This is not a frozen moment. It is a maintained one. The seals are still functioning. The residual power systems are still running. The weep hole is still blocked, and the water pools in the cavity, and the moisture cycles, and the insects cycle, and the temperature cycles, and everything cycles within a range so narrow it is functionally zero. The chamber was built to sustain. It still sustains. It was built to maintain equilibrium. It has achieved one. It will maintain it indefinitely, or until the seals fail, or until the last insect dies, or until the last circuit fails — whichever comes last.

The airlock is not large. Three meters by five, roughly. A room you could walk the perimeter of in thirty seconds. Yet it contains this. Every surface is built. Every surface is coated. Every cubic meter hums. You press your ear to the inner wall and feel the tone in the metal itself — a steady, unmodulated frequency, the sound of equilibrium, the sound of a system running exactly as designed, decades past the point where it was supposed to stop.
