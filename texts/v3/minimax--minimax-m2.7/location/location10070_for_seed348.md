# The Undersea

You descend through an airlock into a world that has no memory of sky.

The station—though no one calls it that anymore, if anyone remains to call it anything—lies buried beneath a city that once mattered. You are in its gut now, its mechanical viscera, a cathedral-scale hollow of arched tile and wrought-iron latticework that carries the weight of earth and the breathing of machines. The ceiling vaults forty meters above, lost in shadow, ribbed with ducts and pipes that branch like the bones of something vast and long-dead. Normal light persists here but weakly, filtered through gratings that admit only a thin amber from sources above—sources you cannot see, sources that never vary. The illumination pools on platforms, halos around junction boxes, leaving the corners and the deeper alcoves in a sepulchral half-dark. The light does not change. It does not know how.

Moisture is everywhere in the way water is everywhere in old stone—it has made its peace with this place. Condensation beads on metal railings, on the brass fittings of the ventilation housings, on the ceramic tiles that line the walls in repeating panels of ochre and brown. Water seeps through cracks in the mortar, traces dark veins down the concrete, pools in low places where the grade dips just enough to catch it. The air is damp and cool and tastes of rust and iron and something older, something mineral, something that has been wet for so long it has forgotten any other state. Your breath fogs. Your clothes grow heavy.

No plants live here. You understand this not as absence but as law. This is a mineral world, a world of stone and iron and tile and the slick surfaces of things that were never meant to support life. Fungi appear sometimes—pale lobes of something that might be fungal, clinging to damp wood that has somehow survived, feeding on accumulated grime—but the boundary is firm: nothing green, nothing flowering, nothing that remembers sunlight. The creatures here have adapted to a world without chlorophyll, without soil, without the whole apparatus of terrestrial life. They do not miss it. They have never known it.

The creatures. The fauna. You will call it fauna because you have no other word, but the word is insufficient. They are not numerous in the way that rats are numerous, present and manageable. They are numerous in the way that water is numerous, in the way that darkness is numerous—a presence so constant it becomes environment, so saturated it becomes the defining feature of the space. They fill the station. They fill its margins and its interstices. They pour through the grated walkways and cluster in the corners where the lights don't reach, their bodies pressed together in fevers of motion, their sounds a continuous murmur that rises and falls like breathing. When you walk, they part around you—a wave of motion, a rustling, a chittering that sounds almost like language—and then close behind, as if you had never interrupted anything.

The sound is everywhere. This is not a quiet place. The ventilation systems push air through ducts that have not been serviced in living memory, a mechanical breath that never varies, never quickens, never slows. Beneath it, the hum of transformers and the distant rumble of things that move through the tunnels on fixed schedules, engines that have been running since before you were born and will continue running after you are forgotten. Speakers crackle in the deep alcoves, broadcasting announcements in a language you almost understand, the words worn away to a phoneme-soup that could be anything, could be comfort, could be warning. The creatures contribute their own frequencies: the scurry of feet on tile, the flutter of wings in the vaulted dark, the low constant thrum of ten thousand bodies moving in a space meant for hundreds. The sound does not stop. The sound has never stopped. The sound is the only thing that has never stopped.

The erosion is patient here, the way erosion is patient in all old things. Water has worked at the mortar, at the grout between tiles, at the seams where metal meets stone. Rust has bloomed on every ferrous surface, blooming and flaking and blooming again. The platforms show the wear of countless feet, shallow grooves worn into the tile at the places where passengers once stood—and perhaps still stand, in some sense, in some other shift of the world, though you cannot see them. The infrastructure has settled into itself, found its equilibrium, stopped changing because change would require more than this world can give. Nothing new happens here. Nothing old is repaired. The trains run on their schedules, the lights maintain their vigil, the creatures persist in their numbers. The station is a held breath, a frozen moment, a system running in perfect stability because it has been sealed away from everything that might destabilize it.

You walk the platforms. The creatures watch you with eyes that have adapted to darkness—large, pale, liquid, set in faces that do not look quite like any face you have seen above. They do not flee. They do not advance. They simply are, a presence as solid as the tile beneath your feet, as permanent as the iron ribs of the vaulting above. You are the visitor here. You are the anomaly, the disturbance, the brief pattern that will dissolve and leave them to their patient waiting. The airlock seals behind you, and the sound of the world above—wind, rain, the green rustling of things that grow—fades to nothing, to silence, to the low hum of machines that will never stop, in a station that will never change, beneath a city that perhaps no longer exists, attended by creatures that perhaps were never meant to be.
