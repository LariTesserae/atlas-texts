# The Singing Basin

The basin sings. This is the first thing you learn about the place, even before you understand it: the way sound accumulates here, pools against the hard valley walls, finding channels carved by water that has been working these rocks since they were young and sharp. The water is everywhere at the surface—not flooding, but present, threading through cracks and seeping from seams in the stone, collecting in dark pools that mirror the dim gray light and give it back dulled.

The basin floor is gray-green, a color that belongs to nothing alive. It is mineral crust: silica and calcite and something that might beiteite or might be something with no name in any language that still speaks of living things. The crust cracks underfoot with a high, ringing note, and beneath it the water moves in darkness, and the sound it makes is the loudest thing here—not rushing, not violent, but constant. A deep hum that lives in the bones of the stone itself.

There are no plants. There was never anything here that needed light in the way plants need it. What there is instead is density: the basin floor is not flat but folded, creased, thrown into small ridges and hollows that catch the water and hold it in shadow. The water pools in these hollows and does not evaporate because the air is always damp, always close, pressing against the skin like something remembering what breath used to feel like. Condensation forms on the undersides of overhangs and drips in a rhythm that is not quite regular, that seems to hesitate, as if waiting.

In the scattered places where the crust has cracked more deeply, you can see that the rock beneath is young. The erosion here is early work: edges that should be rounded are still sharp, formations that should be softened are still angular. Something made this basin recently, in geological terms—tectonic settling, or a change in the water's path that happened while dinosaurs were still dying elsewhere, while the forests were still being forests they could never have imagined. The basin remembers its own birth. The stone has not yet forgotten how to hold a fracture.

The structures are small and far apart and broken. They might be ruins. They might be something else: formations that water built, mineral deposits that grew in patterns that meant something or meant nothing. There are spires ofiteite that rise from the basin floor in clusters, their surfaces grooved by water and time, and sometimes the grooves make angles that seem deliberate, seem to point toward something no longer here. The largest of them is perhaps waist-high, its top open to the sky, its interior dark and still. You do not want to reach your hand inside.

What moves here moves rarely, and you do not see it. Sometimes, at the edge of hearing, there is a sound like a call: high, thin, questioning. It does not come from any throat you would recognize. It might be a creature that evolved here in the deep water, blind and slow, using sound the way light is used elsewhere. It might be the water itself, remembering what it was before it learned to be only water. You do not investigate the sound. The basin discourages investigation. The basin wants you to move through it, to be part of its slow rhythm, its constant drip and hum and ring.

The gray light does not change much. The basin is always dim, always cool, always wet enough that the air feels like a second skin, heavy and close. Water drips from the overhangs into pools that ring with a sound that seems too large for them, that fills the hollows and rolls out across the basin floor. The sound does not echo so much as it accumulates. It layers. It becomes something present, something solid, a medium you move through.

This is a world that found one thing to be—a mineral world that decided to be wet, and cold, and loud, and strange—and it has been that for so long that the strangeness has worn smooth, has become the way things are. The structures that are scattered here were perhaps built by something that came before the strangeness settled into itself, something that looked at this basin and saw in it what it needed: a place to leave a mark, a place to build. Or perhaps they were always like this. Perhaps the spires grew the way they did because the basin wanted them, because the water and the minerals and the slow cold pressure found their own way to make something that could hold water, that could ring when struck, that could last longer than anything soft could last.
