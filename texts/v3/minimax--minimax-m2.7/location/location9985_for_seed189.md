# The Gullet of Green

The sound arrives first — a deep resonant hum that vibrates in the sternum rather than the ears. It comes from everywhere and nowhere, the way pressure does, and in the wet warmth of this place, you understand that sound is a kind of weather.

You are standing in a pocket of stone no larger than a generous cellar, carved from pale granite softened by recent water. The erosion here is fresh — the kind the earth makes when it sweats. Water still moves through the rock, seeping through invisible channels, pooling in mineral cups that glisten under the ordinary light. The walls weep. This is a place of condensation and slow seepage, warm as a living body, and the granite has only recently given way to create this hollow.

But the granite is not what you would see, if you looked at the walls.

Everything is covered. Everything grows.

A substrate of living green coats every surface — not moss, not exactly, but something that serves the same purpose and has forgotten the name. It is thick in places, papery and translucent in others, and where it clusters thickest, it becomes architecture: shelves of spongy growth, curtains of trailing filaments, canopies of broad leaves that unfurl from nothing you can call root or stem. The plants here do not distinguish between themselves. A single organism — or a thousand that have forgotten they are separate — fills every available surface with wet, breathing tissue.

The air smells of rain that hasn't fallen. The moisture is constant — not mist, not drizzle, but the feeling of air that has never known dryness. Condensation beads on every leaf, every stone, every visible surface, and the dripping is musical, arrhythmic, a kind of percussion underlying the hum.

You hear it more than you see it at first: the processes of growth. The tiny sounds of cell division, of roots seeking purchase in stone, of sporulation. These should be inaudible, but here the boundary between silence and sound has worn thin, or perhaps the plants have simply refused to keep their lives quiet. The gullet is active — everything growing, spreading, reaching, digesting stone and water and whatever else feeds this improbable abundance.

There is almost nothing here besides the plants and the wet stone and the light that enters through no visible source — a warm, diffuse glow that seems to come from the air itself, as if the atmosphere is phosphorescent with humidity. Almost nothing. But almost is not nothing.

A pale fish — eyeless, finless, a thing more like a lung than a fish — hangs motionless in a seep-pool at the base of the wall. It does not breathe. It seems to wait. Beyond it, in a crack too narrow for anything larger than a finger, you see the ghost of movement: something that might be centipede, might be root, might be neither. The fauna is rare here. This place is not for them. It is for the plants, and the plants have made it for themselves.

The weirdness is not in any single element but in the failure of the place to feel like a place. The light should cast shadows, but it doesn't. The sound should echo, but it doesn't — it simply is, omnipresent, a hum that has no source because the source is the space itself, the living metabolism of a pocket ecosystem that should not exist. The granite is soft with moisture, but you cannot scratch it with your fingernail. The plants grow downward and upward with equal ease, defying some sense of direction you cannot name.

You are in something that might be an organ. A ventricle or a follicle in the body of a world that breathes green instead of air. The walls expand and contract — you are imagining this, but you are also sure you are not — and the hum rises and falls with them, and the water beads and drips, and the vegetation shifts, patient, vast, total, and the space remains nearly empty, almost hollow, a warm wet pocket in the stone where the real has simply stopped insisting on itself so hard.

The fish in the pool flickers. Something has entered from a crack you cannot see. The sound changes — not louder, but different, a harmonic that was not there before, and the light seems to pulse, and the plants turn — slowly, imperceptibly — and you understand that you are being noticed, that the gullet of green has another feature you had not counted, something that was waiting for you, something that has been listening all along to the sound of your heartbeat in this warm and breathing dark.
