The plateau sits high. It always has. Whatever mountains rose here are long gone, worn to gentle swells by the patient work of time, so old that even the erosion has eroded. The surface is hard and dry — mineral crust over salt-crustedite, faintly dark, broken by occasional fissures that drop vertically into nothing and carry no moisture. Your feet kick up a thin, pale dust that hangs and drifts and settles, because there is nothing to move it, no wind strong enough, no water to weigh it down, no life to stir it. It has been settling for so long that it has achieved equilibrium: each particle precisely where it will remain. The air tastes of warmth and metal. Not unpleasant, not clean. Just — held.

The city spreads across this surface in every direction. Hadrath, if it was ever called anything else, no longer remembers its true name. The structures are low, wide, built for density rather than height — block after block of industrial construction, tunnels and covered walkways and low buildings whose functions have flattened into the same dark silhouette. Ventilation stacks rise like broken teeth. Cooling towers stand in their clusters, long cooled, their chimneys exhaling nothing visible. Pipes run along the ground in bundles, along walls, down into basements and maintenance tunnels, carrying heat and coolant and the remnants of something that was once processed here. Every surface is warm to the touch. The warmth comes from below — from the engines and reactors and processing systems that still run, deep in the infrastructure, maintained by machines that were maintained by machines whose origins are lost to the same erosion that wore the mountains flat.

Light exists here, but barely. Amber and deep orange, sourceless and dim, bleeding from behind grating and through dusty window-glass and up from grated floors — enough to navigate, enough to see that the ground is dark and the structures are darker, enough to make the shadows deeper rather than dispelling them. The glow has never been brighter and will never be dimmer. It hums at the frequency of the machines, a low vibration in the air that the eye learns to stop seeing and the ear begins to confuse with silence.

There is no sound, or nearly none. The machines below produce a sound too deep and diffuse to call noise — a pressure, a hum, felt as much as heard, like holding your hand against a wall that is breathing. Occasionally a metal plate contracts or expands and clicks once, two sharp notes, and then silence re-fills the space with the weight of deep time. Wind moves across the plateau in slow currents, but it is a warm wind and carries nothing.

Nothing lives here. The surface is too dry, the light too faint, the temperature too stable for any cycle of growth or decay. In the deep maintenance tunnels, where the machines run hottest and condensation gathers in narrow channels cut into the stone floor, something persists — a thin ecology of organisms so simple they are barely recognizable as life, feeding on moisture that the machinery produces and gives off like breath. They have no name. They are not beautiful. They are the minimum required for life to exist in this place, and they have existed at this minimum for longer than the plateau was old when the first of the machines was built. They will persist until the machines stop, and the machines do not stop.

The strangeness is not dramatic. The buildings do not lean at wrong angles. The light is not the wrong color in any way the eye can name. But there is something in the structure of it — in the fact that every system here, every pipe and duct and tunnel and tower, was built for a purpose that is no longer being served, and continues to serve that purpose anyway, perfectly, without adjustment, without end. The warmth does not fluctuate. The light does not fade. The dust does not move. The machines below do not accelerate or slow. The plateau has reached its final form, and the final form is this: a warm dark place of ancient industrial construction, where the dust of ages lies in perfect stillness and the hum of engines sounds like silence, and everything that will happen here has already happened, and will happen again, and nothing will change.
