# The Rookery at Silt Crag

The cave mouth exhales before you—a breath of cold, mineral-sharp air that tastes of iron and age. You are standing inside a volcanic fissure in a mountain so young its bones still show through. The mountain has not yet learned to be kind to flesh.

You are very high. Your ears ring with the altitude and the cold makes your joints stiff even in summer. The moisture here is not water, exactly—it is the mountain's sweat, condensation that forms on the rocks at dawn and drips in irregular percussion through the dark. Pools collect in pockets where the ancient lava once pooled, their surfaces still as black mirrors, disturbed only when something breaks the surface and vanishes.

The scale of this place is impossible to determine at first. The fissure narrows and widens, tightens and releases, a throat of stone that opens into chambers large enough to hold cathedrals and closes again to corridors barely wide enough to turn around in. The walls close in on you from all sides—not threatening but *present*, enfolding, a space that has boundaries even when you cannot see them. You are inside something. You have always been inside something.

The darkness is not absence but presence here—it has weight and texture, pressing against your eyes, filling the air with almost-solid pressure. Your torch opens a wound in it, a raw sphere of yellow light that the dark fights back against at every edge. Beyond the light's reach, the darkness is absolute. You stop reaching for it.

And the sound. At first you mistake it for wind, for the mountain's breath—but no, this is something else. A dense, layered thing, a wall of noise that presses against you. It comes from everywhere: the high chittering shriek of bats, thousands of them, their colonies so thick that individual voices dissolve into collective chorus. The soft clatter of cave crickets, their bodies covering the walls in grey-brown clusters, each one making its small percussive contribution. The distant, rhythmic dripping of condensation into mineral pools, syncopated against the deeper sounds—something in the deep pools, something in the walls, something in the spaces between stone.

The fauna is not merely present. It is *overwhelming*—so dense that you cannot take a step without displacing something. The floor writhes with isopods, pale and eyeless, their bodies stacking and overlapping in patterns that seem almost deliberate. Cave beetles the size of your thumb patrol in patient lines. Cave spiders have woven webs so thick and numerous that they form a second architecture, a city of silk strung between rock formations. In the deepest pools—those still black mirrors—eyeless fish move in slow, deliberate circuits, their bodies white and translucent, organs visible beneath the skin. The bats above you hang in clusters so dense they look like fruit, like growths on the ceiling, their small eyes glinting where your light finds them.

You have not seen all of them. Your light is a small thing here, and the cave is large, and the dark is full. There are movements at the edge of the light, arrivals and departures you cannot account for. Shapes that suggest themselves and vanish. The sound changes sometimes—drops into registers too low or too high for human hearing, as if the cave is speaking to something beyond your range. You feel these frequencies in your teeth, in the base of your skull.

The vegetation—if it can be called that—is sparse but strange. Mats of white fungus cling to the walls near the pools, soft and slow-growing, feeding on the endless rain of bat guano from above. Lichen, but not like any lichen you've seen: pale, almost luminous, forming patterns that look almost like script. The mountain drips minerals onto everything, and where the water touches, thin filaments of something greenish-gold grow in hair-like clusters. Life here is not lush but it is *patient*, persistent in its strangeness, adapted to this darkness so thoroughly that light would burn it.

And there is strangeness beyond the fauna, beyond the white fish and the eyeless things. The rock formations seem to breathe—or you imagine they do. The pools reflect surfaces that don't match the ceiling. The sound sometimes comes from the wrong direction. You turn and the torch catches something in the corner of your eye: a shape that is not a bat, not a beetle, not a spider, something shaped wrong— and when you look directly, it's gone. Or it's always been there. You are no longer certain.

You are not alone in the Rookery. You have never been alone here. The bats know you came; the cave knows you are here now; the pools have recorded your ripples. When you leave—and you will leave, though the exit seems further now than when you entered—the mountain will hold your shape in its walls, the imprint of your passage pressed into the young stone, waiting for the next ones who come to wonder at the sharpness of the world.
