# The Underhalls of Brethem

The town of Brethem exists beneath the valley floor, carved into stone that has not yet learned to be old. The walls bear the marks of picks and hand-drills, the chisel-scars still raw where centuries should have worn them smooth. This is a young underworld — the chambers opened within living memory, or so the elders say, and the rock still weeps at every joint and seam.

Water moves through it constantly. Not flooding, not danger — just presence, a perpetual dampness that beads on every surface and pools in corners where drainage lags behind. The air tastes of mineral cold and wet earth. Occasional drips mark time in the deeper corridors. Channels cut into the floor carry the seepage toward collection cisterns, and the citizens have learned to live where water permits.

It is cool here — never cold enough to freeze, never warm enough for comfort. A perpetual underbreath of chill that settles in the bones. The inhabitants dress accordingly, in heavy woven cloth and leather, and the common halls stay populated simply because shared body heat makes the damp more bearable.

The streets of Brethem are narrow and layered. Where the natural caverns permitted, the founders built up and over — suspended walkways, tiered platforms, chambers hollowed from solid stone and connected by stairs. The construction is primitive: hand-cut blocks, timber beams, joinery pegged rather than nailed. You see no metal here save the simplest tools — knives, awls, tent-hooks. Everything is manual, everything labor-intensive. A society organized around the work of hands.

The density is what strikes you first. Brethem is full — packed tight with bodies and belongings, with stalls and storehouses, with livestock in penned alcoves and workshops in carved recesses. There is no waste space. Every square foot serves. The people are thick here, moving through thoroughfares that groan under decades of compaction, past walls blackened by generations of torch-smoke.

Light comes from oil and tallow, from fires in iron baskets, from the rare shafts that pierce upward to daylight. It is enough. The light pools and moves, casting shadows across packed-earth floors and slick-stone walls.

Vegetation is sparse — moss in the wettest corners, fungus cultivated in dark chambers, tough herbs hung in bundles from smoke-blackened rafters. The air is too damp for most growth, but the people grow what they can, and the market-square traders hawk dried greens and pickled roots alongside hand-carved tools and hide bundles.

Fauna is rare. Some rats in the granary, a few bats in the upper chimneys. The town has not yet accumulated the ecological depth of older settlements. You might hear, at odd hours, the sound of something larger moving in the unmapped passages — but nothing has been confirmed, and the townsfolk prefer not to investigate.

Sound travels strangely in young stone. Brethem is mostly quiet — the murmur of voices, the splash of water, the creak of timber settling into its fixtures. But there is underlying motion: water cycling through its channels, stone slowly compacting, the daily rhythms of a society that has organized itself around the damp and the dark.

It is an ordinary place. There is no magic here, no strangeness. Just people living beneath the earth, in a town still learning how to be old.
