A basin. That is the first thing that settles into the understanding as you stand at the edge of the plain: you are inside something, not standing outside it. The horizon curves almost imperceptibly — not enough to see as a curve, but enough to feel that the world is bending around you, that your vantage point is not on the world but within it. A basin the size of a continent, walled by low ridges of dark mineral that catch the light at their crests and hold shadow in their folds. The sky above is pale and still. There is no wind.

The ground is firm and dry. Not sand — salt would be closer, but it is not quite salt. It is mineralite pressed and compacted by time into something that yields slightly underfoot, pale gray and faintly crystalline, and it stretches in every direction until it meets the structures.

The structures.

They rise from the basin floor in clusters, in chains, in formations that suggest intention without containing any. Call them growths. Call them colonies. Call them — but the word *city* will not leave, even though nothing here is built. They are dense vertical formations of biological material — a woody, fibrous substance that branches and joins and branches again, reaching six, eight, twelve meters into the still air. From a distance they read as a canopy, but up close you see that they have no leaves. They are all stem and fiber, all architecture and no foliage, and the spaces between them are narrow — corridors, alleys, the tight passages of something packed to capacity. You could walk between them, but not easily. You would have to turn sideways. The basin floor between the structures is bare mineralite, cracked in places with fine lines like dried mud, though there is no mud here, has never been mud here, and yet moisture moves through this place in ways that leave no surface trace.

Between the structures, the vegetation grows. Not on them — in the spaces beside them, around them, threading through the mineralite in a low, dense mat of roots and rhizomes and short broad-leafed plants whose surfaces are warm to the touch and faintly damp. They grow in the shade of the structures, and where the structures thin out, they grow thicker, until the boundary between colony and plain is a place of extraordinary density — root tangles visible above the surface, root mats thick enough to walk on in places, and among them the small, the moving, the alive. Insects and burrowing things, soft-bodied and quick, threading through the mat in constant motion. Lizards of two or three kinds, flat and wide, pressed close to the warm ground. Things that climb — not the structures, but the roots, small creatures with grippy pads on their feet, moving vertically through the root-matrix as though it were a wall. The fauna is not loud. None of it calls. There is no vocalization, no song, no alarm. Only the soft sound of movement — the whisper of something crossing the mat, the faint click of a limb against fiber, the settling sound of weight redistributing. Then stillness. Then movement again somewhere else.

The air is warm. Not oppressive, not stifling — a warmth that is simply the condition of the world, as ambient and undramatic as the light, which falls evenly from a pale sky without visible source. There is no sun. Or rather: the sun is present but diffuse, its light scattered by a thin atmospheric haze that removes its directionality, so that shadows fall from everything at once and from nothing clearly. Shadows are soft here. They do not define edges. They pool without origin. You can see far — the basin is immense — but the far distance has no landmarks, only the faint shimmer of heat, and the structures in the distance are the same shapes as the structures near you, the same clusters and chains and density, repeating toward a horizon that never resolves into anything different.

The geological texture is present everywhere if you look for it. The mineralite surface is young — not in the way of fresh-laid pavement but in the way of stone still becoming stone, still compacting, still developing the hairline fractures that will one day deepen into channels. In places the surface has buckled, raised into a ridge a hand's height above the surrounding plain, and at the ridge's crest you can see where the material is still changing — different in color, different in texture, rawer, as though the basin floor is not finished yet, as though the ground is still deciding what it is. These buckles and swells are not mountains. They will never be mountains. They are the smallest possible expression of geological force — enough to change the texture of walking, enough to create a sheltered side where the mat plants grow thicker, enough to make a place for a creature to rest in the minimal shade of a six-centimeter rise. They are the erosion-as-emergence that the parameters named: not worn away, but still arriving.

The moisture is here. It is in the root mat. It is in the structures, which are cool to the touch near their bases and warm higher up, as though something is moving through them. It is in the air — a faint heaviness, a thickness that settles in the lungs not as discomfort but as presence. The world is not dry. The world has no water on its surface, but the world is saturated in the way that a living body is saturated: fluid held within, fluid moving through, fluid everywhere inside the system even though none of it is exposed. The plants do not hold rain. They hold something older, something drawn from deep in the mineralite or exhaled by the structures or condensed from the thin, warm atmosphere in ways that leave no dew, no puddle, no evidence of where the water has been except that the plants are alive and the ground is not cracked into dust and the creatures that move through the mat are not desiccated.

The creatures are everywhere. Not in swarms — not in the overwhelming, visible density of a insect cloud or a bird flock — but in the way that a forest floor is full: present at every scale, in every direction, if you are still long enough to see them. The mat hides many. The structures shelter many more. At ground level the density is highest: layers of small life occupying the same three-dimensional space through vertical partitioning, the way a forest canopy does — ground-dwellers near the mineralite, root-dwellers in the mat, climbers in the lower structures, perchers in the upper branches, each level full, each level separate, all of it sharing the same air, the same warmth, the same quiet.

And the quiet. That is the strangest thing, if anything here can be called strange. The world is full of life and full of process — you can see it moving, see the subtle shifts in the mat, the slow adjustments of the structures (they move, almost imperceptibly, a branch reorienting over the course of an hour), see the creatures in their layers and passages — and none of it makes sound. Or rather: all of it makes sound, but the sound is absorbed by the sheer density of material, by the fiber of the structures and the mat of the roots and the warm mineralite that is neither acoustically dead nor alive but simply present, absorbing and softening and holding every sound until it is less than a whisper, until the world is full of near-silence, a texture of presence rather than a texture of noise. Your own footsteps make a sound. Your own breathing makes a sound. But the world — the vast, packed, living world — breathes with you, silently, the way a crowd breathes when it is too crowded to speak.
