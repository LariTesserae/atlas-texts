The basin has been here since before memory. It is a shallow depression in low country, so shallow it might almost be a flaw in the flatness, except that the water pools where it should not and the air hangs differently here, thick and breathing. The erosion is ancient past any reckoning—the stone of the basin floor has been worn to a softness, a patience, as if it has waited so long to be this shape that it no longer remembers what it was before. The land around the basin is nearly featureless, the hills long since milled to dust and carried away, and only the depression itself remains, a record of some vanished violence.

Water comes from everywhere and nowhere. It seeps upward through the ancient stone, not springs exactly but the slow exudation of a saturated world, and at night it condenses on every surface in a constant fine precipitation. The rocks weep. The leaves of the broad-bladed plants weep. Even the strange structures—the ones built from something that looks almost like bone but rings like glass when struck—collect moisture on their surfaces and let it run down in thin bright lines. The basin is perpetually wet, perpetually warm, and the air carries a green smell so dense it has weight.

The vegetation is lush but not crowded. Individual plants stand in space from one another, their fronds and canopies defining paths and clearings rather than massing into wall. You can see through it. You can see the creatures.

The fauna is too much. The fauna is the thing you cannot look at directly. Not insects—they would be easier, you could call them insects. The creatures here have too many symmetries, or not enough, or the wrong ones. Their bodies are organized according to principles that work but that you cannot hold in your mind for long. They move through the plants with a fluid predator's ease, and there are so many of them, thousands at least, filling the basin in a slow continuous motion, and the basin is not large—a cathedral could fit inside, or a grove, or the remnants of a single building whose walls are now low and broken and colonized by the weeping stone.

The building is the only structure that remains intact. The rest are partial remains, faint marks in the earth, foundations that have almost returned to the soil. Something was built here once. Something built from beyond-modern engineering, from materials that current physics only half-describes, and the basin held it, and the basin has been returning it to itself ever since. The strange structures look organic but they are not. They hum at frequencies just below hearing, and their surfaces are warm, and the creatures seem to avoid them with a carefulness that suggests either reverence or fear.

The light is bright but not harsh. It comes from above the basin in a diffuse fall, sourceless, and it illuminates everything with a clarity that makes the individual creature visible: the fine hair along a spine, the compound eyes, the mouthparts that open and close in patterns you almost understand. The creatures do not make sounds. The basin is quiet enough to hear the water moving through stone, the faint hum of the structures, the wet small sounds of vegetation growing.

But there is movement everywhere. The creatures hunt each other. The water rises and falls in daily cycles. The moisture condenses and evaporates and condenses again. The plants turn toward the light. Something is always happening, always being eaten or grown or transformed, and the basin continues its ancient patient erosion, holding all of it, this overwhelming fauna, this strange technology, this small bounded wet world that is still here, still going, after all this time.
