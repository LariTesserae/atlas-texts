# The Weeping Architecture of Kel-Veth

High on the Kel-Veth Plateau, at an altitude where the air holds moisture like a secret it cannot quite keep, the city rises from the rock—or rather, the city *is* the rock. Everything here has been made. The terraces that step down the plateau's eastern face, the channels that carve slow paths between structures, the facades that catch the thin bright light and throw it back in pale fragments—all of it constructed, all of it seamless, no natural substrate anywhere in sight. The builders are long gone, or have become something else, or perhaps were never biological at all. What remains is their work, dense as packed earth, stretching to the horizon in every direction.

There are no plants. None. The moisture that seeps from every joint and seam, that pools in low places and runs in thin rivulets down vertical surfaces, has nothing to drink. The walls weep. The floors weep. In the mornings, especially, condensation gathers on every horizontal plane and drips in irregular rhythms, the only percussion in a landscape that has forgotten how to make sounds. The water must go somewhere—it gathers in channels, fills basins, disappears into drains that lead down into depths no one visits anymore. But nothing grows from it. Nothing blooms or sprouts or decays. The wetness is a property of the world, not a condition that sustains life.
