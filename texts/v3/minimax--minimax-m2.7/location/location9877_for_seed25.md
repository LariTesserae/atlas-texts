The stone remembers water. Not as flow now, but as memory — a sheen that survives in places where surfaces dip and the air goes still. Thin moisture beads on mineral faces in the low places, enough to taste metallic on the tongue if you pressed it to the rock, enough to keep everything slick and glistening when the ambient dampness rolls through. There are no rivers, no pools, but everywhere the geometry of former wetness: carved channels that haven't carried anything in ages, hollows that were shaped by fluid. The landscape is a dry thing dreaming of its wet past, and the present damp keeps that dream alive without fulfilling it.

What covers the ground is not vegetation. Nothing with chlorophyll, nothing with roots reaching into soil that isn't there. What there is instead: a skin. A pale, almost luminous layer that spreads across the grey-brown stone in sheets, pulsing faintly in the darkness with its own slow metabolisms. It is fungal in the way that word is insufficient — it is membrane and network and process all at once, a living film that grows and recedes and grows again in waves. Where it thickens it forms ridges, crusts, low structures that aren't quite organisms but aren't quite geology either. The skin breathes. You can hear it, or think you can, if you stand still enough.

And it moves. The skin is not alone.

The fauna is everywhere. Overwhelming doesn't capture it — the word implies a quantity that could be counted. Here it cannot be. The air swarms. Crawling things the size of your hand, many-jointed, legs adapted to grip the slick stone and the slicker skin, bodies adapted to a darkness that never lifts. They move in layers, in waves, in constant motion that has nothing to do with anything so simple as hunger. They feed on the skin. They feed on each other. They cluster in densities that blur into motion, into noise, into a texture that covers everything.

Sound is a physical presence here. Clicking, chitinous, endless. The scrape of legs on stone. The soft wet sounds of feeding. The deeper bass of larger things moving somewhere in the dark. It builds and builds and never stops. To stand in it is to stand inside a machine that will never be turned off.

The erosion speaks of time. The stone has been here long enough to forget its own sharp edges. It has been worn, polished, pitted by the uncountable passage of small bodies over uncountable generations. The skin grows where the stone is softest and wears away where it is hardest. The creatures die and their bodies add to the sediment — a fine grey powder that fills the low places, that holds the moisture, that feeds the skin, that feeds the things that eat the skin and the things that eat those things. It is a world that runs on itself. It has been running on itself for longer than anything else in this region.

The darkness is not complete. There is a dim grey light that leaks from nowhere and everywhere — enough to see by, barely, enough to reveal the layers of motion and the slick surfaces and the low structures of the skin. Enough to see that the landscape extends in every direction, open and featureless in the way of things that have been covered rather than shaped. No mountains, no valleys, just the flat low stone surface going on and on with all its density of life.

Nothing built. Nothing made. The creatures build nothing — they are the built thing, the result of processes that require no design. The skin builds nothing — it is the architecture of its own continuation. What you see is what has always been here, and what has always been here is alive in every direction, in every layer, in every moment. The coolness is constant. The moisture is constant. The noise is constant. The crawling, feeding, dying, growing, moving, overwhelming constant world that has no name and no need for one.
