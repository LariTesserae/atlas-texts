# The Conservatory at the Crest

A chamber. A room-sized valley pressed into the upper dark of the world, where the air is so thick with moisture it has weight.

The walls are stone—or what serves for stone here: composite, mineral-bonded, cast in panels that meet in seams still visible, still new. Water weeps from the joints where panel meets panel, tracing black threads down the grey. Condensation gathers on every surface in fat drops that merge and fall, never quite pooling, always finding a slope, a channel, a grate. The moisture here feels deliberate, choreographed. Someone mapped its path before it fell.

Lush. The word undersells what is happening on these walls. Moss has been introduced—or has introduced itself—in thick quilts that are still learning to adhere. Ferns sprout from gaps in the paneling, their fronds pale with the eagerness of new growth. Vines have been encouraged to climb the support columns, wrapping around metal frameworks, their tendrils probing, testing. The greenery has a quality of *arrival*, as though it has just finished disembarking and is still orienting itself to its new home.

There is no fauna. Almost none. A single moth, grey and battered, rests on the underside of a leaf and does not move. Perhaps it is sleeping. Perhaps it is dead and has been incorporated into the moss without anyone noticing. There is no sound except the soft, arrhythmic tick of condensation striking metal somewhere in the upper dark, and the almost-subliminal hum of pumps, the circulation of water through channels cut into the floor. The pumps are industrial—constant, reliable, ugly in their utility—and they provide the pulse around which everything else has been arranged.

And the floor. Ah, the floor.

It is composite, yes, like the walls, but someone was still learning how to make it. The surface bears the memory of casting: subtle waves, shallow bowls, a topology that does not quite know what it is for. Erosion has not had time to soften any of it. The channels for water runoff are too regular, too geometric, and yet they are already staining with mineral deposits, with the first faint bloom of algae. In a thousand years, if this place were allowed to weather, the channels would erode into something organic. The edges would soften. The panels would separate and the rock beneath would emerge.

But that will not happen here. The pumps keep running. The moisture cycles. The vegetation thickens, layer by layer, while the substrate beneath remains sealed in its own permanence, its own artificial childhood, forever.

The light comes from above, from fixtures that buzz faintly and cast everything in a warm yellow-white glow. It is the light of an earlier century, perhaps, when this place was new—when the engineers were still arguing about drainage and someone insisted on the weep channels instead of drains, and the argument was recorded in the construction notes and now exists only as a mystery for whoever comes after.

There is something sealed in here, something the conservatory protects. A cabinet. A chamber within the chamber. Its walls are glass, fogged at the edges, and no amount of condensation has yet revealed its contents. It hums faintly. It is the source of the water—or one of them—and the moisture here rises from it and settles on the walls and feeds the moss and the ferns and the patient, probing vines, and they grow, and they thicken, and they do not know what they are growing toward.

The moth, grey and still. The tick of condensation. The hum of machinery. The slow, still-alien lushness of everything, pressed close.
