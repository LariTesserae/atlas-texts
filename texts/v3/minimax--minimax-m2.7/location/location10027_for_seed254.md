# The Undergarden

You descend through a narrow stairwell, and the air changes. At the bottom, the walls themselves are wet — not just damp but actively weeping, moisture beading on every surface and trickling in thin sheets down the worked stone. The water comes from everywhere and nowhere: seeping from cracks you can't see the tops of, pooling in corners, rising as vapor that condenses on your skin. The settlement was built here because of this water, once — underground springs feeding wells and cisterns and the cooling systems of engines that still run somewhere in the walls. Now the water has become the condition of everything.

And the plants have come because of the water.

What you find below is not a greenhouse. A greenhouse implies a boundary between cultivated and wild, interior and exterior. This place has no such boundary. The plants *are* the architecture. Vines the thickness of your arm have been woven into railings, railings that still function, still bear weight. Trees — gnarled, reaching, their roots split across the ceiling — have been encouraged, shaped, guided over centuries into living columns that hold up vaults of worked stone. Where the stone has cracked, roots have filled the gaps. Where roots have died back, moss has taken hold, and then ferns, and then something higher. The whole space is a single interwoven mass: green and gray and black, living and dead and decomposing, no space between.

You are standing in what was once a central square, or perhaps a market hall. The iron lamp-posts still stand, but their light comes from gas-jets beneath globes of glass so thick with algae you can barely see the flame within. The light is dim, constant, warm-edged. You can see perhaps thirty feet in any direction before the green closes in. Somewhere above you — or perhaps beside you, orientation is difficult — a steam-pump sighs and gasps, the rhythm of a body rather than a machine. The sound is loud in the way that a river is loud, or rain: present, continuous, occupying the lower frequencies of the air until you stop hearing it and start hearing only the space it creates.

There are no animals. You have noticed this slowly, the way you notice the absence of a smell. No insects humming in the flowers. No birds in the high branches. No rats in the litter at the base of the roots. The plants do not seem to suffer from this. Perhaps they do not need pollination. Perhaps this is simply a world that has moved past the need for fauna, has simplified itself down to water and green and the slow digestion of stone.

The temperature is warm — not the warmth of sunlight but the warmth of breath, of metabolism, of decay. Your clothes cling damply to your skin. Water drips from a hanging root and strikes the stone floor with a sound like a distant clock. The air tastes of soil and iron, of something sweet beneath.

You walk along a path that is still a path: flagstones set and settled, worn by generations of feet. On either side, the green rises and closes overhead, forming a tunnel, a corridor, a processional way through the living mass. The roots have grown through the stones in places, cracking them slowly, patient as water, patient as gravity. In other places the stones have been set deep enough, or cut precisely enough, that they hold — this is a place of settlement-scale construction, of infrastructure laid down in layers, and even though the plants have reclaimed every surface, you can feel the shape of what was built before. A city, perhaps. Or a vault. The scale is cathedral-small, intimate, bounded. You cannot see the edges, but you sense them.

The change here is slow. You could watch a vine for an hour and see nothing. But the steam-pump stirs, and a root shifts imperceptibly, and somewhere water finds a new path through the stone, and in a year, or a decade, the path you are walking will be different. The square will be a grove. The grove will be a single tree. The tree will be a cathedral of root and hollow, and someone else will descend the stairwell into a different place.

But for now: water, green, warm dark, the hum of engines. The Undergarden.
