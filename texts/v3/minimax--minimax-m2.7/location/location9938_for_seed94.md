# The Breathing Heights

The plateau stretches beyond the limits of sight, a world unto itself, tilted toward clouds that never fully clear. This is high country where moisture has claimed everything, where the air itself is half water, where what falls as rain upon the eastern slopes seeps upward through porous stone on the western edge as springs and seeps that feed hanging bogs and moss channels that weep year-round without ceasing.

It is cool here — not cold enough for ice, but cold enough that breath becomes visible, cold enough that the perpetually saturated ground holds a chill that seeps through hide and boot leather. The warmth of exertion fades quickly. At night, frost crowns the broad leaves of giant ferns, and by midmorning the sun has burned it away in a slow exhalation of steam from every surface. The temperature rises and falls with the sun's passage through the haze, a rhythm as steady as a pulse, a breath drawn in and released.

Vegetation crowds every surface. Where there is stone, there is moss — not patches but continents of it, layered in emerald and ochre and rust, thick enough to cushion footsteps, to hold water in its cellular body like a reservoir. Where there is soil, there is forest: dense, vertical, packed so tight that movement through it requires purpose. The trees here grow tall and lean, their trunks wrapped in vascular vines, their canopies so interlaced that the sky is visible only in fragments, glimpsed rather than seen. Below the canopy, a second and third story of vegetation exists — shrubs adapted to permanent shade, ferns taller than a person, flowers that have lost all color in the dim light filtering through, their petals translucent as rain.

The plateau is ancient. Water has been working its calculus here for epochs, and the land has settled into the shapes that solution and abrasion require. Carved valleys whose walls are smooth where water once ran and rough where it no longer does. Flat-topped ridges where harder stone has resisted the patient chemistry of acidic rain. Terraces where successive water tables left their marks. The land does not feel young or fresh; it feels *found*, like something that has been looking for exactly this shape and has finally arrived.

And yet it moves. The plateau is active in the way that only vast systems can be — not with drama but with persistence. Weather systems form in the belly of this high world and dissipate against its edges. Fog rolls in from the south each evening, thickening through the night until the world beyond arm's reach ceases to exist, then burns back in the mornings in a slow evacuation. Water percolates downward and emerges elsewhere. Root systems shift and crack and remake stone grain by grain. The animals move through their patterns — great herds whose names have been forgotten by any human tongue, flocks that darken the sky in their season, predators that are felt more than seen, whose presence is announced by the sudden silence that falls before they pass.

Here, too, are the vents.

They rise from the plateau's belly like the breathing of something immense and slow. Not fire, not heat enough for fire — but warmth in a cold world, a place where water that has traveled deep returns carrying the taste of metal and sulfur, emerging as steam columns that the wind shreds into ribbons. The locals — those few who still remember these heights — call them the breathings. They hiss and sigh with a constancy that becomes silence, that becomes just another dimension of the quiet. Geothermal processes pump heat through the stone in patterns that are invisible but felt: a warmth in the soil here, a cold seep there, the surface temperature varying across the plateau like the skin of a living thing. It is this that the cool air clings to, that the mist forms around, that the vegetation follows in gradients too subtle for any map.

Animal life abounds. The plateau supports it, packed as it is with organisms. Insects swarm in the still air, their collective motion catching light. Birds nest in the upper canopy, their calls echoing strangely in the fog. Herds of heavy, slow-moving creatures graze the higher meadows, their dung nourishing the soil that feeds the grasses that feed them, a cycle closed and self-contained. In the denser stretches, there are things with more teeth — predators whose eyes gleam in the perpetual half-light, whose tracks mark the mud at water crossings. The smell of life is everywhere: loam and moss and the iron tang of mineral water and the musk of animals and the sweet decay of fallen things returning to the ground.

The plateau is untouched by hand or tool. No walls, no roads, no cleared land. The breathings have left no scarrings that will not heal in a geological eyeblink. But it is not *uninhabited*. It is industrial in the way that it functions — a vast processing system, a machine made of water and stone and carbon, turning atmosphere into growth, turning death into life, cycling and recyling in processes that operate at scale, that move materials by the ton, that run day and night without cease. This is not a garden. It is a factory of the old kind, before electricity, before steel: driven by heat from below and water from above, by time and pressure and the patient chemistry of the earth.

In the silence — the profound, fog-thick silence of this high place — the processes continue. Steam rises. Water drips. Moss grows. The great bodies of the herd animals shift and graze. The predators wait. The plateau breathes.

And it will go on breathing, long after any sound from here could reach the world below.
