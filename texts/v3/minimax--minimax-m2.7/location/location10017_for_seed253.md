Water has no surface here. There is no air to speak of — only water in a condition beyond liquid, beyond vapor, thicker than rain, thinner than brine, saturated so completely through every pocket of space that breathing would be meaningless. You breathe water or you don't breathe at all. The world is not flooded; it was never anything else. The whole of it has always been this: a single, endless submersion, from the deepest strata of whatever this place once was, up through what remains of its upper reaches. You are inside the ocean. You have always been inside the ocean. The ocean is everything, and it is still.

Vegetation grew here once — a word that fails the event. Vegetation didn't grow. It *became*. For an interval of time so vast it has no name, plants spread, thickened, layered, climbed over themselves, fused, calcified in the sense of converting but never hardening, and eventually became the only ground there is. Root and stem and frond and spore-matter blended into a material so dense and so continuous that it is indistinguishable from stone, except that it breathes. It still breathes — not with anything like lungs, but with slow chemic tides: pulling, cycling, renewing. The plants didn't die. They won. They filled every surface until the surface was all plant, until there was no distinction between ground and wall and ceiling, until the world was a single living mass with no seams. You stand on it or in it or within it; the word matters less than the fact that nothing else exists. The substrate is plant. Every path you could walk is plant. Every surface you could touch is plant, and it yields slightly, like packed moss, like something that remembers being soft.

The elevation is extreme, and it is ancient. These are not contradictory facts here — they are the same fact. The highest point of this world, the last place water recedes to before it claims everything, is the oldest ground. It has been here the longest. It has been here so long that the concept of a peak has worn away into something nearly level, something so eroded that what remains is almost flat — not flat in the way of a planned surface, but flat in the way of a thing worn to the limit of its own structure, a ridge planed down to a whisper. You walk on what was once a mountain. It feels like a plateau now, but you can tell from the way water moves — how it hesitates, how it pools at certain angles and rushes at others — that there is still a direction to the slope, still a topology, just one so old it has almost forgotten its own shape. The rock beneath the plants has been here since the beginning, compressed and re-compressed, its minerals rearranged by pressures that had no urgency. Nothing rushed. Nothing needed to. The mountain settled into its final form so long ago that stability is not a condition here; it is the only condition.

You are alone in the sense that nothing is near you, and not alone in the sense that everything is made. The space between things is immense and open, but the things are not few — they are simply spaced far apart, scattered through the vast plant-mass like seeds in soil. Each presence is a life form, a growth, a structure of some kind, and between each one lies an emptiness that is not empty but simply quiet — a gap in the pressing living surface, a pocket where water moves with nothing to interrupt it. The world is not crowded. It is generous with its distance. You can see far. You can see the faint, slow curvature of the plant-surface as it dips and rises across a valley-scale span, and you can see that the curvature has purpose, that the plants have organized themselves into forms that channel water, that direct it, that perform a kind of civil engineering so ancient and so total that it looks like geology. Everything is made. Every ridge is a pattern the plants grew. Every channel is an intention, if intention is the right word for something that has no mind. The world is constructed. The world is made. Nothing here was placed by a hand, but nothing here is natural in the way the word usually means, because nature, as a concept, ended when the plants became the world, when they covered everything so completely that nothing could be considered raw or untouched. There is no stone here that is not also plant. There is no water here that does not carry plant-matter in solution. The made-world and the living-world are the same thing, so thoroughly fused that the word *built* has lost its distinction from the word *grown*. The world was not constructed. The world grew until construction was indistinguishable from growth, and then it kept growing, and now it is here, and here is everything.

No tool has ever been made here. No device has ever been conceived. There are no bones shaped into levers, no fibres woven into nets, no chemistry harnessed into flame. Everything that exists is biological. Everything that moves does so because biology moves it — slowly, without gears or circuits, with membranes and pressure and the slow cunning of life that has never seen light and has never needed to. The creatures of this place navigate by chemistry, by the faint taste of gradients in the water, by the slow vibration of plant-biomass as other creatures move through it. There are no eyes because there is nothing to see. There is no light. The darkness is not a feature of this place; it is the place. It is the medium in which everything exists, a darkness so complete and so old that it is no longer darkness at all but simply the condition, the baseline, the nothing-from-which-everything-continues. The dark is the world, and the world is the dark, and between them there is no difference.

And yet there is life. Abundant, in its way. Not in the sense of teeming, not in the sense of the jungle or the reef. Abundant in the sense that if you wait — and you will wait, because waiting is all there is — you will see them. Slow movements at the edges of perception. Shadows in the water that move with purpose and then stop, and remain, and move again. Creatures that live in the plant-mass, feeding on it, being fed on by it, part of the same slow metabolism that keeps the whole world alive. Some are large — large enough that their movement displaces the water in ways you feel rather than see, pressure waves against your skin, a slow shudder in the plant-matter as something the size of a building shifts its weight. Some are small, small and numerous, flickering at the edge of the dark like motes in a slow current, clustering where the plant-surface is thickest, feeding on the soft and ancient tissue that still breathes, still cycles, still holds the memory of sunlight that this world has not seen in an epoch. They glow — faintly, dimly, with a light that is less light than it is a slow chemical decay, a luminescence that is not intentional but inevitable, the waste-light of processes that have no other output. The glow is weak. It illuminates nothing. It barely marks the presence of the creature that carries it. But in the absolute dark, it is enough to know that something is there, that the world is not empty, that the silence is not the silence of death but the silence of something alive that has never needed to make a sound.

The sound is present. Not loud in the way of surf or storm — louder in the way of a machine idling, a constant low hum that is the sound of the water moving through plant-matter, the slow seep and cycle of the world breathing. There is dripping, if dripping is the right word for water that condenses on one surface and falls, over distances too great to measure, into a pool that has been forming for a thousand years. There is a creaking that might be the sound of the plant-mass adjusting to its own weight, slowly compacting, slowly rearranging, the way a forest settles but across geological time. There is the faint, almost subliminal sound of creatures moving — the slow displacement of water, the scrape of a body against a stem, the exhalation of something that breathes in water and breathes out something else, something that joins the cycle, that feeds the plants, that keeps the whole closed system turning. The sound is steady. It does not build or fade. It has always been this loud and no louder, and it will be this loud when there is nothing left to listen.

Nothing changes here. This is not a place that was once different. This is not a place that will be different. The water moves, but it moves in the same paths it has always moved, along channels the plants grew and the water wore into permanence. The creatures live and die, but the species do not change — they are what they are, perfect in the way of things that have had no pressure to change, no light to respond to, no season to anticipate. The plants grow, but the growth is so slow and so balanced against decay that the shape of the world is fixed, set, settled into a form so final that calling it equilibrium is generous — it is beyond equilibrium. It is a world that has run out of things to do. The dynamic parameter is nearly zero, and you feel that as you move through it: the sense that nothing is happening for the first time, the sense that you could leave and return and everything would be exactly the same, not because it is frozen but because it has finished. The mountain settled. The plants finished growing. The creatures finished evolving. The water finished filling every space. And now the world is here, complete, whole, and entirely itself, and it will be here for as long as there is a here to be in, and nothing you do will change a single thing about it, and nothing will change you either, because in a place like this, stillness is not a condition — it is a philosophy, and it is the only one that has ever made sense here, in the dark, in the water, in the total and ancient and breathing silence of everything made of everything.
