The fog comes in from the riverine lowlands and doesn't leave. It pools in the valley like something poured, settling against the city's chest, thickening in the narrow streets where buildings lean against each other for support. The moisture doesn't fall as rain—not often—but it gathers on every surface, beads on brick, runs in thin rivulets down stone facades, pools in courtyard cobblestones, gathers in the rust-eaten gutters. The whole city sweats.

And the city is vast. It spreads across the valley floor and climbs the far ridges, its packed density reducing every space between presences to nothing. Streets barely accommodate two people walking abreast. Buildings so old their origins have been forgotten press against structures of riveted steel and corrugated sheet metal, all of it leaning together in a conspiracy of mutual support. Nothing stands alone here. Everything holds everything else up. The density isn't planned; it's accumulated—layer upon layer, generation upon generation, like sediment compressed by its own weight.

The ground beneath is pre-geology. Unstable. The valley's floor was once marshland, or lake-bed, or something less definable—it hasn't decided yet. Foundations sink and shift. Cracks appear in walls overnight. Streets buckle after heavy machinery passes. The whole city is perpetually under repair, under construction, under a kind of siege from its own unstable body. They build anyway. They have always built anyway. The emerging earth yields and they adapt, tunneling, piloting, forcing their structures down into substrate that hasn't finished becoming substrate. It is work that will never be finished, and they know this, and they do it anyway, with the anthill-energy of a place that cannot rest.

Industry runs beneath the fog. The city is electrified—arc lights hum in factory windows, and the glow of incandescent streets pushes back the darkness enough that the fog turns the color of weak milk. Engines run somewhere in the middle distance, always, a low mechanical pulse that has become part of the city's heartbeat so completely that it disappears into silence. Factories breathe smoke that mingles with the moisture until the fog tastes faintly of copper, of burning oil, of something chemical and clean. Workshops open onto streets, and what they make here is not always clear—the city produces, but its products are sometimes abstract, sometimes puzzling, sometimes shapes that suggest purposes no one will name.

Yet the vegetation comes anyway. Lush, persistent, adapted to cold and damp and the poisoned breath of industry. Ferns crowd the courtyards. Ivy climbs every wall that offers purchase. Trees in the squares have bark black with mold and leaves that drip perpetually, their roots cracking pavement, finding purchase in the cracks that the unstable earth provides. Gardens grow on rooftops, tended by people who understand that soil here is precious, that growing things need to be insisted upon. The plants are dark, dense, heavy with moisture—unhurried in their growth, patient in ways the city itself is not.

The fauna is abundant but quiet. The city's rats are enormous, well-fed, and they watch from gutters without flinching. Gulls wheel overhead, their cries sharp against the general silence. In the drainage channels and the slow-moving river that threads the valley's floor, something else moves—something that has adapted to the channels, to the dark water, to the city's particular brand of industrial runoff. Most people don't speak of it. Most people don't look. At night, on the peripheries, there are sounds that might be breathing, that might be movement, that might be the river itself remembering what it was before the city came.

The quiet is the strangest thing. The city is turbulent—the fever-energy of a million lives intersecting, goods moving, arguments conducted in doorways, entire economies conducted in the narrow alleys—but the sound of it is low, contained, a murmur against the fog. Voices are muffled. Machinery is muffled. The dynamism is there in the movement, in the press of bodies, in the constant visible activity, but it has learned to be quiet, as if noise would disturb something the city cannot name.

And there is strangeness here, too. Buildings whose doors open onto walls. Factories that employ no one visible. Rooftop gardens that no one tends but that bloom anyway, flowers in colors that don't belong to any known species. Sometimes, in the fog, you see shapes that might be people but move wrong, or might be architecture but breathe. The locals have adapted; they walk without looking, speak without listening, live in a city that operates according to principles just adjacent to the ones they learned as children. The fog is constant, and it holds things close, and if you stare at any single piece of the city for too long, you'll find you've lost the thread of what you were looking at. The city is not hostile. It is simply vast in ways that don't map onto ordinary size, and strange in ways that don't map onto ordinary strangeness.

They live here. They work here. They raise children here who will raise children here. The city is cold and wet and built on ground that hasn't decided what it wants to be, and it is louder than silence and quieter than sound, and it is home, and they wouldn't know what to do with anything else.
