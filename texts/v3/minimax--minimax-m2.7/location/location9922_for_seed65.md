The plateau sits three thousand meters above sea level, a flat expanse of exposed stone stretching toward a horizon that curves slightly with the earth's shape. From here, the world below looks folded and distant, like a map spread on a table. There is nothing between you and the sky.

The stone beneath your feet is granite, pale and ancient, its surface pocked and smoothed by windblown grit over millennia. The erosion here has reached a kind of completion — the rocks are shaped as they will remain, holding their final forms until some far future event reshapes them. Shallow pools of water gather in depressions that the weather has carved, their edges stained with mineral deposits, their surfaces still when the wind pauses. Dew forms in the pre-dawn stillness, beading on every grain of exposed quartz, then vanishes by midmorning as the sun climbs and the air warms just enough to drink it. Between these pools, the stone is dry. The moisture comes and goes like breath.

Sparse vegetation persists in the cracks where soil has accumulated — stubborn tufts of grass bleached pale by sun, isolated lichen patches the color of rust or gold, the occasional low shrub that has grown horizontal by the constant wind. These plants are not thriving; they are enduring. They have found the thin margins where life remains possible and have claimed them. Each tuft is a small victory against the height, the cold, the exposure. Between them, the ground is bare stone and thin scree, enough to walk on easily.

The temperature is cool year-round, a perpetual autumn that never warms and never freezes. At this elevation, the sun's heat arrives directly, without atmosphere to soften it, and the air above the stone reads cooler than the stone itself. You feel this difference on your skin as a paradox — bright light that does not warm, shadows that are not cold. The effect is disorienting, like standing in a lit room that is refrigerated.

What fills this space is wind. Not wind as weather, but wind as fact — the plateau exists in a band of the atmosphere where a steady current has established itself, a river of air that flows from west to east with only minor variations in speed and direction. The sound it makes is the dominant fact of this landscape: a continuous roar, layered with harmonics, punctuated by higher-pitched whistles as the air funnels through rock formations. It is so constant that it becomes the silence of this place, the background against which all other sound is measured. Between the gusts, there are moments of held-breath stillness so complete that the absence of noise becomes a physical pressure, and then the wind returns, and the roar resumes, and the silence feels like something that only the wind makes possible.

Fauna concentrates where the wind allows it. Hawks ride the currents in wide circles, their calls thin and sharp against the roar. Small mammals — pikas, voles, shrews — occupy the rock crevices, emerging in brief sprints between gusts, gathering the sparse vegetation, storing it in their burrows against the winter when the wind will howl with ice. Insects are abundant in their season, hatching from the pools, mating in the brief calms, their lives measured in hours or days but their numbers vast. Lizards appear on the sunniest stones, frozen when the wind rises, then gone in a blur of motion when they sense a pause. The plateau is not empty of life, but the life is spread thin, each creature occupying its own narrow window of possibility.

There is no construction here. No artifact marks the stone. The only signs of presence are the trails worn into the scree by generations of small mammals, and the faint traces where human passage has disturbed the dust — a footprint, a scrape, evidence of someone who came and left. The plateau does not invite or resist. It simply is, in a way that makes presence feel like intrusion, even your own.

The light at midday is relentless, bleaching everything pale, eliminating shadow. The sun hangs in a sky so blue it is nearly violet, and the UV index here is dangerous enough that exposed skin burns within minutes. By afternoon, when the light has moved and the wind has strengthened, the world feels like a stage set — too bright, too open, too exposed. You want to find a shadow, but the shadows have been flattened by the angle and the clarity. Only in the rock crevices, where the walls block the light, is there relief.

And yet the plateau holds. It is not dying or changing; it is simply here, in its state of long equilibrium. The erosion has been complete for ages, the wind has found its groove, the creatures have found their niches, and all of it continues as it has, as it will. The dynamic is in the name — the wind moves, the creatures move, the water moves, but the place itself does not change. It holds still in the middle of motion, a fixed point in the flowing air.

This is a place of extremes and absences. Extreme height, extreme brightness, extreme sound. Empty of density, empty of construction, empty of variation. The abundance of fauna contradicts the emptiness until you realize: abundance is relative. Life is here, but life is small. Life is brief. Life has clustered itself into the only available shapes and has let the rest of the space remain hollow. This is not a place where life is easy or full. It is a place where life is possible, and that is enough.
