The plateau extends under light so fierce and direct it seems to flatten the terrain into a diagram. Everything is mineral. Everything is wet.

Water emerges everywhere but nowhere in particular. It seeps from the bases of dark cliffs, tracing silver threads down faces of pale granite before vanishing into sand made of crushed feldspar. It pools in slight depressions, shallow and still, their surfaces holding the sky's reflection like broken mirrors. The ground is damp in the way a stone is damp — not soft, not giving, but bearing moisture as a condition of its being. Thin fog gathers in the lower troughs at midday, hugging the earth before wind pulls it apart. The air tastes of metal and something almost organic, though nothing recognizable grows. The moisture is geological. The rocks weep and have always wept.

Between the water-threads and pooling basins, the ground is dense with other things: cobbles of volcanic basalt, tumbled and sorted by uncountable time into neat arrangements. Rounded boulders sit in clusters, their surfaces dark and glassy, water sheening across their curves. Chert nodules break through the soil at angles. The plateau's surface is a catalog of stone — basalt and quartzite and something pale that looks likeite but isn'tite quite — all of it occupying space shoulder to shoulder, no bare ground visible, the surface as packed and continuous as a masonry floor. The elevation means the air is thin enough to make sound carry strangely: distant things seem close, near things seem to come from everywhere at once.

And the sound is relentless. Wind pours across the plateau and finds ten thousand edges to speak to. It sighs through cracks in the granite. It shivers across taut pebble surfaces with a sound like distant applause. Water moves constantly — trickling, dripping, the soft percussion of drops falling from overhangs into basins below — and each sound in the thin air carries clearly, layering into a continuous murmur punctuated by the sharp clack of stone striking stone as the wind lifts and drops cobbles. Somewhere far below, a waterfall throws its voice up the cliff face, and though it cannot be seen from here, its roar seems to come from everywhere at once. The sound has a quality of being structural — not weather, not event, but the landscape's own utterance, as constant as the light.

The brightness is total. The pale stone of the plateau reflects sun back into sky, and the moisture on every surface catches and scatters it into a soft, directionless luminance. Shadows are crisp but brief; the sun moves and the light follows, and there's no dusk at this altitude, only a quick fading into cold dark. Everything is visible, exposed, revealed in full detail. No secrets here. No dim corners.

Life exists, but barely. A thin biofilm covers some of the wetted stones — gray-green, almost invisible unless you lie close to the ground and let your eye adjust. Not plant, not lichen as you would understand lichen, but some simpler arrangement of moisture and mineral and time, the planet's most basic trick of making something that moves and eats and persists. Occasional pale creatures, the size of large insects, scramble across the wet stone when the wind drops — six-legged or eight-legged, it can be hard to tell in the bright light, their bodies dry and hard-shelled, moving with the quick, purposeful urgency of things that know they are rare. They drink from the thin pools, they shelter under the largest cobbles during the worst wind, and they do not gather or call or leave any sign of their presence except this: the occasional motion in an otherwise static world.

The plateau has always been like this. The erosion here is not dramatic — no great cliffs crumbling, no rivers cutting new channels. The water that moves across it has been moving the same way for so long that the stone has reached an equilibrium with its own dissolution. The surface is settled into its shapes. The water follows paths established before anything here was alive. Nothing changes quickly. The rare creatures do not evolve; they persist, exactly as they are, in the margins of a world that does not encourage them but also does not end them. The wind shapes the sound but not the shape. The light illuminates but does not warm enough to change anything. The plateau is a stable condition, a held breath that has never been released, a landscape that has found the configuration it will keep until something distant and enormous finally shifts the equilibrium and it begins, very slowly, to become something else.
