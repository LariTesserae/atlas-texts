# The Hollow Cathedral

The air tastes green. Not the clean green of morning dew, but the heavy, fermenting green of a space where photosynthesis has won its war against everything else—a space where the light itself seems filtered through living tissue, leaving only a humid, golden half-clarity that doesn't quite touch the ground.

What rises here is architecture, though *built* feels like the wrong word. Walls of petrified wood stretch upward in buttresses and ribbons, their grain still visible, still breathing through centuries of mineral patience. Between them, the gaps are not empty so much as *withheld*—vast hollows where the air sits undisturbed while the rest of the world moves. You could stand in these clearings and believe yourself alone, until you notice the walls are watching.

The roots are the builders. This is the truth the ground has been trying to tell you since you entered the grove's threshold. Tap-roots as thick as your torso plunge and curve through the mud, binding soil that has no right to be this dark, this alive. They form the pews, the altar-stones, the threshold lintels. They have grown around what they found—around a fountain choked with reed-grass, around benches ofite that have gone soft with moss, around a well whose lip has been worn smooth by hands no longer present.

And the sound. The sound is the first thing you understand, before you see, before you smell. It comes from everywhere: the creak of wood-cell dividing, the hum of pollination dragged through ten thousand open blooms, the subsonic groan of root-tip boring through clay. Birdsong here is not melody but punctuation—a bright, sharp thing that punctuates the longer sentences of insect drone and the deep, arrhythmic percussion of something large settling its weight somewhere in the canopy above. The canopy. You keep forgetting to look up because the scale keeps betraying you: what you thought was a wall is a trunk, what you thought was a clearing is a wound in the canopy's fabric, and what you thought was silence was only the pause between one reality's exhale and its next inhale.

The fauna is abundant in the way that teeth are abundant in a mouth—every surface has its tenant. Lizards on the mineral-walls, their scales taking on a pale, almost luminous quality as though they've borrowed the light for themselves. Termites have constructed their columns along the north face of what might have been a doorway, their mounds reaching three meters before toppingple over in silent, patient conquest. Somewhere in the upper dark, something that is either a bat or a leaf or both shifts against the wood and falls, briefly, before catching itself on a thread you cannot see.

The weirdness is not an event here. It is the condition. The shadows do not behave. The light falls at angles that no sun could justify, pooling in places that should be dark, sliding off surfaces that should absorb. You realize you've been standing in the same clearing for what feels like an hour, and the well is closer than it was, or you are closer to it, or the well has grown. The water inside, when you finally approach, is still—but it is still the way a held breath is still, and you understand that it is waiting for something to disturb it, and you are not that something, and you may never have been.

The erosion is established: the stones are not merely weathered but *weathered into*. The grooves in the ancient wood carry water during rain, and during rain (which comes every few hours, a warm and sudden weeping from a sky that might be real or might be the canopy's own condensation) the hollow cathedral fills with the sound of its own plumbing. The water finds channels cut by centuries of patient intent, flows through carved channels, pools in basins where things with too many legs come to drink.

You sit on the root-bench, because it asks you to, and because your legs have become unreliable in the presence of so much life pressing itself against the edges of what you thought was possible. The bark is warm. The air is thick. The sounds layer and overlap until they become texture rather than signal—a constant, living hiss that will follow you home and live in your ears for days.

You will come back. They always come back. The hollow cathedral is patient in the way only ancient plants can be patient, and it has already begun to grow around the place where you were sitting, filling in the shape of your absence, preparing it for your return.
