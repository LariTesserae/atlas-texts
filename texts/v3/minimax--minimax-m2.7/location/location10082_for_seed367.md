The courtyard is the size of a large room — perhaps twelve paces across, perhaps less — yet within its walls, the world has not stopped. It has simply gone small. Everything in it has adapted to the geometry of enclosure: the stone beneath is so ancient that erosion has worn it near-smooth, flat as a palm, and the walls that ring this space rise so sheer and high they cut out most of the light, leaving only a dim amber quality that never quite brightens or fades.

The moisture comes at night. The courtyard sits high enough — in the ruins of a settlement that clung to this ridge before memory — that the air draws in thick and cold after dark, and water collects on surfaces the way breath collects on glass. It beads on the massive flat stones, runs in thin dark threads along the grooves carved by centuries of runoff. There is no spring, no stream, no pool. But the aggregate of night's moisture, wicked up by roots and drawn from the crevices of the worn masonry, is enough.

The vegetation is dense — dense in the way that only a space with nowhere to expand can be. Every surface is rooted. Thick vines haul themselves up the inner walls, their tendrils thick as fingers, their leaves broad and coated with a fine dust that catches the dim light. Ground-level is a close-canopy of shrubs with waxy dark-green leaves, their branches so tangled that to push through them is to feel the whole mass shift, alive. They bloom in small pale flowers that open at dusk and close before true dark. Between the shrubs, seedlings sprout in the cracks of the stone like a question that has been asked so many times it has become architecture. The air is green and close and smells of mineral oxidation and old leaf.

The fauna is overwhelming — not in the sense of number alone, but in the sense of *presence*. It is impossible to stand here and feel alone. Insects crowd every surface: beetles with ridged shells moving in deliberate lines across the stone, their antennae testing the air; ants in long files crossing the roots of the shrubs in both directions, carrying fragments too large to see. Above, small lizards flatten themselves against the upper vine-tangled walls where the light is thinnest, watching. Birds — tiny, quick, bright-eyed — move through the upper branches in bursts of motion, chirping in sharp exchanges. At dusk, something larger moves in the canopy: the rustle of it bends the vines, and then there is a silence that lasts exactly long enough to make you uncertain whether the silence is yours or the animal's.

The settlement that built this space is gone, but its construction remains. The walls are fitted and dressed stone, each block heavy enough that even the erosion of deep time has not loosened them from their courses. There are no metal tools marks on them — whatever carved and placed these stones did so with blade and lever, with sand and patience. In the far wall, a low doorway opens into darkness, too small to pass through comfortably, and beyond it the air does not move. That direction is sealed, or collapsed, or leads to something the world has chosen not to finish. Within the courtyard itself, the people who lived here left traces: a shallow basin cut into one flagstone to catch dew, its lip worn thin by hands; holes in the walls near the ground where rainwater — or ritual water, or both — was once poured and drained away through channels too fine to follow with a finger.

Sound fills the space. Not the loudness of open air, but the loudness of *fullness*: the pulse of insect activity against stone, the scrape of beetle legs, the distant drip of collected moisture finding its crack, the constant low hum that may be the vibration of the colony or may be the sound of the air itself moving through leaves. When one of the small birds calls sharply, the others answer, and the exchange builds for a moment into something that is almost song, then collapses back into the hum.

The dynamic state is the thing that makes it a place and not a photograph. Nothing here has settled. The vines grow visibly — you can see the new growth at the tips, pale green and slightly translucent. The erosion on the stone is ancient, yes, complete in its work, but on top of that ancient erosion, new patterns are being carved by roots: thin black threads of penetration splitting the stone at the level of a hair's breadth, widening season by season. The lizards change positions. The beetles' paths are different each time. The moisture arrives each night, and each morning some of it is gone, and the plants are a millimeter taller, and the cracks are a fraction wider, and the ancient surface is being slowly, methodically unmade by the same green life that chose to make its home here.

You stand at the edge of it — there is no room to stand at the center; the shrubs press in — and the walls keep the world out and the life in, and the air is warm and close and smells of growing, and the stone remembers hands, and the roots are patient, and the small bright birds are singing in a language that is only about the size of this room.
