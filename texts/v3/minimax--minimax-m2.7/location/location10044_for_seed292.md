# The Drowned Uplands of Verath

The plateau of Verath sits seven thousand meters above what was once the western sea, though the sea has long since receded and returned and receded again, and now the only water below is the mineral-white crust of a vanished ocean floor. From the edge of the plateau's great northern cliff, you can see three hundred kilometers of open air before the terrain drops away into the sub-arid basins that still hold the memory of water. Above, the sky is a different matter entirely.

Verath is drowning. Not in the way of lowland floods, the patient rise that takes a century to swallow a village. Here, the water comes down—or rather, comes *across*—in walls of horizontal rain that hammer the exposed rock faces with the patience of geological time. The storms form where the plateau's thermal mass meets the cold upper atmosphere, building day after day in the late summer months until the sky loses its coherence and becomes a single unbroken motion of vapor and wind. During the storm season, which lasts four months, you cannot see the far edge of the plateau. You can only hear it: the roar that never stops, the percussion of ten thousand waterfalls running sideways across the ancient stone.

The erosion here is total. Three billion years of water, wind, and thermal cycling have reduced the original mountain range to something that is no longer quite a mountain range and not yet a plain. The ridges that remain are smooth, flowing, like the surface of melted glass that has been drawn slowly upward and frozen in the act of flowing. The valleys between them have been cut so deep and wide that the plateau's interior has become a labyrinth of shadow, with floors that never see direct sunlight even in the dry months. Water moves through these shadow-valleys in braided rivers that branch and rejoin with the regularity of veins, carrying the silt of ancient seabeds down toward the cliffs' edge, where it tumbles out into the lower air in continuous waterfalls that feed the storms that feed the waterfalls. The cycle has no beginning you could point to and no end you could imagine.

And yet it is lush. This is the strangest fact of Verath: beneath the rain and the mist and the perpetual damp that seeps into everything, life has taken hold with a tenacity that defies the cold and the exposure. The rock itself is colonized by thick mats of dark vegetation—lichen of impossible colors, mosses that grow in shelves and cascades, small vascular plants with waxy leaves that catch the light like scattered coins. In the sheltered valleys, where the water pools in flat black basins between the eroded fins of stone, there are forests. They are not tall forests—they cannot be, with soil measured in centimeters and wind that snaps anything above three meters. But they are dense, a tangle of twisted conifers and broadleaf evergreens that have found their equilibrium with the landscape, their branches all leaning east from a lifetime of facing the storm winds. The trees are old for their size. Some of them, growing in the rock crevices where soil has accumulated over centuries, have root systems that wrap around stones like fingers, gripping the bones of the mountain with a patience that matches the erosion itself.

The fauna is abundant in the way of places that have had a long time to diversify without large predators. Verath has no large predators—or rather, it has none that originated here. The creatures of the plateau are small to medium, climbers and burrowers and a surprising number of aquatic species adapted to the cold, fast streams. There are fish in the high lakes, pale and slow-growing, some of them reaching ages that rival the trees. There are insects in clouds so thick they become visible as weather patterns themselves, rising and falling with the thermal currents. There are birds—endemic species that have traded the ability to fly for the ability to run along the cliff edges, their vestigial wings used only for display and balance. And there are the remains of others: bones in caves, footprints preserved in mudstone that hardened forty million years ago, the remnants of megafauna that came and went while the plateau continued its slow flattening.

Because the plateau has been eroding at its maximum rate for so long that it has almost, but not quite, finished the job. The original peaks are gone, their mass distributed across millions of square kilometers of ancient seabed in the basins below. What remains are the bones—the hardest ridges, the quartz veins that refused to yield, the dykes of basalt that intruded into the older rock and now stand as the last vertical elements in a landscape that has otherwise become horizontal, flowing, patient. The plateau is not flat, but it is flat*ting*, moving toward a state of completion that it will reach in another few hundred million years. It is an end-state landscape, a place that has settled into its final form without yet having arrived there, and this gives it a quality of exhaustion that is not the same as deadness. The energy is still there—the storms, the water, the heat exchange at the thermal boundary—but it is energy spent in the service of completion rather than change. The mountain is almost finished becoming a plain.

And scattered across this ancient surface, faint and partial, are the marks of the builders.

They came late in the plateau's long history—the industrial cultures that built at the same scale as the natural processes, with the same ambition, the same disregard for the depth of time. Their works are still visible, but barely. A concrete platform near the southern cliff edge, its surface cracked and heaved by frost wedging, its machinery long since scavenged or corroded into unreadability. The supports of a bridge that once spanned one of the shadow-valleys, now ending in mid-air where the far side of the gorge has collapsed. The foundations of buildings that have been absorbed into the rock—actually incorporated, their walls now part of the stone, their floors now indistinguishable from the natural surface beneath. There are fewer structures here than in the lowlands, because building on Verath was always a losing proposition, a race against the erosion and the weather and the simple mathematics of cost and maintenance at that altitude. Those who came here came for reasons that made sense to them: the rare minerals in the ancient seabed, the wind for power generation, the strategic value of a high place in a world that still had wars. They built their engines and their factories and their housing blocks, and the plateau absorbed them the way it absorbs everything, slowly, patiently, with the certainty of geological time.

What remains is the noise. The industrial sound is present but not dominant—a hum in certain valleys, a mechanical groaning from the remains of wind turbines on the exposed ridges, the occasional clang of settling metal that echoes in the stone. The dominant sound is the water: the hiss of rain on rock, the roar of the horizontal storms, the splash and rush of the braided rivers, the deep boom of waterfalls at the cliff edge. This is a loud place, a place where silence would be a warning rather than a comfort, where the absence of sound would mean the storms have broken or the rivers have frozen or something has gone wrong in the delicate machinery of this ancient, still-evolving system.

At night, in the dry season, the plateau is quiet enough to hear the stars. The stars are sharp here, unobscured by the haze of lower altitudes, and they make a sound—or rather, the silence between them has a quality that is not the silence of empty rooms but the silence of distance, of space measured in light-years. In the shadow-valleys, there are creatures that navigate by this light, their eyes huge and reflective, their movements silent on the wet stone. In the open areas, where the wind still moves even in calm, the vegetation creaks and rustles with a sound like whispered conversation.

This is Verath: a drowned high place, still being worn down by water that came from the sea and will return to the sea, where life has found its niche in the boundary between the ancient and the immediate, where the works of industry have been added to the catalogue of erosion, where the storms build and break and build again, where the plateau waits, with the patience of stone, to become something else.
