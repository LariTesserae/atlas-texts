The ground is not ground. It was made. It was laid, or poured, or assembled from vast geometries of material that have no natural origin — flat planes of pale mineral composite stretching to horizons in every direction, interrupted only by the shallow folds and terraces of manufactured topography. This is a place made for a world, a foundation slab at planetary scale, and the ground accepts the weight of everything above it without complaint because it was designed to do exactly that.

Moisture arrives in pulses. The heat here is relentless — a thick, pressing warmth that does not fluctuate much from day to night, drawing moisture up from somewhere deep in the infrastructure, from condensation channels and sealed reservoirs that weep in ways the original builders may not have anticipated. The air becomes heavy with it for a few hours, and water beads on every surface, filling the shallow channels cut between the terraces with thin, still sheets that glow copper under the light. Then the wind shifts or the cycle completes, and everything dries within a day. The planes darken from wet mineral-gray to a lighter, powdery pale, and the air thins back to something that feels like breathing through warm glass.

Vegetation did not come with the design. Something arrived — drifted, was carried, took root in the rare pockets where moisture lingers long enough — and it survives. Low, tough growths push up through seams in the composite: wiry and drought-adapted, with small leaves and deep roots that follow the faint seam between sections. They are not lush. They are not many. Here and there across the vast plain, a cluster of them marks a place where water pools long enough to sustain something green, but most of the territory is bare and hard and geometric.

There are no animals. Nothing moves here except the processes of the place itself: the slow pulse of water through the infrastructure, the rhythm of condensation and evaporation, the heat shimmer that bends the light over the longer expanses. Wind moves across the planes in long, uninterrupted strokes, carrying dust that settles in the low channels but does not accumulate anywhere else. Sound is minimal — a faint hum from deep within the structure, felt more than heard, and the soft, brief percussion of rain on the composite when the moisture comes: a tapping so light it barely registers, then silence.

The erosion here is almost nothing. The surfaces are raw. Edges are crisp where two constructed planes meet, unweathered, sharp enough to catch light at its angles. Where water has crossed the same paths repeatedly over whatever time has passed, there is the faintest rounding beginning — a softness at the lip of a channel, a slight gloss on a low spot where moisture lingers — but it is early. The planet is still learning how to wear itself down.

There are structures here too, though not many. They rise from the composite in simple geometries — low walls, raised platforms, columns — and they serve no function that can be identified from the surface. They were part of the original build, or they were added later, or they are the exposed remnants of something larger. Their surfaces are as raw and unfinished as the plain itself. They cast shadows of perfect clarity.

At night the heat releases slowly. The sky here is ordinary — clear, deep blue during the day, and at night a sky of quiet stars that means nothing to the landscape and offers nothing in return. The composite planes cool first along their edges, where wind reaches them, and warmth holds longest in the low channels where water settled that day. A place of small, still pockets of warmth in a world designed to be indifferent.
