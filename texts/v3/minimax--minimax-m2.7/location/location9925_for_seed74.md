# The Undergrove of Caldtow

You arrive at the edge of it before you understand what *it* is.

The road ends in a carpet of moss, green and worn to a shine where water sits in the hollows of its surface. Beyond, the settlement begins — or rather, the place where settlement and forest have stopped pretending to be separate things. Caldtow was a mill town once, four centuries past, when the river still turned gears and the smoke from brick kilns hung in the valley like weather. Now the kilns are gone, the river reduced to a sinuous damp through the lower quarter, and what remains is a place that has learned to grow.

The structures are still legible as structures. Brick walls stand to their second stories, softened now at the base where roots have thickened into pillars. The mortar is gone — pulled out by moss, by time, by the patient chemistry of rain on lime — and in its place: soil. A thin, dark soil, spongy underfoot, fed by four centuries of leaf-fall and decay. The buildings have become the ground, and the ground has become a meadow.

Trees grow through window openings. Not beside the buildings — *through* them. An elm rose inside what was a textile hall, its trunk now the load-bearing column, the walls split and draped around it like canvas. You can still see the brick, beneath the moss, beneath the ferns that hang from every ledge in green curtains. The elm is old. Its bark is silvered, its canopy spreading through the gap where a roof used to be, now open to a sky that seems wider here, in this small valley, than it should.

The air is cool. Not cold — cool, the way a cellar is cool, or a forest floor at morning. Moisture hangs in it, a gentle damp that doesn't quite become rain. The river valley catches clouds, holds them, lets them press against the stones and brick until the surfaces weep. Everything is damp. The wood, the stone, the ropes of lichen that hang from every branch. Your breath doesn't quite fog but leaves a faint ghost on the air. You drink water from a spring box near the old millrace — the water tastes of iron and green things.

What strikes you is the silence, punctuated.

The silence is the first thing you notice: a deep, felt quiet, like cotton in the ears. No engines. No voices carrying from far away. The settlement is still inhabited — perhaps sixty people live in Caldtow now, in the parts that have been made stable — but they move quietly, their footsteps muffled by living ground. The industrial machinery is gone, replaced by small systems: a waterwheel that still turns in the old race, powering a generator that charges batteries for the households that have electrified. Windlasses and hand-cranks and the occasional diesel pump for the deep wells. The tech is here, but subdued. It lives alongside the moss, the pipes threaded with algae, the light fixtures wrapped in vine.

The punctuated part comes from water.

The river is never fully absent. It moves beneath the moss, through channels cut by centuries of drainage, surfacing in places as pools or seeps or small waterfalls over mossy stone. In the lowest part of the quarter, where the valley floor dips, there's a permanent marsh — cattails and water shamrocks, dragonflies in the copper evening light. The water moves constantly, in small sounds: drip, trickle, the soft burble of a rill finding its way through a crack. It is the pulse of the place. You hear it everywhere, if you listen. You stop noticing it, and then you notice it again, and each time it surprises you, like a heartbeat you forgot was there.

Fauna is rare but present. You see birds — crows in the high elms, small brown things that move too fast to name, a heron that stands in the marsh at dusk with the patience of stone. There's a population of dormice in the old mill, and a pair of foxes whose den is somewhere in the root-system of a fallen oak that has become a hill. Insects are abundant: gnats in the shafts of light, beetles in the rotting wood, the constant hum of things too small to see. Larger animals have not returned in numbers. The valley is too settled, too small, too bounded by hills that funnel the wind through it rather than shelter it. You are as large as anything gets here, except the trees.

The plants are everything.

This is not a place where plants grow. This is a place where plants *are*. The substrate — what you stand on, what the buildings rise from, what holds the water and the roots and the spores — is plant-matter. Dead wood softened to soil. Generations of moss layered into a felt that holds water like a sponge. The stone walls are green at the base, and the green climbs. Ferns sprout from cracks in the brick. Ivy has thickened into rope-thick cables that brace crumbling walls. The trees — ash, elm, oak, and an ash that may be a different species now, evolved or mutated or simply patient — have become the architecture. They stand where walls fell, hold up what remains, reach through windows and across courtyards until you cannot tell which branch belongs to which tree.

In the old church — still recognizable, its nave open to the sky where the roof collapsed, its stone pews now platforms for moss and seedlings — a fig has taken root in the altar. The roots spread across the floor like veins, and where they meet the walls they thicken, and where they meet the ceiling they turn to branches, and the branches have leaves, and the leaves are in the light. It is the most unusual thing in Caldtow, though it is not alone in its strangeness. There is a tree in the millrace that has grown into a spiral, its trunk twisted by centuries of seeking light through a gap in the buildings. There is a willow that has grown across a courtyard and taken root on the other side, creating an archway of green, a living gate. There are fungi — shelves of them, orange and white and black — growing on every surface that stays damp, turning the town into something between a forest and a reef.

And yet — sparse.

You must hold this against the weight of the plants: the space between things is vast. The settlement is small — the whole of Caldtow fits within a valley floor perhaps two hundred meters across, bounded by hills that are green but not overgrown, grazed by the few goats the residents keep. Within this, the buildings are scattered. Courtyards open to sky. Gardens have been cut into the living ground, paths laid with flat stones that are clean of moss, cleared by hands. The people of Caldtow have made a life not by fighting the growth but by directing it. They prune, they train, they harvest. The wood is fuel. The fiber is cordage. The leaves are compost. The boundary between human and plant is not a war here; it is a conversation, centuries long, still ongoing.

You hear a child somewhere, laughing. The sound travels clearly in the damp air, bright and odd against the moss.

The dynamic is what holds it all together: the weather, the water, the growth. It has rained in the night, lightly, and the morning is cool and gray, and every surface drips with a kind of slow persistence. The moss glows green. A beetle moves across a stone, its carapace dark and wet. A crow calls once, twice, then is silent. The waterwheel turns. A woman hangs laundry on a line strung between two oaks — the oaks creak faintly in a wind you cannot feel, high above. The fig in the church shifts its leaves. The marsh at the valley's edge shivers with insects.

Caldtow is a place that has found its shape. The erosion is not of stone but of boundaries — between built and grown, between old and new, between the industrial and the organic. The wear of centuries has smoothed everything into one thing. The weirdness is mild — a spiral tree, a fig in a church, the slow disappearance of walls into ground — but it is present, a slight tilt in the ordinary that makes you notice things you would not otherwise see. You notice the way the roots have grown into the shape of the bricks they replaced. You notice the way the light falls through the canopy onto the moss on the stones, and how the moss grows thicker where the light has been, as if the light is a hand that touches and leaves a mark.

You stay for three days. You sleep in a room whose walls are half-brick and half-ivy. You eat bread made from grain grown in a garden that sits atop what was once a factory floor, the soil deep now, dark, rich with the slow work of time. You drink the water from the spring box and listen to the drip of condensation on every surface and the small, constant sound of the river beneath the moss.

On the morning you leave, the fog is thick. You cannot see the edge of the settlement — only the first few meters of mossy road, and then the white, and then the silence.

You do not look back.

You already know what it looks like.
