The city climbs. Not upward so much as outward, spreading across the high plateau in every direction until the eye finds no horizon, only the dark suggestion of more—the next tower, the next complex, the next node in a system too large to comprehend from within. The plateau sits high enough that the thin atmosphere carries only a whisper of warmth, a temperature that should freeze water solid, except the water doesn't freeze. It hangs in the air as condensation, beads on every surface, pools in channels cut along the bases of structures, flows through pipes that run along every wall in a second skin of infrastructure. The city's engineering keeps the water liquid at temperatures where it should be ice—this is known, documented, even celebrated as proof of the technology's power—but knowing doesn't make the cold less present. You feel it in the walls, in the ground, in the way your breath fogs and the fog settles and never quite dissipates.

There are no plants. This was noticed early, in the first surveys, and it remains true. The city rises from bare rock, from silicate and mineral composites grown rather than built, from materials that incorporate water-harvesting lattices and thermal-regulation weaves into their molecular structure. The rock beneath is older than the city, still raw, still cooling from processes that began when the planet was young—new geology pushing upward at the margins of the plateau, slow intrusion, lava crystallizing into obsidian, basalt cooling in formations that haven't yet learned their final shapes. The erosion parameter doesn't measure rain or wind but the absence of settling: the rock is still becoming rock. The infrastructure sits on a world in formation, its foundations anchored to material that shifts and compacts and reshapes itself over centuries.

You cannot see the ground clearly. Not because of darkness alone—the darkness is near total, a deep black broken only by the city's own lights and the faint luminescence of its inhabitants—but because the ground isn't a single surface. It is layered, stratified, a palimpsest of construction and colonization and cohabitation. Pathways worn by the constant passage of creatures. Channels cut by water, maintained by water, carrying water in directions that serve purposes no observer can immediately parse. Towers that rise from the infrastructure below, connected by cables and pipes and gangways, their bases lost in the dense medium of the city itself. The scale is town-size or valley-size at minimum, regional at best guess, and the guess is uncertain because the city extends in all directions and no survey has mapped it completely.

The creatures. The fauna parameter is the highest besides tech, and you understand why the moment you enter the city proper. They are everywhere. Not visible everywhere—not in swarms or crowds or densities that block light—but present everywhere, filling every niche, occupying every surface, moving through channels and pipes and the spaces between structures in a constant, overwhelming circulation. Some are small, the size of rats or insects, and you hear them more than see them: the click of chitin on metal, the soft wet sounds of bodies moving through condensation, the rustle of creatures in pipes. Others are large, slow, attached to buildings like the buildings are their hosts—生物 that have merged with infrastructure so completely that the boundary between organism and structure is lost. Others still move through the air, through the dark, leaving trails of light in their wake—bioluminescent or photonic or something that uses light as locomotion, their forms visible only as glow and motion.

They are not all the same species. Not all the same biology. The engineering has created organisms from silicon and carbon and metal and light, organisms that thrive in cold and moisture, that need the city's infrastructure the way a body needs blood. They fill the city not because the city allows it but because they are part of the city, because the city is their ecology, because the saturation of the density parameter is not about space but about interdependence. Every creature has a function. Every movement serves a process. The city breathes with them, its metabolism conducted by a million small bodies and a thousand large ones, its circulation threaded through organisms that have become indistinguishable from the pipes they inhabit.

The sound reflects this. Not noise but information—the constant hum of systems processing, the wet sounds of organisms feeding or moving or reproducing, the high faint whine of electronics, the deeper pulse of whatever serves as the city's heart. You hear water in channels, the trickling that never stops, and you hear creatures in the water, in the walls, in the air. You hear the city itself, the groan of infrastructure settling, the tick of thermal regulation adjusting, the low vibration that might be geological or might be mechanical or might be the city dreaming. The sound is everywhere, layered, impossible to isolate into component parts. It is not unpleasant but it is not comfortable. It is the sound of a world running.

The light comes from the city. Glow panels embedded in surfaces, bioluminescent organisms in pipes, the faint light of creatures moving through dark channels. It is not darkness exactly but dimness, a low constant illumination that reveals shapes without edges, structures without detail, the suggestion of more beyond every illuminated patch. In the light you see water on surfaces, condensation beading on metal and glass and organic composite, the wet gleam of the city maintaining its own moisture in its own engineered cold. In the light you see creatures—your first clear sight of them, the way they move, the way they fit into the city's systems. Some have eyes. Some do not. Some reflect the light; others seem to absorb it, their bodies dark and formless until they move and reveal their shapes.

The weirdness is not in single elements but in the whole: the combination of wet and cold and barren, of city and creature and geology still becoming, of technology so advanced it bends physics and biology so fundamental it predates the technology, of darkness that is not empty but full. The city is not alien in the way of foreign planets but in the way of collapsed categories. You cannot say where the built ends and the grown begins, where the organism ends and the machine begins, where the city ends and the world begins. The infrastructure saturates everything, but the saturation is not imposition—it is integration, the way blood saturates tissue, the way roots saturate soil. The city is a metabolism, and the creatures are its cells, and the rock beneath is its skeleton still forming, and the water is its blood held liquid by processes you do not understand.

You stand in a channel of flowing water and feel the cold through your boots. You hear creatures in the walls. You see light from above, from below, from directions you cannot identify. The city breathes around you, and you are inside it, and you are part of its fauna now, your presence another data point in its slow processing, another body in its dense circulation. The geology beneath you shifts—imperceptibly, over centuries—new rock forming at the margins of the plateau. The creatures above you move through their channels, filling niches, serving processes, living lives measured in cycles you cannot perceive. The city holds you. The city holds everything. And the plateau rises toward a dark sky, and the water flows, and the cold remains, and the light holds steady, and the creatures keep moving, and the sound keeps sounding, and the world keeps becoming.
