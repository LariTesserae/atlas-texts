# The Drowned Grove

Water fills everything here, but not as a single body. It moves in threads—thin sheets cascading between root-columns, pools nested in the crooks of living architecture, mist that gathers and falls and gathers again in endless small circulations. The world is soaked through, every surface slick, every breath drawn wet. In the deeper hollows between the platforms of root and vine, the water pools dark and still, and you sense it goes far down—fathoms into the body of whatever grows here.

The platforms themselves are not built so much as grown. Over generations, the people who lived here—and some still live here, in the upper reaches where the air reaches—trained the roots of the great trees into forms that hold their shape: bridges, walkways, platforms barely wide enough for two to pass. The trees obliged. Their wood is soft, almost spongy, and it takes a primitive edge well. You can cut a notch and the tree will grow around it, incorporate it. The oldest walkways are nearly indistinguishable from the oldest branches—worked wood merged so thoroughly into living wood that you cannot say where craft ended and growth began.

This is a high place, though you might not know it from within. The water masks it. But beyond the curtain of roots and mist, you can glimpse the sky pressing close, and sometimes the wind that finds its way down carries a thinness—the air of altitude, of height. The settlement was built here deliberately, on a plateau that rises from whatever vast lowland surrounds it, and the water that feeds the grove comes from somewhere above still, seeps through stone, gathers in the root-mats, spills out over the edges into the long fall below. You do not see the fall. But you hear it, sometimes—a distant white noise beneath the other noises, a presence you feel in the chest more than the ear.

The cool is constant. Not cold—that would kill the green—but perpetually cool, like morning before it decides to become afternoon. The people here wear layered plant-fiber clothing, simple weaves that dry slowly and must be tended. They have fire, but small fires, contained in stone hearths grown into the larger root structures, feeding on wood that the grove provides in endless supply. The smoke rises through channels carved into the living wood and exits somewhere high, dispersing into the mist.

The sound here is everything. Water dripping from leaf to leaf, pooling, moving, dripping again. The creak of wood under its own weight. The calls of creatures—frogs in the deep pools, birds in the upper canopy whose names you don't know, insects that fill the air with a chord of buzz and trill that rises and falls with the temperature and the light. The people sing sometimes, or shout, or hammer—wood on wood, a rhythm that the grove absorbs and throws back transformed. You cannot be quiet here. Even silence is full.

The fauna are abundant but not overwhelming. They are woven into the system. Frogs rest on leaves and eat what falls into the water. Birds nest in the angles where root meets root and catch insects on the wing. Small creatures—lizards, rodents, things between—move through the architecture of the grove as if it were made for them, and perhaps it was. The people who trained the roots also shaped the spaces between, made ledges and hollows, created conditions for the creatures they needed and discouraged those they didn't. Generations of patient adjustment. A whole ecology built around a single intent: abundance for those who live here.

Sparse, though. For all its richness, the grove is not crowded. The platforms are separated by water and by the deep shadow of root. You must travel by boat, or by the root-bridges, or by swimming if you're willing. Between each cluster of dwellings, each hearth and storehouse and drying-ledge, there is water, and there is green, and there is the sense that the space could hold many more but chooses not to. The people here know emptiness. They have made peace with it. It is part of the design.

And the design is strange, if you sit with it. A flooded highland. A grove that is also a town. Roots that are also roads. The water comes from above but also below; the light filters through a thousand small gaps; the creatures move through three dimensions—water, wood, air—as if the boundaries between them were suggestions. You are a visitor here, and you feel it in your bones. This place is not made for you. But it permits you, and that permission is not nothing. You move through it carefully, respectfully, and the grove does not object. It has seen stranger things than you. It has been here longer than your kind has been anywhere.
