The ground goes on forever, and every inch of it is made.

No natural substrate penetrates this world. The surface beneath everything is metal alloy—porous in places, polished in others, engineered at industrial scale to form a seamless floor across a basin that might be the size of an inland sea. Where the surface has been recently cast or extruded, edges remain sharp: corners on channel-edges where moisture collects, creases where modular sections were joined. Nothing has softened. Moisture rises through joints and condenses on undersides of overhanging structures, creating a perpetual dampness that pools in low channels and seeps upward through engineered substrate, keeping everything slick and warm.

The warmth is ambient—it seeps from beneath, radiates from machinery that has run so long it has become part of the landscape's temperature. The air is heavy with humidity. Moisture settles on surfaces like breath on glass. In places, the condensation runs in thin sheets down vertical faces of built structures—storage tanks, process housings, conduit towers—feeding into channels that run between them in grids so vast they disappear into haze at the horizon. The scale makes weather of its own: thermal columns rise where the machinery exhales heat, moisture condenses in the upper layers, falls as perpetual drizzle that feeds back into the channels. A hydrology that has no sun, no season, no variation. A closed system in perfect equilibrium.

There are no plants. The world is mineral and made and populated by things that are not plants. The creatures are everywhere—layered, near-touching, saturating every volume of space. They move along channels, cluster in low areas where moisture collects, cling to vertical surfaces, fill the spaces between structures in densities so high that movement ripples through them like wind through grass, except the grass is made of ten thousand organisms whose surfaces are engineered tissue—membrane, fiber, engineered flesh that breathes and pulses and digests and reproduces within parameters that never vary. They are not natural fauna. They are constructed life, bred over timescales that make the landscape itself seem transient, grown in vats the size of coastal seas, seeded into environments where they thrive in the damp and the warm. They have no predators because nothing dies here in the conventional sense. They live and reproduce and are processed—their byproducts flowing through industrial channels toward processing centers that return their materials to the system. A closed loop. A fauna that exists because it was made to exist, maintained at population densities that have never fluctuated in the memory of its engineers.

The sounds are minimal. The machinery hums at frequencies that blend into the warmth. The creatures move with wet, soft sounds—shuffling, settling, the whisper of membrane on metal—but these sounds are so consistent, so evenly distributed across the vastness, that they become a texture rather than an event. Silence would be more noticeable. The quiet is not absence but presence: the hum of engines, the soft breath of fauna, the faint patter of condensation falling through channels. All of it steady. All of it unvarying. All of it exactly as it was a century ago and as it will be a century hence.

The weirdness lives in the stability. A world this vast, this populated, this saturated with industrial infrastructure and engineered life, should show signs of use. There should be wear, gradients, decay, change. But the surfaces are as sharp as they were when they were made. The fauna densities have not shifted. The moisture levels hold constant. The machinery runs at the same efficiency it did when it was new—or if it has degraded, the degradation is so slow that it registers as no change at all. The world exists in perfect equilibrium: no part of it pulling ahead, no part falling behind. A system that has found its lowest energy state and cannot find reason to leave it.

If you stood at the center of the basin and looked out in every direction, you would see the same thing: a grid of built structures extending to the horizon, channels of moisture reflecting light from sources embedded in the construction, organisms moving in patterns so consistent they might be mistaken for texture. Nothing ages. Nothing grows. Nothing ends. The world is warm, wet, alive with constructed fauna, and utterly still.
