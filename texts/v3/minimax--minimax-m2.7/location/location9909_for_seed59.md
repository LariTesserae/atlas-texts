Water rises to within a hand's depth of the surface everywhere. The ground is not ground so much as a mat — a living skin of sphagnum and root-fiber and wet mineral dark that holds you up until it doesn't, until you step through into something that closes around your ankles. There is nowhere here that is dry. Mist gathers in the canopy above and falls as a fine, constant rain, beading on every surface. The air is cool on the skin and carries the smell of decay and growth so intertwined they are indistinguishable — one breath is both.

The elevation is real but modest: a raised shelf of land, perhaps a hundred meters above a distant floodplain, though the distant is hard to see because nothing here is built for sight-lines. The vegetation does not grow from the land so much as it constitutes it. Bamboo and cane and a species of woody fern that reaches four meters and leans against everything around it — against other ferns, against the thick-boled trees that are the only truly solid vertical things here, against the platforms and walkways that the residents have built and that the plants have already begun to overgrow. Plants seeded themselves into the fiber-and-bone lattice of the walkways within days of construction. The buildings — if they can be called buildings — are frameworks of split bamboo lashed with vine, their walls stuffed with dried moss and reed matting, their roofs thatched in overlapping sheets of palm bark. But the roof's edges already curl. Green is coming. The settlement is a temporary arrangement within an ecology that is indifferent to it and will, in time, absorb it entirely.

The scale is intimate. From any point in the hamlet you can reach its edges in fifty steps. But the edges are not empty — they are dense. The trees close in, their canopy so interlocking that the light below is green and directionless, the same at noon as at dusk. The trunks are close enough that you could lean from one to another without stepping down. The air between them is thick with things: wasps' nests the size of headscarves suspended from leaf-nodes, fungal shelves growing from the dark of every split bark, orchids clinging to vertical surfaces at every height. Nothing is unoccupied. The space is crowded with living matter to the point where individual organisms become hard to distinguish — it is more like a single, massive, breathing tissue than a collection of separate things.

And the animals. The fauna is not present so much as omnipresent. Birds are the least of it — they are the easy part to see. They move in the upper canopy in constant motion, shifting positions, calling, nesting. Below them, things move through the understory that you hear before you see: the dry-scrape of something heavy through reed-thick, the sudden splash of waterfowl startled from a pool you didn't notice until you were knee-deep in it. Insects are the real weight of it — a wall of sound that is not quite sound, a vibration that lives in the air and registers more as pressure than as hearing. Mosquitoes are constant but not alone. Something clicks in the bamboo at dusk. Something whistles from the water in the early morning, a three-note call that repeats until you realize it has been repeating for an hour and you have simply stopped counting. The frogs begin at sunset and do not stop. There is no silence here and there has never been silence here and there will not be silence here.

The built structures are woven into the living mass. Walkways are lashed to the standing trunks and float above the water on bundles of reed and cane; they sway when you walk on them. Platforms are lashed to the broad forks of trees where three or four trunks converge, and on these platforms the houses sit — small, one-room structures with smoke-blackened thatch and drying racks and nets hanging from every beam. The tools you see are simple: hand-axes of volcanic stone lashed to wooden handles, woven fish-traps, rope made from the inner bark of a plant you do not know the name of. The people here know what they are doing — the construction is skilled, the knots are tight, the placement of platforms shows understanding of load and leverage — but the technology is elemental. Bone, wood, fiber, stone. Fire, smoke, water.

The water is everywhere and below. Dig a hand into the moss-mat and you reach it within seconds — black, cold, mineral-rich. The pools between the hummocks of solid ground are stagnant and dark and full of things you feel rather than see moving under the surface. Water flows somewhere beneath, in channels and sumps, feeding the moss, feeding the ferns, feeding the roots of everything. The hamlet sits on a hummock large enough to hold thirty people and their structures, and the hummock is itself a living thing — its surface is green and soft and will not bear weight everywhere, and the edges of it are seeping.

The place is turbulent in its energy. Not violent — there is no violence here, or not often — but restless. Things grow here at a rate that is visible. A wound in a cane stalk will be sealed by fungal tissue within a day. A cleared patch of ground will be green with new growth within a week. The animals are always moving — migrations of insects in season, birds coming and going, the daily movement of amphibians from water to shade and back. The weather changes quickly: brief, sharp rains, then clearing, then mist again, then a wind that comes from nowhere and moves through the canopy and is gone. The settlement's inhabitants are busy people — there is always something to tend, somewhere to reinforce, something growing or dying or being eaten that requires attention. Nothing here holds still.

And yet it is old. The hummock has been here, in this form, for generations. The trees have been this thick for longer than anyone's memory. The water has risen and fallen and risen again along the same channels, and the moss has grown and died and grown back, and the settlement has been rebuilt in the same location so many times that the oldest platforms have become part of the landscape — their wood fused with fungal growth and root-work into something that is neither constructed nor grown but both simultaneously. The erosion is not dramatic. Nothing is being stripped away. But the forms have settled: the water's paths are fixed, the hummock's shape is stable, the canopy has found its height and holds it. It is a place in balance, turbulent and dense and alive, held in a state of constant small change that moves in large circles and returns.
