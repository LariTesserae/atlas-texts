# The Plateau at the End of the Aqueduct

The stone remembers water differently here. It is a high plateau—high enough for the air to thin just slightly, for the sky to broaden, for distance to become a real thing you can measure in hours rather than minutes—but the stone remembers when rivers moved through it. Deep channels are carved along the plateau's western face in a pattern too regular for rain, too deliberate for time alone. They are the old irrigation cuts, the aqueduct gutters, the drainage gutters of a city that has stood here for centuries, and the plateau has accepted this memory so completely that water still moves through it: not in the open channels but everywhere else, seeping upward through jointed basalt, condensing on the undersides of overhanging shelf rock, pooling in the pitted surfaces that look like weathered skin. Everything above the third terrace is wet to the touch. Everything below is dry as paper.

The vegetation clings to what it can get. Sparse dryland scrub—silverleaf and hardgrass, some kind of waxy succulent that blooms once a year in the early dark of morning—roots itself in the crevices where the ancient cut-stone joins meet. Along the eastern perimeter, where the plateau drops away into a broad valley, the ground is harder and paler, and the plants grow thinner still, spaced far apart, each one alone. There is no canopy. No shade except what the buildings make. The warm air rises from the valley's floor and pools against the plateau's flatness, and it has the particular quality of warmth that comes off waterlogged stone: not uncomfortable but insistent, something you feel in your teeth and the backs of your eyes.

And the city holds it all together.

The settlement on the plateau is dense in its center and then stops. You can walk from the main industrial quarter to open stone in four minutes, stepping over a low wall that was once a boundary and is now just a ledge, and there you are in the scrubland, the silence, the smell of dry mineral and warm basalt. The buildings are old—some of them very old, stone foundations that predate the industrial era by centuries—and newer constructions have been built on top and alongside them with the casual logic of a city that has always been here, that has never stopped adding to itself. Pipes run aboveground because the ground is unreliable, because water moves through it unpredictably, because the engineers of several generations made different choices and none of the choices were undone. Conduit and iron gutter and ceramic duct and cable bundled in black coating run along every surface, connecting the factories to the reservoir tanks to the pumping stations to the filtration housings that stand like white blisters on the plateau's northern edge.

The factories themselves are not beautiful. They are wide, low, brick-and-concrete, with corrugated metal roofs that have been patched and re-patched until the patchwork becomes the texture. Steam rises from vents in their walls in continuous plumes that the warm air bends and dissipates. You hear them from everywhere on the plateau because there is no valley to trap sound and no wind to carry it away; the sound simply moves outward from the machinery at its source and fills the plateau like a substance, like water filling a bowl. Engines run continuously—large diesel engines for the pumping stations, smaller ones for the conveyor lines that move aggregate and processed stone from the quarries on the plateau's southern edge. The air is full of a deep industrial bass that you feel in your sternum when you stand still, and layered above it are the higher sounds: the clatter of metal on metal, the rhythmic thump of something large and hydraulic, a constant electrical hum that seems to come from the rock itself.

Fauna is present in the way that absence is present. Occasionally a bird crosses the plateau—not often, and the birds are small, dark, fast, the kind that do not sing. A few insects near the wet stone. The rats in the industrial quarter are large and unhurried and do not seem to fear people, which is its own kind of wrongness, the way adaptation to a place can make something that should be skittish into something that simply stares. You do not see anything large. You do not see anything wild in the full sense of the word. Whatever was here before the city is either gone or hiding in the deep erosion channels on the plateau's far side, where the stone has been carved away to expose layers of old sediment that no one goes near.

Because the strangeness lives in the stone.

Not dramatically. Not in a way that announces itself. But if you stand at the plateau's western edge and look down at the drainage channels, you notice that the water—always moving, seeping upward through the basalt—has left mineral deposits in shapes that are not quite natural. They follow the channels, yes, but they also follow each other, overlapping in patterns that feel repeated, that feel chosen. And if you press your hand to the wet stone, it is warm in the way that warm stone should be warm, but there is a faint vibration beneath the warmth, a subsonic pulse that might be the engines or might be something else, and you cannot tell because the engines are loud everywhere and the vibration is everywhere too. At night, the steam vents glow with a faint orange light from the combustion within, and the plateau's flat surface becomes an island of heat and noise and mineral wetness, surrounded by the dark valley below where nothing is visible and nothing is audible except the distant animal sound of wind moving through dry grass.

The plateau has been here a long time. The water has been moving through it longer than the city. The erosion did not begin with the settlers; it began with something older, something patient, something that had already carved the channels and the basins and the deep smooth gullies by the time the first foundation stone was laid. The city built over what was already there, built its pipes and channels and factories to channel what was already moving, and in doing so it made itself part of the plateau's logic—a logic of water and stone and warmth, of sound that accumulates because there is nowhere for it to go, of vegetation that survives in small strong pockets, of a strangeness that is not in any one place but in the fact that the place holds together at all, that it persists, that the wet stone and the dry stone and the loud machines and the empty scrubland exist in the same place and make, against all odds, a single landscape.
