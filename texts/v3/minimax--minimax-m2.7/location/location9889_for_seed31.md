# The Still Vale of Olorith

The plateau rises suddenly from the lower lands, a shelf of new stone cut clean against older geography. Around its edges, the mountains that ring the valley are still sharp—peaks that haven't yet learned to be mountains, their ridges like broken teeth against the sky. The valleys between them are raw, V-shaped cuts into stone that hasn't been exposed to weather long enough to soften. Everything has an unfinished quality. The roads are new. The walls are new. The canal stones are fitted and grouted and still sharp-edged, as if the place was carved yesterday.

And perhaps it was.

Water is the first thing you notice, and the second, and the third. It seeps from the rock face at the valley's eastern edge—not a spring exactly, more a weeping, a slow exudation of moisture from porous stone that has never fully dried. The water runs in channels that were cut and fitted, not eroded. It follows the roads, parallels them, crosses under them through culverts of fitted stone. The canals are open, lined with clay that has darkened with constant wet, and the water in them is still. Not stagnant—clearer than that, more held. It moves slowly, barely moving, and the surface reflects the sky without ripple.

Where the water runs, the stone weeps. Dark patches spread across the pale rock, and where water has touched the same place for a long time, a thin crust of mineral deposits has formed—white and gray, streaking downward like frozen tears. The moss grows thickest here. Not the bright green of growing things but a deep, almost black green, wet and heavy, clinging to stone that never dries. Ferns grow from the cracks in the roads. Broad-leafed plants unfurl along the canal edges, their leaves beaded with moisture that refuses to evaporate.

The air is warm and wet in equal measure. Humidity sits like a weight, not oppressive but present, a pressure against the skin that never lifts. The sun comes through pale and diffuse, softened by the moisture in the atmosphere, and everything is lit evenly, shadows soft, no harsh edges. The light does not change. The light is always this—mid-afternoon, diffuse, even.

Olorith spreads across the plateau in its incompleteness. Buildings of fitted stone stand in small clusters, separated by cultivated beds and open ground. The construction is careful—good joints, true lines, wood used where wood should be used and stone where stone is needed. There are no metal fixtures visible, but the nail holes are there, and the brackets, and somewhere a blacksmith has forged them, somewhere a mason has cut the stone, and the work is good. The work is thorough. The roads between the buildings are graded and drained, with canal water running alongside them, and the water does not overflow and the roads do not flood. Someone understood what they were building.

But no one walks them.

The fields beyond the center are planted. You can see the furrows, the careful rows, the evidence of tending. Grain grows—or something like grain, broad-leafed and green and heavy with moisture, bending slightly under the weight of water it cannot transpire fast enough. The fields should want harvest. They do not seem to want anything. They simply grow, and grow, and remain, exactly as they are, year without change.

The silence here is not empty. It is complete. No birds call or wheel overhead. No insect hums in the wet air. No wind moves through the leaves of the grain or the broad-leafed plants. The water should make a sound—it flows, however slowly—but there is only the sense of motion, the appearance of flow, and a silence beneath it like held breath.

The place is in equilibrium. Water enters from the weeping rock and exits through channels that carry it to the valley's edge and over the sharp cliffs into the lower lands. Plants grow and do not die. The sun warms and the moisture holds and nothing changes. The roads stay clean. The canals stay clear. The buildings stand and will stand, because nothing here is old enough to fall.

Perhaps this is what the valley was made for: a city that would hold its own weight, that would sustain itself, that would be ready. Perhaps the people who built it left before they arrived, or never came at all, and the infrastructure keeps its own slow time, water moving through channels cut for water, crops growing in soil that needs no farmer, the architecture holding itself upright because it was built well enough that it does not yet know how to fall.

The mountains are still young. The stone is still sharp. And Olorith waits at their center, wet and warm and silent, the roads clean for walking, the fields heavy with grain that no one will ever harvest, the water flowing through channels that no one will ever tend, in a stillness that has no reason to break.
