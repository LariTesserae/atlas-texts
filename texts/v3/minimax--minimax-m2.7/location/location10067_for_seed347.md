# The Underhalls of Vethros

At the edge of what was once a river delta, the land simply… stops. Not with a cliff or a canyon wall, but with a soft surrender—a vast depression where the surface dissolves into wet earth, then pools, then sinks entirely into the earthworks of the Underhalls.

The city spreads below like a cupped palm holding water. Streets descend in organic switchbacks, carved by centuries of feet and rain, the stone here dark and dense, prone to weeping. Every surface is slick—the cobblestones, the staircases, the bases of walls—because the water table here sits just inches beneath the flagstones. Dig your heel in at any corner and you will see it: a dark seam, a slow seep, the subterranean river announcing itself through capillary action. In the lowest quarters, the drainage channels run ankle-deep and move slowly, carrying the mineral taste of deep places.

---

## Water and Wet

The Vethros marsh runs where the old riverbed broadens out beneath the city's southern quarter. Reeds grow thick along the margins where standing water meets the carved stone promenades—locals have woven lattice-work bridges across the reed-beds, barely wide enough for a single file of people. The bridges sway. The reed-beds breathe with small movements, and what moves in the reed-beds you do not always see: only hear, in the quiet hours before dawn, a soft displacement and slide.

The water here does not feel threatening. It feels *present*—the way breath is present, or gravity. It gathers in hollows between old building foundations, fills the irrigation channels that feed the city's cultivation terraces, pools in the cellars of the oldest halls. In some districts, the floors of ground-level rooms are permanently damp, and the inhabitants have simply adapted: raised stone platforms, wooden grating, footpaths of stacked flat stone.

The walls sweat. This is something strangers notice immediately. In the upper corridors of the administrative quarter, you can press your palm to the dressed stone and feel it cold and damp, and if you stay long enough, you begin to feel the dampness not as a flaw but as a quality—like the pulse of a living thing.

---

## Light and Air

Light enters the Underhalls in ways that seem strange until you understand them. The depression's rim is high enough to catch and hold sun, so the upper terraces receive direct illumination for several hours each morning—the southern exposure, where the markets cluster. The deeper you go, the more the light shifts: reflected off the water, softened by the marsh mist, filtered through gaps and cracks in the overlying stone until it becomes a diffuse ambient glow, silver and diffuse, not quite daylight and not quite dark.

The cold comes from below. Not dramatically—not howling or freezing—but present, a steady coolness that rises from the deep channels and settles in the lower districts. People in the market terraces wear light layers; people who live below the third tier wear heavier wool, and in the deepest residential warrens, braziers burn through the night. The cold is not unpleasant. It is simply *there*, the way it is in any place where stone meets water and the sun does not reach.

---

## The Architecture of Occupied Space

The Underhalls are dense. There is no empty ground. The city grew over generations in a pragmatic scramble to fill every usable hollow, and now the settlement has achieved a kind of saturation—buildings stack on top of buildings, balconies overhang streets that are also bridges, rooms open onto the backs of other rooms across narrow light-wells. To stand in any public space is to be surrounded on three or more sides by the walls of other people's lives.

The construction is old in the upper tiers—carved directly into the sedimentary rock, fitted with timber framing for upper floors and extensions—but the lower you go, the more recent the work, and the more improvisational. The newest quarters are barely a generation old: stone shored up with treated wood, floors laid directly over packed earth, walls papered with woven reed matting to manage the damp. The city has no master plan. It has only *need*, and need keeps finding new surfaces to fill.

Fauna moves through the occupied spaces with a kind of proprietorial ease. The Underhalls have their own species now—or adaptations of surface species that have diverged enough to be considered distinct. The damp-welling beetles are everywhere, metallic-backed and slow, climbing the weeping walls in spiraling migrations at dusk. The marsh-eels occupy the lower channels and are considered a delicacy. Bats nest in the upper cornices; their droppings are collected and used in the cultivation terraces below. The people of the Underhalls know these creatures the way any farmer knows her livestock: not intimately, but functionally, with a working understanding of patterns and seasons.

---

## Sound

The Underhalls are quiet. Not silent—nothing is silent—but quiet in the way that only occupied places can be quiet. The noise of living is constant but low: footsteps on stone, the murmur of voices through walls, the creak of timber adjusting to moisture. Water makes its own sound: the tick of drips in the deep channels, the slow shush of the marsh channels when the wind rises above, the subterranean pulse that locals learn to hear and visitors do not notice until they have been there long enough for it to stop.

When the city sleeps—and here, "sleep" means something looser than it does on the surface, spread across hours rather than concentrated—the Underhalls hold only water-sounds and the soft communications of creatures. The silence, at those hours, is not empty. It is full. It is the kind of quiet that has texture, that presses gently against the ear.

---

## Dynamic Systems

The Underhalls are not still. The water table rises and falls with the season, feeding the marsh in spring and retreating in autumn, carrying the city's internal rhythms. The cultivation terraces are planted and harvested in cycles calibrated to depth and light. The creatures of the Underhalls move in patterns that are beginning to be understood: the migrations of the wall-beetles, the breeding cycles of the marsh-eels, the emergence seasons of the blind cave-crickets that surface in the lower channels.

Weather, as surface people understand it, does not reach here—but its effects do. Rain above produces drip and seep below. Heat above draws moisture up through the stone. Cold above sharpens the underground chill. The city's inhabitants read these signals the way sailors read the sea: as information, as negotiation, as an ongoing conversation with forces they can influence but not control.

---

What the Underhalls are, above all, is *full*. Every hollow filled, every surface used, every channel and corridor and light-well crowded with the evidence of living. The density is not oppressive—it is simply a fact, like the damp in the walls, like the cold from below. The city is a living system in which water and stone and creature and human have reached an arrangement, imperfect and ongoing, a place where below-surface elevation meets urban saturation and produces something that has been growing for longer than anyone remembers.
