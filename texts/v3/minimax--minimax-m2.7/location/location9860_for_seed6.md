# The Propagation Hall

You stand in a space that has never known a natural surface.

The floor is a grid of perforated titanium, and beneath it water moves—slowly, endlessly, in channels carved and welded rather than worn. Water here is not a resource or a feature; it is the medium. It falls in fine mists from pipes routed through the vaulted ceiling, condenses on every surface, trickles through channels between the packed lattice of growing-tanks and cultivation racks. The air itself holds a saturated weight, like breathing at the bottom of a slow waterfall.

Everything is built. Every wall, every walkway, every curved vessel and rectilinear channel—manufactured, assembled, engineered. No stone, no soil, no natural substrate. The ground beneath the grating is polymer-lined channels, the ceiling is a lattice of glass tubes carrying thermal exchange fluid, the walls are modular panels bolted to structural ribs. The place has the quality of a machine that happens to grow things.

Yet the vegetation is lush. Vines run through the channels like wiring, their tendrils following the courses of water with deliberate precision. Moss covers every vertical surface in thick, engineered mats—genetically uniform, dark green, pulling humidity from the air. The growing-tanks hold stacked plant-mass in various stages of cultivation: pale shoots reaching toward the filtered light from above, root systems suspended in flowing nutrient solution, fruiting bodies heavy in their fixed positions. The plants are not wild. They are crop. But they are everywhere, filling every surface, climbing every available edge, pressing against each other in dense columns that leave no space unoccupied.

You notice the sharpness. Edges are clean-cut, not weathered. Joints are tight. Panels bear the faint residue of recent assembly—oils not yet evaporated, protective films still peeling at corners. Whatever this place is, it is new. Recently constructed. The machinery has not yet had time to accumulate the patina of years.

The scale is intimate despite its complexity. You are in a cathedral-sized enclosure, a single room perhaps—no, a single *purpose*—perhaps fifty meters across, its ceiling arching overhead in a series of structural ribs. You could walk its perimeter in minutes. But the density defeats any sense of openness. The growing-tanks stand in rows so close you must turn sideways to pass between them. The walkways are narrow, grated, pressed between walls of engineered vegetation. Everything is packed, saturated, fully occupied. There is no emptiness here.

You hear the industrial hum beneath the quiet. The pumps are deep, turned slow—they move immense volume through vast conduits, but at low velocity, a patient circulation that does not hurry. The water itself speaks in soft percussions: drip, trickle, the settling of mist on leaves. Occasionally a mechanism shifts—a valve adjusts, a rack descends on its counterweight—and the sound is a low, hydraulic groan that fades into the general silence. This is not a place of noise. It is a place of slow motion, patient processes, gradual cycles. The plants themselves seem to move at a different tempo than natural things—their growth visible, their orientation shifting across hours rather than days.

Light is normal, ambient—filtered down from panels in the ceiling that emit a steady, diffuse glow without visible source. Not sunlight. Not natural. The illumination is even, shadowless, the kind of light that makes everything feel suspended in its own existence.

There is no fauna. No insects. No birds. The plants are tended by mechanisms, not animals. The silence belongs to something that was never alive in the first place—or to something that has been refined out of existence, stripped to the essential loop of water, nutrients, light, and growth. A system that does not need mouths, or legs, or the chaos of metabolism.

The warmth is constant, tropical, the warmth of a body or a functioning engine. Your skin feels it immediately—the place holds heat the way a closed fist holds water.

You become aware of the structure around you as a kind of organism. The walls breathe with condensation. The ceiling rains in measured cycles. The floor carries the slow pulse of circulated fluid. Everything is made—made metal, made polymer, made light—but everything is also alive in its way, in the sense of being active, purposeful, purposeful in a way that a forest is not. This is a constructed thing that grows. A built thing that lives.

And in the packed density of it, in the warm wet air and the soft industrial sounds and the sharp edges of new machinery surrounding the patient riot of engineered green, something is strange. Not wrong. Not threatening. Just—with a weight of purpose you cannot quite read. A space designed for propagation, for multiplication, for the systematic cultivation of abundance. But whose abundance, and toward what end, you cannot say.

The water moves below. The plants grow. The machines hum. The mist falls.

And nothing else breathes.
