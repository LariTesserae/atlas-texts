The plateau stretches in every direction under a light that seems to come from everywhere and nowhere, a diffuse brilliance that leaves no shadow whole. Everything here is made: the ground beneath, the air above, the moisture that beads on surfaces and pools in seams between structures. There is no natural substrate—not stone, not soil, not sediment. The surface you stand on is poured, pressed, cast, assembled. Joints and seams run in grids and arcs, interrupted by geometries that serve no obvious purpose, by structures that have no visible door.

This is a young place. The features are sharp. Erosion has barely begun its patient work; edges haven't dulled, surfaces haven't accumulated the patina of slow weather. What looks like stone is perhaps sixty years old, perhaps six. The rust on some fittings is the only softness, and even that seems recently applied, like makeup rather than age. In the low places where water gathers—channels that might be drainage or irrigation or something else entirely—surfaces are darker, slick, filmed with moisture that condensed from air that itself seems manufactured, as if the humidity is pumped rather than evaporated.

Vegetation is sparse but present: thin stands of something drought-resistant and tenacious pushing through joints, creeping along channels where moisture lingers. They look synthetic even when they aren't, their green too precise, their growth too aligned with manufactured seams. Fauna, though, is abundant—insects that move in murmurations, small creatures that have found the rhythms of this place, birds that nest in structures too high to examine. They are everywhere, a population density that would suggest wilderness if not for the quality of the light, the sharpness of the edges, the fact that everything you see was made.

The structures are industrial: factories without smokestacks, power stations whose function is unclear, warehouses that hold something but never open. Pipes run along surfaces and disappear into them. Platforms and walkways span distances that seem to serve no logistical purpose. The density is overwhelming—not crowded, exactly, but packed, every surface holding something, every vantage revealing more: towers and tanks, gantries and housings, things that hum and things that don't. Nothing is quiet exactly, but the sound is minimal—a low electrical tone, the distant mechanism of moving parts, the faint patter of condensation falling into pools. The silence is more present than the noise.

And there is the weirdness. It manifests not as spectacle but as wrongness, as details that don't cohere. A staircase that leads up to a wall and then continues, implying a floor that doesn't exist. A window that shows a sky but the wrong sky, stars in positions that don't correspond. Pipes that carry water uphill. Shadows that fall at angles that don't match the light sources. Here, where the plateau's edge drops away into cloud and distance, the horizon shimmers with something that might be elevation haze or might be the surface thinning, the made-ground approaching its edge.

It is cool here—permanently cool, as if the temperature is regulated along with everything else. The moisture in the air is controlled, channeled, managed. Nothing grows wild; nothing decays naturally. Everything is made, everything is maintained, everything is running, and the running has been running for a long time that feels like no time at all, since the edges are still sharp, since the structures still feel new. A place that was built and is still building, that was manufactured and continues to manufacture itself, on a plateau that sits above clouds and below a light that illuminates too much, that makes everything visible and leaves nowhere to hide from the fact that this is all constructed, all managed, all operating on principles that almost make sense but don't, quite, and the almost is what you feel in your stomach when you look too long at the horizon.
