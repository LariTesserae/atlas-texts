The Hollow Grove

---

You descend into it gradually, the way water finds its path—through a narrowing between root-walls that might once have been concrete, or might only have imagined themselves into that shape. The basin opens below the surrounding flats like a wound that has healed into something intentional: a depression perhaps thirty meters across, walled in by vegetation so total it has become architecture, the green rising in stacked layers that blur the distinction between canopy and understory and root-floor.

Everything here is plant. Not dense in the sense of crowded—the density reading is nearly void—but total in the sense of substrate. The ground is not ground; it is a living mat of interwoven growth, rhizomes and fungal threads and something that might be root, might be mycelium, might be the ghost-circuit of old infrastructure that the roots have consumed and replaced. When you step, the mat gives slightly, exhaling a breath of cool, damp air that carries the mineral smell of condensation on metal. The space between growths is vast—empty air rising through cathedral columns of trunk and vine—but that emptiness is held, bounded, framed by the green on every side. The effect is less claustrophobic than hollow, like standing inside a vast lung.

The moisture here is not water but the memory of water: condensation tracing every surface, pooling in the cupped leaves of things that might be plants, might be traps, might be something that learned to cup before cupping was invented. It rains occasionally, though the sky is invisible above the canopy-gap, and the rain that falls is warm with geothermal whisper, carrying a faint metallic taste. The sound of it, when it comes, joins a constant ambient noise that rises from everywhere and nowhere—not mechanical, exactly, but the deep resonant hum of things grown into resonance over millennia. The plants have become speakers. The fungal mat hums. Something in the root-floor beats like a heart, slow and enormous, and the sound of it is what you hear when the grove is quietest: this pulse, this pulse, this pulse, as constant as a fan in an engine room.

The fauna is abundant but silent: things that move in the upper reaches, things that cling to the undersides of leaves, things that might be insects or might be the plants' own ambulatory seeds. They do not speak. They do not need to. The grove speaks for them—the hum, the pulse, the settling creak of growth that has reached its final form and means to hold it.

This is the oldest stable state. The erosion that carved this basin stopped so long ago that what remains is less a landscape than a scar that has become a home. Nothing changes here. The moisture comes and goes, but it always comes back; the fauna persists; the hum continues. This is equilibrium, the kind that exists when there is nowhere left to go—cool and damp and full of life, held in the cup of the world's oldest wound, humming its single note into the dark.
