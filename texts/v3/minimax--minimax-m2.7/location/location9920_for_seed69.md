# The Weeping Plateau

The water here does not fall. It *exhales*.

From every vertical surface—the weathered faces of sedimentary stone, the corroded hulls of abandoned processing facilities, the broad leaves of ferns that grow in impossible profusion—a perpetual moisture beads and trickles. The plateau is so vast that weather systems breed within its own mass, banks of fog rolling across thousand-square-kilometers of summit like the breath of some sleeping leviathan. When you stand at the plateau's edge and look out, you see only fog, layer upon layer, cream-colored and luminous where the light fights through, dark where the moisture has thickened it to opacity. Nothing in that distance is still.

The plateau surface is a world unto itself. Ancient beyond reckoning, its stone has been grooved and channeled by uncountable centuries of water into sinuous furrows, each one a tributary in a continental drainage system that has no outlet. Water pools in every depression, in vast shallow lakes whose surfaces mirror nothing, in streams that vanish into sinkholes only to reappear miles away in waterfalls that pour from cliffsides without obvious source. The rock here is soft limestone, honeycombed with caves, and it weeps. Weeps constantly. Dripping, trickling, the whole plateau exhaling water the way a forest exhales oxygen—invisible tonally, but impossible to ignore as a presence. The humidity here is not a condition. It is a medium. You move through it like a fish through water.

Above the fern forests, the trees that survive on the plateau grow crooked and draped, their bark black with moisture, their canopies catching fog until they seem to be slowly drowning in the air itself. Where the forest thins, the stone takes over—broad expanses of karst pavement, wrinkled and folded, every hollow pooled, every ridge slick. It would be easy to walk these surfaces quickly if not for the fact that they are treacherous with life. Moss covers everything. Algae paints the standing water in slicks of iridescent green and purple. Insects rise in clouds as you pass, then settle back, indifferent. The plateau is not uninhabited. It is so inhabited that the air itself feels crowded.

And the fauna knows it. The lakeside shallows churn with life—things with too many legs, things with too few, things that pulse and things that dart. In the fern forests, the canopy shakes constantly with movement: creatures the size of small dogs moving in murmuration-like coordination, their cries a constant chatter that rises and falls like mechanical noise. By the industrial ruins, something large moves through the underbrush, heavy enough to snap branches, unseen but audible—breathing, or something like breathing, rhythmic and wet. The ecosystem here is not pristine. It is teeming with opportunistic life, the kind that thrives where something vast has broken down and the pieces are still rotting. The plateau eats. It digests. It exhales. The insects are so numerous that their collective drone becomes a kind of music, a chord of buzz and hum that varies by time of day, by cloud cover, by the movement of fog banks across the fern forests.

The light is wrong. This is the first thing anyone notices, and the last thing anyone stops noticing. There is no sun here. There may never have been a sun here. What illumination exists comes from below—the pale fungal glow of bioluminescent growths that colonize every moist surface, the dull red pulse of something in the deep caves that surfaces only rarely, the sourceless gleam that sometimes drifts through the fog like a reflection from a light that isn't there. Old industrial structures provide their own illumination in places: emergency lights still blinking after decades without maintenance, the faint electric hum of buried power infrastructure, the distant orange glow of furnaces that have apparently never been turned off. The darkness is not absolute. It is worse than absolute. It is *inconsistent*. You can see, but you cannot see well, and your eyes never adjust because the light is never stable, always shifting, always coming from a different angle or depth, always failing you at the crucial moment. The plateau is three-dimensional in its darkness. Things exist in layers of shadow, near and far and unknowable.

The industry is everywhere and nowhere. Scattered structures dot the plateau surface: processing facilities whose purposes are no longer identifiable, their smokestacks trailing steam and something else, something that smells like copper and rot; towers of rusted scaffolding that support nothing; pipelines that run from pools of standing water into the earth, feeding something deep underground, their joints weeping with mineral deposits. In the caves, the industry continues. The plateau is hollow, and in its hollows, machines still run. You hear them before you see them—the grinding of gears, the hiss of pressurized steam, the rhythmic clanging of mechanisms whose function is unclear. These are not the machines of any civilization you could name. They are older. They are *correct* for this place, as though they evolved here, as though the plateau grew them the way it grows its ferns and its fungi. Lights pulse in the cave mouths. Heat rises. The industrial hum joins the insect drone and the water noise into a single constant sound, a chord of presence that defines the place as thoroughly as its humidity.

What you hear, finally, is everything at once. The water sings in a thousand registers: the high thin note of drips falling onto stone, the deep resonant thrum of subterranean rivers, the white-noise rush of the great waterfalls that spring from cliffsides with no visible source. The machines add their contribution—mechanical rhythms, clicks and clanks, the arrhythmic pulse of something large and moving underground. The fauna adds its layer: the constant chatter of creatures in the fern forests, the distant bellow of something in the lake shallows, the rustling whisper of a thousand small things moving through the underbrush. And beneath it all, a sound that might be the plateau itself—a low, subsonic hum, felt more than heard, as though the karst stone is vibrating at some frequency too low for ears but too present for comfort.

The scale is the hardest thing to hold in the mind. You cannot see the plateau's edges. When you walk, you walk for days without reaching them. When you climb to high ground—which is everywhere, the plateau is a world of ridges and shallow peaks—the fog reveals only more plateau, an infinite crumpling of stone and water and life that extends in every direction until it is swallowed by the curvature of the horizon. This is not a landscape you experience. It is a landscape you *inhabit*, and the inhabiting takes longer than you expect, because the scale keeps expanding, because every time you think you have grasped its dimensions, you find a new valley, a new fog bank, a new cascade falling from a height you had not known existed.

You do not understand the rules of this place. Nobody does. The plateau is in balance—the water erodes the stone and the stone channels the water, the flora photosynthesizes in the near-dark and the fauna preys and is preyed upon, the machines run and the caves grow and the fog banks build and dissipate in their cycles. But the balance is not natural. It is not sustainable in any way you recognize. It is a balance maintained by forces you cannot identify, for purposes you cannot conceive. The animals move in patterns that seem almost correct but not quite. The water flows in channels that seem almost logical but not quite. The light comes from sources that seem almost real but not quite. You have been walking for six hours. You are reasonably certain you have been walking in a circle, but the circle is not closed—it is a spiral, or something more complex, something that folds through itself the way fog folds through fog, and you are not certain that you will ever reach the same place twice, or that there is a "same place," or that "twice" means what it has always meant.

The plateau breathes.

The water drips.

The machines run.
