# The Wide Warm Plateaus of Aevum

The plateau is old. Older than the memory of mountains. Its surface has been worn to a gentle patience—rounded hills, shallow valleys, stone so smooth it almost looks poured rather than carved. Water moves through it everywhere: seeping upward through porous rock, pooling in mineral flats, trickling down the faces of cliffs in thin persistent veils that never quite dry. The rock weeps. It has wept for so long that the surfaces it stains have become part of the landscape's grammar—dark streaks, pale blooms of mineral residue, the smell of hot stone and wet earth that never quite washes away.

There are no plants. None. The bones of this world are entirely mineral, and whatever once held chlorophyll or spores or pollen has long since been consumed, transformed, or simply never arrived. Instead, the ground is covered in something else: a fine gray mat, barely visible, that pulses faintly with moisture. Not plant. Not fungal. Something that predates the distinction—a living mineral-lace that clings to surfaces, digests trace elements from the stone itself, and produces a faint warm exhalation that contributes to the plateau's perpetual breath of humidity. Where the mat pools in standing water, the surface shimmers with a faint iridescence. Life, perhaps, but not life as anything below would recognize.

The warmth here is constant and sourceless. The altitude is high enough that cold should rule—the plateau rises into thinner air where the sky is a deeper blue and clouds form their own separate weather below—but the warmth persists. It rises from below. Somewhere in the deep stone, ancient heat continues to bleed upward, and the cities have learned to live with it, around it, through it.

For there are cities.

The plateau is urban, though not in any way that would have been predicted. The structures here were not built so much as they were grown—or rather, excavated. The original architects (whatever they were, whoever they were) carved downward as much as outward, and the plateau is riddled with inhabited space: tunnels that became halls that became nested chambers, connected by channels that serve as streets. On the surface, only partial structures emerge—archways opening into nothing, walls half-sunk into the hills, towers that might be ventilation or communication or ceremony, the purposes long since forgotten. Pipes run everywhere. Some still carry fluid: a thick, warm liquid that smells of copper and something organic, processed through a hundred stages of filtration before it reaches the surface pools. Others carry heat. Others carry things less nameable. The infrastructure of Aevum is not hidden but displayed—conduits and aqueducts and cable-stalks made visible by the erosion that smoothed everything else, a nervous system exposed by the weathering away of skin.

The industrial light burns constant. The plateau has its own weather: a slow rotation of haze and clarity, pressure systems that build over the course of months and release in slow days of mist. Within this weather, the cities glow. Electric light, yes, but also bioluminescence—cultivated organisms that pulse in the water channels, casting shifting blue-green patterns on the carved stone. The light is not uniform. It clusters where the factories are, dimmers where the population thins, and there are regions—vast regions—where the glow has faded to a bare flicker, maintained but no longer needed, a habit the plateau has not yet broken.

It is loud. The machinery still runs: engines that thrum at frequencies felt more than heard, pumps that push the warm fluid through miles of conduit, fans that cool the deep chambers. The sound layers on itself, a continuous low drone broken by higher tones: the hiss of pressurized release, the rhythmic clank of mechanisms whose purpose has shifted but not stopped. In the tunnels, the sound echoes and multiplies until it becomes a kind of silence, a presence so constant it ceases to register as noise. And beneath it, always, the wet sound of water moving through stone.

The overwhelming presence, though—the factor that shapes everything else—is the fauna.

The plateau is saturated with it. Not large fauna, for the most part. The creatures of Aevum have adapted to the vertical world, the carved spaces, the warm wet channels: things that cling to pipe-walls, things that nest in conduit-joints, things that move through the water itself with bodies like questions. They are not insects, though they fill the same ecological role. They are not fish. They are not anything that evolved in sunlight. They have been here, in this engineered darkness, for so long that they have diverged into a thousand forms, a million, each filling a niche that the original architects never consciously created. They swarm in the surface pools, clouding the warm water until it looks alive. They crawl on the exterior walls of the deep structures, leaving trails that cross and recross in patterns too regular to be random. They fly—not birds, not bats, but membrane-winged things that cluster on lights and flutter in the exhaust vents and sometimes, in the deep hours, rise in murmurations that darken the sky.

The fauna is not dangerous. Or rather, its dangers have been mapped and managed. The creatures of Aevum are adapted to the infrastructure, not its destruction. They are part of the system now—the metabolism of the plateau made visible. And they are everywhere, in numbers beyond counting, a living tide that moves through every space the cities have not explicitly sealed.

The cities themselves? The people?

That is where the strangeness lies—not in the landscape but in its absence. The plateau was built. The infrastructure was designed. The fauna was, at some point, introduced or encouraged or at least permitted. But the intelligence that made these choices is long gone, and in its place: nothing. Aevum runs itself. The factories process. The channels flow. The creatures swarm and nest and die and are reborn. The light cycles through its programmed patterns. The warmth persists. The water weeps through the stone. And no one tends any of it.

Or rather: no one that can be found. There are structures that might be living quarters, long abandoned. There are records that no longer decode. There are murals, faded to abstraction, that might once have depicted faces. The plateau remembers its builders the way stone remembers water—by the shapes it leaves behind. But the builders themselves have moved on, or diminished, or merged with the infrastructure they created, or simply ended.

What remains is vast and warm and wet and alive, a city that became a landscape, a machine that became an ecosystem. The plateau does not mourn. It simply continues.

Above, the thin air holds a faint haze of mineral dust, caught in slow suspension. Below, the warmth rises. The pipes hum. The water seeps. The creatures of Aevum pulse in their endless cycles, patient and numerous, witnesses to nothing, witnesses to everything.

And the stone weeps, and waits, and does not remember what it was before.
