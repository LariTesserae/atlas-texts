# The Processing Plateau

The plateau rises into the thin air like the spine of some half-formed continent—vast beyond easy comprehension, stretching from what might be horizon to horizon under a sky that feels closer than it should. At this elevation the air bites constantly, a cold that never relents, though it rarely deepens into true cruelty. The temperature sits at that knife-edge where water stays liquid only when it must, where the occasional rainfall leaves the ground damp but never soaked, where condensation gathers in the early morning hours and then vanishes before most creatures stir.

The geology here is young. Not young in years—though no one would guess the true age from looking—but young in *feeling*. The valleys cut sharp and steep, V-shaped and unyielding. Ridgelines rise to points that haven't yet been softened by the patient work of weather. Exposed faces of dark stone gleam where ancient rock has been recently torn free, and the soil where it exists at all is thin, raw, a skin stretched tight over bone. Water moves only intermittently—seasonal streams that run briefly in the brief warmth, pockets of snowmelt gathering in hollows before sinking back into the ground or evaporating away. The land drinks when it can and dries again, over and over.

And yet the vegetation is thick. Dense mosses carpet the moister ground, and where the damp collects—in hollows, along those brief stream-ways, in the lee of ridges—sedges and hardy grasses grow in quantities that seem impossible on such thin soil. Scrub willows crowd into the sheltered places, stunted and wind-trained, their forms pressed flat by decades of constant cold wind. Lichens crust the rocks in pale and colored patches, and even the exposed stone seems often to be wearing a skin of something alive. The density of it, the way life has filled every possible niche, speaks to a place that has been *saturated*—not with water but with the simple fact of presence. Life here has been turned to an industrial output, a production that scales across the continent without pause.

Because the fauna does not merely exist here—it *dominates*. The numbers are beyond easy estimation. Migrating herds move across the plateau in waves that take days to pass, dense masses of organisms that churn the thin soil where they walk, their passage a constant low thunder across the land. They come in forms adapted to this specific cold and altitude—forms that have become part of the landscape the way weather is part of it, the way geology is part of it. They do not build, they do not construct—they simply *are*, in such overwhelming quantity that the built environment of their bodies and their movement has become a kind of infrastructure of its own. The density is absolute. There is no space here without presence.

And then there are the machines.

They do not build. They leave nothing behind—no structures, no warrens, no hives—but they *operate*. They move across the plateau in patterns that suggest purpose without revealing it, their operations on a scale that can only be called industrial: engines that process, that harvest, that refine the raw materials of this place into something else. The tech rating demands presence, and the built rating demands its absence, and so the machines exist in a kind of paradox—they transform the world, they reshape it, they operate at massive scale and with industrial efficiency, but they leave no mark that remains once they have passed. The ground is churned and then settles. The vegetation is taken and then regrows. The animals are sorted, processed, their matter redistributed, and the plateau does not remember.

The sound is the sound of industry, layered over the sound of life. The constant hum of engines, the rumble of movement, the chitter and call and cry of fauna present in numbers that make silence impossible. The brightness comes from the sky at this elevation, thin and clear, and from something else—some quality of light that the density of presence refracts and amplifies. Even when clouds come, and they come often here, the world feels luminous, lit from within by the sheer amount of life and motion and mechanism.

The weirdness is subtle. Nothing about this place is alien, exactly. The vegetation is just vegetation, the animals just animals, the machines just machines. And yet the scale, the density, the industrial quality of existence here—it creates a place that feels like it operates under different rules. Life at this scale stops being individual and starts being elemental. The herds are not herds—they are phenomena, geological in their weight, weather in their passage. The machines are not tools—they are processes, as natural as erosion, as patient as growth. Something is being produced here, at continental scale, but what it is and where it goes and why it must be made in such vast quantities without ever accumulating into structure or artifact—this is the question the plateau refuses to answer.

The dynamic is slow. The seasons turn, and the migration patterns shift by degrees over years. The machines complete their circuits over timescales that make tracking them an exercise in patience. Erosion works its patient work, and the vegetation regrows, and the young geology slowly begins to smooth. But the slowness is deceptive—the scale is so vast that even slow change becomes immense. Even patience, at this size, becomes overwhelming.
