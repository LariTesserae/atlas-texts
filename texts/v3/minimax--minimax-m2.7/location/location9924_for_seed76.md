The plateau stretches beyond the horizon in every direction, a continent of stone and silence lifted high into thin, bright air. At this elevation, the atmosphere has grown lean, stripping color from the distant ridges until they appear almost like brushstrokes—violet-gray smudges layered over one another toward the edge of the world. The light here does not fall softly; it arrives with intention, pressing down from a sky so deeply blue it approaches violet, burning bright and cool at once, the way light feels when seen through glass.

No rain falls. The clouds, when they come, drift in from distant lowlands like slow gray refugees, carrying moisture they gather from seas that no longer exist. They arrive as fog rather than storm, as mist rather than rain—a thin, silver veil that settles over the mineral plain each morning and retreats by midday, leaving behind a latticework of dew on every surface. The plants have learned to drink from this: deep root systems tap into aquifers that have not seen a river in geological ages, drawing up ancient water molecule by molecule, while broad waxy leaves and fine silver hairs catch the fog and coax it into droplets that roll down into soil. The vegetation is not dense, but it is present everywhere—a scattered ecology of low, tough growths that carpet the gentler slopes and cling to the edges of arroyos, filling the silence with the slow work of living.

The erosion here is old. The arroyos do not cut sharply; their walls have been worn soft by centuries of thermal expansion and contraction, by the patient friction of wind carrying fine mineral dust. Freeze-thaw cycles have pried apart the stone in some places, leaving talus slopes of pale, angular fragments that shift underfoot with a sound like distant applause. In others, the rock has been carved into forms that seem almost deliberate—arches and pillars and hollowed bowls that the wind has sculpted over epochs. The plateau does not feel dead. It feels *settled*, like something that has finished becoming itself and now simply endures.

At night, the temperature drops sharply, and the silence deepens into something almost solid. The stars here are violent with brightness, the Milky Way a smear of light across the zenith. Occasional sounds emerge: the low groan of stone adjusting to cold, the whisper of a night breeze finding its way between ridges, and rarer still, the cry of something that lives in the deeper arroyos—a creature whose call is less a song than a question, asked once into the dark and unanswered. These animals are few, well adapted to drought and thin air, existing in the interstices of the plateau's larger rhythms. They have learned the fog schedule, the temperature windows, the places where water lingers longest in the soil. They are not numerous, but they are patient.

The plateau has its own weather, though it is subtle. Each day, the sun heats the stone until the air above it begins to rise, drawing in cooler air from the edges in slow, laminar flows. The fog arrives according to this pattern, always from the same direction, always receding by the same hour. Weather fronts, when they pass, do so across the vastness like events in slow motion—wind picking up dust from a far slope, light shifting to amber, the temperature dropping several degrees in an hour, then recovering. The plateau breathes. It has been breathing for so long that the very rock has learned to expand and contract with the rhythm.

What the land lacks in water it compensates in space. The distances here are philosophical—hours of walking to cross what appears to be a single undulation, horizons that reveal themselves to be merely the shoulders of larger ridges. Between each feature lies emptiness, a mineral plain of fine ochre dust or cracked white salt-crusted clay, and yet even in this emptiness, life persists. The plants grow low and wide here, forming mats that trap moisture and sediment, slowly building soil where there was none. Given enough time, the plateau might grow dense with green. But time here moves differently. The landscape operates on scales that render individual lives almost invisible, processes so slow that even the erosion—the patient, constant erosion—appears frozen. And yet it is not. It is only that the plateau has learned to move at its own pace, to exist in its own tempo, bright and cool and vast and quiet, a world still becoming.
