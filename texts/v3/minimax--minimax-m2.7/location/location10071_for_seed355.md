# The Weeping City

The ruins remember what built them, even if nothing else does.

You know this was urban once — the scale of it demands that recognition. Foundations stretching beyond any single glance, the deliberate geometry of footings and platforms laid out in a pattern too regular for nature. Streets, perhaps, or canals, or the flattened beds where roads once made order of the earth. But the stone speaks a language no longer spoken: vesicular, porous, as though the rock itself exhaled when it formed. Basalt, you might call it, though calling it anything feels like a promise you're not sure you can keep.

The heat rises from below. Not from the sky — the sky is a black ceiling, or perhaps simply absent, and the darkness here is not the darkness of night but the darkness of depth, of enclosure, of a place that exists beneath the concept of sky. What little illumination reaches this place moves strangely, pooling in some areas and failing to cross others, as though light has forgotten how to travel straight. But the warmth! The warmth is insistent, geological, coming up through the cracked ground in slow tidal pulses. It mingles with the water — and the water is everywhere here, not falling but *emerging*. Condensing on every surface, beading on stone, gathering in the low places where centuries of patient settling have created shallow basins. The stones weep. The walls glisten. In the deeper channels where the streets once ran, dark water stands stagnant and hot, and the air above it moves in slow convective spirals, carrying moisture that will never fall.

The erosion here is ancient — not the dramatic erosion of cliffsides and canyons, but the settled erosion of long existence. The kind that wears edges round over centuries, that polishes stone to something almost like skin. The buildings (or what remains of them) have not collapsed so much as surrendered, their walls spreading outward at the base like something tired finally allowing itself to rest. They are built of material that holds warmth and shrugs off the constant damp: dense, dark, formed by fire once, cooled slowly in the grip of this subterranean heat. No mortar. The pieces fit together with the precision of controlled fracture, interlocking without glue, which means the whole thing holds by friction and geometry alone — and holds still, after however long.

Vegetation exists here, but barely. Occasional patches of something leathery and grey-green, growing in the cracks where dust has gathered. Xeric adaptations on plants that never expected to live in a place this wet. They don't photosynthesize so much as persist — slow, stunted, drinking from the air itself. The spaces between everything are vast. This is a city of absence as much as presence: empty doorways leading to rooms that lead to nothing, collapsed roof-beams creating cave-like shelters that no one enters, open plazas where the wind (and there is wind, slow and hot) moves through without obstruction. The density is low despite the scale. Many structures, but most are half-ghosts, the suggestion of walls rather than walls themselves.

The silence is not complete. There are sounds — water trickling through hidden channels, stone settling in its slow collapse, the occasional groan of thermal expansion as the earth breathes its buried heat. But the fauna parameter holds: no animal life. No insects. No birdsong or rodent rustle. The silence is a held breath that has been held so long it has forgotten why.

This is where the parameter *weirdness* becomes the ground you're standing on.

The streets do not quite run where they should. You have walked what felt like a straight line, and now you are somewhere you have been before, though the landmarks have changed — the doorway that was on your left is now ahead, or perhaps there is no doorway at all. The water moves uphill here. You watched it: a thin black thread of condensation sliding up a wall, pooling in a depression that should have drained by gravity but instead rises, slowly, as though filling from beneath. Time does not feel continuous. You have been walking for an hour; you have been walking for three minutes. Both feel equally true, and neither is quite correct.

And the buildings — the buildings are occupied, though not by anything you can see.

You feel them. The pressure of presence in empty rooms. The way your attention slides away from certain doorways, not from fear but from disinterest, as though your mind has decided these paths are unremarkable and therefore not worth noticing. When you turn your head quickly, you catch afterimages: something that might have been a window, something that might have been a face in profile, something that might have been watching from an angle that no architecture should allow. The city is full of watchers who exist just outside the frame of direct observation. They are patient. They have been here since the stone was hot.

The built environment is urban: organized, deliberate, scaled for hundreds or thousands. But it was built without your kind of technology — stone and heat and water and time, nothing more. Perhaps a civilization that understood water differently, that knew how to ask condensing atmosphere to bear weight, to create foundations that seep and sweat and endure. Perhaps the buildings grew more than they were built. Perhaps this is not a ruin at all, but a growth.

The hot water near the surface has created something that is not quite marsh, not quite city, not quite cave. It is a place in the process of becoming something else — or in the process of remembering what it used to be, before the distinction between remembering and becoming collapsed entirely.

You do not know how long you have been here. You do not know if you are still arriving.
