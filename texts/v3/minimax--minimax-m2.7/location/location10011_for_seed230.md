# The Town of Still Water

High on a plateau of ancient stone, where the land rises beyond the river valleys below and the air thins to something clean and sharp, the town sits in its own permanence. The elevation here is not dramatic—no peaks or sudden cliffs—but a slow climb from the lowlands that brings you into a different climate, a place where moisture gathers in the morning hours and the stone never fully dries.

---

The stone is everything. Not dramatic cliffs or boulder fields, but the quiet geology of a plateau worn smooth over centuries. The streets are paved with it. The walls are built from it. The terraces that step down the slopes are held by it. Weather has rounded the edges, left the surfaces fine and gray, and where foot traffic has worn channels into the pavements, those channels catch the moisture that trickles down from the heights above. Water here does not rush or flood; it seeps. It beads in the morning and gathers in low places, in wheel-ruts, in the corners where walls meet foundations. A permanent dampness lives in the joints between stones, in the shaded alleys that never see full sun. You feel it on your skin, a cool mineral wetness that never quite dries.

Sparseness defines the landscape as much as the stone. Sparse grass pushes up through the cracks in the outer walls. Sparse lichen marks the north faces of buildings in pale green and gray. Sparse scrub clings to the terraces—hardy plants with small leaves that know how to hold what moisture falls. The vegetation is not abundant enough to soften the place, not barren enough to feel waste. It exists in the margins, in the spaces between. Along the drainage channels that run down the center of many streets, thin mosses and creeping plants follow the water's path, the only green trails in a town of gray.

The temperature never quite rises to warmth. Even at midday, the air carries a coolness that makes you aware of breathing. When the sun sets—and it sets quickly here, the plateau's elevation stealing an hour of light in the evening—the chill settles in and stays. The stone holds the cold. The buildings, built thick for insulation, hold it too. Fires are lit in hearths and braziers, smoke curling from chimneys, and in the evenings the town glows warm against the dusk. But the default is cool. The damp and the cool are companions here, the one feeding the other.

---

The town is organized. This is evident in the way the streets align, the way the buildings cluster, the way the drainage channels converge toward a central point. It is urban in its planning—if not in scale. A fortified wall circles the settlement, rough stone mortared tight, patched here and there where time has opened gaps. Iron gates stand at the cardinal points, heavy and creaking, and beyond them the roads continue toward other places you cannot see. Inside the wall, the town is dense enough to feel inhabited but sparse enough that you can see the sky between rooftops, can follow the line of stone walls up the slopes to the fields and terraces above.

Each quarter has its logic. Near the southern gate, where the road from the lowlands arrives, the smithy and the tanner work where the wind carries their smoke and smell away from the center. Near the well—the deep well at the heart of the town, stone-ringed and ancient—the houses crowd closer, the streets narrow, the walls higher. The market square opens where three streets meet, a wide space of paving stones where awnings are strung in the summer months and fires are lit in the winter. A small temple of plain stone stands at the square's edge, unadorned, its door always open. A bell tower rises from the temple roof; the bell is rung at dawn, at midday, at dusk, its voice carrying clear and true across the plateau.

The construction is simple. Iron nails, wooden beams, stone blocks fitted and mortared. The technology here is handcraft—the hammer, the chisel, the loom, the spindle. You could count the tools in the smithy on two hands. The buildings are not elaborate; they are made to shelter and endure. But endurance is what they have achieved. The oldest walls were laid centuries ago, the stone smoothed by rain and wind, and on those walls you can see the history of the place in the different joints, the repairs, the new construction built against the old. The town has grown slowly, carefully, each generation adding to what the last left behind. There is no single style; there is only stone, fitted to stone, generation after generation, until the town became the thing it is.

---

At this scale—small, hamlet-sized, bounded by walls—a person can walk from one edge to the other in an hour. You can stand in the market square and see both the gate and the temple. You can hear a voice from one end of the town to the other, when the wind is right. The town is a room as much as a place, a space defined by walls and streets and the ordering of stone. When you are inside it, you know where you are. When you leave, the plateau opens around you, vast and sparse, and the town becomes a mark on the landscape, a smudge of gray against the gray-brown earth.

The silence here is not emptiness but equilibrium. The default state is quiet. When you stop walking, when the conversation pauses, you hear the wind moving over the walls, a low constant murmur. You hear the drip of water from a ledge, the catch of a breath. You hear, occasionally, the bell, its voice hanging in the air until it fades. But there is no noise—no clamor, no rush, no din. The sounds are spaced. The silences are full. The town breathes.

Fauna is rare in the way that implies domestication and adaptation, not absence. Birds pass overhead, their calls thin and high against the sky. Goats are kept, tethered on the terraces where the scrub grows. A dog barks sometimes near the gates, a cat watches from a windowsill. The creatures that live here have learned the town's rhythms, the timing of the gates and the bell, the routes of the streets. They belong to it as much as the stones.

---

What strikes you most, if you stay long enough to notice, is the stillness. The town does not change. Not in the way of decay or growth or ruin, but in the way of completion. The stone that cracked in the last century's freeze remains cracked unless someone chooses to mend it. The fountain in the square has not run since before your grandparents' time; its basin is dry, its iron grate rusted, and no one has built a new one. The collapsed shed in the eastern quarter has lain open to the sky for as long as anyone remembers, its timbers rotting in the damp, and still no one clears it away. These are not failures of maintenance. They are the marks of a place that has decided what it is, and has stopped. The town is a fixed point. It holds itself in balance against time.

You could leave and return in a year and find nothing altered. The water still seeps in the joints. The lichen still spreads a little wider on the north walls. The bell still rings at dawn, at midday, at dusk. The cool still lives in the air. The town waits at the edge of the plateau, damp and sparse and permanent, a small human arrangement held steady against the slow wearing of the world.
