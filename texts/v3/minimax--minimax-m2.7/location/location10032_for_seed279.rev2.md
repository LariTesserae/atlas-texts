The basin exhales.

Not literally — or perhaps literally, if one stayed long enough to learn its rhythms — but the effect is the same. Water rises through the fractured dark stone in a slow, persistent seep, emerging from countless small fissures across the basin floor and lower slopes like the ground itself is weeping. The surfaces stay damp. Boots leave dark prints that fade slowly, absorbed back into the stone.

This is a young landscape, geologically speaking. The rock still holds its sharp edges — angular ridges, fresh fracture faces, crevices that haven't yet softened under the patient work of water and time. The basin itself might be a collapsed caldera, or a section of crust that subsided in some ancient disruption, or simply a place where the water table runs high and erosion has found easy purchase in whatever broke here first. The floor extends outward for miles, rolling gently between low hills, punctuated by clusters of dark stone formations that rise like seltzer frozen mid-burst — sharp, branching, dense with mineral complexity.

There are no plants. The damp has not invited green things; the stone offers no purchase for roots; the water carries something in its mineral load that discourages or forbids the familiar forms of life. Instead, there are other presences. Thin, pale films spread across wet stone surfaces, their edges creeping outward over days or weeks — barely organisms, barely alive, but responsive, changing. In hollows where water collects and evaporates in slow cycles, soft white growths appear: fungal in character but stranger, without caps or stems, just smooth, yielding masses that dissolve when touched. Occasional forms cling to vertical faces — small, slow, translucent, their movements imperceptible until noticed, gone before understood. Fauna is rare, but present: you might see one, or think you do, in the corner of the eye.

The structures are scattered and worn. Low walls of fitted stone, half-buried in silt and mineral deposit. Platforms of cracked basalt, their surfaces etched by water until the edges blur. A single archway, perfectly round, standing alone on a ridge where nothing else remains — nothing to show who built it, or why, or how long the arch has endured while its builders have not. These are not ruins so much as remnants, worn to the point of mystery, their purposes dissolved along with their details.

Sound fills the basin. Water moving through stone makes a sound like no single source — everywhere and nowhere, a constant wet murmur that echoes off the sharp slopes and carries in the heavy air. Dripping. Trickling. The soft roar of thin water running over mineral-encrusted surfaces. In the morning, when the light is dimmest and the moisture hangs thick, the sound seems to amplify, to acquire a resonance, as if the basin were shaped to hold and amplify it. The old structures, hollow and worn, catch and redistribute the noise in subtle ways that feel — not intentional, not malevolent, but not entirely natural either.

The light is gray and even, diffused through perpetual thin clouds and the moisture suspended in the air itself. The basin's low elevation holds the mist close; the high water table feeds it upward; the cool temperature keeps it from burning off. Everything is silver and shadow. The dark stone drinks the light; the water reflects it softly; the pale growths glow faintly in the damp. The scale is open — the far walls are visible but distant, the basin a world unto itself, large enough that the scattered structures could each be miles from the next, unreachable by evening if one started now.

What holds the landscape together is density: the accumulation of mineral deposit on every surface, building up slowly in pale crusts that catch the light and give the stone a kind of bloom. The water does not merely wet; it salts. The stone weeps and leaves traces. The air carries moisture and mineral smell, iron and something older, something that might be the basin remembering what it was before the water rose. The formations accumulate. The pale growths spread. The structures sink deeper each century, their stones loosened by frost and seep, their purposes effaced. The water keeps moving, keeps rising, keeps wearing at the sharp young edges until — in a million years? ten million? — the basin will be smooth and soft, and the structures will be gone entirely, and even the formations will have rounded into something patient and old.

But not yet.

Now the basin is dense with itself. The water seeps. The pale films creep. The structures stand, barely. The sound fills every hollow and ridge, the sound of a wet world doing what wet worlds do: moving slowly, always slowly, wearing and depositing, weeping and forgetting, building itself up even as it washes away.
