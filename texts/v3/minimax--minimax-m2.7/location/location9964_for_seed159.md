# The Hollow Below

The chamber does not end. That is the first thing you understand, and the last thing you stop learning.

It stretches in every direction with the imprecision of something still becoming itself — walls that rise and then curve back into blackness above, floors that slope without destination, ceilings that breathe with moisture and open into galleries no eye has measured. The space is not a cavern so much as a rumor of one: raw limestone veined with copper and iron oxide, walls sweating in the warmth, surfaces still carrying the memory of formation — striations and flow-lines and the ghost-geometry of water that cut yesterday, or a thousand years ago, or an hour before you descended. Erosion has barely begun its work here. The stone is eager and unfinished, soft in places where mineral crusts haven't yet hardened, sharp in others where something tore through and never healed. Your fingers come away orange-dusted.

You are below the surface. The parameter almost doesn't matter — this place has been excavated by something, or is excavating itself, and the ceiling above you runs thick and uneven, breached in stretches by veins of secondary mineral that glow: not sunlight, not quite, but a pale warm luminescence that bleeds through the stone in fault-lines, giving the underground a dusk-like quality, almost habitable, almost familiar. Light without source. Light that seems to remember the outside even if the outside has forgotten this place.

Water comes in pockets. Not flowing rivers but seepages and still pools that appear in hollows, connected by nothing, as though the moisture itself is lost and wandering. Some pools sit in basins carved by condensation and gravity, edges crusted with white mineral deposit. Others gleam in the joints of stone where two formations meet and a seam holds moisture like a secret. Here, in this stretch, the walls are slick and close and the air tastes of wet calcium. Ten meters on, it dries again — cracked and powdery, a desert compressed into a corridor. The dampness is a wanderer here. It has not decided where to live.

Nothing grows. But the word *nothing* is imprecise.

Where plant-life might be, there is instead a kind of mineral accretion — pale threads that cling to rock faces in patterns too deliberate to be geological, too sparse to be intentional. Fungal in character but not quite fungal. They branch. They cluster at moisture-sources and go brittle in the dry stretches. They do not respond to your presence but they do seem to have responded to something's — tunnels of them trace walls that would otherwise be featureless, as though some ancient creature once moved along these routes and the accretion grew in its wake, patient and structural, a fossil-record made ofite mineral rather than bone. You step over them. They crunch like dead coral.

The warmth is constant and unvarying. Not the warmth of fire or depth — there is no geothermal signature in the stone's temperature — but something ambient, pervasive, a background hum of heat that makes the air feel close and thick. Your clothes cling. The sweat comes and stays. This is not an uncomfortable warmth; it is simply *there*, the way silence is there in other underground places. You stop noticing it only because everything else demands notice.

Because there is so much of it.

The fauna does not merely occupy this space — it saturates it. Movement everywhere, at every scale, in every material. Small things: arthropods the length of your thumb with iridescent carapaces that catch the mineral light, moving in lines that look purposeful but aren't, or are, or are something between. Insects with too many legs. Worms the color of wet stone pushing through sediment at the base of walls. Clicking things. Skittering things. Things that move so quickly the eye loses them. You hear them before you see them: a constant percussion of small movements layered over each other like rain on a tin roof, punctuated by sharper sounds — the snap of mandibles, the scrape of carapace against stone, a high keening note from somewhere deep in the structure that rises and falls with no pattern you can identify.

Larger presences too, though you do not see them directly. Shadows that move wrong against the light. Bulk in the corner of your vision that resolves into nothing when you turn. A low rhythmic sound — breath or tide or something tectonic — that recurs at intervals of thirty seconds or so, coming from below, or behind, or from the walls themselves. The density is the thing. Every surface has a tenant. Every seam, every projection, every flat face hosts something alive or recently alive or made by something that was. The walls are layered with it. You cannot find a square meter of stone that is purely stone.

And it changes. The landscape is not static — it *heaves*. Populations shift. A cluster of the iridescent arthropods that occupied a low-ceilinged alcove when you passed it has dispersed by the time you circle back, leaving only the faint iridescent smear of residue on the rock. Something moves through a pool and leaves it still and dark. Sounds build and release in waves, as though the fauna is engaged in some collective behavior just beyond your comprehension — a migration, a breeding event, an alarm system, a language. The turbulence is constant. You are inside something alive and chaotic and enormous.

The scattered structures add a layer of wrongness to this.

They are small — single blocks of shaped stone, or partial walls, or stone rings set into the floor that suggest deliberate placement but no apparent purpose. One: a platform raised three courses above the floor, its surface worn smooth in a way stone does not wear naturally, the edges chamfered. Another: a set of channels cut into a wall at varying angles, deep enough to admit a finger, spaced with a regularity that implies measurement. A third: a cluster of flat stones arranged in a rough spiral on the floor, each one the size of a fist, arranged by something with hands or a hand-equivalent. They are primitive. The shaping is rough — early-tool, manual-craft, a human or human-adjacent intelligence working stone with hammer-stones and patience. But no tool-marks remain. The surfaces are too worn. Whatever was here finished its work long ago and left nothing behind but the geometries.

You find a small object in a seam between two boulders. A carved stone, thumb-sized, with a hole through it. A bead, or a pendant, or a connector. Primitive. Warm from the ambient heat. You pocket it without thinking and then think about it for a long time.

Sound: the layered clicking and skittering, the low rhythmic pulse from below, the high keening note, the drip and trickle of water in the damp stretches, the creak of stone adjusting to pressure somewhere in the structure's depth, the echo of your own movement in a space too large to hold your sound. Loud but not deafening — layered, like a room full of conversations where no conversation is audible and the aggregate is a roar that never breaks into words.

The light diffuses. The warmth endures. The fauna churns. The water hides in its pockets. The stone goes on, unfinished, not yet carved into what it will be, and you cross it with your primitive bead in your pocket and your sweat-stiff clothes and your inability to see the ceiling or the far wall or the bottom of what you have descended into, and you understand that this place is not waiting for you.

It is not waiting for anything.

It is simply, turbulently, in the middle of becoming.
