The hollow sits low in the terrain, barely a depression but enough to gather what falls from the air. Rain does not sheet through here—it settles, condenses, soaks into everything and stays. The ground is not soil but mineral: pale stone veined with darker streaks, compact and dense, where weak places have worn away and left shallow pools no deeper than a hand's breadth. The water in them does not look still; it is always moving slowly, drawn upward by warmth, sinking back, cycling in place without ever leaving.

There is no life here. There may never have been, or it has been gone so long that only the absence remains—a mineral world where the rock has always been rock, wet but never softened by rot, never broken down into something that could grow. Occasionally you might find the ghost of it: a dark stain on a stone, a threadlike marking in the sediment that was once root or fiber, now completely integrated back into the mineral. There is nothing to feed decay. The dampness and warmth that should invite breakdown simply stop at the surface of things.

The built remains are scattered across the hollow like objects left out in weather. A short stretch of wall, its stones fitted with care but now separated by wide cracks. Two pillars standing close together, their surfaces worn smooth, one canted slightly as if tired. Flagstones half-buried in debris, their pattern just visible beneath the silt the water has redeposited. An archway standing alone, its stones shaped and placed with evident intention, but no structure remains to give it purpose—just the arch, open to the gray sky, framing a view of more wet stone and more silence. In one place, small flat stones have been laid deliberately in a ring, surrounding a darker patch of ground. In another, a basin carved from a single block of stone rests half-buried, its interior smooth, collecting water that never evaporates.

The light is dim because the humidity is always high, a fine mist that diffuses what little comes through. The sky above is a pale, even gray—not overcast so much as perpetual, never brightening, never darkening. Everything here has the same quality: settled, damp, stable. Erosion has done its work over a very long time, smoothing angles, rounding stone edges, wearing everything down to what it is now. But that work stopped long ago, when there was nothing left to erode. The fragments lie in place as if held by the damp itself, too heavy to shift, too settled to move.

A beetle might cross the basin occasionally, small and patient, finding nothing alive to eat. A worm, rarely, in the deepest pools where water pools longest. The creatures here are wanderers from outside, passing through or lingering past their time, leaving no mark on the place. The silence is nearly complete: no wind, no birds, no insect hum. Occasionally, if you are still, you might hear the faint sound of water moving through stone—a trickle somewhere beneath the surface, finding its way to a lower pool. Or a drop falling from the overhang of the arch, landing in still water with a sound like a single breath.

This is a place of stasis. Everything that could change has already changed, and what remains is only the residue of change: mineral, water, and the faint marks of hands that shaped stone long ago. The warmth and wetness persist, but they no longer do anything. They simply are, held in balance, like the light, like the silence, like the fragments that no longer belong to anything but themselves.
