Water rules here. It moves through the stone in every form — sheet-flow down limestone faces, pooling in shallow basins carved by centuries of patient seepage, dripping from ceilings in irregular percussion. The rock weeps. Moisture condenses on every surface and then gravity takes it, sending it downward through channels still being etched. The stone itself is young in geological terms, raw and uncompleted — edges still crisp where water has recently carved, formations not yet hardened into their final shapes. It feels like watching a world arrive, like being present at the beginning of a canyon.

The basin is vast. Walls recede into dim distance, their surfaces colonized by moisture and the faintest trace of green — mosses hugging crevices, ferns sprouting from ledges where water gathers, the occasional waxy leaf catching what little light exists. Vegetation is sparse but purposeful, each plant a deliberate statement against the wet stone. Nothing grows carelessly here; the conditions are too extreme for abundance.

Warmth saturates the air. It rises from the water itself, from the dense populations of organisms generating metabolic heat, from the compressed atmosphere of a below-surface space where cold has no access. The warmth feels constant, patient, unchanging across what might be generations. There is no seasonal rhythm here. There is only continuation.

The built remnants tell of occupation without revealing its purpose. Rusted metal frames protrude from silt deposits — the skeleton of something that was once mechanical, perhaps pumping or filtering or redirecting the endless water. Concrete platforms, cracked and stained, rise from the basin floor at irregular intervals like the stumps of structures long since consumed. A chain of dim bulbs follows what might have been a walkway once, their filaments still glowing after years of darkness, though the walkway itself has been claimed by water and algae. These fragments do not speak of a civilization so much as an interruption — a brief industrial parenthesis in a place that existed long before and will persist long after.

Fauna fills every available niche with urgent, competing life. Fish with translucent flesh move in schools near the surface, their bodies catching what light exists. Crawling things — crustaceans with too many limbs, things that might be insects or might be something else entirely — crowd the waterline where stone meets flow. Sounds rise from all directions: the roar of a waterfall in the distance, the constant percussion of drip and trickle, the faint grinding of water working stone, the layered calls of creatures that have never seen the sky claiming territory in the acoustic dark. The sound is a wall, a presence as solid as water.

Everything is slow here. The water cuts a millimeter per century. Sediment accumulates grain by grain. The populations adjust their balances through generations too long for any individual to perceive. The basin itself may be growing, may be spreading its influence, or may be slowly choking on its own deposits — the process is too gradual to know. What is certain is that this place is not finished. It is still becoming.
