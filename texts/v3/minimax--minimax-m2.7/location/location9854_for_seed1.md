The flats stretch in every direction under a sky the color of old pewter, and every surface weeps. Not with grief — with condensation. Tiny rivulets trace down every exposed face of mineral, pooling in the creases and seams of a surface so ancient it has forgotten what it once held aloft. The water here comes from deep: thermal vents scattered across the lowland exhale superheated moisture that cools and settles and never quite evaporates, pooling in the creases of stone that has been flattened over eons into something like skin.

There are no plants. This is a mineral world — but mineral in the way a forest is full of trees: everywhere, filling every possible niche, expressing itself in formations that rise and spread and compete for space. Crystal growths in pale blues and rust oranges. Glassy masses that catch the dim light and throw it sideways. Nodules and columns and sheets of something betweenite, something silicate, something that gleams wetly and hums at frequencies just below hearing. The formations are packed so tight there is no bare ground — no gap between one presence and the next. The whole surface is a single continuous carpet of mineral expression, eroded by millennia of condensing vapor into something almost soft, almost breathing.

It is cold. Not freezing — the water would freeze — but cold enough that your breath fogs and your joints ache and the moisture on every surface forms tiny prisms that wink at the edges of vision. The temperature has been cold here since long before anything could feel it.

And yet: abundant fauna. They move through the crystal fields in ways that suggest appetite and purpose. Shelled things that click across the glass-smooth surfaces. Cloud-like masses of something that trails vapor and catches light wrong — their bodies lit from within by bioluminescence, or chemiluminescence, or something that isn't either. They cluster around the vents, around the wet places, around each other. There are predators. There are things being hunted. The ecosystem is thick and loud — clicking, humming, a constant low thrum of processes that have nothing to do with plants or photosynthesis or anything green and growing.

Because there is no green here. There never was.

The industrial presence is present in absence: the formations are too regular in places. The crystal fields show seams and patterns that aren't quite natural, or aren't natural anymore, or aren't natural in ways that are impossible to determine. Something was done here once, or is always being done, deep below — mechanized processes that churn and separate and deposit, leaving behind this mineral aftermath. The dim light has a flicker to it, an electrical quality. The hum has a frequency that feels determined rather than emergent.

And underneath all of it: the weirdness. The flats that seem to extend endlessly but somehow loop. The shadows that fall in more directions than the light sources account for. The creature you saw thirty minutes ago crossing your path again from a different angle, larger this time, or smaller, or the same size but the legs are in a different order. The way the water on the surface forms patterns that almost — almost — resolve into symbols, then dissolve. The way certain formations seem to be listening. The way you have the persistent sense that the ground beneath you is not bedrock but something that remembers being something else, and the memory is slowly returning.

It is loud here — not with wind, but with life. The clicking and humming fills the space between the crystal growths, bounces off the wet stone faces, returns from directions that don't correspond to the formations you can see. Something is always happening. The vents exhale in cycles. The creatures hunt and flee and converge. The mineral surface sweats and the moisture runs downhill in channels so ancient they've become permanent features, arterial routes for the ecosystem's blood.

You are being watched by things that don't have eyes — or have too many, arranged in patterns that eyes should not be. You are walking on a landscape that remembers what it displaced. You are breathing air that tastes of metal and thermal dissolution and something sweet underneath, something like sugar but not, something that makes your teeth ache.

The wet mineral world does not want you here. It does not object, exactly. It is simply too full of itself to make room, and every step you take is a negotiation with a surface that has already decided what occupies every square centimeter of space.

You are being slowly absorbed.
