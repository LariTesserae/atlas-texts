The city climbs the mountain like a living thing, pressing itself into every available surface, hanging from cliff faces, bridging chasms, stacking itself in vertical layers that disappear upward into low-hanging cloud. Rain arrives almost daily—sometimes mist, sometimes downpour—and the built environment has long since learned to catch it, channel it, drink it. Water runs through pipes and open channels both, traces mineral lines down facades, pools in courtyards and cisterns, feeds the plants that grow from every joint and seam.

Vegetation is everywhere. Not wild—nothing here is truly wild anymore—but not merely ornamental either. The city planted green into its foundations generations ago, and now it grows through them. Vines climb the iron frameworks of aqueducts. Moss thickens on cobblestones that haven't been cleared in decades. Trees push up through grated plazas, their roots displacing stone, their canopies spreading across rooftops where people once walked. The green is dense, layered, a second city superimposed on the first, so packed with life that the eye cannot settle.

The air is cool and damp and carries the smell of rust and wet stone and something faintly chemical—oil or smoke, present but not dominant. Light is muted. The clouds hang close, and the dense construction filters what reaches street level into something dim and even. Lanterns burn in some quarters, their glow pooling in fog. In others, people move in relative darkness, the shapes of machines and buildings the only guide. The city does not feel unsafe in this dimness; it feels established, a place that knows its own dark.

Everything is built. There is no ground that is not pavement, no wall that is not mortar and stone or poured concrete. The substrate is entirely made. Foundations go deep. Where natural rock remains exposed, it has been carved, notched, fitted with infrastructure. The city has consumed the mountain so completely that what remains of the original terrain is architectural—cliffs repurposed as walls, crevices bridged or channeled. This was not built quickly. The wear is visible: edges rounded by water, surfaces stained and streaked, joints loosened by frost cycles that still come with winter. The place has been in its current state long enough for weathering to become part of its character.

The industry is heavy and constant. Engines drive pumps that move water through the city's veins. Factories occupy the lower quarters where weight and smoke don't matter. The sound is not loud—there are no sudden clangs or explosions—but it is perpetual: a low mechanical murmur, the hiss of steam vents, the slosh and trickle of water in motion. Machines run. They have always run. At this scale, with this density of construction, there is no moment of silence because there is no space where sound can dissipate; it is absorbed by walls, reflected by stone, filtered through water and vegetation until it becomes ambient, part of the city's breathing.

Fauna is rare. Some birds nest in the higher towers. Rats in the lower channels. The city's appetite for space has left little room for them. What fills the density instead is the built environment itself and the vegetation that accompanies it—organisms, but not animal, not mobile, not behaving. The packed presence is stone and metal and plant.

And yet there is turbulence here. Not chaos exactly—the city is old enough that its movements have patterns—but energy, restlessness, the sense that nothing is ever still. Water flows. Machines turn. Steam vents. People move through streets on errands that never end. The weather changes. The light shifts. The plants grow. The city does not rest. It exists in a state of continuous operation, a system so large and so complex that its motion is simply what it is to be here. Stability is a false word for this place; balance is more accurate—the balance of something that has been working so long it has forgotten how to stop.
