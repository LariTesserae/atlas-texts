# The Condensation Gardens

A basin in the clouds. The walls rise sharply on all sides—pale granite and obsidianite, still showing their birth lines, the flow textures of rock that has not yet learned to be old. This is not erosion as ruin; this is erosion as surgery, as the land still bleeding edge from whatever upthrust or eruption raised it here, above the rainline yet perpetually wet.

Water does not fall here so much as *arrive*. Springs weep from seams in the cliff walls, thin threads of moisture that catch on every surface and gather into the air itself. The humidity sits at that threshold where it becomes atmosphere—breathable, drinkable, a warmth against the skin that is not temperature alone but the presence of water in vapor and droplet, condensing on skin, on stone, on the broad dark leaves of the tree ferns that crowd every ledge and fault. They grow in clusters, in overlapping tiers, their fronds pressing together overhead until the basin becomes a ceiling of green. Beneath them, mosses have claimed every horizontal surface: the rocks wear soft pelts of emerald and chartreuse, and when water moves through them—and it is always moving, trickling and dripping and seeping—the sound is continuous, a murmur at the edge of hearing that never ceases.

The scale is intimate. One could stand in the basin's center and speak, and the sound would not carry far before the walls returned it, dampened and softened. The tree ferns do not tower; they crowd at shoulder-height and eye-level, a dense growth that forces movement through channels, through narrow passages between trunks and through curtains of hanging fronds. The effect is of traveling through a space that resists you, that has its own geometry and will.

Here and there the growth parts around structures. Small ones. A waterwheel set into a channel where a spring exits the rock, its paddles thick with moss but still turning, slow and heavy, driving a mechanism whose purpose is not immediately obvious—a series of brass pipes that vanish into the ground, or a belt that connects to a shrouded box. Elsewhere, a building: one room, walls of dark stone fitted without mortar, roof of corroded metal that sweats and drips, its interior an unlovely tangle of pipes and gauges. These are not ruins—the machinery still functions, the structures still shelter something—but they are not maintained either. They exist in a state of stubborn continuation, artifacts of a purpose no longer remembered or never fully understood.

The sound, beneath the murmur of water, is mechanical: the creak and thunk of the wheel, a low electrical hum from somewhere deeper in the rock, the drip and splash of overflow. Loud enough to notice, to fill the small space, but not aggressively so—more like a heartbeat, a slow pulse that suggests life without demanding attention.

Fauna is rare but present. Something moves in the upper fronds, too quick to see—scale-feet or feathered, impossible to say. Occasionally, at the basin's edge where the cliff walls are wettest, dark shapes press against the rock, breathing. Gastropods the size of hands, their shells iridescent with mineral deposits. They do not flee; they do not acknowledge. They are simply there, another presence in the dense layering of this place.

The light is ordinary—diffuse, white, the kind of light that comes from no particular direction because the sky above is uniform, because the moisture scatters and softens everything. At midday the shadows are faint; at other hours they are absent altogether. The darkness comes not from shade but from the density of the growth itself, from the layers of frond and leaf that filter and diminish.

What is strange about this place is not its features but their arrangement. The warmth should not exist at this elevation; the wetness should not persist without rain; the machinery should not function without tending. Yet here they are, coexisting, each feeding the others—the springs feed the ferns, the ferns hold the moisture, the moisture feeds the machinery that does something to the springs. The loop is closed. The basin breathes.

There are no visitors here. There is no path down into it, no road or trail. The structures were built by people, but the people are gone or absent, and in their absence the place has continued without them, finding its own equilibrium. The tree ferns have grown over the waterwheel's frame. The pipes have mineralized at their joints. The building's door hangs open on hinges that no longer move. But the wheel still turns. The water still weeps. The air still holds its weight of moisture, pressing close, and the small, bounded space maintains its slow, patient breath.
