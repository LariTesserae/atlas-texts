The plateau has no name, or has too many names spoken in tongues that left no speakers behind.

It rises above the possibility of clouds, a table of stone nine thousand meters into air too thin to be called wind. The rock beneath your feet is dark, almost black, veined with something that glimmers not like quartz but like the memory of glass—surfaces that haven't finished cooling, geometries that almost hold their shape. Where you step, the stone gives slightly, not like sand but like something that hasn't decided yet whether to be solid. Here and there, fissures open without warning: not deep, but dark in a way that darkness isn't supposed to be, a darkness that seems to go inward rather than simply away from light. You step around them. You step carefully. The ground is pre-geology, a first draft of rock, and it resents the pressure of your weight with a faint, almost inaudible groan that comes not from the surface but from somewhere beneath it, from the place where heat and pressure and time are still arguing about what this mountain will eventually become.

Among this raw stone, the gardens grow.

They are not impossible—they are here, after all, growing—but they are not comprehensible. Pale trunks rise in clusters and corridors, bark the color of bone, branching in patterns that don't follow any arboreal logic the eye can follow. The canopy is dense in patches, open in others, and the leaves are not green but something between silver and the pale amber of old paper, thin as skin, moving in slow currents of air that you cannot feel on your skin. They reach toward the light. They have no roots you can see. Where they stand, the dark stone beneath them is not covered so much as *incorporated*—the boundary between mineral and organic is a blur, a gradual handshake, as if the plants are drawing their sustenance not from water but from the stone and the strange, thin light and the barely-existent atmosphere itself, from some chemistry that doesn't require the thing you would otherwise call wetness. They grow. They die. The dead ones don't fall so much as flatten, become part of the ground, a pale and papery mat that crunches underfoot with a sound like dry insects, like paper burning at the edges. Beneath the living trees, the dead ones carpet everything, thousands of seasons of growth and decay in a place where nothing digests, nothing decays properly, nothing recycles. The garden is lush. The garden is sterile. It grows into itself and dies into itself and no one tends it, no one planted it, no one will clear the old growth or prune the new. It is a garden that has forgotten it was ever planted.

There is no water. None. You understand this the way you understand the weight of silence—the absence is total, structural, a fundamental parameter of this place. Your mouth is dry. Your lips crack if you舔 them. The air is not humid. There are no streams, no pools, no moisture beaded on stone. The vegetation has simply opted out of the arrangement that the rest of the living world maintains with rivers and rain. What it drinks instead, you cannot say. Perhaps it doesn't drink. Perhaps it *is* the drinking, the act of consumption without the need for anything to be consumed.

There are no animals. Not the absence of animals is the silence of a meadow where you expect birdsong and hear none—it is the total negation, the structural impossibility. No insects in the dead-leaf mat, no worms breaking it down, no carrion birds circling the carcasses of the old trees. The air does not buzz. Nothing moves except the slow rotation of the canopies, which turn not toward any sun but toward a fixed point in the pale, thin sky where the light is marginally less dim, a place that might be the sun or might be something else wearing the sun's position. The silence of the fauna is so complete it becomes a presence, a pressure against the ears, which makes what fills those ears—the sound—almost unbearable by contrast.

Because the sound is everything.

It rises from the stone. It comes from the air. It is inside your molars and your femur and the liquid behind your eyes. It is a deep, subsonic drone, a grinding and a groaning, the sound of a world still being born from the rock beneath you—tectonic plates that haven't settled into their roles, stone under pressure that doesn't know yet whether it's mountain or valley, the whole vast plateau sighing in the voice of geology at the beginning of geology. Layered above it, a high thin shrieking, like metal under stress, like the air itself being stretched past the point where air should be able to stretch. And beneath it all, a tone so low it isn't heard so much as felt, a pulse that matches no heartbeat, a rhythm that might be the frequency at which this place is slowly, slowly becoming real. The sound has no source you can point to. It is *here*. It is the here-ness of the place, the fact of its existence crying out in frequencies that a stable world would have settled long ago into silence.

The structures are few and far between and each one is a small catastrophe of understanding.

You see them first as shapes in the pale forest—taller than the trees or squat against the stone, built of material that is almost but not quite like the rock, as if they were grown rather than assembled, as if the boundary between building and landscape is a suggestion the stone politely declined. They are primitive in the way a stone circle is primitive: early-tool-use, hand-formed, the work of hands or something like hands. Some are hollow. Some are full of something that might be sediment or might be the dried remains of something that was not an animal but was not not-an-animal either. The surfaces are covered in marks—grooves, notches, spirals, patterns that almost resolve into meaning before dissolving back into noise. The people who made them, if they were people, are long gone. Perhaps they never were fully solid. Perhaps this place breaks down solidity as casually as it breaks down water as a necessity, and they simply came apart over the centuries, unwound into the thin air, left their shapes behind like molds from which the castings were never made.

The light is dim in the way that deep water is dim—not dark, but filtered, reduced, a light that has traveled too far or through too much and arrived at its destination already tired, already thin. The sky is not black. It is a gray so pale it might be white seen through fog, or white seen through the memory of fog, and at its center, the not-quite-sun burns without heat, a disk that offers direction without offering warmth, the only landmark in a world that otherwise refuses to organize itself into geography. You cannot tell how far the plateau extends. The edge, if there is one, is lost in haze—not cloud, not mist, but a thinning of the atmosphere itself, a place where the air stops being air and becomes the idea of air, and beyond that, nothing. Not void. Nothing. The category hasn't been invented yet.

And the weirdness. The weirdness is everything.

It is not a feeling you have about this place. It is a property of the place, an environmental fact as fundamental as the elevation or the absence of water. You feel it in the way your hands keep expecting the stone to behave like stone and it doesn't, keeps doing small wrong things—refracting light at angles that don't match the geometry, warming under your palm as if it has a pulse. You feel it in the way the vegetation shifts when you're not looking directly at it, rearranging itself in your peripheral vision, and when you turn, nothing has moved, and you cannot be sure. You feel it in your own thoughts, which here have a different texture—slower, heavier, as if they too are made of something that hasn't decided what it is yet. The threshold between matter and meaning is thin here, permeable, and on the other side of it, you glimpse things that might be laws of nature in a universe that hasn't finished drafting them. A tree that is also a sound. A stone that is also a memory. The sound that is also the shape of the silence that would exist if this place were real.

It is slow here. You have been on the plateau for what might be days or might be hours, and time moves like the sound—deep, resonant, glacial. The changes, when they come, are so gradual you perceive them only in comparison, only when you notice that the pale sun has shifted a degree, or that a tree that was standing is now lying flat, its trunk broken not by wind but by the slow, patient fact of its own weight in a place where gravity is a courtesy rather than a law. The plateau is patient. It has been here since before here was a coherent concept, and it will be here after after settles into its meaning. It does not wait for you. It simply is, in the way that a sentence is before the words arrive, the shape of something not yet spoken, the ground on which reality is still practicing.
