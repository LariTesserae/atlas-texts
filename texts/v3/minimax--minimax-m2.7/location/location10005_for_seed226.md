The town of Threnn has no business existing here, and yet it has existed for three hundred years, and will exist for three hundred more, or until the mountain decides otherwise.

It sits on a saddle of exposed basalt at the edge of a high plateau, where the ground water table pushes close enough to the surface that the earth never quite dries. Mosses colonize every crack in the stone—not the thin clinging crust of lowland rock, but deep, plush cushions that hold water like sponges, weeping continuously from their edges. The town's flagstones are slick beneath the feet of its inhabitants, darkened with moisture that seeps up from below, and the air tastes of wet mineral, of deep places.

What is unusual about Threnn is the geology. The basalt is raw. Not weathered, not rounded by millennia of rain and freeze-thaw. The columns rise from the plateau edge like the walls of a half-built cathedral, their fractures still sharp, their surfaces still holding the memory of the fire that formed them. Erosion has barely touched them. The mountain here is young, or perhaps merely reluctant to submit to time. Lichens have only begun their slow work, leaving halos of pale green on the darkest stone. To walk through Threnn's streets is to walk through a place still becoming itself, where the work of geology and the work of the town's builders proceed in uneasy parallel.

The settlement is small—contained, bounded, a dozen structures at most clustered around a spring that rises through a circle of carved stone. The houses are built of the same basalt, fitted with a precision that speaks of careful hand-tools, adzes and wedges, the work of generations who learned the stone's ways. Roofs are steeply pitched to shed the frequent mists, their eaves hung with sod that supports small gardens of hardy herbs and fog-loving ferns. Between the buildings, narrow lanes open onto the vast emptiness of the plateau—the meadow grass, the standing water in low places, the distant peaks disappearing into cloud. The town is a room, and beyond it the world continues, immense and unpopulated.

Sound carries strangely here. The wet stone catches and holds noise, and the thin air carries it far. Voices from the spring circle reach the outermost houses without effort. But the dominant sounds are not human: the constant whisper of water moving through saturated ground, the drip and trickle that fills every hollow; the wind pulling through the moss, a low continuous tone like breath through cloth; the occasional hard rain that drums on the stone and transforms the lanes into shallow streams. At night, when the town's inhabitants sleep, there is only the water and the wind, speaking to the mountain.

Fauna is rare. A few hardy birds pass through on migration, their calls sharp and solitary in the morning mist. Voles in the wet grass. Once, a heron standing motionless by the spring, watching nothing. The town is not a place that welcomes wildlife, but it does not drive them away either. They simply find it easier to exist elsewhere.

Vegetation beyond the immediate settlement is lush for this elevation—the plateau supports grasses knee-high, with interspersed sedge and rush in the wetter depressions, and beyond the meadow's edge, a dark belt of pine and juniper that crowds the ridgeline. But the lushness is specific, constrained by the altitude and the rawness of the soil. Plants cling to their patches. The rock beneath offers little purchase, and what soil exists is thin and young.

Threnn persists because its people understood something essential about this place: the land is not finished, and so it must be held. The spring is kept clear. The stone foundations are checked each generation for shifts, for new fractures. The drainage channels are maintained with care, lest the water that gives life also bring the slow collapse of the streets. They have learned to build with the mountain's unfinished nature, to make their structures flexible enough to absorb the minute movements that would shatter rigid architecture.

Whether the town will outlast the geological forces that shaped it, no one knows. The elders say the columns at the plateau's edge are taller each year, though this may be memory rather than measurement. The springs' flow has not changed in living recollection. Perhaps the mountain will settle. Perhaps the town will fall. But for now, in this small bounded space of wet stone and patient water, in the clear cold light of the high plateau, Threnn continues.
