# The Filter Basins

The basin exhales. Water presses upward through cracks in the stone floor—not flooding, not quite, but seeping, persistent, as though the earth itself is sweating. The surfaces are dark and slick, volcanic glass worn smooth by years of this slow weeping, this constant moisture. Every rock face gleams with condensation, and the air carries a mineral chill that settles into cloth and bone.

There is no vegetation. None. The surfaces that aren't wet are colonized instead by something else—a pale, fibrous growth that crumbles at the touch, or else a thin crystalline deposit that catches light like flawed glass. These are not plants. They don't photosynthesize, don't reach, don't root in any way that makes sense. They simply *are*, occupying stone the way mold occupies bread, patient and alien.

Within the basin—because it does have walls, a cathedral-sized enclosure of quarried stone, its sides too steep to climb, its rim lost in dimness above—the world is dense. The water pools in depressions and channels, black and still, and across its surface the films and mats do not move like vegetation but like something between organism and structure. Layers. Near-touching. Every pocket of darkness holds movement: small soft-bodied things with too many legs, their bodies translucent enough to show the organs within, twitching in the wet cracks, breeding in the silt.

The industrial presence is here but intimate—pipes that snake between pools, small engines bolted to stone, a filtration system that hums at a frequency just below thought. Someone built this once. The marks remain, faint letters stenciled on metal, a faded symbol on a pipe junction. The machinery still runs because something maintains it, or because the basin itself has grown around the function, biomechanical, no longer separable.

Sound is mostly quiet: the drip and catch of water finding its level, the hum of the engines, the soft click and slide of organisms moving through moisture. Occasionally, from somewhere deep in the pools, a sound like singing—a resonance that is not mechanical, not biological, not explainable.

The basin is active. Water moves through it in cycles. The organisms breed and die and breed again. The engines cycle. The fungal things advance over stone a centimeter per year, or retreat, the balance never quite settled. This has been happening long enough for the stone to forget its quarry-sharp edges, long enough for silt to accumulate in the low places, long enough for the machinery to grow into the landscape like a root system.

You are very small here. The walls press close. The moisture finds every seam in your clothing. The organisms in the cracks watch with organs you cannot identify. The engines hum their patient, grinding song.

You do not know if you are inside something, or if something is inside you.
