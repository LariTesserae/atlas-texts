The gorge exists in silence between sounds. When the water permits.

You descend through no entrance you could name—just a narrowing, a pressing-together, and then you are inside a space that has no business being this loud. The walls are close enough to touch on either side if you stretch, and they rise straight up, and they are wet. Everything here is wet. Water emerges from cracks in the rock face in threads and ropes, joins other threads, becomes rivulets, becomes sheets, becomes a continuous crash and thunder somewhere below and ahead that you cannot yet see. The acoustics of the narrow space turn this into something immense, disorienting, a sound that lives in your chest.

The darkness is not total—you can see your hands, occasionally—but it is dense. The gorge narrows above into a seam of grey sky that yields no direct light, only a suggestion of open air. Shadows pool in the crevices where the ferns grow thick, where the moss has climbed eight feet up the rock face and higher, where water hangs in the air as fog that never quite settles. You breathe it in. It tastes like stone and cold.

There is a height to this place that your body understands even before your eyes adjust. You are inside something tall. The walls are basalt, or something like basalt—dark, fine-grained, recently cracked. The fractures are sharp enough to catch skin. You see this in the way the light falls across the rock: angles that haven't softened, edges that haven't rounded. The gorge is new. The gorge is being made right now, in fact—water and gravity and the slow violence of uplift, still arguing over who will own the shape of it. Loose rock shifts underfoot when you step wrong, and the sound of it is swallowed immediately by the larger sound, the roar that is always there, the roar that is the space itself.

The vegetation should not be this dense at this elevation. You know this. You have been climbing. But the gorge has created its own weather, its own humidity, its own warmth held close against the cold air above, and the plants have responded by becoming a second architecture layered over the stone. Roots hang from the top like fingers gripping the edge. Moss carpets everything that is flat enough to hold it. Ferns grow in the crevices in great dark clumps, uncurling even here in the darkness. Lichens have made the rock face into a topographic map in grey and green and orange, and when water runs over them they darken and glisten and the smell rises—the smell of wet rock, wet earth, the mineral sharpness of a place where water has not stopped moving for longer than you have been alive.

The loudness is not steady. This is part of what makes it violent. Water falls from somewhere above in a cascade that breaks and rejoins and breaks again, and the wind that comes with it moves in gusts that feel personal, directional, intent. Fog banks roll through the narrow space and then retreat. Spray comes off the walls in volleys when the wind catches a fall wrong. The sound of it is layered—you hear the crash of the main water, the hiss of spray, the rush of smaller channels, the percussion of drips and drops and trickles, and beneath all of it a deeper sound, a vibration, a hum that might be the rock itself absorbing and releasing the force of so much water moving through so young a channel. Your ears ring with it. The gorge does not permit silence.

You are alone here. There is no animal life. No birds in the canopy. No fish in the pools you can see, no insects on the wet stone. Only the water, which is alive in its own way—not in the way of organisms but in the way of processes, forces, a system so far from equilibrium that it has become its own kind of presence. The gorge is turbulent. It is always changing, always in motion, always loud and wet and dark and new. The stone remembers being underground. The water has not yet learned where it wants to go. The ferns and mosses and lichens have colonized every surface that will hold them, and they hold on, and they wait for the rock to finish becoming what it will be.

You could stand here for a long time. You could stand here and the gorge would keep roaring and keep dripping and keep being what it is: a small, loud, wet, high, raw, impossible space where the world is still being born, and the birth is very, very loud.
