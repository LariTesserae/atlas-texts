# The Plateau of Drowned Summits

The air here tastes of iron and rain. Though the elevation is extreme—your lungs labor for each breath, and the sky presses close in its thinness—the warmth surprises you, rising from some deep source beneath the worn stone, geothermal breath that keeps the winds from biting. You stand on what was once the summit of a range, you realize, but eons of patient water have worn the peaks down to wide, almost-flat plateaus separated by gorges so deep they seem bottomless. The mountains are old. They have finished dying. What remains is a vast, eroded topography of mesas and buttes and winding tablelands, the stone faces glazed with centuries of condensation, streaked with mineral deposits that catch the light in pale threads.

Rain comes without warning. The sky churns with turbulent clouds—here, the atmosphere is never settled—and then the rain arrives in sudden sheets, running down the weathered rock in ephemeral cascades before the sun breaks through and dries everything again within the hour. You learn not to trust the weather. You learn not to trust the silence, either, because the sounds here are relentless. The wind tears across the plateau in sustained howls, threading through the ancient erosion channels with an almost architectural precision, and every gust is layered with the distant thunder of water moving through the gorge system below, the creak of stone settling, the dry whistle of loose scree sliding in some far canyon you cannot see. Where water collects—in shallow depressions worn into the ancient stone, in pockets that catch the rare rain and hold it in thin, muddy pools—the sound becomes almost musical, as if the land were drinking. But the drinking never lasts. The sun returns, the pools shrink to dark stains, and the damp bakes back into the rock.

Vegetation grows here, but not uniformly. The lushness is focal, not diffuse—dense clusters of broad-leafed plants in the water-pockets, thickets of something like ferns where the condensation pools, vines that crawl up the edges of the gorges in long green ropes. In the plateaus' flat centers there is almost nothing, just bare stone and the occasional stubborn tree, gnarled and low, its roots anchored in a crack where it has found enough moisture to persist. The plants are survivors. They are opportunistic, quick to bloom after rain, quick to go dormant in the dry stretches. The overall effect is of a landscape dotted with small green miracles, isolated pockets of life surrounded by the emptiness of the ancient rock.

There are almost no people here, and what few signs exist are scattered and partial. Far below, at the bottom of one of the gorges, you catch sight of a structure—walls of stacked stone, a roof long collapsed—partially hidden by the trailing vine-growth. Another, on a distant plateau, shows only a low wall, a foundation, a few hand-hewn timbers left to weather. These remains are old. The people who built them used hand-tools, simple instruments, and whatever the land provided: stone for walls, wood for frames, reeds or fibers woven for shelter. Primitive, but not absent. Someone lived here once. Many someones, perhaps. And then they left, or diminished, or were worn away like the stone itself.

The sky, when you look up, is the most unsettling element. The turbulence here is not metaphor—it is meteorology. The clouds roil and rebuild themselves continuously, a process of creation and destruction that never pauses. The light shifts from bright to shadowed to bright again within minutes, and the interplay of sun and cloud on the ancient stone creates patterns that seem almost intentional, like the land is trying to communicate something in a language that was lost before the mountains finished eroding. You find yourself pausing often. You find yourself listening to the silence when it comes—the brief, rare silences—because even the silences here are full, pregnant with the sounds that are about to return: the wind, the water, the rock, the weight of the open sky.
