# The Terrace-Made World

This place has forgotten its foundations. You descend into it through the air, arriving by cable or stair or some long spiraling road carved into stone that is not quite stone anymore—it has been worn smooth by centuries of use, darkened by moisture, softened at its edges where roots have worked their way into every seam and joint. The plateau sits high in the mountains, or what remains of them, for the mountains themselves have been terraced and paved and built upon until their original shapes are matters of scholarly dispute. There are places where the rock face still shows, cliffs of pale conglomerate streaked with mineral runoff, but these are like the bones of some great creature exposed by erosion—here and there, the skeleton beneath the skin.

The skin is everything else. The skin is built. Platforms of poured concrete and fitted stone extend outward from the mountain in enormous steps, each one large enough to hold neighborhoods, fountains, garden courts, market squares. You can stand at the eastern edge of one terrace and look west to where the next terrace rises, its walls hung with cultivated vines heavy with purple fruit, its lower levels carved into arcades where people walk in the shade. You can look down through gaps in the platform flooring—designed gaps, not accidents—and see the level below, a whole other world of streets and canals and trees that have grown so tall their canopies reach to your feet. The scale is disorienting. This is not a building you can see the edges of. It is a landscape that has been made.

And it is alive. The vegetation did not wait politely at the edges. It grew into the construction the way water grows into cracks, the way light grows into shadow. The concrete is streaked with dark patterns where moss has taken hold in the temperate microclimate the city has made for itself. Trees push up through drainage gratings, their roots following the pipes down into the dark, their branches spreading across rooftops until the distinction between roof and canopy dissolves. On the lower terraces, where the air is stiller, the humidity higher, the vegetation is so thick it forms a kind of second city in the air—a canopy of roots and leaves and hanging flowers through which the lower streets are visible only as glints of water and stone. You hear the birds more than you see them. They nest in the steel lattice of old structural supports, in the hollows of carved stonework, in the dense tangles of roots where the trees have grown together and their bark has fused into a single rough surface. The birds sing in layers, a chorus that rises from below, from above, from everywhere at once, punctuated by the mechanical calls of some species that has adapted to mimic the city's machinery.

The water moves. But slowly. It comes from somewhere deep, from cisterns carved into the mountain's heart, and it is pumped upward through shafts of copper and iron that sweat in the humid air, their surfaces green with condensation. The water feeds fountains that no one watches anymore—they have been flowing for so long that they have become features of the landscape, the way a rock formation might be. The water pools in channels cut into the terraces, channels wide enough for small boats but kept slow, their currents barely visible, their surfaces still enough to reflect the green of the surrounding plants. Occasionally there is rain. It falls from somewhere above the plateau—a weather system the city has made for itself, warm rain that comes when the humid air rises high enough to cool against the real sky—and when it falls, the people stand in it, and the plants drink, and for a little while the world is nothing but water and the sound of water and the smell of wet stone.

The machines are always humming. You feel it more than hear it—a vibration in the pavement, in the walls, in the metal railings along the terraced edges. Somewhere below, engines turn, pumps push, mechanisms that have been maintained so long by so many generations that no one person understands them anymore. The tech is industrial but ancient: there are factories here, but they are built into the terraces themselves, their smokestacks integrated into the landscape, their waste heat channeled into the heating system that keeps the plateau warm even when the real mountains below are locked in snow. The air smells of water and growing things and something else underneath—a faint metallic mineral smell, the scent of the mountain itself, of stone that has been soaked in engineered weather for so long it has taken on a new character.

The light is everywhere. The plateau sits above the cloud layer on clear days, and the sun comes fierce and bright, and the water channels blaze with reflected light, and the glass surfaces of old greenhouse arcades throw prismatic patterns across the stone. When the plateau is in cloud—and it often is, the clouds rising from the valleys below and pooling against the terraces like water against a dam—the light becomes diffuse, silver, soft. The edges of things blur. The city hums and breathes. Some nights the stars come out, cold and hard points of light, and the terraces below are dark but for the glow of phosphorescent moss in the garden courts, and the slow pulse of machinery below. The city dreams then, perhaps. Or remembers. It has been here so long that the difference between forgetting and remembering has blurred, the same way the distinction between stone and root has blurred, the same way the line between building and garden has become impossible to find. You could walk this place for years and still not know if you are walking through a forest that has been built or a city that has become a forest. The place does not care about your confusion. It goes on being itself.
