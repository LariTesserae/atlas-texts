The city of Veth Saline rises from what was once a mountain range so ancient that the very concept of summit has eroded into soft, rounded geography. The peaks here wore themselves down over epochs, leaving broad basins and gentle swells that collect moisture the way cupped palms hold water — always some, never enough to spill. Mist gathers in the evenings, pooling in the hollows between the city's districts like something breathing. The air carries that particular coolness of altitude, the kind that settles into bone and stays, a permanent October.

Everything is plant.

Not in the way that vines climb walls or forests cover hillsides — in the way that stone does not. The ground itself is a living substrate, a vast fungal-root matrix interlaced with the pale filaments of root networks that have woven themselves into the ancient bedrock until geology and biology are no longer distinguishable. The streets are packed plant-matter, firm as concrete but yielding slightly underfoot, breathable. Buildings grow rather than stand: their walls are thick tissue, wood-that-is-not-quite-wood, bark-textured and warm. Towers spiral upward on some metabolic imperative, their uppermost rooms still growing, still adding cells, still alive. When it rains — and it rains often enough, though lightly, the moisture coming in from the distant sea in steady drizzles — the water beads on leaf-surfaces and runs down channels cut deliberately into the plant-matter, feeding the structure even as it carries waste toward the processing roots far below.

The people of Veth Saline built here three centuries ago, when the industrialization of the old lowland cities drove them to seek altitude, distance, atmosphere. They brought machines. They brought engines and smelting chambers and the knowledge of how to fuse copper into wire and iron into girder. What they did not bring, or what they could not bring, was the conventional ecosystem: the insects and birds and mammals that once moved through human settlements. Animal life exists here only in the most sparing whispers — a rare salamander in the perpetual moisture of the eastern district, glimpsed once and not seen again; a moth species that has adapted to feed on the nectar of building-flowers; something that might be a small rodent but might be a growth, a tumor of the substrate mistaken for a creature. The absence of fauna gives the city a particular silence beneath its sounds, a gap like a missing tooth in a jaw.

The sounds, indeed, are persistent. The industrial heart of Veth Saline beats in its lower districts, where the substrate has been channeled and shaped to conduct steam and vapor between processing centers. Engines hiss and whistle; pressure releases with a sound like sighing; great looms of plant-fiber clatter through their cycles in textile mills that feel more like bodies than buildings. From the higher districts come subtler sounds: the creak of walls that are also wood, the distant gurgle of water moving through arterial channels, the occasional groan of a tower adjusting its height as its crown grows. The city is never quiet. The city is never still.

And yet the place feels empty. The density of presences — people, structures, the active markers of urban life — is sparse. Districts connect by avenues that stretch long between clusters of buildings, leaving wide swaths of living ground where no construction has taken root. The population is perhaps ten thousand, a small number for a city that spreads across three river-valleys and four distinct bio-zones. The spaces between things are not merely unbuilt but un-built, deliberate emptinesses maintained by some collective decision or simply by the slow pace at which the builders work, the substrate growing faster than the will to fill it.

In the oldest districts, where the first foundations were laid over two hundred years ago, the erosion of distinction has progressed furthest. The factories are half-consumed, their metal frameworks wound through with root and fiber until machine and organism are indistinguishable. Steam pipes emerge from walls like circulatory vessels; the gears of early engines have become the joints of walking structures, biomechanical relics that still function, still produce, still contribute to the city's output of processed material and generated power. The residents of these districts have adapted their gait, their posture, their entire manner of moving through spaces that shift slightly underfoot as the substrate respires. They do not seem to mind. They have lived here their whole lives, and this is simply how things are.

The weirdness does not announce itself. It is present in the peripheral vision, in the moments when the light falls wrong or the shadows move independently of their sources. It is present in the substrate that seems to respond to attention, the way a stretch of ground will become firmer where a person walks frequently, softer in unused corners. It is present in the buildings that grow toward the sun but also toward each other, in the towers that tilt toward their neighbors as if in conversation. The people of Veth Saline have learned not to notice. They have built a civilization on and within a living world, and the world has become, in some fundamental way, aware of them.

The rare animals — the salamander, the moth, the uncertain rodent — are not absent by accident. They were once abundant, and then they were not. Something changed in the ecosystem, some shift in the balance of the substrate's metabolism, and the fauna could not adapt as the flora could. What remains are the survivors, the wanderers, the species whose lifecycles fit within the rhythms of a world that is no longer theirs. They move carefully through the spaces of the city, watched by the buildings that have become the dominant life form.

Veth Saline at dusk: the mist rises from the hollows, carrying the sound of the lower districts up through the avenues, a mechanical murmur beneath the natural hush of a highland evening. The towers glow faintly, their bark-skin bioluminescent in the dark, a warmth that the residents have bred into the substrate over generations. The people walk home through streets that flex gently beneath their weight, past buildings that lean slightly to observe them, toward homes that have grown to fit their families, their habits, their specific arrangements of objects on shelves. The city breathes. The city dreams. The city is not finished becoming whatever it is becoming.

In the morning, the rain will come again, soft and persistent, and the channels will fill, and the substrate will drink, and the factories will resume their ancient rhythms. The city will not be quiet. The city will not be still. But the emptiness between things will remain, the patient space waiting for growth that does not hurry, for a civilization that has learned to let its world become itself.
