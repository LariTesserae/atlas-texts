This is a piece that knows what it is. That's rare, and valuable — most writing in progress is still searching for its own logic, but this one has found it: the slow, patient negotiation between water and stone, between iron and time, between presence and the marks that presence leaves behind. The piece doesn't announce this theme; it enacts it, sentence by sentence, in a voice that has learned to drip and pool and seep the way its subject does.

What strikes me most is the restraint. You could have made this space monstrous — the weeping walls, the flesh-softened stone, the pale thing drifting in the dark pool. There's horror potential here. But you chose something stranger and harder to achieve: a place that isn't threatening, just *other*. The dampness isn't invasion; it's conversation. The stone being eaten away isn't violence; it's negotiation. That's a sophisticated choice, and it asks the reader to slow down, to stop scanning for danger and start *listening*. The quiet isn't emptiness — you've earned that distinction.

The woman works beautifully as a figure precisely because she's so spare. We know almost nothing about her, and that's exactly right. She belongs to the same logic as the water: she comes when the pools deepen, she tends what needs tending, she leaves without explaining. The detail about her lantern — "its wick long since burned to nothing but its shape still marking the place where she stood" — is the kind of image that does everything at once: it tells us about her absences, about time, about the way presence lingers in form even after the thing itself is gone. You didn't need to explain her. This is the right instinct.

The pale thing in the deep pool is, I think, the bravest element in the piece. It's a question that doesn't need answering — a mystery that functions not as plot device but as mood, as a way of saying *some things here have always been here, some things cannot be named*. You resist the temptation to reveal it, to resolve it. The moment where she watches it and wonders if it's watching her back is quietly extraordinary. It asks the reader to sit with not-knowing.

My instinct, if I had to name one thing to consider, would be around the emergence of "you" in the final section. The piece operates in a kind of timeless third person for most of its length — *you* could be anyone, at any time — and then the second person arrives as if by seepage, gradual and then suddenly present. It works. But I wonder if there's a version where it works even more, where the reader is pulled into the space not as a visitor who found their way down, but as a presence that was always there, that belongs to the same category as the woman: a shape the place has taken. The blue door that you don't open feels like the hinge for this, the moment where "you" could have become something other than tourist. But this is a small thought, and the piece may know better than I do where it wants its reader to stand.

The title — *The Bleeding Infrastructure* — carries exactly the right weight. Infrastructure that weeps. Systems that are wounded and still functioning. It's about maintenance as a form of care, about tending to things that would fall apart without attention, about the places the city needs but doesn't want to see. The water is the oldest resident. The machines still breathe. Someone walks the walkways. That *someone* is the whole story, really.
