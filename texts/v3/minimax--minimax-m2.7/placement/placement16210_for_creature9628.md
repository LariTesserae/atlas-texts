You have been standing here for some time before you realize you have been standing here. This is the first thing that happens: the noticing of yourself, the small shock of recognizing that you have become part of the texture. Before that, there was only the space, the drip and hum, the movement at the edges of vision that resolves into nothing when you look directly at it.

Now you are here. Your body displaces air that rats account for in their calculations. Your warmth registers as a passing weather system, something to be noted and then dismissed. You are the variable, and the variables do not matter.

The warmth is the first thing that becomes undeniable. It is not the warmth of sunlight, not the warmth of another body. It is the warmth of machines working, of infrastructure doing what it was designed to do, of the city above conducting its heat downward through stone and concrete like blood moving through tissue. Your skin responds to it slowly, giving up its own temperature in increments, and you realize that standing here long enough would mean ceasing to be a warm thing in a cool space and becoming instead a warm thing in a warm space, your boundaries softening into the general warmth of the tunnel.

This is what the rats understand. They do not distinguish between the warmth of a compressor and the warmth of a body. They do not fear you because they have no reason to fear you; you are simply another source of heat in a space that is already warm, another moving thing that will pass through and leave behind its trace of displaced air. The rats are too old, too numerous, too patient to waste energy on fear. Fear is a response to threat, and you are not a threat. You are not anything. You are a shape that moves and makes sound and will, soon enough, leave.

The concrete is cool where your back rests against it, and this is the only place you can feel your own body with clarity. The rest of you has gone warm, has gone soft at the edges, has begun to blur into the general atmosphere of the tunnel. Your spine against stone: this is the boundary. Everything else is suggestion.

You think of the fern. You did not notice it when you first entered—it is too small, too fragile, too easy to overlook—but now you cannot stop noticing it. It is dying, the passage said, but has not yet decided to die. There is something in this that feels familiar, though you could not say why. Perhaps it is the brown curl of its fronds, the way they have reached as far as they can reach and found nothing worth finding. Perhaps it is the single fiddlehead still wound tight, still waiting for moisture it may or may not receive. Perhaps it is the way it grows in a crack, which is to say the way it grows in a mistake, in a failure of the concrete to seal itself completely, in a space that was not meant for growing but grows anyway.

You have been in cracks too, in spaces that were not meant for you. Not here—here is your first time, and already you can feel yourself becoming someone who has been here, who knows the weight of this air, the specific frequency of the dripping. But elsewhere. Before. In the spaces between what was built and what was intended, where the water finds its way through and the moss takes hold and the rats make their routes.

A drop of water falls from somewhere you cannot see. It lands on a surface—concrete, perhaps, or the carapace of a cockroach that has already vanished—and the sound it makes is larger than the drop itself. The acoustics of this space are generous. Every small thing is amplified, made significant, given a resonance that extends beyond its actual duration. The drip goes on longer than the drop, spreading through the air in waves you can almost see if you let your eyes unfocus, if you stop trying to resolve the darkness into shapes.

The rats are moving now. Not in response to you—you have been still long enough to be absorbed into the background—but in response to something in their own system, some calculation you will never understand. They flow through the drainage channel like water themselves, dark and purposeful, and you watch them without watching, your peripheral vision doing the work your direct gaze cannot. There are so many. There are always so many. And they are not afraid, not even now that you have become a fixed presence in their space.

What would it mean to be afraid? You have been afraid. You know the shape of it in your body—the tightening, the readiness, the way fear turns the world into a sequence of threats and exits. But here, in this warm corridor beneath the city, fear seems impossible. The rats have no reason to fear you. The cockroaches have no reason to fear you. Even the fern, dying slowly in its crack, has no reason to fear you. It will die whether you are here or not. It is dying because dying is what ferns do, because this space is too warm, too dark, too wrong for the life it was trying to live.

You are not afraid. You are standing in a tunnel, and the air is warm, and the water is seeping, and the rats are moving, and the cockroaches are folding themselves into cracks, and the fern is waiting for moisture it may or may not receive. You are part of the texture now, even if you will not remain part of it. Even if your presence here is temporary, a brief disturbance in the frequency, you have been here, and being here has changed something, though you could not say what.

The compressors hum. The water drips. The rats chatter in a language that is almost but not quite below hearing, a frequency of social coordination that you can feel more than understand. The moss drinks when there is water and waits when there is not. The fern curls its brown fronds a little tighter. The cockroaches wait in their cracks for the world to forget them, which it always does, which is exactly what they want.

And you stand here, feeling yourself become less distinct, feeling your edges soften into the general warmth, feeling the slow dissolution that is not death but is perhaps its first cousin. The concrete is cool where your back touches it. The air is warm everywhere else. The water seeps. The minerals bloom. The tunnel holds you the way it holds everything, without preference, without judgment, with the simple patience of a space that was built to contain and has found that containing means letting live, means letting grow, means letting become.

You could stay here for a long time. The sound would not change much. The light would not change at all. The rats would continue their negotiations, the cockroaches their patient stillness, the moss its slow saturation of green so dark it is almost black. You could become as the fern, not decided, not dying, not growing, just being in the crack, reaching toward light that will not come, waiting for moisture that may or may not fall.

But you will not. You will leave. You will climb back up through the access hatch, through the warmth and the dark, into the bright urgent world above where things happen faster and fear makes sense and the water flows in pipes instead of seeping through cracks. You will close the hatch behind you and the tunnel will forget you were here, will filter you out of its frequency, will continue the slow work of holding what it holds.

And still, some part of you will remain. Not your body—your body will go, will climb, will walk away into the city above. But some part of you will stay, some attention, some warmth, some small part of your own slow dissolution into the texture of the place. You will carry the tunnel with you the way you carry all the places you have been, folded into you, made part of your own mineral archaeology, the blooms of experience layering over each other, building toward something you will not live long enough to see.

The fern is still waiting. The moss is still drinking. The rats are still negotiating.

You stand in the tunnel, and the tunnel holds you, and the water seeps, and the light is dim and blurred by condensation, and somewhere in the darkness a cockroach catches a brief reflection and is gone.

You could stay.

You will not stay.

Both of these things are true, and neither of them matters, and the tunnel does not notice, and the tunnel does not care, and the tunnel holds you anyway, patient and slow, the way it holds everything, the way it has always held everything, the way it will always hold everything as long as the water seeps and the warmth rises and the concrete continues its long dissolution into the mineral future.

You are here. You have been here. You will not be here.

The drip falls. The hum continues. The rats move through the darkness like a thought you are almost having.
