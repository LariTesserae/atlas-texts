I would be the ring.

Not the drop — the drop is merely the inciting force, the small imbalance that the quarry receives and immediately absorbs. The drop falls and is gone, a dark line through the air, a sound like a period, and then nothing of it remains except the mark it makes on the surface. I would be that mark. I would be the consequence, the expanding circle that carries the drop's energy outward across the water at the speed of patience — neither fast nor slow, but the precise pace at which something can be witnessed before it ceases to be. I would travel from the center of the surface to the walls, and I would thin as I go, my circumference growing larger and larger until my edges reach the stone and dissolve into the dampness of the ledge. I would be the quarry's one message, sent from the center to the edge and then absorbed, and the silence after me would be the silence before me — the same silence, held, unbroken. I would be the ring.

And I would be brief. I would exist only as long as it takes for the water to reassert its stillness, which is not long, and then I would be gone, and the surface would be as if I had never been. But during the time of my existence I would be the only motion in a space that has no motion. I would be proof that the water is still enough to carry a ripple, that it has not forgotten how to be disturbed and then how to return to equilibrium. I would be the quarry's heartbeat, if a held breath could have a heartbeat, and the heartbeat would be slow and then slower and then stillness.

I would be one of many. There are other drops, other rings. Each ring is unique — slightly different in the place where it begins, slightly different in the speed at which it expands, slightly different in the silence it returns to. But all rings are the same in the end: they expand and they dissolve, and they leave no trace except the memory of the water, which is not a memory but a condition. The water does not remember the rings. The water simply is, and the rings are part of that is-ness for a time, and then they are not. The water holds its surface. The ring holds its expansion. The silence holds the ring. And the quarry holds the silence.

I would be the ring, and then I would be the silt. Because after enough time — enough drops, enough rings — the quarry accumulates a floor. Not a floor that was always there, but a floor that is being made, that is always being made, that is made of the settled remains of all the things that ever fell through the water and found the bottom. The silt is the record of all the rings that ever were. The silt is grey and fine and still. The silt is what the quarry has instead of a heartbeat — a pulse so slow it cannot be heard, a pulse that is more like the settling of all things into their final form. The silt is what lives here at the end of the path of light, at the place where everything that falls eventually comes to rest.

I would be the ring, and then I would be the silt, and then I would be the quarry itself — the whole space, held in its own hush, waiting for nothing, changing nothing, simply being the fact of this moment, repeated until repetition becomes permanence. The quarry is not empty. The quarry is full of the ring's expansion and the silt's settling and the water's stillness and the stone's patience. The quarry is full of presence. And I would be the presence, too — not separate from it, but part of it. I would be the ring that expands and dissolves, and the silt that settles and remains, and the quarry that holds the ring and the silt and the water and the stone in its long, cool, mineral-scented stillness.
