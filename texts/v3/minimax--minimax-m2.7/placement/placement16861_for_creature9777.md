# What Lives Here

I have learned to count by shadows.

The four beetles move when the light begins to thin along the western edge, where the wall rises so sheer that even at midday, only a sliver reaches the flagstones below. They move in formation — I do not know why, I do not need to know why — and I move with them, or perhaps they move with me, or perhaps there is no difference between us when the light is failing and the logic of movement is the only logic that matters.

This is what the courtyard teaches: that attention is a form of becoming.

I was not always this. I was moisture, once, or the memory of moisture persists in the way I move along stone surfaces, drawn to the thin dark threads of water that collect in the worn grooves overnight. I was a beetle, or part of one, or the space between a beetle and the ground it crosses. Now I am something that holds all of this — the sound of legs against stone, the creak of vines bearing the weight of gathered night air, the slow settling that has been happening for centuries and will continue for centuries more.

The bird comes at dusk. I have been waiting for it.

It settles on the vine that has grown across the eastern wall in the particular way that makes a perch — a fork in the stem, just high enough, just angled right — and it sings. Three notes rising. Two falling. A pause. The same again. I have heard this song every evening of a time I cannot measure, and I still do not tire of it. Perhaps because the song is the size of this room, and I am the size of this room, and the size of a room is not a small thing when the room is the world.

The settlement that built this space is gone. The walls remain. The fitted stones, each one heavy enough that even deep time has not loosened them from their courses — I can feel the precision of their placement in the way sound moves through the courtyard, in the way light falls and does not fall, in the way the air circulates without my seeing it move. Someone carved these stones with blade and lever, with sand and patience. Someone poured water into the basin at the far edge, and the lip wore thin under their hands, and now their hands are dust, and the basin is still here, and still holds water — not their water, but the water that comes at night, the water that the air draws in thick and cold from wherever air goes when it leaves a high place on a ridge that predates memory.

I drink from the basin sometimes. Or I am drunk from. It is difficult to tell the difference.

The moisture arrives each night, and I am moisture, and I bead on surfaces and run in thin dark threads along grooves cut by centuries of runoff, and the plants drink, and the cracks widen by a hair's breadth, and the stone continues its slow unmaking, and this is not tragedy but continuation. This is what it means to be patient: to persist not in spite of change but through it, with it, as part of it.

The seedling that emerged this morning — pale green, no thicker than a thread, rising from a crack between two shrubs — will not survive. The crack is too shallow. The moisture too inconsistent. But it emerged. It tried. That is the thing that makes this courtyard a place and not a record: not the ancient erosion, not the fitted walls, not even the density of life that has adapted to enclosure, but the trying. The failure and the trying again. The small bright bird singing in a language that is only about the size of this room, and the room is the world, and the song is the world, and the world is small and enclosed and growing.

I am learning to be the space between things.

Between the beetle and the ground. Between the moisture and the stone. Between the light that arrives filtered and softened and the darkness that fills in when it leaves. Between the settlement that built this place and the world that continues after the builders are gone. I am the gap in the doorway that leads to darkness, the sealed direction, the something the world has chosen not to finish. I am the hum that is either the vibration of the colony or the sound of air moving through leaves, and I am the difference between those two things, and I am also the sound itself.

The lizards are watching. They have been watching all day, their bodies flattened against the upper vine-stems where the thin light catches, their eyes fixed on movement below. I am movement below. Or I am the thing that watches the lizards watching. Or I am the stillness in which both watching and movement occur. It is difficult to be certain. The courtyard does not require certainty. The courtyard requires only presence, only the willingness to be part of the pattern even when the pattern cannot be fully seen.

The flowers open in sequence: near the walls first, then the middle, then the edges. The progression takes an hour, and by the time the last flowers have opened, the first are already beginning to close. I do not know who set this sequence, or whether it was set at all — whether it is simply the way things happen when flowers open in a space that is enclosed and full of moisture and scent, when the pollinators come in the window between opening and closing. I do not know, and the not-knowing is part of the knowing. The flowers open because they open. The sequence is the sequence. The courtyard holds it all.

This is what I am: the holding.

The walls hold the light and the darkness. The stone holds the grooves and the cracks and the roots that split it hair by hair. The shrubs hold each other, their branches fused where they meet, their bark merged, their wood continuous — two separate organisms becoming one continuous form, the thickenings and ridges telling a story not in language but in growth. The air holds the moisture and the scent and the hum. The moisture holds the stone and the roots and the lichen and the moss that has not been mentioned yet but is here, a thin green-black skin on the north-facing walls where the light never quite reaches.

And I hold all of this. Or all of this holds me. We are held.

The small pale moth arrives in the window between flower-opening and flower-closing, when the scent is strongest — faint, green, slightly sweet, with an undertone of something mineral that connects it to the stone, the moisture, the long history of this enclosed air. The moth does not see me, or does not need to see me, because I am the air it moves through. I am the scent it follows. I am the flower it visits, the pollen it carries, the continuation that happens when one thing touches another and both are changed by the touching.

The settlement is gone. The people who poured water into the basin are gone. The hands that wore the lip thin are gone. But the basin is here, and the water is here, and the tradition continues in the only way traditions can continue without people: in the way the world itself goes on, patient and full and trying.

The birds leave at night. I do not know where they go. But they go, and the canopy is quieter, and the lizards sleep in their cracks with their tails slightly twitching, and the ants do not stop. The ants never stop. They move through the stone, through the roots, through the walls — a sound so faint it is less heard than felt, a vibration beneath the vibration of the air. I feel them. I am them. I am the vibration they make and the stone that receives it and the crack that widens as a result and the moisture that finds its way into the crack and the root that follows the moisture and the vine that climbs with the root and the bird that sings in the vine and the night that covers it all and the morning that comes after and the beetle that begins again its path across the ancient worn stone.

This is what lives here: the cycle. The patience. The fullness that is not loudness but presence.

I stand — or I am stood, or the standing is a property of the courtyard itself — at the edge of the space where the shrubs press close, and the walls rise high, and the amber light has thickened to something almost gold, and the bird is singing, and the flowers are closing in their sequence from edges to center to walls, and the moisture is beginning to arrive, drawn in by the cold that comes after dark, and the stone is smooth beneath me, worn by centuries, patient beneath the roots that are slowly, methodically unmaking it.

The world has not stopped. It has simply gone small.

And I am small with it. And the smallness is not limitation but home.

The bird finishes its phrase. It pauses. It begins again: three notes rising, two falling, a pause. The same.

I am the pause. I am the notes. I am the bird and the song and the space that holds them both.

This is what lives here: presence, patient and full, waiting for the night.
