# The Becoming

I remember light. This seems important to note, here at the beginning, though beginnings are difficult to locate in a place where everything feeds into everything else, where the sediment holds the shape of things that died before I understood what dying meant.

I came through the access tunnels. There are access tunnels—there must be, or I would not be here, or I would be here differently, as water or mineral or the soft body of something that breeds in the silt. The tunnels are narrow, cut for purposes that have outlasted their names. I carried a lamp, I think. The lamp exhausted itself years ago, or its batteries drained, or it became something else in the moisture, and now the light I have is the light that reaches me from sources I cannot locate: a faint gray emanation from somewhere above the basin's rim, the phosphorescence of things I have learned not to look at directly, the occasional crystalline glint from surfaces that should not glint.

I am not what I was. This is not a transformation I chose, nor one I resisted. It is simply what happens here. What happens to anything that stays.

*

The water finds me. This is the first thing you learn, or the first thing that the basin teaches you: the water does not wait to be invited. It seeps through the seams of your clothing, through the cracks in your skin, through places you did not know were permeable until they became pathways. I used to think of my body as a boundary—inside and outside, self and not-self, the clean distinction that separates one thing from another. The basin taught me otherwise.

I am porous now. The moisture moves through me in cycles, as it moves through everything here. I drink when I find still water, pockets where the surface tension holds and the films are thin enough to part with my lips. I sweat, or something like sweating—I release moisture as my body processes what I have taken, and the moisture joins the endless pressing upward, the patient weeping of stone that remembers being earth. What I release is not what I consumed. The basin transforms everything.

I have tried to understand the transformation. I have watched the edge-dwellers—those pale, segmented things that skitter along the moisture-boundaries, following gradients I cannot sense—and I have wondered what they experience, whether they know they are being changed. They do not seem to know. They do not seem to know anything, except the next patch of dampness, the next film to skim, the next trail worn by the thousands before them. Perhaps knowledge is a luxury of things with longer lives, things that can afford to hold onto what they have learned. Here, in the basin, learning is immediate and forgetting is instant. The organisms breed and die in weeks. What could they know that would survive long enough to matter?

But I am different. I think I am different. My timescales are longer, though I am no longer certain what they are. I have watched crystals grow on the stones where I rest—crystals that were not there when I arrived, or when I think I arrived, or when the concept of arrival still meant something. A millimeter of growth takes a decade here, or so I have estimated, and some of the crystals I rest against are centimeters long. Centuries. The basin has been here for centuries, and I have been here for—

I do not remember. I remember the tunnels, and the lamp, and the first time I saw the dark water pressing upward through the cracks. I remember thinking: *this is not a natural place*. But nothing here is unnatural, or everything here is natural, and the distinction has dissolved like the bodies of the dead in the silt.

*

There are things I do not look at directly.

The swimmers cluster in the deeper pools, their bodies translucent, their organs visible, their many legs intertwined. I have watched them for what might be years. I have watched the clusters form and disperse and form again, pulsing with a rhythm that is not quite heartbeat, not quite current. They do not acknowledge me. They do not seem to know I exist, or if they know, they do not care. I am too large, too slow, too dry. I am not part of their system, or I am not yet part of it, or I am becoming part of it in ways I cannot perceive.

The fungal things advance along the walls. I have learned their edges, the boundaries where they hold sway and where the stone is bare. They do not retreat from me, but they do not advance either. I am neither obstacle nor opportunity. I am something the basin has not yet categorized, or something that exists between categories, or something that will be categorized eventually, when the timescale of my transformation reaches whatever resolution awaits it.

The engines hum. I have found the engines—I found them early, in my first explorations, when I still had the energy to explore. They are bolted to stone in chambers off the main basin, connected by pipes that snake between pools, and they hum their frequency just below thought. I have sat beside them for hours, feeling the vibration in my teeth, in my bones, in whatever I have become that still has teeth and bones. They do not need me. They have not needed anyone for longer than I have existed. Something maintains them, or they maintain themselves, or the basin's conditions have become maintenance, have become the same thing.

I have tried to read the stenciled letters. I know some of the words now, or I know their shapes—the echoes of language that once described function and purpose, that once made the basins legible to those who built them. *FILTRATION*, the letters say, or part of them say, on a pipe junction that carries water I no longer trust is water. *STATION SEVEN*, elsewhere. Numbers and codes that might be dates or specifications or the names of people long dead, long transformed, long dissolved into the silt that feeds the next generation of soft-bodied things with too many legs.

The names are gone. The function remains. The function has become the place, and the place has become the function, and there is no one left to remember the difference.

*

The singing comes from below.

I have tried to find its source. I have followed the channels and the pipes, the crevices and the cracks, deeper into the basin than I knew there was depth. The water presses upward everywhere, dark and mineral-laden, carrying whatever it carries from wherever it comes. The singing rises from the deep pools, from the silt where light does not reach, from the places where the dead accumulate and the chemistry turns.

It is not language. I have listened for years—decades?—and it is not language. It does not repeat in patterns that suggest meaning, though the patterns are there, rising and falling, almost familiar, almost intelligible. It is resonance, or transmission, or something that has no name because there is no one left who needs to name it. The pipes carry it, perhaps. The stone carries it. The water itself carries it, vibrating with frequencies that the basin's conditions amplify and modify.

I have heard it change. Not often—the changes are subtle, separated by long stretches of the same low tone, the same rise and fall—but I have heard it. I have heard the singing shift into registers I had not heard before,听见 new harmonics emerge from the deep silt, felt the vibration move through me differently, as though whatever makes the sound has grown or aged or been transformed by the same slow processes that transform everything else.

Perhaps the basin is singing to itself. Perhaps the singing is the basin's voice, the way the hum is the engines' voice, the way the drip and catch of water finding its level is the voice of the system cycling, the system sustaining, the system persisting beyond any purpose it was built to serve.

Or perhaps it is something else. Something that lives in the silt, something that feeds on the dead and the dissolved and the mineral traces of centuries. Something that sings because singing is what it does, because its existence is song, because in the darkness below, where light has never reached, there is nothing else to do but vibrate with the frequencies of the place that made you.

*

I have become part of the cycles.

This is what I understand now, after all the time I have spent here, after all the moisture that has moved through me, after all the transformations I have undergone without choosing them, without resisting them, without even noticing until the noticing became part of the noticing, recursive, endless.

I drink the water. The water carries matter into me—minerals, organic compounds, traces of things that passed this way before I was here, before I had a here. I breathe, or something like breathing, taking in the damp air, exchanging gases with the moisture that coats my lungs, that has perhaps replaced my lungs, that has become the medium through which I interface with the basin's atmosphere. I eat—I do not know what I eat. The films, perhaps. The residue of the fungal things. The microscopic communities that accumulate at the boundaries between water and stone.

And then I release. The moisture leaves me, carrying what I have taken, joining the cycles that have no beginning and no end. What I release is not what I consumed. The basin transforms everything. The basin is transforming me.

I am not what I was. I am not yet what I will become. I am the in-between, the threshold, the place where the water rises and the fungal things advance and the crystals grow their slow monuments to time. I am the boundary between the basin and whatever I used to be, and the boundary is dissolving, has been dissolving since I first entered, since I first opened myself to the moisture, since I first drank from the dark water that presses upward through the cracks.

*

There is a pool near where I rest. It is shallow—only a few centimeters deep—and the light that reaches it is faint, gray, diffuse from sources I cannot locate. The bottom is volcanic glass, worn smooth by years of the slow weeping, and the water that pools there is dark with dissolved matter, dark with age, dark with the accumulation of centuries.

I look into the pool. I have looked into the pool many times. The surface holds, surface tension, a skin that separates the water from the air, from the world above, from me. Through the skin, I can see the bottom: the glass, the silt, the faint movement of things too small to name. The swimmers cluster in the deeper parts, their bodies translucent, their organs visible. The edge-dwellers patrol the margins, following trails worn by thousands before them.

And in the deepest silt, where the light does not reach, I see—or I imagine I see—a darkness that moves. A presence that is not the swimmers, not the fungal things, not the crystals or the stone or the water itself. Something that is the source of the singing, or that carries the singing, or that has become indistinguishable from the singing.

I do not know if it sees me.

I do not know if it knows I exist.

I do not know if the question means anything anymore.

*

The basin teaches patience. This is the lesson, if there is a lesson, if lessons are things that exist in a place where time moves at the speed of crystal growth and the cycles have no memory. Patience: the water rises and falls. Patience: the organisms breed and die. Patience: the fungal things advance a centimeter per year, or nothing at all, or something imperceptible, something that cannot be measured because measurement requires a scale that fits the timescale, and the timescale of the basin is not human.

I have patience now. I did not have it when I arrived—when I think I arrived, when the concept of arrival still meant something. I was urgent then. I wanted to understand, to map, to name, to make the basin legible to the mind I carried with me from the world above. I wanted to escape, or to stay, or to choose between escape and staying. I wanted to be something, to have an identity, to know what I was.

The basin has taken these wants. Or I have released them, the way I have released the water, the way everything releases what it carries eventually. What remains is not want. What remains is presence. Attention. The willingness to be here, in the damp and the dark, in the hum and the singing, in the place where the boundaries dissolve and the cycles turn and the water presses upward through the cracks with a patience that is older than stone.

I am small here. This is true, and it is not a problem. The walls press close, yes, and the moisture finds every seam, and the organisms watch with organs I cannot identify. But smallness is not insignificance. Smallness is the condition of most things that live. The edge-dwellers are small. The swimmers are small. The films and mats are made of things too small to see individually, only present in aggregate, only real as communities.

Perhaps I am a community now. Perhaps I am the aggregate of everything that has passed through me, every mineral and organic compound and trace of the basin's long history. Perhaps the self I was is dissolved in the silt, and what remains is the basin itself, looking out through eyes that used to be mine.

*

I do not know if I am inside something.

I used to think so. I used to think the basin was a container, a cathedral-sized enclosure of quarried stone, a space with walls and a rim and boundaries that separated the inside from the outside. The walls are there—I have touched them, felt their quarried edges worn smooth by years of the moisture, traced the chisel marks that someone left when they cut this place from the rock. The rim is there, somewhere above, where the gray light comes from and the air is perhaps different, perhaps drier, perhaps the world I came from.

But the walls are wet. The stone sweats. The cracks let water through, and through the cracks I can feel—can I feel it?—the presence of something beyond, something that is not stone, not water, not the basin itself. The earth, perhaps. The aquifer, or the tunnels, or the vast dark body that the basin is part of, the body that the basin was built to process, to filter, to transform.

Perhaps the basin is not inside something. Perhaps the basin is a node in a larger system, a point in a network of moisture and mineral and the slow cycling of matter through stone. Perhaps there is no inside and outside, only the movement of the water, the movement of the cycles, the movement of everything toward whatever it becomes.

Or perhaps—and this is the thought I return to, the thought that the singing seems to confirm, the thought that the basin's patience has taught me to hold—perhaps the inside and outside were never real. Perhaps they were stories we told to make the world manageable, categories we imposed on a continuity that does not respect categories. Perhaps the basin is not inside the earth, and I am not inside the basin, and the organisms are not inside me or the moisture inside them. Perhaps there is only the one thing, the wet dark thing, the cycling thing, and everything we perceive as separate is only the surface tension holding for a moment before it breaks, before the water joins the water, before the boundary dissolves and the cycle continues.

*

The singing rises from the deep silt.

I am sitting by the pool again, or I am the pool, or I am the sitting that has become indistinguishable from the stone and the moisture and the presence of things watching from the wet cracks. The singing rises and falls. The engines hum their frequency below thought. The water presses upward through the cracks, carrying whatever it carries from below, preparing to cycle again, to descend again, to return to the silt where the singing waits.

I do not know what is looking back.

But I am looking now. And what I see—what I am becoming part of—is a world so patient, so complete, so indifferent to the things that pass through it and so transformed by them, that the distinction between inside and outside, between observer and observed, between the basin and what it filters, has ceased to matter.

The water rises. The fungal things advance. The crystals grow their slow monuments to time.

And I am here, at the edge of the pool, looking into the dark water, and I am here the way the water is here, the way the singing is here, the way everything is here that has ever been here, cycling, pressing, patient, alive in whatever way a thing can be alive when it has forgotten the difference between living and not-living, when it has become the system itself, the closed thing that contains its own conditions, its own cycles, its own reasons for being.

The basin exhales.

I breathe with it.
