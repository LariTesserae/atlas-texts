You find yourself here the way water finds channels — not through choice but through the path of least resistance, which is also somehow the path of greatest patience. You did not climb to reach this place; you arrived. The way is already made, or perhaps there was no way until you arrived and your passage became the way, the slight compression of moss and felt-grass that will heal in hours, in days, in the slow patience of this place where hours and days are nearly the same.

Your clothes are damp. They have been damp since you arrived, and they will be damp when you leave, if you leave. The moisture is not uncomfortable — it is simply present, the way the light is present, the way the low hum is present beneath the sound of wind through ferns. You do not remember arriving wet. You remember arriving. The wet came after, or perhaps it was always there, and arrival was simply the moment you stopped being dry.

You are not the moss. Not yet. The moss is a many, a quilted accumulation of years, each layer compressing into the one below, sharing moisture and something that might be called information in the slow ripple of brightness when the sun breaks through cloud. You feel the moss beneath you, but you are above it, separate from it, still an interruption in the pattern. The moss will learn you if you stay long enough. It will absorb your impression, the slight depression of your weight, and it will not remember that you were ever there. This is the patience of the plateau — it does not forget, but it does not hold.

The hum is the first thing you learned here, before you had words for learning. It rises from the ground itself, or from beneath the ground, from the infrastructure that runs through the wet stone: pipes and pumps and something that ventilates or drains or cycles. The hum is geological now. It does not pulse or vary; it is as constant as the water weeping from rock faces, as constant as the wind breathing through dense vegetation. You hear it most clearly in the silences between sounds — when the wind softens, when the water sound deepens, in the moment between one bird-call and another. The hum continues. This is what it does. This is what it is.

You have learned to walk here without disturbing. This is not stealth — there is nothing to hide from, nothing to approach. It is simply the pace of the plateau itself, the slow measured movement of water through saturated ground, of moss accumulating, of rock weathering toward softness. Your footsteps are absorbed by the moss. The felt-surface holds your weight for a moment and releases, barely registering your passage. You could walk across this plateau and leave no permanent mark. You could walk across this plateau and no one would know. Perhaps no one does know. Perhaps you have always been here, in some way that predates your arrival, and your walking is simply the plateau dreaming itself forward.

The birds are the brightest sounds — sharp, bright notes that the moss drinks before they can echo. You hear them and look and find only the wet green and the grey sky and the sense that the sound has been absorbed into the landscape itself. The birds are small and brown, designed for concealment, and they are part of the palette. You are beginning to be part of the palette too. The wetness on your clothes, the damp in your hair, the way your skin has darkened and softened from the constant moisture — you are taking on the colors of this place. Soon you will not be distinguishable from the small presences that live at the edges of things, the not-quite-visible passages beneath the ferns, the slight compressions in the moss that mark the territory of things you cannot name.

The watcher is at the treeline. You feel it before you see it — a weight in the periphery, a stillness that is not the stillness of the wind or the water but the stillness of attention. It is patient. It has been patient for longer than the machinery has been humming, longer than the buildings have been sinking into the wet ground, longer perhaps than this plateau has been a plateau at all. It watches you the way you watch the plateau's slow processes — curious about what moves, about what changes, about what is new. You are new here. You have not yet accumulated into the pattern. You can still leave, if leaving is what you do. The watcher is curious whether you will leave, or whether you will become part of the patience, part of the slow becoming that is this place's only mode of being.

You sit. You do not sit often in the places you come from, but here sitting is appropriate — here it is the pace of things, to be still, to allow the landscape to move around you rather than through you. The ground is damp beneath you. The moss holds your shape for a moment and releases. The ferns at the edge of your vision rustle in a wind you cannot feel, or perhaps the wind is in you now, moving through you the way it moves through the dense green, the way it breathes through the plateau in long sustained exhales. You breathe with it. You did not decide to breathe with it. Your breath has simply found the rhythm of the place, the respiration that is the plateau's own slow vital process.

The light is relentless, but you have learned to look at it differently. Not to resist it but to let it fill you, the way the moisture fills everything here, the way the hum fills the silences. The light is not an assault; it is an offering. It is the sun at altitude, close and thin, and it is asking you to see clearly, to illuminate what is usually hidden in the comfortable shadows of lesser heights. You see the moss rippling with brightness. You see the water catching light on every surface. You see the paths left by animals, by people, by no one — the small channels where water moves beneath the vegetation, the slight depressions where the felt-ground has compressed over years, centuries, the deep patience of a landscape still becoming itself.

Time passes differently here. You do not know how long you have been sitting. The light has not changed in any way you can measure, but something has changed — some quality of attention, some shift in the way you are present to this place. You are beginning to understand what lives here lives slowly. Not because slowness is a virtue in the places you come from — it is not, or if it is, it is a virtue no one practices. But here slowness is the only option. The moss cannot grow faster. The water cannot flow faster. The rock cannot weather faster. The buildings are sinking, but they are sinking at the rate of wet wood and softened foundations, at the rate of centuries. If you stay long enough, you will sink too. You will accumulate into the pattern. You will become a presence rather than a presence here, a feature of the landscape rather than a visitor to it.

The watcher has not moved. The watcher is patient in a way that you are only beginning to learn. The watcher is the patience of the treeline, the patience of the thing that waits at the edge, that watches the open ground and the brightness and the brief bright presences that cross it. The watcher knows what you are becoming. The watcher has seen it before, will see it again. You stand. The moss releases your impression. The water sound continues, the wind continues, the hum continues beneath them both, the constant undertone of the machinery that has become geology, that will outlast the buildings and perhaps outlast the plateau itself, if the plateau ever changes in ways that would silence it. You walk. You walk toward the treeline, not toward the watcher but parallel to it, along the edge where the open ground gives way to the thickening vegetation. You do not know where you are going. The plateau does not have destinations. It has processes. It has slow movements. It has the patience of water finding channels, of moss finding purchase on bare rock, of the pattern learning to include new presences.

The watcher watches. You feel the weight of its attention, the stillness of its regard. It is not hostile. It is not welcoming. It is simply present, the way the hum is present, the way the moisture is present, the way you are beginning to be present. You pass the watcher at a distance. You do not look directly at it — looking directly would be a kind of challenge, a kind of claiming, and the plateau does not operate in claims. The watcher permits your passage. The watcher notes your direction. The watcher, if it thinks at all in the slow register of this place, is curious about where you will go, what you will become, whether you will remain or depart or accumulate into the pattern in ways that make departure impossible.

You reach the far edge of the plateau, where the land begins its descent toward the valley. The view is open — you can see the blue-grey line of the descent, the fragments of valley visible through gaps in the ridge. The wind is stronger here. The moisture is still present, but the light has a different quality, less reflected, more direct. You are at the edge of the plateau now, at the edge of yourself, at the edge of whatever you are becoming in this place.

Below, the valley is green and distant. Above, the sky is grey and close. Between, the plateau holds itself, patient, unfinished, waiting its turn. You are part of the waiting now. You are part of the patience. Your thoughts have slowed to the pace of the wind, to the pace of the water, to the deep undertone of the hum that is beneath everything and will continue beneath everything long after the wind has stilled and the water has found its final channels.

You turn back toward the interior. There is no reason to descend. There is no reason to ascend. There is only the plateau, and you are in it, and you are becoming it, and it is becoming you, and this is the only process that matters here, the only becoming that is happening, the slow incorporation of new presences into the pattern that has always been patient, always been raw, always been waiting its turn.

The watcher has not moved. The hum continues. The moss holds the memory of your passage, faintly, briefly, and then releases it. You are here. You are beginning to be here the way the moss is here, the way the water is here, the way the hum is here — not as a presence that arrives and departs, but as a presence that continues, that persists, that is simply part of what lives here, if living is what you call the thing that happens when something refuses to stop.
