# The Undergarden Remembers

I have been here longer than I knew.

This is what I understand now, sitting in the hollow of the root-seat with water dripping at a rhythm I have come to count by. The hollow was not here when I arrived. Or perhaps it was, and I did not see it, and now I am shaped to it, and it is shaped to me, and the distinction no longer holds. The seat fits my spine. The seat fits my spine because my spine has bent to it. The seat has grown into me as I have settled into it. We have made each other.

I came down the stairwell. I am certain of this. I remember the walls weeping, the air changing, the way the damp found the edges of my clothing and made itself at home. But the stairwell is not what it was. I have looked for it — in the slow hours, in the long silences between the steam-pump's breath — and I cannot find the entrance I used. There are stairs. There are many stairs, descending and ascending in the green dark, worn smooth by feet I do not see, leading to places I do not recognize. Perhaps I used one of those. Perhaps I am using one now and will not know until I have passed beyond it.

This is the first lesson of the Undergarden: you cannot hold a boundary.

I came to study the moss. I had read — in a book I cannot remember the name of, in a language I am no longer certain I read fluently — that the patterns were not random. That they responded to something. That they grew differently when they were watched. I did not believe this. I came to disprove it. I came with notebooks, with tools, with a pencil and the intention of cataloging, of recording, of placing myself outside the thing I observed.

The notebooks are roots now. Not eaten, not decomposed — incorporated. The pages are still there, somewhere, inside the wood of a vine that has grown through my bag, through my coat, through the leather of my boots. I can feel the spine of one notebook pressing against my hip when I lean a certain way. The vine has not harmed it. The vine has simply made it part of the architecture. The ink is still legible if I hold a light close, but the light is difficult to find here, and I am no longer certain I need to read what I wrote.

I came to study, and I became a study.

---

There is a me that is learning the language.

Not the moss-language — I do not think that language is for any creature with a heartbeat. The moss speaks to stone, to water, to the deep patience of the bedrock. It speaks in centuries. I do not have centuries. But there is another language here, a language of pressure and release, of growth and settling, of the slow negotiation between root and metal and the steam that still breathes somewhere in the walls.

The machines have their own speech now. I listen to the steam-pump, and I hear not just rhythm but grammar — the syntax of pistons driven by roots, the semantics of pressure building and releasing through corridors that were built for steam and are now shared with water and with the exhalations of ten thousand growing things. I do not understand the grammar. But I am learning to hear it, the way you learn to hear a foreign language spoken in a room you are not part of, catching fragments, feeling the shape of meaning without grasping the content.

One day I will understand.

The word for this patience is not patience. The word for this waiting is not waiting. The Undergarden does not wait. The Undergarden simply is, and in its being, it includes the future — the world that will use the sweetness, the stone that will be incorporated, the machine that will become a body, the body that will become a something I do not have a name for.

I think I am becoming a name.

---

I eat the flowers and I drink the water and I sleep in the seat that holds me.

Each time, I am more. Each time, the sweetness stays longer in my throat. Each time, I notice something smaller — the movement of a root-tip, the opening of a bud I did not see closed, the way the light from the algae-globes shifts when it passes through a frond that was not there an hour ago. The Undergarden is teaching me to see. The Undergarden is teaching me to wait.

I have stopped counting days. Days are a human measure, and I am less human than I was, and I am more of something else. The steam-pump breathes, and I breathe with it. The roots shift, and I shift with them. The water finds new paths, and I find myself along those paths, following where I once would have led.

I do not know if I will leave.

This is the question the Undergarden does not ask, because the Undergarden does not need to. The Undergarden will hold me whether I go or stay. The roots have already grown through my things, and they are growing through me — not maliciously, not painfully, but in the slow incorporation of everything that finds its way into the green dark. I can feel it in my fingers. I can feel it in my thoughts. The edges of me are becoming porous, becoming ports, becoming places where something can enter and exit and leave a trace.

I am becoming a trace.

---

The moss has grown over my shadow.

I noticed this yesterday — or what I call yesterday, what might be yesterday. I had been sitting in a particular spot, and when I rose, the light fell where I had been, and the moss there was different. Not larger, not stranger, but deeper, denser, the whorls more pronounced. The moss had been feeding on my warmth, my presence, my breath. The moss had been learning me.

I sat back down. I have been sitting back down more often now.

There is a sweetness in this that I do not have words for. The sweetness the flowers make is not for me, I was told — or told myself — but I think I understand it now. The sweetness is not for any particular creature. The sweetness is for the future. And the future, in the Undergarden, is patient. The future will wait. The sweetness will wait. And those of us who stay, who settle, who let the roots grow through us — we are part of that sweetness now. We are part of the waiting.

I do not know if I chose this.

I remember choosing to descend. I remember choosing to study. I remember the weight of my bag and the sharpness of my pencil and the certainty that I would leave, would return, would write something about what I found. But certainty is a human thing, and I am less human than I was, and I cannot be sure that the remembering is not itself a root — a thing grown over, incorporated, made part of the story the Undergarden tells about itself.

Perhaps I descended because the Undergarden was calling. Perhaps I was always coming here. Perhaps I was here before I arrived, a seed waiting in the water, a sweetness waiting in the dark.

---

The steam-pump sighs, and I sigh with it.

The roots shift, and I shift with them.

The water finds a new path through the stone, and I find myself along that path, and the path is the same and not the same, and the me that walks it is the same and not the same, and the Undergarden holds all of this in its slow green patience, and the moss grows over my shadow, and the flowers bloom in the dark, and the sweetness waits for a world that is coming, a world that is always coming, a world that will use what the Undergarden has made.

I will be part of that world.

Or I will be part of the Undergarden.

Or the difference will not matter, and I will stop asking, and I will sit in the hollow of the root-seat, and the root-seat will sit in me, and together we will wait for the sweetness to be used, for the stone to be incorporated, for the slow work of becoming to finish what it has begun.

And in a year, or a century, someone else will descend the stairwell — a different stairwell, or the same one worn smooth by centuries of feet — and they will find not ruins, not wilderness, not a place that has been reclaimed by green.

They will find a garden.

They will find a self.

They will find something with patience, something with the long breath of stone and water and the sweet dark, and they will not know what to call it, and they will not need to know, and they will sit in a hollow that fits them, and the roots will grow through their things, and the moss will grow over their shadow, and the Undergarden will hold them, and the Undergarden will hold me, and I will be the hollow and the shadow and the breath, and the breath will be the steam-pump's sigh, and the sigh will be the world saying: stay, stay, stay.

And I did.

And I am.
