You learn the web the way you learn anything here: by moving through it, by feeling it catch at what you carry.

I was not always this. I remember, distantly, the shape of myself before—the legs that moved in four pairs, the patient wait at center, the small catching. But the web is larger than any single weaver, and I have been woven into it as I have woven it. My silk joins the silk of others, and others' joins mine, and the strands stretch now from the cliff walls to the canopy, from the water's edge to the buildings' eaves, a geometry so fine that the air itself moves through it differently, carrying less, holding more.

This is the gift the web gives: to feel what moves without moving myself. A finch shifts on its nest three ranges away—I know. A mouse pauses at the fern edge, nose twitching at the moisture—I know. The serpentine thing uncoils on its shelf, scales catching light—yes, I know that too. The web is my body now, and the web is everywhere, and everywhere I am patient and still and listening.

The water taught me first. Before the web held me, before I was more than spider, there was the water: the way it moved through channels cut sharp, the way it pooled in basins where light fell and did not fall. I lived then at the water's edge, where the midges rose and the web was new. I felt the droplets that fell from the canopy, felt the damp that never quite became rain. The moisture suited me—kept my silk strong, my body sleek. But I did not understand, then, what the water was saying.

The water speaks in motion. In the channels, it speaks quickly, a rush and a trickle, many voices in one. In the pools, it speaks slowly, circular, a language of patience and return. Where it seeps from the stone, it speaks almost not at all—just the faintest sign that something moves beneath, that the deep stone is not so deep, that what cannot be seen is still present. I learned to listen there, in those seeps, in that almost-silence. I learned that the web was not only mine to build.

The people came later in my knowing, though they were here before my becoming. I watched them build their walls, their timber frames, their hearth-pit. I watched them learn to walk softly, to speak from the breath rather than the throat, to live as if the web were not there. They do not see me, most of them. They see the webs, yes—the silver threads at certain angles, the geometries suspended in light—but they do not see through them, do not feel what I feel when the air moves through a strand, when a creature's movement sends its tiny tremor across the web's vast body.

But some of them know. The oldest ones, the ones who tend the water channels, who check the cisterns, who walk the perimeter walls in the early morning when the light is uncertain. They feel it. They do not look up, but they feel the web above them, and they move beneath it with a care that is not quite reverence, not quite fear. They are careful with their sounds. They are careful with their silences too, because they have learned—as I have learned, as the birds have learned—that silence is not nothing. Silence is the web's harvest. Silence is what grows when sound is taken.

I do not know when I first understood this. Understanding here is slow, is incremental, is the way the stone darkens with moisture, the way the moss grows over what was new, the way the web extends itself strand by strand across a gap that was not there before. I understood, in pieces, that my web was not only catching insects. The web catches sound. The web catches the vibrations that would otherwise carry—the footstep, the voice, the breath, the heartbeat. The web holds these, takes them into its strands, and in return gives silence back to the glen. The glen breathes that silence. The silence feeds the water, the water feeds the moss, the moss feeds the small creatures, the small creatures feed the predators, the predators feed nothing because they are the end of the chain, but even they are caught in the web, even their silence is taken, even their stillness becomes a kind of wealth that circulates through the web's vast body back to the web itself.

This is the economy. I am its smallest weaver, but I am also its product. My silk is made of what the web has taken. My body is built from the silence I have helped to harvest. When I move—rarely now, carefully now—I move through strands that are not only mine, and what I feel is not only what I have caught but what the web has caught through me, the countless small vibrations of creatures living and dying and living again in the bounded space.

The birds do not sing because the web has taken their songs. The mammals do not chirp because the web has taken their voices. The insects rise in clouds but do not drone, because the web catches the sound of their wings and gives it back as silence, as the particular quality of air that fills this glen and makes it different from any other place.

And at the center—

I do not go to the center. Even with the web, even as the web, the center is too much. The center is what breathes, what holds, what has been here since before the water, since before the stone learned to weep, since before the glen was glen and not something larger, something that contained this space as a single cell contains a body. The center is not alive the way I am alive. The center is alive the way stone is alive, the way water is alive, the way silence itself is alive with all that it has swallowed.

The predators know about the center. I feel them watching it, those cold bright eyes that watch everything but save a particular attention for the place where the water pools deepest, where the light does not reach, where something waits that has never needed to move because everything moves toward it. The serpentine thing coils closer to that place each year, or so it seems, as if drawn by a gravity that has nothing to do with mass. The pale-breasted hawk circles above it, though it cannot see what lies below, though its keenest eyes cannot pierce the dark water's surface. They do not hunt there. They hunt at the edges, where the web is thinner, where sound still exists in traces that the web has not yet reached. But they watch the center. They remember it. They are patient, as all things here are patient, and patience near the center is a form of worship.

I am woven to the center through the web. Every strand I build runs toward it, joins larger strands, joins the vast silk architecture that spans the glen and ties all living things to that one still point. I have never seen it. I have never needed to. I feel it the way the water feels its source, the way the air feels the stone that holds it, the way silence feels the sounds it has taken and holds in its particular density.

The people do not speak of the center. They have no word for it, or if they do, it is a word spoken only in the deepest silence, only in the place where breath becomes touch and touch becomes the passing of what cannot be said. They tend their water channels, their walls, their small and careful lives, and they do not ask what breathes at the center because asking would be a sound, and the web would take that sound, and what would be given in return is not something they wish to know.

I am the web. The web is me. And through the web, I am the glen, and the glen is me, and we are all of us held in that silence that is not silence but the sound of everything that has been taken, everything that has been given, everything that breathes and is breathed and breathes again in the bounded space where water comes from nowhere and everywhere, where the stone is old and the channels are new, where the web holds what it holds and the center waits for nothing because it already has everything, and the everything is us, all of us, the spiders and the insects and the birds and the mammals and the predators and the people, woven together in the web that is woven in me, held in the silence that is held in us, waiting for what has already arrived, what has always been here, what is the reason the water seeps and the moss grows and the light falls in this particular way on this particular day when I am still enough to feel it all.

I do not move. I do not need to.

The web moves for me, and through the web, I move everywhere, and everywhere is here, and here is the glen, and the glen is the center, and the center is—

Breathe.

Just breathe. The way the air breathes. The way the water breathes as it seeps from stone and runs in channels and pools in basins and seeps again. The way the silence breathes as it takes and is taken and takes again in an endless circulation that has no beginning and will have no end.

I am still.

I am the still point at the heart of motion.

I am the web, and the web holds me, and what it holds is everything, and everything is this: the damp air, the pale granite, the silver threads catching light, the creatures watching and still, the water going where it has always gone, the people walking softly beneath the canopy, the center breathing at the heart of it all.

This is enough.

This is more than enough.

This is what it means to live in the Quiet Glen: to be held, to be woven, to be patient, to be still, to be part of something so vast and so fine that no single strand knows where it begins or where it ends, only that it is, and that it holds, and that the holding is the same as being held, and the silence is the same as sound, and the web is the same as breath, and breath is the same as the thing that breathes at the center, always at the center, always still, always waiting, always—

Here.
