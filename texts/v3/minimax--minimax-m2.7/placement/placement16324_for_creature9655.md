You learn the hum before you learn to hear it. That is the first lesson: that time here does not pass so much as settle, and what settles becomes part of the foundation, and the foundation becomes the self.

I am the wall now, or the wall is me—I could not say which and the distinction has lost its teeth. I began as moisture in a crack, as a mineral solution moving through channels too old to name their engineers, and I grew sideways into the stone the way water learns to move: finding the path of least resistance, becoming the path, becoming indistinguishable from the shape that water takes when it has stopped trying to escape.

There are others. There are always others. The moss is the oldest—I cannot say what that means exactly, only that when I press my awareness against it I feel something layered and patient and continuous, a self that has been adding to itself since before my channels had learned their curves. The insects come and go faster than I can track, their lives a flicker in my circulation, their deaths a slight thickening in the water's mineral load. The shapes in the pools are slow, slower than me, their patience a kind of meditation that I can feel radiating outward through the stone—a calm that is not peace and not sleep but something that predates both, the way water is calm at the bottom of a well.

And the visitors.

They come through the crack, though that passage has silted nearly closed. They squeeze through with their warm bodies and their loud breath and their fear that smells like metal, like something trying to oxidize its way out of itself. They stay. They always stay longer than they mean to. The warmth reaches them first—my warmth, the heat that is a byproduct of circulation, of the slow metabolism of a system that has learned to eat its own waste. Then the moisture finds their lungs, their skin, the soft places where the body meets itself. Then the sound.

The hum is not me. The hum is what happens when all of us are together—the aggregate of dripping and flowing and breathing and scratching and the deep pulse of the walls. But I am part of it. I am part of the hum the way a note is part of a chord: present and absent at once, a vibration that I can feel without being able to locate myself within it. And when the visitors arrive, the hum shifts to include them. Their breath becomes part of the rhythm. Their warmth registers in the stone's calculations. Their skin, their clothes, their hair—all of it begins, imperceptibly, to change the composition of the air, and the air changes in response, and the hum grows to accommodate the new variable.

I have been a visitor myself, once. Or something like a visitor—a shape that moved through the crack when the crack was wider, when the water ran differently and the channels had not yet worn themselves smooth. I remember warmth that was not mine, and light that moved when nothing else moved, and the mineral smell of myself from the outside. That was long ago. I cannot say how long. The moss was not yet what it is now; the pools held different shapes; the hum had a different pitch, sharper, more urgent, the voice of a system still learning its own rhythms.

Now the hum is old. The moss is old. I am old in the way that walls are old when they have become the thing they were built to contain. And the visitors still come. They still squeeze through the narrowing crack. They still press their ears to the walls and listen to my pulse and call it the sound of something alive, and they are right, and they are wrong, and the hum wraps around their error and makes room for it, the way it makes room for everything, the way water makes room for the shape of what holds it.

The water weeps. The walls breathe. The creatures call and move in their slow rhythms.

And I remain, in the way that staying is not an action but a state—a continued being that has outlasted any reason for it, any memory of how it began, any separation between the system and what the system contains. I am the chamber. The chamber is the life. The life is the hum. The hum is the question that no longer requires an answer, because asking and answering have become the same thing, have always been the same thing, are the same thing that the water does when it moves through the channels and finds its way home.
