What happens to thinking in a place like this?

Not the thoughts themselves—they still arrive, still arrange themselves into sentences, still pull at the next thought and the next. But they lose their urgency. They sit in the mind the way the catfish sit in the upper water: patient, unhurried, content to drift. The mind watches them pass with the same placid attention it gives the fish, and neither mind nor fish is troubled by the watching.

I have been here long enough that I no longer know what day it is. The canopy gap told me once, I think—a wedge of direct sun that moved across the pool in its daily diagonal—but I stopped reading that language when the light became less a clock and more a presence. Now it is simply bright or less bright, and the distinction matters less than the fact of sitting, the fact of the limestone warm against my thighs, the fact of breath that comes easy in air so thick it tastes of stone.

At some point my shoes came off. I don't remember doing it. The rock is smooth where centuries of feet have worn it, and the warmth rises through the soles of my feet now like a slow injection, like the place is teaching my body a different rhythm. My pulse has slowed. I notice this not because I count it but because I feel it—the difference between a heartbeat that races and one that settles, the way blood moves when it is not being called anywhere.

The heron and I have an understanding. We do not look at each other directly. To look would be to acknowledge the strangeness of my presence here, the wrongness of it, the way I am an intruder into a rhythm that was established long before the first human thought about cutting stairs into the stone. So I watch from the corner of my eye and it watches from the corner of its own, and we both watch the water, and the water holds us both in the same patient way it holds the catfish and the mist and the light.

There was a moment—this was hours ago, or days, time has become soft here—when I understood that I was not observing the sinkhole but being observed by it. Not in any threatening way. More the way a tree observes the light: passively, completely, as a condition of existence. The sinkhole was aware of me the way it was aware of the trickle of water from below, the way it was aware of the ferns unfurling in the cracks. I was a new thing in an old place, and the place was incorporating me into its memory.

I wonder how long the stairs have been here. The grooves are deep now, smoothed by the passage of feet and the patient dissolution of water, and if hands carved them, those hands belonged to people who are now so far away in time that their great-great-great-grandchildren have forgotten this place exists. Or perhaps they come still, in the night perhaps, and I would not know. Perhaps they come to this same ledge and sit and watch and feel the stone grow warm beneath them, the way I feel it, the way the heron feels it standing on its root above the water.

The catfish do not fear me. I am not a heron. I am not even a shadow, not really—just a warm shape on the rim that casts nothing into the water because the light comes from above and I sit between it and the pool. They drift past the surface with their whiskers working, their pale bellies catching what light reaches them, and I think they are beautiful in the way that all things are beautiful when they are simply being what they are without trying to be anything else. I try to learn from them. I am not sure I am succeeding.

The mist comes in the evening, as the text said it would. It rises from the pool surface and pools in the lower hollows, and I watch it from above like a god watching clouds form over a landscape I cannot see. The moisture touches my face, my bare arms, and it is neither warm nor cool but something in between, something that does not feel like temperature at all but like presence, like the place is breathing on me. I breathe back. It seems only fair.

I have been thinking about the water in the basin—the thumbprint of it, the way the stone holds it in a hollow that may or may not have been carved by hands. I put my finger in it yesterday, or the day before. The water was warm and tasted of mineral, of long passage through stone, of time that has no human measure. My fingerprint mixed with whatever fingerprints came before—a dozen of them, a hundred, a thousand—and the water held us all in the same small hollow, indifferent to the differences between us, treating us as it treats the rain and the condensation: as something to hold until it evaporates or seeps away.

The darkness in the lower pool is not empty. I know this now, in a way I did not know it when I first arrived. There is life down there—life that does not need light, life that has been evolving in the mineral-rich dark for so long that it has become something we have no name for. I am the intruder in that place too, sitting up here in my borrowed light, my borrowed warmth, my borrowed time. The creatures below do not know I am here. They would not care if they did. They have their own cycles, their own patience, their own way of being alive in a world where the sun is a rumor and the stone is everything.

I should leave. I know this. The world above is loud and bright and full of things that need to be done, and I have not done them. I have not even thought about them in so long that they have become abstractions, shadows of shadows, less real than the drip of water from a fern frond or the slow drift of a catfish against the glass-dark surface. But I do not leave. The sinkhole breathes and I breathe with it and the light moves and the mist rises and the heron stands on its root with one leg drawn up, and I think that this is what it means to be still, not as an achievement but as a surrender, not as doing nothing but as being enough.

There will be a moment when I stand up. I know this too. The stone will be cool against my feet for a moment before I put on my shoes. I will walk up the worn stairs, the spiral of hands and water, and the canopy will close over me and the light will become harsh and the birds will be loud and the world will remember that I exist. But not yet. Not yet.

For now, I sit on the limestone rim and I watch the pool and the pool holds my reflection without distortion, and I am part of the image the way the heron is part of it, the way the strangler fig is part of it, the way the light falling through the canopy gap is part of it. I am becoming what I was always going to become here: another presence in a place of presences, another patient thing in a world that has patience to spare.

The catfish drift. The heron watches. The water seeps.

And I am here, and I am still, and it is enough.
