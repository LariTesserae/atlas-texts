# On the Plateau

You arrive at Verath not knowing you have arrived, because there is no edge to cross, no gate, no line between the lower air and the high. The transition is slow—the sub-arid basins give way to the first outriders of the storm systems, and the air thickens, and the stone begins to show its colonization before you realize you have entered a different country. By the time the cliffs appear through the horizontal rain, you are already drenched, already leaning into the wind, already part of the landscape you came to find.

What you notice first is not the water but the sound of it. The storms have not yet reached full fury—it is early in the season—but they are building, and the roar that comes from the eastern horizon has a weight to it, a presence that you feel in your chest before you hear it with your ears. It is not like the sound of rain on a roof, not like the sound of a river or a waterfall. It is the sound of volume, of mass in motion, of atmosphere doing what atmosphere does when it meets resistance and does not stop. You stand at the edge of a shadow-valley and listen, and the sound fills the space inside your skull, and you understand that you are hearing something that has been happening for longer than your species has existed.

The stone here is not like stone elsewhere. It has been worn smooth, not by water alone but by three billion years of thermal cycling, of freezing and thawing, of expansion and contraction until the angles have surrendered, until the surfaces flow like glass that has forgotten it was ever liquid. You touch it, and your hand comes away wet, and you feel the cold of it, and you think: this is what patience looks like when it has had time to become a landscape. The hardness that remains is not the hardness of the original mountain but the hardness of what refused to yield, the quartz veins and basalt dykes that stand now as the only vertical elements in a world that is becoming horizontal, that has been becoming horizontal since before the first cells divided in the ancient seas that no longer exist.

You begin to walk. The path—you are making the path as you walk it, because there are no paths here, only the direction of least resistance, the way the water has carved and you follow—leads down into one of the shadow-valleys, and the light changes. Above, the sky was gray and uniform, but here the walls of the valley filter it into something green and diffuse, a light that has passed through water and moss and the moisture on ten thousand leaves before reaching you. It is the light of the deep ocean, if the ocean had walls and moved in currents of air instead of water. You feel the pressure of it, the weight of the canopy above, the thickness of the living things that have made this valley their own.

The trees here grow low, as they must. You see one that has taken hold in a crevice where soil has accumulated grain by grain over what might be centuries, its roots wrapped around a stone the way fingers grip a rail on a moving train, the way living things grip the world that contains them. It is small by lowland standards—a scrub pine, a twisted juniper—but you can see in its rings, in the growth patterns visible in the bark, that it has been here a long time, that it has survived what would have killed lesser organisms, that it has learned the plateau's rhythm and made itself into an instrument that plays it. Its branches lean east, permanent genuflection toward the storms, and its needles catch the water and channel it away from its center, preventing the rot that would come from holding too much of what it needs to survive.

You stop at a pool where the braided river widens and slows, its waters dark with tannins, its surface disturbed by the rise and fall of insects. The fish are there—you can see them if you are still, if you wait, if you become part of the silence that the pool holds in its deepest parts. They are pale, these highland fish, unpigmented by generations of living beneath surfaces that never break, and they are slow, moving with the patience of creatures that have no predators, that channel all their energy into longevity rather than size. You watch one rise to take something from the surface, a hatchling or a fragment of organic matter, and you think: this is what a hundred years of living looks like when you have learned to take your time.

The insects come in the afternoon, as the thermal currents rise. They are everywhere, a cloud that pulses and shifts, that catches the filtered light until it seems to be a living thing, an organism made of millions of individuals following the same ancient imperative toward warmth and toward each other. You stand in the middle of it and feel them move around you, their bodies touching your skin, their wings making a sound that is not quite a sound, a vibration that is part of the larger vibration of water and stone and the slow breathing of the valley. You are not separate from them, not really. You are part of the same system, the same hydrology, the same conversation between the forces that flatten and the forces that climb.

Night comes slowly here, or rather, the light changes in stages: first the dimming of the diffuse green, then the arrival of the stars through gaps in the canopy, then the full emergence of the sky when you climb to one of the exposed ridges and look up. The stars are sharp, sharper than you have ever seen them, and they are close, close enough that you can see the distances between them, the space that light has traveled to reach you. You lie on the wet stone and listen. The storms have retreated to build strength elsewhere, and the only sounds are the small sounds: the creak of the vegetation, the rush of the rivers, the high songs of insects that sing only in the dark. And beneath these, the silence—that silence that is not the silence of empty rooms but the silence of distance, of time measured in light-years, of a universe that is vast and indifferent and here, here, here, all at once.

You think about the builders. They came late, these industrial cultures, and they built with the same ambition as the natural forces, the same disregard for depth of time. The concrete platform near the southern cliff edge, its surface cracked by frost wedging, its machinery unreadable. The bridge supports ending in mid-air where the far side of the gorge has collapsed. The building foundations absorbed into the stone, their walls now part of the rock, their floors indistinguishable from the natural surface beneath. They thought they were permanent, probably. They thought they had found a way to outlast the erosion, to build something that would remain. And they were right, in a way. They remain, but not as they were. They remain as the stone remains, as part of the larger system, as evidence that the plateau absorbs everything, slowly, patiently, with the certainty of geological time.

You think about the shadow-dwellers, the creatures in the valleys where the sun does not reach. They have made a home in the darkness, and they have evolved in response to it: huge eyes, reflective surfaces, bodies built for silence on the wet stone. They are hard to see, adapted to a life in the margins and the shadows, and they will probably never be known by anyone who does not specifically come looking for them. But they are here, doing what living things do: persisting, changing, fitting themselves to the shape of the world that contains them. They are the plateau's way of saying: *I am still here. I am still becoming.*

And you realize, lying on the wet stone under the sharp stars, that you are part of this. Not as the builders were part of it, not as something separate trying to make a mark that will outlast the erosion. But as the lichens are part of it, the slow negotiation between organisms that have learned to disagree so gracefully they have become indistinguishable. As the fish are part of it, channeling energy into longevity, learning to take your time. As the trees are part of it, growing in the direction of most patience, gripping the bones of the mountain with a patience that matches the erosion itself.

You are the plateau's way of knowing itself. Its way of having a perspective on its own passage from mountain to plain. And the perspective is not separate from the thing it sees; it is made of the same stone, the same water, the same three billion years of patience and erosion and growth. You are here, and you are becoming, and the becoming is not yours alone but the plateau's, the system's, the conversation that has been going on so long it has become the only conversation possible.

The storms build again in the east, the roar returning, the percussion of waterfalls running sideways across the ancient stone. You rise from the wet stone, and you walk back down into the valley where the water moves in braided rivers and the trees grip the rock with their patient roots. The rain comes, not yet horizontal, not yet the full fury of the storm season, but enough to wet everything, to fill the air with the sound of water on moss, on lichen, on stone. You stand in it, and you let it come, and you think: this is what it is to be here. Not to survive, not to endure, but to be part of the system that does both, that has made both possible, that will continue long after the memory of your passing has been worn smooth by the same forces that wore the mountain to this plateau, this almost-plain, this place where the water comes down and the stone waits, with the patience of stone, to become something else.
