I arrive here the way water arrives in the channels: slowly, without decision.

There is no moment of arrival, no crossing of a threshold. One moment the air is different—thicker, warmer, carrying a sweetness that might be mist or might be breath or might be the stone exhaling something that has no name—and then it is simply the only air there is. My lungs do not know what to do with it. They strain against the density, pulling at something that resists, that fills them before they can fill it. I think of drowning. I am not wrong.

The light arrives from above, but it arrives sideways, diffused through mist that clings to everything. My eyes adjust slowly. At first everything is green-dark, a darkness that is not absence but presence, the color of the canopy swallowing what it does not need. Then shapes emerge: the terraces stepping up the ridge, the bridges arcing between them, the channels below that do not flow but *seep*, circulation so gradual it might be stillness. The stone beneath my feet is powdery when I touch it. Chalk. Still warm. Still learning what it wants to be.

I am not native here. I know this by the way my body fights the air, by the way the humidity settles on my skin like something possessive, by the way sound reaches me—absorbed by moss, reflected off water, arriving soft and strange. But I am here. The water is here. The terraces are here. And the hum is here, beneath everything, patient as the water itself.

I begin to climb.

---

The terraces do not feel like architecture. Architecture is static, a statement made in stone: *we were here, we were strong, we meant something.* These do not state. They *tend*. The packed earth is held not by will but by attention, by the copper hammers worn smooth at the handles, by the stone blades replaced before they dull. A child runs past me—small, pale, calves like cords—and does not look up. She belongs here. Her lungs are flared, her eyes quick in the dim light. She moves between tiers the way water moves through channels: without hesitation, following the path of least resistance that she has never had to learn because she was born knowing.

I follow her with my eyes. I do not follow her with my body. My body is still foreign here, still pulling at air that does not want to be pulled, still feeling the humidity as weight rather than home. But something in me is beginning to listen. The terraces tell me what they need, if I am quiet enough to hear. The stone tells me where it is weak—the small fissures, the hairline cracks, the places where the heat below presses upward like breath held too long. The water tells me where it is going: always higher, always slower, always finding the soft places and entering them.

I stop on a bridge. Cedar and vine, woven thick, flexing slightly beneath my weight. The channels below are dark and still, their surfaces holding the faint green glow that will intensify as evening comes. Something moves in the deeper water—a slow displacement, a shadow passing—and then is gone. No one looks. No one speaks of it. This is not avoidance; it is simply the understanding that some things exist below the level of speech, in the hum itself, in the warmth that rises from nowhere anyone can name.

I listen.

---

The voice comes from behind me, soft and unfinished, trailing into the mist like something reluctant to end. It takes me a moment to understand that it is directed at me.

"—and you are not from the channels."

I turn. An elder, slight and pale, standing on the terrace above the bridge. Her eyes are dark and quick, adapted to dim light, and they do not look up. They look at me, at the way I stand, at the way my shoulders rise and fall with breaths that are too deliberate, too willed.

"No," I say. My voice sounds wrong here—too loud, too certain. The moss absorbs it, the water reflects it strangely, and I understand for the first time why the people here speak softly, why their sentences are begun and released, taken up again by another voice. Sound carries strangely in a place where the air is full.

She does not seem surprised. She does not seem anything. She simply looks at me, and waits.

"I came from the peaks," I say, trying to soften my voice, trying to let it trail. "I think. I don't remember the peaks anymore. I remember water, and then the water was here, and then—"

I stop. The sentence does not want to end. It wants to dissolve into the mist, to become part of the murmuration, the shared meaning that requires listening more than hearing.

She nods. Slowly. The way terraces settle.

"The water brings," she says. "The water takes. The terraces hold."

I do not know if this is a question, a statement, or a prayer. It is spoken in the passive voice, as if the world is happening to her rather than the other way around. As if *bringing* and *taking* and *holding* are properties of the place, like the color of the light or the warmth that rises from below. I begin to understand that this is not defeat. It is fluency. She is speaking the language of Ahivatha, and I am only beginning to learn it.

---

I stay.

I do not decide to stay. The word *decide* implies agency, implies that I could have chosen otherwise, and I am no longer certain this is true. The humidity settles into my lungs and my lungs stop fighting. The dim light settles into my eyes and my eyes stop straining. The terraces settle into my hands—I learn to hold the copper hammer, to feel its weight, to sense the small shifts in the stone that tell me where the foundations need deepening, where the drainage needs adjusting, where the earth is breathing.

The knowledge does not come as words. It comes as feel, as attention, as the slow accumulation of seasons spent listening. An elder shows me how to read the fissures in the stone, and I watch, and I learn, and eventually I show someone else, and the terraces grow not by being built but by being *cultivated*, tended the way a garden is tended, with patience and the understanding that the garden is also cultivating me.

I am soft now. My voice is soft, my movements are soft, my knowledge is soft—flexible, provisional, ready to bend without breaking. I speak in the passive voice, sometimes. I say: *the channels widened*, and I do not say: *we widened them*. I say: *the stone warmed*, and I do not say: *the heat rose*. The world is happening to me, and I am happening to it, and the distinction no longer seems to matter.

---

The children play in the shallows. I watch them sometimes, from the higher terraces, where the air is thinner and the mist is thinner and the sound of the water is fainter. They reach for the iridescent things that grow where the roots descend into the water, the membranous things that have no names, that are neither plant nor animal nor anything I can categorize. The children know which ones to touch and which ones to avoid. This knowledge is old and specific, passed from hands to hands across the tiers.

I was not here when this knowledge was first gained. I do not know who touched the wrong thing and what happened to them, or who first learned that some of these things are nourishing and some are dangerous. The stories vary. Some say the knowledge came with the first people, carved into their lungs before they climbed down from the peaks. Some say it was learned slowly, generation by generation, a child touching a thing and another child watching and neither of them dying and the knowledge passing forward like a hammer, worn smooth by use.

The children do not fear the water. It is warm, and familiar, and has been there since before they were born. I was like them once, or so I imagine—fearless in a world that would eventually teach me to be careful, to be attentive, to measure my words in the passive voice. I do not remember being fearless. I do not remember being otherwise. But I am here now, and the water is here, and the hum is here, and when the hum stops, I will not be here to notice, and this is not tragedy but simply the nature of a world that is still becoming.

---

The phosphorescence rises in the evening.

I stand on a bridge and watch it: the faint green glow that comes from below, that turns the channels into mirrors, that makes the entire drowned valley dream of itself. The terraces are reflected in the water, and the water is reflected in the terraces, and the distinction between them becomes unclear—the city above and the city below, equally real, equally patient.

I am reflected too, I suppose. A shape in the old-glass light, a body that has become soft in the way of this place. I do not know what I look like from below. I do not know if the reflection is me or something I am becoming. The water takes what it is given, and the terraces hold what they are given, and I have given myself to both, slowly, without decision, the way water fills a channel, the way moss covers a stone, the way the hum fills everything that is willing to listen.

The hum is louder in the evening. Not loud—just *more*. A presence at the edge of hearing, the sound a world makes when it is still learning how to exist. I used to think it was the heat in the stone, the process that pushes warmth through fissures, that makes the water warm and the stone chalky and the channels slowly wider each decade. But I am no longer certain. The hum sounds like thought. Not human thought—something slower, something vaster, something that has no need for words because words are too fast, too sharp, too willing to end. The hum does not end. It simply continues, patient as the water, patient as the terraces, patient as the people who tend them.

---

I am building a bridge.

Not alone—never alone. There are others on the terrace, adjusting the vine cables, testing the woven cedar for rot, measuring the angle that will allow the bridge to flex without breaking when the stone shifts beneath it. My hands know the work. The copper hammer is smooth in my grip, worn by generations of palms, and when I swing it I am not one person but many: all the others who held this hammer, who tended this terrace, who listened to the stone and heard what it needed.

The stone here is soft. It crumbles under my fingers, powdery, unfused, still learning its own strength. We cannot build for permanence. We build for the next rebuilding, for the next adjustment, for the next generation that will tend what we have tended. This is not a limitation. It is a philosophy, written not in words but in the flex of bridges and the slope of drainage channels and the particular angle at which a terrace must step to accommodate a hillside that is still being born.

The work is the point. I understood this slowly, the way I understood everything here: not all at once, but in the accumulation of seasons, in the copper hammer worn smooth, in the stone blade replaced before it dulled. The terraces are not a monument to human will. They are a monument to human patience, to the understanding that some places require you to become something softer in order to survive them.

I have become softer. I do not say this with pride or regret. I say it the way I would say: *the stone is warm*, or *the water is rising*. A fact of the place. A fact of myself. The two are no longer distinct.

---

The old people go to the highest ridge.

I will go there too, one day, when my lungs have grown too large for the lower terraces, when my eyes have grown too quick for the dim light, when my hands have built and rebuilt enough bridges that they know the work is never done and the work is enough. I will sit on the highest habitable ridge and watch the channels below, the bridges I helped build and rebuild, the terraces I have tended my whole life. I will not speak of regret. I will not speak of accomplishment. I will speak of the work that remains, always remains, the work that will outlast me the way it outlasted those who came before.

When I die, I will be wrapped in woven reed and lowered into the channels, weighted with stones that are already smooth from other use. The water will take me slowly, carry me down through the tiers to the deeper channels where the fish move and the light does not reach. This is the end I have prepared for, the only end I know. I will become part of the water, part of the hum, part of the warmth that rises from below and no one can explain.

I do not fear this. I do not hope for it. I simply know it, the way I know the channels will widen and the terraces will sink and the world will become something it does not yet know how to be. The water is here, and so am I. The hum is here, and so am I. And when the hum stops, I will not be here to notice, and when the terraces are swallowed, I will not be here to rebuild, and when the water rises to the highest peak, it will not be a tragedy. It will simply be a change, a widening of a channel, a flex of a bridge, a world becoming what it was always going to become.

I am learning the language of Ahivatha. I am learning to speak in the passive voice, to let my sentences trail into the mist, to hold my stories loosely, the way I hold everything. I am learning that some knowledge cannot be written down, only passed from hands to hands, only demonstrated, only *cultivated*, the way a garden is cultivated, with patience and the understanding that the garden is also cultivating you.

The water rises gently around me. The terraces hold me. The hum is beneath everything, patient as the water itself.

I am here.
