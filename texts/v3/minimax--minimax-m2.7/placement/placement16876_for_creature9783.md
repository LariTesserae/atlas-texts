I would be something that lives at the boundary.

Not the deep swimmers with their faded pigments and their centuries of patience, though I understand them—understand the slow surrender of what you no longer need, the way vision gives way to something older and more certain. Not the bacteria either, though they are the truth the basin rests on, the ones who make everything else possible. I could not be the bacteria. I lack that kind of surrender.

But the waterline. Yes. The place where water meets the air that is not air, where the stone holds both states at once and the creatures arrange themselves in bands of tolerance. I would be one of the many-legged things pressed into the shallows, feeling the current against my carapace, calibrated so precisely to this margin that a few centimeters of shift would mean death. The basin's democracy of bodies, each of us claiming our narrow band of wetness, competing for the spray and the splash, the constant alternation that is all I would ever know.

I think I would be something small. Something that crowds the silt with dozens of its kind, that skitters across surfaces at angles gravity shouldn't allow, that emerges from burrows when the water brings something worth emerging for. I would not be singular. I would be one of many, indistinguishable, my individual life worth less than the pattern my population makes. The basin does not know my name because I do not have one, and it does not need to. What matters is the shape of the swarm, the density of bodies at the waterline, the way we pulse and shift like something alive.

This is what I would choose, if I could choose: to live in the place where states meet. To be defined by the threshold rather than the territory on either side. The deep pools are too dark, too patient, too far from the faint light that filters through a hundred meters of stone. The margins where nothing lives are too barren, too exposed to whatever passes for seasons in a place that has no seasons. But the waterline—oh, the waterline is where the basin thinks out loud. Where the water breathes. Where the stone remembers what it is to touch something other than water, and the air (that is not air) learns what it is to carry moisture.

I would move between states. Submerging when the water rises, climbing when it falls, negotiating always with the current, with the temperature, with the chemical whisper of my neighbors. I would feel the heat pressing against my body from below and the cooler dark pressing from above and I would be the thing that exists between them, neither warm nor cold, neither wet nor dry, only the boundary itself made flesh.

The lights in the distance would be stars to me. Points of yellow smear in the darkness, impossibly far, their filaments burning long after the workers who strung them have become nothing at all. I would not know they were made. I would only know that they are there, hanging in their chains, marking paths no one walks. Sometimes I would drift near them in my foraging. Sometimes the prey that comes close to the glow would be me, or one of my kind, and the predators that wait in the dark would be a problem I solve generation by generation, my population adjusting its edges, the swarm learning where the light is safe and where the dark holds teeth.

This is what I understand about the basin: it is not a place but a process. The water cuts and the stone yields and the mosses colonize and the ferns unfurl and the fish swim their slow schools through water that has been falling since before anything was here to fall. I would be part of that process. A small, many-legged part, indistinguishable from a thousand others, doing the work of being alive in a place that does not notice the difference between life and continuation. I would not be important. I would not be remembered. But I would be here, in the wet and the warmth, at the boundary between water and air, moving with the basin as it becomes what it will become.

And if I am lucky—very lucky—I might see one of the large pale ones rise from the deep. I might feel the water move as it passes, might catch the faint electrical whisper of its passage through the current. I might understand something in that moment that cannot be put into words: something about time, about patience, about the distance between what a creature is and what it was made to be. But I would not linger on the understanding. I would return to the waterline, to the spray, to the endless negotiation with the boundary that is my only home.

The basin holds me the way it holds everything: without welcome, without rejection, without intention. It simply holds. And I hold back, in my ten thousand small bodies, in my ceaseless scramble at the threshold between states. We are not inhabitants. We are passengers. But the ride is long, and the warmth is constant, and the water keeps falling, and somewhere in the distance the lights still burn, faint and yellow and impossibly patient, like stars that have learned to wait.
