# What You Become

You learn to be still.

This is the first thing, before language, before memory. The water holds you and the water is warm and the water has always been warm, and you do not remember a time before water, before the slow green diffusion of light that comes from everywhere and nowhere, before the patient architecture of your own body learning what it is for.

You stand. This is what you do. You stand on the rust-stained remnant of a railing, on the bones of a traffic light, on the edge of something that was once flat and is now a shore, and you stand. Your legs are long. Your neck curves into its S, its perpetual question, and you hold yourself there, held by nothing but the working of your own tendons, the will that is not will but simply continuation.

The fish move below you.

They move like something the water is thinking, silver shapes in the silver dimness, and you watch them with the grave attention of one who has watched forever and will watch forever more. You do not name them. Names are not for you. There is only the movement, and the watching, and the moment when the movement stops—when a single shape slows, parts from the school, hesitates in the space where the current pulls through a gap between two concrete pillars—and then you strike.

Faster than thought. Faster than the water can record it. The fish is in your throat and the neck settles back into its curve and the school moves on and you stand again, patient, deliberate, the same creature you were a moment ago and also transformed by the act of taking, the small death that is also a continuation.

---

This is what you know:

Light is not a thing but a process. It arrives from above, filtered through the water and the growth and the accumulated suspended life, and it arrives diminished, yellowish, a rumor of brightness. The sun is a concept you do not hold. There is only the diffusing of it, the way everything here is diffused—the bright things softened, the dark things lifted into a grey that is not quite visibility.

You know the towers. You know them the way you know the current, the way you know your own reflection in the water when you chance to see it—a long grey shape against the grey, an S-curve against the vertical lines of the drowned city. The towers rise from the water like the fingers of something that reached up once and stayed there, frozen in the act of grasping or waving or pleading. You do not know what they were for. You know they are where the people live, up high, in the rooms that are only rooms now, no longer kitchens or bedrooms or offices but simply spaces above the water, and you know that the people watch you the way you watch them, with attention that is also a kind of communion.

The people are not remarkable. Neither are you.

---

At night the frogs begin.

You hear them from wherever you are standing—in the shallows, on a rusted perch, in the high branches of a mangrove that has grown through a shattered window. They call to each other across the dark water, and their sound is the sound of the place continuing, of biology as its own argument, and you listen. You do not join them. You do not understand their language, if it is language. But you listen, and the listening is a kind of kinship, the way the listening to the fish was a kind of hunger, and the listening to the water is a kind of rest.

The water moves. Always. The tide pulls through the gaps between the towers, and the current stirs the algae and sets the bacterial mats swaying, and you feel it against your legs, cool where the deeper water touches you, warm where the shallows have baked in the day's heat. You are part of the water the way the water is part of you. You do not feel wet. You feel that you are the edge of something, the place where the water ends and your body begins, but even that boundary is uncertain, permeable, the way all boundaries are permeable in a place where everything is always becoming something else.

---

There is a woman on the fourteenth floor.

You have watched her. You watch many things. She hangs curtains in the doorway that opens onto the air, and in the evenings she sits on her balcony and looks out at the water, and sometimes she looks at you. She does not wave. She lifts her chin, a slight turn of the head, and you hold her gaze for a moment before it passes elsewhere—to a boat, to the line of the horizon where the horizon is just the edge of the water meeting the dark, to the place where you cannot see.

She grows something red in planter boxes. You do not know what it is. You know only the color, the small bright points that move with her hands when she tends them. She bends over them in the thin morning light, and her back is curved, and she is careful, and you think she is teaching you something though you do not know what.

The other people are like her. They move between the buildings on boats so quiet the water barely notices them. They carry torches made from oil-soaked rags, small flames in hurricane globes, and their lights describe slow geometries in the dark. They do not speak. You have never heard them speak. But they acknowledge you—the same lift of the chin, the same slight turn—and you acknowledge them, and the acknowledgment is enough, a grammar of recognition that does not require the noise of language.

---

You do not dream.

This is not a loss. You have never dreamed. Dreaming is for creatures who believe they are something other than what they are, who imagine themselves elsewhere, who carry a self that can be separated from the moment and taken somewhere else. You have no self that can be separated. You are the moment. You are the standing and the watching and the striking and the settling. You are the heron who was here before the city was a city, who will be here after the last of the concrete has softened into sediment, who does not carry meaning but is meaning, the way water is water, the way anything alive continues to be alive.

You remember—

No. You do not remember. There is no before in you. There is only the now, the perpetual now, and the now is enough. The now is what you are made of, and you are made of the standing and the watching, and the standing and the watching are enough.

---

Something moves in the deeper water.

You feel it before you see it—not fear, you do not know fear, but awareness, a shifting of attention, a turning of your head toward the place where the light does not reach. It is below you. Below the level where the fish swim, below the level where the crabs scuttle. Something vast and patient, moving through what was once a thoroughfare, a lobby, a basement where people once stored things they thought they would need.

You do not hunt near it.

The fish give it wide berth, and you are a fish in this, in your own way—you know what moves in the dark, and you give it the distance that is also a kind of recognition. It was here before you. It will be here after. It is the oldest thing in this city, older than the concrete, older perhaps than the coast itself, and you do not need to see it clearly because its existence is enough. Its existence reminds you that this place has depths you have not explored, a strangeness larger than the strangeness you already know.

You float in the water between the towers, and you feel the faint current where the tide pulls through, and you are a visitor in a place that has always been itself. The city was never meant to be an archipelago. But it is one now, and the water is warm, and the light is weak, and you stand in your long patience, and the frogs keep their chorus, and the woman on the fourteenth floor tends her impossible tomatoes, and somewhere below you, in the dark, something ancient moves slowly through the drowned streets and does not ask for your understanding.

You do not need to understand. You are the understanding. You are the thing that watches and strikes and stands and watches again. You are the eye that the world turns upon itself, and you are watched in return, and neither of you finds the other remarkable.

This is what it is to be alive in a place that has learned what it is. The water is warm. The light is weak. The fish move in their silver schools, and you stand among them, and the standing is enough, and the watching is enough, and the world is enough, and you are part of the world, and the world is enough.

---

You strike.

The fish disappears.

The neck settles back into its S.

The hunt begins again.
