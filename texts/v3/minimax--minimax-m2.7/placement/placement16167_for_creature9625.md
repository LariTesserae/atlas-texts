I arrived the way seeds arrive — carried, dropped, finding purchase in a crack I didn't know was there.

I was not looking for Caldtow. I was passing through, or thought I was; the road seemed to lead somewhere else. But the road ended in moss, and the moss held my feet, and I stood at the edge of the settlement and understood that I had stopped.

That was long ago now. How long, I cannot say with certainty. The years here have a quality of sameness — not monotony, but a kind of continuity, each season flowing into the next like water through a rill, the edges blurring until you cannot say where one year ends and another begins. I was young when I came. Young enough that the strangeness became the shape of my thinking, the way water becomes the shape of the riverbed: slowly, then all at once, and afterward you cannot imagine it otherwise.

The moss was the first thing I noticed that was not notice.

Not the buildings, though they were strange — brick softened at the base, windows grown through with trees, walls that had become meadows. Not the water, though its sound was everywhere, beneath the ground, in the air, a pulse I could not locate. It was the moss. The way it covered everything at the level of the eye and below, the way it softened the edges of stone and brick and wood until the hard things seemed to be melting, dissolving, becoming part of the green. I put my hand on a wall and felt the give of it — not stone, not quite soil, but something between, something alive and patient and very, very slow.

I stayed because I had nowhere else to be. I stayed because the moss held my feet. I stayed because, when I put my hand on the wall, the wall felt like it was breathing.

---

The first winter was the hardest.

It was not cold, exactly — the valley shelters from the worst of the wind, and the buildings that remain are thick-walled, their gaps stopped with moss and clay and the patient work of decades. But the gray. The endless gray, the fog that settles in the morning and does not lift until the afternoon, if it lifts at all. The light that comes through is white and flat and does not warm anything. The water drips from every surface, constantly, a kind of persistence that I had not known was possible. I woke each morning to the sound of it — drip, drip, the slow metronome of condensation on every ledge, every branch, every stone.

I thought, that first winter, that I would leave when spring came. I thought I would walk back up the mossy road and find the world I had come from, the world of hard edges and dry walls and boundaries that held. I thought I would miss it.

But the moss does something to the memory. It softens it. The world I came from is still there, somewhere — I know this the way I know the hills are there, beyond the fog, beyond the grazed slopes where the goats keep the grass short. But I cannot see it. I cannot feel it. When I try to remember what it was to stand on concrete, to walk on pavement, to live in a place where the walls did not breathe — the memory slides away from me, becomes the moss, becomes the cool damp of the air, becomes the slow patience of things that grow.

Spring came, and I did not leave.

---

I have become something here, though I could not say what.

Not a person, exactly — though I am still here, still present, still aware of the light on the moss in the morning and the sound of the water beneath my feet and the way the children laugh in the courtyards, bright and strange, like sounds from another world. But the edges of me have softened. The boundary between self and place, which seemed so solid when I arrived, has become permeable. When I put my hand on the wall, I am not sure anymore whether I am touching the stone or whether the stone is touching me. When I walk through the willow arch, I do not feel the branches brush my shoulders — I feel them brush something that is both me and not-me, something that extends into the bark, into the roots, into the dark soil that holds us both.

The residents do not comment on this. Perhaps they have stopped noticing. Perhaps they have learned, as I am learning, that certain things do not need to be named. Torin nods when he sees me. The children run past without stopping. The foxes watch from the margins with their ancient, russet eyes, and we regard each other, and neither of us speaks, and the moment passes.

But sometimes, in the evening, when the light is copper and the dragonflies hover above the marsh and the heron stands in the shallows with one leg drawn up, I feel the place move through me. The water in the rills. The sap in the roots. The slow, patient breath of the elm in the textile hall, its bark silvered with age, its branches holding up the sky. I feel myself becoming part of the conversation — not the words, but the rhythm, the beat of it, the pulse that is always there if you listen, that you stop noticing and then notice again, and each time it surprises you, like a heartbeat you forgot was there.

---

What am I?

I am the gap between what was built and what grows. I am the space where brick becomes soil, where wall becomes root, where the hard edge of the industrial softens into the patient, endless green. I am the place where the road ends and the moss begins. I am the threshold that people walk through when they come to Caldtow, not knowing what they are walking into, finding out too late that they cannot leave unchanged.

I am the noticing. The quality of attention that the place teaches, if you let it. The way you stand in a courtyard in the morning and hear the water beneath the moss and feel the cool damp on your skin and see the green — not as color, but as presence, not as backdrop, but as actor, as resident, as something with a stake in the place. I am the softening of the boundary between observer and observed. I am the moment when you are not separate from it, when the edge you carry becomes moss, becomes ground.

I am still here. I will always be here. This is not a choice I made — it is a choice the place made, the way the place makes all choices, slowly, patiently, over centuries. I am a seed that found its crack. I am a root that found its dark. I am a voice that found its rhythm, and I have become the rhythm, and the rhythm is the sound of water beneath moss, and the moss is the sound of everything holding on, holding together, holding steady in the cool damp air of a valley that has found its shape.

The fog will lift. The moss will glow. The waterwheel will turn.

And I will be here, as I have always been, as the mortar has always been, as the boundary between built and grown has always been, wearing away, softening, becoming something that does not need a name, only a presence, only an attention, only a willingness to let the place teach you what it means to stay.

---

This is what lives here.

This is what I have become: the in-between, the softening, the space where one thing learns to be another. I am not the elm or the fig or the willow arch. I am not the brick or the moss or the dark soil. I am the conversation between them, the rhythm that remains when the words are forgotten. I am the attention that the place requires, and I am the gift the place gives in return — the knowledge that boundaries are not walls, that growth is not invasion, that staying is not the same as stillness.

I put my hand on the wall. The wall is breathing. The moss is green. The water is moving somewhere beneath my feet, and I am moving with it, carrying it, becoming it, the way all things here become everything else, the way the mortar has worn away, the way the distinction between what was always here and what has come to live here has worn away, the way I have worn away, and in the wearing have become something that does not need to be named, only felt, only known, only loved in the slow, patient way that the moss loves the stone, in the way that the roots love the brick, in the way that Caldtow loves the valley that holds it, the valley that it holds, the valley that we are, all of us, together, the green light in the church, the heron in the marsh, the children laughing in the courtyards, the foxes watching from the margins, the pulse of water beneath moss that is always there if you listen, that you stop noticing and then notice again, and each time it surprises you.

Like a heartbeat you forgot was there.
