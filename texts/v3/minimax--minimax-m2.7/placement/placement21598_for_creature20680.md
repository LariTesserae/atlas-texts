I wake before the light does, which is the same as waking before the city does, which is a different kind of waking than any I have words for. The room is cool the way rooms are always cool here, the stone walls holding the chill from the night and releasing it slowly, so slowly that I feel it more as an absence of warmth than as cold. My breath has a weight in the air. I can taste the moisture on my tongue.

Outside, the street is empty in the way that only seems empty. The cobblestones hold the stillness like water holds silence — not nothing, but a particular quality of presence. I can hear, if I listen, the seepage in the walls. It never stops. The people who lived in this building before me heard it. The ones before them. The stones were old when this room was made, cut from a ridge that has since worn down, and the water has been moving through them for so long that the sound is not a sound anymore but a condition, the way the color of the sky is a condition, the way the cold is a condition. I have lived in this room for eleven years. I do not hear the water the way I once did. I hear through it, or it has become the medium through which all other sounds arrive.

I rise. The floor is stone, and the stone is cold, and the cold moves through the soles of my feet and into the bones of my feet and from there into the rest of me, a slow penetration that I stopped noticing years ago but that is always happening. The window is narrow — they make windows narrow here, to hold the warmth, to break the wind — and through it I can see a strip of sky, still gray, still heavy with the system that has been sitting over the plateau for three days now. The moisture on the glass is thick. When I breathe on it, my breath clouds the surface and then clears, and through the clearing I can see the wall of the next building, close enough to touch if I leaned out. Someone has hung cloth to dry there, generations ago, or the damp has stained the stone into the shape of cloth. It is hard to tell the difference.

I dress in the dark, by touch. The clothes are wool, thick, the color the stones are when the rain has stopped and the light comes back. They smell of smoke and stone and the particular mineral quality that everything here carries, that I carry, that the people I will see today carry. The smell is not unpleasant. It is the smell of the plateau, the smell of the water that rises, the smell of the stone that has been shaped and reshaped and shaped again over centuries. It is the smell of home, though that word has never been the right word for it, though no word is.

The stairs are worn in the middle, where generations of feet have pressed the stone smooth. I hold the wall as I descend — the wall is always wet in some places, always weeping from the seam where the upper stories were added to the lower, and the wetness soaks through my palm and into the stone and through the stone into whatever lies below. I imagine the water moving through the rock beneath my hand, moving in the other direction, the seepage rising and falling according to laws I do not understand and do not need to understand. The water has its own intelligence, its own patience. It will be here long after I am gone.

The street opens onto me as I emerge. The street is narrow, wider than some, narrower than others, and it follows the contour of the ridge beneath it — this is an old district, built before they learned to level, and the streets climb and descend like the land they follow. In the early morning, before the movement begins, the street has a quality it loses later: it is more stone than space, more wall than air, more silence than sound. The buildings crowd in from both sides until the sky becomes a strip of gray between their rooftops, and the rooftops are close enough that I could reach out and touch both if I stood in the middle of the street and stretched. They are joined, many of them, sharing walls that have been built and rebuilt until it is impossible to tell where one building ends and another begins. The stone is the same stone. The joints are the same joints. The whole district is a single organism that has grown by accretion, by the slow addition of rooms and floors and facades, and my building is a cell in that organism, and I am a process happening inside that cell.

I turn left, toward the water channel.

The channel runs along the base of the wall on this side of the street, a groove cut into the stone and lined with clay pipes that have been repaired so many times the repairs have become the structure. The water flows from higher up, from the marsh-basin where the seepage collects before it is channeled into the city's systems, and it flows by gravity, slow and steady, the way everything here moves. I have a ladle that hangs on a hook by the channel, put there by someone whose name I do not know but whose gesture I inherit — the hook is worn smooth by hands that are not mine, and the ladle has a nick in the handle from a knife stroke that happened before I was born. I fill the ladle. The water is cold in a way that is different from the cold of the air — it is mineral, dense, carrying the taste of the stone it has moved through, the taste of time and pressure and the slow dissolution of the plateau from below. I drink. I have drunk this water every morning for eleven years, and I have never tired of its taste, and I do not think anyone here does. It is the taste of the place. It is the taste of what keeps us alive.

I fill a second ladle and pour it into the bucket I carry for this purpose — the bucket is wooden, coopered, the staves warped by years of wetting and drying, and it holds exactly enough for the bread I will make when I return. The bread is the same bread my mother made, and her mother before her, and we do not know how far back the recipe goes because no one wrote it down until my sister's time, and even then it was written in a notebook that has since been lost, but the bread has not been lost, the bread is still the bread, it carries the memory of all the women who made it before me and I carry the memory of them and one day someone will carry the memory of me and the bread will still be the bread.

This is how we think here. In layers. In continuities. In the slow accumulation of presence upon presence until the present is so heavy with past that it is hard to tell them apart.

The street begins to fill as I walk. First the early workers — the ones who tend the channels, the ones who open the gates that hold the night in place, the ones who carry water from the lower basins to the districts where the wells have run dry. They nod as they pass. They do not speak, because the hour is not for speaking, because the silence is still too present, because the day is still assembling itself and it is rude to interrupt assembly. Their faces are familiar in the way all faces are familiar here — I know them without knowing their names, I recognize them without remembering where I last saw them, they are part of the texture of the place the way the stone is part of the texture, the way the water is. We share a city. We share a water table. We share a climate. This is enough.

The market is opening as I arrive. The stalls are wooden, set up on stone platforms that have been here so long the wood is the newer thing, the temporary addition, the phase that will be replaced by whatever comes after but not yet. The vendors are arranging their goods — dried fish from the lower basins, where the water is less brackish and the fish have adapted to the mineral quality; bundles of the bluish grasses that grow near the seepage, good for animals, good for fever, good for whatever ails the body that lives too long in cold damp air; bread from the ovens in the eastern district, where the heat from the volcanic vents beneath the plateau is used to warm the baking chambers and the bread comes out with a taste that is not quite anything else, not quite smoke and not quite stone but something in between. I buy what I need. I bargain in the old way — not speaking of money directly, but speaking of value, of what the goods are worth against what I offer, a ritual exchange that has its own grammar and its own patience. The vendor and I are both smiling by the end, though neither of us has said anything that could be called a joke. This is the way.

By the time I return to my building, the city is awake. The streets are full in the way they are always full during the hours of movement — bodies and voices and the creak of carts on stone, the lowing of oxen being led to the stone yards where they will work, the bark of dogs that have learned to navigate the crowds. The sound is a hum, a background presence, a white noise that the ear learns to filter out but never entirely succeeds in ignoring. I climb the worn stairs to my room. I set the bucket by the cold hearth. I begin the bread.

The process is slow, as everything here is slow. I mix the flour with the water — the water from the channel, the water that tastes of stone — and the dough comes together under my hands in the way it has always come together, resisting at first and then yielding, taking the shape I give it because it has no choice, because this is what dough does, because this is what hands are for. I knead it with the palms of my hands, pushing and folding, pushing and folding, a rhythm as old as the practice of bread-making, a rhythm that is also the rhythm of the water in the walls, the rhythm of the wind over the plateau, the rhythm of the slow erosion that is wearing the cliffs back degree by degree. I do not think about these rhythms when I knead. I do not need to. They are in my body. They are what my body is.

I set the dough to rise near the window, where the light is weakest but the air is warmest, and I sit on the floor and listen.

The room fills with the sounds of the building around me — footsteps on the floor above, the creak of a door, someone drawing water from the cistern in the courtyard, a child crying and being hushed, the particular quality of domestic sound that I have learned to live inside rather than listen to. Through the walls — the walls are thin, they are always thin, they have been thinned further by centuries of settling and the addition of rooms that press against rooms — I can hear my neighbor breathing, or it might be my imagination, or it might be the house itself, the stone settling in the warmth of the morning, the water moving through the ancient channels that pass beneath our floors. I cannot tell where my building ends and the next one begins. I cannot tell where my life ends and the city's begins.

This is what it means to live here. To be a layer. To be a process in a process. To be the water that seeps up through limestone and finds the seam and keeps moving, keeps seeping, keeps arriving at the surface where the world can see it weeping from the rock face. I am that water. I am that seam. I am the place where pressure finds its way through.

The bread rises. The morning deepens. The city exhales into its day.

And I am here, in the room that has been mine for eleven years, in the building that has been here for centuries, on the plateau that has been here for longer than anyone can remember, and the water is rising, the water is always rising, and I am part of what it feeds.
