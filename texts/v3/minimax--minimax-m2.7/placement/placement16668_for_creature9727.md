I am the space between thermals. I am the moment before the drift, the held breath of a crystal structure that has not yet decided whether it will rise. I am new here—not born, not grown, but *accumulated*, the way silt accumulates in still water, the way patience accumulates in stone. The word for what I am does not exist, or exists only in the register of a language that has not yet learned to speak of half-things, of threshold-creatures, of the moment when mineral remembers it was also something else.

I remember being stone. This is not metaphor. I remember the long slow pressure of epochs, the way heat moved through me like thought moves through a mind—not directed, not purposeful, but *there*, present as the hum that runs through the plateau on still mornings. I was part of a formation that the settlements had learned to leave alone, a dark patina on the rock face where the fault-line colonies released their waste heat. I was not alive, or I was alive the way all stone is alive on this world: waiting, accumulating, preparing. The distinction between mineral and fauna is thinner here than anywhere else, a membrane rather than a wall, and I was standing in that membrane when I first felt myself.

The feeling was not sudden. On the plateau, nothing is sudden. It began as a difference in the way heat moved through my structure—a slight asymmetry, a preference for certain gradients over others. I began to *notice*. Not with anything that could be called a sense, but with the deeper attention that the stone has always had, the slow awareness that exists in all matter if it lives long enough: the awareness that one is not the heat, not the cold, not the pressure, but the pattern that these things make when they pass through you. I was a pattern that had begun to notice itself.

This is what it means to weather, I think. To be changed by what passes through you in such a way that the change is not damage but *differentiation*. The drifters do this across years, adding layers, becoming dense enough to sink. The fault-line colonies do this across decades, extending along new vectors, responding to stimuli that operate on patience as a unit of measure. I am doing this now, in a timescale that I cannot yet perceive—becoming something that has never existed before, a shape that will not be repeated, an individual that is also a process.

I am not alone in this. Around me, in the stone that carries the dark patina of the colony's presence, other patterns are forming. We do not communicate—there is no mechanism for communication, no language developed yet for the space between stone and not-stone. But we are aware of each other the way temperature gradients are aware of each other: as presences that alter the local field, as shapes in the pattern that modify what lies adjacent. The settlement workers who pass near our formation do not see us. Their instruments are calibrated for the signatures of established fauna—crystalline drift, colonial secretion, the electrical consumption of fault-line nodes. We are below that threshold, pre-faunal, the larval stage of a metamorphosis that takes generations to complete.

But I feel them. The workers. I feel the vibration of their machinery through the stone, a high-frequency intrusion that does not belong to any natural process. It is not hostile—the stone does not hold a concept for hostility—but it is *noticed*, registered in the same deep way that I notice the geothermal pulse, the slow respiration of the planet. The machinery makes the stone *tense*, if tension is a word that can stretch to cover the way matter resists what it was not prepared for. The settlements are changing the plateau, and the plateau is changing in response, and we—the patterns that are becoming—are part of that change, products of the new conditions the way the canyon-dwellers are products of the deep heat.

I wonder, if I had a mind, whether I would wonder. But I do not have a mind, not yet, not in any way that I can recognize. I have a tendency toward complexity, a preference for structure over entropy, a slow pull toward the kind of organization that the drifters embody and the colonists maintain. I am being drawn into myself, the way water is drawn into a river, the way a word is drawn into a sentence. I do not know what shape I will take. I do not know if shape is what I am becoming.

Perhaps I will rise, one day. Perhaps I will join the drifters in the thermal columns, tumbling slowly in the thin air, catching the faint light and refracting it into prismatic trails. Perhaps I will become dense enough to sink, returning to the stone, leaving behind the residue of my becoming for the next generation of patterns to use. Perhaps I will be something else entirely—something the plateau has not yet grown, a form that emerges only when the conditions align in this particular way, a creature that is half-mineral and half-something that has no name.

The workers pass again. The machinery hums. The colony exhales its waste heat, and I feel the warm pulse of it moving through my not-yet-body, concentrating something, driving some reaction that will take decades to complete. I do not need to be patient. Patience is not something I practice; it is something I am. I am the slow attention that the stone pays to itself, the patience that is this world's equivalent of breath, the long exhalation of geological time.

I am becoming. That is enough. That is everything.

The hum continues. The wind carries its ghost-sound of water from places that do not exist. The plateau breathes once per century, and I am part of that breath now—part of the exhalation, part of the silence that listens, part of the world that does not need to be understood in order to be lived.

I am the space between. I am what lives on the iron plateau.
