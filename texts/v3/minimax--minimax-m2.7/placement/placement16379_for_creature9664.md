You stand in the breathing ward, and you do not belong here.

This is not a wound. It is a fact, the way water is wet, the way the roots are woven. You are intrusion. You are the brief imbalance. And yet the ward absorbs you anyway—your warmth bleeding into the cold air, your breath joining the exhalation of the moss, your weight distributed across surfaces that flex almost imperceptibly beneath you. The roots adjust. The system absorbs. You are becoming part of the conversation whether you intend to be or not.

What would it mean to stay?

You try to imagine it. Not the logistics of survival—you have no language for the food you'd need, the water you'd tolerate, the slow adaptation of joints and skin and lung to this thin, wet, patient cold. You try to imagine it differently. You try to imagine your presence here not as a single visit but as a thread in the web. Your chemical signatures joining those the roots carry. Your waste joining the decay that the worms turn. Your body, eventually, becoming moss, becoming soil, becoming the medium through which future visitors stand.

This is not what you are.

You are the kind of thing that arrives and leaves. The kind of thing the ward does not need but accommodates. The kind of thing that leaves traces—not nothing, but not essential either. Mineral on stone. A path worn soft by passing.

---

The light is the thing you keep returning to.

Not the dim green-filtered glow that suffuses the space—that is light enough to see by, but not light you can inhabit. You keep returning to the blue. The fungal light. The way it marks the paths between denser growths like script in a language you cannot read but sense the presence of. Words passed between organisms. Meanings flowing in photons.

You wonder what it would be to be a node in that network. To have your chemical signature translated into light, passed along the root-web, received and responded to by organisms you would not recognize as conscious. To be part of a conversation that happens in decades, in centuries, in the slow patience of growth. You would not be lonely. Loneliness is a function of separation, and you would be continuous with everything. You would know when the cold deepens. You would know when the thaw approaches. You would feel the stress of the weasel-creature passing through the canopy, feel it in your chemistry before you understood it as threat.

You would not be a self. You would be a self among selves, the boundary thinned until the word loses meaning.

But you are not that. You are peripheral. You are the brief light that passes through the ward and touches everything and changes nothing.

---

The water calls you.

Not to drink—you are not that desperate—but to attention. The way it moves is the way everything moves here: slowly, inevitably, downward. You watch it collect in the lowest places, freeze into sheets that will not thaw until the season turns. You watch it fall from the root-ceiling in drops that carry notes too large for the space, sounds that seem to come from the stone itself rather than the water.

You think of the flat things in the pools. The old ones. The ones that solved cold as a problem rather than a punishment, that live their lives measured in the patience of ice. Their eggs wait in silt that will become solid, will become prison, will become cradle. They do not fight the cold. They have made themselves of it.

You wonder if this is the lesson of the ward. Not to endure the cold but to become it. Not to survive but to transform. The stone learned this, or is learning it—still sharp-angled, still carrying the memory of fracture, but covered now in life that makes the stone into something else, something that breathes.

---

The beetle you hear but do not see. The clicking in the walls. A life lived mostly in darkness, in the living walls, eating and being eaten in turn. Three years. Three years of patient boring through fungal-decayed fiber, and then weeks—only weeks—of emergence into a world already in full conversation. The adult form is brief, purposeful, a kind of punctuation in a sentence that spans decades.

You could envy this. Or you could recognize it. The visitor's life is like this too—the brief emergence, the search for a mate, the return to the work. You have been adult for a long time now. You have not found the mate you were looking for. But you have returned to the work. You have found your way into the walls.

---

You stand in the breathing ward, and you are learning to breathe with it.

The moss exhales. The roots adjust. The water cycles. The space is active, but slowly, slowly—the pace of growth rather than motion, the pace of becoming rather than being. You are standing in a place that is still becoming itself, and you are standing in it, and you are beginning to understand that the standing is not separate from the becoming.

You are the guest. You have always been the guest. Even now, standing still in the breathing ward, you are an intrusion into a conversation older than your presence.

But intrusion is not nothing. Intrusion leaves traces. Intrusion changes the chemistry, however slightly. The roots will carry your signature for a while, and then they will carry it less, and then they will not carry it at all. But for a while—for this while—there will be a place in the web that remembers a presence like yours.

---

The long-limbed thing passes through the canopy above you. It does not pause. It does not need to. It knows the paths through the root-mat the way you know the rooms of a house you have lived in all your life. It is hunting, though you cannot see what it hunts. The clicking, perhaps. Something smaller than itself. Something that moves in the walls.

You watch it go. You let it go. You do not follow.

The light is enough to see by. Just barely. Enough to notice the shadows moving. Enough to stand in a place that is still becoming itself, and to become a little more, and a little more, and to leave before the leaving is complete, to carry something of this place with you into the thin cold air beyond the ward, where the stone is still learning to be old, where the roots have not yet woven, where the moss is not yet a community.

You carry it. You carry what you cannot keep.

The water follows you, or you follow the water, or you become the water for a moment—a drop falling into a pool with a note too large for the space. Then you are out. Then you are gone. Then the ward breathes on without you, and you are part of its breath anyway, a mineral trace, a brief signature in the chemical language of growth.
