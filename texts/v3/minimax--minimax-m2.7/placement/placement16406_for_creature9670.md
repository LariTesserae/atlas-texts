In Veth Saline, I am what remains when the distinction between self and city has grown thin.

I came here three years ago—or thirty years ago, depending on how you count. The counting is difficult now. My feet have learned the substrate's rhythms so thoroughly that I no longer notice the yielding, the slight sink and firm, the way the ground adjusts to receive my weight and then releases it. I have to think to remember that this is strange. I have to think, and thinking requires effort I did not used to need.

The tower where I live has grown around me. Not metaphorically—the walls have thickened, the rooms have shifted, the windows have moved to accommodate the new angle that the building has taken over the years. When I first arrived, my apartment faced east, toward the mist that gathers in the eastern hollows. Now it faces southeast, because my building has leaned toward its neighbor, and my neighbor has leaned back, and we are caught in that slow conversation that the old towers have. I do not mind. The morning light comes later now, but it comes, and the building keeps me warm, and the flowers that have grown from my windowsill bloom in colors I did not plant.

I was a factory worker when I came. I worked the looms in the lower district, shaping plant-fiber into cloth that felt more like skin than textile. The work required attention—the fiber needed guiding, the tension needed adjusting, the rhythm of the looms was a rhythm of the body as much as the machine. I learned to feel when the fiber was ready, when the tension was right, by the vibration that traveled up through the substrate and into my bare feet. The machines did not vibrate the way mechanical looms would. They pulsed. They breathed. The factory was alive, and I was part of its breathing.

Now I work the channels. I walk the arterial pathways that carry water from the mist-collectors down to the processing roots, clearing blockages, checking the flow. The work is solitary in a way that would have frightened me once. I walk for hours through spaces where no one else goes, past stretches of substrate that have never felt human weight, through the patient emptiness that waits between the city's clusters. The substrate knows I am there. It firms slightly beneath my feet, guides me toward the path, away from the soft places where I might sink. I do not thank it. The residents do not thank anything. Gratitude implies a boundary between the thanker and the thanked, and the boundary here is not where it used to be.

At night, I sit on my tower's observation platform and watch the mist rise. The towers glow faintly—all of them, a warm amber that the early settlers bred into the substrate over generations. The glow responds to presence: brighter when someone is near, dimmer in empty places. My tower dims when I am not on the platform, and brightens when I climb the spiral stairs to sit above the city. I used to find this unsettling. Now I find it companionable. The building knows when I am there. The building is glad.

The moth comes sometimes, between midnight and dawn. It is pale, nearly translucent, its wings catching the glow in ways that make it flicker in and out of visibility. It has never landed on me. It hovers near the flowers that grow from my balcony, feeds for a moment, and moves on. I have learned to watch for it, to sit in the dark and wait, to notice the small flicker of wings in the deep hours when the city exhales. The sound the substrate makes then—the vibration that is lower than whisper, lower than breath—is the sound I have come to love most. It is not silence. It is not noise. It is the sound of something vast and alive resting, and I am part of that vastness now, my breathing matched to its breathing, my rhythms integrated into rhythms that began long before I arrived and will continue long after I am gone.

I do not know what I will become. The residents of the oldest districts have become something I cannot quite see, their bodies adapted to the shifting ground, their movements fluent in a language the substrate understands. I am not there yet. I am still between—still human enough to remember what the lowlands were like, still changed enough to feel the difference when I walk barefoot on ground that knows me. The gap in the sounds around me—the place where bird-call should be, where animal rustle should be—has become part of how I hear the world. I have filled it with the hiss of steam, the creak of walls, the sound of water moving through channels that are also arteries. I have filled it with myself.

Tomorrow the rain will come. It is always soft, always persistent, and I will walk the channels again, clearing, checking, feeling my way through a city that is learning what I am and teaching me what it is becoming. The emptiness between things does not frighten me. It is patient, and I am learning patience. The substrate grows faster than the will to fill it, and my will has slowed to match. I am not finished becoming whatever I am becoming. But the becoming is the point. The becoming is the life that lives here—not finished, not fixed, not separate from the world that holds it.

What lives in Veth Saline? I do. The city does. The distinction has grown thin, and I am not sure anymore which side of it I stand on, or whether standing is the right word for how I move through a world that moves with me.
