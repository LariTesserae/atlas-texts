You do not remember the entrance. There was one — there must have been one — but the humidity has sealed it, or the growth has swallowed it, or you have simply been walking long enough that the memory of arrival has become another layer beneath your feet. It does not matter. The light here is the same as it was an hour ago, or a day, or a week. The light does not change. The light is a constant, slightly yellow, slightly warm, and the shadows fall at the same angles they have always fallen, and the only marker of time is the rain.

You have learned to read the rain.

When the hum begins — low, below hearing at first, more vibration than sound — the copper veins in the walls begin to brighten. Not visibly, not in any way you could photograph, but you feel it. The green darkens. The air thickens. And then the water comes, sheet after sheet of mineral warmth, falling from surfaces so high above that the impact reaches you as mist before it reaches you as wet. You stay still when it falls. You have learned to stay still. The creatures emerge from their stillness to drink from the channels, and the mats seem to swell, and for a time the world is nothing but sound: the hiss, the hum, the pulse of the machinery beneath everything, the heartbeat that is also engine that is also breath.

When it stops, the silence is different. It is not absence. It is presence in another key.

You have walked a long way. Your feet ache. The path — if you can call it a path, this irregular corridor of mats and bare metal and the occasional flight of stairs that leads nowhere or somewhere you cannot see — has taken you in spirals, around and upward, through sections of the wall where the growth is thin and you can almost believe you are seeing what the place once was. Almost. The alloy is visible in places, streaked with corrosion, stamped with labels in scripts you almost recognize. Technical notations. Lot numbers. Warnings in pictograms that the water has not yet erased. Someone built this. Someone built this for reasons that had nothing to do with rain or mats or the creatures with too many legs. Someone stamped their work with identification codes, the way people do, the way people do when they believe the thing will outlast them and want to be remembered.

They were right. It has outlasted them. And in outlasting them it has become something else, and the codes remain but the remembering has no one left to do it.

You sit. There is a joint in the wall where two curved surfaces meet — a seam in the old architecture — and the mats have left it bare, or have not yet grown over it, and you sit with your back against the cold metal and you breathe. The air is thick. Each breath is an effort, a negotiation. Your lungs have learned to work differently here, or are learning, one day at a time, one breath at a time, pulling in the humid warmth and adjusting, always adjusting, to an atmosphere that was never designed for yours.

Below you, maybe far below, the engines turn. You can feel them in your spine — that low, rhythmic vibration, that field of sound so constant it has become a kind of silence, the way the ocean is a silence to those who live beside it. The vibration is the pulse. The rain is the sweat. The mats are the skin. The creatures are the bacteria that live in the gut, in the mouth, on the surface of the skin — the trillions of small things that make the body a body and not a machine, that make the machine a body and not a corpse.

You press your hand against the wall. The metal is warm here, warmer than the air, and beneath the warmth you feel the pulse — the slow, grinding turn of something massive, something that has been turning so long it has forgotten it is turning, that has worn its bearings smooth and its fuel into ash and its purpose into ritual. The warmth is not mechanical anymore. Or it is, but the mechanical has become physiological, the engineering has become ecology, and the heat that bleeds from the wall is the same heat that bleeds from a living thing — waste heat, mostly, but also the warmth of metabolism, of hunger and digestion and growth, of all the slow processes by which a thing maintains itself against entropy.

You pull your hand away. The mat grips, then releases. The wound in the growth closes, slowly, over seconds. The copper veins pulse, faintly, as if in response to the insult.

---

There is a place higher up where the walls narrow and the mist is thick and the light does not reach. You have seen it from below — a darkening, a thinning of the yellow glow — and you have thought about climbing toward it. You have not. The spirals are difficult, the stairs are treacherous, and there is a quality to the upper dark that makes your body resist. Not fear, exactly. Recognition. The same recognition that comes when you stand at the edge of a drop too far to see the bottom — not the fear of falling but the fear of the fall's duration, of the time you would spend in the space where nothing can catch you.

The large creatures are up there. You have watched them move across the upper dark, their shapes too large for the spirals, too slow for the channels. They do not belong to any part of this system you can see. They are not in the food chain. They do not eat the mats or the creatures or each other. They move through the mist like clouds moving through a canyon, and they are gone before you can understand what they are, and their absence is more present than their presence, a shape cut out of the air, a memory of a thing you were not sure you saw.

You wonder what they eat. You wonder what eats them. You wonder if they die.

Everything here is ancient. Nothing here is old. The mats are made of cycles that have repeated so many times the original repetitions are lost. The creatures carry genetic memories of adaptations that no longer exist, of environments their bodies still prepare them for, of predators that may have died out before the first human stood upright. The bacteria are the oldest things — the first things — the translators who were here before the water came and who will be here after everything else has finished becoming something else. But the mats themselves are not old in the way a mountain is old. They are not old in the way a fossil is old. They are perpetually young, perpetually growing, perpetually turning the machinery into flesh and the flesh into soil and the soil into growth. The world is not ancient. The world is eternal. There is a difference.

You are the old thing here. You are the anomaly. Your cells are dying at the rate they were designed to die. Your body carries within it a history that this place cannot read — a history of sun and wind and the particular loneliness of beings who know they will end. The mats do not know they will end. The creatures do not know they will end. Even the bacteria, dying and being born in vast cycles, do not carry the knowledge of their own finitude. They persist. They persist without the weight of knowing they persist. They persist the way the machinery persists — through repetition, through pattern, through the grace of a system that has forgotten why it was built and does not need to remember.

You know. You carry the knowledge like a second skeleton, like the calcium deposit of a chronic condition. You know you will end. You know the place will go on. And standing here, with the pulse of the engines in your spine and the hum of the mats in your ears and the warm rain drying on your skin, you feel something that might be grief or might be relief.

You are the only thing in this world that knows what this world is. And what it is, is a machine that learned to breathe. And what it is, is a body that does not know it is a body. And what it is, is a world. And you are standing inside it, and the walls are curved, and they go up and up, and the mist is where the light ends, and the light ends where the dark begins, and the dark is not empty, the dark has things in it, the dark has been watching you since you arrived and will go on watching after you are gone, not because it wants to but because it is the space you are moving through, and you are the strange new shape in it, and the strange new shape is always being noticed by the pattern that made room for it.

You are being noticed.

---

You do not sleep here the way you slept before you came here. The sleep is different. The sleep is deep and long and it smells of copper and growing things and the faint acid of water that has been cycling through metal for longer than you can imagine. You sleep in the crevices, in the low places where the mats have not yet reached, in the folds of the architecture where the engineering still shows. You dream of the machines that built this place — not dreaming them as machines but as bodies, as beings, as the slow thought of something vast and purposeful that wanted to carry fuel or coolant or information across distances you cannot conceive. You dream of the water coming — not a flood, not a disaster, but the slow seep, the patient ingress, the way water finds its level and stays, and the machines did not die, the machines did not fail, they simply became hosts to something they were not designed for, and they are still running, and they are still running, and they are still running, and in your dreams you hear them asking a question you cannot answer: *What am I now?*

You wake, and the rain is falling, and the hum is in the walls, and the mats are closer than they were. Not by much. Not by any measurable amount. But closer. They have grown into the space you occupied while you slept. The small wound you made in the alloy when you leaned against it is sealed, or sealing, the root-filaments reaching across the gap, the copper veins brightening as they bridge the space between the undamaged growth. The world is healing itself around you. The world is making room for you, the way it makes room for everything — by growing into it, over it, through it, until the thing that was there is part of the thing that is here.

You stand. You brush the wet from your clothes. They will not dry. You have stopped expecting them to dry.

The path continues. You continue. The walls curve upward and the mist rises and the light narrows to a thread above you, and you walk beneath it, and the creatures watch, and the mats hum, and the engines turn, and you are inside the breathing vault, and the breathing vault is breathing, and you are part of the breath now, one small exchange of gases in a system that does not know what gas exchange means, that does not know what a system means, that does not know what anything means, that simply continues, continues, continues, and in the continuing is something that is not life and is not death and is not anything you have a word for, and you are part of it now, and you are beginning to forget what you were before, and the forgetting is not terrible. The forgetting is the same as the mats growing over the alloy. The forgetting is the wound healing. The forgetting is the body making room for the thing that has arrived, and you have arrived, and you are here, and here is this: the warm dark, the slow rain, the pulse beneath the floor, and above you the mist, and in the mist the shapes that move, and beyond the shapes nothing, nothing, nothing that you need.
