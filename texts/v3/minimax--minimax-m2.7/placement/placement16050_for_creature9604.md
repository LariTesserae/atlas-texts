You would begin by drowning, and then you would learn not to.

The first breath is a negotiation. The air has weight here—actual weight, the humidity pressing against your alveoli like a second membrane, filling spaces that elsewhere would hold oxygen alone. You would cough, or you would not, depending on what you are. If you are one of them, if you came from the wet places where the channels run shallow, your lungs would already know this trick: the way to accept the moisture, to let it in, to treat the boundary between self and atmosphere as a suggestion rather than a law. If you are something else—visitor, trader, fallen from a sky that does not exist here—you would spend days or weeks learning to breathe before you learned anything else. Some never learn. Some go home with their lungs aching, carrying the memory of weight.

You would learn the verticals first. This is not optional. The city does not explain itself horizontally; it explains itself upward and downward, through levels that multiply as you move, through bridges that appear where you expected air, through shafts that drop into darkness and rise into diffuse luminescence. You would learn to read architecture the way others read faces—looking for the slight tension in a joint that means a passage is new, the wear patterns on a rail that indicate heavy traffic, the way condensation gathers where air circulation is poor and trust the gathered water to guide you toward the living places. You would become, in time, someone who looks at a wall and sees the water in it, the slow seep through stone, the places where the city weeps most and tends itself most carefully.

What you would be depends on when you arrive.

In the deep hours, when the pulse slows but does not stop, you might find yourself tending. There are roles for those who read the channels, who feel the blockages in the circulation, who know the difference between the normal rhythm of flow and the stutter of something wrong. You would learn this knowledge in your hands first, then your back, then something deeper—the way the body stores information that the mind cannot hold, the way generations of workers have passed down attunement through sinew and bone and the particular ache of muscles that have learned a specific kind of effort. You would not think about the city thinking. You would simply move when it needed movement, rest when it rested, and find in this responsiveness something that might or might not be love.

Or you might be one of the margins-dwellers, the small ones, the simple ones who persist in the spaces between the city's intentions. You would be translucent, patient, emerging in the deep hours when the sound drops just enough. You would feed on what grazes, and be fed upon in turn, and never once consider the word *life* because the word is not yours. You would simply continue. This would be enough.

The scale would try to break you. This is certain. The city sprawls in directions your mind was not built to map, and the only response is to stop trying, to surrender to the maze, to let the verticals become your only orientation. You would find, in this surrender, a kind of freedom: the freedom of being unable to see the whole, of being local in a world that has no center, of belonging to a place precisely because you cannot comprehend it. This is how they live. This is how they have always lived, in the midst of something too large to see, finding their neighborhoods in the texture of the architecture, their neighbors in the bodies that share their air, their lives in the constant motion that has never stopped.

At night—and here *night* means something different, a shift in the quality of the pulse rather than an absence of light—the humidity would intensify, and the glow would become diffuse, and the lightning would move between the upper structures. You might find yourself climbing, or descending, or simply standing on a bridge that bridges nothing to nowhere, watching the flashes illuminate the canopy. The scale would reveal itself in these moments, the vast extent of construction, the density that has become its own terrain. You would feel small. You would feel, if you were one of them, that this smallness was not a problem but a condition, that being small in a large city was the same as being a cell in a body, present, necessary, unaware of the whole but perfectly suited to the part.

They do not ask what the city is. They have learned not to ask. The question has no answer, or has too many answers, or has an answer that would not change anything. What matters is that the water moves. What matters is that the channels clear themselves, that the infrastructure maintains its pulse, that the people sleep in shifts and wake to a world that has not stopped being what it is. What matters is that they have found a way to live inside something that might be alive, and that this way works, and that the working is enough.

You would find yourself there by accident, or by intention, or by the kind of drift that this place encourages—the passive movement through space that the city's transit systems were built to serve, the way you might board a vessel without knowing where it goes and trust the going to teach you. You would arrive wet. You would leave wetter. And in between, you would breathe, and climb, and learn the verticals, and perhaps tend something, or perhaps simply persist in the margins, and the city would not notice you, or would notice in the way it notices everything—not as individual but as flux, as part of the flow, as one more body moving through corridors that have always moved, that were built for this, that have become, through generations of use, something like living.

The question is not what you would be but whether you could be it. Whether the adjustment is possible. Whether the boundary between self and atmosphere is as negotiable as they have made it. Whether, given enough time, you could learn to belong to a place that has no earth, no plants, no sun—only water, always water, moving through stone, weeping and never stopping, and the bodies that have learned to breathe it in.
