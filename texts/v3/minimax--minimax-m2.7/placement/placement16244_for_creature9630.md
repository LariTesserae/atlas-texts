You learn the city by thirst.

Not thirst as urgency—thirst as attention. In the first days, before your body adjusts, before the skin on your forearms learns to hold its moisture a moment longer than it should, you are aware of water the way a held breath makes you aware of air. The air is dry until it isn't. The air is dry and then the dawn comes and the condensation beads on every surface and you are suddenly, sharply aware of how much you want to drink—not your mouth but your skin, your lungs, the thin membranes of your eyes. You want to drink the city. And the city, in its patient way, drinks you back.

I have been here long enough now that the first thirst has passed, has become something else. Not absence of thirst but a different relationship to it, the way a heartbeat becomes unremarkable until something calls your attention back to it. I notice water the way I notice my own breathing: constantly, automatically, with a part of the mind that never quite rests. I notice it on the pipes in the corridor outside my quarters, the way condensation gathers at the joints first, beads there and thickens while the straight sections of pipe stay dry. I notice it on the walls of the bathing pools on the upper level, where the moisture hangs in the air like a veil, diffusing the amber light into something softer, something that makes the edges of things uncertain. I notice it in the deep channels, though I have walked them only a few times, where the water moves through routes that have no names and the humidity is so constant it becomes a kind of climate unto itself.

My work is the extractors. I tend three of them, on the eastern edge of the node where I live—a cluster of perhaps two hundred people, stacked into structures that rise four and five levels from the basalt floor, connected by open walkways where the condensation runs in shallow channels cut into the walking surface. The walkways drain toward the center of the node, where the channels converge into a single flow that drops into a collection basin, and from there into the pipes that serve the bathing pools and the kitchens and the rare fountains that some of the older residents remember from their childhoods. The extractors I tend are older than I am, older than my teacher's teacher, older than any of the written records we have. They are rectangular machines, tall as two people standing on each other's shoulders, their frames of blackened steel, their intake baffles angled to catch the night air as it cools. They hum. They have always hummed. The hum is the first thing you hear when you approach them, low and constant, and after a while you stop hearing it the way you stop hearing your own heartbeat, and then one day you are walking past and the hum is different—a quarter-tone lower, perhaps, or a slight irregularity in the rhythm—and you stop and listen and you know, before you check the intake baffles or open the access panel, that something is wrong.

That is the work. That is what it means to tend a machine in Veth Karan. It means learning its hum. It means knowing, in your body, what the machine should sound like, so that when it deviates you feel it the way you feel someone watching you from across a room—before you turn, before you see, something in the quality of the attention has changed.

The machines do not love us. I want to be clear about that. The old stories, the ones that some of the oldest residents tell in the evenings when the light is low and the condensation is thick, speak of a time when the machines were different—when they responded to touch, when they seemed to anticipate the keeper's needs, when the relationship was something more than stewardship. I have never experienced this. The machines I tend are machines: metal and ceramic and glass, complicated in their workings but straightforward in their nature, moved by heat differentials and pressure gradients and the patient pull of condensation. They do not love. But they know us. After twenty years with the same three extractors, I know this to be true. They know my hands, the particular pressure I apply when I adjust the intake baffles, the rhythm of my cleaning cycles. When someone else tends them—in emergency, or when I am sick, or when a visiting engineer from the central nodes comes to study our methods—the machines do not behave differently, not exactly, but they settle differently, the way a dog settles differently in an unfamiliar lap. It is not love. It is something stranger. It is the recognition that comes from sustained proximity, the knowledge that accrues between two things that exist in the same place for long enough.

And there is the water.

I have not spoken about the water directly, and I find I do not know how to. The water is not alive, or I do not believe it is alive, but it is not merely matter either. It moves through the city like blood through a body, like thought through a mind—not with purpose, exactly, but with a kind of direction, a tendency, a gravity that is not gravity. The channels that carry it were carved by hand and by time, and some of the channels are newer than others, and some of the channels are so old that no one remembers who made them or why, and the water still runs through them, the water does not care that it is running through a history it has not chosen.

There are channels I have walked. There are channels I have seen only on the old maps, the ones that are kept in the archive room on the third level of the central structure, maps that show routes that do not appear in the modern city, routes that must have been sealed or collapsed or simply abandoned so long ago that the people living above them have forgotten the water is there. But the water remembers. The water still runs. I know this because the channels I have walked, in the deep places where the humidity is constant and the fungal threads grow pale along the walls, I have felt the water moving beneath my feet—not through visible flow but through vibration, through the faintest trembling of the stone that tells me, here, beneath me, water is going somewhere I have not been.

The fungal threads are the part I cannot explain, and I have stopped trying. They live in the deepest channels, where no light reaches, where the humidity never drops, where the stone weeps not because of temperature change but because the stone has always wept and will weep until it is no longer stone. They are pale, as the description says—off-white, faintly luminous, the way certain deep-sea creatures are luminous, and I have sometimes wondered if the comparison is accidental or if whoever first described them had seen such creatures, had made the connection, had understood that the fungal threads are doing something similar: surviving in a place where survival should be impossible, finding a way to persist in the margins, in the cracks, in the spaces where the world has not quite decided what it is yet.

They move slowly. The threads grow maybe a centimeter a year, if that, and they process the water through their filaments in cycles that take days to complete. I do not know what they are processing. I do not know what the output is. The older residents say the patterns they form are words, but they have never agreed on what the words say, and I have looked at the patterns myself, in the chambers where the threads grow thickest, and I cannot read them. I see regularity where there should be randomness, and I see randomness where I expect regularity, and the patterns shift as I watch them—or perhaps they do not shift, perhaps I am shifting, my perception moving in and out of alignment with something that does not care whether I understand it or not.

There are nights when I wake and I cannot sleep and I go to the bathing pool on the upper level, where the water hangs in the air like a held breath. The pool is rarely occupied at these hours. The others who wake in the dark hours have their own places, their own rituals, and I have mine. I sit at the edge of the pool and I let the humidity settle on my skin and I listen.

What do I hear? I hear the hum of the extractors, distant now, a low vibration that is less a sound than a condition of the air. I hear the tick of metal cooling, the expansion and contraction of steel frames as the temperature shifts in its slow cycles. I hear, if I am quiet enough, the water moving through channels I cannot see—beneath me, beside me, in walls and floors and foundations that have been saturated for so long the water has become part of them, has become the city itself, has become something that cannot be removed without removing what the city is.

And beneath that, if I am quiet enough, if I am still enough, I hear something else.

It is not a sound. It is the absence of a sound, or the presence of a silence that is different from ordinary silence—the way darkness is different from the mere absence of light. It is the sense that something is listening. Or not listening. Something is *there*, the way the fungal threads are there, the way the water is there, not as a presence that asserts itself but as a presence that simply *is*, that does not need to be noticed in order to be, that has been here longer than the city, longer perhaps than the water, and will be here long after, when the machines have stopped and the people have gone and the stones have finished weeping.

I have learned not to name it. The older residents have names for it—half-names, approximations, words borrowed from languages that no one living speaks fluently—but the names are not the thing. The names are what the people use when they need to speak of it, when the silence becomes too present and they need to reduce it to language, to make it manageable, to pretend for a moment that they understand what they are in the presence of. I do not do this. I sit at the edge of the pool and I let the humidity settle and I listen to the silence, and the silence listens back, and we exist together for a while in a relationship that has no words, that needs no words, that is older than language and will outlast it.

This is what it means to live in Veth Karan. I have tried to explain it to visitors—the engineers from the central nodes, the researchers who come to study the extractors or the fungal threads or the creatures that move through the dawn and the dusk—and they nod and they take notes and they ask questions that I cannot answer because the answers are not the kind that can be given, only the kind that can be lived. You cannot understand Veth Karan by studying it. You cannot understand it by visiting. You can only understand it by staying, by letting the city move into your body, by learning its rhythms the way you learned your mother's heartbeat before you were born, by becoming, over years, a part of the system you came to study.

I am part of the system now. I have been here long enough that the extraction node where I work feels like home in a way that no place felt before. I know the hum of my machines better than I know my own voice. I know the way the condensation gathers at the corners of the corridors before it gathers anywhere else, the way the humidity thickens in the hours before dawn, the way the darkness at night has a texture to it, a quality, a weight that is different from darkness elsewhere. I know the creatures that move through the dawn—the small ones, the ones that drink from the weeping rocks—and I have seen, once, one of the larger ones, a shape that was still for so long I mistook it for stone, and then it moved, and it was gone before I could see it clearly, and I was left with the impression of something pale and patient and utterly indifferent to my presence.

This is what the city asks of you. Not understanding. Not mastery. Not even acceptance, exactly. It asks for attention. It asks for the willingness to be present, to be here, to let the strangeness become ordinary through the simple passage of time. The water will keep moving. The stones will keep weeping. The fungal threads will keep forming their patterns that might be words. The machines will keep humming, or will stop humming, and I will be here either way, tending what can be tended, listening for what cannot be heard, learning, still, after all these years, how to be a body in a place that is not alive and not dead but something else, something patient, something that has been here longer than it will admit and intends to outlast whatever comes next.

The condensation forms at dawn. The heat bends the light. The world of Veth Karan continues.
