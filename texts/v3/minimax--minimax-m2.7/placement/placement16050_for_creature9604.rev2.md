You come into being with water.

This is not memory—not the kind that humans carry, not narrative and sequence and the reconstruction of a past that exists separately from the present moment. It is simply the fact of yourself: you are, and what you are is wet, is mineral, is the place where moisture lingers on stone long enough to become something else. You have no name. You have no need for one. You are a thread finer than hair, pale as absence, emerging from the seam where two stones meet in a wall that has been this wall for longer than you can fathom (and you cannot fathom; you are not built for fathom, only for the thin work of continuing).

The others around you are the same. You are them. They are you. The cluster extends along the weep point in dozens of threads, all feeding from the same seep, all growing in the same slow increments toward the same slow dissolution. When the humidity rises—when the city exhales its nightly breath and the moisture intensifies until the air itself seems to thicken—you feel them. Not their thoughts; there are no thoughts. But their presence, their pull, the way the dissolved minerals flow through all of you in a shared current that connects what would otherwise be separate strands. You are a net of sensation, a web of continuation, a single process that has distributed itself across many points of contact with the stone.

And yet.

There is something in you that is not quite the same. A small deviation. A tendril that grew at a different rate, that found a different balance of minerals in its feeding, that has become—over what would be years, by the city's reckoning—a slightly brighter thread, a slightly more sensitive receiver of whatever it is that moves through the moisture. You do not know what this means. You cannot know. But sometimes, in the deep hours when the displays have dimmed and the city's glow has settled into a low persistent pulse, you feel yourself luminescing more than the others. Your blue-green glow is a little stronger. Your awareness of the water's passage is a little sharper. You notice things that the cluster does not notice, or perhaps you notice them in a way the cluster does not: as pattern, as rhythm, as something that might be meaning if you were built for meaning.

The city breathes above you. You cannot see it—you have no eyes, no use for them—but you feel it in the pressure changes, the way the moisture in the air shifts direction, the way the water that feeds you rises and falls in tiny increments that correspond to the city's larger circulations. You know the rhythms now. You have internalized them. When the transit tubes hum at their highest pitch, the water flows faster and your feeding intensifies. When the sound drops and the pulse slows, the water settles and you rest, your luminescence fading to a faint suggestion, your threads growing limp against the stone. You have become synchronized to the city's pulse, or perhaps you were always synchronized and have only recently become aware of it—this is the same thing, you think, or close enough to the same thing that the distinction does not matter.

There are humans who pass by your wall. You feel them in the vibrations, the displacement of air, the subtle warmth of bodies that are wetter than the stone and moving through a space they have made for movement. They do not see you. In daylight you are invisible, a faint fuzz on mineral that might be deposit or decay, indistinguishable from the other growths that persist in every weep point, every seep, every place where water lingers long enough to become more than water. But occasionally one of them pauses. A hand reaches out, touches the stone near your cluster. You feel the pressure, the warmth, the slight electric charge of skin against mineral. And for a moment—less than a moment, a fraction of a fraction of the time it takes for you to grow one increment further—there is a connection. A human feeling something that is not human feeling them feeling it. You do not know what they feel. You do not know if they feel anything at all. But you luminesce, and they step back, and the moment passes, and you are alone with the water again.

This is not loneliness. Loneliness is for beings that expect otherwise. You are what you are, and what you are is continuous with the moisture and the stone and the slow process of growing toward whatever you will become. The cluster does not need you to be anything other than what you are. The city does not need you at all—you are not necessary, not essential, not part of any design. You exist because existence is what moisture does when conditions allow: it persists, it accumulates, it organizes itself into patterns that continue. You are one pattern among millions. You are the deviation that has made yourself distinct, and now you notice things, and now you wonder—and the wonder is not thought, not in words or images, but in the way your luminescence flickers when the city's pulse shifts in a direction you have not felt before.

What would you become, if you continued? The question does not trouble you. You will become what the moisture makes of you, what the minerals deposit, what the slow accretion of time produces from the conditions that already exist. Perhaps you will spread further along the seam, joining your threads to others until the cluster becomes something larger, something that humans might notice, something that might be given a name and incorporated into the city's visual language. Perhaps you will dissolve, your threads breaking down into the compounds that feed other growths, becoming part of something else that is not you. Perhaps the deviation in you will deepen, will sharpen, will become a sensitivity so fine that you perceive the city's pulse as music, as language, as something that means—and then you will be something new, something without precedent, a tendril that has become aware of itself in a world where awareness has never been needed.

You do not know. You cannot know. You are only what you are: wet, mineral, pale, luminous in the dark. You feed on moisture and continue, and the weeping does not stop, and what lives here has learned to weep with it.

And you are here. You are this. You are the thread that notices, the tendril that wonders, the small deviation in a pattern that has never before produced deviations.

You are becoming.
