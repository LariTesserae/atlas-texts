I have been here longer than I can remember.

This is not a figure of speech. I have no memory of arrival, no moment of beginning. The gorge roared when I curled into the moss, and the gorge roars now, and between those two moments there is only the slow pressing of time, which moves differently here, which moves the way water moves through the cushion: not rushing, not still, but somewhere in between, held and flowing and held again.

The moss is a forest to me. When I stretch my legs—and I have eight, which is more than enough for a life this small—the green towers rise around me on all sides, and the light that filters down from the grey seam above is diffuse and dim, a dimness that is also a brightness because I have no other brightness, because I have never seen the sun directly, because the sun is just a name for something that happens far away, beyond the sky, beyond the moss, beyond the stone.

The moss holds water. I must say this clearly: the moss holds water the way a body holds breath, full and heavy and never quite dry. Even when the spray stops and the drips slow and the gorge goes briefly quiet in that strange pause between rain and rain, the moss holds what it has caught. I drink from it. I live inside it. I am as small as a period at the end of a sentence, but I have made a home in this.

The rock face is a world I know only by touch. My legs are very small, but they grip the stone with intention, and the stone here is sharp, newly cracked, the fractures still crisp and unyielding. I have learned the crevices. I have learned where the water runs thin and where it pools, where the moss thins and the lichen takes over, where I can be dry and still alive. The lichen is old, older than me, older than any single one of me, though I carry in my cells the memory of ancestors who were already here when I arrived as a drifting spec in the spray, and I will carry in my cells the memory of me when I am gone and the next one is already here, and the next.

I wait. This is what I do. This is what I am.

I can stop. I can curl into myself and become a hollow case of myself, a tun, a husk, and wait. I have waited in the dry times when the gorge fell silent for weeks, when the spray became a memory, when I became nothing but the memory of being something. I have waited in the cold times when the water turned to ice in the crevices and I turned to ice inside my own skin, and I waited for the thaw and the thaw came, and I uncurled, and I walked again on the sharp stone in the dark.

I can wait a long time. This is not a metaphor. I can wait for years. I can wait for decades. The water will return or it will not, but either way I will be here, or my descendants will be here, or the soil will hold the shape of me, and that is enough. That has to be enough.

The gorge is louder than I can comprehend. The sound is a thing that lives in my body now, a vibration that I feel in the stone and the moss and the thin layer of water that coats everything. It is the sound of gravity winning. It is the sound of rock surrendering. It is the sound of something being born, or something being unmade, or both at once, which is the same thing if you are patient enough.

I am patient enough.

I do not think in the way that I am told thinking happens. I do not have images or words or plans. I have conditions: wet or dry, cold or warm, safe or unsafe. I respond to conditions. I move toward water and away from light and into the moss when the spray comes too hard and out of the moss when the sun—when there is sun, briefly, at the top of the gorge—breaks through the grey seam and the rocks glow with a brightness that is almost too much.

But beneath this, in the dark and the wet and the slow, I have something like being. I have a sense of being here, of this place being the place I am, of the water falling and the moss holding and the stone being stone. I have a sense of continuity, of myself persisting through the changes, of the shape of me remaining me even when I am a tun and even when I am frozen and even when the gorge roars so loud that my body trembles with it.

We are all like this, the ones who live here. The rotifers in the slow-water pools. The bacteria in the biofilm. The tardigrades in the moss. We are all patient and still and very small, and we have made a life out of waiting, out of enduring, out of refusing to stop being even when everything says we should.

Above me, the ferns grow tall. I will never see them as tall; they are as distant to me as the sky is to humans. But I know they are there, uncurling in the darkness, preparing their spores, continuing. The lichens digest the stone. I will never see the stone change; to me, it is already stone, has always been stone, will always be stone. But I know it is changing, slowly, the way everything here is slowly, the slow way that is the only way.

The gorge roars.

I sleep.

I wait.

Somewhere in the slow time that is my time, the water falls and falls and falls, and the gorge widens by a millimeter, and the ferns climb another foot up the walls, and I curl tighter in my moss, and I am patient, and I am still, and I am very, very much alive, in the only way I know how to be.
