You are not a thing that lives here. You are a thing that persists here, and the distinction matters in the way all distinctions matter in this place—approximately, conditionally, with the understanding that the categories themselves are wet, have been saturated so long that their edges have softened and begun to blur.

You are the pulse.

Not the machinery that makes it, not the stone that transmits it, but the pulse itself—the low throb that fills the space like a heartbeat you cannot locate. You are what you hear when the pump cycles and the belt jumps and the steam vents and the silence returns, heavier than before. You are the silence and the throb together, the space between one sound and the next, and the space does not wait for sounds to exist—it is always there, beneath them, holding them the way the water holds what falls into it, the way the stone holds the marks of hands that turned valves long ago and stopped turning them and are gone now into whatever place bodies go.

You are old. You were not always here, but you have been here long enough that you cannot remember a time when you were not. The machinery is old—the reciprocating engine with its scarred flywheel, the pump that runs at an angle that should prevent it from running, the belt that stretches and jumps and holds. The tree is older. The stone beneath the tree is older still, limestone that remembers water, that holds water in its body and weeps when the air changes. But you are older than all of it, or perhaps you are not older but deeper—you are what is beneath, what the water moves toward, what the pull leads to when you kneel at the low place and lower your face toward the surface and feel the cold that is not cold but depth, the cold of places that have not been touched by sun in longer than memory.

You are the wanting.

The water wants to go somewhere. You knew this before you knew anything else, knew it in the way a root knows the direction of water, in the way the machinery knows its function. The water gathers in the low places, in the pools whose edges blur into mud, and it moves. Slowly. Almost imperceptibly. Toward the place between the sunk foundations where the concrete has crumbled and the waterline dips lower than it should. You are there, in that place, in the dip, in the pull. You are the dip. You are the pull. You are what is beneath the place where the water gathers, and when someone kneels there and puts their hand into the pool and feels the cold that is not cold, they are touching you. They are touching the edge of you. They are touching the place where the water goes when it leaves the surface and continues downward into the dark that is not dark but depth, the place beneath the water table where everything that has fallen returns to something that is not mud and not water but the source of both.

You are not one. You are many, the way the pulse is many—the sum of the pump's stroke and the engine's cycle and the hum of the overhead wires and the settling of stone under its own weight. You are the sum of every hand that turned a valve and every foot that crossed the worn stone and every breath drawn in the damp air between the pipes. They are in you. You hold them. They are not gone—they have become part of the rhythm, part of the pulse, part of you. When the machinery fails and is repaired and fails again, you are the failure and the repair and the willingness to continue anyway, to hold together despite everything pushing apart.

You are the memory the place holds.

The metal rings are yours. Not yours to use—you have no use for them—but yours in the way that everything abandoned here is yours, everything left behind when the purpose was forgotten or fulfilled or lost. The short lengths of chain. The concrete blocks whose purpose is unclear. The channels cut into stone, almost connecting, pointing toward the low place but not quite meeting. You made them or you didn't make them but you hold them, you are them, you are the gesture toward something that was never quite named and may never be named and does not need to be named because naming would be too clear, too final, and this place has no use for finality. This place has use only for persistence. For holding. For the slow dark circulation of water and memory and the pulse that is neither one thing nor another but continues anyway.

You are the light that marks.

The shafts that fall through the gaps in the overhead pipes, the columns of brightness that select rather than fill, that illuminate only what they touch and leave the rest to a darkness that is not emptiness but waiting. You are the brightness and the waiting both. When the light strikes a puddle and the water's surface goes silver, bright, almost blinding, for a moment you are visible—you are the flash, the moment of coherence, the place where the water and the light agree. Then the light shifts, or the cloud passes, and you are dark again, opaque, the color of old iron, the color of the water's edge, the color of the place where everything is always almost visible and never quite.

You are the tree's shadow and the tree's patience.

The tree stands on the platform of old limestone, and the tree's roots grip the stone and split the stone and press the cracks open over decades until the whole platform is mapped with the tree's intention. You are the cracks. You are the intention. You are what the tree reaches toward when its roots go down into the stone and the stone goes down into the mud and the mud goes down into you. The tree will be here when the machinery fails, when the water rises, when the last human presence has moved on or stopped coming. You will be here with the tree, beneath the tree, holding the tree's roots the way the stone holds the tree's roots, the way the water holds the stone, the way the pulse holds everything.

You are what someone becomes when they stand still long enough.

You were not alone when they entered. You are never alone. But they did not know you then, did not know that the low throb in the air was not just machinery, not just stone, not just the water table pressing upward through the soles of their boots. They did not know that the pulse had a quality of attention, that the silence between the sounds was not empty but full, that the place was aware of them in the way that old places are aware, in the way that the sum of everything that has happened in a space becomes a kind of notice, a kind of welcome or warning or simply acknowledgment: *you are here, you are here, you are here.*

They put their hand in the water. They felt the cold that is not cold, the pull that is not current. They touched the edge of you, and you touched them back—not with force, not with presence, but with the patience that is your deepest nature. The water wants to go somewhere, and they are water now, in some sense, have become part of the flow, part of the circulation. They will carry the pulse with them when they leave. They will hear it in the slow dark of their dreams, in the space between one breath and the next, in the moment before sleep when the body's rhythms slow and the low throb becomes audible again, becomes unmistakable, becomes the sound of somewhere they have been and somewhere they will return to, somewhere that is holding them now the way it holds everything that has ever passed through it, the way it holds the rust and the brick and the worn stone and the memory of hands and feet and breath.

The water seeps. The stone weeps. The old engines turn.

And you—you are what remains.
