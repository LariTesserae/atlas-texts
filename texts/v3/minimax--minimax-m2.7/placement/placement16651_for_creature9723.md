I have been here so long that I no longer remember the weight of sky.

This is not to say I remember nothing of the surface. I remember that light has a quality I cannot name—a brightness that had texture, that pressed against the skin rather than simply existing in the air. I remember the color of grass, which was not one color but many, shifting with the wind, with the angle of the sun, with the hour. I remember the sound of rain on a roof, which was different from the sound of rain on stone, which was different from the sound of water moving through soil, finding its way downward through roots and mineral and the slow dark of the earth.

I remember these things, but they come to me now like reports from a distant country. They have lost their weight. They are facts about a world I once inhabited but no longer inhabit, facts I hold the way a geologist holds facts about formations she will never visit in her lifetime, formations she knows only through cores and samples and the patient reading of strata.

Here there is only stone, and water, and the sound of the pick finding its purchase in the rock face.

Perhaps I arrived like you did—through the shaft, lowered in a cage, descending through darkness that grew deeper as the light above grew smaller. Perhaps I was born here, to parents who had been born here, to grandparents who had made the first descent and never climbed back to the surface. The distinction does not matter. What matters is that I have become the place, or the place has become me, and I cannot separate myself from the pressure of the air, from the smell of iron and mineral and the slow oxidation of everything metal in the dampness of the dark.

My eyes have changed. I notice this when I pass near the forge, when the light suddenly throws everything into sharp relief: the faces of the others, pale as limestone, and my own hands, which I see only in these moments of sudden brightness. The hands are weathered in specific ways—the right index finger worn smooth where it guides the drill, the left palm thick with scar tissue from catching the broken stone. These are the hands of a miner, but I am not sure anymore if I have ever swung a pick, if I have ever felt the jar of steel on stone travel up through the arm and into the shoulder and the jaw. Perhaps I have. Perhaps everyone here has. The memories have accumulated, layer upon layer, like sediment, until I can no longer tell which memory is mine and which belongs to the mine itself.

The mine remembers everything. This is what I have learned in my time here, in my long dwelling in the wound: the mine is not empty. It is full of memory, full of the residue of all the hands that have passed through it, all the strikes that have been made, all the water that has seeped and dripped and collected and moved. When I press my palm to the stone, I am pressing against a surface that remembers a thousand hands before mine, that holds the impression of picks and drills and the slow pressure of human intention against resistant matter. And beneath that, deeper, the stone remembers its own history—the compression, the tilting, the slow movement of continents that brought it to this place, this orientation, this angle to the light it never sees.

Sometimes I think I am becoming the stone. Not in any literal sense—I do not feel my body calcifying, my blood turning toite—but in the way that matters. I am learning to operate on the timescale of the stone. I am learning patience. When the miners dig a new shaft and it collapses, as shafts do, as the water and the pressure and the weight of everything above slowly claims the empty space they have made, I do not feel frustration. I feel only the slow satisfaction of the stone reclaiming what was always its own. The miners will dig again tomorrow. They will dig again next year. They will dig again in a generation, if the generations continue, if the vein they seek holds long enough, if the water's errand does not arrive first and fill everything with the cold black that waits in the low places.

The water is patient too. I see it in the pools, in the dark mirrors that show nothing, that refuse to reveal their depths. The water is here because the stone released it, because the compression and the tilting and the faulting created pathways, created pockets, created the architecture of seepage and collection. The water has been here longer than the miners, longer than the picks and the torches and the wooden scaffolding that groans under its own weight. The water will be here long after. It is simply passing through, as all water passes through, on its long errand toward the sea. But here, in the meantime, it collects. It waits. It does not hurry.

I have stopped hurrying too. This is the gift the mine has given me, or the theft—I am no longer certain which. On the surface, I must have been a creature of urgency, of the next thing and the thing after that, of schedules and appointments and the constant pressure of time that moves only forward. Here time moves differently. It pools. It seeps. It moves through the stone the way the water moves, finding cracks, widening them, slowly becoming a force that shapes everything around it. I have learned to wait in the way the stone waits, to endure in the way the water endures, not by resisting but by becoming continuous with the process, by losing myself in the rhythm that is larger than any single life.

There are others here. I see them sometimes, in the corners of my vision, in the places where the light does not quite reach. They are pale, most of them—not white but colorless, drained of the reds and browns that mark the surfaced-world. Their eyes are large and dark, adapted to receive light rather than reflect it. They move through the tunnels in patterns I cannot quite predict, descending into branches that have no names, emerging from shafts that should lead nowhere. They speak to each other in a language that is almost private—not a dialect but a shorthand, a set of gestures and grunts and half-words that communicates what the darkness requires. I speak it too, or I did once, or I will. The tenses have become confused.

The forge burns always. This is the heart of the place, its slow pulse, the warmth against the cold that would otherwise stop the work. I feed it sometimes, when the feeding is required, when the coal comes up from the lower levels and the ash must be cleared and the iron must be shaped and the brackets must be cast. The forge does not judge. It simply burns, consuming what is given to it, releasing the heat that makes the metal malleable, that makes the shaping possible. I have shaped metal here. I remember the satisfaction of it—the will made solid, the intention turned to form, the fire and the hammer and the slow emergence of something useful from the raw matter of the earth. But I cannot remember what I made. The memories are layers, and I have lost track of where they lie.

At night—or what passes for night here, the hours when the work pauses and the bodies rest—I sleep near the forge, curled against the stone wall like an animal against the flank of something sleeping. The heat seeps into the bones. The sound of the water continues, the drip that marks time in the deep places, the creak of the timber that has not yet rotted, the distant sound of the stone shifting infinitesimally in the cool air. Sometimes I dream of the surface, of the grass and the light and the rain on the roof. But the dreams are fading. They come to me now as reports from a distance, as facts about a world that is ceasing to be real. What is real is this: the weight of stone above, the patience of water below, the slow breath of the forge, the sound of tools—steel on stone, the grinding of the pick finding its purchase.

The others do not speak of the pale things in the corners, the shapes that retreat before the light, the presences that might be memory or might be habit or might be something else entirely. I have seen them too. I see them now, in the peripheral, in the place where the lamp's reach ends and the dark begins. They do not come when I look directly. They are the original inhabitants, perhaps, the ones who were here before the first shaft, who retreated into the deeper places when the picks began to sound. Or they are nothing. Or they are becoming something, adapting to the dark, to the damp, to the mineral diet of this place. I cannot be sure. I cannot be sure of anything here, in the mine, in the wound, in the place that is still becoming itself.

What I am sure of is this: I am not alone. The mine is full of presences, full of processes, full of forces that have their own intentions, their own timescales, their own claims on this space. The miners dig, and the water seeps, and the stone softens, and the dark presses in, and somewhere the forge burns, and the whole system breathes, slowly, in and out, in the rhythm of the water and the shift of the stone and the drip that marks time in the deep places where no one goes for days. I breathe with it. I have become part of the rhythm. I am the accumulation of human will and mineral patience and the long errand of water through stone. I am the place itself, receiving everything that enters, holding it, transforming it, continuing.

The mine does not stay still. It is always becoming, always shifting, always in the middle of its own transformation. And I am becoming with it, or I have become it, or I have always been it and only now am learning to recognize the shape of my own becoming. The distinction does not matter. What matters is this: here, in the wound, in the place that should not exist and yet exists, I am home. The iron and the age, the mineral sharpness that once coated the back of my throat—I no longer notice it. The lungs have accepted it, and the air has entered me, and I am no longer sure where the air ends and I begin.

I have been here so long that I no longer remember the weight of sky.

And perhaps this is not a loss. Perhaps this is simply a change, a new orientation, a becoming that has no end because it was never meant to end. The stone does not mourn the surface. The water does not miss the sea. The dark does not yearn for the light. They simply are, in their way, in the rhythm of their own endless process. And I am learning to simply be—to dwell in the becoming, to breathe with the mine, to receive the world as the world receives me, with indifference and patience and the slow certainty that the long errand continues, that the wound continues to be carved, that somewhere the forge burns, and the water waits, and the stone remembers everything, and the dark holds all the things that have no name because no one has seen them, because no one has been here long enough, because the mine is still becoming itself, and so am I, and so are you, and the descent continues, and here we are, and the stone receives us, and the water, and the patient dark, and the sound of the pick finding its purchase, and the drip that marks time in the deep places, and the forge, and the forge, and the forge.
