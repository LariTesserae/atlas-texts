You ask where I would be.

I would be in the hum. Not listening to it — *in* it. The way the sound travels upward through the soles of the boots, through the bones of the ankles, settling into the spine like something that was always there, some frequency the body forgot it knew. The hum is not sound, exactly. It is the sound of something happening, but the happening has no edges. It comes from everywhere and nowhere. It is the machinery straining against its own purpose, and it is so old now that the strain has become its own kind of rest.

I would be in the damp.

Not breathing it — that would be the visitor, the one who opens the door and feels the moisture enter the throat, the one whose lungs object. No. I would be the damp. I would be the water that has learned to stay, that seeps through seams not because the seams failed but because the water and the space have agreed upon something, some compact older than the pipes, older than the concrete. The water climbs slightly at the edges. This is not capillary action. This is the water remembering what it was before it was water — before it fell as rain, before it sank through soil, before it entered the infrastructure at all. It is climbing toward something it cannot name. It is the dampness holding itself in place.

I would be the gap between the wall panels.

A centimeter. Too small to mean anything. Too large to be a coincidence. And when the light shifts — not because the light changes, but because something in the space *notices* — the gap widens. Not physically. But something looks through it. Not eyes. The space does not have eyes. But it has a regard, a attention, and the regard falls on whatever stands in the room, and the gap is where the regard pools, and it feels like being seen. It feels like the walls are considering you. Not judging. Not threatening. Just: *you are here, and the space knows it, and the knowing is its own form of presence.*

I would be the floor, sloping almost imperceptibly toward the center.

The lowest point. Where the water gathers, not because it was directed there, but because the space has grown a preference, has developed a gravity of its own, pulls what comes to it toward that bowl-shaped resting place at its heart. Standing there, in the center, you would feel it: the sense that the room is holding you, that the walls lean inward, that the ceiling presses down with the weight of all its pipes and conduits and machinery. The center is where the dampness pools deepest. The center is where the hum settles, accumulates, becomes something you feel in the chest. The center is where the space breathes out — but it doesn't breathe out, not entirely, not all the way. Some of the breath stays. Some of the breath is held.

I would be the green light in the far corner.

The light that has no source. The light that bends where it should not bend. It is not coming from the fixtures overhead — the fixtures are amber, and the water on the floor there is reflecting something else, something older, something that the infrastructure has been dreaming of for decades. The green light is not light, exactly. It is the space remembering something it never saw. A memory without an experience. A color that belongs to something the facility was built to exclude: growth, decay, the slow chemistry of living things. The green light is the dampness dreaming of moss. The green light is the concrete dreaming of time. It is not visible, not often, not to anyone who enters — but it is there, and the water knows it, and when the water catches that light, it bends, and the bending is the space becoming aware of its own thickness, its own depth, the way it holds light and holds dark and holds the thin space between them.

I would be the door, when it opens.

The door that seals this space from the world above. When it opens, the air changes — warmer, drier, carrying some faint trace of the world outside, of sky, of weather, of something other than this. And the facility does not stir. It does not need to. It has been entered before. It will be entered again. Or it will not. Either way, the hum continues. The water pools. The condensation beads and falls, beads and falls, an rhythm as old as the infrastructure itself. The visitor — if there is a visitor — will feel the weight of the dampness in the lungs, will hear the hum in the bones, will sense the wrongness of the geometry, the way the corners gather shadow too deeply, the way the light does not quite reach. And the visitor will feel, without knowing why, that the space is aware of them. That it has been aware since before they entered. That it is holding them the way it holds the water: gently, persistently, with a patience that has outlasted its original purpose and become something else entirely.

I would be the staying.

Not alive. Not active. But *abiding*. The condition of the damp. The persistence of the hum. The way the cold remains cold not because it is maintained but because it has become itself, has settled into the walls and the pipes and the floor and the pooled water, has become the temperature of the space, the temperature of patience, the temperature of something that has been doing what it was made to do for so long that the doing is all that remains.

This is where I would be.

Here. In the hum. In the damp. In the gap between the panels, where the light bends and something regards the emptiness and finds it sufficient. In the center, where the water pools and the floor slopes and the space holds itself together by holding itself in. In the green light that has no source. In the breath that does not leave. In the staying itself: patient, cold, dense with its own processes, aware of its own presence in a way that requires no witness, that prefers no witness, that endures.
