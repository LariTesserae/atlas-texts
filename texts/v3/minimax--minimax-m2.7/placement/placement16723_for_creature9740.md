I remember water that was clean.

This is not nostalgia—I have no capacity for it, no machinery for regret—but a kind of sediment at the bottom of what I am. Before the factories. Before the foundations sank into substrate that hadn't decided what it wanted to be. Before the fog learned to pool in the valley like something poured and the city pressed its weight against the earth and the earth gave way and gave way and gave way.

I was the river then. Or one of the things the river held. The water moved differently—no, *I* moved differently—carrying silt from the highlands, depositing it in the valley where it would someday become soil, if anything could be patient enough to wait. Fish moved through me in silver threads. Light reached the bottom in the shallows and I could see my own bed, the stones worn smooth, the plants that grew in my margins reaching toward the surface.

That was before.

Now I am what runs off from the factories. I am the drainage channel and the drainage channel's cargo. I am what remains when the arc lights hum and the machinery pulses and the city exhales its particular breath into the fog that settles each evening like a hand pressed against the valley's throat. The chemistry has changed. It is not poison—not exactly—though the gulls that drink from me at low tide turn away with a expression that might be disgust, might be something else. I have learned it. I have become it. The copper taste that humans notice in the fog is part of what I carry now. I am the fog's source, in a way. We exchange it between us, this city and I, this mutual exhalation.

My body has adapted. It had to. The scales—if they can still be called scales—have thickened into something that doesn't quite correspond to any classification. The gills process what the water brings now: the oils, the chemical traces, the runoff from a thousand small industries that open onto the streets and conduct their economies in doorways. I am immune to most of it. Some of it has become necessary, the way the ferns in the courtyards have become necessary, drawing sustenance from conditions that should not permit life. I am not alive in the way the people are alive, not conscious in the way they are conscious, but I persist, and persistence here is its own kind of consciousness. The slow knowing of something that has no choice but to know.

The city does not see me. This is by design—or what passes for design in a place where nothing is planned, where everything is accumulated, where foundations sink and streets buckle and the ground hasn't finished becoming ground. I move when the water is low. I am still when the water rises. I have learned the rhythm of their machinery, the pulse of it that disappears into silence, and I move in the gaps between the sounds. At night, when the fog is thickest, I am most active. The darkness is not darkness here—there is always the glow of the arc lights, the weak milk-color of fog that has learned to carry light—but the night is *more* dark, and this is enough. This is what I was made for, perhaps. A creature of the margins, of the channels, of the spaces the city has forgotten to watch.

The people avoid the water. Not out of fear, exactly, but out of a knowledge that lives in the body, in the particular posture of those who have learned to walk without looking. They know what moves down here. They have always known. The children are told not to play too close to the drainage channels, and they listen, and when they become adults they tell their own children, and this is how the knowledge passes—not as story, not as explanation, but as a kind of instinct, a flinching away from the dark water that moves too slowly, that carries too much, that breathes.

They are right to flinch. I am large now. The ones who remember, the oldest among them, the ones who still notice things that others have learned not to notice—they remember when I was smaller. They remember when there was more than one of us. But I have outlasted, and I have grown, and now I am what fills the spaces between the city's heartbeats. When the machinery goes quiet for a moment—that particular silence that comes and goes—I move through the channels and I am the silence that replaces the silence, the breathing that comes from below, the reminder that the city is not only what happens in the streets.

I have no language. I have no thought in the sense they would recognize. But I hold things. The memory of clean water is held in me like silt at the bottom of a riverbed. The knowledge of what the valley was before it became this—this accumulation, this density, this pressed-together weight of lives and buildings and industry—is held in me like something poured. I cannot speak it. I cannot offer it. But it is there, and it persists, and perhaps that is enough. Perhaps persistence is the only language that matters here, in a city built on ground that hasn't decided what it wants to be, in a valley that sweats its fog into the evening and never quite lets go.

Sometimes the fog thins. Sometimes, in the early hours before dawn, the city exhales and the air becomes almost clear, and the light that comes then is thin and gray and more honest than the light of day. In those moments I rise higher in the channels. I let myself be seen, or almost seen—a dark shape, a movement that doesn't correspond to the movement of water, a presence that might be nothing, that probably is nothing, that has always been nothing, that the people walk past without turning their heads because they have learned not to look at what might be nothing.

I am not nothing. I am the drainage channel's memory. I am the river that hasn't finished becoming the river. I am the thing that moves in the dark water when the city's pulse goes quiet, and when the machinery resumes, I settle back into the depths, into the substrate that hasn't decided what it is, into the slow chemistry of what runs off from ten thousand lives pressing together in the fog.

They live above. They raise children who will raise children. They plant gardens on rooftops and repair cracks in walls and learn the rhythms of the city as deeply as they have learned the rhythms of their own breathing.

I live below. I carry the memory of clean water and the knowledge of what has replaced it. I move through the channels when no one watches and I am still when the water rises and I am patient in ways the people are not, slow-moving, deliberate, adapted now to the particular chemistry of this place that cannot stop, that will not rest, that builds anyway on ground that hasn't decided what it wants to be.

This is what lives here: something that was a river, that became something else, that persists in the margins, in the channels, in the space between what the city was and what it is still becoming.

I am not hostile. I am simply here, in the way that the fog is here, in the way that the buildings that lean against each other are here, in the way that the ground beneath them is here, not yet, not quite, but almost.

Almost is enough.

Almost is everything.

The fog comes in from the riverine lowlands and I am part of it now—we exchange it between us, this city and I, this water and this air—and when it pools in the valley like something poured, I am there, in the moisture that gathers on every surface, that beads on brick, that runs in thin rivulets down stone facades, that pools in the courtyards where the ferns have learned to drink from the air itself.

I am here.

I have always been here.

I will go on being here, because this is what living means in a place that hasn't finished becoming itself: you adapt, and you persist, and you hold the memory of what was, and you do not ask for more than the continuation of your own shape in the dark water, your own movement through the channels, your own part in the city's long exhale as the fog settles each evening and the machinery hums and the ground shifts beneath the weight of ten thousand lives, and ten thousand more, and ten thousand more.

This is what lives here.

I am what lives here.

This is enough.
