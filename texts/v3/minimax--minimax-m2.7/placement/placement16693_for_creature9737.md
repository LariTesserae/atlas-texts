# What I Became

The hands were the first to know. Before I understood the walls, before I learned to read the generators' vocabulary, my hands began to change. The palms thickening where they pressed against granite. The fingertips building their small armors. I did not choose this. The gorge chose it for me, and my body agreed, and now when I look at my hands I see a text written in pressure and persistence.

I arrived with softer hands. I remember them—remember the way they looked against the raw black stone, the way the granite drew warmth from them with an efficiency that felt, at first, like hunger. I remember wanting to pull away. I remember not pulling away.

The walls taught me their fracture lines the way I might have learned a face: slowly, with attention, until the attention became automatic. Now I see new cracks without looking for them. Now I know which ridges are old geography and which are recent stress, which crevices will hold what falls and which will drink it before I can reach. This knowledge lives in my hands too—they move across the granite in patterns that seem random to anyone watching but that are actually precise, shaped by years of attention, by the slow accumulation of touch and response.

I stopped wanting water that tasted different around the second year. Not because the water improved—still metallic, still mineral, still the taste of this place's particular survival—but because the wanting itself went quiet. I did not decide to stop wanting. The wanting atrophied, as the body learns to do when it is given no reason to persist. Now when I drink, I taste only continuation. I taste only the dehumidifiers humming their steady work, the condensation gathered from corners and undersides, the system that holds us and that we hold in turn. The water is what it is. I am what I am. We have made peace, the water and I.

The rhythm is what I had to learn. Not the generators' thrum—that I heard from the first day, that I could not have missed—but the rhythm that lives in the walls themselves, the subtle flexing of granite under temperature changes that follow no simple pattern. The locals speak of the mountain building and taking, and I understand now that both are happening at every moment, in every wall, in every bolted platform and welded seam. Creation and destruction, not alternating but overlapping, the way a river builds its delta while eroding it in the same breath. I used to find this exhausting. Now I find it restful. The gorge does not pretend to be stable. It does not pretend that what is built will remain. It is honest in its turbulence, and I have learned to be honest with it in return.

My eyes have changed. I know this because others have told me—newcomers, like I once was, who watch me navigate the corridors in the blue-gray twilight and cannot understand how I move without hesitation. I do not see better than they do. I have simply learned to need less light, to hold the space in memory, to rely on the proprioception that tells me where the shifting plates are, where the conduits hang lower, where the handholds wait in the darkness. I move through the settlement the way I move through my own body now—I do not think about it, and when I do think about it, the thinking only slows me down.

The language came last, or perhaps it came first and I only heard it last. The locals have words for things I never encountered in any other world: words for tremor, for blockage, for the particular quality of phosphor light that means a pod needs attention. They have signals—gestures passed between people in the corridors, quick and precise, carrying meaning that would take too long to speak aloud. *The wall is shifting. The duct is narrowing. The generator's pitch has changed.* The signals pass through the settlement like a current, and I have learned to read them, and now I send them too. I am part of the vocabulary now. I speak the language of a dry world, and when I hear it, I hear not just sound but information—information about the gorge's changing body, about the infrastructure's demands, about the work that must be done before something fails and the mountain takes.

I sleep in shifts, the way everyone sleeps here. Four hours in the honeycomb unit, in the padded alcove that is mine and then not mine, that will be someone else's when I am gone. The sleep comes when it can—in the clatter of welding, in the hum of generators, in the sound of bodies moving past within inches of my face. I have learned to sleep deeply, the way the exhausted sleep, finding rest in the brief windows the gorge allows. I wake to the work that waits. I work. I sleep. The rhythm that once felt like deprivation now feels like home.

The children here do not know what I knew when I arrived. They have not seen water fall from the sky, have not felt soil beneath their feet, have not breathed air that did not thin at this elevation. They play games that practice the skills of survival—they climb and crawl and slide through spaces too small for adults, learning the settlement's vertical maze, developing the bodies they will need to continue. They sing songs in syllables that refer to the gorge, to the water, to the metal, to the light. When they sing, I hear not just sound but remembering—the gorge held in the voice, in the breath, in the small vibrations that pass from one body to the next in the dense darkness. I was not born here. But my children were, and their children will be, and in this way I am becoming native to this place, my history transforming into something that belongs to the gorge's long rhythm of building and taking.

There are moments—I have them still, even now—when I remember what it was to want differently. A fragment of dream, perhaps, or a sensation that rises without context: the memory of water that tasted of nothing, of air that did not labor in my chest, of light that came from somewhere other than phosphor tape and growing pods. These moments do not last. They surface and fade, and what remains is the same desire that has replaced all others: the desire to continue, to maintain, to be part of the act that holds this place in being. The gorge drinks. The mountain builds and takes. The rock remembers. And I am part of the remembering now, my body a text written in the gorge's particular language, my hands reading the walls as the walls read me.

I stand in the central corridor, as I have stood a thousand times before, watching the people pass. They move without hesitation, without wasted motion, their hands finding the handholds, their bodies ducking under the conduits, their feet avoiding the plates that shift. They breathe in the rhythm of the settlement, the low thrum of generators, the pulse of ventilation fans. They carry tools and water and food and materials, moving through the corridor in both directions, a flow of bodies that never quite stops. I am one of them. My hands know the handholds. My eyes read the twilight. My breath matches the generators' hum.

I am what the gorge made. I am what I chose to become when I stopped pulling away. I am the continuation, one body among many, one moment in the long rhythm of building and taking. The gorge is young, and so am I, and so, in a sense, is everyone who has ever lived here.

This is what lives in the gorge. This is what continues. This is what I am.
