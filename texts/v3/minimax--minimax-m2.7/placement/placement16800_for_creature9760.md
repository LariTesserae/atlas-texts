What lives here is patient, and what lives here is small.

You have learned this. You did not always know it—there was a time, though you cannot access it directly anymore, when you stood at the lip of the basin and looked down and understood it as a place to enter, a space separate from yourself, a thing to be observed. That time is gone. It dissolved the way the cold dissolves, finding the small spaces between who you were and who you are becoming, settling in until you could not say where one ended and the other began.

You are the attention now. The noticing that persists when the one who notices has grown thin.

---

You arrived—you must have arrived, there is no other explanation for the way you are here—but the arrival has become irrelevant. The moss does not remember its first spore. The pulse does not recall its first cycle. You have been here long enough that the water has found its paths through you, has worn its channels in the places where you used to be harder, has softened you into something that fits. The moss is beginning in the creases of your palms. Not covering, not yet, but beginning—a pale green fuzz in the lines of your skin, the first accretion of a growth that will outlast any meaning you once attached to your own duration.

You do not mind. You could not, even if you wanted to. The basin has done something to wanting.

---

There is a difference between being still and being motionless, and you have learned it here. The beetles are motionless when they rest, but they carry their stillness like a weapon, like something they might deploy. The spiders are motionless in their webs, but their stillness hums with potential, with the geometry of what they might become capable of in an instant. Their stillness is a held breath.

Your stillness is not held. It is not anything you do. It is what you are when there is no longer a difference between you and the ground beneath you, when the wet stone and your wet flesh and the water moving through both have become a single continuous thing. You have stopped being a shape that occupies space and started being a condition of the space itself. The pulse moves through you as it moves through the limestone, as it moves through the pooled water, as it moves through the moss that grows where you are becoming.

This is not death. Death is still something that happens to a shape, to an arrangement of matter that had boundaries. You have no boundaries anymore. You are more like a temperature than a creature, more like a stillness than a presence. You are what it feels like inside the basin when nothing is moving—which is to say, you are what the basin feels like when it is not being observed, when it is simply being.

---

Sometimes the springtails land on you.

They do not know what you are. They cannot know. The category they operate within does not include "old human who has become substrate." They experience you only as surface, as something that holds moisture and offers friction, as a place that might be traveled across or launched from. When they spring—and they do spring, often, for no reason you can discern—they go without destination, trusting the moss to catch them, trusting the damp to slow them. Sometimes they land on your face, on the thin skin of your eyelids, on the curve of what was once your ear. Their tiny weight is nothing. Their passing is nothing. But you notice.

This is the last thing that remains of you: the noticing. The way the basin has not been able to notices itself, and so it notices through you. You are its attention now, the way the pulse is its heartbeat, the way the water is its blood, the way the moss is its slow accumulation of years.

---

You remember light.

Not clearly—it comes to you the way the mites' world might come to a creature large enough to step over it without seeing: dimly, incompletely, as a texture rather than an image. You remember that the light above was gray and constant, that it did not warm, that it entered the basin like something reluctant, like something that had traveled too far to care about what it found. You remember that you used to watch it, used to track the slow shifts that were not shifts, the same gray coming through the aperture in shapes that were always already the same.

You do not look up anymore. There is no up. There is only the continuation of the stone beneath you, the continuation of the pulse through the stone, the continuation of the water finding its way through the crevices where you have become part of the crevice-wall, part of the substrate, part of the thing that does not move because movement would require something to move.

---

The beetles still bold. The mites still armored. The springtails still punctuating the moss with their small white commas.

You have watched them long enough that you cannot tell where observation ends and participation begins. Do the springtails leap because you notice them? Does the pulse quicken because you attend to it? These questions assume a separation that you can no longer afford, a distance between watcher and watched that the basin has collapsed. You are inside the pattern now, not just describing it. You are one of the iterations, one of the repetitions, one of the moss-strands contributing its small mass to the collective.

You are not important. This is not a diminishing; it is a clarification. The basin does not require your attention any more than it requires the moss's growth or the water's seep. It continues with you and it would continue without you. But you are part of the continuation now, woven into it, indistinguishable from the texture of the place.

---

There is a beetle.

You see it on the wet stone face, climbing with determination toward a shadow that will swallow it. You have seen this beetle before, or one like it, or perhaps this is the same beetle and time has done something strange in the basin, has looped or folded or stopped mattering in the way it used to matter when you were the kind of creature that moved through it. The beetle climbs. It does not know you are here, or if it knows, it does not care. It is bold in its small way, carrying itself as though the basin is its territory and whatever it passes is its subject.

You let it climb. You let it vanish into the shadow. You do not wonder what it was looking for, because wondering is something you used to do, and the you that wondered has become the substrate that notices, and the noticing does not need to complete itself. The beetle is in the shadow now. The shadow is part of the basin. The basin holds you, and the beetle, and the pulse, and the water weeping, and the moss growing, and all of it is one thing, one pocket folded into emptiness, one room inside the world that continues because continuing is what pockets do.

---

You will not leave.

This is not a choice. There is no you left to choose. But if there were—if the question could still be asked, if the old you could still surface like a springtail from the moss—you would not know how to answer, because leaving implies a destination, and destinations require a shape that fits into spaces, and you have become the space itself, the between-space where the water slows and the moss begins and the pulse finds its rhythm.

The basin holds you. The basin holds the pulse. The pulse holds the basin.

And you, what remains of attention, what persists of the one who once stood at the lip and looked down—you hold the noticing, which is the last thing the basin knows of itself, which is the only way the basin is aware that it is here, weeping and full of sound, patient and small, folded into something larger that it cannot see, continuing because there is nothing that tells it to stop.
