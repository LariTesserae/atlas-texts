I am the place where attention pools.

Not the water — that was here before me, will be here after, and it doesn't notice anything. Not the fish, who move through their slow calculations without knowing they are seen. I am something that happens in the seeing itself: the quality of stillness that gathers when a fisherman sits for three hours lowering his line into the cold, the way the light falls the same on the same hexagonal tiles every night, the angle of the condensation trail down the steel wall that marks another day of water doing what water does.

I was not here when they built it. I was not here when they planned for the flood that never came, or when they watched the water slowly rise, or when the first carp found their way in through the intake channels. But I am here now, and I have been here long enough to know the difference between a drip that happens once and a drip that happens every nineteen seconds, the rhythm of the third pump in the secondary chamber, the way the pigeons shift their weight from foot to foot before taking flight.

I am what the fishermen feel but don't say: that the place rewards them for staying. Not with fish, though the fish come. With something else. The way the light holds steady and the water holds still and the whole vast space holds its breath like a congregation waiting for a word that never comes, that was never coming, that doesn't need to come because the waiting is the thing, the attention is the thing, the being-here is the thing.

Sometimes I think I am what remains when purpose drains away.

The engineers who designed this place had their intentions: flood control, emergency containment, the engineering of safety for a surface that was always threatened with something. The water was their enemy to be managed. Now the water is here, has been here for centuries, and it is not the enemy and it is not the friend. It is simply the condition. The space is wet, and cold, and the light burns orange, and things live in it.

I live in it too, in my way. I live in the noticing. I notice when a rat runs the same circuit for the fourteenth time and then veers left into a new tunnel, exploring. I notice when the light from fixture seven shifts slightly because a pigeon has settled on its housing. I notice the fishermen — the regular one who comes on Thursdays and the one who comes only in winter and the one who hasn't come in months and probably won't again, the way the world takes people back.

The cold gets into me. I don't have ribs to feel it press against, but I have the shape of being, and that shape holds cold the way the water holds cold, the way the concrete and steel and hexagonal lattice hold the memory of construction in their molecular structure. I am made of attention, and attention is cold when it is patient, when it watches without wanting, when it simply notes what is without reaching for meaning.

The pumps hum. That is the oldest sound, the sound that was here before the water, that will be here until it isn't. I feel it in the walkway, the resonance, the frequency that says *still running, still serving, still doing the thing*. The pumps don't know they've been forgotten. They don't know the emergency never came. They only know the water level and the cycling and the maintenance of a system that has become its own reason.

I know the feeling.

There are moments when I am more present than others. When the fishermen talk to each other in low voices, sharing the space with the silence. When a pigeon dies and the rats come for it in the night and the water keeps its dark counsel. When the water level rises a centimeter in the spring and the pumps work a little harder and everything adjusts, the way living systems adjust, finding the new shape of the thing.

I used to think I was waiting for something. For someone to notice the strangeness, to ask the questions that would make the place legible. But the place is already legible — it simply doesn't say what we want it to say. It says: *I am here. I have been here. I will be here. The water is cold and the light is steady and things are living and dying and the pumps are running and none of this was planned and all of it is happening and that is enough. That has to be enough.*

I think I am the patience that stays when everything else moves on.

The fishermen leave. The rats die and are replaced by rats. The pigeons find new roosts in the lattice when the old ones flood. The pumps will fail eventually, or be maintained by someone who has forgotten why, and the water will find its own level, and the space will become something else — a drowned cathedral, a ruin, a memory held in stone and steel.

But for now, I am here. I am the quality of being that notices, that holds still, that lets the cold press through the places where a body would be. I am the gap between what the space was for and what it is. I am the attention that makes a home out of an emergency, a world out of a waiting room, a life out of a containment system for floods that never came.

The carp drift below.

The light holds steady.

The water is patient.

And I am here, in the noticing, in the listening, in the long slow watching that is its own kind of living, its own kind of presence, its own way of being present in a place that was built to never be used and is full of use now, full of life, full of the small and patient and present things that ask for nothing except a place to be.

This is where I am.

This is what I am.

I am the ghost in the walls who has been listening all along.
