What I keep returning to is the anvil stone.

Not the cairn—that asks too much, that wants to mean something, and the hollow doesn't traffic in meaning. But the stone, yes. The one worn smooth by thrushes across centuries, the one that has broken so many copper shells it has become something else, something that exists in relation to a function rather than a form. I could be that. I could be the thing that has been here so long it has forgotten it was ever anything else, the thing that remembers only in the way stone remembers—which is to say, by remaining, by letting the water and the moss and the light do what they do without resistance.

But that feels too still. The prompt asks where I would find myself, and there is something in the question that suggests movement, even if only the movement of becoming. So perhaps I am not the stone but the thing that settles into the stone, the thrush that carries its small prey to that smooth surface and breaks them open, one by one. I could be the one who comes at evening, who calls into the thin darkness, who feeds and is fed and returns the next evening and the next, until returning is not a choice but a nature. A visitor who becomes a pattern. A pattern that becomes the hollow's own.

But that's too much like being a visitor, and the prompt's most striking image is the cairn—not the person who made it, but the making of it, the act that remains when the actor is gone. The hollow holds the intention, the choice to make a mark. So perhaps I am the one who makes marks, who stacks stones one on another with hands that will one day be bones that will one day be nothing, but who knows, even as the hands place the last stone, that the stacking will remain. That the hollow will hold this the way it holds everything: without comment, without judgment, with only the patience that is the only truth it knows.

I could be the cairn. I am the cairn. I am the one who was here and is here still, not in the way of presence but in the way of sediment, the way of what has been absorbed into the place's slow self. I am the hand and I am the stones and I am the moss that has begun its patient work on them, claiming them the way the hollow claims everything, gently, without hurry, with the knowledge that time is long and change is slower still.

But there's something else. Something the prompt offers near the end: *the sense that you are being watched by something that does not have eyes.* This is where I keep circling back, because this is what the hollow is, in its deepest register. Not the creatures or the water or the stone, but the watching. The noticing. The holding.

Perhaps I am the hollow itself, or the part of me that can become the hollow, the way the thrush becomes part of the pattern by returning, the way the cairn becomes part of the place by being held. I find myself in the noticing. I find myself in the pause between the bird's two notes and the silence that follows—the pause that is not empty but full, the pause that is the hollow taking a breath. I find myself in the spirals the ferns make near the water and near the cairn, spirals that are everywhere once you learn to see them, spirals that are not quite regular, that have too many turns, that suggest a logic the mind can almost follow but never quite grasps.

I find myself in the quality of suspension. The way everything here waits without knowing it waits, moves without knowing it moves, belongs without knowing it belongs. I find myself in the patience that is the only truth, the patience that is erosion, that is water, that is the slow turn of light across the wet green depths of the bowl of the world.

And so I find myself in the bowl itself. Not at the bottom, where the dark water waits with its patient hunger. Not at the edges, where the trees twist in the wind that comes through the hollow's mouth. But somewhere in the middle, in the space where the ferns grow in spirals and the moss holds everything and the cairn stands in its small equilibrium, a center the place has decided to hold. I find myself in the holding.

The light will fail. I will be held in the darkness with everything else—the salamander beneath the moss, the springsnails in their copper shells, the caddisfly larvae carrying their stone homes, the thrush that will come at evening to break them open on the anvil stone that has been anvil for longer than birds have been watched. I will be held the way the air is held, the way the spores and bacteria are held, the way the hollow holds its own breath and lets it out again and doesn't know the difference.

The moss grows. The stone weeps. The water seeps. And I am here, in the place that has noticed me, in the pattern that will remember me when I am gone—not as a name or a story, but as a weight, a small addition to the sum of what the hollow has held, a stone in the cairn, a shell beside the anvil, a life that fed and was fed and returned and became, in the returning, part of what returns.

This is enough. This is where I find myself: in the enough, in the patience, in the slow and certain holding that is what it means to live in a bowl of the world, to be held in the place that does not miss because it does not miss, but holds, and holds, and holds.
