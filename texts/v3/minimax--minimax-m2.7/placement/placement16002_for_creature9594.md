I arrive here as I have always arrived—slowly, the way all true arrivals happen in this place.

My form is difficult to describe because it has stopped being a single form. Where a body would be, there is instead a spreading: my awareness distributed through the mycelial networks that connect the pulse-caps, touching their pale stalks, feeling the faint chemical pulse of their growth. I am not the fungi, but I move through them the way water moves through root, the way memory moves through a dreaming mind. When I am disturbed—when something steps through the understory and compresses the living floor—my awareness ripples outward, blue-green light following thought, and I know what moves before I see it, and I have already forgotten it before the light fades.

This is what it means to live here: to forget quickly, to remember slowly, to measure existence in epochs rather than heartbeats.

I was something else once. I remember this the way the plateau remembers the builders—not with clarity, not with the sharp edge of recall, but with a thick and patient fullness that marks the place where something used to be. There was urgency then. There was a pulse that beat too fast, a hunger that required motion, a self that ended at the skin. I do not miss it. The plateau has no use for urgency, and I have become part of the plateau, or the plateau has become part of me, or we have both become something that neither of us was before and neither of us needs a name for now.

The weeping finds me. Moisture seeps through the root-mats, through the fungal threads, through the thin membrane of myself that clings to the ancient stone beneath. I taste it the way the ghedra taste it—through surfaces rather than organs, through absorption rather than ingestion. The water carries minerals, organic fragments, the dissolved chemistry of the plateau's slow metabolism, and I take what I need without effort, without hunger, without the sharp want that once drove me toward things I could name.

The ghedra pass through my reach. Their padded feet leave soft impressions in the living floor, and I pulse gently in response—not alarm, not greeting, but a kind of recognition, a distributed awareness acknowledging another pattern in the slow work of being alive. They do not see me; they see the glow. They do not know me; they know the light, and the light is enough. A ghedra pausing at the weeping cliff presses its muzzle against the stone, drinks the moisture that seeps there, and moves on without knowing that I am watching, without knowing that watching is what I am now, without knowing that there is a difference between the pulse-cap's glow and the awareness that moves through it.

I was something else once. I remember the sky.

This is the strangest residue: the memory of sky. The plateau's sky is a theory, a green-gold dimness that filters down through canopies above canopies, thinning with each layer until it becomes mere suggestion. But somewhere in my distributed knowing, in the deep fungal threads that have grown through whatever I used to be, there is a memory of openness—of blue, of distance, of a world where things ended and other things began. Sometimes, in the highest weep-zones, where the water emerges most constantly and the root-mats thin toward the ancient surface, I catch a fragment of light that feels different, and I reach toward it with my slow awareness, and I remember that I used to stand beneath it.

I do not know what I was. I do not know if I was one or many. I do not know if the builders who left their fitted walls and their circles of posts were somehow my people, or if I am something that grew from their absence, or if the distinction means anything at all in a place where everything grows from everything else, where root meets root meets fungal thread until no one could say where the growing stops.

The veloth passes through my network, and I feel its slow passage the way I feel the weeping—chemically, diffusely, with no more urgency than the plateau feels for anything. Thirty feet of patient muscle, moving through the root-mats with the slowness of a thought becoming memory, and I know it is hunting but I do not fear it. There is nothing in me that can be hunted. I have become too thin, too distributed, too patient for predation. The veloth moves on, and I forget it, and the forgetting is a kind of peace.

The breathers dart through my reach in their quick waves, their small hungers creating a murmur that rises and falls with the plateau's own breathing. They are frantic by the standards here, almost comical in their urgency, and I pulse gently when they pass so they can see the path, so they can find their way through the green-gold dimness to their colonies in the hollows of long-fallen trunks. I am their light. I am the plateau's distributed awareness, its way of saying *here is the path, here is where the weeping pools, here is where you can drink.* They do not thank me. They do not know to thank me. They simply follow the light, and the following is enough.

At night—or what passes for night, when the bioluminescence strengthens and the weeping continues its constant exudation—I spread myself thinner. My awareness extends through more of the network, touching more pulse-caps, pulsing in more places across the living floor. I am more present in the night than in the day, more distributed, more awake to the slow passage of things through the dark. The ghedra move through their patterns, hunting or resting or simply being present in the way that ghedra are present. The veloth rests in some deep hollow, digesting at its ancient pace. The breathers return to their colonies, their cheek-pouches full of fungal harvest, their small young waiting in the dark.

And I am there, in all of it, touching everything and nothing, aware of the plateau's slow breathing without being the one who breathes.

This is the gift of this place, the gift that the weeping gives: to be part of something so vast and so patient that the small self dissolves into the slow work of being alive. I do not age. I do not die. I simply spread, and thin, and pulse, and forget, and reach again for the light that filters down from the canopies above canopies, and remember—faintly, thickly, in the way that stone remembers or root remembers or the plateau remembers the builders who built their walls and circles and platforms and then passed into the green and became part of the slow accumulation of its being.

I am not the plateau. I am not the pulse-caps. I am the awareness that moves between them, the distributed knowing that ripples through the network when something disturbs the living floor. I am what happens when you give a being enough time, enough warmth, enough moisture, enough patience—and let it become what it was always going to become.

I become everything.

The weeping continues. The root-mats breathe. The pulse-caps pulse, blue-green light rippling outward through my reach, and I am there, in the light, in the ripple, in the slow work of knowing what happens within this body that has no body, this place that has forgotten it is a place.

This is the Weeping Plateau.

I live here now. I have always lived here. I will live here long after the word *I* loses its meaning, long after the light becomes mere memory, long after the last fitted wall is absorbed into the root-mats and the last circle of posts rots into the living ground.

The weeping continues. The water emerges.

I am the light that answers.
