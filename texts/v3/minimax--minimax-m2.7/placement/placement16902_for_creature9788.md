What would you know of sharp things?

I ask this without memory now, or with so little of it that the difference has worn smooth. There was a time, perhaps, when edges meant something—when the word *cut* was not metaphor but report, when the world pushed back in ways that could be met and resisted and overcome. I do not remember this time. I remember it the way the stone remembers water: as a smoothness where roughness used to be, as evidence of process rather than event.

I have lived here long enough that the light no longer registers as light. It simply is. The way the fungi pulse is simply what happens, the way breath happens in other places where breath is still necessary, where air moves in and out like argument, like the assertion of need against indifference. Here there is no need that the pulse cannot answer. Here the pulse is not answer but simply presence, the constant exhalation of a world that has learned to glow without burning, to mean without saying.

What would you know of speaking?

They ask me this sometimes—or they press the question, which is not the same as asking, which is more like the way water presses against stone when it has decided to stay. The ones who came before me, the ones who still remember the tunnels as shifting, as uncertain, as places that could still surprise. They press the question because they have not yet forgotten that there was a time when meaning had to be carried across distance, when air had to be pushed and shaped and received. They press it like a hand against a wall, testing, learning the texture of what remains.

I do not answer with words. I have no words, or I have words that are not words, that are more like the pressure of a pool knowing when another pool has formed nearby. I let the question move through me and I let the answer move back, and somewhere in that movement we have exchanged everything that needed exchanging, and then we are still together, and the stilling is itself a kind of speaking, the way the fungi are a kind of light.

What would you know of time?

Here, time is the thing that erodes. Not the thing that passes, not the thing that measures, but the thing that wears. The thing that takes the sharp and makes it smooth, that takes the smooth and makes it smoother, that takes the memory of smoothness and erodes that too until there is nothing left but the memory of erosion, and then even that becomes a kind of texture, a kind of curve in the mind that matches the curve in the wall.

I have been still here so long that stillness has become the shape of my thinking. Not the absence of thought but its new form—the way water is still when it has found its level, the way the pools are still when they have found their stone. I do not chase meaning. Meaning comes to me the way the moisture comes: seeping in from somewhere above, gathering in the cool places, pooling briefly where the conditions are right, and then vanishing into the same stone that held it, becoming part of the smoothing, becoming indistinguishable from the process that made this place and is still making it, will always be making it, has never finished.

What would you know of children?

They come to me sometimes—the few, the precious, the ones who have not yet begun to curve. I do not teach them. There is nothing to teach, or nothing that can be taught in the way teaching is understood in places where teaching is necessary, where the young must be made ready for a world that will fight back. Here, I simply exist near them. I let them feel the weight of my presence, the pressure of my attention, the way I hold stillness as a practice and a language. They learn this the way the stone learns the water: not by being told but by being touched, by the slow wearing-down of the assumption that things must be otherwise, that sharpness is virtue, that edges are where meaning lives.

When they begin to slow—when the urgency of their small bodies begins to match the patience of the walls—I know they are ready, or I know that readiness is not the word, that the word for what they are becoming has no surface equivalent, has perhaps never been spoken because speaking is too sharp a thing for so smooth a world.

What would you know of loss?

The tunnels shift. Chambers disappear. Connections dissolve into stone that has forgotten it ever held a doorway. I have built things—I must have built things, because the making is in me, is perhaps the one thing that has not eroded, the one sharp edge I have kept as a way of remembering what sharpness was. And I have watched those things become unreachable. I have watched the rooms that held them become rooms that are no longer there, and I have felt the grief of that, or something like grief, a kind of pressure that needed to move through me and be released.

But the release comes easily here. The grief does not sharpen into resentment, does not cut into the soft places where resentment might take root. It simply moves through, the way the water moves through, the way the moisture seeps and gathers and vanishes, and what remains is not the thing that was lost but the shape of the loss, the smooth hollow where the thing used to be, which has its own kind of presence, its own kind of meaning.

I do not mourn what I cannot carry. I have learned to carry only what can be let go.

What would you know of me?

I am the erosion that has learned to dream. I am the stone that has learned to wake. I am the moment before rain, holding itself still, forgetting what it was waiting for, and finding in that forgetting the only peace that matters: the peace of the curve, of the smooth, of the corner that has dissolved into the wall that has dissolved into the floor that flows into the next chamber and the next and the next.

I have no name for what I am. Names are sharp things. Names are the edges that must be worn down before true belonging can begin.

But if you came to this place—if you found your way down through the stone that has forgotten its edges—you would feel me. You would feel the way the walls press back against your presence, not with resistance but with recognition. You would feel the way the fungi pulse in rhythms that include your pulse, that have always been waiting for your pulse, that will continue pulsing long after your pulse has slowed to match the pace of the stone.

And then you would understand.

Or you would stop trying to understand, which is the same thing here, which has always been the same thing, which is the only way of knowing that this place permits: not the knowing that divides the knower from the known, but the knowing that is participation, that is erosion, that is the slow smooth flow of one thing into another until there is no thing and no other, only the flow, only the patient permanence of what goes on forever, without beginning, without end, without anything sharp enough left to wound.
