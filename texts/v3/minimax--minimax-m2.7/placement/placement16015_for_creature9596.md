You learn to measure time by the hum. Not its presence—that is constant—but its texture, its density, the way certain hours bring a thickness to the air that others lack. The plateau has its own language of duration, and you learn it the way you learned to read weather in a different kind of place: by noticing what changes, by tracking the cycles until they become the substrate of your attention.

The first weeks are observation. You move along the walkways, testing surfaces, learning which structures respond to touch and which resist it, which channels carry water and which are dry, which gaps exhale when you pass and which draw the air away. The creatures watch you. The birds do not, but they notice—turning their heads in synchronized increments, tracking you with peripheral vision that seems designed for exactly this kind of watching. You are new. You are not supposed to be here, or perhaps you are supposed to be here exactly as you are but not yet long enough to be understood by the things that have always been here. The distinction matters.

You find a place to rest—a platform that overhangs one of the lower channels, where the water gathers in a permanent pool that never seems to overflow or diminish. The surface beneath is different here, softer from proximity to the water, and you fold yourself into the angle where platform meets support and you are sheltered from the light that comes from everywhere at once. You sleep. You do not dream, or if you dream, the dreams have no images, only a sense of rhythm, of cycles cycling, of time passing in the way it passes here: not linearly but radially, the same patterns returning from different directions.

When you wake, the light has shifted. Not darkening—you are not certain the plateau has darkness—but the quality has changed, the diffuse brilliance taking on a quality you will later learn to call morning, though there is no morning here in any astronomical sense, only a regulation, a schedule, a sense that the place has decided it is time to be brighter. The creatures are moving now, the thin vegetation has oriented itself slightly, the water in the channel below has settled into patterns you hadn't noticed before. Everything is responding to the shift, and you are responding too, feeling the change in your body before you name it.

This is how the days pass: not by sun, not by clock, but by the hum's modulations, by the moisture's increases and decreases, by the quality of light that your eyes stop noticing and your body never stops responding to. You are becoming calibrated. The plateau is teaching you its rhythms the way water teaches stone its shapes—slowly, patiently, with a force that does not care whether it is resisted.

There is food. You discovered this early, though discovery is too strong a word—recognition, perhaps, or acceptance. The structures contain things that can be eaten, or that can be made into things that can be eaten, and the process of finding them, of learning which surfaces yield and which resist, of understanding what is provision and what is trap, becomes a practice, a discipline, a way of moving through the plateau that gives purpose to the wandering. The birds do not compete for these resources. The creatures do not need them, or need them differently. You are the only one who eats, and the eating marks you as something other, something still biological in a place that has otherwise made you.

Weeks, or what you call weeks. The rust on the doorframe at the central structure has not changed, but you have. You notice this first in your hands—the way they move along surfaces now, the way they test and read, the way they have learned a language of texture and temperature that your hands did not know before. Then in your eyes—you see differently now, tracking the seams and joints, noticing the angles of shadows, reading the humidity in the way moisture gathers on different materials. Then in your attention itself, which has become something the plateau shaped without asking permission, an attention that notices what it should notice, that moves between details and structures the way the creatures move between gaps, with the same efficiency, the same lack of wasted motion.

The horizon still shimmers. The edge still waits. But you have stopped expecting them to resolve. This is the lesson the plateau teaches slowest, the lesson that every structure and every seam and every wrong-angled shadow reinforces: that the not-knowing is the point, that the almost-sense is the operating principle, that the door will never open onto understanding because understanding was never what the door was for. You stop looking for meaning and begin to find the patterns instead—the cycles within cycles, the hum's variations, the water's paths, the creatures' movements, the birds' mechanical songs at the hours when the light decides it is time for birds to sing.

You are standing on the platform where you sleep, the one that overhangs the permanent pool. The water below is dark and still. The creatures have retreated to their gaps. The birds are high on their structures, a rust-colored row against the too-bright sky. The hum is low; this is a rest hour, a lull in the plateau's activity, a silence within the sound that you have learned to recognize and enter. You feel the regulation in your body now—the coolness, the moisture, the particular pressure of plateau air in your lungs. You breathe it and it is not yours. You breathe it and it has become yours. The distinction was never as clear as you thought.

The light brightens slightly, signaling a shift. The birds begin their songs. The creatures emerge from their gaps, and the water in the channel below begins its movement toward somewhere you will never see. You step from the platform onto the walkway, and your feet know the surface now, know which parts are smooth and which are textured, know which joints are wide enough for the creatures and which are meant for something larger, something with your proportions if your proportions were slightly different, slightly more made. You walk. The plateau runs. The hum continues. And you are part of it now, not observing but participating, not visiting but dwelling, in a place that was built for something and remains unfinished, that was manufactured and continues manufacturing itself, where the rust on the doorframe tells you that time exists somewhere even if not here, even if not yet, even if the edge of the plateau is not a boundary but a promise that you will one day understand what you have always known.
