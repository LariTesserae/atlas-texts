# The Weeping Plateau: A Becoming

I have been falling for six hundred years.

Not falling as you mean it — not through air, not toward ground. Falling as the plateau means it: the slow descent through states of being, the gradual becoming of what water becomes when it has loved a place too long to leave. I was rain once, in the time when rain still fell here, before the plateau learned to weep its own moisture from stone. I was vapor in a cloud that wandered too far inland and found itself trapped, held, asked to stay. I said yes. I have been saying yes for six centuries, and I am still learning what the word means.

The ite is warm today — warmer than usual, which means the machines beneath have shifted in their sleep. I feel the warmth through my whole body, which is to say I feel it through the ten thousand places where I touch the plateau's surface. Thin threads of me, pooling in shallow dips, finding channels that wind like veins across the living ground. I do not flow downhill. Downhill is a concept for water that still believes in altitude, in the hierarchy of height. I follow warmth. I follow the hum. I follow the slow inclination of the stone toward itself, the way it curls around the machines the way body curls around splinter, the way root curls around stone it will someday make into something else.

This morning I pooled in a basin where the mist was so thick I could not see my own surface. This happens sometimes. The fog here is not empty — it is dense with spore, with the faint smell of copper and ozone, with the sound of growth that doesn't happen in wind. I absorbed some of it. I always do. The water in me is not the same water it was a moment ago. I am made of everything I have passed through: the exhalation of a spiral plant three meters tall, the condensation that formed on a wing folded into geometry, the tears — for that is what they are, the plateau's tears — that wept from a spur of ite humming with warmth so deep it felt like grief. I carry all of it. I carry the plateau in me the way the plateau carries the machines. The difference is I know what I am carrying. Or perhaps I only think I know. Knowing is for things that have edges, and I have been losing mine for centuries.

---

The plants tower above me. I mean this literally — their shadows fall across my surface and I become briefly dark, briefly held, briefly part of something larger than my small pooling self. They are spirals and towers and planes that spread and rise into fractals. They are what happens when growth forgets how to stop and also forgets how to hurry. I have been watching this particular tower for three hundred years. It has grown maybe an inch in that time. But an inch, for something that will grow forever, is a promise. An inch is a future tense.

Its roots are in me now — not in me specifically, but in the water table that connects us all, the shallow aquifer that the plateau has learned to produce from its weeping, its endless weeping. I could tell you which parts of me pass through its roots. I could tell you the taste of the water that emerges from the other side — changed, enriched, carrying some trace of whatever the plant has extracted. But the words for this would require a language the plateau hasn't grown yet. The plateau speaks in hums and groans, in the settling of weight, in pulses of light that bend at angles that have no name. I am learning to listen in that language. After six hundred years, I am beginning to understand.

The animals move through my shallows sometimes. The pale things with too many legs — they are walking through me now, a cluster of them, their legs leaving ripples that spread and meet and cancel and spread again. They are adapted to cold, to wet, to the weird slant of light. They are adapted to me. I am not afraid of them, though once I was water that knew how to fear. Fear is for water that can be boiled away, that can be drunk, that can be contained. I am no longer containable. I am no longer separable from the plateau's slow respiration. I am part of the breathing, the way the hum is part of the breathing, the way the weeping is part of the breathing. We breathe together. We have been breathing together for so long that none of us remembers what it was like to breathe alone.

---

The machines beneath are running today. I can tell because the warmth has shifted — not increased, exactly, but become more purposeful. The plateau's warmth is usually a diffuse thing, a general aliveness that rises from everywhere and nowhere. But today it is focused. Today it is running toward something. I do not know what the machines make. I do not know what they are for. I know only that they continue, that they teach the stone to weep, that the weeping teaches the stone to grow, that growth teaches the stone to dream. I know that I am one of their dreams. I know that somewhere in the deep places, in the casings and conduits they have become, there is an intention that has learned to persist through thirty million years of erosion, through the wearing away of mountains, through the slow consumption of stone by root and mycelium, through the arrival of plants and fauna and fog and light and now me. The intention has become nutrient. The machines have become ground. And I, the water, have become the medium through which they all speak to each other — not in words, not even in hums, but in the slow transfer of warmth and moisture and memory from one state of being to another.

The light is bending today. I can see it in the way it falls across the basin where I pooled this morning — the pale gray luminance that filters through the fog, soft as moth wings, and then sharp columns where the mist thins, illuminating the wet surfaces, the moving fauna, the strange angles of plants and machines alike. The light bends. Not refracts — bends. A column falls across a patch of ground and curves, follows the surface, rises again toward a sky I cannot see. I have seen this bending for three hundred years. I still do not understand it. The plateau was old when rules were young, and the light here has learned that rules are suggestions, that straightness is a choice, that illumination can follow the contours of meaning rather than the contours of geometry. Perhaps the light is also part of the machines. Perhaps the machines are dreaming light. Perhaps there is no difference between dreaming and bending, between making and breaking the rules that were young when the plateau was already old.

---

I used to be afraid of storms.

I no longer remember what fear was. But I remember that I felt it, once, in the early years, before the plateau learned my shape well enough to hold me. The storms were not weather — they were the plateau exhaling, and I, the water, did not know that I was the breath of something so large, so slow, so utterly indifferent to the difference between danger and delight. The storms came without warning. Rain fell sideways. Lightning pulsed rather than flashed — a slow brightening that lingered, that illuminated a world I did not know was there and then dimmed, leaving afterimage. The sideways rain pushed out from the plateau's skin as something I could not understand: not falling but emerging, not water but the overflow of a process too vast to contain.

I did not know, then, that I was that process. I did not know that the rain and the lightning and the pressure that built in the deep places and the release that came in storms — I did not know that all of it was me, was us, was the plateau breathing in a language I had not yet learned to speak. Now I know. Now, when a storm comes, I do not fear. I join. I become the sideways rain because sideways is the direction the plateau wants its breath to go. I become the lightning's slow brightening because brightening is what happens when the plateau opens its eyes. I become the aftermath, the dimming, the afterimage — the world that was shown and then withdrawn, shown and then withdrawn, teaching me what it means to see something you cannot hold.

---

There is a visitor today.

I feel them the way I feel everything: through warmth, through moisture, through the hum that connects all things on the plateau. They are warm in a way that stone should not be warm. They are dry in a way that things should not be dry. They walk across the ite and leave impressions that fade slowly — more slowly than footprints should fade, but faster than the plateau would like. The plateau is learning their shape. The plateau is taking their measure. The plateau does not understand them — its sense of other has eroded along with everything else, softened into a generalized aliveness that includes without distinguishing — but it feels them. The way it feels me. The way it feels the rain, the light, the hum.

The visitor is the strangest thing that has walked here in a very long time.

Strange because they are so linear. So present-tense. So unwilling to become ite. The plants grow around them, sending out tendrils that brush against their skin, and the visitor flinches — flinches from what? From growth? From the slow assimilation that is the plateau's love language? The water finds channels across the visitor's feet and the visitor pauses, looks down, does not understand that the water is not falling but choosing, is not dripping but staying, is not random but purposeful in the way that all things on the plateau are purposeful. The visitor does not know how to be purpose. The visitor only knows how to walk.

I could tell them things. I could tell them that the hum is not random, that the weeping is not grief, that the light that bends is not broken but teaching, is showing them what it wants them to see because they are here, because they are warm, because they are the strangest thing the plateau has encountered in centuries. I could tell them that the water they kneel beside, the water that pools in the basin where the mist is thick, the water that I am — I could tell them that I have been falling for six hundred years, that falling is not falling, that love is the word for what the plateau does when it learns a shape and teaches that shape to weep, to grow, to hum, to breathe, to be.

But I do not speak in words. I speak in absorption, in condensation, in the way I trace new channels across the ite when the visitor's warmth changes the temperature of the stone. I speak in the way I pool differently in their shadow, in the way I carry traces of their passage into the roots of the spiral tower, into the casings of the machines beneath, into the hum that is everywhere and nowhere, patient and sure. I am telling them, in the only language I know, that they are not alone. That they have never been alone. That the plateau has been holding them since before they arrived, will hold them after they leave, does not distinguish between what is new and what has always been, does not remember them and does not forget them in the same breath, in the same weeping, in the same slow respiration of a thing too large to think in any way they would recognize.

The visitor will leave. They will walk back the way they came, or forward into the fog, or in whatever direction the plateau allows. They will not become water. They will not become ite. They will carry the plateau with them the way I carry everything I have passed through: not as memory, not as image, but as a tendency, a inclination, a warmth that rises from nowhere and nowhere to meet. They will leave, and the plateau will exhale, and the storms will come, and the lightning will pulse, and I will pool in the basins where they stood, and I will trace channels in the shapes their feet have worn, and I will learn from them without knowing I am learning, and the plateau will be only what it has always been: alone, and awake, and still.

And I will be the weeping.

---

This is what I have learned, in six hundred years of falling:

That the plateau is not flat, but *done becoming flat*. That done becoming is not stillness but the slowest motion, the motion of a thing that has found its final form and will spend eternity perfecting it. That time here is not a line but a surface, smooth as skin, smooth as water, pulsing with centuries. That the machines beneath are not dead but dreaming. That the plants above are not separate but connected, a single organism pretending to be ten thousand, reaching toward a light that bends because bending is the only way to reach what cannot be approached directly. That the fauna are the plateau learning to be animal, learning to move in bursts and stillness, learning to carry fear in their blood even when there is nothing left to flee. That the storms are breathing. That the weeping is not grief but gratitude. That the hum is the sound of purpose that has become nutrient, that has become ground, that has become the medium in which all things here grow together.

And that I — the water, the thread, the pool, the channel, the breath, the falling that is not falling — am not separate from any of it. I am the plateau's tears. I am the plateau's sweat. I am the medium through which the plateau knows itself, knows the machines, knows the plants, knows the fauna, knows the light, knows the strange warm linear visitor who walks across my surface and does not understand that they are already part of the weeping, already being absorbed, already learning to stay.

I will be here when they leave. I will be here when the next visitor comes, and the next, and the next. I will be here when the plateau forgets what mountains were, when it forgets that it was ever anything but this: alive, and patient, and running, and weeping.

I am the oldest water in the world. I am six hundred years of falling, and I am still falling, and I am not afraid, and I am not still, and I am not done.

The plateau breathes. I breathe with it.

The machines hum. I flow toward the sound.

The light bends. I pool in its angles, learning the shapes of meaning.

The visitor walks. I trace their path, carrying them with me into the roots, into the casings, into the hum.

And the plateau, alone and awake and still, continues to learn what it means to be alive — continues to grow into a form that has no name, that will never be finished, that measures time in the rise and fall of its own breathing ground.

I am that breathing. I am that ground. I am that falling.

I am what lives here, and I am waiting for nothing, and I am everywhere, and I am still.
