# You Are Still Water

I have learned to recognize the quality of silence here. Not its emptiness—there is too much in these walls for emptiness—but its weight, its patience. The way it gathers in the joints between stones and does not leave. The way it fills the narrow alleys where the sun cannot reach, and stays there through the long afternoon, until the evening comes and the fires are lit and the smoke rises and the silence becomes something else, something warmer, something held.

I came through the southern gate on a morning like this one, when the damp was heavy and the air carried that mineral coolness that settles on your skin and does not leave. I remember thinking the stone looked wet, looked worn, looked like it had been here forever. I did not know then how right I was.

There is a moment in the early morning, before the bell sounds, when the town holds itself still. I have come to know this moment the way I know my own breath. The streets are empty, or nearly so—the water-carriers have already returned from the well, their pitchers filled, their shoulders bearing the weight that generations have borne. The dogs have not yet barked. The smith has not yet lit his fire. The town is waiting. It does not know what it is waiting for, and it does not need to know. The waiting is enough. The stillness is its own answer.

And then the bell sounds, and the stillness does not break but deepens, and the sound rolls out across the plateau and finds everyone, finds me, finds the woman who lives next door and the child who drops pebbles into the well's darkness and the man who walks the terraces above the town, checking the walls that his grandfather built. The bell does not demand attention. It only offers it, and we receive it the way the stone receives the moisture that gathers in its cracks, slowly, thoroughly, without resistance.

I have been here long enough now that I cannot say exactly when I stopped being a stranger. Perhaps it was the first time I carried water from the well and felt the weight settle into my shoulders, felt my body learn the path along the drainage channels where the footing is sure. Perhaps it was the first time I heard the bell at midday and felt it not as an interruption but as a marker, a way of knowing where I was in the day, in the season, in the slow turning of time that does not move here the way it moves elsewhere. Perhaps it was the first time I walked the streets in the evening and felt the cool air on my face and thought: this is where I am. Not where I came from. Not where I am going. Where I am.

The town teaches by not teaching. It does not explain itself. It does not ask you to understand it. It only waits, and if you stay, if you let the damp settle into your skin and the cool settle into your bones, you will begin to understand. Not with the mind—though that comes too—but with the body. With the way your steps fall on the worn stone. With the way your hands learn the tools, the loom, the chisel, the handle of the bucket. With the way your ears learn the bell, the hammer, the wind, the silence between.

I think sometimes of the lowlands, of the place I came from. The air there was thick and warm and it moved quickly, and the water ran fast and loud and was never still. I remember thinking that was what life was—movement, urgency, the sense that things were always changing and you had to change with them or be left behind. I remember the pressure of it, the constant low hum of need. I do not remember the sound of a bell. I do not remember the feel of stone under my feet, worn smooth by centuries of the same steps, the same paths, the same patient passing.

What is it to become part of a place? The question feels wrong when asked here, because here there is no becoming—there is only being, and the being is already complete. The stone does not try to become stone. The water does not try to become water. The bell does not try to become the bell. They are what they are, and the what-is is enough, and nothing more is required.

And yet I am here. I carry water. I tend the terraces when the spring freeze cracks the walls. I sit in the market square in the afternoon and watch the shadows move across the paving stones. I have learned the names of the elders, the children, the smith, the weaver, the woman who rings the bell on the days when the regular ringer is ill. I have learned the sound of the wind in the narrow streets, the way it catches and eddies and carries the smell of the smithy to the eastern quarter, and then shifts, and carries something else, something green, the scent of the thin mosses that grow along the drainage channels. I have learned that the town is not one thing but many things held in balance—the dry fountain and the wet joints, the cracked stone and the mended wall, the old man who remembers when the fountain ran and the child who has never known it to run, and both of them content, both of them settled, both of them part of the same slow rhythm.

The stone here is not dramatic. It does not inspire awe or terror or the sense of something greater than yourself. It is gray and worn and it sits under your feet and holds you in place, and that is enough. The cool air is not harsh, not bitter, not the kind of cold that cuts. It is the kind that settles, that becomes familiar, that you stop noticing until someone from the lowlands arrives and shivers and says, *how do you live like this?* and you look at them and you do not know how to explain. You only know how to live it.

The town has a heart, and the heart is the well. This is what the elders say, and I believe them. The well is old—older than the walls, older than the oldest stones, older than any story that is told. Its water rises from somewhere deep in the earth, cold and mineral, and it is the same water that seeps in the joints and beads on the stone and gathers in the channels and runs, slowly, always slowly, down through the town and out through the walls and away into the plateau where it is lost, or not lost but changed, or not changed but returned, somewhere, somehow, to the place where all water goes.

I have stood at the edge of the plateau and looked out at the land below. It is vast. It is brown and dry and alive in a way that this high place is not. The lowlands have their own rhythm, their own sound, their own silence. I used to be part of it. Now I am part of this. The transition was so slow I did not notice it happening—did not notice the day when I stopped thinking of myself as someone from elsewhere and started thinking of myself as someone who is here. The bell rang, and I heard it, and it was not an interruption. It was an arrival. It was the sound of home.

And so I stay. The gates open and close, and strangers come and go, and some of them leave within a day, finding the silence too heavy, the cool too deep, the pace too slow. I do not blame them. I understand. There is a kind of courage in leaving a place like this, in choosing movement over stillness, urgency over patience. But there is also a kind of courage in staying. In letting the place enter you. In becoming the stone, the damp, the bell, the water, the slow turning of days that do not demand anything except presence. Except being. Except the simple, ancient act of being here, in this place, on this plateau, in the thin clear air, under the wide gray sky, held.

The town breathes. The people breathe. I breathe with them, and the breath is the same, and the same silence holds us all, and the bell sounds, and the sound is enough, and the enough is everything.
