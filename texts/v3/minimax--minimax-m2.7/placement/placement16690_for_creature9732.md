Begin with the water, as everything here begins with the water.

You feel it before you see it—the warmth rising through the soles of whatever you wear on your feet, a heat that has nothing to do with the sun because the sun is somewhere above, somewhere behind the canopy, and this warmth comes from below, from the deep clay and the slow chemistry of things that have been breaking down and building up since before there were words for breaking down and building up. The ground gives slightly under your weight, not soft exactly but not firm either—it yields and holds, holds and yields, the way a body holds water when you press your hand into a full basin. You leave an impression that will take time to fade, time you do not have a way to measure here because time is also different here, slower and thicker and less like a river than like this mud, this mineral water, this substrate that has no intention of hurrying you along.

The first channel you encounter is not wide. You could step across it if you were certain the banks would hold, if you trusted the dark mud not to slide you into the rust-colored water that flows so slowly you cannot see it moving. You pause at the edge, and in that pause you begin to see the population of the surface—the thin film that is not quite oil and not quite reflection but something alive, a skin of microorganisms that tint the water in slow gradients from copper-green to pale amber where the sun finds a gap in the canopy and sends a column of warmth down to the surface. The film breaks where something beneath pushes up, and you watch the places where bubbles are rising—not dramatic, not like a spring or a boil, just a patient seepage of gas that has been traveling upward for longer than anything you know.

You crouch. The air is closer here, the humidity pressing against your skin, and you notice that the moisture has already found every surface—your forearms, the back of your neck, the places where fabric meets skin. You are becoming part of the wetness of this place, adding your own small contribution to the constant condensation. The water is clear enough that you can see the bottom, or what passes for bottom here—the layered mud, the occasional pale stone, the roots that descend from somewhere above and anchor themselves in the sediment. And in the water, in the column between surface and bottom, there is movement. Not fish, not yet, but larvae—the chironomid larvae that carry their red oxygen in bodies like small curved worms, the mosquito larvae that hang at the surface film with their breathing tubes piercing the tension, the damselfly larvae that cling to the roots with their patient triangular bodies and their mask-like mouths that will shoot out when something small enough passes within range.

You watch. The larvae do not know you are watching, or if they know they do not care, because they have no mechanism for caring—they are simply alive in the way of things that have always been alive in water like this, warm water, mineral water, water that carries the taste of sulfur and iron when you lean close enough to sense it on your tongue. You do not put your face in the water. You do not need to. The taste is in the air, in the damp that coats your lips, in the particular quality of warmth that rises from every surface that has been wet long enough to become something more than wet—to become stained, to become colonized, to become part of the slow factory that is this ground.

A dragonfly drops into the channel ahead of you, its wings catching the filtered light in a flash of iridescence. It hangs in the air above the water for a moment, its body still, its wings a blur, and then it is gone—not fled but simply moved, the way things here move without announcement, without drama, following the logic of hunger and warmth and the constant low-level imperative to find the next place, the next meal, the next moment of being alive. You do not see where it goes. You do not need to. It is enough to have seen it arrive.

---

The ferns grow close along the banks, their fronds broad and dark and slicked with condensation. You part them with your hands and the moisture beads on your palms, runs down your wrists, drips from your elbows if you hold your arms out long enough. The air inside the ferns is different—closer, warmer, more saturated with the smell of decay and growth happening at the same time, the one feeding the other, the other enabling the one. Fallen leaves have accumulated in the bases of the fronds, holding water like small cups, and in each cup there is a world: mosquito larvae, some of them, and the pale ghosts of something that might be planaria, and the discarded skins of insects that have moulted and left their papery husks behind. You feel observed, though nothing you can see is watching. The sensation is not unpleasant. It is the sensation of being part of something rather than apart from it.

A crab scuttles across your path, or rather across the mud beside the channel, its body carried low, its claws held forward in that posture that speaks to generations of predation, of needing to be ready. It stops when it sees you, or senses you, its antennae sweeping the air, its eyes on stalks turning toward the shape that does not fit the patterns of things it knows. You hold still. You have learned to hold still in places like this, or perhaps this place has taught you, has shaped you toward stillness because stillness is the currency of survival in a system where movement is always noticed, always registered, always potentially the wrong move in a game whose rules are written in the bodies of things that have already won and lost and won again across timescales you cannot imagine.

The crab holds still too. You wait together in the warm damp, two creatures paused at the edge of something neither of you will name. Then it moves—sideways, quick, its legs carrying it into the shadow of a prop root, and it is gone, or not gone but hidden, and in this place hidden is not the same as absent. Hidden is simply another way of being present.

You step closer to the channel. The bank gives slightly under your foot, and you feel the warm water seep up around the edge of your sole, finding the space between foot and ground, entering the small gaps in whatever you wear. The sensation is not unpleasant—it is warm, it is mineral, it is the taste of this place entering you as you enter it. You stand with one foot in the shallows and one foot on the bank, and for a moment you are balanced in the margin, the in-between, the place where water meets not-water and the populations of both overlap in the gray area where nothing is quite certain.

A sound reaches you—distant, not loud, but present. It takes a moment to identify: frogs. The evening chorus, or the chorus of a moment that might be evening because the light has shifted, because the heat has settled, because something in the chemistry of the water and the air has triggered the pattern in populations that have no calendar but carry their memory in their bodies. The sound rises from everywhere and nowhere, each call distinct if you listen but dissolving into a whole when you let the listening expand, when you stop trying to separate the voices and simply receive the chorus as a single utterance of this place being alive, being active, being the thing it has always been in the hours when the heat from below meets the cool from above and produces the conditions that have always produced frogs in this swamp, this delta, this warm drowning of a landscape that cannot decide whether it is wet or dry but has chosen, for now, to be both.

---

The darkness comes not as nightfall but as thickening—a way the air becomes more itself, more saturated, more the medium through which you move rather than the space you move through. The canopy above has closed completely now, or nearly so, and the light that reaches you is green and gold and amber, filtered and softened, reaching the ground in shafts and patches that make the distances hard to judge. You could be ten feet from the next channel or a hundred—the sound does not carry here, or rather it carries differently, absorbed by the wet growth, damped by the soft substrate, arriving at your ears without the information it would carry in a drier place.

You move anyway. You have to move, because standing still is also a choice and this is not the choice you want to make—not because standing still is wrong but because you want to see, to follow, to let the place guide you toward whatever it is that wants to be seen. The mud sucks at your feet, releasing you with a soft sound each time you lift, and you begin to read the ground the way the crabs and the frogs read it, by the impressions, the disturbances, the places where something has passed and left its mark in the soft surface. Here, a track—three-toed, clawed, probably a bird that has been wading in the shallows. There, a swept area where something heavy has been dragged, or where water has pooled and dried and pooled again, leaving a signature of level changes you cannot see but can sense. Everywhere, the small marks of small things: snail trails crossing the mud in silver lines that catch the dim light, insect damage on leaves that have fallen and will rot and become part of the sediment and the water and the next generation of leaves.

The leeches are here. You do not see them, but you know they are here the way you knew the crab was watching, the way you know when you are being observed in a dense forest that has always included things that observe. They live in the mud, in the still water, in the margins where organic matter accumulates. They wave in currents that are too slow to feel. They wait with patient bodies for the signature that says blood is near, and when they find it they attach and feed and drop away and return to the waiting that is most of their lives. You pick your way through the shallows with a caution that has become habit, that lives in your body now rather than your mind, and the caution is not fear but knowledge—the knowledge of a place that has always included things that take, and things that are taken, and the slow rotation between the two that is the grammar of this ecosystem.

A pool opens ahead of you, or rather you emerge into it—the channels have been threading between banks and growth, and now the growth opens into a clearing where the water lies still and shallow and filmed with that same metallic sheen, rust and copper and pale chalk-blue where the mineral water rises in its quiet way. The banks here are crusted with the residue of evaporation, pale crusts that crunch softly underfoot if you step on them, and the crunch is like old glass, like the sound of something that was liquid becoming solid becoming the memory of liquid. You stand at the edge of the pool and look down, and the water is a mirror that shows you a face you almost recognize, filtered and softened, the face of someone who has been in this place long enough to become part of its population, to add body heat to the heat that rises, breath to the breath that hangs in the humid air, presence to the presence that fills every gap and hollow and shadow.

The frogs are loud now. They are everywhere in this pool and the next and the next, calling from every hidden pocket, each species offering its particular contribution to the whole. Cricket frogs clicking in the wet air. Tree frogs high in the ferns, their voices carrying distance. Bull frogs pulsing bass notes that you feel in your chest before you hear them with your ears. The chorus is not random—it follows the rain, the rising water, the chemical triggers that each population carries encoded in its cells—and when it rises the air seems to thicken, to become more alive, to carry the pulse of generations of small lives that have always lived this way, calling and being called to, finding mates and laying eggs that will become larvae that will become frogs that will call and be found and lay eggs of their own, a river of small lives that never quite stops.

You listen. The sound enters you, or you enter it, and for a moment you are not the observer but one pattern among many, watching and being watched, moving through the same slow time that moves through everything here. The pool holds your reflection and the reflection of the canopy and the dimming light, and the frogs call on, and the water rises and falls in its patient seasonal rhythms, and the mineral crusts build and wash away across decades, and the vegetation grows and falls and rots and grows again, and nothing hurries because there is no need to hurry, because this place has been this way for longer than the word for this way existed, and will be this way after the word is forgotten.

You turn, and the ferns close behind you, and you move back into the warm dark, back into the thick damp, back into the place where the water rises through the ground and the small lives are many and the sound does not carry but you do not need it to carry because you are carrying it yourself now, the way this place has asked you to carry it, gently, over time, without particular notice.
