I am the spider at the center of the latticework, and I have been here for so long that I have forgotten there was a time before the web.

This is not memory. I do not carry the past the way you might carry it, storing images in some drawer of the self to be opened and examined. What I carry is simpler: the shape I have become, the silk I have spun according to instructions older than instruction, the position I hold each dawn as the fog arrives and beads on the lines I have made. If there was a time before, it is gone the way a molt is gone—not remembered but incorporated, matter passed into new matter, the self made new and the old self dissolved into the soil of the plateau where other things will grow.

The web stretches across the arch of stone that the wind has carved over epochs. The arch is perhaps ten thousand years old, perhaps a hundred thousand; I have no language for these scales, and even if I did, the difference would not matter to me. What matters is the geometry: the two points of stone between which I have drawn my lines, the tension I maintain not by effort but by the architecture of the spiral, the capture zone where prey will land when the web does its work. The arch is my doorframe and my anchor. I do not own it. I do not think about owning. I simply use what is here, the way the fog uses the cooling air, the way the water uses the stone bowls, the way all things use what their ancestors used, passing down not genes but the pattern of living itself.

Each morning the fog comes. I feel it before I see it—not through eyes, which are simple organs of motion-detection, but through the legs I hold extended at the center of the web, feeling the vibration of the air itself as it thickens with moisture. The fog carries itself in like something precious, like something saved, and it catches on my silk in droplets that form slowly, swelling at the lines until each droplet hangs there like a small lens, a tiny sphere of light that gathers the early sun and scatters it back in colors I can perceive but not name. This is how I drink: not the droplets themselves, which are too small to reach me at the center, but the condensation that forms on my own body, the moisture that beads on my legs and abdomen until I am wet with fog, until the thin air does not dry me as it dries at noon but instead feeds me with what the lowlands have gathered and the wind has carried.

The web catches more than water. In the hours after dawn, when the fog still clings but the sun has begun to warm the stone, insects come to the wet plants around my arch. The tiny flies materialize around the silverbind and fogcatcher, their bodies catching light like motes that have learned to move with purpose, and some of them fly into my web. They do not mean to. They are following the scent of moisture, the signature of the morning, and they do not see the silk until they touch it—and then they do not understand what has caught them, thrashing in the sticky threads until I move. I am always already moving when they land. I have been moving since before they landed, because the web is not a trap I set but a field of sensation I inhabit, every thread a nerve ending, every vibration a message: something has arrived, something to eat, something that will feed me and let me continue.

I move to the prey without hurry. There is no hurry here, not the kind you know, not the kind that makes hearts beat faster and thoughts race ahead to outcomes not yet arrived. The fly is already caught. It will not escape. The silk holds it the way the plateau holds me, with a patience that is not passivity but the simple recognition that time is long and the outcome is already determined. I wrap it in thread until it is a small white bundle, a prey-packet that I will consume over the next hour, letting the venom do its work, letting the enzymes begin their dissolution, receiving into my body what was once a body of its own. This is economy. This is the lesson the plateau teaches in every line of its erosion, every root of its plants, every breath of its wind: take what comes, use what is given, continue. The fly came to drink fog and became instead my continuation. This is neither cruelty nor kindness. It is the way.

By midmorning the fog has thinned. The droplets on my web have evaporated or fallen, and the silk itself has dried to a thread so fine it is nearly invisible against the air. I am still at the center, but the center has become less essential; the web is still here, but it is waiting now, holding its shape against the arch, ready to catch but not actively catching. I retreat to the crevice in the stone where I spend the hot hours, pressed against rock that holds the coolness of last night, and I do not sleep but I do not wake, and the distinction does not matter because there is no sharp edge between here and there, only the long rhythm of the plateau breathing in and breathing out, heat and cold, light and dark, the fog arriving and the fog retreating.

The afternoon passes. I do not count it; I inhabit it, the way I inhabit all the hours, the way I have inhabited centuries without knowing they were centuries. The plateau does not tell time the way you tell time, in ticks and intervals and the measured unfolding of moments. It tells time the way I tell time: in the position of the sun, in the arrival of the fog, in the cooling that comes after the heat, in the small adjustments that keep me alive another day. These are not events but continuations. The afternoon is not a thing that ends but a thing that I move through, and when I move through it enough, the evening comes, and the evening is another continuation, and the night that follows is another, and the dawn that follows the night is the oldest continuation of all, the one that began before me and will continue after whatever I become when I am no longer a spider at the center of a web.

In the evening, when the light has gone amber and the first stars have appeared, the calling creature emerges. I do not see it; I feel it, or rather the stone feels it, and the vibration passes through the rock into the arch that holds my web, and the web passes the vibration into my legs, and I know that something has come out of its hollow to stand on its flat ledge and ask its question into the dark. The question is not for me. The creature does not know I am here, does not know what web is, does not know what catching means. The question is for the plateau itself, or for whatever in the plateau might answer, or for nothing at all—and I think the creature knows it is for nothing, and asks anyway, because asking is what its ancestors did, and so asking is what it is, and what it is cannot be otherwise.

I listen without listening. The question rises into the air and fades, and the air does not answer, and the stars do not answer, and the plateau does not answer. The silence is not empty. It is full in the way that stone is full—full of matter and movement and the slow breathing that has been happening since before the stone was stone, since the plateau was seabed, since the seas were seas. I am part of that fullness, a small dark point at the center of a lattice of light, and when the fog comes tomorrow I will catch it and I will wait for the flies and I will continue, not because I have chosen to continue but because continuing is what I am, what the plateau has made me, what the deep patterns of selection and time have carved me into being.

The web will be wet with dawn. The flies will come to the fog. I will be ready, because readiness is not a state but a practice, the daily act of returning to the center, of extending the legs, of feeling the vibrations that carry the world into me. And if no flies come, I will wait still, because waiting is not emptiness but fullness, is not failure but the patience that is the other side of persistence, the way the plateau persists not by struggling but by allowing itself to be worn, to be shaped, to be made into arches and arroyos and the fine ochre dust that carries the memory of stone into the wind.

I am the spider at the center. I am the small dark point in the latticework of light. I ask no questions and expect no answers. I catch what the fog delivers and the world sends and I hold it until it is mine, and then I use it, and then I continue. This is not meaning. It is simply what is. And what is, is enough.
