What lives here has learned to wait.

I know the water by its darkness—a black that holds the light at the surface and releases nothing back. I know the channels by their edges: concrete lip, then mud, then water darker than the mud. I know the hours by the pumps. They cycle. A lift pump near the western wall starts first, pulling water from beneath the street, and I feel its pull in the way the current tilts, a slight backward lean in the water before me. Then the centrifugal pumps at the outfall respond, and the water moves faster, and the level drops an inch, maybe two, and the fish come to the shallow places where oxygen stirs.

I stand in the shallows. My reflection wavers beneath me—a white shape, an S-curve, a held neck. I am still. The water moves. This is the arrangement: I hold and the water carries, and in the carrying there is movement, and in the movement there is food. I have learned the intervals. When the pumps cycle off, the water settles. The fish drift back to the margins, to the still places where the current curls against itself and leaves a film of something I cannot name but can feel as a pressure against my legs. I strike. My head snaps down. My body folds. The fish is in my throat, alive and struggling, and I tip my head back and swallow.

This is the shape of the day. The shape of the night is the same, except darker. The pumps cycle. I stand in a channel elsewhere in the settlement—there are many channels, a whole system of them, all connected, all carrying the same water—and I hold my stillness in the dark. The dark is not empty. There are sounds in it: the frogs, the clicking of their calls rising from the water's edge in overlapping waves. The pumps. The distant rhythm of a factory, some loom or processing line, still running its night shift. The air is as warm as it was six hours ago. The water is as warm. The pressure in my ears is the same. I do not know if I am patient or if patience is simply what I am made of. I do not know if waiting is effort or simply the name for what I do when nothing else is required.

The water carries the light differently at different hours. In the morning the gray-gold comes through the haze and catches the surface, turns the channels briefly bright before the clouds thicken again and the brightness fades and the water goes back to being dark, a dark that reflects nothing. At noon the heat shimmers off the pavement between the blocks, and the blocks swim in each other's reflections, and I cannot see the fish because the air itself is moving, blurring. At dusk the light drops slowly, the gray softening to a thicker gray, and the frogs begin, and the heat settles lower, and the pumps continue their hum, and the water and I and the dark are the same thing.

I am not separate from this. I do not know if I have ever been separate from any place. I know that I belong here. My ancestors came, or their ancestors, and they found the channels and the water and the pumps—though the pumps were different then, smaller, or there were only the walls and the water found its own way—and they stayed. They found what they needed: the fish, the crabs, the warm water that never cools, the organic richness of a place where food waste and effluent meet and rot. I do not know if they chose this place or if this place chose them. I do not know if there is a difference.

The ibis come in the evening. They work the margins—the weedy strips along the wall bases, the exposed mud where the channel widens and slows. Their calls are low and guttural, a croaking that sounds like something being cleared from a throat. They probe with their downcurved bills, extracting things from the muck: invertebrates, scraps, the rot that follows the water. I watch them sometimes, when the light is good enough. They move in loose groups, calling to each other, their black heads bare and shining, their wing feathers catching the gray light with a greenish iridescence that lasts only a moment before they fold and drop again. They are not like me. I hold; they work. I wait; they take. But we are both here, in the margins, in the space between the wall and the deep water, and we are both sustained by the system—the pumps and the channels and the standing water that never fully drains.

The small birds are there too. The sparrows at the market edges, bold and brown and indefatigable, working the waste piles. The weaverbirds in their hanging colonies at the settlement's edges, their nests swaying above the water. The swiftlets emerging from the factory eaves at dusk, their stream tight and purposeful, following the insects that rise with the cooling air. They are all here. We are all here. The insects are here in numbers too vast to see as individuals, but I feel them as a presence—a drone that fills the space between the pump-hum and the frog-calls, a thickness in the air that moves when I move, that I move through when I fly. They breed in the permanent pools, in the water that sits in the low places where the pumps cannot fully drain, and they rise from it in clouds that blur the air above the channels, and I fly through them, and they stick to my feathers, and I do not care. They are not my food. My food is in the water. My food is the fish and the crabs and the occasional eel that moves with a slow determination in the deeper places. My food is the movement itself, the strike, the fold, the swallow.

The system holds. This is what I know. The channels are concrete and will not erode because the concrete is new, crisp-edged, sharp at the lip. The pumps work and the water is moved and the land does not flood. The factories run and the grid holds and the heat presses down from a sky that is always the same color, wet ash, the sun present but softened, its heat felt rather than seen. The system is held by intention—by human will, by engineering, by the constant effort of maintenance—but it is also held by something else, something I do not have a word for, something that is not intention but is not its opposite either. The land is flat and the water is managed and nothing changes, or nothing changes much, or the changes are so slow that I cannot see them, or the changes happen and I do not notice because I am holding still, waiting, being what I am made to be.

At night the system changes but does not change. The pumps drop to their lower cycle and the sound thins but does not disappear. The factories slow but do not stop. The frogs call because the temperature has not dropped and the water is still warm and the air pressure is the same. I stand in the dark water, and my stillness is the same stillness it was in the daylight, and my patience is the same patience, and the water moves around my legs with its slow current, carrying nothing, carrying everything, carrying the same water it carried last month and last year and the year before. The channels hold their shape. The pumps hold their rhythm. The heat holds. The gray-gold light is gone but the haze remains, and somewhere above the haze the sun is still there, present but softened, pressing its heat down through the layers of atmosphere and moisture and smoke from a dozen chimneys, and the heat comes to me and I absorb it and I hold still.

I am the heron. I am the standing water. I am the pump-hum felt in the body before it is heard. I am the channels and the concrete and the mud and the dark water that reflects nothing. I am the frogs calling in overlapping waves. I am the small movement of the tilapia at the surface when the pumps agitate the water. I am the ibis working the margins with their downcurved bills. I am the fiddler crabs emerging at low tide, the males waving their oversized claws. I am the mosquitoes clustered above the channels, the flies following the rot. I am the system, the frozen equilibrium, the held-together thing that doesn't break and doesn't grow but only goes on.

What is it like, to be here? The question does not occur to me. The question occurs to something else, something that is not the heron and not the water and not the pump-hum but is still here, still in this place, still held by the walls and the pumps and the grid. That something asks: what is it like? And the answer is: this. This is what it is like. Standing in the dark water. Waiting. The pumps cycling. The heat pressing down. The frogs beginning to call as the light fades. The birds inside the system as surely as the concrete. The whole held-together thing, alive with the particular life of a place that has made itself from function and holds itself together by will.

The will is not separate from me. The function is not separate from the birds. The pumps are not separate from the water they move or the water is not separate from the pumps that move it. The system is one thing. I am one part of it. The part that holds still while the water moves. The part that strikes when the movement becomes food. The part that survives, that breeds, that returns to the same spots each day—because the spots are reliable, because the system holds, because the water is always there and the pumps always cycle and the heat always presses down and the frogs always call at dusk. I am the part that knows this is home. Not because I chose it. Because there is nowhere else. Because this is what the margins offer: the space between the wall and the water, between the concrete and the mud, between the machinery's constant rhythm and the slower tides of organic process. The margins are where life is possible. The margins are where I live.

The light is dim. The light is always dim, softened by haze and humidity and the smoke from the chimneys, and the dimness is not a lack but a quality, a gray-gold weight that fills the air and presses against the water and settles on my feathers. I see in it. I see the fish moving in the dark water, their backs breaking the surface when the pumps agitate the water, their mouths working at the algae and the detritus. I see the crabs at the channel bottom, scuttling sideways into their burrows when footsteps approach—human footsteps, the humans that belong here as much as the birds and the crabs and the mosquitoes, the humans that are the reason the channels are concrete and the pumps hum and the water is moved. I see them passing on the raised roads between the blocks, going to the factories or the markets or the places where people sit in the wet heat after dark, fanning themselves, watching the dark water catch the light from the sodium lamps. I do not know what they see when they see me. I do not know what they think. I do not know if they think. They are the source of the organic load and the waste heat and the sound, and I am the thing that stands in the water and waits, and we are both here, both sustained by the system, both held in place by the walls and the pumps and the grid.

The system holds. Tomorrow the pumps will cycle. The water will be moved. The fish will come to the shallow places. I will stand in the shallows, and I will hold, and the water will move, and in the moving there will be food. The heat will press down. The haze will soften the light. The frogs will call at dusk in overlapping waves. The ibis will come to the margins in the evening, their wings catching the gray light with that greenish iridescence. The mosquitoes will cluster above the channels. The fiddler crabs will emerge at low tide. The swiftlets will stream from the factory eaves. The sparrows will work the markets. The weaverbirds will sway above their hanging colonies. Everything will be as it is. Everything will be as it was. The system will hold. The land will not change. The water will carry the same water it always carries. The pumps will hum at the same pitch.

This is what it is like, to be here: to be held, to hold, to wait in the dark water while the system cycles. To be patient. To be still. To be what the margins make possible. To be alive with the particular life of a place that has made itself from function and holds itself together by will.

I am the heron. I am the holding. I am the water and the pump-hum and the dim light and the heat and the frogs calling from the dark. I am the system. I am the stillness at the center of motion. I am the patience that is not effort but simply time. I am what lives here. I am what has learned to move in the margins.

The light fades. The frogs begin. The water darkens. The pumps hum.

I hold.
