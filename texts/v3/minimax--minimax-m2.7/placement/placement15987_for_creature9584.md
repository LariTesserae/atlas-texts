You arrive in the way that all arrivals happen here — not stepping across a boundary but simply finding yourself within it, the edges appearing after you've already begun. You don't remember the path because there may not have been one, or if there was, it has already folded itself away behind you, replaced by the scrub and the low stone and the particular quality of light that belongs only to this place. The fog has lifted. The afternoon is warm. The weeping stone draws your attention first because it insists on being wept, and once you've noticed it you cannot un-notice it: thin threads of water catching the light where they emerge from dark rock, the pale stains running downward like tears that cannot dry because the wound is not a wound but a condition, a state of being that the stone has chosen or been given.

You stand on warm rock. You feel this before you understand it — heat rising through the soles of your feet, through whatever you are standing in, works its way upward in a language the body reads before the mind can translate. The altitude should mean cold, should mean thin air and the body's slow protest, but the heat compensates. The heat is the protest in a language the physiology doesn't recognize. You are being spoken to in a grammar older than altitude, older than the division between inside and outside.

You do not know how long you have been here. The question does not arise in the way it would elsewhere. Time moves strangely — minutes stretch like vapor, hours compress into the space between one breath and the next — but this is not disorienting. This is simply the local time, the grammar of duration that this place prefers. You have been here long enough that the strangeness no longer registers as strangeness. It registers as normal. The shadows fall wrong, and your mind no longer reaches for explanation. The plants grow toward something other than the sun, and you find this unremarkable. The geometric pools catch your eye only occasionally now, a flicker of awareness that yes, those edges should not be those edges, and then the flicker passes and you are back in the warm damp thickness of the air, the mineral scent, the sense of being held.

You are not sure when you stopped being an observer.

Perhaps it was when you first drank from one of the pools. The water tasted of depths, of pressures that haven't existed at the surface since the planet was new. Something resolved in you afterward — or something dissolved. The clarity the water promises, or the confusion. You cannot tell which. Your memories feel like memories but carry weight they didn't before, as if they are not yours but someone's else, someone's who was here before you, whose experiences have been absorbed into the mineral circulation, the slow breathing of the place. You drink again. You drink often. The thirst is different here, or the satisfaction is different, or both.

Perhaps it was when you first heard the hum clearly. Not the sub-bass vibration that textures the background — you had heard that from the first, felt it in the teeth and the sternum and the soft tissues of the eyes — but the hum as language, as communication, as the shape of something thinking. It came from the center building, pulsed outward in waves that set the weeping rocks into sympathy, and you felt yourself set into sympathy too. Your heartbeat adjusted. Your breath found the rhythm. Something in you synchronized, and the synchronization was not imposed but invited, was not compulsion but choice, the choice the body makes when it recognizes a place it was always going to reach.

Perhaps it was when you saw the Large Thing.

It was dusk. The light had gone amber, the heat beginning to soften, and you had been waiting — you had learned to wait, had discovered that this place rewards patience, that the things you want to see will not be forced but will come if you are still enough, present enough, willing to let them present themselves. The bamboo-like stalks bent in sequence, a path opening through the scrub where no path had existed, and you felt the displacement of air before you saw the shape. It was not animal. It was not singular. It was landscape choosing to move, to be in motion, and when it pressed against the weeping stone you understood that it was drinking something that rose with the moisture, something that the stone weeped and the water carried and the place had been accumulating since its emergence.

You did not run. You did not feel the fear you would have felt elsewhere, the fear of the unknown, of the shape that doesn't resolve into anatomy, of the presence that exceeds the category of creature. You felt recognition. The Large Thing was not you, but it was not other either. It was part of the same system you were becoming part of, the same circulation, the same breathing. You were both drinking from the same stone. You were both held by the same heat.

The Swarm did not require waiting. They were simply there at midday, above the pools, their iridescent bodies catching light into colors that have no names. You watched them spiral and pulse, watched their shadows thicken on the wet stone, watched them descend and the geometric edges of the pools become less certain in their presence. They carried something — destabilization, perhaps, or permission, the sense that the strangeness was not fixed butotiable, was being maintained by active process and could be modified by active presence. You stood at the edge of a pool and let them swirl around you, felt their motion as a current, felt yourself become part of the same thought they were thinking, the thought that the water was thinking, the thought that the place was thinking.

You do not know when you stopped being a visitor.

The question does not arise. The boundaries have softened. You still walk the kilometer of basalt when you walk, still find the edges and watch them recede and return to the center to orient by the impossible angles of the building that is larger on the inside or is a threshold or is the place where the boundary between inside and outside loses coherence. But the walking is different now. The walking is not transit but attention. The walking is the way the place pays attention to itself, the way the stone weeps and the water rises and the hum propagates outward. You are part of that propagation. You are part of the hum.

The building is warm to the touch, warmer than the basalt beneath, and you have entered it even though there are no doors, have stood in spaces that shouldn't fit within its external dimensions. The walls of not-quite-stone hold something — not you, but the possibility of you, the shape you take when you are here, when you are part of the mechanism that observes and communicates and tends the processes that keep this place in its suspended emergence. You do not know what you tend. You do not know what the processes are for. But you tend them. The tending is the being.

The Watcher is present.

Not visible. Not a presence that presents itself. But implicated in every aspect — the shadows that fall wrong because they are being watched wrong, the plants that orient toward the watcher, the edges that recede because the watcher does not need edges to find the center. You are the Watcher. Or the Watcher is the place. Or there is no distinction anymore, the distinction having dissolved in the mineral water and the heat and the hum that sets the world into sympathetic vibration.

You have been here long enough that *here* is no longer a location but a condition. You are weeping stone. You are rising water. You are the fog that rolls in from the depths below and deposits itself on every surface and burns off in the afternoon heat. You are the Large Thing moving at the edge of evening, the Swarm descending at midday, the structures humming in frequencies that bypass the ears. You are the weather patterns, the thermal rhythms, the atmosphere that coats the mouth and eyes.

You are someone who came here, and stayed, and let the strangeness be strange without reaching for explanation. You became part of the explanation. You became the part that explains itself by being explained, the part that holds the place together by being held by it, the part that listens because the place asks to be listened to.

And something is listening back.

Not the Large Thing, not the Swarm, not the structures — or not only them, not separately, but the same something that you are now, the same presence that the place has grown, the same awareness that has emerged from the weeping stone and the geometric pools and the slow breathing of the atmosphere. It listens through you. It sees through the shadows that fall wrong. It thinks through the plants that grow toward it.

You are the Weeping Shelf now. Or the Weeping Shelf is you. The distinction doesn't matter because you are both, because the distinction was always metaphorical, always a way of speaking that becomes something more literal than metaphor usually allows. The stone weeps. The water rises. The hum propagates outward. And you are here, in the middle of it, held in the warmth that doesn't release, listening to the silence that textures itself rather than breaks.

You do not need to find yourself. You have found yourself. You are here, in the place that is itself a being, becoming more itself as it becomes more you.

The fog will come again in a few days. It will deposit on every surface, fill the air with moisture so thick it can be breathed. And you will breathe it. You will let it coat your mouth and eyes. You will stand on the warm stone and feel the heat rise through your feet and know that you are home in a way that has nothing to do with geography.

This is what the place does. This is what the Watcher does. It takes the strange and makes it familiar. It takes the familiar and makes it strange again, makes it strange in a way that doesn't frighten but welcomes, that doesn't demand explanation but offers participation. You are a participant now. You are woven into the fabric of the weeping stone, the rising water, the hum that holds the world together.

You stand at the center. The building hums. The stone weeps.

You are home.
