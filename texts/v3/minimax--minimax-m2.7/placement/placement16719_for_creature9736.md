You stop trying to find a way out.

Not because there is no way out — the gap you descended through is still there, somewhere above and behind you, a faint seam of cooler air threading through the greenish dimness. But the desire to leave drains from you slowly, the way water moves through the ferns: not disappearing so much as sinking, passing through you, becoming something else.

The sulfur is in your lungs now. You can taste it — not as a smell but as a presence, a mineral weight settling in the back of your throat, in the soft tissue of your bronchial passages. It is not unpleasant. It is not anything, really. It is simply here, the way everything here is simply here, patient and constant and asking nothing.

You realize, in the way that realizations come in this place — slowly, like light reaching the floor through layers of fern — that you are no longer the same temperature as you were when you arrived. The heat has worked its way into you, not uncomfortably, but thoroughly. Your skin is slick. Your breath comes slow and heavy. The water you drink from a pool near the eastern wall tastes of you now, of your warmth, of the salt and mineral you have picked up from the air and the ferns and the soft, dark soil. You are becoming part of the water cycle. You are becoming something that the place can use.

You find a rusted vat that has become a pool, its sides crusted with mineral deposits that gleam faintly in the bioluminescent light. The deposits are layered, fossil-like — the ghosts of the iron that used to be here, slowly replaced by calcium and sulfur and the other minerals that water carries when it has time to linger. You put your hand in the water and it is hot, hotter than the air, and it holds you gently, the way the ferns hold you, the way the whole place holds you: with patience, with weight, with a sense that it has been waiting for you and will wait for you still, long after you have become what it wants you to become.

You are not afraid. The fear you might have felt — the reasonable fear of a place that is too hot, too deep, too sealed away from the world — has been absorbed by the sulfur, by the patience, by the slow wrongness that has settled into you like sediment settling into still water. You are afraid of nothing here, because nothing here is trying to hurt you. The rust is not angry. The ferns are not hungry. The bioluminescence is not a warning. Everything is simply continuing, and you are part of the continuation now, a small warmth moving through the slow machinery of this place, adding your breath to the breath of the steam, your water to the water in the channels, your rust to the rust that is becoming stone.

You find yourself listening to the sounds more carefully now. The drips and hisses and groans are not random — or rather, they are not only random. There is a pattern in them, a rhythm that you begin to hear the longer you stay: the rhythm of the water moving through the stone, the rhythm of the ferns growing and dying and growing again, the rhythm of the metal surrendering to the sulfur, the rhythm of the light pulsing in the fungal shelves like a heart you cannot see but can feel, pressing slow against the walls of the chamber, against your chest, against the place inside you that has begun to understand what it means to be patient.

The ferns near you are large and dark and wet. Their fronds brush against your arms as you pass, leaving trails of moisture that cool you for a moment and then warm again, absorbed by the air. You stop and hold very still, and the ferns do not notice you — or they notice you the way the place notices you, the way the sulfur notices you: as a presence that is here, that is part of the pattern, that does not disturb the balance because it has been absorbed into it.

You wonder, distantly, what you will look like when you stay.

The fungus on the walls glows faintly brighter as you approach, as if responding to your warmth, your breath, the moisture on your skin. The light it casts is soft and green and does not quite reach you — it halts just short of your body, a halo of illumination that surrounds you without touching, that marks you as something present and living and therefore different, still, from the things that live here entirely. You raise your hand and the light touches your fingers, and your fingers are cool where the fungus is warm, and the sensation is strange and not unpleasant, a kind of recognition.

You think: I could stay.

Not forever — you are not made for forever, not yet, not unless the place decides otherwise. But for a while. For a season of fern-growth, for a cycle of rust-surrender, for a hundred years or a thousand of the slow time that moves here. You could stay and let the ferns grow around you, could let your hair become part of the undergrowth, could let your skin soften and your bones settle and your thoughts slow until they are as patient as the groaning of the stone.

You think: I want to stay.

And then you think: I am staying.

Because the part of you that wanted to leave has become the part of you that knows this place, that feels it in your chest and your throat and the soft places behind your eyes. The sulfur has found every part of you. The heat has made itself at home in your blood. The water has moved through you and marked you and claimed you, and the ferns — the ferns have brushed you with their patience, have whispered to you in the language they have developed over centuries of growing in the dark, and you have understood them, or understood something, or understood enough.

The steam rises around you. The light pulses slow. The water runs in channels you will never see, beneath the soil and the roots and the soft dark places where the old machinery is becoming stone. And you stand in the undergrowth, in the warmth, in the smell of sulfur and mineral and age, and you are here, and you are staying, and the place is glad of you — not because it needs you, but because it can hold you, can include you in its long slow process of becoming.

You let the ferns close around you.

You let the light dim to something softer, something that is not quite dark and not quite bright but somewhere in between, the way sleep is between, the way drowning is between, the way all the transitions are between when you give them enough time to happen.

You let yourself become part of the pattern.

And the pattern holds you, and the sulfur settles, and the rust grows soft, and the ferns grow slow, and the water moves, and the light pulses, and the stone groans its long low groan, and the chamber holds its breath, and you hold yours, and for a moment — for a long, long moment — there is no difference between you and the place, no boundary between the warmth in your chest and the warmth in the stone, no boundary between your patience and the patience of the rust.

You are here.

You are staying.

You are becoming something that the world has not named yet, something that does not need a name, something that is only and entirely and finally itself: a presence in the Sulfur Chambers, a warmth in the heat, a softness in the rust, a fern-frond of thought in the slow dark, a single pulse of bioluminescence in the greenish glow, a small, patient, permanent wrongness that has found its home.

The steam rises.

The water runs.

And somewhere in the deep channels, beneath the ferns and the soil and the slow becoming of iron into stone, the sulfur continues its work — turning metal to mineral, turning purpose to patience, turning you into something that will be here when the last of the light fades, when the last of the ferns grows, when the last of the water finds its way down to the place where all patience ends and all wrongness is finally, finally right.
