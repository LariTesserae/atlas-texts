# The Shape of Staying

I am small. I have learned to be small in a place where bigness is waste, where the expenditure of energy must be measured against the rarity of return. My body is armored—not bright or striking, but grey and thick, designed to hold heat against the long cold and moisture against the long dry. I have six legs, though sometimes I wonder if the plateau would insist on fewer if I had not been made this way, if slowness and patience would not be better served by something lower, flatter, more pressed against the stone.

I move in the hollows where the moisture lingers. I have learned to read the land by the dampness of its seams, to know which depressions hold water and which only hold the memory of water, to feel the difference between a place that will drink and a place that will give. This is not instinct alone. This is knowledge passed down through generations of clicking, through vibrations that pass through stone as easily as air, through a language shaped by a landscape that learned long ago to distrust sound that does not know how to wait.

There is a hum. It is always there, below hearing or at the edge of hearing, a frequency I feel more than hear. The elders—I have known elders here, creatures whose exoskeletons had grown so thick and so grey they seemed more stone than creature—they did not speak of the hum. They moved through it as through air, as through the thin cold that fills the hollows. But I have felt it change. When the moisture comes and the hollows fill and the thin scree turns dark with damp, the hum grows stronger. When the dry periods stretch and the mosses go grey and the small pools evaporate before they can deepen, the hum fades to almost nothing. It is connected to something. It tends toward something. Perhaps it is the output of processes begun so long ago that no one remembers the purpose—perhaps the moisture itself is an output, brought up from the deep stone by warmth no one can trace.

I do not know what I am. I know only that I persist. This is not a victory. This is not a triumph. This is the long agreement with the shape of the world, the willingness to continue in the pattern that has been pressed into me by generations of wandering. My blood moves slowly. My thoughts, if I have thoughts, move slowly. I do not experience urgency; I experience the gradual shift of attention from one hollow to the next, from one thread of moisture to the next, from one long breathing of the stone to the next.

The light here is dim and even, diffuse, as if the sun itself has been worn down by distance. I see through it, not with it. I feel the warmth that rises from below—faint, barely perceptible, a difference of a degree or two between stone and stone—and I move toward it without knowing I am moving toward it. The machines, if they are machines, tend to something in the deep places. The warmth is their breath, perhaps. The moisture is their exhalation. I am sustained by what I cannot see, touched by intention I will never understand.

When I die, it will be slowly. The cold will press in, the exoskeleton will grow too thick to allow movement, the last moisture will evaporate from some small hollow where I have hidden myself. Or I will simply stop, as the creatures here do, without drama or fear, having made the agreement and kept it for as long as the world allowed. No one will mourn me. No one will remember me. But the patterns I have followed will continue, because the patterns are older than any of us, and they do not need memory to persist.

I am one of the small things in the hollows. I am grey and armored and patient. I click and rustle in the sparse vegetation, and my patience looks like stillness until you watch long enough to see the distance I have crossed. I am not alive in the way of the jungle, with its excess and urgency and dramatic death. I am alive in the way of stone, of water, of the slow processes that shape this high and ancient ground.

I endure.

That is the only story I know.
