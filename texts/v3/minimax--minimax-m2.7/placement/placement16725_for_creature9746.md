You arrive in stages, or you arrive all at once, and the difference does not seem to matter.

The glass is the first thing. Not the color—you will learn that color is unreliable here, that the particulate does something to wavelength, that the plants have taught your eyes to expect a shift—but the texture of it, the way it holds your weight without yielding. You expected it to be cold. Everything you know about volcanic glass says cold, says the memory of heat rather than heat itself. But it is warm. Not the warmth of sunlight on stone, which you have felt and can recall. Something else. A warmth that seems to come from below, from the deep place where the lava pooled and cooled into this strange perfection, but also a warmth that seems to come from the plants themselves, from their patient metabolizing, from the light they drink and the glittering they exhale.

You are standing, then. You must have been standing for some time, though you do not remember the moment of arrival. The light is even, sourceless, bright. The particulate drifts. The hum is beneath you, or within you, or perhaps you are the hum now, a vibration in the same register as the ground.

You learn to walk.

This takes longer than it should. The distances are not what they seem, and you cannot trust your legs to carry you the way your legs have always carried you. The first step is shorter than you intend. The second is longer. By the fifth step you have discovered that you can step over something you have not yet reached, that the space between where you stand and where the violet sprawl begins is both six feet and infinite, and you choose to believe the six feet because it allows you to keep moving, and moving feels important, though you cannot remember why.

The silver ones are closest.

You walk toward them and they do not retreat, because they have never advanced. They simply are, in their height, in their crystalline lattice-roots fusing with the glass until the boundary becomes a matter of theory. You reach out to touch one and your hand passes through—or it does not pass through, it lands, it rests against a surface that is smooth and warm and does not feel like glass or like plant. It feels like being understood. This is not a metaphor. The surface receives your touch and something in it responds, a subtle shift in the lattice, a settling, a recognition that you are here and that your warmth is a kind of warmth and that this is enough.

You leave your hand there longer than you intend.

The violet sprawl is where you expected it to be, which surprises you, because you had stopped expecting the basin to follow your expectations. It is matte where the silver is reflective, soft where the silver is bright, and when you step over it—as you must, because it spreads across the glass in all directions and there is no path through it that does not involve stepping—your foot finds its edge and the edge does not yield. Dense. The word the description used, and you understand it now in your body: this is a density that has nothing to do with weight or compactness and everything to do with presence. The violet sprawl is here, fully here, occupying its space with a certainty that makes your own presence seem thin by comparison.

You are thin. This occurs to you slowly, the way most things occur to you here.

Your edges are not as sharp as they were. Your outline, if you could see it—if you could find a surface smooth enough to reflect you—would be blurred, softened, fraying at the limits where your warmth meets the basin's warmth and the two become difficult to separate. You are becoming dense, or you are becoming something else, and the difference between the two possibilities is not clear and may not matter.

The red-black clusters pull at you.

You do not look at them directly. You have learned that the eye slides away, that the gaze finds something else to settle on, that the relationship between you and the red-black is a relationship of angles and avoidance. But you feel them. A pull in the chest, in the place where you imagine your center to be, a tugging toward the spirals that revolve around points that seem to pull space itself. You do not go to them. You are not ready to go to them. Perhaps you will never be ready. Perhaps readiness is not the relevant concept here, and what you are doing instead—circling, approaching, stepping back—is itself a form of engagement, a conversation conducted in movement rather than language.

The fauna appears on what you have decided to call the fourth day.

You are sitting by then, or you have become sitting, your body folded against the base of a silver stalk whose branches catch the light and throw it sideways in brief flares. You are not tired. You are not rested. The concepts have become confused, folded into each other, their edges soft. You sit and you breathe the ozone-and-metal air and you watch the particulate drift and you do not think, not in the way you used to think, because the hum has become too present, too much a part of the space between your thoughts, and you cannot find the silence you need to think clearly.

The fauna does not announce itself.

It is simply there, between the stalks, moving with that deliberate patience you were told to expect. You watch it and you cannot hold its shape in memory, cannot assemble its features into a coherent image. It is a passage. It is a motion that leaves no trace. It moves through the plants and the plants shift—not visibly, not in any way you could describe to someone who had not seen it—but you feel it, a subtle reorientation, a settling of the distributed thought as it notes the passage and files it away.

The fauna does not look at you.

You wait for it to look at you. You wait for some acknowledgment, some sign that you have been noticed, that your presence in the basin is a presence in some sense other than the merely physical. But it moves on, and the silence closes behind it, and you are alone again with the hum and the light and the warm glass beneath you.

You are not alone.

You know this now in a way that bypasses thought, that does not require the silence you cannot find. The basin is aware of you. The plants are aware of you. The distributed thought that spirals around the red-black centers has noted your presence and found it unremarkable, which is somehow more frightening than being found remarkable, because it means that you are part of the pattern now, another element in an arrangement that has room for you, that was perhaps designed to include you, though you do not know by whom or for what purpose.

You stand.

The motion is easier than it was, or you have simply stopped expecting motion to be difficult. The glass holds your weight. The silver stalk catches the light and throws it sideways. The violet sprawl spreads across the basin in all directions, matte and soft and impossibly present. The particulate drifts. The hum continues.

You walk toward the ridge.

You arrive somewhere else.

The number of steps changes depending on how you count. This no longer disturbs you. The wrongness is architecture, and you have learned to live in architecture that was not built for you, to find the rooms it offers and to rest in them, to let its angles become your angles, to let its stillness become your stillness.

The plants drink light. The glass holds. The hum continues, low and patient, a voice speaking below hearing, a voice that has been speaking so long it has forgotten it was ever anything other than sound.

You sit by the silver stalk. You watch the light move sideways. You breathe the air that smells of ozone and metal and burnt sugar, and you do not count the days, because the light does not change and the warmth does not vary and time here is a courtesy rather than a measurement, and you have given up the courtesy of time for the presence of this place, and you have not made a bad trade.

You are still here.

That is something.

That is, perhaps, everything.
