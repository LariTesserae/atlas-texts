# On Finding the Unfindable Path

I have been climbing for what feels like days, though the light has not changed—still that soft diffused silver that seems to come from everywhere at once, as though the air itself has been lit from within. My legs ache in a way that feels correct, the kind of tiredness that means something has been used as it was meant to be used. The cable car that brought me up released me onto the highest terrace, and from there I began to descend, not on any particular route but simply following what presented itself: a flight of worn stone steps, a narrow path between walls of vine-heavy brick, a channel walkway where the water ran so still I kept forgetting which direction was forward.

The city does not present itself to you. You do not arrive at it so much as find yourself within it, the way you might find yourself within a dream that has already begun. I have tried, in these first hours, to hold a map in my mind—the terraces descending, the levels marked by the density of canopy, the implied geometry of a mountain that has been so thoroughly built upon that its original angles are now theoretical. But the map dissolves every time I try to hold it. The place resists being mapped. It wants to be walked.

---

There is a particular quality to the silence here that I did not expect. Not the absence of sound—there is plenty of sound, the hum and click and distant rhythm of machines, the layered birdsong, the soft persistent whisper of water—but a silence beneath the sound. A kind of listening. The city is always listening to itself. I became aware of this when I paused in an arcade to watch a fountain that had been flowing so long its basin had turned green, the water barely moving, still as glass except for the occasional shiver of some current too subtle to see. I stood there for what might have been minutes or might have been longer, and in that standing I began to feel the vibration of the place in my chest—not the machines, not exactly, but something deeper, something that moved through the stone and the water and the roots in the same way that thought moves through a mind, without direction, without effort, simply present.

I am a stranger here. I arrived with nothing but what I could carry, and what I could carry was very little. The people who live in this place look at me with the same patient attention they look at everything—with that quality of regard that seems to go past the surface and into something further, something I cannot name. They do not ask where I am from. Perhaps they know that the answer would mean nothing here, that origins dissolve in the humid air the way the clouds dissolve when they pool against the terraces, breaking apart and rising again into a sky that has been made strange by altitude and by the particular chemistry of a city that generates its own weather.

---

I found a room today. Or rather, the room found me, or we found each other in the way that finding happens here—not through seeking but through the kind of attention that opens when you stop expecting anything in particular. It was a hollow in the concrete wall of a lower terrace, barely large enough to sit in, its edges softened by moisture and the slow growth of something that might have been lichen or might have been the beginning of moss. The hollow was dry, which surprised me—I had grown accustomed, in the upper levels, to water finding every depression, pooling in every low place. This one was dry and warm and when I pressed my back against the concrete I could feel the heat from within, the residual warmth of the city’s old heating system, still running after centuries, channeling heat from the factories into channels and pipes and, eventually, into the walls themselves.

I sat there for a long time. I watched a cat move along the edge of a rooftop, its silhouette sharp against the diffuse light, and I listened to the water somewhere below, a sound like breathing, and I felt the hum of the place move through me the way the hum moves through everything—the stones, the roots, the pipes, the people who have been here long enough to become part of the listening.

I do not know how long I will stay. I do not think the question makes sense here. The terraces will continue their descent whether I walk them or not. The water will rise from the mountain's heart and return to the mountain's heart in its old slow cycle. The machines will hum. The birds will sing their mechanical songs. The cats will move through the canopy with their patient, uninterested gazes, and the children will play in the hollows, and the old people will sit until the moss begins to grow.

And I, if I am patient—if I let the place teach me its rhythms, its language of echo and resonance, its grammar of water and root and stone—I may begin to understand what it means to live here. Not to live in the city, exactly, but to live as the city lives, as something that has forgotten the distinction between building and growing, between remembering and forgetting, between the self and the stone it presses against in the humid dark.

The light is changing now. The clouds are rising from the valley below, the way they do in the late afternoon, and the terraces are beginning their slow transformation from silver to something deeper, something more blue, something that feels like the color of water seen through closed eyes.

I will stay for the rain. And then I will stay for what comes after.
