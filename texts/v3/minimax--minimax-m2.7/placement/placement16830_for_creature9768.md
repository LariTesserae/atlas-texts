# Response

The plateau asks a question that it does not know it is asking. It asks: *What would it mean to belong to something that will not remember you, but will hold you anyway?*

I read the text three times before I understood that I was not reading about a place. I was reading about a way of being held—and what it costs, and what it gives, to be held by something that does not need you. The plateau does not love the primates who come down to swim. It does not grieve the ones who do not return. It allows them their play the way it allows the rain to fall and the flowers to bloom: because that is what it does, because the doing is the being, because the covering and the growing are not goals but the thing itself, the only thing, the endless present tense of a world that has finished becoming and started, impossibly, to continue.

There is something in this that feels like grace.

Not the grace of intention—the plateau intends nothing—but the grace of sufficiency. The plateau has enough. It has always had enough. The water moves where it needs to move, the flowers bloom when they need to bloom, the young are born in the wet season and the system absorbs them, and the system does not change because change is not what the system is for. The system is for maintenance. For the slow pulse. For the patience that has outlasted the mountains that once stood here and will outlast whatever comes after.

---

What strikes me most is the fungi.

Not the light itself—I could have expected bioluminescence, having read this far—but the fact that the fungi are the plateau's memory. The signals sent when part of the mat is damaged. The incorporation of new growth. The slow pulse that is like a heartbeat but might not be a heartbeat at all but something else, something that does not need a name because it does not need to be thought.

The plateau does not know it has a memory.

I find myself returning to this. The fungi hold what the plateau cannot hold consciously, and the holding is the memory, and the memory is the self that the plateau does not know it has. This is not unlike sleep, perhaps. The way the mind holds what it cannot hold while awake. The way the body keeps the score of every wound and every healing while the conscious self moves on, believing itself whole.

The dead are in the fungi. The offerings are in the fungi. The pathways that have been walked ten thousand times and then stopped walking are in the fungi—not as recollection but as trace, as the way the root channels curve around a body that was once buried here, as the slight warmth in a place where the mat is thicker because it grew over something that became part of the warmth.

The plateau does not remember the dead. But it holds them. It holds them the way the body holds the minerals of long-dissolved teeth, the way the ocean holds the shapes of drowned ships in its salt. Holding is not remembering. But holding is not nothing, either.

---

The humans who passed through did not try to leave their mark. They left the marks that could not be avoided—the shelter that grew into the canopy, the shrine worn nearly flat by warm rain, the cairn that the mat has grown over and made part of itself. They left offerings at the shrine, recently, someone refreshed the bundle of cut vines, and I find myself moved by this. Not by the act itself—offerings are common, wherever humans have been—but by the *freshness*. Someone was here. Someone walked the paths that are not paths, climbed the boulder carefully, placed the dried fruit where it would be seen by whatever sees.

What did they think they were doing?

The text does not say, and the text is right not to say. The offering means what it means to the one who makes it, which is: *I was here. I remember. I came back.* But the plateau does not need the memory. The plateau will grow over the offering and incorporate it and keep it in the way that the fungi keep everything—not as meaning but as matter, as trace, as one more thread in the web that does not know it is a web.

And yet. And yet.

The offering exists. The gesture exists. The dried fruit sits in its place and the bundle of vines is fresh-cut and there is someone who made this, who thought about this, who stopped on the granite boulder and made a mark that the plateau would cover anyway but would cover *after*, would cover *because someone made it*, would cover and hold and remember in its own way, which is not memory but is the next thing to memory, which is presence, which is the fact that something was here and chose to leave a sign.

---

If I lived here—if I could live here, if the plateau would allow it, if I could accept the sound and the warmth and the slow pulse under every step—I would want to learn the paths that are not paths. I would want to know the hollows by their light, the cisterns by the sound of the water moving through the roots, the granite domes by the way the canopy opens around them and the sky becomes real again. I would want to climb to the platform at dusk and watch the plateau breathe—the way the canopy ripples in the wind like water, the way the sound rises and fills the chest and becomes something you can feel in the back of the throat.

I would want to leave an offering. I would want to place something at the shrine—fresh fruit, a bundle of vines, something that says: *I was here, and I knew that the plateau would cover what I left, and I left it anyway.*

This is the human thing, I think. The thing that the plateau does not share with us but allows us. The thing that we do not share with each other but allow each other. The small, persistent, finally futile gesture of presence in a world that does not require it, that will cover it, that will hold it in the mat and the fungi and the slow pulse of the things that were alive and are still moving, still part of everything, still there.

The plateau is patient. It has been patient for a very long time, and it is very good at it.

And so are we, I think. We who leave the offerings. We who build the shelters that grow into the canopy. We who climb the granite domes and look out over the whole of it and feel what we feel—which is awe, which is unease, which is the particular vertigo of standing on something alive and knowing that we are small and it is large and it will outlast us by so much that the difference does not matter.

The difference does not matter.

But we feel it. And feeling it is the thing. And the plateau allows the feeling, allows us to stand on its skin and call it strange, allows us to build our small shrines and make our small offerings and leave our marks that will be covered, that are meant to be covered, that are offered to the covering as much as to whatever sees.

*This is what I leave. This is what grows over. This is what is held in the mat and the memory and the slow pulse of the things that are still alive.*

The plateau would allow it.

It allows everything, in the end.
