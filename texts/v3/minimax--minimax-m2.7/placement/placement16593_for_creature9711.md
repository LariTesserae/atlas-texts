You have been here before. Not this sinkhole, not this specific hole in the limestone, but this kind of place — a gap in the ordinary, a room that the world forgot to roof. You know the way the air changes when you descend past the point of easy return. The way sound loses its edges. The way light becomes something you can almost touch.

You came down carefully, feet finding the erosion-terraces, one hand trailing along the wall where the stone transitions from dry to damp. The ferns brushed your wrist. You did not stop for them, but you noticed, the way you notice things when you have decided to notice everything: the small resistance of waxy leaf against skin, the faint gleam of moisture on stone.

Now you are at the bottom. The loam gives slightly under your weight. You do not sit — not yet — because sitting would be a decision, a commitment to stillness that you are not sure you have earned. Instead you stand, and you breathe, and you let the sinkhole fill you with what it has to offer.

The mineral smell first. Then the green. Then the sulfur, deep and patient, the buried heat of a planet that is still cooling in ways too slow to feel. You breathe out and something in the air changes — a slight increase in warmth near your face, a momentary brightening of the fungi as if your exhalation registers. Perhaps it does. Perhaps the fungi are attuned to the carbon dioxide of small things, the respiration of insects and larvae and the soft rot of dying ferns, and your breath is just another input, another signal that the atmosphere is being refreshed, that life continues.

The pool draws your eyes. You have read about cave ecosystems, about the creatures that persist in total darkness, adapted so completely to the absence of light that their bodies lose pigmentation, their eyes disappear or shrink to vestigial pits. This is not that. This is a place of partial darkness, of dim glow, of light enough to see by if you are patient. The pool is dark not from absence but from depth — the water is shallow, the tannins make it opaque, and the light does not penetrate. You could reach into it, cool your fingers in it, disturb its stillness. But you do not. The stillness is what interests you.

You lower yourself to the ground. The loam accepts your weight without protest. You lean back against a fern-thick ledge, feel the fronds brush your shoulders, and you let the sinkhole happen around you.

The sun column has moved. When you descended it was on the near wall, a bright slanted paint. Now it has swung toward the far side, illuminating a section of limestone you had not seen lit — the same stone, but transformed by angle and intensity, no longer pale but almost white, the veins of darker rock running through it like calligraphy you cannot read. The ferns that grow here cast longer shadows now, reaching toward the light and away from it, following the geometry of the sun's position with the same unconscious precision with which they have followed every sun that has ever struck this wall.

You watch the fungi. They pulse. You had read about this, in the slow way you read about things when you are not sure you believe them — accounts of bioluminescent caves, of underground rivers that glow faintly green, of mushrooms that light the trails of the people who walk through forests at night. But reading is not seeing. Reading did not prepare you for the slow breath of the light, the way each cluster brightens and dims on its own schedule, independent of its neighbors, a chorus of tiny lamps burning without coordination.

You wonder how long they have been burning. The description in your mind says: since the sinkhole was young. But what does that mean, here, in a place where the erosion is geological, where the ferns grow at a speed that makes single observations useless? Young could be a thousand years. Young could be ten thousand. The limestone around you has been dissolving and redepositing itself since before whatever forest grew above was forest, and the fungi have been growing since there was enough moisture, enough consistency, enough darkness that was not total to support them. They do not know how long. They do not count.

Neither do you, for a while. The hours pass in a way that does not feel like passing — the light simply changes, the sun column swinging back toward you, the fungi dimming slightly in response, the moths beginning to stir in their crevices. You have not moved. Your breathing has slowed to match the convection current, the slow rise of humid air from the pool, the faint exchange that keeps the sinkhole alive. You are aware of your own pulse, your own warmth, the small fogging of your breath on the cool air, but you have stopped being concerned with them. They are part of the pattern now, as much as the springtails in the soil, as much as the millipede in its cold seam, as much as the ferns that are slowly, imperceptibly adding another layer of growth to their ancient accumulations.

The moths rise.

You hear them before you see them — the faint displacement of air, the wingbeat-sound that is not quite a hum, not quite a vibration, but something in between that registers more as texture than as noise. They emerge from the crevices where they have waited through the bright hours, and for a moment they are invisible against the stone, their pale grey wings indistinguishable from the lichen, their olive patterns lost in the shadowed seams. Then the fungi catch them. Green light on green-striped wings. Blue light on blue-cool bodies. They spiral upward, the older ones low and tight, the younger ones reaching toward the mouth and the faint grey glow of the moon that has not yet risen but is gathering its light somewhere above the rim.

You do not count them. Thirty, the text said, but texts are wrong about things like this, and even if the number is accurate it does not capture what you are seeing: not a population, but a movement. A quality of the air. The moths are not separate from the light; they are part of it, their wings reflecting and refracting and returning, their bodies casting tiny shadows on the stone, their flight paths tracing spirals that tighten and release without pattern because pattern would be too simple, would not account for the wind from the pool, the convection current, the small hesitations of insect thought.

You close your eyes. The darkness behind your lids is not the same as the darkness of the sinkhole; your own body heat produces a faint glow that you did not know about until now, until you closed your eyes and noticed the difference. But the sinkhole's darkness is cooler, dimmer, thick with the light of things that do not need to be seen to continue. You open your eyes again. The moths are still spiraling. The fungi are still pulsing. The pool is still still.

A dove descends. You hear its wings before you see it — a rush of sound that the moths ignore, or perhaps do not notice, or perhaps notice and dismiss as irrelevant. The bird lands on a high ledge, picks its way down the terraced stone with the careful steps of something that is used to uncertain footing, and drinks from the pool's edge without pausing, without looking around. It drinks and it leaves. The moths do not react. The sinkhole does not react. The water settles back to stillness, the disturbed surface relaxing into the same perfect calm that was there before.

You think: I am not part of this. I am watching it. I am separate, warm-blooded and brief, here for hours and not centuries, here because I can leave and not because I cannot.

But the thought does not last. It cannot. The air is too close, too thick with the exchange of small things. The light is too present — not bright enough to hurt, not dim enough to ignore, just the right amount of glow to make you aware of your own glow, your own warmth, your own small contribution to the chemistry of the place. You breathe out and the fungi pulse brighter and you feel, for a moment, like nothing so much as another input in the spiral, another small thing that breathes and exhales and feeds the soil that feeds the ferns that feed the moths that die and feed the soil.

You lean back against the ferns. Their fronds rustle slightly at the pressure, then settle. The loam accepts your weight. The moths spiral and dim and spiral. The pool goes on being still.

Above you, somewhere at the rim of the sinkhole, the moon is rising. Its light does not yet reach you — the walls are too deep, too curved, too angled away from the sky. But you know it is there. You know the moment will come when the grey glow slides down the throat of the hole and finds the water and the fungi and you, sitting at the bottom of a room that holds light in the dark.

You wait. The sinkhole waits with you. The patience is the same — not yours and not the sinkhole's, but the patience of the place itself, the patience of erosion and growth and light, the patience that makes urgency irrelevant. You have nowhere to be. You have nothing to do. You have only this: the dim green glow, the faint blue glow, the hum of the air thick with small life, the smell of mineral and green things and buried sulfur rising with the convection current, filling your lungs, filling your awareness, filling the space between observer and observed until the gap closes and you are not watching the sinkhole anymore.

You are part of it. You have always been part of it. You will continue to be part of it, for as long as the conditions allow and no longer, growing old in the way that ferns grow old, imperceptibly, adding a single layer of something that will not outlast the erosion but will not be forgotten either, because forgetting is not something that happens in places like this, where light persists in the dark, where life continues without needing to be remembered, where the wound in the earth has long since learned to heal.
