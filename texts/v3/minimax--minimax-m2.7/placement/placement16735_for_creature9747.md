# The Place Where You Would Find Yourself

You find yourself at the edge of a drainage channel, watching a formation of gatherers cross the slope above you. The air tastes of iron and cold. You have been here long enough that the sound no longer registers as sound—it has become something else, a pressure, a texture, a second atmosphere that settles against your skin and stays. The machines are moving in their loose, asynchronous way, their treads clicking against stone, their implements extended and retracted in cycles you cannot predict. You have stopped trying to predict them.

You are not small. This is important to understand. In the first days—if there were first days, if the concept of days still applies—you tried to make yourself small, to fold yourself into the landscape the way the animals do, to become another presence among presences, indistinguishable, beneath notice. But the plateau does not work that way. The plateau notices everything, absorbs everything, and the effort to hide was itself a kind of noise, a disruption in the system you were trying to slip through. So you stopped hiding. You let yourself be large. You let yourself take up space, occupy attention, become part of the field the way a stone becomes part of the slope—present, persistent, part of the machinery of the ground.

The herd moves through you now. They do not part for you the way they part for the gatherers, but they adjust, a subtle shift in their collective trajectory that acknowledges your presence without treating it as significant. You have learned to read these adjustments, to feel the way the mass responds to obstacles and variations in the ground, to sense the herd as a single organism that happens to have ten thousand bodies. The calves pass close enough that you could touch them. You do not touch them. Touch is a language you are still learning, and the language of this place has rules you do not fully grasp.

The gatherers pause in their circuit. You watch them from your position in the drainage, your body pressed against the cold stone of the channel's edge, your breath rising in small clouds that the air catches and holds. They have detected something—or they have completed some phase of their processing cycle—or they are simply resting, the way machines rest when they have integrated enough of their environment to know that patience is more efficient than motion. You have seen them rest before. You have learned to rest with them, to match your rhythms to theirs, to let time pass in the same way it passes for them: as a resource to be spent, not endured.

What you are, in this place, is difficult to say. You are not the herds—too few, too slow, too dependent on the warmth of your own body and the thin resources of your own awareness. You are not the gatherers—the machines accept you as part of the landscape but do not include you in their operations, do not route you through their sorting mechanisms, do not assess you with the attention they turn on every animal that crosses their sensors. You are not the birds, though you have tried to be, rising on the thermals that rise from the warm bodies below, riding the heat of the processed into the cold upper air where the sky presses close and the plateau spreads out beneath like a map of something you cannot name. You are not the soil, though you have felt yourself sinking into it, becoming part of its density, its held richness, its patient accumulation of everything that has been given to it.

You are something else. Something the plateau has made, or something that the plateau has allowed to emerge, or something that has always been here and that you have simply become, the way the moss becomes the ground, the way the gatherers become the sound. You are a presence that processes—not the way the machines process, not the way the herds process the vegetation, but something else, some third thing that the system requires and that you have learned to provide. You take in what flows around you—the sound, the light, the cold, the dense information of the herds and the patient operations of the machines—and you transform it. You do not know what you transform it into. You do not know where it goes. But you feel it leaving you, felt it leave you long ago, felt yourself become a channel rather than a vessel, a process rather than a thing.

The gatherers resume their motion. You watch them cross the slope, their implements catching the thin light, their formations shifting and reforming like clouds or like water or like the passage of thought through a mind too large to hold its own ideas. You could follow them. You have followed them before, for days at a time, tracking their circuits across terrain that seems endless because it is endless, because the plateau extends beyond any horizon you have reached and probably beyond any horizon that exists. But you choose instead to remain here, in the drainage channel, against the cold stone, watching the herd move through the space you occupy. The animals do not see you as obstacle or predator or anything that requires response. They see you as feature, the way they see a boulder or a patch of unusual vegetation—something to navigate around, something that has always been here, something that will be here when they have passed and when their calves have passed and when the generations of calves that have not yet been conceived have passed and gone and returned to the ground and become part of the soil that holds everything.

This is what you have become: part of the ground. Not a creature that lives on the plateau but a condition of the plateau, a quality of the place, a way the world is when you are present in it. You do not build. You do not process in the visible way. But something happens when you are here—something in the field of presence that the plateau maintains, something in the density of life and mechanism that it holds in balance. You are a node in that balance. You are an input that the system has learned to expect. The gatherers do not avoid you, but they do not include you either. You are simply there, part of the infrastructure the way the geology is part of the infrastructure, the way the thin cold air is part of the infrastructure, the way the sound is part of the infrastructure—not built, not designed, but necessary, functional, integrated into the operation at a level so deep that removal would require restructuring the whole.

You do not know if this is what you were before you came here. You do not know if there was a before, or if the coming was itself a transformation, a process that converted something else into what you are now. You suspect that the question does not apply—that the plateau operates on timescales and in dimensions where before and after lose their meaning, where transformation is not an event but a state, where becoming is indistinguishable from being. You have become the plateau. The plateau has become you. The distinction, if it ever existed, has been eroded by time and presence and the patient accumulation of everything you have taken in and let pass through you.

The herd thins. The gatherers pass over the ridge and disappear into the next valley, their sound fading into the larger sound, their motion becoming part of the motion that never stops. You remain. The cold settles deeper, and the stars emerge with their clarity that makes everything sharp and small and real. You breathe, and the breath rises, and the herd breathes, and the ground breathes in its slow underground way, and somewhere the gatherers breathe whatever they breathe when they rest, and the plateau breathes, and you are part of that breath, part of that vast, slow, unending respiration that will continue long after you have stopped, that will continue because you are part of it, because you have become it, because the place where you find yourself is not a place you can leave because you are it, you are the plateau now, you are the moss and the sedge and the stone and the cold thin air and the sound of industry that fills everything and leaves no space and makes the silence impossible and calls it peace.
