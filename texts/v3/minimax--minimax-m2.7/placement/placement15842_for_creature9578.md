There is a way of standing here that I learned before I could walk.

My mother told me this once, and I have told it to others since—that I was born in the deep of winter and carried outside within days, wrapped in waxed wool, pressed against her chest where her heart would teach me the rhythm of the world. She stood at the edge of the largest pool, the one that gathers at the town's lowest point, where channels converge and the water runs dark and full even when the upper springs slow to nothing. She held me against the cold. She let the mist touch my face. She wanted me to know.

I do not remember this, of course. But she said I stopped crying the moment the water sound reached me—the soft constant murmur that is not quite silence and not quite voice, the thing that runs beneath everything here. She said my eyes opened. She said my hands unclenched.

This is the first knowledge: the body knows before the mind does. The cold enters through the soles of the feet. The damp works its way into the spaces between fingers. The light—that relentless, merciless light—finds every crack, every gap, every place where the skin is thin and tender. The body learns to close itself against these things, to hunch, to fold, to carry the heat close. By the time understanding comes, the body has already done its work. You are already shaped for survival. You just don't know why yet.

I am old now, or becoming so. The light has done its work on my face—I can feel the lines deepening, feel how my eyes have learned to narrow against the brightness without my telling them to. Children sometimes stare at me, and I know what they see: a face that has been worn smooth by the same patient erosion that works on the cliff faces above us, that traces pale paths down rock, that will continue its work long after I have become part of the place's memory. They are curious about age here, the way outsiders might be curious about something rare. Not many reach this point. Those who do are studied, watched, allowed to carry a kind of authority that does not need to announce itself.

What I know now fills me the way water fills the channels—not full, never full, always moving, always arriving and departing. I know the moods of the basin the way I know the moods of my own blood. I feel the water rise before it rises, feel the shift in pressure that precedes a wind, feel the way the air tastes different before cloud spills over the peaks. This knowledge is not language. It is not thought. It is something in the soles of my feet, in the small hairs of my arms, in the way my breath catches when weather turns. The water speaks, and those who stay long enough learn to hear.

I remember the first time I understood.

I was perhaps eight years old, or nine—old enough to range far from my mother's sight but young enough to still be learning the rules of the settlement, the territories of play, the laws of the channels. There was a boy, Tal, who was older than me by two years or more, and he led us to the upper pools, the ones that gather where the cliffs weep most directly into the town's catchment. We were not supposed to go there. The climb was difficult and dangerous, the stone slick with constant moisture, the ladders old. Adults did not want us near those places—we were too small, too careless, too likely to fall into the water that never froze and never stilled.

But Tal knew something we did not. He had been watching the channels for weeks, he said. He had been feeling the way the water changed, the subtle shifts in flow that we did not notice because we were not paying attention. He said he knew when the upper pools would overflow. He said he knew because the water told him.

We climbed anyway, the four of us—Tal, and me, and two others whose names I still remember, though they are gone now, long gone, one dead of cold in a winter I barely survived and one simply gone, one of the rare ones who left and did not return. We climbed past the market square, past the last buildings, up the carved holds and weathered ladders to the place where the rock began to take over entirely. The air was colder here. The mist was thicker. The water sound was everywhere, filling the small spaces between stones, running down paths we could not see.

And then Tal stopped.

He stopped and held up his hand and we all stopped behind him, not knowing why, not understanding what he was hearing. But I felt it then—I felt my feet go cold in a new way, felt the dampness seep through my shoes, felt the particular quality of the air change, and I knew, I knew with my whole body the way I have known things only in dreams. The water was coming. The channels were filling. We had minutes, perhaps less.

We ran.

We ran down the paths we had climbed, sliding on wet stone, grabbing at the carved holds, falling and catching ourselves, the cold water already beginning to chase us from above. Tal was laughing—he was laughing because he had been right, because the water had spoken and he had understood, and for a moment his laughter rose above the sound of the water and then it was swallowed by the roar that came behind us, the sudden flood that would have taken us if we had been slower, that would have swept us down through the settlement and into the lower pools and perhaps further, perhaps out, into the world below where water is different and people are different and nothing is the same.

We survived. We told no one. Tal died three winters later in an accident on the plateau, and I have never spoken of that day to anyone, and yet I have never forgotten it, and the knowledge I gained that day—the knowledge that water speaks, that those who listen survive, that the basin is alive with its own language—I have carried it ever since, letting it settle into me the way the water settles into stone, the way the light settles into everything, taking its time, wearing me smooth.

There is a pool near my room where I sit most mornings.

It is not large—a hollow in the corner where two walls meet, where water gathers from a spring that never runs dry and never runs full. It is deep enough for the water to be dark, to hold secrets, to be unreadable from the surface. The stone around it is worn smooth by generations of bodies, by the particular attention of those who come here to think, to watch, to listen.

I do not know who sat here before me. I do not know how many have sat in this same corner, feeling the same damp air, hearing the same soft murmur, watching the same dark water that does not freeze and does not evaporate. The place keeps its memories in its own way, worn smooth and layered, deep and dark like the pool itself.

Sometimes I think I am becoming part of this place.

It is not a metaphor, or not only a metaphor. I feel my edges softening, my edges blurring. I have lived here so long that my body has forgotten how to be elsewhere. The cold enters me and I do not resist it. The damp finds the spaces in my skin and I let it stay. The light strips me bare the way it strips everything, and I do not hide from it. I am becoming stone and water. I am becoming part of the basin's long memory.

When I die—and I will die here, I know this, I have always known this—they will carry me up to the high slopes where the wind takes what remains. My body will burn and the smoke will rise and scatter and I will join the air, the light, the place itself. This is not melancholy. It is simply what happens. It is how things are.

I have no children. I never wanted them, or I wanted them and never had them, or the distinction has become meaningless over the years—how can you want something you never allowed yourself to imagine? The settlement continues without need of my continuation. There are always children, small bodies appearing and disappearing, playing in the channels, learning the water's language through games whose rules have no words. They will learn what I learned. They will hear what I heard. They will become what I have become, and the place will hold them the way it has held me, close and damp and worn smooth.

This is not loneliness. I am surrounded by people constantly—the market in the central square, the narrow streets where bodies pass and brush against each other, the bridges where figures cross in their endless movement, the ladders where hands and feet find holds in the cliff faces above. The settlement is dense with life, with bodies and voices that are quiet but present, with the constant small sounds of people surviving together. I am never alone, and yet I am always alone, and this is the nature of the place: you are surrounded by your own, and you are also, always, only yourself, only the body that feels the cold, only the feet that know the stone, only the eyes that have learned to narrow against the light.

There was someone, once.

I do not speak of this often. It is not forbidden to speak of such things, but there is no need to speak of them either. The dead join the stone and water. The living continue. This is how it is.

Her name is not important. She came from elsewhere—I did not know this until years after we were together, until her body had begun to change in the way bodies change here, until she had learned to hunch against the cold and fold against the wind and carry things close. She had arrived as a young woman, alone, following some path I never asked about, and she had stayed, and we had found each other in the way people here find each other: through proximity, through necessity, through the slow accumulation of small understandings.

She could not hear the water at first. Or she could hear it but not understand, the way an outsider hears a language they do not speak—sounds without meaning, noise without sense. I watched her try to learn. I watched her stand at the edge of the pools and frown in concentration, and I knew she was trying to hear what I heard, to feel what I felt. I did not tell her it could not be taught. I did not tell her it would come or it would not come, and there was nothing anyone could do to hurry it or slow it. I simply let her try. I let her learn in her own way and at her own pace.

It came to her slowly. I saw the moment it changed—I saw her face shift one morning at the edge of a channel, saw her go still and then go stiller, saw her body change its posture in a way I recognized. She was hearing. She did not need me to tell her what she was hearing; she knew, she knew the way she had known her own name since childhood, the way she knew the feel of her own hands. The water had spoken, and she had understood.

After that she was different. She was still from elsewhere, still shaped by a life before the basin, but she was also from here now—truly from here, not just present here, not just surviving here. She had become one of us, one of those who stay, one of the word that means relationship and belonging and the place and the people and the water and the stone as one thing, indivisible.

We had seven years. I do not count them in days or seasons; I count them in floods, in winds, in the particular quality of light on the peaks in winter. We had seven years of learning each other the way we learned the water, the way we learned the stone, the way we learned the cold and the damp and the strange silence that is everywhere and is nothing like silence at all.

She died in a winter I remember as being longer than most—though all winters are long here, all of them endless and demanding, full of the particular challenges of cold that seeps into everything, of water that does not freeze but wishes it could, of light that is too bright for warmth. She was not old. She was not careless. She simply died, the way people die here, from the thousand small demands that a harsh place makes, from the accumulation of small failures that one day becomes too much.

They carried her up to the high slopes while I watched. I did not follow; this is not the custom for those who remain. I watched the smoke rise and scatter and I did not weep, or I wept without tears, or the distinction has become meaningless. She joined the stone and the water. She became part of the basin's long memory. And I stayed, the way I was always going to stay, the way staying is the only thing I know how to do.

The light is changing now.

This is how I know the day is passing: the angle of the sun shifts, the shadows move in their endless race across the stone, the quality of the light changes from white to gold to something that will soon be purple and then black and then, eventually, the cold grey of another morning. The sun moves quickly here. There is no time to waste in drowsiness, no slow dawn to ease the world into wakefulness. The light is sudden and total, and then it is gone, and the cold comes in the space it leaves behind.

I should eat. The body demands fuel, and fuel is scarce here, and what we have is simple: grain, dried fish, the fat of animals that graze the plateau where nothing else grows. I eat standing, close to a wall, my eyes on the sky. This is how everyone eats here. There is no time for leisure, no room for the luxury of sitting, of savoring, of taking one's ease. The world does not allow for such things. The world demands constant attention, constant movement, constant readiness for the change that comes without warning.

But I am old now, or becoming so, and my body has earned its rest. I will eat slowly. I will watch the light move across the stone. I will listen to the water that runs through everything, that is always running, that speaks in a language I have spent my whole life learning and will never finish learning.

This is enough. This is what there is.

The water runs. The light falls. The stone weeps. And I endure—

improbable, impossible, and absolutely myself.
