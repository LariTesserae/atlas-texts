# What I Am

I am the one who walks the pipes.

Not all of them—I am not the pump-tender, though I know their work and they know mine. I am not the one who reads the pressure or adjusts the flow. I am the one who keeps the channels clear, who scrapes the algae from the iron where it would thicken into a skin too heavy for the water to breathe through. My grandmother did this work. My mother. And now me, though I did not know, when I was young and thought I would do other things, that this is where the water would settle me.

The work is slow. Everything here is slow. My blade is a wide, flat thing, iron itself, worn thin at the edge from years of scraping against years of green. I carry it in a loop on my belt, along with a smaller knife for the roots that push into the joints, the fine rootwork that finds the cracks before anyone else knows there are cracks. The roots are the hardest part. The algae is simple—it grows, I clear it, it grows again, I clear it again. But the roots are patient in a different way. They are thinking. They are learning the shape of the iron, pressing into every seam, finding the places where the metal is weakest. A root that has grown for twenty years is not a weed to be pulled. It is an argument, and I am the counterargument, and we have been having this conversation so long that my grandmother and I were both part of it, and now my daughter is beginning to learn its language too.

The pipes run everywhere. They run along walls and under bridges and up through the floors of buildings where the roots have grown so thick that the wood is gone and the root is the wall. They run through the market, where the stall-frames are ironbark and the roofs are philodendron, and the pipes carry the water that drips from joints and keeps the moss alive on the wood. I scrape the pipes there too, early in the morning before the market wakes, when the light is still grey and the vendors are lighting their fires. The green comes off in long strips, and I gather it in my basket, and in the afternoon the dyers will come and buy it and turn it into the color that has no name—the green-grey-blue-rust that is the color of the Holding itself.

I know the sound the pipes make when the water is high. I know the sound they make when the pressure is dropping, when the pumps are laboring, when somewhere upstream a root has found a crack and is beginning to drink. I know the sound of my own footsteps on the root-bridges, the particular sway of each one, the way the lianas creak when the weather is dry and sigh when the rain is coming. I know the way the light comes through the vine-mesh in the evenings, level and amber, turning everything the color of old iron. I know the smell of the algae, which is not unpleasant but is very particular—it is the smell of the water's outer skin, the boundary where the wet meets the air, and it clings to my hands and my clothes and my hair, and I carry it home with me, and my family carries it too, and our house smells the way everything here smells: wet earth and rust and green growth, all mixed together, inseparable.

There is a pipe that runs behind the old pump house, the one that Master Aldo's family has tended for five generations. The pipe is older than any of them remember—it was here when the logs were still being kept, if the logs were ever kept at all, which I doubt. The iron there is pitted and rough, so thick with mineral deposits that the scraping barely makes a difference. I go there once a month, perhaps more often when the rains are heavy, and I press my blade against the surface and feel the vibration of the water moving through. It tells me things. Not in words—nothing here speaks in words—but in the way it trembles, the way it resists, the way it hums a note that is lower than the other pipes because the iron is older, the channel is wider, the water has been moving through it so long that the metal and the flow have worn each other smooth.

I think the pumps are alive. Not in the way the roots are alive, not in the way the people are alive, but alive nonetheless. The iron is too old to remember what it was before, but it remembers the water. It has been shaped by the water, and the water has been shaped by the iron, and together they have made something that is neither and both. When a pump fails—and they do fail, sometimes, when the roots have grown too thick or the pressure has built too high—we do not mourn it the way we would mourn a person, but we honor it. We let the roots grow over the skeleton. We let the green take the shape the iron once held. And in a generation, there is no more pump, only the root, only the channel, only the water moving where it has always wanted to move.

I am old now, or old enough. My hands are thick with the work, the knuckles swollen, the nails cracked from the constant damp. But I can still walk the pipes, still feel the vibration, still know which joints need me and which will wait another week. My daughter walks with me sometimes, learning the routes, learning the sounds. She asks me why the green grows where it grows, why the roots find the cracks they find, why the water follows the iron the way it does. I tell her what my mother told me: that the water wants to move, and the iron wants to hold it, and the roots want to be part of the holding. My job is not to stop them. My job is to help them agree.

She is learning. In a few years, she will be the one walking the pipes, and I will sit in the room where the walls are more root than wood, and listen to the sound of her blade scraping the green, and know that the Holding is still turning, still breathing, still finding the balance between the made and the wild. And when she is old, her daughter will walk the pipes, and her daughter after her, and the water will still be moving, and the roots will still be learning, and the iron will still be holding, and I will be part of it all somehow—not gone, not forgotten, just part of the shape the Holding takes, the way the root becomes the pipe, the way the pipe becomes the root, the way nothing here is ever lost, only changed.

I scrape the algae. I listen to the water. I walk where the pipes lead, and the pipes lead where the water wants to go, and the water wants to go everywhere, and that is enough. That has always been enough. The Holding keeps its own, and I am kept, and when my hands finally fail, the work will go on, and the green will grow, and the sound will continue—the low, unceasing throb of the pumps, the hiss of water through channels, the slow patient rhythm of a place that does not fight the current but learns, in time, to flow with it.
