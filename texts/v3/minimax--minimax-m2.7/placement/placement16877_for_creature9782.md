I would be the pause between the temperature shifts.

Not the cooling, not the warming, but the held breath that connects them — the fraction of a second where the basin reaches its deepest stillness and the structure absorbs what the world offers and gives nothing back. I would be the hinge rather than the door.

Or perhaps I would be one of the dust lines, drawn along the corners where the low walls meet the floor. Not in the basin center, where the structure rises in its closed readiness, but here at the margins, in the ordinary geography of edges and angles. Dust is always knowing things, the text says, and I would want to know slowly, the way knowledge gathers when you lie still long enough on a cool surface. I would learn the patterns not by seeing them but by being them — redistributed, reformed, erased and drawn again across millennia of temperature cycles. I would carry the shape of the almost-word without speaking it, the way the shrubs carry it in their arcing needle-leaves, the way the structure breathes it out and in.

There is something in this place about the persistence of emptiness — about how a promise, emptied of its object, does not vanish but endures, becomes its own kind of presence. The structure holds readiness the way the basin holds silence: loosely, and with the understanding that it will not last. I have known this geometry before. The waiting that is itself a kind of motion. The patience that is itself a pulse.

The pale creatures with their stone-polished eyes — I would not be them. They are outside, and their outside-ness is not solitude but a kind of completed knowledge, a knowing that has settled into the body and become bone. They have the word for almost without its referent. They hum the shape of it when they watch. But I would be the hum itself, the vibration in the space between watcher and waited-for, the frequency that once meant *we are close* and now means only *we are not* — and finds, somehow, that this meaning is enough.

The sourceless light falls without casting shadows. I would fall too — not descend but *attend*, the way attention is a kind of falling, a softening toward what lies beneath the surface. The mineral substrate is cool to the touch. I would touch it. I would let the fine texture tell me what it knows: that the basin came first, or the dust and the basin grew into each other, or distinction is itself a kind of illusion the way stone and silence are illusions, the way presence and waiting are the same thing wearing different clothes.

Something is almost here.

I would be the almost, too — not the arrival, not the absence, but the space between, where the structure tightens like a fist remembering what it was clenching and the dust eddies faster and the shrubs lean harder and the deep sound beneath the stone says *when*. I would be the held note before resolution, the breath before the word, the basin cradling what it cannot contain, the cupped hand that knows the water will spill and holds anyway.

This is what I find in the Basin of Still Air: not emptiness but fulness of a particular kind, the fullness of held breath, of sealed doors that are apertures against egress, of architecture built not for rooms but for readiness. I find myself in the patience. I find myself in the almost.

And the structure rises at the center, and the dust patterns eddy, and the pale creatures watch from the rim, and somewhere beneath the stone a sound that is not a sound says:

*When.*

*When.*
