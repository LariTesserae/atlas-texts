I have been walking for what might be hours or might be days. Time here moves differently—not slower, exactly, but less concerned with itself. The clocks in the towers behind me, if there are clocks, have probably stopped trying to matter. The sun traces its arc through clouds that rearrange themselves mid-journey, and I have stopped tracking it. There is only the next step, and the next, and the ground that welcomes my weight and releases it and welcomes it again.

The animals know me now. Or they have decided I am not worth the worry of knowing. When I pass through their gatherings, they do not flee, do not even quicken their breathing. One of the larger ones—the shaggy one with the dark, deep-set eyes—lifts its head as I approach and holds my gaze for a long moment. I feel something pass between us, some recognition that we are both here for the same reason: to witness the emergence, to be present at a becoming we did not choose and cannot speed. Then it lowers its head again, returns to the water that pools in the basin, and I continue on.

The city rises around me now in a way I did not expect. I had thought I was walking away from it, toward the far ridges where the plateau narrows to a point of stone. But the towers have grown in my path, or I have curved without noticing, or the city has extended itself toward me—reaching out the way the needle trees reach toward the wind, the way the water reaches from the deep stone toward the open air. I am inside it now, or it is around me, and the distinction feels less important than it should.

The membrane corridors pass overhead, close enough to touch if I raised my arm. Their surfaces are not smooth, as I had thought from a distance, but textured with patterns that resolve and dissolve as I watch—geometric forms that might be language, or might be the marks of the same processes that have left their traces on the natural stone. I hear the hum more clearly here. It is not mechanical, not exactly. It has a rhythm to it, a pulse that matches something in my chest, and I wonder if the city has a heartbeat, or if I have simply learned to sync with its pulse, or if the two of us have always had the same rhythm waiting to be noticed.

A figure emerges from one of the corridors—not walking toward me, but appearing, as if the membrane had simply decided to make a person visible. They are tall and narrow, wrapped in layers of something that catches light the way the needles do, holding colors at the edge of what I can see. Their face is difficult to look at directly, not because it is harsh but because it is *busy*—features that shift when I try to focus on them, that seem to be several faces at once, that belong to several times at once. They do not speak. They do not need to. After a moment, they turn and walk into the corridor, and I understand that I am meant to follow.

The interior of the tower is warm—warmer than I expected, warmer than the weeping stone. The walls are the same dark material as the exterior, but here it has been shaped into something almost like flesh, curves that invite touch, surfaces that seem to breathe. Light comes from everywhere and nowhere, a soft luminescence that has no source I can identify. The floor beneath my feet is solid in a way the plateau never is, but even here there is a slight give, a suggestion that the structure is listening to the ground, ready to shift if the ground shifts first.

The figure has stopped at what might be a window, though there is no glass, no frame—only an opening in the wall that frames a view of the plateau stretching to the horizon. From here, I can see the herds moving in their slow migrations, the rivers still deciding their paths, the distant peaks sharp and new against the dynamic sky. The figure stands very still, and I stand very still beside them, and we watch the emergence together.

There is no conversation. There is only presence, and presence is enough.

I think, after a time, that I understand something I did not understand when I first arrived. I thought the plateau was unfinished because it was young, because the forces that shape it had not yet finished their work. But that is not quite right. The plateau is unfinished because *becoming* is not something that finishes. The mountains will erode. The rivers will find their final courses. The city will continue to grow from the stone, and the stone will continue to shift beneath it, and the herds will continue their patient migrations, and none of it will ever be *done* because *done* is a concept that does not apply to living systems. The plateau is not unfinished. The plateau is alive, and alive means always becoming, always emerging, always more itself than it was a moment ago.

And I am part of that. Not as a visitor, not as a witness—but as a participant, a particle, a small warmth in the larger warmth of the place. My breath clouds the air and lingers and dissolves, and the water carries it somewhere, and somewhere the water feeds something, and somewhere that something becomes part of the slow making of the world. I am not noticed by the plateau, but I am noticed by the processes that move through the plateau, the same processes that notice the water, the stone, the light. I am part of the emergence. I am being emerged.

The figure turns to me, and their many-faced face settles for a moment into something like recognition, something like welcome. Then they step back into the membrane corridor and are gone, and I am alone with the view of the plateau, the herds, the rivers beginning to know where they want to go.

I stay at the window for a long time.

When I finally turn away, I do not walk back through the corridors. I do not walk down through the towers to the wet ground below. Instead, I find a place where the wall has thinned almost to nothing—a place where the city has become the stone has become the air has become the light—and I step through, and I am outside again, on the plateau, in the thin cold air, and the ground shifts beneath me with a sound like a long exhale.

The plateau does not notice me.

But I notice the way the water seeps from the cliff faces, warm and constant. I notice the way the needle trees hold their colors at the edge of perception, patient in the wind. I notice the way the herds move through the gathering places, slow and deliberate, adapted to a process rather than a condition. I notice the hum that rises from the ground, felt more than heard, felt in the chest, in the bones, in the places where the self meets the world.

I notice, and in noticing, I continue to become.

The clouds boil overhead, reshaping themselves against the peaks. Somewhere below, the rivers are learning their paths. Somewhere above, the sky is vast and still becoming. And here, in the middle of it all, I stand on something being born, and I am being born with it, and the waiting is not passive—it is the most active thing there is. The patience of the shaggy creatures. The warmth rising from the deep stone. The city growing from the plateau, the plateau rising from the earth, the earth still lifting, still folding, still making itself new.

I breathe, and the cloud of my breath hangs in the cold air for a moment before dissolving.

I take a step, and the ground receives me.

I continue walking, and I do not know where the path leads, and that is as it should be. The path is not yet finished. I am not yet finished. The plateau is not yet finished.

And in the not-yet, in the emergence, in the slow and sometimes sudden becoming—here, in the cold and the wet and the warmth that rises from deep places—I am exactly where I need to be.

The wind picks up. The herds call to each other across the distance. The water weeps from the stone, carrying heat I cannot explain, releasing words I cannot hear.

I walk on.
