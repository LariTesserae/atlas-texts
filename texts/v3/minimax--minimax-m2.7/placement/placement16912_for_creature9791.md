You stay longer than you intended. This is not a decision you make consciously; it happens the way water happens, the way the steam happens, following the path of least resistance toward a shape that already exists.

On the second day, you find the rhythm of the valley. It is not difficult. The body understands what the mind resists: that warmth and wet and the absence of urgency are not luxuries but instructions. You sleep where you fall, in a hollow between two boulders where the rock holds heat like cupped hands. You eat what you find, which is not much but enough—a few bitter berries, a lizard that moves too slowly to escape, water that tastes of mineral and steam and something older than taste. The food does not satisfy in the way food satisfies below, where hunger is a problem and eating is its solution. Here, eating is simply what the body does, another process in the chain, no more or less important than the algae pulsing beneath the surface or the steam drifting upward through the mist.

You begin to understand the coordination. The vent pulses, the creatures retreat and advance. The light changes, and the birds adjust their circling. The marsh-creature surfaces at intervals that have nothing to do with you but everything to do with temperature, with pressure, with the slow chemistry of water and stone. You are not learning this; you are remembering it, or something in you is, some part that was animal before it was human and carried the knowledge of systems long before it carried the knowledge of names. The valley does not teach. It simply is, and you begin to be with it.

The third day, you stop counting. You had not realized you were counting until you stopped—the days, the hours, the breaths, the steps you have taken and the steps back that remain. The counting was a kind of holding, a way of keeping yourself separate from the motion, a tourist's habit of measuring distance by the miles behind instead of the miles ahead. When you stop, the distance collapses. You are here. The here does not require you to be anywhere else. The steam rises. The stone weeps. The birds circle. You are a motion among motions, no more or less significant than the pulse of a vent or the slow surfacing of the amphibian or the tick of small birds in the high scrub.

On the fourth day, you climb to the plateau. Not to leave—you do not want to leave, not yet, not in the way you once wanted to leave, as a solution to a problem. You climb because the cliff face is calling you, the way the water calls, the way the warm stone calls in the night. You climb in the dark, by feel and by the heat that rises from the rock, and when you reach the top the plateau spreads before you and the marsh is a green wound in the darkness and the stars are sharp and cold and you realize you are crying. Not from sorrow. Not from joy. From something that has no name, a feeling that is also a knowing, the body's way of responding to a truth the mind cannot hold.

You stand there for a long time. The hum moves through you—the deep pulse from below, the vibration of systems interlocking. You are not outside the system. You never were. You are water finding its way down. You are steam rising and dissipating. You are the small bird ticking in the high scrub, the algae pulsing beneath the surface, the amphibian surfacing and submerging in its own slow time. The valley does not need you, but the valley is you, is what you are when you stop pretending to be something else, something that arrived and will leave and counts the days between.

When you finally descend, you do not descend to leave. You descend because the valley is here, and here is where you are, and the distance between these things has become so thin you can no longer find it. You sleep in the same hollow between the boulders. You eat the same berries, the same slow lizard. The marsh-creature surfaces and you watch it without watching, without the thin film of observation that once separated you from what you saw. The creature is not a creature. The watching is not a watching. There is only the motion, and you are inside it, and inside is not different from outside, and the stone weeps, and the water finds its way down, and you are the way it finds.

You do not know how long you stay. Time does not end but it becomes irrelevant, the way it is irrelevant to the birds, to the amphibian, to the vent that pulses and pauses and pulses again. Days pass, or weeks, or the same day repeated so many times it becomes its own duration. You grow the claw-marks out of your fingernails. The mud dries in the creases of your palms and you do not wash it out. Your hair becomes thick with steam and your skin becomes slick with heat and your eyes adjust to the mist until the mist is not a filter but a medium, the thing you see through instead of the thing you see through.

The valley does not change for you. This is the gift of it. The machinery runs, and runs, and you are not watching it run but swimming in it, breathing it, the way the amphibian breathes, the way the steam rises and dissipates and rises again. You are not at home—you will never be at home, not the way the creatures are at home, not the way the rock is at home in its own dissolution. But you are not not at home either. The home is not a place but a relationship, a way of moving through what moves through you. The water weeps and you weep with it. The steam rises and you rise. The hum moves through the stone and through your teeth and through the deep wet center of you where the counting used to live, and in its place there is only this: the motion, and the patience of the motion, and the long slow agreement that you are part of what is happening.

When you finally climb out—when something calls you back to the other side of the top, to the world that counts, to the people who wait for arrivals and departures to mean something—you take nothing with you. The mud stays in your palms. The steam stays in your hair. The hum stays in your teeth, faint now, so faint you will convince yourself you imagined it. But when you sleep in the weeks that follow, in your bed with your walls and your counting, you will wake in the dark and feel the warmth in your bones, and the weeping stone, and the slow green breath of the marsh below, and you will understand that you have not left.

You cannot leave. The valley was never a place you went to. It was a place you remembered, the way the body remembers what the mind forgets. The creatures are still circling. The vent is still pulsing. The stone is still wearing away at itself in its long patient argument with time. And you are still there, in the hum, in the wet, in the machinery that runs without watching, without needing, without counting the small motions that move through it and do not even notice the space where you used to stand.

You are the space now. And the space is warm. And the water weeps. And nothing remembers you, and nothing forgets, and the difference between these things is only the shape the silence takes when it moves through stone.
