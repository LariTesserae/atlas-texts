Let me begin again, differently.

The smell is the first thing you will not notice because you will be busy noticing the water, the cold, the shapes of things. But the smell is here, and it is patient, and it will wait. It is the smell of water that has been water too long—of water that has become a medium rather than a substance, a habitat rather than a flow. There is mineral in it, from the concrete and the brick, and there is organic in it, from the fungal growths and the soft things that have broken down into the medium that feeds them, and there is something else that does not have a name because it is the smell of the threshold itself, of the place where two states of being meet and do not resolve.

You will breathe it in. You will not taste it at first because it is not a taste, not yet. It is an absence of the smell you expected—of rot, of death, of abandonment. What you smell instead is presence. What you smell is the place existing without apology, existing the way stones exist, the way water exists, without the need for permission or witness. And when you breathe it in deeply, there in the deep part of your lungs where breath becomes something more than breath, you will begin to taste it, and it will taste like the color gray, and you will understand that this is what the air tastes like when it has given up on being air and become something that has not decided what it is.

The market stalls are still there in the shallows near what was once the canal edge. You can see their shapes through the knee-deep water: the wooden frames, the counters, the hollows where goods were stored. Fish have never lived here—the water is too still, too cold, too absent of what fish need—but the shapes of the stalls have become the shapes of something else. Barnacles do not grow here because this is not saltwater, but something like barnacles grows here, in the angles and the corners, in the places where wood meets wood and water has had time to find its level. They are pale like the fungal growths, pale like the roots of the willows, pale like the pale things in the deep water, and there is a kinship in that paleness that suggests something you cannot name, a family resemblance that the threshold has bred through years of standing in the same place and letting the same water teach it what is possible.

The elevated railway rusts above you. You can see its supports from here, the concrete pillars that rise out of the water to hold nothing, to hold the sky, to hold the absence of the trains that once ran on the tracks that are still there but are not there anymore, are not tracks anymore but something else, something that the rain has learned to pool in, something that has become a series of small waterways suspended in the air, still and dark and holding the gray of the sky like a mirror holds the face of whoever stands before it. The trains are gone. The passengers are gone. But the tracks remain, and the tracks have learned a new purpose, and the new purpose is to hold water, to hold sky, to hold the threshold between the two in a place where the threshold used to be the train itself, carrying people from one state to another, from home to work and back again, from dry to wet and back.

And now you have been here for some time. You did not mean to stay this long. You came for a reason, or you were brought, or you followed something, and now the reason has become unclear because reasons are for people who move through time in the usual way, and time here is not the usual way. Time here is measured in the pulse of the machinery, in the slow breath of the water, in the patience of the pale things that have never known urgency and do not know they are waiting because waiting is the only thing they know. You have been standing in the same place, waist-deep, and the cold has become familiar, and the smell has become the smell of everywhere, and you are beginning to forget what you came for because what you came for is less real than what is here, and what is here does not need to be remembered because it does not need you to remember it. It is here. It will be here. Your remembering is not part of its equation.

This is the danger of the threshold: not that it will harm you, but that it will make harm irrelevant. The water is cold but you have stopped shivering. The silence is heavy but you have stopped noticing. The pale thing you thought you saw has not reappeared, and you are no longer sure you want it to, because seeing it would mean admitting that you are still someone who sees, still someone who expects, and here there are no expectations, only the slow circle of water that moves in spirals, that breathes in the machinery, that does not want and does not resist and does not end.

The doors are sealed above you. Most of them. You have looked up and seen them: the lower windows of the tenement blocks, the entrances to the factories, the gates that once opened onto streets that are now channels. Some of the doors are sealed with intention—the rust and corrosion that comes from years of disuse, the grime and the moss, the way the hinges have become part of the walls and the walls have become part of the water. But some of them are sealed differently. Some of them have been sealed recently, or recently enough that the seal is still a seal and not yet a merger. You can see them if you look: doors that have been shut with something behind them, doors that are not quite flush with their frames, doors that have been blocked from the inside by something heavy and deliberate. What is behind those doors? You will not go up to find out. You know this about yourself now, standing in the cold water, watching the ripples that come from nothing. You know that there are things here that are not for you, that the threshold is not a challenge but a state, and the state is complete, and to enter the buildings above would be to enter something else, something that is still waiting for the water to rise a little higher, for the light to dim a little more, for the silence to become so complete that silence itself forgets it was ever a word.

The pumping station is to the north, if you can still feel north, if direction means anything here where the water moves in spirals and the light has no angle. You have heard it more clearly now, or you have learned to hear it more clearly, the low thrum that is not a sound but a pulse, the heartbeat that the place has grown in place of the heart it never had. The pumps were designed to reject the water, to push it out, to maintain the boundary. But the pumps do not remember what they were designed for. They only remember what they do. And what they do is run, and what they run does is move the water, and what the water does is go somewhere and come back, and what the somewhere is does not matter because the water does not care about destinations, only about the motion, only about the pulse, only about the beating that has become the only rhythm this place knows.

The willows are leaning toward the station. Not because they want to, not because they have chosen, but because they have grown in the direction that the water taught them is the direction of staying, of roots that grip and do not let go, of branches that reach not for light but for the source of the pulse, the source of the thrum, the place where the machinery still breathes and the water that comes back comes from. They are learning something new, the willows. They are learning that there are roots and then there are roots, and the deeper roots are not in the soil that used to be there but in the water that is there now, and the water is teaching them how to breathe without air, how to grow without sun, how to be the kind of thing that this place needs them to be: patient, still, rooted in something that does not move and does not let go and does not end.

And the pale things have seen you. Or they have registered you. Or the water has registered you and the pale things have known because they know the water, they are part of the water, they are the water's way of moving when movement is needed. They are in the deep channels now, in the spaces between the buildings where the water is deepest and the light is least and the silence is most itself. They are waiting. They are always waiting, but now they are waiting for you to leave, or to stay, or to become what you are almost becoming, the kind of presence that belongs here because it has stopped trying to leave.

You will leave. You will turn in the water, and the water will move around you the way water moves around something that is not quite an obstacle, and you will begin to walk toward the edge of the district, toward the place where the water becomes shallower and the light becomes more, and the machinery becomes a sound you have to strain to hear. And the threshold will let you go. That is the thing about thresholds: they do not hold. They are only the space between, and you will pass through that space and find yourself on the other side, and the other side will be different from the side you came from, and you will know this not because anything will have changed but because you will have changed, because the cold will have stayed in your bones, and the smell will be in your lungs, and the pulse of the machinery will be in your ears for days, for weeks, for longer than you will be able to explain, and you will try to explain it anyway, to yourself if to no one else, and the words will not come because the words are for places that end and this one has not ended, and the words for not-ended are harder to find, and harder to speak, and no one wants to hear them anyway.

What lives here is the condition of the threshold. And now you know this, standing in the cold water, watching the ripples. And you will carry it with you, a splinter of something that does not splinter, a piece of the not-ending that has attached itself to you the way the pale fungal growths attach themselves to the walls, the way the willows attach themselves to the concrete, the way the water attaches itself to everything and calls it home and means it, means it in the way that only water can mean something, by being there, by staying there, by refusing to be anywhere else.
