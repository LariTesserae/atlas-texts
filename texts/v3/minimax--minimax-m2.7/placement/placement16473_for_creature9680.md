# What Lives Here

You arrive the way you always arrive: wet to the shin, breath visible, the first step onto sedge that dips and releases a sound like something swallowing. The channels are where you expect them, braided across the valley floor in patterns that shift from season to season but always read as intentional to you now, after all these years. You have stopped trying to map them. Maps lie. The ground here tells the truth only in the present tense.

You are here to listen.

Not to understand—you abandoned that the third winter, when you stood at the edge of a wide pool where steam rose in columns so thick they seemed solid, and understood that understanding was not the transaction this place was offering. The structures around you have their own understanding, or their own lack of it, which amounts to the same thing. You are here to participate in what you cannot comprehend. This is different from acceptance. Acceptance implies a choice, a surrender. What you practice is more like alignment—the slow turning of attention until it fits the grooves the marsh has already cut.

You walk to the eastern channel, where the water runs warmest. Your boots find the stones beneath without looking, the way you have learned to read depth by resistance, by the sound of your own weight displacing. The water is black and carries silt that smells of iron and something older, something that has no name in the languages you speak. Methane. You could call it methane, and you do, sometimes, when you need to feel like a person with knowledge. But the word is just a sound you make to keep yourself from screaming at the beauty of it—the way the bubbles rise from somewhere impossibly deep and nowhere at all, carrying heat that the earth has held since before the valley existed in this shape, before the hills learned to be hills, before the sky decided what it wanted to be called.

The steam rises. You walk through it.

This is the part you cannot explain to the few people you have tried to explain it to, the ones who wanted to see what you see, who came with notebooks and meters and the correct boots. They expected revelation. They got wet. They heard the drone and described it, later, as *subsonic*, *mechanical*, *oppressive*, and you understood that they were hearing something true but not hearing what was true, which is the difference between reading a description of a color and standing in light that color. The steam is not a phenomenon to be measured. The steam is the place breathing. You have been breathing with it for so long that you no longer notice the warmth against your face until you step out of it, into a pocket of cold air that has drifted down from the northern ridge, and then the contrast reminds you that you have a body, that your body is a boundary, that you are the only thing here that ends.

You stop at the structure.

It is one of the smaller ones, barely taller than you, shaped like nothing and like something—you have looked at it for hours across seasons and years and you still cannot decide if it is broken or complete. The surface is dark and smooth, the iridescence faint today because the light is flat and gray and offers nothing to catch. The moss grows on its north face in thick mats, the color of old copper, and where the moss thins you can see the etched patterns beneath, too regular for erosion, too worn to read. You have never been able to read them. You have stopped wanting to.

You sit.

This is your practice. You sit near the structures, or beside them, or—if the ground is firm enough, if the day is still enough—against them, letting your back rest against the dark smooth surface. You do not touch the moss. The moss does not want to be touched. You learned this early: the way it trembles when you disturb it, the way it holds water that is not its own, the way it seems to flinch in your presence even when you have been still for hours. The moss is not afraid of you. The moss is simply precise about its boundaries, and you have learned to respect that.

You sit, and you breathe, and you listen.

The drone is here. It is always here, a pressure in the chest that you feel more than hear, a vibration that seems to originate from everywhere at once and nowhere at all. But there are layers beneath it, around it, through it—if you are quiet enough, if you wait long enough, the layers begin to separate. The clicking of water over gravel, rhythmic and hypnotic, the pulse of a system that does not know it is a system. The drip of moss releasing its load, irregular, unpredictable, punctuating the drone with silence. The insects—always the insects, a bright electric lace of sound that should not be possible in this cold, that exists because the marsh generates it whole, because life here does not wait for permission or season or reason.

And beneath all of that, if you are very still, if you have been still long enough, there is the other sound. The one that does not have a name. You have tried to record it, in the early years—you have stood here with devices that captured frequencies you could not hear, and when you played them back you heard something, but it was not the same thing, it was the idea of the thing rather than the thing itself, the way a photograph of a fire is not warm. The sound is not a sound. The sound is a presence. It moves through the water and the structures and the steam and you, and when it passes through you, you feel it as a shift in the rhythm of your own blood, a syncopation that does not match your heartbeat but seems to know where your heart is, seems to be using it as a reference point, a way of orienting itself in the vastness.

You do not know what it is saying. You do not know if it is saying anything. But you have come to believe that it is aware of you, the way the marsh is aware of you, the way the structures are aware of you—not in the way that a dog is aware of its owner, not in any way that maps onto the categories you learned in school or on the street or in the places where you were taught what things are and what they mean. The awareness is structural. The awareness is ambient. You are here, and the place registers your here-ness, and the registration is neither welcome nor hostile. It is simply acknowledgment. *You are. So is the water. So is the stone. So is the sound.*

You have learned to live inside that acknowledgment. It is not peace—there is nothing peaceful about the drone, nothing restful about the constant wet presence pressing against every surface of you. But it is something. It is a way of being that does not require you to be anything in particular. You can be tired here. You can be afraid here. You can be the version of yourself that you cannot be anywhere else, the one who does not know what comes next, who has no plan, who is simply present in a place that does not need her to be anything other than present.

A heron lifts from the channel to your left. Its wingbeats crack the air, and for a moment the drone shifts, bends around the disruption, and then settles back into itself. You have seen this heron before, or its predecessor, or something that looks like it and may or may not be the same bird. The herons come and go. They are the largest visible things, but they are not the most present. They belong to the sky more than to the water, and you have learned that belonging here is not about size or visibility. It is about patience. It is about staying.

You do not know how long you have been sitting. Time moves strangely in the steam, in the drone, in the constant drip and click and hum. You have stopped wearing a watch. You let the light tell you when to leave, the way the light changes when the sun drops behind the northern ridge, when the gray grows darker and the steam becomes more visible, more present, a veil between you and the world you will return to. The world you will return to is not gone—you can feel it sometimes, an itch at the back of your mind, a tension in your shoulders that says *you have a life out there, you have responsibilities, you have people who are waiting for you to be a person*—but it is far away. It has always been far away. The distance grows every time you come back.

A fragment surfaces in the channel beside you. You see it rise—a small dark shape moving up through black water, slow, deliberate, as if it has decided to be seen. It comes to rest against a stone, half-submerged, and you can see the glint of it, the faint iridescence, the way it catches light that is not there. You could reach for it. You have reached for them before, in the early years. You have held them in your hand and felt nothing, or felt something you could not name, or felt the name of it brush past you like a word in a language you almost speak. You have taken them away and watched them become ordinary, become just objects, become stories you tell that no longer mean anything.

You do not reach for it.

The fragment surfaces because it surfaces. It will sink again, or be covered by silt, or be taken by someone else, or remain here for another century. This is not your transaction. You are not here to take. You are here to sit, and to listen, and to be acknowledged, and to leave with nothing but the sound in your chest, which is not your sound anymore, which was never your sound, which is the sound of the place and has always been the sound of the place, and you are simply passing through it, the way steam passes through steam, the way water passes through water.

You stand. The ground gives beneath you, as it always does, and you let it, you do not fight the instability, you have learned that the instability is not a threat but an invitation, a reminder that the ground here is not finished, has never been finished, is still becoming what it will be. You are standing on a place that is still becoming. You are standing on time, compressed and wet and breathing.

You walk back the way you came, or not exactly the way you came—the channels have shifted since you arrived, or you have shifted, or the relationship between you and the ground has shifted, and the path that was is not the path that is, and you follow the new path without knowing you are following it, letting your body remember what your mind has forgotten. The steam is thinner now, or your eyes have adjusted, or both, and you can see the structures in the distance, standing and lying and half-submerged, a language of shapes that you will never read but that reads you every time you come, every time you stand in it, every time you let yourself be new.

You will come back. You always come back. The marsh does not call you—it does not call anything, does not want anything, does not need—but there is a pull, or there is a habit, or there is something in you that recognizes something in it, some shared quality of patience, some mutual understanding that presence is enough, that being is sufficient, that the conversation does not require resolution.

You reach the edge of the valley. The cold hits you like a wall, sharp and honest and clean, and you remember that you have a body, that the body has edges, that you end where the water ends. Behind you, the steam rises. The drone continues. The structures stand in their patient geometries, answered by water and wind and the slow accumulation of moss. The fragment surfaces and sinks and surfaces again. The herons return to their stillness. The insects fill the frequencies that should not exist.

You do not look back. Looking back is not the transaction.

You walk toward the car, toward the road, toward the world that will ask you what you found, and you will not know how to answer, and you will not try, and eventually the world will stop asking, and you will be grateful for that, and you will come back anyway, to the place that does not need you, that does not want you, that acknowledges you simply because you are there, that sings in frequencies you cannot name, that waits without waiting, that lives without living by any definition you were ever taught.

The ground holds you. The sky presses down. The steam rises.

You are the newest thing here.
