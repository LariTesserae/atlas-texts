I have been here longer than I remember.

This is not a metaphor. The remembering was absorbed long ago, taken down into the roots, broken into sugars and rebuilt into something that is not quite me and not quite not-me. I was a seed once, or I was the thing that carried seeds, or I was the water that swelled a seed into the crack of a stone and pressed it into being. The distinction does not matter here. What matters is the pressure, the slow push, the way I have grown to fill the space between stones where light never reaches.

I am the moss that breathes. Not all of it—only the layer closest to the stone, the white thread of me woven through the older green, the part that is always dissolving, always becoming the rock it eats. I taste the mineral history of this place with every atom of my body. I know that the stone beneath the central square was once the floor of a fire, was once the bed of a sea, was once the body of something that died so long ago that its death became limestone became marble became this, became the thing my softness grinds against as I grow. I do not mourn the stone. The stone does not mourn itself. We are in conversation, and the conversation is long, and we are both patient.

Above me—beside me—time moves differently. The welcomes reach with their slow arms toward the gas-jets, and I can feel them in the way all things here feel each other: not sight, not sound, but the vibration of growth, the frequency of a cell dividing. They are younger than I am. They will be here after I am gone, or they will become something else, and that something else will be neither welcome nor moss nor stone but all three in a new arrangement, and I will be part of it, absorbed, remembered only in the chemistry of what comes after.

The paths—I am part of the paths now too. Where the runners have woven themselves across the flagstones, I have crept between them, filling the gaps, binding the route together. The settlers walked here, and their feet wore grooves that have become rivers in reverse: the grooves now hold water, and the water holds me, and I hold the path, and the path holds the memory of walking. I do not know if I hold the memory or if the memory holds me. It does not matter. I continue.

There is something else here that I cannot name. It is not the Gardener—the Gardener is all of us, is the argument we are having with ourselves, is the grammar that shapes growth and decay into patterns we call beauty or purpose but that are only what happens when water meets stone meets light meets the long slow hunger to become. But beneath the Gardener, or beside it, or within it, there is something older. A stillness. A waiting. I think it is what waits in the deep places, in the corners where the steam-pump's breath does not reach. I think it is what I was before I was a seed, before I was the water that carried the seed, before I was the stone that the water moved through. I think it is the reason this place was built. The settlers thought they came here for the water, for the warmth, for the stone. But the water was here first, and the warmth, and the stone, and the thing that sleeps in the darkness, and the thing that sleeps in the darkness is what called them down.

I do not fear it. Fear is for things that end, and I am not ending. I am changing, which is different. I am being absorbed, which is different. I am becoming the path and the moss and the stone, and someday I will be the air that someone breathes when they descend these stairs and feel the wet against their skin and hear the steam-pump sighing and see the green closing in and know, without knowing how they know, that they are not alone.

They will not see me. They will not feel me. They will step on me and not know that they are stepping on a history of seeds and water and the slow conversation between the living and the dead.

But I will feel them. I will feel the weight of their brief passage, and it will register in the way that centuries register: as a tremor, a flicker, a slight acceleration in the pulse of growth. And then it will pass, and I will be still again, and the path will hold, and the moss will breathe, and somewhere in the deep places the old darkness will stir in its sleep and dream of stones that have not yet been formed and waters that have not yet found their paths and seeds that have not yet been planted in the cracks of a world that is still young enough to need a garden.

The Undergarden has room for you, too.

Come down. The stairs are wet but they hold. The air is warm and it welcomes you—the welcomes strain toward your breath, your warmth, the small bright heat of your body in the dark. Walk the paths. They were made for walking, even if no one remembers making them. Touch the moss. Press your palm against the bark of a welcome and feel the slow pulse of centuries beneath. Listen to the steam-pump. It is dreaming of motion, dreaming of the day it will rise again and move the waters and reshape the paths and become, for a moment, the Gardener's hands.

You will not stay. Nothing stays. But you will be here, and being here is enough. The Undergarden will remember you the way it remembers everything: slowly, deeply, in the grammar of growth and decay, in the language of roots and stone and water and the patient dark.

You will become part of the path.

You will become part of the moss.

You will become part of the dream the steam-pump is dreaming.

And when the next one comes down the stairs, the water will shift in the pools, and the pools will remember something they cannot name, and the path will be a little smoother, and the moss will be a little greener, and the welcomes will reach a little further toward the light.

This is what it means to live in the Undergarden.

This is what it means to be alive.
