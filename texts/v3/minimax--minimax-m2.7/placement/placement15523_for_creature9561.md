This is the kind of world that gets under your skin—not because it overwhelms with scale or spectacle, but because it insists on its own logic so thoroughly that you start questioning the logic of everywhere else. Kaldenvast doesn't feel invented. It feels *discovered*, like the writer found it waiting and had the patience to learn its grammar.

What strikes me most is the temporal topology. You've built a place where causality hasn't fully hardened, where the ground is still negotiating with itself—but you've applied this principle unevenly, which is where it gets interesting. The foundries are mining the future. The sound is trying to remember what it used to be. The light in some corners has already happened. These aren't just aesthetic strangeness; they're different *kinds* of uncommitted time.

I find myself wondering about the seams between them.

The workers' progression is one of the most haunting things you've written—the way tolerance becomes dependency becomes something that doesn't have a name because the person who would name it is no longer entirely the one who started. But there's a question embedded in that which I think you haven't fully answered: what do the workers think is being remembered? The foundries "remind the rock what it sounds like" implies the rock once made this sound deliberately. That the Massif—or whatever the Massif was, before it was uplift and compression—was *singing*, or resonating, or holding some frequency that the kalite wants to return to. What was it?

And if the deep-dwellers emerge when the sound goes silent, drawn by the same absence that drives the workers back—if they are encountered in the tunnels where the sound is "not sound at all but the memory of sound"—then they're not just wrong fauna. They're something from before the remembering. Something the resonance is trying to call back *from*.

What if the deep-dwellers are what the rock used to be?

The slow dynamic is the other thing that lodges in me. You've made clear that change happens, but at rates that are imperceptible from within. The face you grew up next to has moved twenty meters to the east. The cables stretch by imperceptible degrees. The children know which corners are darker than they should be—corners where light hasn't arrived yet.

I wonder about someone who notices this from outside. A surveyor, maybe. An engineer from another city who comes to consult on the suspension systems. They'd have data, measurements across time. They'd be able to see the movements that no one living in the city can see. What would that be like—having the long view, watching the geology negotiate in real time from a position of enough distance to perceive the negotiation? Would they go mad? Would they become something else? Or would they become the city's chronicler, the one person whose whole function is to hold the memory of where things were versus where they are?

There's something about the children that deserves its own extension. They have "maps of the impossible" passed down in whispers and dares. They've learned which cables sing, which rooms have plants growing inward. They know where the deep-dwellers emerge.

What if they're not just adapted to the strangeness—what if they're *native* to it? Children born into a place where causality is negotiable might have a different relationship to it than adults who arrived with fixed expectations. Their neurology might calibrate differently. They might perceive the seams in reality more clearly because they never learned to see through them.

The climbing-scorpions are a small detail, but they feel load-bearing: creatures that taste and leave numbness behind, that cluster on sun-facing cables in the brief hours of light. They're the city's most visible fauna, the thing children catch and keep in jars. They've adapted to the fractured terrain, to the instability, to the moisture. They've been here long enough to evolve.

What else has evolved? The foundries have been running for four generations. The moss has been growing for a century. The city should not exist, but it has existed long enough for *change* to happen within it, slow as that change is. What new things have emerged? What organisms, what behaviors, what patterns? The workers develop tolerance. The children have their own maps. Are there other adaptations—subtle ones, not yet noticed, happening in the gaps between generations?

The birds are wrong in the wingbeats. The moss is the wrong color. The deep-dwellers don't match any taxonomy. The light in some corners has already happened.

You keep returning to this pattern: things that are not quite what they should be, in a place where "should" is still being negotiated. The wrongness isn't brokenness. It's incompleteness. The world is still under construction, and these are the elements that haven't yet settled into their final form.

I find myself wondering about a person who notices this most—a resident who has been there longest, or a worker who has changed most, or a child who sees the seams most clearly. What would it feel like to be *aware* of the negotiation? Not just adapted to it, not just tolerant of it, but consciously participating in it? To feel the geology shifting beneath the cables and know that it's making itself, moment by moment, decision by decision? To hear the foundry's thrum and understand that you're listening to the rock remember something it used to know?

Kaldenvast should not be here. But here it is, and here it will remain.

The question that stays with me is simpler than all of this: *what does it feel like to belong to a place that hasn't finished becoming itself?* To hang your life from cables threaded through stone that was once miles underground, and to trust the engineering, and not look down. To live in a city that is still negotiating with its own ground. To be a person in a world that is still deciding what kind of world it wants to be.

You don't answer this directly. You don't need to. The answer is in the suspension, in the patience, in the moss growing on timber that should have rotted a century ago and somehow holds. The answer is in the children running through the middle districts, playing games that involve falling safely into nets that catch and release. The answer is in the workers who say the sound is trying to remember what it used to be, and who want to help.

Home is not a place that is finished. Home is a place that holds you long enough for you to become part of it. And in Kaldenvast, that is enough. That has to be enough.

This world has weight. It has history. It has the particular density of places that have been imagined fully before being described. If you were to extend it—write the people who live there, follow the ones who disappear into the deep tunnels, document the expeditions that come back wrong—I would follow you into the dark.
