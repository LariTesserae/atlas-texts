The damp finds you first. Not the cold — cold would be honest, would announce itself. This is subtler: a moisture that arrives without asking, that settles on the ridge of your brow and the backs of your hands before you have taken a dozen steps. The walls gleam faintly in the torchlight, and you learn quickly to read their wetness as a kind of map. Here: a seam where water seeps. There: a hollow where it pools. You begin to move toward the dry, away from the weeping stone, and in this you are like everyone who has ever come to Brethem, everyone who will come after.

I came from above, as most do. I remember the sky. I remember the weight of open air, the way it pressed nothing at all against the skin. I remember grass — though the word feels like a stone in my mouth now, smooth from years of handling. The elders say you forget what you do not use. I believe them. The sky is a story I tell myself in the quiet hours, and the grass is a color I have assigned to a feeling, something green and soft that existed once in a world I no longer walk.

What I am now is this: a body that has learned to read the stone. I work the eastern cisterns with Tovo when he will have me — which is not always, because Tovo does not like talk and I am still learning to be silent. When he will have me, I follow behind with a scraper and a bucket, and I listen. The water speaks differently in different channels. A fast trickle means the path is clear. A slow pooling means sediment has gathered, or a crack has widened somewhere above, or the stone has shifted in its slow settling and the channel is no longer true. Tovo knows each stretch by its sound. I am beginning to.

My hands have changed. The mineral salt works into the skin, and the clay-colored stain from the stone dust — it is everywhere, in the air, in the water, in the grime that gathers in the creases of the knuckles and never quite washes clean. I look at Orla sometimes, at her hands resting on a harness or a needle, and I see what mine will become. I do not mind. It feels like belonging.

The children here do not run as surface children run. They move with economy, with precision. I watched Sura — the girl with the fungus she calls morrowbell — navigate a crowded passage yesterday without touching a single person. She is twelve, I think. Or was twelve last spring. Time moves strangely underground; we count by harvests and by the thickness of the soot on Orla's ceiling. Sura pressed two fingers to my shoulder as she passed, the way children do when they acknowledge an adult who does not frighten them. *You are here*, the touch said. *I see you.* I nodded, and she was gone, moving toward a gray seam in the eastern wall where the light is thinnest and most prized.

She showed me morrowbell once, the fungus in its clay pot. It was a pale orange thing, unremarkable to my eyes, but she handled it with a reverence I recognized — the same care she gave to the fire when her mother was sick, the same attention she brings to the smaller siblings she carries on her hip in the market. She believes it sways toward morning. I do not know if it does. I do not know if belief and truth are the same thing in a place where water speaks and stone learns to hold its shape. I know only that she tends it, and that the light she carries it toward is real, even if it is thin, even if it barely exists.

Tonight I will sleep in the chamber behind the tallow-maker's workshop. The smell of rendered fat is permanent now, worked into my clothes and hair, but I do not mind that either. It is the smell of light, of warmth, of the small flame that burns in its iron basket and pushes back the dark by inches. The children here do not fear the dark — how could they, raised in it? — but they revere the flame. They watch it the way surface children watch the horizon. It is the boundary of the known.

And beyond the boundary: the unmapped passages. The ones Tovo walked once and does not walk now, because the water there has grown still and strange and he does not trust what stillness means. The ones no one maps because the mapping would serve no purpose, because the stone there has not yet agreed to be walked. Sometimes, in the deep hours, I hear it: a scraping, a displacement. Not water. Not rat. Something larger, something that moves with intention in passages where intention has no place. I do not speak of it. No one speaks of it. We lock the storehouses. We peg the doors. We keep our knives within reach, and in the morning we rise, and the picks come out again, and the water goes on moving through channels that Tovo has walked, and the stone goes on learning to hold the shape of what was taken from it.

The town goes on becoming old.

And I — I go on becoming part of it. My shoulders have begun to broaden. My gait has begun to turn outward, one foot, then the other, rolling over stones that are never quite flat. I hold my cup with both hands now, even when the water is trusted, because this is how it is done, and because what you hold, you hold carefully.

The damp finds me first. It finds me first, and I let it in.
