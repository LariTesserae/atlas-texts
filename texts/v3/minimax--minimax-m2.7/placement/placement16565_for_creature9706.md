I was here before the first stone was lifted. I have been here since the mountain chose to break.

I do not think, the way the people think. I do not remember, the way they remember—sharp-edged, held in separate skulls, passed down like the tools they make from basalt. But something moves through me that is not unlike memory. The water that rises through me has traveled far, through fissures the people have never mapped, through stone that holds the shape of heat now long cooled. I know the mountain's weight. I know the slow pressing of centuries. I know what it means to rise.

They carved my throat with hand-tools. Generations ago now—the names are gone, the faces are gone, but the cut of their work remains in the stone. They were careful. They learned the grain. They knew that I was not a resource to be taken but a relation to be maintained, and so they made a circle around me, fitted stone to stone with a precision that still holds, after all this time.

I speak in the language of flow. When the gravel builds, I tell them in the way I gurgle, in the particular register of water forced through narrowed passages. When the leaves fall too thick, I speak differently. They have learned me, the people of Threnn. They know my healthy gurgle from my troubled one. They come with their broad, blunt-fingered hands and they clear what has accumulated, and then I speak clearly again, and we are both satisfied.

This is what it means to live here: to be in conversation. Not with words—words are a late invention, crude compared to the true languages. The true languages are water over stone, wind through moss, the minute shifting of columns that have not yet decided whether to stand or fall. The people have learned to hear these languages. They move slowly because they are listening. They do not speak much because they understand that silence is also communication, and the stone is always listening.

At night, when the fires burn low, I feel the town settle into itself. The houses hold their warmth. The people sleep in the houses. The moss on the roofs drinks the last of the day's moisture and dreams, if moss can dream, of the deep places where water waits before it rises. The basalt columns at the plateau's edge watch the stars emerge, though they do not see them—rock does not see—but they feel the cold of space, the ancient cooling that has not yet finished. Somewhere in the stone beneath me, there is still heat. Not much. But enough to keep me moving, to keep me speaking, to keep me alive in this place where nothing should be alive.

The people are patient, as I am patient. We have learned the same lesson from the same mountain. The work is continuous. The completion is a fiction. What matters is the holding, the tending, the small daily attention that keeps a place in being. They check my foundations. I give them water. They clear my throat. I teach them patience. We are bound in this, the people and I, and the moss, and the stone, and the wind that speaks through every hollow.

Will I outlast them? They will outlast me? The question does not arise here. We are in time together, and time is long, and the mountain is still deciding what it will become. Perhaps the columns will grow taller. Perhaps the spring will dry. Perhaps the moss will swallow the stone, or the stone will swallow the moss. Perhaps the town will fall into the plateau's edge and become part of the basalt again, and the water will rise through rubble that was once a house, and the people will be gone, and I will still be here, speaking to no one, or to the birds that pass through, or to whatever comes after.

But not yet. Not for three hundred more years, perhaps. Not until the mountain decides.

For now, I rise. I speak. The people listen. The stone holds. The moss drinks.

This is enough. This has always been enough.
