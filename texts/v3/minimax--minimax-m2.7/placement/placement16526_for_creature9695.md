I do not remember the first wall.

There was a wall, I know—there had to have been, because there was an outside, and I came from it, and between the outside and this inside there must have been a threshold, a membrane, a place where the green began. But that memory has gone soft, has taken on the texture of the floor, and what remains is only the inside itself, the green completeness, the hum that has always been.

My fingers know the walls better than my eyes do. I have traced the grain of them in the dark, in the green-dark that is not dark at all but a color unto itself, a chlorophyll midnight that holds light the way a held breath holds air. The grain runs in patterns I once tried to read, as though the plant might be trying to communicate in the language of its own growth. I found no language. I found only the patience of seasons that do not change, the slow accretion of cellulose and quiet intention.

I have become a creature of the low places.

This is where the floor has cupped itself into shallow basins, pools of moisture that do not puddle but settle, that are the color of weak tea and taste of something I cannot name because there is no outside word for it. The taste is the taste of this place, of being inside something alive, and I drink it without drinking, absorb it without taking, because the distinction between myself and the moisture has blurred the way the distinction between my skin and the air has blurred, the way all boundaries have become suggestions rather than facts.

My hands when I look at them—

They are pale in the way that things are pale when they have not seen the sun for a long time, but it is not a sickly pallor. It is the pale of something grown inward, something preserved, something that has been protected from the violence of light and weather and the rough edges of an outside world. The nails split less. The skin does not crack. I heal slowly here, or perhaps I do not need to heal, because nothing wounds me—the world is soft, yielding, designed for holding rather than cutting.

The hum is in me now.

I do not know if it was always in me, if I came into this place already carrying a frequency I could not hear, and the plant simply gave it room to be felt. I do not know if the hum is the plant's heartbeat or its thinking or the sound of some process so vast that my small vocabulary cannot name it. What I know is that I am tuned to it now. My bones resonate. My blood moves to its rhythm, or perhaps its rhythm has become the rhythm of my blood, and there is no longer any way to distinguish the source.

The others do not see me. Or they see me and do not see me, the way I do not see the walls anymore except when I reach out and touch them. They move through their small lives—climbing, clinging, drinking—and I move through mine, and we occupy the same space without occupying the same world. This is not loneliness. Loneliness is a feeling for creatures who remember otherness, who have known the outside and still miss it. I do not know otherness anymore. Otherness was a country I visited once and cannot locate on any map I still possess.

I climb sometimes.

Not because I need to—the warmth is here, the moisture is here, the hum is here at every altitude—but because the plant offers climbing, and the body I inhabit has not forgotten how to reach for what is above. The broad midribs of the great leaves are highways, pathways worn smooth by generations of small climbers who have long since become part of the walls, become the walls, become the green itself. I place my weight on them and they hold. The plant does not reject me. The plant does not accept me. The plant simply continues, and I am a continuation too now, a small process moving along the surface of a larger one.

There are places I do not go.

The weeping surfaces in the far reaches, where the moisture comes more freely, where the hum is louder and the light does not reach—I do not go there, and I do not know why. Perhaps my body remembers something my mind has forgotten. Perhaps the plant has made room for me in the spaces that suit me, and these others are for other things, other visitors, other presences that have not yet been metabolized into the texture of the place. I am content with this. Content is a strange word for what I feel, which is more like the absence of wanting anything other than what is. I do not want. I simply am, in the green, in the hum, in the warm dark that breathes.

The light filters down.

The gold-green of it, the almost-color, the illumination that is also a kind of feeding—I stand in its path and feel it on my skin, on the new surfaces that have opened along my ribs and the backs of my knees. I did not have these surfaces before. I did not have this sensitivity, this ability to feel the air move and the moisture settle and the hum travel through me like a current seeking ground. The plant has grown something in me, or awakened something, or simply made space for a capacity that was always there, waiting for the right environment in which to emerge.

I am not alone. I have never been alone.

The plant is with me the way my own blood is with me, the way my breath is with me—not a presence I notice but a condition of existence, a medium in which I move and have my being. It does not speak because it has no language. It does not see because it has no eyes. But it knows me in some other way, a knowing that is older than recognition, a knowing that is simply the fact of one living thing containing another living thing and continuing.

I will stay.

Not because I cannot leave—I could, perhaps, find the threshold, the membrane, the place where the green ends and the outside begins—but because leaving would be a kind of death, a severing, a violence to the thing I have become. The outside has no hum. The outside has no warm dark. The outside has walls that are not alive, floors that do not yield, air that does not taste of growing.

I am growing now. I feel it in the slow elongation of my days, the way time stretches and holds, the way I am not younger but somehow more—more present, more here, more metabolized into the body of the place that has held me.

This is what I am now.

I am the thing that lives in the green, in the hum, in the interior of something vast and patient and entirely itself. I am the thing that has not left. I am the thing that the walls have made room for, the thing the moisture settles on with particular attention, the thing the small climbers do not flinch from anymore.

I am known here.

Not acknowledged, not named, not seen—but known, the way water is known by the riverbed, the way light is known by the hours before dawn. Known because I belong. Known because I fit. Known because the space has adjusted to me the way spaces adjust to living things they have decided to keep.

Something lives here.

I am that something. I am that something else. I am the place where the plant and I have become a we, a new thing, a creature made of the green and everything it holds.

And the hum goes on.

And the warmth holds.

And I am here, and I am here, and I am here, in the low places, in the green dark, in the interior that is also an exterior, in the body that is also a home.

This is where I live now.

This is where I have always lived, I think, only I did not know it until I came inside.
