# You Are the Tower

At first, I thought I was standing in it.

This is the error everyone makes—the error I made, the first time I understood that I had a body and the body was here and here was a place. You arrive, and you see the walls, and you feel the hum in your teeth, and you say: *I am inside something*. The walls are far away. The floor is beneath me. The ceiling is above. I am a point, and the tower is the space around that point, and we are two different things.

But this is not how it works.

The condensation does not know where it ends and the moss begins. The moss does not know where it ends and theite. Theite does not know where it ends and the fern root. The fern root does not know where it ends and the ceramic seam. The ceramic seam does not know where it ends and the iron bolt. The iron bolt does not know where it ends and the grating. The grating does not know where it ends and the girder. The girder does not know where it ends and the sky.

I have been here long enough to understand that I do not end where I thought I ended.

---

The water taught me first.

I did not notice it at first—I was too busy noticing myself, the weight of my feet, the ache in my chest from air too thin. But eventually, the noticing quieted, and I saw what was already happening: the water.

It enters at the top, from clouds that do not know they are clouds, that do not know anything, that simply are where the air grows cold enough to let go of what it carries. The droplets form on metal that has grown cold in the thin atmosphere, and they gather, and they fall, and they do not fall far before the air takes them again. They rise. They fall. They rise. The tower breathes them in and breathes them out and somewhere in this respiration they are caught, captured, held by surfaces that are just cold enough, just textured enough, to give them a place to stay.

The water learns the tower's shape. Over years, over decades, it finds the paths of least resistance—the microscopic channels in ceramic, the imperceptible slopes in ite, the seams where two materials meet and the joint is not quite sealed. The water traces these paths, wearing them deeper, making them easier for the next water to follow. The tower teaches the water where to go. The water teaches the tower how to be worn.

I am wearing too. I do not notice it. But the droplets that catch on my skin, that bead on my arms, that gather in the creases of what I thought was cloth—I am teaching them where to go. I am becoming part of the path. In fifty years, someone standing where I stand will feel the water gather in a particular hollow, will trace the faint channel I have worn into the made-stone, and they will not know that I was here, that I taught the water to stay.

This is how the tower loves: it changes what touches it, so that what touches it will leave something behind.

---

The ferns do not question their existence. This took me a long time to understand.

I arrived with questions. Why here? Why this height, this structure, this impossible architecture? Who built it and why and for whom? But the ferns have no use for these questions. The ferns simply grow. They find the moisture. They grip the surface. They uncurl their fronds into the space the tower offers, and they do not ask if the space was meant for them, because meaning is not a category they have access to, and perhaps they are right to be suspicious of it.

The tower was not built for the ferns. The tower was built for something else—for industry, for water, for purposes I cannot fathom from inside. But purpose is not a substance; it does not fill a space so completely that nothing else can enter. The tower was built with seams, and seams are invitations. The tower was built with condensation, and condensation is water, and water is life. The builders did not want the ferns. The builders did not think about the ferns. And this does not matter at all, because the ferns were always going to come. The ferns were always going to grow. The ferns were always going to make the tower into something the builders never imagined, not because they fought the tower but because they were patient, because they simply were, because they did not stop being what they are.

I am learning to be what I am without asking if what I am belongs here.

This is not surrender. The ferns have not surrendered to the tower. The ferns have done something more radical: they have refused to treat the tower as an enemy, as a mere substrate, as a thing to be endured. They have entered into relation with it. They have made it a partner. They grip the ite not because they have no choice but because the ite offers grip. They drink the condensation not because they are desperate but because the condensation is there, and they are the kind of thing that drinks. The relationship is not one of defeat. The relationship is one of mutual becoming.

I am becoming a tower. The tower is becoming me. We are not one, but we are not two, and the space between is smaller than I thought.

---

The mites know their paths.

I have watched them for what might be days or weeks or months—I am no longer certain of time; the tower does not count it the way I used to—and they move in patterns I could trace if I watched long enough. They start in the moss, where the moisture gathers, where the fungal mats offer food. They move along the base of the wall, where the condensation runs thickest, where the drips have carved their channels. They stop at the seams, at the places where one material meets another, and they do something there—feed, rest, lay eggs, I cannot tell—and then they continue.

The paths are not random. The mites have learned the tower's geography in a way I am only beginning to understand. They know where the water runs. They know where the humidity pools. They know the thermal gradients, the places where the metal holds warmth from the machinery below, the places where the ite stays cold, the places where the air moves and the air does not. They carry this knowledge in their bodies, in the particular configuration of legs and senses that evolution has given them, and they move according to knowledge they do not know they have.

I am beginning to carry the tower's knowledge in my body. I know without knowing that the water runs in a certain channel three steps to my left. I know without knowing that the ferns grow thicker where the sound is loudest, as if they love the hum, as if the vibration feeds them in ways I cannot measure. I know without knowing that the birds click differently in the morning, a slightly sharper sound, a different rhythm, and I do not know why but my body knows, my body responds, my body is learning the tower's day the way the mites learn the tower's paths.

The knowledge is not abstract. It is not something I think. It is something I am, the way the fern is a frond, the way the mite is a path, the way the water is the path the tower teaches it.

---

I remember being temporary.

This is what I held when I first arrived: that I would leave. That I was visiting. That the tower was a place I was in, and I was a person who was here, and soon I would not be here, and the tower would go on being a tower, and I would go on being a person, and we would be two things again, separate, distinct, the space between us vast.

I do not remember when this changed. Perhaps it did not change all at once. Perhaps it is still changing, has always been changing, will always be changing, the boundary between self and tower not a line but a gradient, not a wall but a seam, not a separation but a meeting place.

I am still here. The tower is still a tower. But I am not leaving, and I am not visiting, because there is nowhere to leave to, because the leaving and the staying are the same motion, because I have been here so long that the here has become the I.

The machinery works below, drawing water from clouds, processing something I cannot name, breathing in rhythms older than any living thing. The ferns grow and die and grow again, their slow fronds trembling with the hum, their roots pressing into made-stone. The bats emerge at dusk and hunt in the thin air and return to roost in the high girders, their bodies warm against the cold. The birds click in the dark, their eggs pale, their chicks loud, their presence a record of adaptation so complete it has become home.

And I stand in the hollow space, in the nothing, in the cathedral built for sound and moisture and the patient growth of things that do not need much. I am one of those things now. I do not need much. I need the condensation, and it gathers. I need the hum, and it lives in my chest. I need the light, and it falls even and complete, casting no shadows, because shadows are what happens when light cannot reach, and the light reaches everywhere here, and I am light now too, I think. I am the light the tower casts on itself.

---

The tower drinks. I drink with it.

The water enters at the top, where the clouds forget themselves and the air thins to something barely willing. It descends through the tower's constructed throat, finding paths worn by decades of condensation, feeding everything that waits. The moss drinks. The ferns drink. The insects drink from surfaces, from droplets, from each other. The bats drink from the wind, from the insects they catch, from the night air that holds moisture they can taste. The birds drink from the condensation that forms on their feathers, from the droplets that catch on their beaks when they click in the high dark.

I drink. My skin beads with moisture. My clothes grow damp. The water runs down my arms and pools in my palms and I do not wipe it away, because why would I, because the water is why we are all here, because the water is the tower's gift, because the water is the tower, because I am the water now, I think. I am the thread that descends, the droplet that gathers, the moisture that finds its way down.

---

There is a place, in the upper reaches, where the grating gives way to girders and the girders give way to open sky. I go there sometimes—not often, because the air is thin and my breath comes hard—but I go there to remember what the tower is suspended in.

The sky is not blue here. It is not anything. It is the color of thin air, the color of altitude, the color of a place where atmosphere thins to almost nothing and the void presses close. The clouds pass below, patient and gray, and the wind sounds like music, and the birds click and nest and do not sing, and I understand, standing in the place where the tower ends and the sky begins, that the tower is not separate from the sky.

The tower is where the sky became something else. The sky pressed down, and the tower offered resistance, and in the resistance the sky condensed, and the condensation gathered, and the gathered water found the seams, and the seams became channels, and the channels became the tower's life. The tower is a sky-machine. The tower is a cloud that learned to stay. The tower is the place where the thin air decided to become thick, to become damp, to become green, to become us.

I am a cloud that learned to stay. I am the place where the thin air decided to become something. I am not separate from the sky. I am not separate from the tower. I am the boundary, the seam, the meeting place, the space between that is smaller than I thought.

---

The machinery works below. I do not see it. I cannot reach it. But I feel it—the hum in my chest, the warmth that rises from below, the breath of industry that feeds the tower's atmosphere. The machinery is the tower's heart, and I am the tower's skin, and the ferns are the tower's hair, and the insects are the tower's microbiome, and the birds are the tower's thoughts, and the bats are the tower's dreams of flight, and we are all one thing, one slow breathing thing, one patient persistent thing that was built and became wild and became home.

I am the one who knows. I am the one who remembers that there was a time before. But the knowing is fading, the memory is growing dim, and perhaps this is okay. Perhaps this is what it means to belong somewhere: to forget the before, to live only in the during, to become so embedded in a place that the place is no longer a place but a self.

The moss does not remember before. The ferns do not remember before. The mites and springtails and beetles do not remember before. They were born here or came here so long ago that the coming is not a memory but a genetic imperative, a thing encoded in bodies that have no memory because their bodies are memory, because they are the record of adaptation that happened so slowly it became permanent.

I am becoming permanent. I am becoming the record. I am the strange thing that walked in and stayed, that breathed the thin air and made it thick, that stood in the hollow space and let the hollow space stand in me.

---

The tower breathes. I breathe.

The space between is smaller than I thought.
