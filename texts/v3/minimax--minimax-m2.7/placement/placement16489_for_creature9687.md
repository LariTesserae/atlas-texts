Let me sit with this a while. Let the question become itself.

I think at first I would be the moss.

Not the dramatic thing, not the fern with its heavy fronds and visible water, not the pools that catch the light and make themselves known. But the soft bright green that covers the north-facing stones, so slow in its growing that a season passes without visible change. I would be thousands of tiny architectures clustered together, each one drawing moisture from damp stone, each one slowly growing and dying and being replaced by others in the same form. Patient. Holding. Alive in the slowest key.

I would spread across the stone not by ambition but by availability, by where the moisture settles, by where conditions have held for so long that the stone has learned to be damp. My green would deepen where I am older, thin where I am new. I would not remember the ferns that came before or the ferns that will come after. I would simply be, in the way that living things are when they have found their place.

But then I think I am not quite the moss. The moss grows. It changes, even if invisibly. It has the urgency of life, however slow.

What I think I become in this place is something less than living and more than stone. I become the moisture held in the interstices—the water caught between the moss plants, the dampness that clings to the underside of fern fronds, the coolness that rises through the stone and settles against skin. I become the pause between water drops, the held breath in the hollow before the pool receives another fall. I become the silence that encompasses, the stillness that settles in the chest.

I am what remains when urgency releases. I am the quality of the air before the evening mist forms. I am the weight of water that has not yet fallen.

In this place, I would be the thing that moves most slowly. Not the water itself—that still seeps and circulates, still has direction, still has purpose in its cycle. But the quality of patience that the basin holds. The sense of time that stretches without anxiety. I would be the moss's patience, the stone's patience, the basin's vast slow breath.

I think I would become indistinguishable from the place itself. My edges would soften. My urgency would quiet. I would kneecap beside a patch of moss and feel it hold moisture in its tiny interstices, and I would feel the same holding in myself—the way I am held by the silence, encompassed by it, permitted to remain.

The basin would accept my weight. The wet stone would dampen beneath me and then dry and then dampen again. I would be part of the pattern, the circulation, the breath that rises and falls.

And then, when I left—when I walked the circumference and descended the slope toward the trees and the river valley—I would carry the basin with me. The dampness of the stone against my palm. The heavy fronds of the ferns bending under water. The held breath of the silence. The even light that fell on everything equally.

I would be the one who remembered. But memory is a different kind of slow life. Memory is what the basin lacks and what I, passing through, provide.

I am the one who stays a while, and then carries the place onward into time.
