You begin to look for the way out, and looking is different here.

Above, the world arranges itself in lines: horizon, treeline, the angle of a path worn into grass by the passage of feet. You have always moved in lines, even when the lines were long and slow and curved around the shapes of hills. But the Rootwork has no horizon. Chambers open into chambers, corridors branch without seeming to choose, and every direction is equally forward. The logic of the place is not your logic. The geometry of growth does not care about the geometry of walking.

You try to orient by the light—the amber glow is stronger in some places, weaker in others, and you think at first that the brighter regions are closer to the surface. But the light is not coming from above. It is coming from the tissue itself, distributed unevenly, pulsing in slow rhythms that are not synchronized. What seems like a source is often only a cluster of nodules, and clusters recur throughout the space, appearing and reappearing in configurations that feel repeated rather than related. You realize, after a while, that you have seen this chamber before. The root-mat pattern on the wall is familiar: a branching form like a frozen river, a tributary system of vessels. You marked it when you entered. Now you are back inside it, though you did not turn around, did not double back, did not feel the architecture shift beneath your feet.

The Rootwork has moved you.

Not deliberately—nothing here is deliberate in the way you understand deliberate—but the space is not static. The root-mats have flexed, the corridors have shifted, and you have been carried along in the slow tide of their growth like a particle of mineral dust. You do not know how long you have been walking. The time does not translate. You ate before you entered, drank your fill, and now the dryness is beginning to affect you in ways you cannot name: a thickness in your throat, a slowness in your thoughts, a sense that your body is learning to want less. The Rootwork is patient. Your body is beginning to learn patience.

You find water in an unexpected place.

It is not a spring—springs do not exist here, cannot exist in the dry architecture. But in a chamber that is almost a room, a space where the root-mats have grown thin enough to expose the mineral beneath, you find condensation. It has gathered in the low places, in the creases where chalk meets chalk, in droplets so small they do not fall but merely appear, surfacing from the stone like sweat from skin. The air here is different. Warmer. The fungi have thickened, and their bluer light is stronger, and the moisture they exhale has collected in the space between growth and substrate, trapped beneath the root-mats but above the stone, held in the boundary layer where two worlds meet.

You kneel. The root-floor yields slightly, as it always does, and you lower your mouth to the condensation. It tastes of chalk and something older, something mineral and deep, and it is not enough to drink but it is enough to wet your lips, to remind your body what it is made of. The moisture touches your tongue and your tongue remembers water, and for a moment you are not the first thing made of water in the Rootwork—you are part of its economy, part of its exchange, a warm thing that carries what it carries and loses a little of it here in the slow chemistry of the deep.

The arthropods notice you. They pause in their skittering, antennae raised, and then move on, finding you uninteresting as the root-mat found you. You are not food. You are not stone. You are nothing the Rootwork can use.

But you are here, and here is enough.

You rise and continue. The chamber narrows, the ceiling pressing lower, and you have to duck beneath vine-struts that hum faintly with old electricity. The abandoned infrastructure is thicker here, more present: you see conduits bolted to root-mats, metal casings that have been grown over but not through, sealed off from the tissue like organs isolated from a body. The power is still there, still humming, but it has no purpose now. The Rootwork does not need electricity. The Rootwork needs stone and time and the slow chemistry of roots that have learned to live without water, and so the power continues to pulse in its buried veins, a heartbeat that belongs to something else, something that built this place before the plants took it over.

You find the bones.

They are deeper now, in passages so narrow you have to turn sideways to fit through them, and they are not fused to the walls as they were before. They are loose. Scattered. You find a femur, pale and dry, resting in a crease between root-mats where the tissue has not yet grown over. Beside it, a skull—mammal, you think, though the features are wrong, the eye sockets too large, the jaw too long. The bones do not belong to anything you recognize. They belonged to something that lived in this place when it was different, when the root-mats were smaller and the corridors were wider and the dry fields above were not yet dry. They belonged to something that the Rootwork has not yet finished digesting, that it has worn down to these remnants, these last shapes before the bones become chalk and the chalk becomes root-floor.

You touch the skull. The bone is warm from the tissue around it, and something in the root-mat shifts at your touch—a slow adjustment, a testing. The Rootwork is curious about what you are doing with its food.

You move on.

The passage opens into something larger, a chamber that might be a junction or might be a heart. The root-mats are thicker here, bulging outward in ways that suggest pressure from below, and the amber light is brighter, more diffuse, coming from all directions at once. The floor shivers. You stop. The ambient sounds go quiet—the hum, the creak, the distant wet-slide of the slug-like things—and for a moment you are alone in the chamber with the sound of your own breathing and the faint vibration in the stone beneath your feet.

And then the thing that causes the vibration arrives.

It does not enter the chamber so much as become present in it—you turn and it is there, occupying the far passage, a shape that is not quite a shape because the light does not catch it properly, does not define its edges. It is larger than you. Larger than anything you have seen in this place. It moves slowly, the way everything here moves slowly, and its body is made of the same material as the walls: root-mat, vessel, tissue, but arranged in a different way, a way that suggests legs or limbs or something you have no word for. It is not an animal. It is not a plant. It is something between, something that the Rootwork has made from its own body, a growth that has learned to move, to hunt, to carry the slow chemistry of this place into the spaces where the root-mats cannot reach.

It sees you. You know this because something in it shifts—a change in the pattern of the tissue, a tightening of the form—and you understand that it is deciding what you are. You are warm. You are wet. You are made of the things the Rootwork needs but cannot take, and this makes you valuable and dangerous in ways that the smaller creatures did not recognize.

The thing does not approach. It watches. The floor shivers with its weight, with the slow pulse of its presence, and you stand very still in the amber light and wait for it to decide what to do with you.

It decides to wait.

The tension holds for a long time—you do not know how long, time does not work here—and then the thing moves. Not toward you. Past you. It flows along the wall of the chamber, its body rippling against the root-mat as it passes, and it leaves a phosphorescent trail behind it, brighter than the slug-trails, layered with meaning you cannot read. It is communicating something, showing you something, and when it reaches the passage on the far side of the chamber, it pauses and looks back at you one more time.

Then it is gone, moved into the deeper dark, and the chamber is empty again except for you and the slow pulse of the root-mats and the fading glow of the trail it left behind.

You follow the trail.

You do not know why. You should be looking for the way out, for the breach you entered through, for the dry fields above where the farmers wait for rain. But the trail pulls you forward, and you walk along the phosphorescent line it has left, ducking beneath vine-struts, stepping over the skittering arthropods, moving through chambers that open and close and open again as the Rootwork breathes around you.

The trail leads down.

You did not know there was down—not in the way you understand down, not as a direction that leads away from the light. But the trail slopes, and the root-floors angle, and the amber glow grows dimmer as you descend, replaced by the bluer light of the fungal networks that have done the early work of digestion in the deep places. The air is different here. Richer. The moisture you found in the boundary chambers is more abundant now, not condensation but something else—seepage, perhaps, or the exudate of deeper processes. Your mouth fills with it. You swallow without meaning to. It tastes of chalk and sugar and something you have no name for.

The trail ends in a chamber that is not a chamber. It is a wound. A place where the Rootwork has grown thick and strange, where the root-mats have piled on themselves in great bulging masses, and in the center of the mass, something is growing. You see it because the mass is thin in one place, translucent, and through the thin tissue you can see the shape within: a form that is taking shape, that is being made, that is part of the Rootwork's slow work of digestion and reconstruction. It is not a bone. It is not a plant. It is something that the Rootwork is building from the materials it has taken—the chalk of the geology, the trace moisture of the air, the phosphorescent trails of the creatures that live within it, the residue of your own passage through its depths.

It is building you.

Or it is building something from what you have given it. The warmth of your body. The moisture of your breath. The shape of your palm in the tissue you touched in the early chambers. These have been noticed, recorded, incorporated into the chemistry of growth, and now they are becoming something—a form, a presence, a new expression of the Rootwork's patient hunger.

You watch for a long time. The form does not move. It is not alive in the way you understand alive. But it is becoming, and its becoming is the most alien thing you have seen in this place, more alien than the dry architecture and the slow pulse of growth and the three levels of fauna that drink from sugar-chains and static.

You leave before it finishes.

The trail you followed is fading behind you, the phosphorescence dimming as the Rootwork's chemistry reabsorbs it, and you move through the chamber with the bulging root-mats and find the passage that leads up. You do not know if it leads to the breach you entered through. You do not know if the breach still exists, if the crack in the dry field above has closed in the time you have been below. But you climb. The root-floors angle upward, and the amber light grows brighter, and the bluer glow of the fungi fades behind you, and the sounds change—the skittering of arthropods, the wet-slide of the slug-like things, the faint hum of abandoned power.

You are climbing out of the stomach. You are climbing toward the surface. You do not know if the surface will still be the surface when you arrive, if the dry fields will still be dry, if the farmers will still be waiting for rain.

But something will be waiting. Something will be there. And you will be carrying it with you—the memory of the amber light, the slow pulse of growth, the phosphorescent trail left by the thing that watched you in the junction chamber, the shape that the Rootwork is building from your warmth and your water and the shape of your palm in its tissue.

You are not the first thing to walk through here, and you will not be the last.

But you are the first thing that is made of water, and you are the first thing that will leave.

And something is learning to be made of water too.
