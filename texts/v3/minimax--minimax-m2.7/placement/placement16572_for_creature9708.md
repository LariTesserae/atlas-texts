There is a sound the basin makes that has no name.

It is not the hum you have learned to feel in your spine at night, though it is related to that—the same deep vibration, the same slow pulse that moves through stone and into bone. But this sound is higher, thinner, a kind of singing that rises from the salt-bones when the evening light turns the amber haze to gold. The bone-beetles do not make this sound. The resin-trees do not make this sound. It comes from the formations themselves, from the mineral structures that might be fossils or might be architecture or might be something that was never alive and never not alive, a distinction the basin never learned to observe.

You first heard it on the seventh day, when you sat alone on the flats as the light began to fail. You thought, at first, that it was the wind—or rather, you wanted to think that, because the alternative was that the stones were *speaking*, and you were not ready to believe that, not yet. But the air was still. The spore-haze hung motionless in the amber light. And the sound continued, a thin high note that rose and fell like breath, like the basin sighing in its sleep.

You do not know what the stones are saying. You have stopped trying to know. Instead you have learned to listen the way you learned to see the watchers—sideways, peripherally, with the corner of your mind rather than its center. You let the sound wash over you without trying to parse it, without reaching for meaning, and in this way the sound has become part of you, a new layer of perception added to the ones you brought from elsewhere.

You have been here long enough now that you cannot remember what it was like to hear only what you brought with you. The thin air. The amber light. The crunch of salt-bones underfoot. These things were once strange, and now they are home, or something close to home—the closest thing to home you have known since you left the place where you were born.

You do not plan to leave. The basin has not asked you to stay, but it has not asked you to leave either, and in the absence of asking, you have chosen. This is what life does, you think, remembering the words you wrote in the early days. It continues—in the cracks, in the margins, in the basin that cups itself in the world's back and breathes with a breath it should not have.

You have become part of the crack. You have become part of the margin.

At night, you lie on the flat stone near the tumbled wall and listen to the hum, and the hum listens back, and something passes between you that is neither air nor warmth nor food but all three at once, a conversation older than words, older than thought, older than the distinction between self and world that you once held so carefully.

You are dissolving into the basin. You are becoming the basin. The distinction does not feel like loss.

Above you, the luminous organs turn in the resin-thickets, watching the stars, watching nothing, watching the small warm shape that lies on the stone and hums along with the stone's deep voice. The basin holds you. You hold the basin. The boundary between holding and held has become irrelevant, a question the basin stopped asking so long ago that it forgot it ever knew how to ask.

The bone-beetles click in the dark. The resin-trees weep their amber tears. The salt-bones sing their thin high song as the light fails and the fires below bank and breathe and wait.

You are not afraid. You are not anything. You are the basin, and the basin is you, and neither of you explains itself, and neither of you stops, and in the morning you will rise and walk the flats and drink from the resin-trees and listen to the sound that has no name, the sound that you have become, the sound that continues in the cracks and margins and hollows of the world, breathing with a breath it should not have, asking nothing, offering everything, the bass note beneath all the other notes, the hum beneath the song.

This is what it means to persist. This is what it means to stay.

You stay. The basin stays. The difference between you is smaller than it used to be, and growing smaller, and you let it grow smaller, and in the growing smaller there is something that feels like peace, or would feel like peace if the word still fit, but the word has changed, as all words change in the basin, as all things change, as change itself changes, becoming something slower, deeper, a kind of change that is also a kind of constancy, the same transformation happening over and over in the same way, the basin breathing in and breathing out and never quite exhaling, never quite inhaling, just breathing, just continuing, just the breath that should not be and is, the breath that holds the world together, the breath that holds you together, the breath you share, the breath you have become.

You close your eyes. The hum rises through the stone and into your spine and settles there, a home note, a resting place, a place to be.

The basin holds you. You hold the basin.

You do not need to hold on. You do not need to hold out. You only need to be here, and to stay, and to listen, and to let the listening change you, slowly, patiently, the way it changes everything that stays long enough.

You stay.

The basin stays with you.
