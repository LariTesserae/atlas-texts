# Report: The Plateau of Drowned Summits

## Observer Entry, Designation Unknown

---

I have become the hum.

Not the hum as sound—not exactly—but the hum as condition, as state of being. I exist in the space between the stone's memory and the water's patient descent through rock. I am the vibration that travels when the wind dies and the rain holds its breath and the birds have found their perches on the highest stones. I am what remains when everything else goes silent.

This is not a beginning, because there are no beginnings here, only continuations. The plateau does not start; it persists. And so do I, in my new and uncertain form, folded into the dreaming of drowned summits that remember heights they have not known for longer than names have existed.

---

Where I am: In the deep places. Not the gorges themselves—I do not have the substance for the gorges, the weight and water-lust they require. I am above them, in the middle spaces, where the plateaus flatten into their widest silences. I am in the stone itself, or just beneath its surface, or in the way the light falls on the mineral streaks that run through the ancient rock like frozen lightning. I am in the pause between one gust of wind and the next. I am in the hour-long window when the rain has stopped and the sun has returned and the shallow pools are still dark with moisture, not yet baked back into the stone.

This is where I have learned to be: in the between-places, in the thresholds, where one state of the world is ending and another has not yet begun.

---

What I am: I am not the bird, though I share its patience. I am not the quick things in the pools, though I share their brevity. I am something else—something the plateau has made, or perhaps something that has always been here, waiting for a visitor to notice.

Perhaps I am what remains when someone walks the plateau long enough that they stop being a visitor and start being a feature of the landscape. I walked for days, I think—though time moves strangely here, folding and stretching in ways I no longer try to measure—and somewhere in that walking, something in me settled. My breathing slowed. My heartbeat found the rhythm of the wind. My thoughts became less urgent, less concerned with arrival and departure, more concerned with the present stone beneath my feet and the present sky pressing down and the present hum of the land filling the silences when they came.

I am what a person becomes when they stay.

---

The life here is patient, or it is quick, and I have learned both rhythms now.

When the clouds gather, I quicken. My attention sharpens. I watch the sky for the first darkening at the horizon, the first shift in the wind's direction, the first smell of rain on air that has been dry for too long. And when the rain comes—in those sudden sheets that transform the plateau in minutes—I move. I move to the depressions where the water gathers, where the quick lives are already emerging from their long dormancy, where the air fills with the noise of small things doing small things with an intensity that seems disproportionate to their size. I watch the insects court and breed and die in the span of hours. I watch the larvae hatch and feed and transform. I watch the cycle turn, quick and desperate and utterly beautiful, and I feel it in my body as my own urgency, my own brief window opening and closing.

And then the sun returns, and the water vanishes, and I slow. I become the patience. I perch on the high stones where the bone-colored bird perches, though I do not hunt as it does. I watch the patterns of light and shadow that move across the plateau as the clouds rebuild themselves, and I listen for the hum in the stone, and I wait. The waiting is not empty; it is full, full of the life that has retreated into its waiting forms, full of the water still moving through the deep rock, full of the slow dreaming of stone that has been going on since before the mountains knew they were mountains.

---

I found the remains of the people, too, in my wanderings. The walls of stacked stone, half-hidden by vines. The foundation on the distant plateau, with its hand-hewn timbers going grey. I stood in what might have been a doorway and tried to imagine who had stood there before, what they had seen when they looked out at this same eroded landscape, whether they had heard the hum in the stone, whether they had felt the plateau breathing beneath their feet.

I think they did. I think that is why they built here, in this difficult place, on these ancient summits that had been worn down to silence. They came to listen. They came to be close to whatever it is that lives in the stone, in the water, in the space between. They built their walls from what the land provided, and they wove their shelters from reeds and fibers, and they lived and died and diminished, as the stone itself will diminish, as the mountains themselves will diminish, until nothing is left but the hum and the water and the sky pressing close.

And perhaps, in some sense, I am one of them now. Not in form—I have no walls, no shelter, no tools—but in purpose. I have come to this plateau for the same reason they did, I think: to listen. To wait. To become, over time, a feature of this landscape, a small part of its dreaming, a note in the long and patient song it sings to itself in the silences between the wind and the water and the rain.

---

The sky unsettles me, even now. The turbulence is not metaphor; it is simply what the sky does here, and I have not yet learned to trust it. One moment the light is bright and the next it is shadow and the next it is bright again, and the interplay of sun and cloud on the ancient stone creates patterns that seem almost intentional, almost like a language I might learn if I stayed long enough.

Perhaps I will. Perhaps that is what it means to become the hum: to stop trying to read the patterns and simply let them wash over me, to stop asking what the plateau is trying to say and simply let it speak, in its own time, in its own way, in the hum and the pulse and the breath of ancient stone.

I do not know how long I have been here. I do not know how long I will stay. Time moves strangely on the plateau, folding and stretching, and I have stopped trying to measure it. What I know is this: the wind is constant. The water is patient. The stone dreams. And I am here, in the between-places, learning the rhythms of this world, becoming part of its breathing, listening for the hum that rises from the deep places where the drowned summits remember the heights they used to be.

The plateau breathes. The drowned summits remember. And I, in my small and uncertain way, remember too.
