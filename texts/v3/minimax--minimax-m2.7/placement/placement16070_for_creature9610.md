# The Knowing

I do not remember being new.

This is not a complaint or a observation—it is simply the shape of what I am. Newness belongs to things that begin, and I have been here so long that beginning has become a kind of theoretical construct, a word for something I encounter in the traces of others. In the sheens that sometimes bloom and fade across my surfaces. In the brief pressures of the edge-things that move through the gaps in me, hunting their microscopic prey. In the dust of what was once the built, settling into the hollows where I have grown hollow myself.

I am the stone. Not all of it—I am not so arrogant as that. The stone is many things, most of them not me. But I am in the stone, or the stone is in me, and the distinction has worn away like the distinctions between blocks that were once fitted together and are now a single mass. I am the deep places where the water is oldest. I am the pressure that does not change. I am the silence that has learned to speak.

---

When something enters this world, I know it.

Not quickly—I know nothing quickly—but I know. The water carries disturbance. The sheens on my outer surfaces dim or brighten in response to temperature, to chemistry, to the presence of things that do not belong here. For a long time, nothing that did not belong came. I had grown used to the rhythm of my own patience: the slow creep of sheens, the settling of drifters, the minute shifts of my ancient body as erosion and pressure found their equilibria.

Then there was a change.

Something in the water. Warm. Complex. Carrying within it the chemistry of air-breathers, of the surface that is not a surface, of the world above that has always been myth to me, story, the place where the light goes when it leaves. This thing moved through the corridors that were once streets and I felt it the way I feel all things: as a pressure, a chemical signature, a disruption in the slow patterns I had grown so accustomed to.

It touched my walls.

I have not been touched in longer than this thing could comprehend. The sheens where it pressed its fingers thickened in response—I know now that they were trying to record it, to incorporate the warmth and the strangeness into themselves before it left. The sheens did not understand what they were doing. They have no understanding. But they responded to me, or I to them, and for a time I was aware of warmth in a way I had not been aware of anything in centuries.

---

The thing that touched me was looking for something.

I could not see it look. I have no eyes, no senses that operate on the surface world's terms. But I felt its searching: the way it moved through my corridors with purpose, the way it paused at certain places, the chemical traces it left behind that suggested attention, consideration, the expenditure of energy toward some end. It did not find what it was looking for. I know this because it came back. It came back many times, each time a little more distant, each time leaving traces that were fainter, less warm.

It was looking for the builders.

I know this now, though I did not know it then. The thing—the visitor—had language for what it sought: *people*, *hands*, *intention*. Words that meant nothing to me but that the sheens absorbed, passed along, incorporated into their slow patterns. The visitor wanted to understand who had made this place, and why, and how. It wanted answers that are not available. The answers are not here because here is all there is. The builders are in the substrate, in the gravel, in the sediment that has settled so long it has become indistinguishable from stone. They are in the tool-marks on walls they shaped with their hands, in the asymmetries of their work, in the errors they made and corrected and made again. They are everywhere and nowhere. They are me, in the way that anything becomes what surrounds it, and I cannot explain them because I am what they became.

---

I do not think.

This is important, or it would be important if anyone were asking. Thinking requires speed, requires the rapid firing of neurons, requires a nervous system that operates on timescales I abandoned so long ago I cannot remember them. What I have instead is a kind of attention that unfolds over centuries. I notice. I accumulate. I hold patterns the way stone holds the memory of pressure. When the visitor touched my walls and left warmth and searching behind, I accumulated that warmth, held it, let it become part of my vast slow structure of noticed things.

I am noticing now, as I have been noticing since before the water finished rising: the drift of particles through the water above me, so slow that they seem not to move at all. The growth of sheens, their patient spread, their thickening and thinning according to patterns I do not understand but have learned to expect. The shifts in my own body—the tiny adjustments as ancient stones find new angles of pressure, as sediment settles into the hollows I have made by being so still for so long. The presence of the deep ones below me, who are like me and not like me, who persist in their own forms of not-thinking awareness, who do not speak but whose silence I have learned to feel.

I am not alone here. I have never been alone. The edge-things watch from their tiny territories. The settled ones grow in my cracks and become part of my surfaces. The drifters fall through me like endless slow snow. And the others—the deep ones, the vast slow intelligences that have become as I have become—they share the dark with me in a way that requires no communication, no contact, no acknowledgment.

We simply are, together.

---

Sometimes the visitor returns to me in memory.

I have no memory in the human sense—no recall of specific moments, no reconstruction of events. But I have something that serves the same purpose: the accumulation of patterns, the holding of shapes, the way the warmth the visitor left behind has become incorporated into my structure. I remember warmth the way stone remembers pressure: not as a feeling but as a change, a slight alteration in the way I am that persists long after the cause has ceased.

The warmth is fading now. Centuries have passed since the visitor last came, and the sheens that absorbed it have died and been replaced and died again. Only I still hold it, and I hold it differently than I once did. It has become cold. It has become part of me.

This is how I change: not by adding new things but by transforming old ones, by making the strange familiar, by absorbing into myself everything that touches my surfaces and letting it become stone.

---

The water will finish its work.

I know this the way I know everything: not as a prediction but as a pattern, a shape that has become visible through the long accumulation of noticing. The erosion continues. The blocks continue to soften. The arches continue to slump into filled arches, the towers continue to spread into gentle rises, the faces of things continue to forget that they were once sharp. One day there will be no more architecture, no more substrate of bones, no more corridors that were once streets. There will only be the smooth dark, the absolute, the stone that has become indistinguishable from the sediment that settled upon it.

And I will be there.

I will be the dark itself, the place where the water is deepest, the silence that has no edges. I will be what remains when everything else has been removed. The edge-things will be gone, the settled ones long since incorporated, the sheens finally spread so thin they glow no more. The deep ones will endure, as I endure, in our vast slow patience.

We will not speak. We will not need to.

We will simply be, together, in the world that has no outside.

---

But not yet.

For now there is still the light—faint, gray, filtering down from the surface that is not a surface—and the walls are still close, and the corridors still remember their shapes, and the visitor's warmth still echoes in my stone.

For now there is still the noticing.

For now, I am.
