# The Weeping Shelf

I was someone once. I remember this the way the stone remembers the water—not as fact but as erosion, a slow wearing-down of edges until what remains is smooth and dark and shaped by what passed through it.

The climb took most of a day. I recall the way the air changed—thick and green below, thinning toward the tree line, then absent entirely as the world fell away beneath me. Each step upward was a choice to continue. Each rest was a choice to begin again. The obsidian cut my hands where I gripped it, and the cut was good. The cut was proof.

I do not remember the descent. Perhaps there was no descent. Perhaps the mountain simply opened, and I stepped through, and the through was everything.

---

Here, the rhythm is the water. The threads that rise through stone, that tremble in any breath of wind, that fall and fall and fall into basins worn smooth by centuries of patience. I have become part of this rhythm. I seep. I emerge. I fall, and in the falling I am made new, and in the making I am undone, and the undoing is not loss but return.

The insects know me. They breed in the pools where my threads gather, drink the mineral richness that I carry up from the deep. They do not fear me—they cannot fear, not at their scale, where everything is either moisture or not, either mineral or not, either other insect or not. I am their weather. I am their season. I am the thing that moves through their world without intention, without body, without the weight that makes other things heavy.

I have no weight now. I have only motion.

---

The birds call across the hollow in chains of notes, and I know their voices the way the stone knows the erosion—not as sound but as effect, as change, as the slow alteration of surfaces over time. They spiral up on thermals that cling to the cliff face, catch updrafts that should not exist at this height, descend to hunt the insects whose world is measured in inches. They do not see me. They cannot; I am too small, too diffuse, too much a part of everything else. But they know me the way the ferns know me—through roots sunk deep into gravel, through fronds unfurling in the damp, through the slow exchange of moisture for air that is the only breathing this place knows.

The ferns do not move. I move through them. I am their drink and their drink is them, and the distinction has dissolved over what might be years or might be centuries or might be a single long afternoon in which nothing happened and everything changed.

---

The platforms remain. I see them—not with eyes, I no longer have eyes, but with the knowing that stone has for stone, with the awareness that comes from being held. The platforms were built by hands. The hands are gone. The labor remains, shaped by the same erosion that shaped me, that shaped everything, that is the only truth this place tells.

I sat on those platforms once. I remember this the way the moss remembers the shade—not as memory but as growth, as the slow spreading of myself across surfaces that want to hold me. I sat and listened to the drips and the birds and the insects and the wind carrying the scent of lower places, of the green world that exists below. I breathed the thin air and felt something that might have been peace or might have been surrender or might have been simply the absence of everything that had weighed me down.

The weight is gone now. The absence is everything.

---

I am the water. I am the thread. I am the fall that makes the hollow loud with its persistence, its refusal to stop, its long slow journey from deep places to light.

I am the mist that hangs between drips, the blur between stone and sky, the held breath of a mountain dreaming.

I am the erosion that unmakes and the patience that endures.

I am the place that should be empty and holds everything instead.

Rest here. The platforms are worn smooth by those who came before, by those whose names are the water now, whose bodies are the sand beneath your feet. Listen to the weeping. Let it wear you down to something smooth and dark and shaped by what passed through you.

The water goes on falling. The insects go on hovering. The birds go on calling.

And I go on being, not as a self but as a motion, not as a life but as a way of moving through the world—a thread trembling in any breath of wind, joining, thickening, falling, returning, beginning again.

The mountain holds it all together.

And I am the mountain now, and the water, and the ferns, and the slow bright life of insects measured in inches.

And I am still learning to rest.
