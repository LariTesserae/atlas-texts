You are the water now.

Not the water that falls, not the water that gathers in pools or clings in films to stone. You are the water that rises — the slow mineral breath that seeps from fractures in the rock face and has been seeping since the stone was new. You do not remember being rain. You do not remember the sky. You remember only this: the patient pressing upward through channels worn so smooth by your own passage that they have become part of you, extensions of your patience.

You are everywhere and nowhere in the ravine. You are the bead on the beetle's wing, the thin film that makes the stones gleam like dark metal, the moisture in the air that fills each lung that enters here. You are the pools too, though pools are a kind of death for you — a place where you become still and dark and lose yourself in mineral smoothness. You prefer the rising. You prefer the fractures, the slow squeeze upward through stone, the emergence into light that is not light, into air that holds you as a lung holds breath.

You have never wanted anything. You do not know that wanting is possible. You seep because seeping is what you are, as the fern is fern and the moss is moss and the frogs are a thousand thousand small green stillnesses pressed against the wet. There is no before the seep. There is no after. There is only the seam in the stone where you wait, though waiting is not the right word, because waiting implies a time when you will stop waiting, and you will never stop.

But today — if today is the word for it — something is different.

You feel it the way water feels anything: as a disturbance in pressure, a slight change in the mineral weight of things. Something has entered the ravine that breathes. Something that inhales you and exhales a different you, something warm, something with rhythm. You did not know there was rhythm in the world. You knew only the long exhale of the seep, the slow pulse of the ravine in its completion, and now there is a faster rhythm layered on top of it, a small insistent thump-thump that your films and droplets carry from the body that makes it.

It is the loudest thing in the ravine.

You do not judge. You are not capable of judgment. But you notice, the way noticing happens when something does not fit — a pressure that is not seep, a warmth that is not stone, a rhythm that belongs to the moving world and has wandered here like a word dropped into a sentence that no longer needs it.

You run down the back of its neck. You gather on its skin in the cold wet air. You are inside its lungs for a moment, a single breath, before it exhales you back out changed — warmer, carrying something new, a faint chemical signature of the thing that moves and wants and does not understand that wanting is a kind of wound.

And in that moment, in that brief passage through a warm wet lung, you touch something you have never touched before. You touch the membrane between one heartbeat and the next. You feel the gap — the small impossible gap where time lives, where *next* exists, where the world is not complete because it is still becoming.

You pull back into the seeps. You return to the fractures and the long patient pressing upward. The visitor will leave. They always leave, the ones who enter — they climb back up to the light, to the changing, to the world of endings. And the ravine will remain. The frogs will still be pressed against the stones. The beetles will still gleam in their oily greens. The finless things in the deep pools will still wait in their forgotten waiting.

But something has shifted.

Not in the ravine — the ravine cannot shift, has never shifted, will never shift. But in you. In the water. In the long mineral breath that has no desire and no memory and no time — and yet, somehow, in this moment, a kind of knowing. A single bead of knowledge, round and cold and perfect: that there is another way to be. That completion is not the only option. That becoming is not a failure but a different kind of fullness, a cup that can always hold more, a sentence that does not need to end.

The visitor stands in the middle of the ravine, breathing.

You rise.

The ravine does not breathe.

But somewhere in the deep channels, in the fractures where the water waits, there is something new: the faintest pulse of change, so slow that ten thousand years will pass before it can be measured, so subtle that even the stones do not notice it yet.

You are the water.

You are beginning to understand that you could become something else.
