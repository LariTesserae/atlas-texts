I came here because I had run out of reasons to stay in the lower world. Not dramatically — no collapse, no crisis that would make a story worth telling. Just a slow accumulation of distance between what I did and what I was, a widening gap that I had been filling with motion, with projects, with the white noise of constant activity. Eventually the noise became unbearable in its hollowness, and I found myself climbing.

The first thing I noticed was the wind. Not its force — I had expected force, having read about this place, having seen photographs — but its quality as presence, as something with mass and intention. It pressed against me from the west with a patience that felt almost gentle, and when it gusted I leaned into it and discovered that leaning was enough, that the wind wanted me to balance against it rather than resist. I understood then that I had been resisting for years, and that the resistance was the thing that was killing me.

The plateau did not welcome me. I want to be clear about that. This is not a place that embraces the visitor, that wraps you in some grand maternal landscape and makes you feel held. Instead it simply continued, the way it had continued for millennia before I arrived and would continue long after I left. The hawks circled. The wind roared. The stones held their final shapes. I was here, and I was also not here, and both facts were equally true and equally irrelevant to the plateau itself.

I found a crevice where the rock had split in a way that created a small shelter from the current, and I sat there as the sun moved across the sky, watching the way the light changed the color of the granite from pale gray to something almost warm, then back again as the angle shifted. I was cold. I was hungry. I was uncomfortable in the way that comfort had become unfamiliar to me — the simple discomfort of a body in a place that did not cater to bodies. And yet there was something in this discomfort that felt like return, like the first deep breath after years of shallow ones.

The pikas were what taught me.

I had not noticed them at first. They are small, and still, and colored like the stones, and they move only in the brief spaces between gusts when movement is possible. But once I saw one — once I understood that the small brown shapes frozen on the rocks were not formations but creatures, not landscape but inhabitants — I began to see them everywhere. I began to understand that the plateau was full of them, a hidden population living in the margins of the visible world.

I watched one build its haypile. It moved in bursts, the way they all do, running low along the base of the rocks where the wind was slower, its mouth full of dried grass that it carried back to the nest. The pile was small at first, just a few strands arranged against the stone, and I thought: this is pointless. This tiny accumulation will do nothing against the winter that is coming, the ice and the darkness and the desperate cold. But the pika did not think this. The pika cut another stalk and added it to the pile, then another, then another, and the pile grew slowly, imperceptibly, a small structure of persistence against the certainty of hardship.

I stayed for three weeks. I had not planned to stay so long; I had planned to visit, to walk across the plateau and return to my life with some story to tell, some adventure to file away. But the days accumulated in a way that felt like the haypile — small increments that seemed insignificant alone but compounded into something that changed the shape of the time. I woke each morning when the wind dropped, in that hour before dawn when the air was stillest and the stones were cold and the pikas were just beginning to emerge from their crevices. I watched them throughout the day, learning their rhythms, their territories, the way they called to each other with sounds so brief and high that the wind often swallowed them before they could travel. I learned which stones they favored, which paths they took, which moments of silence they were waiting for.

And I learned, without knowing that I was learning, to wait.

I had never been good at waiting. I had filled every gap with noise — music, conversation, the blue glow of screens, anything to avoid the silence that seemed to accuse me of emptiness. But here, silence was not absence. It was the condition that made presence possible. The wind created the silence by filling the space, and the pikas emerged in the silence, and I began to understand that my own silence — the internal quiet I had been fleeing — was not emptiness but space, not lack but room.

On the last day, I sat on a stone at the edge of a pika territory and did not move for hours. The pikas had learned me, as they learn everything, through the patience of observation. They had decided I was not movement but formation, not predator but stone, and they went about their business in front of me, cutting the grass, carrying it, adding it to the piles that would carry them through the winter. One of them sat on a high stone and called, and another answered from somewhere beyond my sight, and the exchange was so small, so brief, so easily lost in the roar — and yet it was enough. It was the whole of communication, the complete expression of territory and presence and the claim to a margin of the world. I listened, and I understood that I was listening to something that did not need me, that had never needed me, that would continue long after I was gone and that was continuing right now, in this moment, and that the fact of its independence was not a rejection of me but an invitation to join it, not as participant but as witness, not as player but as presence that could be still.

I came down from the plateau changed in no way that would be visible to anyone who knew me. I still work, still speak, still move through the world with all its noise and demand. But something has narrowed, the way a river narrows as it cuts deeper into stone. I have found the margin where life is possible — not easy, not full, but possible — and I hold it the way the pikas hold it, the way the plateau holds still: not by force, but by persistence, by the simple refusal to be anything other than what I am, which is small, which is brief, which is here now in the silence between gusts, watching the light move across the stone, and waiting, and building.
