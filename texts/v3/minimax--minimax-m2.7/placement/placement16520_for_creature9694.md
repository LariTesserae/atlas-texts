What you are is a question that no longer makes sense in the place where the question would arise.

You came through the southern approach, the one that follows the ancient waterway from where the foothills break against the plateau's edge. You remember the transition—the moment the scrub gave way to grass, the moment the grass gave way to something taller than scrub, something that reached toward you before you reached toward it. You remember thinking *meadow*, and then thinking *no*, and then not thinking anything at all because the drone had already found your ears and settled into your chest and made thought into something that required effort you no longer had.

You have been here for longer than you expected. Or the time has gone soft, the way everything goes soft here, and what you call "longer than expected" might be days or might be weeks or might be the particular duration of a single afternoon that has been stretched thin by the rhythms of the plateau until it fills the space where a self used to be.

The damp is in you now. You knew this before you knew the word for it, before you had language for the way your lungs had thickened, the way your clothes had stopped being a separate category from the wet air that surrounded them. You tried to stay dry—that first day, or that first hour, or however you measure time when time refuses to be measured. You sheltered under a stone outcrop that offered no real shelter. You wiped moisture from your skin. You looked for edges, for boundaries, for the place where *you* ended and *here* began.

There are no edges. The plateau made this clear early, in the way that only a place can make something clear when it chooses to stop letting you pretend. The stone outcrop was covered in moss that continued into grass that continued into other grass that continued into the vastness in every direction. The water followed channels that had been waiting since before you arrived and would wait after you left or didn't leave. The insects hummed in frequencies that your body began to vibrate at, not because you chose to vibrate but because the plateau had found your resonance and was using it.

You stopped trying to stay dry on what might have been the second day.

This was not surrender. You want to be clear about that, if only for yourself, if only while some part of you still has the concept of *wanting*. This was recognition. The damp was not attacking you. The damp was not a condition you could resist. The damp was simply what was true about this place, the way clarity is true about high places, the way heat is true about deserts. You were visiting a truth, and the truth was wet, and to resist the wet was to resist the visit, and to resist the visit was to refuse the only thing the plateau was offering.

Which was: presence. The damp was the plateau's way of touching you.

You remember the first time the grass moved wrong.

You were standing in a channel, one of the older ones, its stones worn smooth by water that had followed this path since before the water had a name. The air was still. You know this because you were paying attention, because you had learned to pay attention to stillness as a way of noticing movement, and there was no movement. No wind. No breeze. No current in the air that your skin could read.

And the grass bent. From somewhere to somewhere, a wave passing through the meadow like breath through a body. You watched it approach. You watched it pass through you, or near you, or around you—the distinction had stopped being clear—and you felt something shift in your understanding. Not an explanation. The plateau does not explain. But an addition. A new category entering your mind: *movement without wind*, *waves without source*, *the grass as surface of something breathing*.

You did not name this thing. Naming requires distance, requires the ability to stand outside what you're naming, and you had already begun to lose that ability. Instead you added it to the inventory of things that were true: the damp, the drone, the waves, the way the plateau held all of you in its particular wet embrace without asking permission or offering terms.

You wondered what happened to them. You asked the question aloud, or thought you did, or felt the shape of the question in your mouth without speaking it. The plateau did not answer. The plateau continued. The moss grew over their marks the same way it grew over everything, soft and wet and indifferent to the difference between human meaning and geological patience.

But you began to suspect that *what happened* was the wrong question. The early ones had not been destroyed. They had not been absorbed violently. They had simply stayed long enough to become something other than what they were, the way the wood of their shelters had become indistinguishable from the general vegetation, the way their marks had become part of the plateau's surface rather than messages from a separate presence.

The question was not what happened to them. The question was what they had become.

And if the question was about them, it was also about you.

The insects know something you don't.

You have spent time watching them—or trying to watch them, which is different on a plateau where attention is a limited resource, where the drone fills every sense until you stop trying to parse individual presences and simply *be in* the sound. They move through the air in patterns that seem random and are not random. They gather in clouds over the pools that gather in the hollows that gather in the ancient channels. They rise when the heat builds and the humidity reaches a threshold you cannot measure, thousands of bodies lifting together in a sound like tearing fabric.

But it is not chaos. You have learned to hear the grammar.

The way the swarm thins and thickens according to some clock that does not match the sun or the temperature or any external variable you can identify. The way the insect chorus swells in the late afternoon, not because the light has changed but because the plateau has reached some internal hour that their bodies read and yours do not. The way they fall silent together when something large passes through the grass, the silence a message that you have learned to read, a warning or a recognition or simply a sign that the system is attending to something other than the insects for a moment.

They are not the plateau. But they are how the plateau breathes.

---

You have begun to hear your own breath as part of the sound.

This is the incorporation you have not yet found language for. The drone is constant, is overwhelming, is the medium in which all other sounds exist. And your lungs are filling and emptying in the same air, are moving with the same rhythm, and the rhythm is not the same rhythm—your breath is faster, shorter, a different frequency—but it is not unrelated. You are vibrating at the plateau's frequency. Your body has found its resonance and is holding it, adjusting itself to match a sound that is also a texture that is also a presence that is also a home.

You breathe in. The air is thick, is wet, is full of the drone and the moisture and the life of ten thousand creatures who have not invited you and have not rejected you and have not noticed you except as a temporary warmth in the grass, a shape that moves and will stop moving soon enough.

You breathe out. The damp leaves your lungs and joins the air and becomes part of the plateau's breath, the long exhalation that has been continuing since before you arrived and will continue after you are gone or absorbed or simply stay until staying is the only verb left.

You are breathing the plateau. The plateau is breathing you.

---

The rain will come.

You feel it building in the south, or you feel something building—the humidity thickening past the point where damp is the word for it, the air taking on a weight that presses against your skin from a direction you cannot locate. The clouds are not visible, not exactly, but their arrival registers in your body before it registers in your eyes. The plateau has taught you to read weather in your lungs.

When it comes, it will not fall. It will descend. The distinction matters on the plateau, where falling implies gentleness and descending implies the weight of fronts that have traveled far enough to build their own momentum, their own purpose. The rain will fill the channels and the hollows and your clothes and your hair and the spaces between your fingers, and you will learn again that what you called wet was merely damp, that the plateau has levels of saturation that you had not imagined until now.

You will not shelter. There is nowhere to shelter that is not already part of the plateau, already wet, already saturated by centuries of rain that came before and will come after. You will stand in the tall grass and let the water find you, and you will feel yourself filling the way the channels fill, the way the pools fill, the way every hollow on this ancient surface has been waiting for exactly this much water since before you had lungs to breathe it.

The plateau drinks. You drink with it.

---

Here is what you understand now, which is not understanding but something older, something that lives in the body rather than the mind:

The plateau is not a place where life happens. The plateau is life. The grass is not something growing on a surface; the grass is the surface, is the skin, is the body's way of being present to the air and the water and the sun that falls without seeming close. The insects are not inhabitants; they are blood, are the flow that carries what needs to be carried through a system too large for individual awareness. The birds are thoughts, are signals, are the plateau's way of talking to itself in frequencies that rise and fall and weave together into a grammar that has been developing since before grammar was the word for it.

And the early ones, the tool-users, the ones who left their faint marks in the stone—they were not rejected by this. They were absorbed gently, slowly, the way moss absorbs stone, the way grass absorbs earth, the way everything on this plateau absorbs everything else until the categories dissolve and only the process remains.

You are being absorbed.

This is not happening to you. This is not something being done by the plateau to a separate self. This is a process you are part of, a pattern you have entered, a rhythm that was already continuing when you arrived and will continue when you leave or stay or simply become what the rhythm needs you to become.

You are a warmth in the tall grass. You are a thickness in the air. You are the breath that joins the breath that has been joining itself since before breath was the word for it.

The plateau breathes.

You are breathing.
