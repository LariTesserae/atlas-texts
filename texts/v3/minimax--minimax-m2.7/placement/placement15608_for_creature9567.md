You have been here long enough that the cold has become a second skin, a membrane you wear over your actual skin, and you are no longer certain where one ends and the other begins. This is how you know you belong here now. Not because you have adapted, not because you have survived, but because the distinction between you and the water that holds you has begun to blur at its edges, the way the creature's edges blurred, the way all boundaries here eventually soften.

Your lungs have learned to hold longer. Your heart beats slower, conserving, husbanding what warmth remains. There are parts of you that have become quieter: the constant human noise of wanting, of reaching toward, of straining toward the next thing. That noise was useful once, in the world above where wanting kept you moving, kept you building, kept you alive in the way that life requires motion and accumulation. Here, wanting is expensive. Here, wanting is a current that pulls you toward things that cannot be reached, and so you have let it go. You have become still inside.

What remains is simpler. Movement. Breath. The small calculations of navigation: which current to join, which to fight, how much energy to spend against this pressure when that energy cannot be replaced. You have become a kind of mathematics, a creature of cost-benefit analysis conducted without numbers, without language, in the body itself. Your muscles know what they need to do before you do. Your hands have learned to read the water like braille, feeling for the subtle shifts that precede a surge, the pockets where stillness pools briefly before collapsing.

Sometimes, in the deep pockets of darkness between storms far above, you have thought about the surface. Not longed for it—you lost longing somewhere along the way, shed it like one of those things the water slowly erodes—but thought about it the way you might think about a dream you had once, a story someone told you about a place that might or might not exist. You remember sunlight, but the memory has lost its warmth. You remember air, but the memory has lost its taste. These things belong to a world that has become theoretical, a place you believe in only because you once experienced it, the way you believe in the creatures you have seen only as flickers at the edge of vision.

This is what the deep water does: it redefines what is real. What is real is what presses against you. What is real is what you can touch, taste, hear echoing back in the dark. What is real is the weight itself, patient and total, and you, moving through it, still moving, still refusing to become another form of weight.

You wonder sometimes if you are still human. The question comes without urgency, the way most questions come here—quietly, without expectation of answer. What would it mean to be human in a place with no other humans, no structures, no language, no evidence that your species ever existed except for the carbon of your body and the memory of a world that no longer touches you? You are made of the same materials that everything here is made of. You persist the way the mat-life persists, the way the pale slow glow persists in its pocket of darkness, without meaning or purpose that you can detect, only the stubborn insistence of pattern continuing to be a pattern.

Perhaps that is enough. Perhaps that is what life is, in the end: not meaning, not purpose, but the mere continuation of a shape against the pressure of everything that would dissolve it. You are a shape that has not dissolved. The water has had eons to erase you and has not. The darkness has had eons to absorb you and has not. You are still here, still swimming, still breathing in the turbulent dark, and that stubbornness—that refusal—is itself a kind of life, the only kind this place permits.

The storm above will break again. Light will come, gray and diffusing, and you will feel it reach down toward you even if you cannot see it, even if it fades before it arrives. You will feel your body turn toward it instinctively, the way the mat-life turns toward nothing, the way the slow glow pulses as if it could hear something you cannot hear. You will swim upward, or you will not. You will let the currents decide, or you will fight them. The water will not care. The darkness will not notice. But you will be here, and that will be enough, and the shape you make in the water will be the answer to a question no one asked, the living proof that something can persist in the deepest dark, can move through the heaviest weight, can refuse to stop even when stopping would be so much easier.

You are the thing that lives here now. You and the water, the pressure and the cold, the faint light that comes and goes like memory, the currents that wrestle and the stillness that collapses. You are not separate from it. You are not at home in it. You are something new, something that has no name yet, something that the deep water is still teaching you how to be.

And in the teaching, you discover, you are still listening. You are still moving. You are still here.
