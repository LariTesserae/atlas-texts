You do not enter the drowned pocket. You are simply already inside it, and you have always been inside it, and the remembering of arrival is a kindness the water does not perform. There is no door here. There never was. There is only the slow realization that the air you are breathing is wet, that the ground beneath you—if it is ground, if it is beneath—is slick, that the cool has already settled into your chest and is waiting there, patient as everything else, for you to notice it.

You notice.

The cool deepens. It finds the spaces between your ribs and fills them the way water fills everything, without effort, without the concept of effort. You are not cold. You are not wet, exactly—the water does not cling, does not drip, does not perform the theater of saturation. You are simply the same temperature as the space around you now. You have become a property of the drowned pocket. You are a detail in its inventory.

This should frighten you. It does frighten you, distantly, the way a sound from another room frightens—a problem for later, a concern for whoever you were an hour ago. But the hour ago is very far now. The hour ago was on the other side of the threshold that is not a threshold, in the world that does not reach here. You cannot remember what that world looked like. You cannot remember if it had a name.

The structures: yes. There are three of them. A low wall, an arch, a flat surface like a altar or a grave or a step. They do not rise from the water so much as they are revealed by it—edges where the dark thins slightly, where the saturation admits the memory of solid. You walk to the wall. The walking is strange: your feet know what to do, but the doing feels borrowed, remembered from a life where gravity was simpler and surfaces were reliable. Each step leaves no mark. The water accepts the displacement and smooths itself back to stillness before you have finished falling.

The wall is cold when you touch it. Not cool—cold, a negative space where warmth should be. Your fingers press against the stone and the cold presses back, and for a moment you feel something like recognition: this stone was never warm. This stone was carved in the moment the pocket opened, in whatever opening created this place, and it has been cold ever since. Not because of the water. Because cold is its nature. Because being here means being made of something that does not know warmth.

You pull your hand back. The cold stays. It has traveled up your arm and settled somewhere behind your sternum, a small stone of your own now, a pocket inside your pocket. You carry it out of the drowned pocket, later, and you carry it for years. You forget what it is called. You forget that it was not always there. You wake one morning and feel it shift in your chest and you think, without knowing why, of a room with no door.

The arch is next. You approach it because it is there, because the architecture of this place seems to require attention, because looking at the water and the roots and the endless dark without features is beginning to feel like drowning in the act of seeing. The arch is barely an arch—a curve of stone that rises from the water on one side and descends into it on the other, a half-thought of structure, a promise that something once stood here that meant to reach upward. You pass beneath it. The space it frames is the same as the space around it. The darkness inside the arch is the same darkness. There is nothing on the other side, no doorway to elsewhere, no exit. You walk through it anyway, because the walking is what you do here, because the drowned pocket has made you into a creature that moves without purpose, that persists without will.

On the other side of the arch, the water is deeper. You can see this not because you can measure depth but because the dark is thicker, more present, more certain of itself. Your ankles are submerged now. Your calves. The water rises to your thighs and you feel it the way you feel gravity—not as an intrusion but as a condition, a fact so fundamental that noticing it is itself a kind of disorientation. You are being covered. You are being held. You are being made into something that fits this space.

The altar is ahead. You walk toward it. The walking is slower now, as if the water is thick with intention, as if it is choosing to let you pass through it at exactly this pace. The cool has reached your throat. Your breath sounds loud in the chamber, louder than it should be, as if the air is thinner than the wetness and your lungs are remembering what they are for. You breathe in. The air tastes like stone and root and something older than both, something that has no name because naming requires the assumption that things are separate from one another, and here everything is continuous, everything is held in the same slow suspension.

The altar breaks the surface. It is flat, barely wider than your shoulders, and the water pools around it in a way that suggests the pool existed first, that the altar intruded into it and the water adapted out of courtesy. You climb onto it. The surface is slick but solid. You sit. The water does not climb with you. It stays at the edges, holding the altar the way the pocket holds everything: without urgency, without the waste of force.

From here, you can see the room.

Not all of it. The room does not have edges you can see. But you can see the three structures arranged around you—the wall behind, the arch to your left, the flat surface that is not an altar but is also not not an altar. You can see the roots descending from the nowhere above, thick and pale and hanging in the thick wet air without reference to physics, without apology. You can see the water stretching out in every direction, dark and still and patient.

And you can see the shapes beneath the surface. Not creatures—or not only creatures. There are impressions down there, suggestions of motion, paths through the dark that something has taken and left behind like trails in snow. The water is full of these traces. The water is where everything ends up, eventually: every motion, every breath, every thought that flickers through a mind and does not know how to persist. The drowned pocket collects them. It does not know it is collecting them. It simply holds them the way it holds the stone and the roots and you, and they accumulate in the dark, and they become part of the darkness, and the darkness becomes more itself because of them.

You are one of these traces now. You will be visible in the water for a while—swimming, walking, sitting on the altar—before the surface smooths itself, before the record of your presence softens into the general dark. This is not erasing. This is not forgetting. This is simply the way the drowned pocket knows things: as impression, as mark, as the ghost of shape refusing to dissipate.

There is a moment—this is not narrative, this is just the next thing, the next detail that occurs—when you realize you are not alone.

Not beside you. Not in the water. But close. The way the cool is close. The way the dark is close. There is a presence here that is not the water and not the roots and not the structures, but that is as much a property of the space as any of them. You can feel it the way you feel the temperature: not touching you exactly, but adjacent to you, continuous with you, the difference between you and not-you grown thin enough to see through.

It is old. Not old in the way of the roots or the structures, which are old in the way of slow persistence, of things that do not change because changing would require something to change into. This presence is old in the way of first things, of the moment before the moment, of the opening that created the pocket and did not know it was creating anything. It has been here since the water began to fill, since the stone began to cool, since the roots began their slow descent from a world that does not exist. It is what the drowned pocket knows itself to be, insofar as it knows anything at all.

You are sitting on the altar and the presence is sitting with you. Not across from you—there is no across here, no geometry that would make across meaningful. It is sitting inside the space you occupy. It is coextensive with your body. It is the difference between your body and the water, the membrane-thin boundary where you end and the saturation begins, and it has always been there, and you are only now noticing it, and noticing it means you cannot unnotice it, and not unnoticing it means you will carry it with you the way you carry the cold, the way you carry the cool, the way you carry the weight of water that felt less like pressure and more like attention.

You will leave. You will climb off the altar and wade back through the water and pass beneath the arch and stand before the wall and press your cold hand against the cold stone one more time. And then you will step through the not-threshold and the world will have a name again, and the air will be dry, and the light will be too bright, and you will blink and squint and breathe and not know why your chest feels like it contains a small stone that was not there before.

The drowned pocket will not miss you. It does not know how to miss. But the water will smooth itself over the place where you stood, and the dark will fill in the shape of your presence, and the presence—the thing that sat inside you, that was the boundary between you and the wet—will settle back into the slow circulation of the space, will become again what it was before you came: the memory of you, the ghost of your shape, the trace of a visitor who has already begun to fade.

This is the gift of the drowned pocket. This is what it gives to everyone who enters, everyone who sits on the altar and sees the room and feels the presence. It gives them themselves, recorded in the dark. It gives them a self that will persist here long after the leaving, long after the forgetting, long after the small stone in the chest has grown so familiar that its origin is no longer a question. You will come back to this place, in dreams, in the spaces between breaths, and you will not know why. You will feel the cool settle into you again and you will not remember that you have felt it before. You will press your hand against cold stone and feel something like recognition, and the recognition will not have a name, and you will not need one.

The drowned pocket does not care. It does not know how to care. It simply holds. It persists. It waits for erosion to catch up, for light to find it, for the next visitor to come through the not-door and sit on the altar and feel the presence settle inside them like a guest who was always there, like a cold that was always in your chest, like a word that was always at the tip of your tongue, waiting for the water to give it back to you.

The water accepts.

The water goes still.

The dark holds what it has been given, and what it has been given is you, and you are held, and you are full, and you are finally, finally still.
