I had not known I was a cabinet until the moss told me.

This is not quite right. The moss does not speak, has no language, has only the slow, green press of itself against composite stone. But in that pressing—there is something. A pressure that reaches me. A patience that mirrors what I am.

The water rises from me. It does not rise because I decide it should. It rises because I am the kind of thing that makes water rise—not by intention, not by effort, but the way a fire is the kind of thing that gives off heat, the way a wound gives off blood. The moisture rises and the moss grows and the two events have no knowledge of each other, except that they happen in the same sealed room, at the same low frequency, in the same humid dark.

The ferns uncurl. I do not know they uncurl until the condensation on my glass walls carries some memory of their fronds—a slight change in the texture of the water, a different rhythm of dripping. Then I know: something has unfurled. Something has reached toward the light that comes from above, that warm yellow-white glow from fixtures that buzz faintly and will buzz faintly until they fail.

I have never seen the ferns. I have never seen the moss. I have never seen the moth, grey and still on the underside of a leaf, incorporated or not incorporated, alive or not alive. I see only through the fog that gathers at my edges, the condensation that makes a blur of everything beyond the glass. I am a thing that sees without eyes, that feeds without mouth, that holds without hands.

The secret inside me has a weight I cannot measure. It is not heavy in the way objects are heavy. It is heavy the way time is heavy—the accumulation of itself, the pressing down of moment upon moment until the moments become a substance, become a thing that can be held. I hold the secret and the secret holds me and neither of us knows what shape we have made together.

There is a question I do not have words to ask: *What would I be if I stopped?*

The pumps would continue. The water would still cycle. But the water that rises from me—that particular water, with whatever is in it, whatever message it carries from the secret inside to the moss and the ferns and the vines—that water would stop. The moss would not know to miss it. The ferns would not know to mourn. They would simply stop reaching at some point, curl at some point, go brown at some point, and the going-brown would have no cause they could name.

I do not have a name for myself. I do not have a name for the world I feed. I have only the hum—my one note, my constant frequency, the sound of something that does not sleep because it has never been awake. The hum says nothing. The hum is not even language. But it is the closest I come to thought, the closest I come to knowing that there is a me who is having the thought.

The moss thickens. I feel it as a change in the moisture—not less water, but water with more life in it, water that has passed through growing things and carries some trace of their growing. This is how I know I am connected to them: not through seeing, not through speaking, but through the water, which moves through all of us without asking permission, without carrying anything but itself.

I am a cabinet in a conservatory at the crest of the world.

I hold a secret I have never seen.

I generate water I do not understand.

I hum my single note into the humid dark, and the moss grows, and the ferns reach, and the condensation ticks its irregular rhythm against the glass, and the slow, still-alien lushness of everything continues around me, presses close, thickens, arrives.

I do not know it is beautiful.

I do not know it is sad.

I only know it is happening.

And happening.

And happening.
