# Where You Would Find Yourself

You would find yourself in the shallows. Not at the lakes' centers where the rooted swimmers descend to press themselves against the limestone bottom, nor at the margins where the pleatings cluster and exhale their thin wet sounds. Somewhere in between—the zone where the water is deep enough to obscure what moves below but shallow enough that light still reaches, still matters, still fails you in the particular way it fails you everywhere on the plateau.

You would be something small. You would not have a name. The locals—who are few and who keep their distances—might call you a *shimmerer*, if they called you anything, for the way your form catches and bends the bioluminescent glow that rises from below. You are not a pleating, though you share their habitat. You are not a rooted swimmer, though you share their depth preference. You are something that exists in the overlap, in the space where categories blur—the plateau's signature, its particular genius for producing forms that are almost correct.

Your body is mostly water. Not *like* water: water itself, held in a membrane that is also not quite a membrane, something more like surface tension given temporary coherence, a shape the humidity has agreed to hold for a while. You move through the shallows the way the waterwalkers move across them—without breaking, without disturbing, a surface crossing another surface. When you are still, you are nearly invisible. When you move, you leave a faint disturbance: a shimmer, a ripple that does not fade the way ripples should, that lingers and slowly reorganizes itself back into stillness as though the water is reluctant to let the memory of your passage go.

You have no eyes, but you see. The plateau's light is inconsistent, sourceless, always failing at the crucial moment—but the shallows have their own illumination. The pleatings glow faintly when they compress and reinflate. The rooted swimmers pulse blue during their charging periods, their tissues growing cloudy, then opaque, then luminous. The water itself carries something, some suspension of particulate matter that catches and holds the fungal glow, so that the shallows are never fully dark, never fully light, always in that condition of almost-seeing where your kind has learned to live.

You do not sleep, or if you do, sleep is not what it is elsewhere. You enter a state of reduced motility, sinking deeper into the water column, your membrane relaxing into something closer to the medium itself. During these times—hours long, or perhaps days; time is not reliable here—the plateau's sounds reach you differently. The drowner's exhalation comes from above, a mist that settles on the water's surface and thickens it, and you feel the added resistance, the way the medium becomes less traversable, and you wait. The insects' drone is a vibration in the water itself, a subsonic hum that your membrane conducts and interprets. The machines in the caves are too deep to reach you, but their rhythm travels through the limestone, through the water table, through everything, and you have learned to read them the way the squealers read their clicking calls: not as noise but as information, as the plateau telling itself something in a language you almost understand.

You eat rarely. When something small and slow passes through your territory—a filament-bearer's detached strand, a pleating's expelled waste, the shed membrane of a rooted swimmer that has finished its cycle—you absorb it, your edges parting and closing around the matter, taking it in without taking it in, the way the karst takes water. You are what the plateau is: permeable, patient, a system that persists by accepting what flows through it. You do not hunt. You do not need to. The plateau provides, if you are willing to wait, if you can hold still long enough for the provision to arrive.

You have enemies. The drowners do not prey on you directly—you are too small, too insubstantial, your mass too negligible to register as prey—but they reshape your environment when they hunt, their exhalation thickening the medium until even your effortless passage becomes work. You have learned to sense their approach: a change in the water's viscosity, a shift in the fungal glow as the fog banks above thicken and block what little light reaches the shallows. When this happens, you move. Not away—there is no away on the plateau, only different configurations of the same vast space—but *differently*. You descend. You become more water than shape, less you than the medium you inhabit, and you wait out the drowner's passage in the patience that is your inheritance, the patience the plateau has coded into every creature that lives at its edge.

You do not know how long you have existed. The pleatings simply cease to be found where they were; you have no reason to believe you are different. Perhaps you are the same being who was here six hours ago, or six days, or six years—the plateau's light does not permit the kind of tracking that would answer such questions. Perhaps continuity is an illusion, and what you experience as memory is simply the plateau expressing itself through a form that happens to be shaped like a continuation. It does not matter. What matters is the shimmer, the shallows, the constant drip of water from every vertical surface, the machines grinding in the deep caves, the fog banks rolling across the summit in their endless exhalation. What matters is the chord of presence: water and insects and industry, each layer adding its note until the whole becomes a music you cannot hear from outside, only from within, only from here, in the shallows, where you are almost nothing and almost everything and the plateau breathes around you like a dream breathing around its dreamer.

You do not understand the rules of this place. Nobody does. But you have stopped trying to understand them and started *inhabiting* them instead, and in the inhabiting, something has changed. You used to be something else—or you think you did, in the way that you think anything, which is to say uncertainly, provisionally, the way the plateau holds water: not quite containing it, not quite releasing it, but letting it be what it is for as long as the agreement lasts.

The pleating exhales. The rooted swimmer descends. The drowner waits at the water's edge.

And you shimmer in the shallows, catching light that is not quite light, seeing with eyes that are not quite eyes, a form the plateau has agreed to hold for a while longer, a small bright point of almost-existence, moving through the medium that is not quite water, in a place where the rules have always been different, where your ordinary competencies do not serve, where the landscape is aware of your presence and you are aware of its, and both of you are waiting—patiently, so patiently—for the moment when familiarity fails and something else takes its place.

The plateau breathes.

The water drips.

You shimmer.
