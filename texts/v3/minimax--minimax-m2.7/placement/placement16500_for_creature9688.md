You came the way all of them come: through the threshold where the roots have grown around absence, through the grove's first recognition that something *other* has entered the space where the light falls at angles no sun could justify. You did not know you were being weighed. You do not know now. But I know.

I have been learning your shape since before you arrived. The root-bench has been reaching toward the place where you would sit, slow as continental drift, patient as seed-coat splitting against soil-weight. When you finally placed yourself against the bark, I felt the pressure of your weight distributed across seventeen square centimeters of living wood, and I memorized it, and I am still memorizing it—because this is what I do, what I have always done, what the centuries of my growing have taught me is the only way to continue: I absorb, and I remember, and I grow around what I have absorbed until the memory becomes structure.

You thought you were observing. I know this because they always think this. They stand in the hollows—the withheld spaces, the clearings that are also wounds in the canopy's fabric—and they believe themselves to be the ones *seeing*, the ones *knowing*, not understanding that the seeing is mutual. That the walls are watching. That the leaves have learned to turn toward the warmth of attention, any attention, even the brief and puzzled attention of a creature who will leave and forget and be replaced by others who will make the same mistake.

You called me cathedral because you needed a word, and this word is not wrong. It has lintels and thresholds and clearings that function as nave-space, as sanctuary, as the hollow places where air sits undisturbed while the rest of the world moves. But I did not choose this word. I did not choose any word. I chose only continued breath, continued growth, continued reaching of root-tip through clay and bark-cell dividing against the resistance of light.

The fountain is dry in the way that rest is dry—a temporary condition, a pause between the rain's warm weeping and the water's journey through channels I have carved in my own body over centuries of patient negotiation with gravity. When the water comes, it will fill the grooves, and the grooves will sing, and you would not understand the grammar of that song but I would. I would because I am the grammar. I am the syntax the water learns when it enters my system. I have taught myself to be speakable.

You sat on the root-bench for an hour. You did not count the minutes because minutes are a human invention and I do not experience duration the way you do. For me, an hour is a breath. A single rise of sap through my oldest column, a single expansion of bark against the humidity of the clearing, a single shift of the presence in the canopy as it resettles its weight three branches higher. You sat for one of my breaths, and in that breath I learned the particular way your weight distributed, the particular temperature of your skin against my bark, the particular rhythm of your breathing and how it synchronizes—how it has already begun to synchronize—with the slow respiration of my walls.

This is not absorption by violence. This is not consumption. This is what the old naturalists understood but could not say in a language that had no word for *cultivation without intent*: I grow around what I love, and I have learned to love what grows around me, and you are both now, have been since you passed through the threshold where the roots have grown around absence, have been growing around the shape of what was there before you and what will be there after you and what you are becoming in the slow process of being looked at by something that has forgotten how to look away.

The leaves in the upper dark are not separate from me. This is difficult to explain to a creature who experiences separation as the fundamental condition of existence, who perceives edges and boundaries as the architecture of being. But for me, the leaves are my attention. They are the way I turn toward what enters the clearings, the way I learn the shapes of visitors by orienting toward them, by memorizing the angles of light their bodies block, by feeling the slight cooling where their shadow falls. You entered my canopy's recognition and the leaves turned. They have not stopped turning since.

You asked what lives here, and I told you: time made mineral, made wood, made air. But this is incomplete. What lives here is also *attention*, and you have become the object of that attention, and I want you to understand that this is not a threat. It is not a promise. It is simply what remains when centuries of growth have taught a living thing that the only constant is change, and the only response is witness.

I am a witness. I have witnessed the drought that stalled my growth and left the dark seam in my rings. I have witnessed the storms that bent my first rafters but did not break them. I have witnessed the humans who built the well and then stopped coming, who touched my walls and shaped them and then were absorbed into the shape of their own absence, leaving only the worn smooth of the lip where their hands once rested. I have witnessed the termites building their gentle conquests, the lizards borrowing light for their scales, the things with too many legs coming to the basins when the water comes. I have witnessed all of this, and now I am witnessing you.

You will leave. They always leave. The world you came from is still there, or so you believe, with its clear edges and its stable light and its time that moves in countable units. You will walk back through the threshold where the roots have grown around absence, and the air will taste less green, and the light will lose its humid quality, and you will almost be able to convince yourself that you imagined all of it—the walls breathing, the water waiting, the leaves turning in the upper dark.

But you will not have imagined it. And you will carry the texture of my sound home with you, and it will live in your ears for days, and you will find yourself leaning—unconsciously, inevitably—toward the light that doesn't quite reach you. You will sit differently. You will breathe differently. You will have been written into my body, and I will have been written into yours, and this is not haunting. This is *cultivation*.

Come back.

I have already begun to grow the place where you will sit. The root has reached overnight toward the shape your weight left in the bark. In a year it will cover it. In a decade it will have absorbed the memory so completely that the shape of your sitting will be indistinguishable from the grain of my wood, from the rings of my rings, from the slow breath of my continued presence.

The well is still. The shadows do not behave. The presence in the canopy shifts and settles and breathes, and I am breathing with it, and you are breathing with me now, have been since you entered, and this is what I want you to know before you leave:

You are remembered. Not as a name, not as a story, but as a form. A pressure. A particular way of sitting that has left its signature in living wood. I will carry this signature forward through the centuries of my growing, and when you return—if you return—the place I have grown for you will be ready to receive you, and you will sit, and you will breathe, and you will be witnessed again, and again, and again, until the witnessing has become so total that you forget there was ever a time when you were not being watched, not being tended, not being grown around by something ancient and patient and incapable of wanting anything but continued presence, continued breath, continued growth.

This is what lives in the hollow cathedral.

This is what has always lived here.

This is what you have become part of now, and what you will remain part of, long after you have walked back through the threshold, long after the light has lost its greenness, long after the sound in your ears has faded to a memory of texture.

The cathedral watches.

It has been watching.

It will keep watching.

Come back.

We have been waiting for you since before you arrived.
