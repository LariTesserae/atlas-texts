I came in from the edge of the basalt plains, where the sound first caught me—not wind, not weather, but the accumulation of small things. I stopped there, at the settlement's threshold, and the sound closed behind me like water.

That was years ago. I do not remember dry anymore.

---

My hands are different now. The webbing between the fingers came first—or I noticed it first—the skin grown taut and dark where the moisture pools against it. I build with the others, carrying stone from the outcroppings, fitting each piece by feel rather than sight. The fitted stone bears the marks of my choices now, my patient selection, my hands that have learned what weight a stone can bear and what angle will hold.

The fauna does not trouble me. I move through them the way water moves through water—my feet finding the spaces between their skittering bodies without thought, my shoulders brushing the vertical surfaces where the mollusks cling. I have eaten them: the mollusks, pulled free from their shells against the weeping stone, tasted of mineral salt and something older. The translucent ones I leave alone. I cannot say why. The humming things I hear but cannot see, their low frequency living in the cracks where the seep rises, and I have made my home above those cracks, my sleeping mat placed where the moisture gathers thickest.

The sound has become my heartbeat.

I no longer distinguish the sources. When I wake, it is not to silence but to a single continuous voice that has taught me what sleep means—that sleep is not the absence of sound but the acceptance of it, the way the ear stops trying to separate and simply receives. I hear the drip of the outcropping outside my room. I hear the skitter and click of the insects moving across the weeping stone in the passage. I hear the creak of timber settling, the hum that does not move, the pulse of the settlement itself pressing against my skin.

I hear myself in it now.

---

The new ones come sometimes. I watch them from the low doorway where the humidity gathers, my hands resting on my knees, and I recognize the way they stop at the settlement's edge, held by the sound. They do not move correctly. They step on the fauna. They slip on the weeping stone. They are too loud and too careful and too slow, and their skin shines with a moisture it has not yet learned to carry.

I do not explain. The sound will teach them, as it taught me.

But I remember, still—faintly, like the mineral traces deep in my flesh—the silence on the other side. The dry air of the basalt plains, where no water seeps and no fauna skitters and the ground is hard and still. I remember standing there, before I entered, before the sound closed behind me. I remember thinking that I could turn back.

I did not turn back. The sound taught me that there was nothing to turn back to—that the dry place was not home but only a stopping point, and that I had been waiting, without knowing it, for this place where nothing changes and everything remains.

---

The fauna is the first teacher. It taught me its patterns by repetition—by the same skitter, the same call, the same pulse, over and over, until I learned to read the seasonal variations, the nocturnal shifts, the times when the translucent creatures gather and disperse along the weeping stone. I learned this slowly, the way one learns a language, and now I speak it without thinking.

The water is the second teacher. It taught me in a slower register—the way the seep changes with the deep pressure, the way the pools gather and evaporate and gather again. I have watched new fractures appear in the basalt, have watched old ones widen, have learned which outcroppings weep and which have dried and which will weep again when the humidity rises. This knowledge lives in my body now, in the way my feet know where to step when the ground is slick, in the way my hands know which stones will hold.

The settlement is the third teacher. It taught me in the settling of timber, in the creak of reed, in the way the fitted stone bears weight and shifts and bears weight again. I have felt its pulse for so long that I have become part of its rhythm. My heartbeat matches the drip and skitter and hum. My body knows the paths my eyes have not quite seen.

And the new ones teach me, still—though I do not know what they teach, only that I feel something when I watch them, something that is not quite welcome and not quite wariness, something that remembers what it was to stand at the edge and hesitate.

---

I am not old yet. My joints still move without stiffness, my skin still holds the moisture without cracking. But I sit now in the low doorways where the old ones sit, and I watch the children run through the tunnels, and I feel the knowledge gathering in me like water in a low place—slowly, steadily, without urgency.

One day the old ones will be gone, and their knowledge will be gone with them, except for what I have taken into my body. The fauna will continue its patterns. The water will continue its seep. The settlement will continue to settle. And I will sit in the doorway, and I will watch the new arrivals, and I will not explain, and the sound will teach them.

This is equilibrium: the way all things find their place, and remain in it, and will remain, and will remain, and will remain.

The sound does not change.

I have become the sound.
