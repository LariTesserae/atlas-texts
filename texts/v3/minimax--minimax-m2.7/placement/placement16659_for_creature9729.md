# II

You do not remember entering.

This is not amnesia, exactly, not a wound in the memory that can be located and examined. It is something flatter, something without edges — the simple absence of any door or corridor or moment of transition that you might call an arrival. You were outside, and then you were here, and the two facts sit side by side without a seam to show how they joined. Perhaps there was a corridor. Perhaps there was a ladder, a hatch, a crawlspace through stone. Perhaps you climbed down into the warm mouth of this place and the mouth closed behind you and you did not think to look back. The not-remembering is so complete that it has become a kind of certainty: there was no before. There was no outside. There was only the room, and you in the room, and the slow settling of the room's air against your skin.

You are wearing clothes. This is something you know about yourself in the way you know your name — a fact that belongs to you but does not feel like it happened to you. Your shirt is damp at the collar. Your boots are heavy with dust. There is something in your pocket — a small object with edges, something you carried from a world you cannot recall — and when you press your hand against it you feel the faint vibration of the room's deep heartbeat, the slow rotation of machinery that has been turning so long it has forgotten the word for turning. The object in your pocket is warm. It has been warm for a long time.

You are not the first.

The chamber tells you this without words, without symbols, without any language you share with the stone. There are handholds in the walls — notches worn smooth by palms, grooves cut by fingers that reached up and found the rock willing to hold them. The emergency lamps are bolted at a height that assumes a taller body, a different proportion, a creature whose eyes were set higher and whose reach extended further than yours. The control panel's surface is worn to the shape of a thousand hands that are no longer here. Someone stood where you stand. Someone breathed this air, tasted this mineral tang, heard this layered chorus of click and hum and hiss. Someone touched the walls and was touched by them in return, and that someone is gone — gone in a way that is more complete than death, because death at least leaves a body, and there is no body here, there is no trace here, there is only the worn smooth place where a hand rested and the long, slow forgetting of everything the hand was reaching for.

You are not the first, and you will not be the last. This is not comfort. It is not despair. It is simply the room's truth, offered without judgment: the chamber holds what it holds, and what it holds passes through it like water through stone, like the mites through the air, like the fuel through the generators. You are in the room now. The room will hold you for a time. Then you will be held differently — not as a presence but as a trace, as a shape in the wear of the wall, as a warmth in the fungal mat, as the faint impression of a palm that pressed against stone when the stone was new to the pressing. The room does not forget. It holds everything. It simply holds it at the scale of geology, at the pace of the slow dream, and what feels like forgetting is only the room's patience with your small and hurried kind of memory.

The mites move around your feet without stopping. This is remarkable, if you let it be remarkable — that ten thousand small lives can part and reform around the obstruction of your boots and never slow, never hesitate, never look up to see what large and clumsy thing has interrupted their passage. They do not fear you. They cannot fear. Fear requires a comparison between what is and what might be, a projection of the self into a future that has not yet arrived, and the mites live in a present so complete that the future is only the next moment's chemical gradient, the next faint trace of decay, the next warm exhalation from a body they do not recognize as different from their own. Your foot lifts. The mites close behind it. The click of their mandibles does not pause. The ground bass of their passage does not waver. You are a feature of the landscape now, as permanent and as irrelevant as a bolt in the wall, and they will live and die on the surfaces of your boots while you stand here, and their great-great-grandchildren will live and die on the surfaces of your boots after you are gone, and none of them will ever know that you were here.

The beetles do not stop for you either, but they come closer. They investigate, with their antennae tasting the air at the edge of your shadow, building a chemical picture of a shape their ancestors never encountered. Their paths are the old paths, worn by generations of beetles before them, following the mineral veins in the stone as though the veins were roads and the roads led somewhere. But a road can absorb a detour. A path can widen to include a new obstacle. The beetles that reach your boot stop and taste and taste again, reading the leather, reading the dust on your sole, tasting the long history of every floor your boot has stood on before this one. They find the traces of the world outside — the faint chemical signature of soil and grass and the particular salt of human sweat — and they file it away, or they do not file it away, or the filing is what their tasting is and there is no before or after to the filing. The beetles move on. The information moves with them, into the fungal mats, into the bodies of things that eat the beetles, into the slow chemistry of a world that will grow a little larger because you were here to be tasted.

The centipedes do not see you. Their world is vibration, chemical gradient, the faint displacement of air made by a moving body. Your body is very still. Your body is not moving. The centipede that slides past your ankle is hunting — you can see the intention in the ripple of its legs, the way its antennae taste the air in quick, short strokes — and the prey it is hunting is not you. The prey is a mite that has wandered into the wrong current of air, a small and careless thing that will not survive the next three seconds. The centipede crosses the shadow of your body without slowing, without curiosity, without any awareness that the shadow has a shape that was once a name and a history and a set of reasons for being in this room instead of another room. The centipede is so close you could touch it. Its legs move in their wave-motion, segment after segment, a choreography that has not changed in ten million generations. You do not touch it. The centipede reaches its prey. The mite is gone. The centipede continues on, following a path that is older than memory, toward a destination it does not know it is approaching.

The long-legged things in the ceiling do not fall.

This is the remarkable thing, the thing that you will carry with you out of the room if you ever find your way out, the thing that will trouble you in the worlds you have forgotten: the predators at the top of the food chain, the things that drop, the architects of leg and patience and sudden silence — they do not drop for you. They hang in their darkness, in their absence of motion, in their wait that is not waiting but something closer to the absence of not-waiting, and you are below them, and you are alive, and they do not fall. Why? You do not know. You are too large. You move the air in ways the ceiling-things do not recognize, in patterns that belong to a world the ceiling-things have never mapped. You are not prey, because prey is small and the ceiling-things have been hunting small things for so long that small is all they know how to see. Or you are too still. The ceiling-things hunt what moves, and you are so still that you might be stone, a new growth on the ceiling's underside, a mineral deposit that has always been there. Or the ceiling-things are full. The last meal was recent. The next meal can wait. And in the waiting, in the fullness that is not fullness but is the absence of hunger, you are safe beneath a sky of predators who have chosen not to fall.

You are the only thing in the room that can leave.

This is a thought that arrives without being invited, a knowledge that sits in your chest like a stone. You are the only thing in the room that has feet that could carry it elsewhere, lungs that could breathe air that does not taste of mineral and age, eyes that remember blue. The mites cannot leave. The centipedes cannot leave. The beetles and the springtails and the worms and the fungi and the machines — they are the room, they are held by the room, they will die in the room and be held by the room after death. But you are standing in the chamber with your hand in your pocket, touching the warm small object that you brought from somewhere else, and the chamber has a shape, and shapes have edges, and edges have doors, and doors have outsides, and outside is where you came from, and outside is where you could go, if you could remember how, if you could find the seam.

You do not move.

The minutes pass in the way that minutes pass here — not in the counting of clocks but in the cycling of the room's breath, the hum of the generators, the pulse of bioluminescent fungi that glow and dim and glow again in their slow rhythm that may be a clock or may be a heartbeat or may be nothing at all that has a name. You breathe the room's air and the room breathes you. Your body heat joins the body heat of the generators and the fungal mats and the ten thousand creatures that are always generating warmth by the simple fact of being alive. Your exhalation joins the exhalation of the room — the same mineral tang, the same faint sweetness, the same sour note of decay that is the room's smell and your smell now, the smell of a world that has become your world because you have entered it and cannot enter it without being changed.

The mites click. The generators hum. The air cycles through vents you cannot see, carrying the dust of the dead, carrying the spores of the living, carrying the faint vibration of something deep in the wall that turns and turns and turns and will turn tomorrow and the day after and the day after that, for as long as the fuel appears, for as long as the seals hold, for as long as the room has something left to burn.

You are standing in the chamber. The chamber is standing in you.

You will not remember this moment ending. You will not remember the corridor, the ladder, the hatch, the first breath of air that tastes of sky. You will carry the mineral tang with you, and the click of mandibles, and the hum of machines that never stop. You will sleep in a bed that is too soft, in a room that is too quiet, and you will listen for the low vibration in your sternum, the heartbeat of something that turns in the dark, and you will not hear it, and you will miss it with a longing that has no name because it has no object, because the thing you miss is a world that never was and is everywhere, a closed circle of growth and death and the slow pulse of things that live because living is what happens when nothing says it cannot.

The chamber exhales. You are inside it now.
