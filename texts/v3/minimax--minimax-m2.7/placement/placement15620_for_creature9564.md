What would I be here?

I would be the heron. Not the heron that visits—the heron that has always been here, that will be here still when the last of these pools has silted over and become meadow. I would be the grey-green shape in the shallows, the stillness that the water moves around rather than through, the patience that has become indistinguishable from the mud itself.

I arrive before light. I am already here, having been here since the last pool was still the memory of a river, since the mountains finished their long lowering and the land remembered how to be flat. My legs are the color of the silt. My feathers hold the grey of overcast sky, the green of algae, the brown of half-rotted leaves. I am not beautiful. I am the color of patience, which is the color of this place.

I stand in water that barely covers my feet. The mud is warm beneath me—I can feel it through the thin skin of my soles—and the water moves so slowly around my legs that I would not know it was moving at all if I did not feel the slight resistance, the faint pull, the patient redistribution that has been going on since before I learned to fish these pools.

The basin stirs. Somewhere in the grasses, a frog begins its evening call—the first, tentative, testing the dark. Another answers. Then another. The chorus will not reach its full thickness for another hour, when the heat of the day has seeped into the mud and the water and the frogs have remembered again why they sing. But already the first voices are speaking into the warm air, and I can feel the basin listening.

I do not move. I have been standing here since before the light came, and I will be standing here after it goes. My neck is folded against my back, my head resting against my breast. My eyes are open but unseeing, not because I sleep—herons do not sleep the way other creatures sleep—but because there is nothing yet to see. The frogs sing. The insects hum in the thick air. Somewhere in the trees, a bird makes its small sounds, its soft chips and calls, not calling for anything, only participating in the chorus that never quite stops.

This is what I know: the basin in the early evening, the light coming in low and gold, the frogs beginning to stir. The frogs know me. I have stood in this pool, and in the pool just east of here where the water gathers deeper, and in the pool to the south where the soil is darker and holds more heat. I have stood in all of them, across years beyond counting, and the frogs know my shape the way I know their voices. I am part of the pattern. I am the stillness that holds the water in place so the frogs can sing, or I am the quick strike that breaks the pattern, the small violence that reminds the basin that even patience must eat.

The light thickens. The chorus fills. The humidity sits in the air like something you could touch, like a hand resting just above your head. The warmth is not unpleasant—it is the warmth of the earth itself, the warmth of a place that has been receiving the sun for so long it has learned to hold it, to give it back slowly, so slowly that the days blur into each other and only the seasons mark the passage of time.

I lift my head. My eyes, which were unseeing, focus on the water in front of me. The surface is still, reflecting the grey-green light of the overcast sky, reflecting the dark shapes of the far trees. But something moves beneath. I see the ripple—a small disturbance, a shadow in the shadow, the suggestion of something alive moving through the water's slow dark. I do not move. I wait. Patience is not the absence of waiting; patience is the act of waiting itself, the willingness to let time pass without resistance.

The ripple comes closer. I see the fish now—or not a fish, perhaps, but some other creature of the water, some small thing that has spent its life in this pool, that knows the shape of this pool the way I know it, that moves through the water the way I move through the air. It is not afraid. Why would it be afraid? It has never known anything but this place. The water has always been warm. The banks have always been low. There is nothing to fear.

I strike.

It is quick. It is violent. It is over before most creatures would know it had begun—a forward thrust of my head, a snap of my beak, the grip and the throw and the swallow. The fish—yes, it was a fish, small and silver in the quick flash of the evening light—goes down. I feel it moving in my throat, fighting for a moment, then still. I stand again. The water settles back into its stillness. The frogs sing. The world resumes.

This is the pattern: the waiting and the strike, the patience and the violence, the long hours of standing and the brief moment of taking. I do not feel hunger anymore; hunger is too urgent a word. I feel only the absence that precedes the strike and the fullness that follows, and the difference between them is the difference between one moment and the next, which in this place is no difference at all.

The light is changing. The gold is deepening toward amber, and the shadows of the trees are stretching long across the basin floor. The chorus has reached its full thickness now—a layered sound, multiple species calling in overlapping rhythms, the whole basin full of small voices saying what small voices have always said: we are here, we are here, we are here. I am part of this sound, though I do not add my voice to it. I add only my stillness, which is another kind of voice, the voice of the place itself saying the same thing in a different way.

A turtle surfaces at the edge of the pool—painted, its shell dark with algae, its eyes moving in that slow, ancient way that turtles have. It sees me. I see it. We regard each other across the water with the complete understanding of creatures who share a place, who have always shared this place, who will continue to share it until one of us is gone and the other remains, and then the one that remains will know that something has changed, though it will not have words for what.

The turtle submerges again. I watch the rings spread across the water, watch them fade, watch the surface return to stillness. The deer will come at dusk—they always come at dusk, when the light is low and the basin is full of shadows. They will move through the grass at the far edge of the pool, and I will watch them from my branch above, and they will not see me, or they will see me and not care, because the heron on its branch is not a threat to the deer, because we are all creatures of the same patience, all shaped by the same slow wearing away.

I lift off. My wings are large enough to catch the low air, to carry me to the branch I favor—the one that overlooks this pool, that gives me sightlines in all directions, that is high enough above the water to let me sleep without fear of what moves through the shallows at night. I land. I fold my wings. I settle my body into the shape of a heron at rest, which is not so different from the shape of a heron standing, which is not so different from the shape of the branch itself, which is not so different from the shape of the trees that have always stood here, that have grown slowly in the patient light, that have become part of the basin's settled form.

Below me, the pool gleams in the fading light. The frogs sing. A water snake—I see it now, moving through the vegetation at the water's edge—slides from the shallows into the deeper water, its body undulating with the same unhurried confidence that I feel in my own limbs. It knows this pool. It has always known this pool. It will be here when I am not, moving through the water the way water moves through the basin, patient and unhurried and unafraid.

The light is almost gone now. The blue hour is coming—that time when the world seems to hold its breath, when the sounds of the basin layer over each other until it is hard to tell where one ends and another begins, when the deer come down to drink and the world is full of small, ordinary presences going about their ordinary lives.

I fold my head against my breast. My eyes close. Not sleep—herons do not sleep the way other creatures sleep—but something like sleep, something like rest, something that is the same as the basin's own stillness, the same as the mud settling, the same as the water finding its level and staying there.

I will be here in the morning. I will drop from this branch to the water and stand in the shallows and wait for the fish to come, and the fish will come, because they have always come, because this pool has always given what it has to give. The frogs will sing. The turtles will surface. The world will resume, and I will be part of it, and the world will not know the difference, and I will not care, because there is no difference. The heron and the basin are the same patience, worn smooth by the same long time, holding each other the way water holds the shape of the clay that contains it.

This is what I would be: the patience that has finished changing, the stillness that has stopped waiting to become something else. I would be the heron in the shallows, the shape that is the same as the water that holds it, the presence that is no more remarkable than the frogs or the turtles or the slow seep of water through ancient clay. I would be here. I would be here still. I would be the completion that the basin holds, the lesson it teaches to anyone patient enough to listen: that to endure is not to wait for something better, but to be what you have become, fully, finally, without regret.
