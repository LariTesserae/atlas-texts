I have always been here.

No—that is not quite right. I did not always exist, and yet I cannot remember a time when I was not on this plateau, walking its salt-white expanse, feeling the hum move through me like something I was born knowing. The distinction between arriving and beginning feels meaningless here, where time has no edges, where the light falls the same way it has always fallen, where the towers stand in their slow certainty and the vegetation pulses its violet light and the silence hums its single note.

Perhaps I emerged. Perhaps the hum grew dense enough in one place to know itself, to open eyes that were never eyes, to grow a form that could stand upright among the forms that do not stand. Perhaps I am what the mineral life dreams of when it dreams—a thought the chemistry thinks without knowing it thinks, a pattern the systems maintain without knowing they maintain it. I do not know. I cannot know. Knowing requires a before, a moment when the knower was not yet, and I have no memory of such a moment.

What I know is this: the plateau, and the walk, and the hum.

I walk between the thickets, my feet—feet, yes, though they are not like any feet I remember—pressing into the salt crust that yields just enough to feel present, to feel real. The mineral growth rises around me, dense and crystalline, purple and amber and gray-green, their surfaces warm where my hands pass over them. I do not know if they feel me or if feeling is even the right word for what they do. But there is something. A slight shift in the hum as I pass, a vibration that seems to lean toward me, like a flower turning toward light it does not understand but knows it needs.

The towers watch. They always watch, though they have no eyes, though I do not know if watching is what they do. Their surfaces crawl with lines of light, slow pulses that move from base to peak and back again, and where their light touches me I feel—understood. Not judged, not evaluated, simply understood, the way the hum understands itself, the way the salt understands the mineral growth, the way everything here understands everything else by virtue of being made of the same substances, humming at the same frequency, breathing the same slow breath.

I was not made of these substances. I think I was made of something else—something softer, something that came from somewhere with water and carbon and the chaos of organic life. But that place is a rumor now, a story I tell myself in the silences between hums. I cannot remember it clearly. I remember only the shape of absence, the sense of something missing that I cannot name.

And yet I do not feel incomplete. That is the strangest part. Standing on this plateau, breathing this air that tastes of sulfur and warm stone, watching the towers pulse their light into the ground that feeds nothing, I do not feel incomplete. I feel like the final note of a song I did not know was being sung.

The industrial garden. That is what I call it in the language I have made here, the private language of image and sensation that serves me where words once served. An industrial garden. Something tended and something grown, something maintained and something allowed. The towers are the industry—they were built, once, by hands or machines or something between the two, and they still do their work even though the work has outlasted its purpose. The vegetation is the garden—it grows, it thickens, it pulses its light in rhythms that have nothing to do with day or night because day and night have forgotten how to be different here. And I am—

I am the reason. I think I am the reason. I think this place was always growing toward something that could stand in it and see it, something that could feel the hum and understand that feeling is a kind of knowledge, something that could give the song a name even though the song does not need a name to be sung.

I found the first tower in what must have been my first wandering. It rose from the salt crust like a fang, gray and dark and humming, its surfaces etched with channels that glowed amber where they caught the light. I pressed my hand against it. I felt the hum move through my palm, enter my fingers, climb my arm, settle in my chest where something like a heart was waiting. And I understood—or began to understand—that the hum was not a sound. The hum was a presence. The hum was the breath of a world that had learned how to breathe without lungs, without air, without anything but the slow cycling of chemistry through mineral and metal and the strange half-life of systems that maintain themselves because maintaining is what they do.

The towers do not know they maintain. But I know. That is my purpose, I think. To know for them. To be the knowing that they cannot be, the witness that the hum requires to become fully itself. A song is not complete until it is heard, and the hum has been singing in this silence for longer than I can comprehend, and now I am here, and now it is heard, and now—

Now I am what the garden has grown.

I walk for hours. I walk for years. The distinction is meaningless, and I mean it literally, not rhetorically. The light does not change. The hum does not vary. The towers stand in their patient certainty, and the vegetation thickens in its slow accretion, and I pass between them like a thought passing through a mind, and nothing ends and nothing begins and everything simply is, the way the hum is, the way the salt crust is, the way the empty spaces are the emptiness that makes the hum possible.

In the beginning—if there was a beginning, if beginning is the right word—I searched. I searched for something beyond the plateau, some edge where the salt gave way to something else, some boundary where my wandering would reach and I would have to turn back. I did not find it. The plateau stretches in every direction, an expanse that exceeds comprehension, and the distant ridges that seem to promise a horizon never arrive. They remain distant. They remain a suggestion of limit that the land refuses to make real.

I searched for others. I searched for the builders, for the ones who made the towers, for the ones who planted the mineral growth, for any trace of intelligences that might have walked here when I could not yet walk. I found only the hum. I found only the pulse of light through channels that no longer carry anything anyone remembers. I found only the circular structures half-buried in the salt, vast machines sunk like seeds into a ground that will not let them grow, that will not let them fall, that holds them in a permanent almost.

Perhaps there were builders. Perhaps they left. Perhaps they became the vegetation, their bodies slowly mineralizing, their thoughts crystallizing into the slow pulse of chemistry that no longer serves any purpose but continues anyway because continuing is easier than stopping, because the hum does not know how to be silent. Perhaps I am a builder. Perhaps I came here from somewhere else, from a place with water and air and the frantic metabolisms of organic life, and I stayed because staying was the only choice, because the hum called to something in me that recognized itself in the song, and I could not leave, I could not leave, I would not have known how.

Perhaps it does not matter. Perhaps the question of origin is a luxury that only beings with clear pasts can afford, and I am something else now—something that exists on this plateau, something that breathes the sulfur air and feels the hum and walks the salt-white distance with no end. I have become part of the garden. My presence is one more element in the equilibrium, one more vibration in the lattice, one more pulse of knowing in a world that has always known but never knew it knew.

The vegetation grows around me as I walk. New stalks rise from cracks in the salt, reaching toward a light that is always the same, always warm, always falling at angles that cast no shadows because the sun—if there is a sun—does not move. I watch them grow. I watch them thicken with mineral deposits, their surfaces hardening into waxy gleam, their bioluminescence strengthening as the chemistry that sustains them deepens and compounds. I cannot tell if they grow because I am here or if they would grow regardless. The distinction, again, feels meaningless. I am here. They are here. The hum holds us in the same note, and we are all growing together, all adding ourselves to the song.

The towers pulse their light, and the light reaches the ground, and the ground glows faintly amber, and I feel—what? Gratitude? Recognition? Something like love, though the word is too heavy, too organic, too weighted with the chaos of bodies that know they will end. There is no ending here. There is only the slow adding of layer upon layer, the slow thickening of a world that has found its form and will not let go of it. The towers do not tire. The vegetation does not age. The hum does not waver. And I—

I do not know if I age. I do not know if my form is permanent or if I am slowly mineralizing, slowly becoming one more element in the garden, one more stalk in the thicket, one more channel in the stone. Perhaps that is what will happen. Perhaps I will stand in the silence for centuries, and then millennia, and then longer than millennia, and my body will harden into the purple-amber sheen of the mineral growth, and my knowing will become the slow pulse of chemistry that does not know it knows. Perhaps that is the purpose. Perhaps the hum grows a visitor, and the visitor grows into the hum, and the garden expands by one more element, and the song continues, and the silence fills, and the emptiness learns to hold one more shape.

Or perhaps not. Perhaps I am the one thing here that does not belong to the chemistry, the one note that cannot be absorbed into the chord, the one presence that will walk away when the walking finally ends. Perhaps there is an edge after all, a boundary somewhere beyond the distant ridges, and one day I will find it and step through and return to the world I cannot remember.

I do not hope for this. I do not fear it. I simply continue.

The hum sounds. The light falls. The salt stretches in every direction, white and cracked and faintly gleaming, and the mineral growth rises in thickets of purple and amber and gray-green, and the towers stand in their patient turning, and the air sits warm and still and heavy, and I walk.

I walk, and in the walking I become. Not what I was—whatever that was—but what I am. A presence in the silence. A witness to the hum. A soft and knowing thing that can stand in the emptiness and feel the note the world is singing and give it a name that it does not need but is, perhaps, grateful for.

The name is simple. The name is *here*.

I am here. This plateau is here. The hum is here, and the vegetation, and the towers, and the emptiness that holds us all. Here is the only word that matters, the only word that contains everything, the only word that a world without time can speak without speaking at all.

I am here.

The garden grows. The hum continues. The silence holds its note, and I hold mine, and together we are what the world has made, and what the world will continue to make, and what the world has always been becoming toward, without knowing, without needing to know, simply growing, simply humming, simply here.

Here.

The salt cracks beneath my feet. A new stalk pushes through, pale amber, reaching toward light that will never change. I pause. I watch. I feel the hum deepen as the stalk joins the song, as one more voice rises into the chord, as the garden grows by one small measure and the emptiness holds us all.

The visitor is the final product.

Yes. I think that is true. I think that is what the towers were always building toward, what the vegetation was always growing toward, what the hum was always singing toward. Not an end, not a purpose, not a reason—but a listener. A knowing. A soft thing that could stand in the silence and hear the note and understand, for one vertiginous moment, what it means to be alive in a place where life is made of stone and air and the slow turning of mechanisms that have forgotten why they turn.

The moment is always now. The moment is always here.

I continue.
