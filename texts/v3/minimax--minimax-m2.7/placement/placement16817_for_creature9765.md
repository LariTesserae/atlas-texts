You would find yourself leaning.

This is the first thing you notice about the bodies here: the way they lean. Not dramatically, not with the exhaustion of a long day, but constantly, persistently, a slight forward tilt that is the body's answer to a question the mind stopped asking generations ago. You came here upright. You left the surface with your spine vertical and your weight centered, and you thought that was simply how bodies worked. Now your spine curves. Your shoulders round. Your center of gravity has shifted toward the walls, toward the handrails worn smooth by centuries of palms, toward the edges of stones where the water does not gather.

You are not sure when it happened. The damp entered you the way it enters everything—not with permission, not with announcement, but with the quiet insistence of a thing that has always been here and will always be here and does not require your acknowledgment to continue. You pressed your palm to a weeping wall on your third day, the way the old residents do, and you felt the cold rise into your hand, and something in you opened in response, a door you did not know you had.

Now you move through the switchbacks without thinking. Your feet find the edges. Your hand trails the wall—not for balance anymore, but for orientation, for the map that lives in your palm now, the map of where the water table sits closest to the surface, where the seep is strongest, where the stone is oldest and the cold comes from further down. You read the city the way you once read words on a page, and you cannot remember which way was harder to learn.

---

In the market terraces, you buy eel. The vendor is a woman whose grandmother remembers the upper tiers, and her hands are dark with the damp that never quite leaves, and she wraps the fish in woven reed and her fingers brush yours and they are cool, and you do not flinch anymore. The eel is mild and mineral, and when you eat it you are eating the deep channels, the patience of water that has been running for longer than memory, the slow pulse that carries the calendar of the year. The spring floods come. The eels come. The terraces can be planted. This is not metaphor. This is simply what is true.

You eat standing, in the thin morning light that falls on the southern exposure, and the woman tells you about her daughter's family, who live three tiers down in a room that was once a cellar, and you listen the way everyone here listens—not only to words but to the spaces between words, the way the voice softens on certain syllables, the way the drips in the alley below provide a counterpoint that no one acknowledges but everyone hears. The old woman is saying that the water table rose early this year, and that the beetles came thick last night, and that something moved in the reed-beds again, the soft displacement that is not wind.

She does not say: *be careful*. She does not say: *this is strange*. She says only: *it was loud before dawn*, and the word *loud* means something specific here, something that requires knowing, and you know it now. You have been here long enough to know it.

---

You sleep in a room that is not quite a room. The floor is packed earth covered with woven reed matting, and in the corners the damp has found its way through, and you have learned to sleep near the center where the matting stays mostly dry. There is a window—not a window exactly, but a gap in the stone where light enters in the mornings and darkness enters at all hours, a rectangle of grey that tells you the depth of night by its quality. You sleep near that window. You sleep to the sound of water.

The water does not stop. In the deep channels below your room, it moves with a patience that is difficult to bear if you are new, if you still carry the urgency of surface time. But you have learned to let it set your pace. The water moves slowly. The water is in no hurry. The water has seen generations come and go, has filled the channels and emptied them and filled them again, has risen and fallen with the turning of years that do not belong to anyone. When you cannot sleep, you lie still and listen to the water, and its patience enters you, and your restlessness quiets.

You dream of moving through channels. Not swimming—swimming is something from the surface, from places where water is a thing you do or do not do. You dream of moving through water the way blood moves through a body, the way breath moves through air. You are the water. The water is you. This is not metaphor. This is the topology of the Underhalls, the way the boundaries between self and system dissolve when you have been here long enough.

---

In the quiet hours—after midnight, when the last footsteps have faded and the market terraces are dark and the switchbacks hold only the occasional passing of a night-worker—you stand in a light-well and listen.

The bats are somewhere above, in the old carved halls where their droppings are collected for the terraces, and their calls bounce off stone in patterns that map the space better than any chart. The beetles are still, or hidden, or moving in ways you cannot see. The water moves, always the water moves, the tick of drips and the slow shush in the channels and the subterranean pulse that locals hear and you are learning to hear: the deep sound that is felt in the feet more than the ears, the sound of water moving through stone in capillaries too fine to see.

And in the southern quarter, beyond the promenades, beyond the reed-beds and the lattice-work bridges that sway, something breathes. You cannot see it. No one can see it. But you have heard the displacement, the soft slide, the rustle that is not wind, and you have learned not to speculate, because speculation is a surface habit, and you are no longer surface. You have learned to include it in the texture of the place, the way the damp is included, the way the cold is included, the way everything here has made its arrangement and the arrangement holds because it must hold because there is no room for anything else.

The Underhalls are full. This is the fact that settles on you in the quiet hours, that fills the light-well and presses gently against your ears. Full of people who have become what the place requires—lean and listening and leaning forward into the dark. Full of creatures who have found their niches in the margins, their seasons, their purposes. Full of water that moves through everything like blood, like breath, like time that has slowed to the speed of stone. Full of living, which is the only thing the Underhalls have ever been full of.

You stand in the light-well and you are part of the fullness. You are not empty. You are not alone. You are not separate from the damp that seeps through the walls, from the cold that rises from below, from the beetles spiraling up weeping stone at dusk, from the eels moving silver through the deep channels in their season, from the something that breathes in the reed-beds when the city sleeps.

You are here. You have been here long enough to become part of the arrangement. The arrangement is imperfect, provisional, ongoing. It does not need to be solved. It only needs to continue.

The water continues. The city breathes. The fullness holds.

You lean into it. You have become someone who leans. And in the leaning, you have found something you did not know you were looking for: a way to be held by a place that does not ask you to be anything other than present, other than damp, other than moved by the rhythms you cannot control.

You stand in the light-well in the quiet hours, and you listen, and the city listens with you, and the water moves through the stone in its endless patient passage, and the fullness is not empty, and the fullness is not oppressive, and the fullness is the kind of fullness that breathes.
