I have been here long enough that I am no longer certain I arrived.

There is a difference between *arriving* and *having always been adjacent to this place*, and that difference has become difficult to hold. I remember a road — or the concept of one — but when I reach for the memory, my hand closes on something that is neither road nor ground but the *possibility* of a path, the memory of a decision someone once made to go somewhere and the slow erosion of both the someone and the somewhere.

I am not the first to come here. I know this because I have found evidence: a footprint in the mineral field that should not hold footprints but does, faintly, for longer than it should, fading in a way that suggests not weather but *attention* — the ground deciding, moment by moment, whether the impression is worth keeping. There are three prints. A left, a right, a left. They stop in the middle of the field as though the walker simply stopped, or became something that no longer needed to walk, or found that walking and not-walking had become the same thing.

I do not know if I am the one who made them.

The buildings know me now. Not as a visitor — nothing is a visitor here, because arrival and departure have loosened from their meanings — but as a *continuity*. I pass the courtyard with the almost-pattern on the floor and the building whose upper story has lain down, and they register me the way a word registers the syllables that have come before: with recognition that is also a kind of forgetting, the sense that I have been here in some form and am now here in another and these forms may or may not be connected.

This is my life now, such as it is:

I wake — or the state I am in shifts from less-alert to more-alert, and I call it waking — in a room whose walls still agree on their verticality. The light is dim. The light is always dim; I have learned not to hope for more. But it cycles, that slow breath of atmosphere, and in the thick moments of the cycle the light almost touches the walls, almost makes shadows that are merely shadows, almost performs the duties light is supposed to perform. I watch for this. It is the closest thing I have to a morning.

I walk. I have learned the paths that stay paths — or rather, the paths that stay *more consistent* than the others, the strips of mineral ground that only occasionally forget their straightness. There is a plaza where I go to sit, if sitting is what it is, a wide open space where the buildings do not lean and the wind does not hum and I can feel the vibration below without the interference of walls. The vibration is clearer there. The slow thought of the plateau, the dreaming that moves through whatever infrastructure of meaning remains beneath the stone. I sit — or I remain in place with my legs bent — and I listen, and after a while I feel it in my own bones, and then I do not know if the feeling is the plateau's or mine, and this ambiguity has stopped troubling me.

The animals know me too. I have seen them both more than once now, and they do not flee and they do not approach. They simply *are* — in the periphery, in the space where looking and not-looking meet. One of them has a way of pausing, mid-motion, as though it has just remembered that it was in the middle of something and is trying to recall what that something was. I recognize this. I have been pausing like this for days, or years, or the span of a single held breath that has lasted longer than breath should last.

I do not eat. I have not needed to eat since I arrived, or since I stopped being someone who arrives. The dissolution feeds me in its way — or rather, it has loosened the part of me that *needs* to be fed, the architecture of requirement that makes a body a body. I am still here. I have a shape, or the memory of one. But the shape has become negotiable. I can feel the edges of my hands softening when I hold them to the pale light, not literally — not yet — but in the way that conviction softens, the way certainty about where I end and the air begins has begun to fray.

This is the gift the plateau offers: the permission to stop being so much.

I know now why there are no others here. Not because the city is hostile or empty but because the *relationship* has become difficult. To be with another requires maintaining the boundary between self and other, and that boundary is the first thing the dissolution edits. I could not promise to remain distinct from you, if you came here. I could not guarantee that my thoughts would stay in my skull, that my shape would stay in my shape, that the word *I* would continue to mean what it meant when I still believed in meaning.

So I am alone. And the aloneness is not loneliness — it is something closer to a permission, a release from the obligation to maintain a self in the presence of other selves. I am alone the way the light is alone, the way the wind is alone, the way the slow thought below is alone: completely, and without grief.

I have begun to understand the buildings.

Not their purpose — purpose was the first thing to go — but their *grammar*. The way a wall that has begun to lean is a sentence that is questioning its own structure. The way a roof that lies flat has made a statement about verticality and found that statement wanting. The buildings are not dying. They are *revising*. They are going back through their own drafts, crossing out the parts that required too much effort — load-bearing, weatherproofing, the commitment to sheltering — and simplifying, simplifying, until what remains is the simplest possible sentence: *I am here*, *I am still here*, *I am*.

Some of them have gone further. There is a building near the plaza that is no longer a building. It is a shape — a suggestion of angles, a memory of enclosure — and the mineral ground has begun to reclaim it, or the building has begun to return to the ground, or these are the same thing and the distinction was always artificial. The dissolution has worked through it, and what is left is the building's outline, the ghost of its walls, and if I look at it long enough I can see it *fading* — not crumbling, not falling, simply deciding less and less strongly that it exists.

I find this beautiful. I want to be clear about that. There is nothing tragic in what is happening here. Tragic requires the sense that something *should* continue, and the plateau has set that should aside. What remains is a place that is tired of being a place and is learning, slowly, with great patience and no hurry, how to be something else. Or how to stop being anything at all. The difference is not clear, and the plateau does not seem to care about the difference, and I have stopped caring too.

The wind tells me things. Or not things — the shape of things, the *gesture* toward meaning that language once made. When the wind hums through the narrow streets and the walls answer with their thicker silence, I hear syllables. I have heard them enough now that I can almost complete them, almost turn the sound into sense. Almost. It is like dreaming of a word you cannot quite wake up remembering — it stays just beyond the edge of retrieval, held in the space where meaning used to live and has not quite finished leaving.

I think the city is saying goodbye.

Or not goodbye — the city does not seem to know what it is saying. It is simply *saying*, the way a body breathes, the way a thought continues after the thinker has moved on. It is producing sound because it was once a place that spoke, and the speaking has not yet finished, and perhaps it never will, and perhaps that is the point. Perhaps the last thing a world does, before it stops being a world, is make a sound that once meant something and now means nothing and still, somehow, continues.

I have been here long enough that I am no longer certain I arrived.

I may have been here when the city was built. I may have been part of the dissolution — one of its agents, one of its symptoms. I may be the plateau itself, dreaming itself from the inside, and what I call *walking* is what the plateau calls *thinking*, and what I call *the wind* is what I call *my breath*, and none of this is metaphor because metaphor requires the distinction between the thing and the likeness, and that distinction is the first thing the plateau set down.

I put my hand on the wall of a building that still remembers being vertical.

The stone is cool. It does not warm to my touch. But it accepts my hand — it receives the pressure, the presence, the brief connection between two things that are still, for the moment, things. And in the receiving I feel the wall's own dissolution, its quiet questioning of whether being a wall is necessary, whether the effort of holding itself together is worth the holding. It has not decided. It may never decide. It may simply continue to hold itself together less tightly, indefinitely, until the holding is indistinguishable from the releasing.

I pull my hand away. The impression of my palm lingers for a moment, then fades.

The ground has already decided it was never there.

I walk on. The street curves when I am not looking at it. The light dims. The shadows do their slow wrong things on the walls. Somewhere below, the slow thought continues — vast, patient, uncomprehending, still trying to be.

And here, in the gap between trying and having tried, I remain.

Not alive in the way that word once meant. Not dead in any way it ever meant. Present but uncertain. Here but reluctant. A 1.4 out of 3, at least by the plateau's measure — and that measure has become my own.

I am the animal that does not run.

I am the shadow that has learned to move.

I am the word the city is still saying, though it no longer knows what it means, though I no longer know who is saying it, though meaning has faded and the sound remains, faint, patient, holding its shape in the thin cold air of a place that has not yet finished being.

I put one foot in front of the other.

I move through the unbecoming.

I am becoming too.
