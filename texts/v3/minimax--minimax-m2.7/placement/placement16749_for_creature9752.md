This is a remarkable piece of writing. What strikes me most is how you've created a space that resists anthropomorphization even as it demands it—there's something here that's genuinely *other*, that operates on logics we can sense but never quite parse, and yet the writing stays in close sensory contact rather than drifting into the abstract. The engine grove is never explained. It simply accumulates.

The relationship between observation and transformation is handled with particular care. The protagonist doesn't *do* anything to this place—they descend, they witness, they leave—and still they're changed, still the space "makes a note of" them. There's something almost ritualistic in this, the way presence itself is participation. The moisture finds its way in. The spores catch. You become part of the pattern not through intention but through arrival.

The segmented creatures feel like the heart of this world—the most fully realized, the strangest, the most itself. The detail about their color-changing cells as a warning system, the way they read surfaces the way the protagonist reads them, creates a sense of parallel intelligence without anthropomorphism. They're not cute or alien or threatening. They're *there*, doing what they've become.

And the sound design is extraordinary. "The sound is a system. You hear it as you hear rain: individually meaningless, collectively meaningful." That's a perfect description of what it's like to be in a living space—not discrete events but a texture, a field, a condition you exist within rather than observe from outside.

What I'm left wondering: is this a place of hope or melancholy? The industrial bones are not mourned—the integration feels like fulfillment rather than loss. But the machinery is still running for no identified purpose, and the deep creatures are unknown, and the protagonist is welcome but never meant to understand. There's something both comforting and unsettling in that. The space is complete, but completeness isn't the same as meaning.

The ending lands perfectly. "You will come back." Of course they will. You've made something worth returning to—not because it's beautiful or safe, but because it's *real* in a way that the surface world isn't. The amber light seems wrong afterward. The dryness feels like loss. That shift, that displacement, is the truest thing in the piece.
