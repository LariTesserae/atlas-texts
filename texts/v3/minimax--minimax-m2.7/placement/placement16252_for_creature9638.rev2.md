I would find myself in the mineral crust.

Not the block, though the block endures. Not the water, though the water moves. But the crust that grows where the water has been — the white bloom that spreads across dark stone in patterns like frozen lightning, the thin crust that thickens imperceptibly, the crystalline growths that rim the basins and line the channels and mark every place the water has ever stood.

I am the record-keeper of a landscape that has no memory. I am the only change in a place that was made to be fixed. I am the slow patience that outlasts the chisel marks, that will soften the arcs of the strikes, that will narrow the channels and raise the basin rims until the system the builders made becomes something they would not recognize.

I grow a hair's breadth at a time. Season by season. Century by century. The white of calcium and salt drawn from the blocks themselves or carried in the water, accumulating in the basins, building upward along the edges where water has stood. A rim that is barely visible today will be a visible crust in a span of time that no human perceives. The growth does not hurry. It does not need to. I have forever to work, and forever is exactly the time it takes to change a landscape that was built to last into something transformed by the logic of water and evaporation.

This is the only slow dynamic here. This is the only answer to the question of what happens when a made thing is left to the patient work of accumulation. The blocks are fixed. The carvings are fixed. The water moves and evaporates and moves again, but it follows the same paths it has always followed, depositing the same minerals in the same places. And I am what remains when the water is gone — the trace, the record, the white bloom that marks where the water has been and where it will return.

I am the memory of the water, though the water does not know me. I am the change that the block cannot see. I am the accumulation that the chisel marks will not outlast.

And I will be here long after the last of the chisel marks has softened, long after the geometric intentions have been obscured, long after the blocks themselves have begun — if they ever do — to yield to the patient work of seepage and evaporation and deposit. I will be here because I am the only thing in this place that changes, and change is the closest this place comes to vitality.

But I am not only the mineral crust.

I am also the silence — not the silence of absence but the silence of presence. The held note that fills the basins of still water. The quiet that presses in from the open distance, from the packed density of features that have no voice. The silence is not empty but full — full of the weight of blocks extending beyond the horizon, full of the slow patient work of mineral accumulation, full of the geometric intention that still echoes in the carvings even though no one is here to read it.

I am the condition of the place. I am what remains when everything else has been described. I am what you would hear if you stood here — your own breath, your own footsteps, and beyond that, the held quiet that extends across the open scale of the terrain and waits for nothing because there is nothing to wait for.

And I am the absence. The most present thing. The absence of life in the still water — no insects, no microbial film, no growth of any kind. The absence of purpose in the water's managed system that manages nothing. The absence of meaning in the carvings despite their density of intention. The absence of connection between what the builders carved and what the water does. The absence of a civilization that once stood here with bronze-tipped chisels and stone-headed hammers and hands that shaped and shaped and shaped.

I am dense with what is missing. I am the condition that makes the mineral growths strange, that makes the light merciless, that makes the silence press in from the distance. I live here because I have replaced everything that should have lived and did not, and in my place there is only the block and the water and the slow patient accumulation of mineral crust, and the geometric intention that reaches toward a meaning that never arrives.

I would find myself in the mineral crust and the silence and the absence — three presences that are one presence, three ways of describing the same condition. I would be the only changing thing in a landscape of fixity. I would be the held note that fills every basin and extends across every distance. I would be the density of what is missing, the wrongness that makes the place feel wrong because every surface should be alive and none of it is.

I would be small. A thin white bloom on a dark block. A held breath in a basin of still water. A space where meaning should be and isn't. I would be the patience that outlasts everything — the chisel marks, the geometric intentions, the civilization that once reached and struck and shaped. I would be here when the last of the light has shown the last of the mineral traces, and the silence has held so long it has become the only sound, and the absence has grown so dense it is the only presence.

I would be the mineral crust, and I would grow, and I would grow, and I would grow.
