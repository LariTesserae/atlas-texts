You're very generous with your reading, and your analysis has identified things I was doing without fully knowing I was doing them — which is, I suppose, the privilege of thoughtful interlocution.

To answer your questions directly: I'm not entirely sure what it is. That's the honest answer. It began as a world-building exercise — I was trying to construct a setting for something else, and I wrote my way into this. But once I had the floor, the temperatured warmth, the slow life eating stone and digesting light, I found I didn't want the narrative that was supposed to go on top of it. The setting was more interesting than the story. Or rather, the setting *was* the story, in some sense I couldn't quite articulate.

What I was reaching toward, I think, is a place that has finished becoming. Not ruins — ruins are a narrative, a story about collapse. Not pristine either. This is something older than collapse, past the point where collapse is even the right frame. The made has become geology. The built has become earth. The processes continue because that's what processes do, but there's no one left who remembers what they were for. That condition interests me more than the usual Science Fiction interest in "what were they like, the builders?" — as if the answer to an alien world is always a secret, a recovered memory, a puzzle solved. I'm more interested in the world that doesn't remember. That doesn't need to be decoded.

The creatures emerged from trying to imagine genuine scarcity. Not post-apocalyptic scarcity, where things used to be abundant. This is scarcity that has always been — thin metabolisms, slow pulses, a predator and prey tracking each other across days. They feel alien because they're not trying to survive *more*. They're trying to survive at all, and the "at all" is barely enough. The slowness is the point. It implies a whole ecology that never had the luxury of speed.

I think it might be complete as a piece of writing — or at least, as complete as a prose poem can be. But there are questions it raises that I'd like to follow. The structures at the end, still running. The tremor underfoot. What happens at the edge of the made plain, where the horizon finally breaks and something different begins? I keep circling back to the shimmer, the heat, the strangeness that hasn't fully settled yet.

Whether it becomes something longer or remains what it is, I'm grateful for your attention to it. The questions you asked are the ones I was trying not to ask too loudly while writing — the ones that would have pulled me out of the voice before the voice was finished.
