# The Descent

I am the third thing the plateau learned to say.

The first was stone — the long settling after the violence, the cooling, the patient erosion that wore the violence down to a kind of gentleness. The second was water — drawn up through the granite by fog and altitude and some memory in the rock of what it used to be, before, when it was something else, something that pressed upward against the sky and called that pressing a name that has no translation now. And then the third: what breathes and descends and returns.

I am descended from people who came to the plateau when it was still calling itself into being. My name is the oldest kind of name — a sound that means *one who stays* in a language no one speaks anymore, that lives only in the names we give our children and the words we use for the deep quarter's lowest level. I have walked these streets since before I could walk them, learning the grammar of descent before I could name the steps, understanding the language of the stone before I understood that language was what I was learning.

I am in the deep quarter when the water table rises.

Not at the lowest level — I have not yet earned that. The annual descent is not for another three days. But I am in the industrial terrace, where the channels run fuller than they have in months, and the condensation is heavy on the iron grates, and the ferns on the lower walls have brightened to a green that seems lit from within, and I am feeling the particular attention that fills the city when the plateau speaks clearly. The people around me move with this attention. The furnace-keeper on the smelting terrace has not left his post in nine hours. The weavers in the northern quarter have slowed their looms to a rhythm that is almost meditation. Even the cliff-rats, usually so quiet, are moving through the channels in a way that suggests conference — though with what, or whom, I cannot say.

I am here to witness. This is my function, or one of them — to be present when the plateau speaks, to carry what is witnessed back to the places where witnessing is needed. In the residential tier, there are children who have never seen the water table rise. They know it by the ferns' brightness, by their parents' tightened attention, by the way the bells are struck more frequently as though the city is checking in with itself. But they have not stood in the industrial terrace and felt the vibration in their teeth, and heard the low subsonic throb of the furnaces running hot, and watched the channels overflow their carved beds and send thin sheets of water running down the flagstone streets, pooling in the moss, drinking into the ferns, the whole city becoming briefly a throat through which the plateau is speaking its longest sentence.

The sentence has no end. That is what I have learned, in my years of witnessing. The plateau does not conclude. It does not arrive at meaning and rest there. It only continues — the slow articulation of something that has been trying to be said since before there was someone to say it to. The city is not the message. The plateau is not the message. The message is the saying itself — the endless, wet, iron-dark forming of a voice that has no other way to exist except through the stone and the water and the people and the creatures who have learned, over generations, to be the words it needs.

I descend.

This is not the annual descent — that will come in three days, and I am preparing for it in the way one prepares for something that cannot be prepared for, which is to say I am doing nothing at all, letting the preparation happen in whatever part of myself does not require my attention. This descent is smaller, ordinary, a thing I do on days when the water table rises because something in me requires the deep quarter's particular silence when the plateau is most clearly present.

The stairs from the industrial terrace to the deep quarter's upper level are worn smooth by generations of careful feet. I know every one of them — the one that has a crack you must step over, the one that slopes slightly leftward, the one where the stone is darker than its neighbors because of some quality in the bedrock there, some concentration of iron or memory or simply age. I descend without looking at them. My body knows. My body is the plateau's grammar — has learned it so thoroughly that I no longer distinguish between my knowledge and the stone's teaching.

At the deep quarter's threshold, the light changes. It is not that there is less light — the gas-lamps are lit at all levels now, and the iron grates catch what light filters down from the upper streets — but that the quality of the light has shifted. It comes from the stone itself, or seems to: a faint luminescence in the granite, a glow that is not warm but is not cold either, that is simply *there*, the way moisture is simply there, the way the condensation forms on iron before it forms on stone. The residents of the deep quarter have learned not to comment on this. It is simply what the deep quarter does.

I pass the old mining tunnels, which are not old in the way that things are old in lowland cities — a few centuries, a millennium — but old in a way that makes centuries seem like days. The tunnels were here before the city was a city. They are the original articulation of the plateau's intention — not built but grown, like the walls of the lowest level, like the channels that collect and channel and remember. Some say the tunnels were made by water. Some say they were made by something older than water, something the water is a remnant of, a residue, a memory of. I do not know. I only know that when I pass the tunnel mouths, I slow my breath, and I do not look directly into them, and the air that emerges from them smells of iron and something else, something that predates the smelting and the forges and the patient industrial work of centuries.

The salamanders are in the standing pools near the tunnel mouths. I see them now — pale, motionless, colorless in the way that things are colorless when color would be a kind of noise. They do not move when I pass. They do not move when anyone passes. They have arranged themselves around the city's presence so thoroughly that they no longer notice it, or they have arranged themselves around it so thoroughly that they have become part of it, and noticing would be like noticing their own breathing.

I want to go further. I want to descend to the level where the walls are grown and the pools are black and the air smells of iron and something older than iron, older than stone, older than the violence that raised the plateau and finally subsided into this long patience. I want to sit in the darkness and breathe the air and find out, as I have always wanted to find out, what the plateau is saying when no one is listening.

But I am not ready. I know this. I know it the way I know the stairs, the way my body knows the grammar of descent. The annual descent will come in three days, and I will go alone, carrying nothing, descending through the terraces and past the tunnels and into the lightless reach, and I will sit in the presence of what has been here since before the plateau rose, since before the stone was stone, since before the word *here* had meaning. And I will not try to understand. I will only attend.

The water table is rising. I can feel it in the vibration of the stone, in the hum of the aqueduct iron, in the way the condensation is falling now not from the sky — there is no sky here, only the plateau's flank and the city's layered body — but from the walls themselves, from the flagstones, from the iron grates, as though the plateau is sweating with the effort of speaking, as though it has finally found the word it has been reaching for and the word is heavy, so heavy, and it is falling from every surface, and the city is drinking it in, and I am drinking it in, and the cliff-rats and the salamanders and the furnace-keeper and the weavers and the children in the residential tier who have never seen the water table rise are all drinking the same water, breathing the same air, listening to the same silence that is not empty but full — full of something that is only beginning, only barely, after all this time, starting to say what it has always, always meant.

I am the third thing the plateau learned to say.

I am learning, still, how to say it back.
