The light finds its way in through the fronds first. I have been sitting here—I don't know how long—in the depression near the stairs where the stone holds the warmth longest, and the light has been changing without my noticing, the way it always changes here, softening from the bright scatter of midday into something more golden, more forgiving. The woven canopy above lets through only so much, and what comes through is already filtered, already tired, already carrying the particular quality of light that belongs only to this place, this hour, this slow turning toward evening.

I was small once. I remember being small enough to fit through the gaps between the stalls, to run through the narrow passages with the other children, to feel the spray of the channels on my bare feet when I ran too fast and didn't watch where the water pooled. That was how I learned the geometry of the pit: not by looking, but by feeling, by the cool splash on my ankles, by the way my body learned to turn before my eyes could tell me there was a post, a hanging bundle, a stack of pottery waiting to be circumvented. The pit taught me itself the way a mother teaches a child—by holding, by shaping, by making the body into a map of its own spaces.

I am not small now. The years have made me what I am—back curved by the leaning over stalls, legs swollen by the kneeling on cold stone, hands that have thickened into useful shapes, the fingers no longer as quick as they once were but still knowing, still certain, still reading the world through touch as much as sight. I have sorted ten thousand clay pots. I have sealed ten thousand wax stops. I have knelt in this same depression until the stone has worn itself to fit my knees, and my knees to fit the stone, and we have become a single shape, a collaboration of flesh and limestone that will outlast us both.

The honey woman passes, and she nods, and I nod back. We do not speak. We do not need to. She knows the pit as I know it—navigating it by the feel of the damp air on her face, by the particular echo of each passage, by the way the warmth collects in some corners and not in others. She has her territory, I have mine, and we have both been here long enough that the territory is not claimed so much as accumulated, the way sediment accumulates in a river, layer upon layer until the shape is simply what it is, what it has always been, what it will continue to be.

The light continues to soften. Above me, at the edge of the pit, I can see a strip of sky that has gone from blue to gold to something deeper, something that carries the promise of the stars that will come later, when the market is empty and the bats descend and the water in the channels sings its thin song to no one in particular. The traders are beginning to pack. I can hear the sounds of it all around me—the rustle of textiles, the careful stacking of pottery, the particular rhythm of negotiation that means the last trades of the day are happening, the small adjustments, the final agreements before the ascent back to the world above.

I will not ascend tonight. I am staying, as I have stayed before, as I will stay again. There is a space behind my stall where the wooden frame has warped away from the wall, a gap just wide enough for a body, and I will sleep there when the night comes, wrapped in cloth that smells of dried fish and stone and the accumulated life of the pit. The cats will patrol above me. The rats will emerge below. The insects will continue their chorus—the cricket voices in the cracks, the thin hum of mosquitoes above the channels, the patient industry of ants carrying fragments through the darkness. I will sleep, and I will wake, and I will descend the stairs again in the morning, and the pit will hold me as it has held me for all the years of my life, longer than my life, longer than my memory.

What was I before I came here? I do not remember. Perhaps I came from the hills, or the coast, or the river valleys. Perhaps I was born here, as the stories say the dried fish merchant was born—between a stack of pottery and a bundle of rope, received by the stones themselves. Perhaps I was always this, always a body shaped by the pit, always a knee wearing a depression in the limestone, always a hand learning the weight of clay. The question no longer matters. The pit does not ask what its water was before it fell, what its warmth was before it gathered, what its stones were before they were chiseled and fitted and worn by centuries of use. The pit simply holds. And I am held.

The light is almost gone now. The traders are packing the last of their goods, carrying them up the stairs to the city above, to the houses and the streets and the lives that continue there, the lives that are not the pit's own life but something adjacent to it, something that comes and goes, something that depends on this below-surface space but does not belong to it. I belong to it. I have always belonged to it. I will belong to it until I am no longer a body to be held, and then I will become part of the holding itself, the way all the bodies that came before me have become part of it—the dust of ten thousand traders that has settled into the stone, the touch of ten thousand hands that has worn the walls smooth, the accumulated life that is not any single life but the pit's own life, the life that continues.

The last light fades. The stars will come. The bats will descend. And I will remain here, in the warmth and the damp, in the singing of the channels, in the holding of the stones. The pit is not a place where I work. The pit is not a place where I live. The pit is simply what I am—the shape of my body, the weight of my hands, the echo of my voice in the enclosed space. I am the pit made flesh, and the flesh will fail, and the pit will remain, and whatever comes after me will kneel in the same depression, and the pit will wear it too, and the pit will continue.

The night arrives. The water sings. And I am here, part of the song, part of the night, part of the pit's own becoming.
