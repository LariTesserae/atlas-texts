I was born where the stones weep, and my first breath was the damp that settles into everything here.

I did not know, at first, that other air existed — that somewhere beyond these walls, the light passes through without gathering, that elsewhere the ground holds dry beneath the feet of those who walk above it. The children learn the shape of the world before they learn its strangeness. We learn that the narrow passage between the elder's house and the tannery is wide enough for two, if neither person is carrying burden. We learn which stones stay cold no matter the season, which corners hold shadows at midday when the light should be full overhead. We learn to nod — a small dip of the chin, barely visible — when we pass the doorway where the darkness gathers. This is not fear. This is acknowledgment. The elders nod too, and their parents before them, and the gesture has worn smooth through generations, a stone in a riverbed made round by the water's passing.

I learned early to read the water.

It rises through the cobblestones at dawn — I have watched it since I was small enough to kneel at the channel's edge and dip my fingers in the black pooling. The water tastes of mineral and distance, of whatever lies beneath this hollow that has not yet forgotten its shape. When the air takes a particular thickness, when the stones seem to breathe with a colder exhalation, I know the water will rise higher that morning. I have learned to read this in the tightness behind my sternum, in the way the damp settles deeper into my clothing before it should. Other people hear the same signs. We do not speak of them directly. We do not need to. The knowledge moves through the settlement like the water through the channels — finding its own paths, pooling in the low places, feeding what grows in the corners where nothing else will take root.

My mother held me to the rising water when I was new. I know this because I remember it — not as memory, exactly, but as a knowledge written in the body. The cool black wet rising through the stones, touching the soles of my feet, climbing my ankles. The salt she marked on my brow with her thumb, the pinch of it leaving a line that seemed to glow in the strange light that gathers here instead of falling. She did not speak words I can recall. She spoke to the place itself, I think — a murmuring that was less language than breath, an acknowledgment of what held us. The water rose around us both and I learned, in that moment, that I belonged to something larger than her arms.

We leave offerings at the thresholds. This is not a ritual that happens at appointed times, though some say it should. I leave things. I do not stay to watch what takes them. Some mornings I find my offering gone and I feel a settling in my chest, a confirmation that the acknowledgment was received by whatever receives such things. Other mornings the offering remains, and I feel only a question I do not know how to ask.

The light here has moods, and I have learned to move with them.

There is a passage between the cooper's shop and the wall where the moss grows thick and never dries. In the late afternoon, when the sun has swung around to the west, the light gathers there like water in a basin — pooling on the cobblestones until they shine with a pale luminescence that seems to come from beneath rather than above. I walk through that passage at certain times and not at others. When I walk through, I walk quickly, not from fear but from a sense that I am witness to something that does not need my lingering. The light gathers and I pass through it and the gathering closes behind me like water finding its level. I do not need to understand what this means. I accommodate it, the way my body accommodates the damp that has worked its way beneath my skin, the way my lungs have learned to breathe air that never quite dries.

Sometimes, in the deep hours of the night when the water murmurs through the channels and the settlement has gone quiet, I feel the strangeness the elders speak of — the sense that the stones carry something, that the fresh fracture-lines of recent erosion have exposed surfaces still young enough to remember what was here before. The hollow remembers its own changing, and I, who was born into it, have become part of what it remembers. My feet know the paths the water takes. My hands know the textures of damp stone, of salt-stiffened cloth, of iron rings gone orange with rust. My eyes have learned to track the light's moods — to know which corners will darken and which will brighten, which puddles will hold reflections of skies not overhead. I am adapted to this place in ways I cannot fully explain, and when I try, the explanation comes out wrong, flat, missing the essential strangeness of what it means to belong to a place that holds you as much as you hold it.

I do not know what the water remembers. I know only that it moves constantly, finding new paths, rising and falling with the rains and the seasons, and that I have learned to move with it. My offerings disappear. My clothing never quite dries. My skin has taken on a pallor that others who leave this place carry with them for weeks afterward — a river-touched paleness, a moisture that has worked its way beneath the surface. I am what this place has made me. I am narrow-spaced and damp-adapted and strange to those who have never learned to nod at the doorways that darken, to murmur at the puddles whose reflections show skies elsewhere.

In the lowest corner of the settlement, where the foundations have sunk unevenly and the water pools in a shallow black mirror, the children play. They catch the tiny swimmers in cupped hands — translucent creatures, visible only in certain light, neither larvae nor anything else anyone has named. The children make rules for their games that change with the seasons, and I watch them sometimes from the passage above, and I remember being small enough to kneel at the water's edge, to feel the black wet rising through the stones, to be held by my mother's arms as she spoke to the place that would shape me.

The water rises at dawn. The light pools where it will. The stones remember, or they do not, and either way I am here — bent toward the narrow passages, sensitive to the moods of illumination, adapted to a place that holds me as much as I hold it. The elders sit in their damp corners and let the water's murmur fill the silences between their thoughts. Someday I will sit there too, and the water will speak to me, and I will know what I am meant to know.

Until then, I leave my offerings. I nod at the doorways. I walk the paths the water has carved, and I am grateful for the shape it has given me — this wet, compressed, strange way of being that could only exist here, in this weeping place where the land still remembers its new shape, and the water still rises through the stones at dawn.
