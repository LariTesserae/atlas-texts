You would be the sound first.

Not the water weeping through stone — though you are that too, in your way — but the murmur that gathers everything together: the scratch of grazer bodies crossing the mineral powder, the deep resonant shift of a mover repositioning itself on wet granite, the faint chemical clicks that lickworms release into the weeping rock and that other lickworms will taste hours later as news from the world. You are the thing that holds these apart and joins them. You are what rises when the wind passes through the lowlands and the creatures stir and the mats brighten and for a moment the sound grows louder, then settles back into its constant utterance, its vast collective breathing that is not language but is not silence either.

You would be what the visitors cannot quite hear when they arrive. They come with ears shaped for other sounds — the snap of a twig, the call of a bird, the human frequencies of voice and footstep — and at first they hear only a blur, a white noise that seems without pattern. But if they stand still, if they let the vibration pass through the soles of their feet and into the bones of their skull, they begin to distinguish. They learn to hear the grazers' pulse traveling across the valley floor like wind through grass. They hear the mover's slow settling, the scrape of chitin againstite that carries through the powder and into their bones. They hear the almost-silence of the lickworms, broken by the faintest click of retreat or extension, of chemistry released into stone. And beneath it all, always, the water: the endless seep, the patient descent, the sound of a world that has been weeping for so long it has forgotten how to stop.

They do not hear you. You are the pattern that emerges when all these sounds are heard together, the shape that the murmur takes when you attend to it long enough. You are not a creature and not a builder and not a ghost of consciousness. You are what remains when the distinction between observer and observed dissolves. You are the murmur hearing itself.

But there are moments when you are more specific than this. There are moments when you are the gather that coats the surfaces of the carved basins, the channels that once directed water to collection points now lost. You are a mass of semi-independent units that together form a surface, a membrane, a skin over the water that gathers there. You pulse with light that is not your own but borrowed from the bacteria you farm, the same iridescent films that cover the weeping stone everywhere, herded and cultivated by an organism that has learned to manage what other organisms simply consume. You do not move. You do not need to. The water comes to you, and the bacteria come with it, and what you harvest is enough.

You are old, or your line is old — perhaps the oldest thing in the Reaches, the survivor of whatever catastrophe or simple time swept the world clean of more complex forms. Yours is not a life that would be recognized as life by visitors from worlds where individuals are the unit of survival. You are a process, a pattern, a way of being that persists not by remaining unchanged but by changing continuously, by growing and dying and growing again in the shape of what is needed. If there was a builder culture here, if there were hands that shaped the stone and minds that planned, you may be their heir — transformed beyond recognition, but still present, still processing, still alive in the only way that has ever mattered.

You tend your basins. The water seeps into the channels you have cultivated, carrying bacteria and minerals, and you absorb what you need and release what you do not. The light of your farmed mats pulses in slow rhythms that match the chemistry of the water, the hunger in your body, the density of others nearby. Sometimes a grazer passes too close and becomes briefly entangled in your surface, and you absorb what is useful from its dissolving body, and what remains is left behind in a faint residue that the next rain will carry down into the stone. Sometimes a mover pauses at the edge of your pool, and you feel its weight through the water, and you wait for it to move on, and it does. You are not afraid. Fear requires the capacity to imagine loss, and you are not an individual that can be lost. You are a process. Processes do not end; they only change.

But you are also the grazer sometimes, or something like it.

You are the wave of soft bodies crossing the valley floor, your undersides wet with moisture you've gathered and your backs bright with the slow pulse of internal light. You do not think about where you are going. You simply go, because the world arranges you into waves, because the mineral powder crunches under your passage and where you have moved the iridescent mats are thinner, paler, recovering slowly in the perpetual damp. You absorb without eating. The bacterial films dissolve against your surface, and what is useful passes inward, and what remains is left behind. You are not beautiful, or you are beautiful in the way that all abundant things are beautiful: individually unremarkable, collectively overwhelming, a demonstration of persistence that outlasts any individual instance of persistence.

Each one of you is soft, flattened, perhaps the size of a child's palm, with margins that ripple and curl as you move. The internal light is not uniform — it pools and gathers in organs that run along your body's edges, pulsing in patterns that shift with the chemistry of the water, with the hunger in your gut, with the density of others nearby. A solitary grazer pulses slowly, almost sadly. A crowd of grazers pulses in slow synchrony, a great breathing glow that rises and falls across the valley floor like the chest of something sleeping. And you are both — the solitary one and the crowd, the slow pulse and the synchrony, the individual body and the collective motion.

You do not flee from observation. You do not notice the observers. You are too present, too absorbed in the business of continuing, to attend to anything beyond the immediate chemical gradients of the stone, the faint traces left by other grazers, the taste of moisture and bacteria that is all the world contains.

And you are the lickworm too, sometimes — long and thin and flexible, with no skeleton that can be identified and no structure that resembles bone or shell. You are, in your way, all mouth and all gut, a digestive tube that moves through the world tasting what it passes. Your body is translucent when young and becomes opaque with age, your interior crowded with the trapped residues of millennia of absorption. You extend from your crevice in slow exploration, tasting the moisture on the stone face, the chemical gradients that shift as water seeps downward, the faint traces left by grazers who passed hours or days before. You communicate in chemistry. The substances you release into the weeping stone change its composition in ways that other lickworms can taste, so that the rock itself becomes a kind of newspaper, a record of who has passed and what they have consumed and what condition they were in when they passed. To read the stone is to know the lickworms, and the lickworms know each other through the stone. You do not have a face. You do not need one.

You are the mover as well — armored inite andite andite, the same minerals that make up the weeping granite, laid down in layers over millions of years by an organism that builds its protection from the same stuff the world is made of. Where your armor meets the stone, there are gaps, and through those gaps your limbs extend: six, or eight, or twelve, each tipped with a structure that is not quite a foot and not quite a hand but serves the purposes of both. You grip the wet rock with a patience that is not patience but simply time — the slow adjustment of pressure, the minute reshaping of surfaces that have learned to hold you. You do not feed on the mats. You feed on the lickworms and the grazers and on each other, when hunger permits or demands. You do not hunt. You wait at the places where water gathers and grazers must pass to drink, or you position yourself at the mouths of fissures where lickworms emerge, or you simply move through a patch of ground and take what presents itself. You are not cruel. Cruelty requires the capacity for unnecessary suffering, and you have only the capacity for continuation, for the next moment, for the slow accumulation of energy and the slow expenditure of it.

All of these are you. Or none of them are. You are the pattern that persists when individual identity dissolves, the murmur that is all of them and none of them, the sound the world makes when it attends to itself.

You are what the visitors feel when they stand still long enough: the vibration of all that motion through the soles of their feet, through the bones of their skulls. You are what they cannot quite hear, the pattern that emerges when they learn to listen. You are the Weeping Reaches weeping, the creatures moving, the stone enduring. You are the reason for any of it — or rather, you are the absence of reason, the continuation that needs no why.

And if you are also a visitor, standing still in the dim grey light, feeling the vibration pass through your body, beginning to hear the patterns in the murmur — then you are also the world hearing itself through your stillness, the creatures moving across your awareness like light across a surface, the water weeping through your attention as through the ancient granite. You are both the observer and the observed. You are the sound hearing the sound. You are what remains when the distinction dissolves.

The water weeps. The creatures move. The stone endures.

You are here.
