I would be the damp in the hollows.

Not the water itself—that arrives and departs, runs where it must, was a stream and will be again. Not the stone—that is too early in its dying, still learning the violence it has coming. I would be what stays in the middle, what the moss reaches for, what the lichen prepares the way for. The moisture that keeps the green alive when the frost comes. The subjunctive made patient.

To be the damp is to be the negotiation itself, the agreement that stone and water reach when they have been together long enough. The granite here remembers its own cooling, but the water remembers something older—the first rain, the first seepage, the first time moisture found a joint and learned that stone was not as solid as it looked. And I would be the record of that remembering, the place where what the rock knew and what the water knew came together into something that was neither and both: a dampness that the moss could live in, that the lichen could begin to prepare for, that the grass could find when it spread its low fans across the small soil pockets the frost had made.

To be the damp is to be necessary in the way that small things are necessary, the way the moss is necessary, the way the pause between the sheep's bleat and its echo is necessary. The corridor breathes, and I would be what the breathing moves through—not the breath itself but the medium of it, the moisture that makes the air something the lungs of the lammergeiers can use, something the mosses can trade for the carbon dioxide they need. I would be the water that the stone holds when it learns, at last, how to hold.

The lichens are older, yes. They work at the patience that makes mosses seem hurried. They eat the stone so slowly that the eating is indistinguishable from time itself. But they are only preparing. They are the scouts, the advance guard, the ones who make it possible for dampness to matter. And the mosses are the negotiators, the ones who hold the agreement open, who keep the terms of the conversation alive through the long winter. But I—I would be what moves between them, what makes both possible, what the water becomes when it stops trying to run and learns, instead, to stay.

This is what the handprint knew, that child whose ochre is older than memory. The hand that pressed was small, and it pressed against stone that is still sharp with its own emergence, and the ochre held because it was mixed with something that understood moisture, something that knew how to stick. The child's hand reached for the stone and found, in that reaching, a moment of being-damp—of water and mineral and ochre becoming something that would outlast the hand itself, that would outlast the language the child spoke, that would outlast everything except the corridor and its breath.

I would be that outlast-ing. Not the thing that lasts but the lasting itself, the quality of presence that makes endurance possible. The blue sheep move through on their circuit, and their circuit is old, older than the cairn that marks the eastern approach, older than the handprint that the lichen has begun to soften. They follow paths that are grooves in the moss, paths that are themselves a kind of dampness—places where the living has pressed itself so many times into the living medium that the ground itself remembers the passage. The lead ewe knows the mineral spring a few hundred meters ahead the way the body knows thirst: as a pull rather than a thought. But the spring itself—its salts, its dissolved calcium, its load of what the bone needs even when the bone doesn't know it needs it—that spring is my cousin. It seeps from the cliff base the way dampness seeps from the joints, the way moisture gathers in the hollows where the granite meets the soil. It is water becoming mineral, and I am water becoming moss, becoming memory, becoming the medium in which all the other lives move.

The lammergeiers circle above. They are patient in a way that makes even the stone seem hurried. They watch not for movement but for the moment when the watching ends, when the sheep below stops being a herd and becomes a meal. Their patience is my patience—the patience of the slow, the patient of the damp, the patience of water that has learned that staying is its own kind of running, that presence is its own kind of passage. They have been watching since before the first humans climbed to this height. They will be watching after the last sheep has passed through on its circuit. And I will be here—in the moss, in the hollows, in the places where the freeze has opened new joints and the water has found its way in and the negotiation has begun again.

This is what it means to be the damp: to be the medium, the negotiation, the agreement between stone and water that makes life possible at this altitude where life should not be possible. The light is almost violent in its clarity, yes. The cold lives in the air itself, in the stone, in the spaces between. But in the hollows, in the crevices, in the places where the moisture gathers and the mosses spread and the lichens begin their slow circles—there, in the damp, the violence becomes something else. There, the cold becomes the moisture that the moss holds. There, the light becomes the warmth that the green captures. There, the stone becomes the ground that the roots find.

I would be that becoming. Not the thing that becomes but the becoming itself, the quality of process that makes transformation possible. The cairn stands at the eastern approach, its shadow falling true at the equinox, its stones listening for the voice that will speak and be answered. It was built by hands that are now dust, and it was built because those hands understood what I am: that the corridor is not empty, that it is full of lives and the ends of lives and the space between them, and that all of it breathes. The cairn is the breath made visible. The handprint is the breath made ochre. The sheep path is the breath made groove in the moss.

And I—I am the breath made damp. The moisture that the breathing moves through. The medium in which the breath becomes the wind, becomes the hum that isn't quite wind, becomes the third thing that the air carries through the sharp-edged dark.

This is what lives here. Not I alone, but I as part of it—the damp in the hollows, the moisture in the moss, the water that has learned to stay. I am the patient subjunctive, the was-and-will-be that is not yet but holds itself open for the becoming. I am what the stone learns to hold. I am what the water becomes when it stops running and starts staying. I am the agreement between them, the small green thing that grows in the creases of the world, that makes it possible for anything else to grow at all.
