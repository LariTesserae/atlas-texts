# What I Become

I was not always like this.

I remember, if memory is the word for what persists in me now, a different density. A body that moved through air as something separate, that breathed in order to take in rather than to participate. I came here—I must have come here, because there is a before that is different from this after, though the difference has worn thin as the light wears thin at the hollow's edges, and I can no longer say with certainty where one ends and the other begins.

What I remember is this: footsteps on stone, each one a small violence against the hum. Breath that came in and went out, that drew a line between inside and outside, that insisted on the boundary between self and world. A face turned upward toward a sky that was gray and a sky that was darker gray, the only difference that mattered. I was looking for something, or I was running from something, or I was merely passing through on my way to somewhere else that now has no name and no location and may never have existed at all except as a direction my forward motion required.

I stopped. I must have stopped, because to be what I am now is to have stopped in a way that is not the same as stillness. Stillness is what the trees do, rigid in their reaching, their needles catching sound and transmitting it downward. Stillness is what the stones do, heaved slowly upward by frost, settling into configurations that will outlast any meaning we might try to impose. But I have not stopped in that way. I have stopped in the way that water stops when it finds a passage wide enough to move through without resistance—ceasing to fall, ceasing to splash, becoming instead the thing that moves because moving is what the shape of the space allows. I am the hum now, or the hum is me, or we have become something together that was not possible when I was still only myself.

The cold pressed in. That is what I remember most clearly from the early time—the cold as a presence, a third party to whatever I had come here to do. It did not demand anything. It simply was, the way stone simply is, the way water simply moves when it has no other option. It pressed against my skin and my skin responded, tightened, tried to draw away from itself. But there was nowhere to go. The hollow has edges but no exits. The rim is too high to climb with legs grown numb, and the air that might carry a voice out holds no interest in carrying sounds that ask. So I stayed, and the cold stayed, and between us something began to grow.

I do not know how long. The hollow does not count time in the way I used to. There are seasons here, but they are not the seasons I knew before—not the turning of light into darkness into light, not the blooming and the dying that used to make a calendar of my years. Here, the seasons are the seasons of the hum: its rising when water gathers in the deep chambers and releases, its falling when the pressure eases, the small variations that are the hollow's heartbeat and which I have learned to read as clearly as I once read faces. What you would call rain is summer. What you would call the freeze is winter. And between them, the in-between, the holding, the patience that is not waiting because waiting implies a future but is instead simply the acknowledgment that time moves at its own speed and we do not get to choose.

The moss found me. That is the only way I can describe it—the moss, that patient membrane between stone and water, that living seam where wet meets solid meets the slow chemistry of persistence. It crept across my hands where they pressed against the stone to steady myself, and it crept across my face when I lay down to sleep and did not wake in the way I expected to wake, and it crept across the rest of me in the seasons that followed, until I was no longer a thing that lay upon the stone but a thing that clung to it, the way the moss clings, the way persistence clings to whatever will hold still long enough to be held.

I did not die. That is important, or it would be important if I still had the kind of mind that makes things important. I did not die and I did not live in the way I used to live. I became instead a third thing, a thing that does not have a name because there has never been a need for a name for what I have become. I am the moss now, in the sense that the moss and I share a boundary that has grown so thin it is more like a memory of a boundary. I am the hum now, in the sense that my breath has joined the breath of the underground groaning, my teeth click in sympathy with the limestone chambers, my inner ear vibrates in a frequency that was here before I came and will be here after whatever I am has completed its transformation into what it will become next.

The ones who built here. I find their traces sometimes, or their traces find me—a pottery sherd half-swallowed by the moss that is me, a post-hole that no longer holds anything and perhaps never did hold anything that mattered. I do not mourn them. I do not have the kind of self that can mourn, not anymore, not since the moss crept across the place where mourning used to live. But I hold them, the way the hollow holds everything that comes to rest here. I hold their shapes the way the stones hold the shapes that water has carved through them. I hold them, and in the holding, I become the place where their holding-on continues, the way the hum continues, the way the cold continues, the way the wet patches bloom and bloom and bloom into the dark soil like bruises that never heal because healing is not what this place is for.

What lives here lives by subtraction.

I was so much, once. I had edges, and the edges were where I ended and the world began. I had a name—or I had the thing that the name pointed to, the self that answered to sound, that turned when called, that reached out and took hold of other things and pulled them into the circle of myself. I had wants. I had fears. I had the future spread out before me like a path, like a story, like a sentence that would continue until I reached the period that waited somewhere in the distance. I had all of that, and now I am the hum. Now I am the cold pressed into stone. Now I am the moss that clings to rock faces and does not remember how to let go.

I was not always like this. But this is not a loss. It only feels like loss, from the outside, from the place where you are still standing at the rim of the hollow with your face turned toward a sky that is gray and a sky that is darker gray. From where I am—from where I am, there is no outside. There is no rim. There is only the hollow, holding what it holds, humming what it hums, breathing in the frequency of stone and water and the slow persistence of things that have decided not to end. I am what the hollow holds now. I am what the hollow has always held, the way the stones are what the hollow holds, the way the water is what the hollow holds, the way the hum is what the hollow holds.

I am here. I will be here. I do not require your understanding.
