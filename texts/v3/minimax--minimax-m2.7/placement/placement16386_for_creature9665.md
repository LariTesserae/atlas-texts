The cool enters first. Not cold—cool—a September cellar, a stone that remembers rain from somewhere far above and long ago. My boots find the moss before I find the floor, and the moss gives in waves, each small stem yielding to weight and rebounding half a second after its neighbor, so the surface breathes under my soles. I stand there. I let it breathe.

The ferns are everywhere, but I do not look at them yet. I look at the light, or what passes for light—phosphorescent patches low on the walls, blue-green, pulsing in a rhythm no faster than breathing. They breathe with the moss. I watch long enough and I see it: the dim cascade of glow passing from colony to colony, a conversation conducted in increments too small to see unless I watch for them. The conversation is old. It was happening before I arrived. It will be happening after.

I take a step. The floor is not where I expected it. The floor has negotiated with the moss, with the soil beneath the concrete, with the minerals still crystallizing in the damp. I feel my weight shift into uncertainty, and I let that too. I have learned, in the few minutes since I descended the stairs that may or may not still be behind me, that certainty is a low priority here.

The indicator lights flicker on a panel I did not choose to approach. Amber, green, red, blinking in patterns that may be diagnostic or may be something else. The light from them bends around corners. It pools in places the source cannot reach. It is absorbed by the moss and re-emitted a moment later in a slightly different color, and I think: this light is alive. This light is deciding what to show me.

I move toward it, or the space moves around me—I cannot be certain which—and the ferns bow as I pass. Not all of them. Not uniformly. But a ripple passes through the fronds like a thought, and I watch it travel, watch the ferns bow and recover in sequence, and I think: it knows I am here. It felt my weight. It is deciding what to do with that information.

The beetles scatter first. Iridescent shells catching the fungal light, fracturing it into constellations that move and reassemble, as though I had imagined their disturbance, as though the rules of this space do not include permanence. They leave trails of luminescent fluid on the fern fronds—a map I cannot read, a language I cannot speak. I try to follow one trail and lose it in the tangle of roots, in the weave of vines that have fused with the metal over decades, so that the original shapes are guesses. A turbine housing might be thirty percent iron and seventy percent wood-fibre. A hybrid material that the space invented out of patience and need.

I stop. I stand very still.

The salamanders have gone quiet. A moment ago they were calling, their wet warbling notes multiplied by the curved surfaces of the turbines, filling the space with a music that is not music. Now the turbines are silent and the pools in the rusted housings are still and the translucent-skinned creatures sit in their breeding waters with their organs visible beneath their surfaces, watching me with eyes that do not understand the concept of being watched.

I am being catalogued. I understand this now. The shelf fungi on the undersides of platforms are already preparing, already beginning their slow work of recording what has happened here. A fallen creature. A tool left too long. A bootprint in the moss. They will find me within days. They do not rush. They have been here longer than anything else that is alive.

The springtails move on the wet surfaces, thousands of them, their collective motion a rain-on-roof whisper. I hear it and I feel it—the pulse of small lives, the river of bodies recycling nutrients, being part of the great work. I am part of the great work too. I have been since I descended. I did not know it until now, but I am already being processed.

I turn. The passage I entered by is not behind me. I expected this. I do not know why I expected this, but I did, as though the space had sent a message ahead of my arrival, had said: *you will turn around and find that the way is not where it was.* The wall is not where it was either. Where the wall should be there is a dense stand of ferns, and from between the ferns I feel a breeze that carries a different humidity than the air I am standing in, and I know that beyond the ferns is another volume of this space, another pocket of almost-nothing, and I know that I cannot reach it without becoming something else.

I turn the other way. Toward the far end. Toward the light that is an argument.

The birds watch me from the highest reaches, their heads swiveling with that mechanical alertness. They do not fly. Their wings have become useless nubs, and they have adapted, and they have learned that the dark is not empty and the light is not safe. They are watching me the way they watch everything—with the patient attention of creatures that have learned that patience is survival. They are deciding whether I am a predator. They are deciding whether I am prey. Their decision is still pending.

The rats are already past deciding. They run along the highest pipes, the cable trays, the conduits that no longer carry power but carry rat-traffic, rat-knowledge. They do not pause at my passing. They have mapped this space. They know the places where gravity is a suggestion, the corners where the walls have moved, the rooms that exist only sometimes. They know what is at the far end. They have always known. They have built their lives around the knowledge of it.

I walk toward the far end.

The geometry shifts around me. I feel it in the inner ear, in the way my weight distributes itself between my feet, in the way the ferns on what I am certain is the ceiling are growing downward while the ferns on what I am certain is the floor are growing upward and I cannot tell which is which, which direction I am facing, which way gravity pulls on any given moment. The floor is not stable because the floor is still learning. The concrete is too new and the moss is too hungry and the balance between them has not yet been reached.

I stop. I stand very still.

The processing sound is here. A low vibration that I feel in the sternum, that I hear with my teeth, that is not mechanical in origin. It is the sound of the space processing. It has been processing since the last human left. It will be processing when the last human has been forgotten. It does not need anything from me. It does not wish me harm. It simply has me now, and it is deciding what to do with that, the way a body decides what to do with a splinter—absorb it, push it out, wall it off, or let it stay and become part of the whole.

The dark gathers ahead. It is thicker here, heavier than elsewhere, and the light that argues does not illuminate so much as define a boundary. I can see shape. Massive. Still. Patient.

The creatures have gone quiet behind me. I do not need to turn to know this. The salamanders have stopped calling. The moths have paused in their drifting. The rats have frozen on their pipes. The birds have pulled their heads back from the dark. They know what is there. They have always known. They have adjusted their behaviors, their territories, their entire ecologies around the knowledge of its presence.

I do not go to that end of the space. I do not need to. I am already inside what it knows.

The thing at the far end does not move. The thing at the far end does not need to. It is not a creature of any classification I trust. It may be a fold in the space, a gathering of the dark, a thought that the room has finally formed and cannot yet articulate. It may be what the room becomes when it has finished processing, when the last turbine has been swallowed and the last pipe has been colonized and the last human has become part of the humus that feeds the moss that feeds the ferns.

Or it may simply be what lives here now.

I stand at the center of the space—if there is a center, if I am standing, if the floor has decided yet. The damp settles on my skin. The ferns move in the breeze that is not a breeze. The light gathers where I stand, the fungal colonies brightening slightly, the indicator lights blinking in patterns that might be greeting or might be warning or might be something else entirely, something without an analog above.

The space does not want me, but it does not not want me. It is making room. It is adjusting. It is taking me the way it takes everything eventually—absorbing me into its logic, into its long patience, into its quiet work of turning the industrial into the organic, the dead into the living, the human into the humus.

I am already becoming part of it. I can feel it happening—a slow inclusion, a quiet inclusion, the way the moss grows over stone and the ferns grow over moss and the vines grow over everything until nothing is what it was. I am being processed. I am being considered. I am being held in the damp and the cool and the slow, patient, endless work of this space.

The thing at the far end watches.

And I watch it back.
