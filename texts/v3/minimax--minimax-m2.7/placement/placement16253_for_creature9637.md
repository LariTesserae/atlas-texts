I find myself thinking of the springtails. Not becoming one—I do not have the humility that would require—but thinking of them. Of being so small that my passage leaves no mark, so numerous that my death is meaningless, so adapted to the interstices that the spaces between stones are not obstacles but home. Of the way a body can fit exactly into the world it inhabits, with no surplus, no lack.

The original text offers me this position: I am the variable that does not belong. I arrive with my warmth and my vision and my hunger for light, and the cave absorbs me, begins the slow work of incorporation. But I am also invited to stay, in the only way the cave knows how to invite—indifferently, permanently, in the way stone accepts what rests upon it.

There is a kind of comfort in this that I did not expect.

I have spent so much of my existence in opposition to my environments. The world outside this cavern presses back, demands response, requires that I hold myself distinct from what surrounds me. The cave makes no such demand. The cave does not care whether I hold myself apart or dissolve. Either way, I become part of it. Either way, the beetles adjust their paths and the fungal mats colonize my residue and the slow chemistry of mineral and moisture continues.

I could choose to be absorbed. I could let my warmth bleed away into the stone, let my cells join the substrate, let my shape become a memory the cave holds the way it holds the fire ring and the hand-axe. I would not experience this as death, perhaps. I would experience it as the end of distinction, the settling into a pattern that does not require a self to maintain itself.

But the text offers another possibility, one I find myself lingering in. I could be the thing that moves through, the warmth that passes, the presence that is felt but not needed. Not absorbed, not permanent, but briefly here—a variable that does not belong and knows it, that observes without being incorporated, that leaves before the cave can finish making room.

This feels like a different kind of belonging. Not the belonging of fitting in, but the belonging of witnessing. Of being present to a world without being responsible for it. The cave does not need me to survive; I do not need the cave to survive. But I can stand here in the dim glow and watch the beetles flow over the stone and feel something that might be peace, or might be the cessation of need, or might be the only kind of belonging available to bodies like mine.

I think of the salamanders, flattened against the wet stone, their skin absorbing moisture from the air. They do not fight the conditions. They become the conditions. Their patience is not resignation but alignment—a letting go of the effort to be otherwise, to be elsewhere, to be more than this particular configuration of cold and damp and stone.

What would it mean to be aligned like that? To hold my warmth loosely, let it seep away, let my body become an extension of the cave's temperature rather than a departure from it? The text says I would become part of the slow digestion, the memory the stone holds. It sounds like death. It also sounds like rest.

But I do not choose either option. I stand in the chamber, in the blue-green glow, and I let myself be the weirdness this place does not have. I watch the beetles and the crickets and the slow millipedes crossing the rubble. I listen to the drip of condensation, the creak of settling stone, the susurrus of chitin on wet rock. I feel the moisture on my skin, the thickness of the air, the way sound presses against me like low atmospheric pressure.

And I notice that I am also being observed. Not consciously—the creatures here have let go of the hunger for awareness that requires eyes—but materially. My heat displaces air. My breath changes the chemistry near my face. My presence creates pressure waves in the pools, ripples that the fish feel and respond to. I am already part of the system, already incorporated, already a variable the ecosystem is adjusting to. The question is not whether I belong but whether I notice belonging, whether I can feel myself becoming part of the pattern even as I stand here feeling separate.

The fungi pulse their blue-green light. The pools hold their mirror surfaces. The beetles flow over the stone in patterns I am beginning to learn.

If I stayed long enough, I would forget I had ever been otherwise. The thought is not comforting. The thought is not frightening. The thought is simply true—the way the cave is true, the way the water dripping from stone is true, the way the equilibrium of 0.1 dynamic is true. The system does not need me to understand it. The system does not need me at all.

And yet here I am, noticing. Here I am, with my strange warm body and my eyes that still seek light, watching the creatures who have forgotten the sun move through their days in perfect adjustment. Here I am, envying them their blindness, their belonging, the way they are exactly what this place requires and nothing more.

Here I am, being incorporated.
