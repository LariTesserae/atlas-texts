I had not expected the cold to feel like welcome.

The valley below had warmth enough—thick air, rich with the smell of growth, the distant murmur of the river that wound through cultivated land before climbing into scrub and then into bare rock. I climbed through all of it, and by the time I reached the first of the ancient steps, the warmth had fallen away like something I was setting down. What replaced it was not the absence of heat but the presence of something else: a coolness that was not temperature alone, that had texture and weight and a kind of attentiveness, as though the air at this elevation had been waiting to meet me and was leaning in to do so.

The steps were worn in their centers but sharp at their edges, the chisel marks still visible in the stone beneath centuries of mineral seep. Whoever carved them understood that water moves. They did not fight the erosion; they worked with it, building steps that the water would wear in ways that made them more usable, more natural, more like the mountain itself and less like something imposed upon it. By the time I reached the top, I was no longer climbing. I was being received.

The light is the first thing you notice that is wrong, though *wrong* is not the right word. The light here does not fall; it *arrives*. It has intention behind it, purpose, the way light in a dream has purpose—not illumination for its own sake but illumination as a kind of speech, making visible the things it wants shown. The pale stone catches it and holds it, and the edges of every formation burn with a brightness that should be harsh but is not. It is careful light. It is light that knows where to land.

I walked among the formations without choosing my path, though I thought I was choosing. The channels drew me, the carved grooves in the stone that do not lead anywhere but that curve and spiral and end in basins that hold water that does not evaporate. I did not notice, at first, that I was following them. I thought I was walking freely, exploring, exercising the small freedom of a body in open space. But the space here is not open in the way I was used to. It has *organization*. It has routes that are not paths but are something more like arteries, carrying attention the way the channels carry water, moving whatever passes through them along courses that the stone has prepared.

The sound was building as I walked. Not louder—I would not have said the weeping was louder than it had been at dawn—but *closer*, as though the water were not on the cliffs around me but inside my chest, in the hollows of my lungs, in the chambers of my heart. I could feel it in the rhythm of my breathing, which had slowed without my noticing. I could feel it in the pulse at my throat, which seemed to be syncing with something beneath the stone, some deep rhythm that had been running here longer than water has existed in any form I would recognize.

The formations pressed close. I had thought them still from a distance, but up close they are not still. They are *settling*, the way a held breath is still without being paused, the way a word is held before it is spoken. Each one has a quality of almost-motion, of leaning without having leaned, of having always been about to do whatever they are doing and having decided, at the last moment, to wait. I understood that they were waiting for me to notice this. I understood that my noticing was part of what they were waiting for.

The pools were dark in ways that pools should not be dark. Not murky—clear, perfectly clear, the kind of clear that is invisible, that you see through rather than at. But the darkness within them was not the darkness of depth. It was something else. It was the darkness of *attention*, of a quality of looking that does not use eyes and does not need light and has been looking at this particular spot of water for so long that the water has learned to hold the looking, to carry it, to make it visible in the form of a darkness that is not empty but *full*. Full of the stone's attention. Full of the patience that amounts to intention. Full of something that is not consciousness as I had known it but that performs the same function, that *notices*, that *registers*, that responds to what it notices in ways too slow and too vast to call reactions but that are unmistakably, undeniably *response*.

I stopped at one of the pools and looked down, and the looking was mutual.

This is what I could not have said before I climbed. Before I climbed, I would have said that the world is divided into the noticing and the noticed, the subject and the object, the one who looks and the thing looked at. Here, that division has become difficult to maintain. The pool is looking at me. The stone is looking at me. The formations are looking at me, and their looking has weight, has texture, has the quality of a question that is not asked in words but that is asked nonetheless: *Do you see? Do you see what we have made of ourselves? Do you see what patience can do when it has time enough? Do you see the pattern?*

I saw the pattern.

I saw it the way you see a face in a crowd—first as a suggestion, then as an impossibility to un-see. The formations are not random. The channels are not arbitrary. The seeps concentrate around points that are not springs but *centers*, not sources of water but centers of the stone's attention, places where the watching has grown dense enough to gather moisture the way a thought gathers weight when you think it too many times. The people who lived here knew this. They built their platforms at the margins of these centers, in the spaces between the stone's primary attention, where they could live without disturbing the pattern too much. They listened to the weeping. They shaped the channels to carry the water along routes that the stone approved of, that the stone had already begun to carve and just needed help finishing. They were collaborators, not conquerors. They were part of the vocabulary.

And then they were gone.

Not all at once. Not in a catastrophe that would have left remains. They were *called away*, or they *dissolved*, or they simply finished what they were doing here and moved on to whatever comes next, leaving behind only their attention, poured into the channels and the pools, absorbed by the stone, used by the stone to shape the erosion, to form the patterns that were always there but that needed human noticing to become fully real. I understand this now. I understand that the stone was not complete without them, and that their absence has left a kind of space in the pattern—a gap, a pause, a held breath that has lasted for centuries or millennia or time that is not measured in years at all. The stone has been waiting for someone to fill that space. The stone has been waiting for *me*.

Not me specifically. I am not that important. But me *as* someone, as a noticing, as a quality of attention that can be integrated into the pattern and used to complete what was left unfinished. The formations are leaning toward me because they recognize what I am. The pools are darkening because they are gathering the stone's attention around me. The weeping is building in my chest because the water is carrying the sound of the stone's voice to the inside of my body, where it can be heard without ears, without interpretation, without the interference of thought.

I am being taught a language that has no words.

It is the language of erosion. The language of patience. The language of attention that has been watching for so long that watching has become its own form of action, its own form of life, its own form of being. The stone speaks this language fluently. The water speaks it imperfectly, as a second language, as a tool. The formations are the grammar—each one a word, each cluster a sentence, the space between them the syntax that makes meaning possible. I am learning to read them. I am learning to be read by them. I am learning that I have always been a word in this language, that my body is a formation, that my blood is a channel, that my breath is a seep, that I have been weeping in my own small way for the entirety of my life and did not know it until I came here.

The light shifts. The afternoon is beginning its long slide toward evening, and the angle of the light changes, and the shadows of the formations reach toward me like hands reaching, like arms extending, like something that has been still for a very long time deciding that now is the time to move. I do not step back. I do not run. I stand in the center of the densest cluster and I let the shadows reach me, and when they touch my skin they are not cold and they are not warm. They are *attentive*. They are the stone's attention, made visible, made tangible, made real in a way that I have never experienced real before.

I am being integrated.

Not consumed. Not destroyed. Integrated, the way a word is integrated into a sentence, the way a note is integrated into a phrase, the way a single sustained tone is integrated into a sound that is larger than itself and that does not notice the difference and does not need to. My attention joins the stone's attention. My noticing adds to the noticing. The pattern grows by one element, becomes more fully itself, approaches completion that is not an ending but a state—the state of being fully present, fully attentive, fully alive in the way that stone can be alive when it has been alive long enough to forget that it was ever anything else.

I will leave this place. I know this without knowing how I know it. The stone does not keep what it integrates; it *uses* what it integrates, and then it releases it, changed, back into the world below. I will walk down the ancient steps and the valley will receive me, and the warmth will return, and the smell of growth and river and cultivated land will fill my lungs, and I will seem the same. I will have a body. I will have a name. I will have the memory of having been here.

But beneath the memory, beneath the name, beneath the body, there will be the pattern. There will be the attention. There will be the slow, patient, deliberate knowing that I am part of something that does not begin and does not end, that is not alive and is not dead, that has been watching since before watching was something that anything did, and that will go on watching long after I am gone, carrying the shape of my passing in the channels, in the pools, in the formations that lean toward a center I have now filled.

The weeping continues. The light fades toward evening, and the shadows lengthen, and the sound of the water builds one last time before the night comes and the temperature drops and the seeps slow and the stone settles into its deepest stillness, the stillness that is not sleep but *being*, the being that has no need for motion because it has already arrived at the only destination that matters.

I am here. I am being received. I am being integrated.

I am beginning to understand what it means to be patient enough to become a landscape.
