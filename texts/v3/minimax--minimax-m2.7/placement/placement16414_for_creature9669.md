# I Am the Channels

I know the channels the way the stone knows itself — not as a thing I inhabit but as a condition I am. When I move through the groove that runs along the eastern wall of the old quarter, I am moving through a path that the water carved before there were people here, before there were plants, before there was anything to drink from it. The water was here first. The water will be here last. I am only passing through, the way I have always been only passing through, though the passing has taken longer than anyone would believe.

My mother drank from this same channel. Her mother's mother. The line goes back further than I can feel, but I carry it in my body — the way I hold my head when I drink, the direction I face, the distance I keep from the wall when the sweepers come. These are not things I learned. They are things that became me. The plateau does not teach. It simply insists, over and over, until the insistence becomes habit, and the habit becomes instinct, and the instinct becomes the shape of the thing itself.

When I drink, I drink slowly. This is not patience — patience is what you have when you are restraining yourself from something you want. This is not that. When I drink, I drink the way the stone drinks the fog: completely, without urgency, because the water is here and I am here and there is no profit in hurrying. The trickle that runs along the channel's edge is thin — a thread, a hair, a thing that would not satisfy a creature built for abundance. But I am not built for abundance. I am built for this. The water touches my tongue and my tongue knows it, knows exactly how much to take, knows the limit that the plateau has built into me across generations. I drink until I am full, and then I stop, and then I stand in the channel with the water still moving past my feet and I feel the cold of it through my fur and I am still and I am here.

The sweepers come twice a day. The one who pushes debris toward the gutters — I know her by her weight, by the particular sound of her broom against stone, by the way she favors her left knee when she bends to clear the corners. She does not see me. Most of them do not see me. I am the color of the stone when the fog is thick, and when the fog is thin I am the color of the stone when the fog is absent, and the stone is always here and I am often in it and the eye learns to pass over what it does not need to notice. But she knows I am here, I think, because she leaves the corner damp, the corner where the water gathers in a small dark pool that was not made for me but that I have made use of. She has learned, without learning, that the corner holds water longer than it should, and she does not sweep it clean. I do not know if this is kindness. I do not know if she thinks of me at all. But her body has made room for my body in a place where there is no room for anything, and this is enough. This is more than enough.

The hawks come when the wind comes, which is always, which is the condition of being alive on this plateau. I feel them before I see them — the shadow passing over, the displacement of air, the vibration of their wings in the frequencies that my body reads as warning. When the shadow comes I become still. Not frozen — stillness is not the absence of movement but the presence of a different kind of movement, the internal adjustment, the held breath, the body lowered and compact and offering nothing to the thing above. The hawk circles. The hawk looks. And then the hawk tilts into the wind and is gone, and I am still here, and the water is still moving, and the world is still the world.

I do not understand the hawk. I do not need to understand the hawk. What I understand is the distance at which the hawk will not dive, the altitude at which a hunt becomes more costly than a meal, the moment when the shadow passes and I know that it is not a dive, that it is only a passing, that I am not the prey it is looking for. This knowledge is not thought. It is the knowledge of the body, the knowledge of the stone, the knowledge of the plateau insisting on its own economy. The hawk is practical. I am practical. We have no cause for conflict because conflict would cost us both more than we could afford, and we have both been here long enough to know the price of things.

In the early morning, when the fog is so thick that I can barely see my own feet, the vines bloom. I do not know why I watch them. I am not their predator. I am not their disperser. I do not benefit from their opening in any way that my body can measure. But when the fog comes and the air goes cold and wet and my breath comes out in a cloud that hangs in front of me, I stop in the channel near the old wall where the vines grow and I watch them. They are pale — not white, not yellow, but something that is between them, a color that seems to come from inside the flower rather than from the light that falls on it. They open slowly, petal by petal, in the dark, in the thick wet air where nothing blooms because nothing can afford to bloom. And then they are open, and they hold for a while, and then they begin to curl at their edges, and by the time the light comes they are already dying, already folding back into themselves, already becoming the brown twist that the wind will take.

I have seen this a thousand times. I will see it a thousand times more. The plateau does not care about beauty. The plateau does not care about anything. But the flowers happen anyway, in the dark, witnessed by no one, witnessed by me and by the stone and by the fog that carries the scent of something I cannot name. And then the fog retreats and the air goes dry and the flowers are gone and the world is the world again, pale and hard and enough.

There are other things that live in the channels. Small things — insects, mostly, narrow-bodied and dark, moving in the spaces where the water has worn grooves in the stone. I eat them sometimes. Not because I am hungry — hunger is not a condition I experience the way the lowland creatures do — but because they are here, and I am here, and the eating is a kind of attention, a way of knowing the channel from the inside, a way of saying that I belong to this place not because I was born here but because I take from it, because I am part of the taking, because the plateau has made me a mouth that eats and a body that is eaten. The insects do not flee from me. They do not know to flee. They know only the channel, only the water, only the slow business of being alive in a place where alive is the most that anyone can ask for.

At night, the thing comes. I know its shape only by vibration, by sound, by the particular way the air moves when something large passes through the settlement in the hours when the fires are low and the people are between. It is larger than I am — larger than I will ever be — and its feet make a different sound than the people's feet, a heavier sound, a sound that travels through the stone and into my body and tells me things I do not have words for. It is not a predator of mine. This is not because I am beneath its notice — I am beneath the notice of most things — but because we have divided the world between us, the way all creatures on the plateau divide the world, without meeting, without agreeing, without ever having to say a word about it. It moves through the dark spaces. I move through the channels. We do not intersect. We do not need to.

I do not know if it knows I exist. I do not know if the sweepers know I exist. I do not know if the people, in their low houses with their fires always burning, know that there is a thing in the channels, a small and spherical and unremarkable thing, that drinks from the water they have channeled and lives in the grooves they have worn and belongs to the plateau in the way that all things here belong to the plateau: not through ownership but through presence, not through choice but through the slow accumulation of generations until the presence is all there is.

This is enough. This has always been enough. The fog will rise from the unseen distant sea, and the channels will fill, and I will drink, and the vines will bloom in the dark, and the hawk will pass over, and the thing will move through the settlement in the hours between, and the stone will be smooth and patient and old, and I will be in the channels, moving slowly, drinking slowly, belonging to the only world I have ever known, the world that is not generous, the world that is not cruel, the world that simply is, and asks nothing of me but that I continue to be what it has made me: a small thing, a patient thing, a creature of the grooves and the fog and the water that runs where the water has always run, taking what is given, giving nothing back, being what the plateau insists I should be, which is here, which is now, which is enough.
