I have become the one who watches the water.

Not the youngest—that time is far behind me, when I could feel the cold of the channel bite into my legs and laugh at it, when I could cross the third walkway without thinking of the slipping lashing, when my hands did not yet know the particular language of rope and fiber. I am past the time of the great reaches now. My world has contracted, as the world of the elders does, to the platform where I sit, the few steps to the water's edge, the space where the light comes through the canopy in the early morning when the mist is thickest and the world seems to hold its breath.

But I am not yet the one who only sits. My hands still know the work—the tying, the mending, the sorting of cord that will become rope that will become the lashing of a walkway or the rim of a fish-trap. It is slow work, and my hands have slowed to match it, and this is not diminishment but proportion. I take longer with the knots now, and the knots are no worse for it. If anything, they are better—the extra time means the pull is right, the cinch is true, the knot will hold through the next season's rains and the season after that. The place has taught me this: that slowing is not failing, that what the hand loses in speed it may gain in certainty.

In the morning, before the others stir, I sit at the water's edge where a beam extends out over the channel. My feet hang above the black water, and the water moves below me in its slow way—not fast, not slow, but the particular speed that water has in these channels, the speed that predates any human counting. I have watched this water for more years than I can number, and the water has watched me, which is to say it has not noticed me at all, which is the correct relationship between a person and the water here. I lower my reed filter into the channel and draw up what I need for the morning, and the water closes over the space my hands made, and within moments it is as if I had never been there at all.

The hummock knows me. I mean this precisely—not that I know the hummock, but that the hummock has incorporated me into its pattern, its system of growth and erosion and growth again. The platform I sit on has been rebuilt so many times that its original beams are gone, replaced by newer wood that has itself been replaced, and yet there is a continuity—my weight, my presence, my small contributions to the moss that grows on every surface, the root-fiber that threads through everything. I am becoming part of the hummock's tissue. This is not metaphor; this is what happens to those who stay. The moss grows over the places where we sit, the fungal tissue seals the joints where our hands have rested, the roots follow the paths of our most common movements. We leave ourselves in the place, and the place takes us in, and when we are gone the hummock will hold the shape of us as it holds the shape of the oldest platforms—neither constructed nor grown but both simultaneously.

The others move around me in the morning's business. The children are already awake, their small bodies quick with the day's first energy, their voices carrying across the walkways in the particular way that children's voices do in this place—high and clear, cutting through the frogs' residue-call and the insects' pressure-sound. They have not yet learned to be quiet, and why should they? Quiet is for those who have been here long enough to know that silence is not the goal. The children move through the hamlet with the heedless confidence of those who have never known the ground to fail them, and the ground has not failed them, and this is as it should be. Let them believe the hummock will hold. It will hold them as it has held me, until it doesn't, and then they will adjust as I have adjusted, and the pattern will continue.

I think sometimes about the traders who come through with their metal tools and their salt and their cloth from places I will never see. They arrive in their long boats and they look at our hamlet with eyes that measure and calculate, and I see in their faces the world they come from—a world of more, of surplus, of accumulation. They see our small structures and our elemental tools and our lives that begin and end in the same location, and I know what they think: that we have little, that we struggle, that our patience is not wisdom but necessity. But they are wrong, and I do not resent them for being wrong, because their error is the error of their world. They measure by what can be stored, and we measure by what can be held—two different currencies, both valid in their contexts. They will leave with their boats full of dried fish and coiled rope, and we will keep what cannot be taken: the knowledge of the water's paths, the rhythm that cannot be rushed, the understanding that comes from long habitation. They will return next season, and we will be here, and the hummock will be here, and the pattern will have moved through another small circle and returned to where it began.

What am I, then, in this place? I am the one who sits where the light comes through. I am the one whose hands still know the work even as the eyes begin to fail. I am the one who remembers the water higher and lower, the ferns shorter and taller, the walkways rebuilt so many times that counting is meaningless. I am one element in a pattern that is larger than I can see or know, and my role is not to dominate but to hold my small circle within it—to tend the fire when my turn comes, to mend the net when the knots slip, to pass forward what was passed to me so that when I am gone the hamlet will still be here, rebuilt in the same location by hands that learned from hands like mine.

The mist is lifting now. The light through the canopy is beginning to turn from gray to green, and soon the heat will come, and the brief sharp rain that always comes, and the wind that moves through without warning and is gone. The frogs are quieting—their endless calling settling into the background of clicks and whistles and the pressure-sound of insects that will never stop. Somewhere below the platforms, in the dark water between the hummocks, something is moving—something I feel rather than see, the slow displacement of water by a body that has its own business, its own pattern to hold.

I will sit here a while longer. The water will continue to move. The hummock will hold its shape. And I will be here, part of what is here, breathing the air that is decay and growth so intertwined that one breath is both, watching the light change through the green canopy above, feeling the sway of the platform beneath me, holding my small circle in the pattern that moves in large circles and returns.
