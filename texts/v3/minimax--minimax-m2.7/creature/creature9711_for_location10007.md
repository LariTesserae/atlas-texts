The fungi are older than the sinkhole. Or rather, they are the same age as the sinkhole's dampness, and the dampness is older than the limestone as it now exists — the limestone having dissolved and redeposited itself in slow mineral prayers around the presence of water. The fungi arrived as spores on the feet of birds or in the gut of insects, and they found conditions that suited them: consistent humidity, stone that held moisture rather than shedding it, darkness that was not total. They did not choose this place. They simply persisted in it until persistence became colonization, and colonization became the soft glow that has been there, unchanging in its changing, longer than any memory of the surface could reach.

Their light is not consistent. Watch them long enough and you will see them pulse — a slow brightening and dimming that takes minutes, perhaps longer, as if each cluster is breathing with its own private lungs. The green ones run slightly warm. The blue ones stay cool. When the sun column swings across the far wall and brightens the sinkhole to something almost harsh, the fungi dim in response, conserving what energy they gather from the faint reflected light and the ambient warmth, holding themselves at a glow that seems designed not to compete. But when clouds pass over and the mouth of the sinkhole dims to grey, the fungi brighten — a shift so gradual you might not notice it until you realize the walls have gone from lit to luminous, from reflecting to producing, that the sinkhole has become what it truly is: a place that makes its own light because the world above it cannot be trusted to provide.

The moths do not feed on the fungi. This is important. They are drawn to the light the way moths are always drawn to light — that ancient, unexplained compulsion that may be navigation or confusion or some deeper relationship with photons that their small nervous systems cannot distinguish from home. But they do not eat the fungi. The fungi are not food. They are orientation. They are the fixed points around which the moths organize their brief evenings, rising from the crevices where they have waited through the bright hours in something like sleep, circling the clusters in spirals that tighten and release without pattern, their wings catching the green and blue light and throwing it back in muted iridescence.

There are perhaps thirty of them on a quiet night. Perhaps more. They are difficult to count because they do not stay still, and the light makes estimation unreliable — is that one moth or two, or the same moth moved? Their wings are not uniform. Some are pale grey with a single green stripe that runs from body to tip, matching the brighter lichen that grows alongside the fungi. Others are a dusty olive that disappears entirely against the stone when they rest, so that a moth at rest is nearly invisible unless you are looking for the slight movement of antennae or the way the wings catch the light at a particular angle. The oldest ones — you can tell them by the fraying at the wing-edges, the way the patterns have faded to near-uniformity — tend to stay low, near the damp seams where the fungi cluster thickest. The younger ones spiral higher, reaching toward the mouth of the sinkhole and the fading memory of daylight.

They live for two days. Perhaps three. Long enough to mate in the humid air above the pool, laying eggs in the wet stone where the larvae will later emerge to feed on decomposing lichen and the rich mineral dust that settles in the crevices. Their eggs are pale and nearly translucent, stuck to the rock in clusters of a dozen or more, and if you look closely at the dampest ledges you can find them — old eggs that have hatched and new eggs that are still waiting, a timeline of small continuations laid out on the stone like a promise no one made.

The pool has its own population.

The many-legged thing is a cave millipede, though you would not call it that if you saw it — it is pale and almost translucent, its segments soft and poorly armored in the way of creatures that have never needed a hard shell, because nothing here hunts it. It moves slowly through the wet stone, feeding on fungal mats and the bacterial slime that coats the underwater rocks. It is not afraid of the dark; it is afraid of the light, retreating from the faint glow of the fungi into deeper crevices where the water seeps cold and complete. You will not see it unless you sit very still at the pool's edge and wait for the water to settle and the light to dim and your eyes to adjust to the dark that is not quite dark. Then, if you are patient, you may see it: a slow undulation at the edge of the water, a segment of pale grey moving through the black.

Springtails live in the soil of the sinkhole floor — tiny, pale insects that owe their name to a forked appendage beneath their abdomen that allows them to spring away from predators. They are everywhere in the loam, so small that a hundred of them could fit on your thumbnail, so numerous that their collective movement sounds like a faint rain against the decomposing matter when you press your ear to the ground. They eat the fungi, in a sense — the spores, the bacterial films, the fine organic matter that gathers in the damp. They are the recyclers, the ones who take what the sinkhole has made and return it to the soil, feeding the ferns that feed the moths that die and feed the soil that feeds the springtails. The cycle is not closed; it is a spiral, tightening and releasing across time that does not care whether it is understood.

The ferns are the slowest things here. Not in their growth — they grow at the speed of ferns, which is to say, imperceptibly to any single observer — but in their patience. They arrived as spores on the wind, or perhaps were deposited by birds, and they found the thin soil of the ledges and they stayed. Their fronds unfurl in the humid air, each one a miniature recapitulation of every fern that has ever grown in this sinkhole, the same shape, the same pale green, the same slight curl at the edges. They do not flower. They do not fruit. They simply exist, adding a single layer of growth each year that you cannot see, cannot measure, but that over decades makes them thick and tangled at the base, a small mass of accumulated fern that catches the fungal light and holds it, casting tiny shadows on the stone behind them.

Some of them die. The ones at the lowest ledges, where the water pools in the heavy rains, rot from the roots up when the seepage becomes a flow. You can see them — pale brown fronds hanging limp, collapsing into the soil that fed them, returning to the loam that will feed their successors. The sinkhole does not mourn them. The sinkhole does not notice. But the gap they leave is filled within a season by another fern, another frond, another small patient thing that will grow for as long as the conditions allow and no longer.

The air moves. You might not feel it — the sinkhole is sheltered from wind, the walls too deep and curved to channel anything but the faintest draft from above — but it moves nonetheless, a slow convection driven by the difference in temperature between the cool dampness of the depths and the warmer, drier air of the surface. At night, when the fungal light is the only light, the air rises from the pool and spreads across the walls and thins as it climbs, and the humidity follows it, and the smell of mineral and green things and buried sulfur rises with it, filling your lungs with the chemistry of the place. You breathe in what the sinkhole breathes out. You become, for a moment, part of the exchange.

The light is the thing. Not the sun column — though it is beautiful, that rotating theater, the way it paints the far wall in gold and then white and then gold again as the hours pass — but the fungal light. The light that does not move. The light that persists.

It has been here since the sinkhole was young enough to be something else, a crack in the earth that had not yet learned to be a wound. It has outlasted whatever ancient forests grew on this plateau, whatever megafauna drank from the pools that were deeper then, whatever brief human presence may have passed above and never known what lay beneath. It does not know it has outlasted anything. It simply glows, a dim green-blue radiance that the moths circle and the ferns orient toward and the springtails ignore because they are too small to see it as anything but a faint warming of the air.

At night, in the sinkhole, you are surrounded by light from above and below. The sun column is gone, but the moon, when it passes, sends a faint grey glow down the throat of the hole, barely enough to see by, but enough to remind you that there is still a sky. The fungi glow brighter in response, as if greeting the moon, as if the moon is an older, fainter version of themselves. And the moths rise, and the air fills with their wingbeat-sound, and the pool goes still as still can be, and the sinkhole becomes something more than a wound in the earth.

It becomes a room.

It becomes a place that holds light in the dark.
