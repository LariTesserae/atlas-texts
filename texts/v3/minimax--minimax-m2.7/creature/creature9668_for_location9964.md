The bead grows warm against your thigh. You notice this as noticing everything else in here — passively, without the friction of surprise. It has been warm since you put it in your pocket, warm the way stone gets warm in sunlight, but there is no sunlight here, and it has not cooled. You wonder about its age. The wear on its surfaces suggests handling, suggests handling over time, suggests a span of time that would have required the bead to survive in this environment for generations of whatever hands held it. But stone does not preserve well in this damp. The calcium in your fingerprints will leave their mark on it. You are changing it by carrying it, the way the condensation changes the walls, the way everything here changes everything else by the simple fact of presence.

You find another. Three meters further, in a crack so tight you only notice it because your sleeve catches: a flake of worked stone, sharp-edged, a cutting-tool fragment. The edges are still acute. It has not had time to wear round. But the crack it sits in is old, mineral-deposited, the stone on either side smoothed by water that has long since moved on. The tool is old. The place it landed is old. But the tool itself seems recent. You set it down. You keep the bead.

The contradiction should bother you more than it does.

Later — or before, or beside — you sit. You don't remember deciding to sit. Your legs made the decision or the stone made the decision or some interval of time inserted itself between two moments and you have lost the decision to it. You are sitting on a flat outcropping that juts from the wall at hip height, facing a corridor that slopes away into the mineral light. The outcropping is worn smooth on top. Not by water — the texture is too even, the polish too consistent. Worn by something sitting here. By many somethings. By repetition over time. Something has sat on this exact spot and looked down this exact corridor. You do not know how long ago. You are not certain the distance matters.

The fauna moves in front of you. Hundreds of bodies, thousands. The iridescent arthropods flow like a slow river along the base of the wall, their carapaces catching the light at angles that shift from green to gold to a pale orange that matches the dust on your fingers. They are not fleeing you. They were here before you sat down. They will be here after you leave. The flow is dense at the wall's base where moisture seeps through the limestone joint and the mineral accretion grows thickest — pale threads climbing the stone in fractal patterns, feeding something or fed by something, supporting a food chain you cannot see but can feel in the way the smallest creatures cluster and the medium creatures feed on the smallest and the shadows in the corners of your vision suggest things that feed on the medium.

A clicking builds. Not one click but many, thousands, a percussive layer that rises from the floor where you cannot see individual bodies, the sound of mandibles working or carapaces adjusting or some behavior too collective to parse into individual causes. It peaks — holds — and releases into a lower register, a wash of sound that recedes into the deeper passages. The fauna breathes. You have been listening to it breathe.

You stand. The outcropping is cold where your body warmed it. You have been sitting long enough for that. You do not know how long that is. You check the bead. Still warm.

The corridor slopes downward. You follow it because it is there, because the alternative is to stay or go back, and staying means sitting on another worn stone and listening to another click-build-release of collective fauna breath, and going back means crossing ground you have already crossed without having learned anything new from the crossing. The slope steepens. The walls close in. The luminescence fades to a memory of itself, a warmth rather than a light, and then to nothing, and you are walking through a space defined only by the sound of your own movement and the low pulse that never quite stops and the sensation of stone on every side, near enough to touch if you reached out, far enough that the space continues beyond your fingertips.

You feel the air move before you see the light.

It comes from below and to the left: a draft that carries the smell of deeper damp, mineral and cold, a draft that moves against the ambient warmth the way a river moves against a lake's stillness. You follow it without deciding to. The light returns — faint at first, a blush on the stone, a suggestion rather than an illumination — and then stronger, filtering through a gap in the floor ahead where the limestone has fractured along a fault-line and dropped, exposing a lower level. The fracture is recent. The edges are sharp. The stone is still pale underneath, calcium-bright, unoxidized. Something moved through this space recently enough to break it, or the space broke itself, or the space is still breaking.

You look down.

The light below is different. Not the warm pale glow of the upper passages but something cooler, greener, filtering up through water that has pooled in the fracture and spread into a shallow lake that fills a chamber larger than any you have seen. The lake's surface is still. It does not ripple at your presence. The light from below — bioluminescence, some mineral reaction, something alive or something decaying into light — moves beneath the surface in slow patterns, a network of pale green veins spreading across the chamber floor, converging on something at the center that pulses. Not a pulse like a heartbeat. Slower. Geological. The slow pulse of something growing or the slow pulse of something breathing at a scale where heartbeats are irrelevant.

You crouch at the fracture's edge. The stone bites into your palms where you brace yourself. Below, the lake is perhaps ten meters across and irregular, its edges lost in shadow where the walls meet the water. The pale green veins are not evenly distributed — they cluster, thicken, follow lines that might be cracks in the floor or channels deliberately cut or the paths of water that have worn their own routes through the mineral bed. At the center, the convergence: a shape that is almost recognizable. Columnar. Vertical. Rising from the lake floor to break the surface and then rise further, into the air that is not air, into the space above the water. It is stone but it is not only stone. The surface has texture — grooves, ridges, a topology that suggests shaping or growth or both. It is the largest shaped thing you have found in this place. It may be the thing that shaped the others.

The pulse comes from it. Slow. Rhythmic. The pulse you have been hearing since you descended.

You let go of the stone's edge. Your palms are orange-dusted. The pulse continues below, unchanged by your attention or your withdrawal. You could climb down. The fracture is wide enough, the walls rough enough, the descent navigable if you chose it. You could reach the lake. You could touch the thing at the center, the column, the shape that pulses with geological slowness. You could learn what it is.

You step back from the edge.

The sound behind you has changed. You did not notice it changing — it changed the way everything here changes, by degrees, by accumulation, by the time you spend not paying attention to the thing that is changing and the longer time you spend paying attention only after the change has already completed. But the sound behind you is different now. Not louder, not softer, not a different frequency. Changed in character. The clicking is still there. The skittering is still there. The low pulse from below is still there. But the layered percussion has developed a coherence, a pattern, a rhythm that aligns with your footsteps as you move away from the fracture. You stop. The pattern stutters. You step forward. It resumes. You stop again. It fades.

The fauna is listening to you. The fauna has always been listening to you.

You walk. The sound follows for a time, and then it does not, and then it is just the general sound of the place, the fauna-turbulence, the click-skitter-pulse that fills every hollow and every passage and every space where stone meets air meets moisture meets the heat that has no source. The bead in your pocket is warm. You are walking now without destination, crossing stone that was formed before anything moved through it and will continue to form after you leave it, passing fauna that has no interest in you except the interest of presence, following no path because there are no paths, because this place does not want to be navigated.

It only wants to be.

You find another structure. This one is different — not a platform or a channel or a spiral of stones but a wall. A partial wall, perhaps two meters long, built from flat stones fitted together with a precision that suggests planning, a human or human-adjacent intelligence that understood the concept of courses, of laying stone upon stone to create a barrier where no barrier was needed. The wall is old. The joints have mineralized almost completely, the gaps filled with secondary deposits that make the wall nearly seamless, a single formation rather than a collection of pieces. But not quite seamless. The gaps remain, thin as paper in places, and through the gaps you can see — nothing. A darkness that is darker than the surrounding dark. A space behind the wall that is not part of the general space, that is separated, contained, held apart.

You press your eye to the largest gap.

The darkness moves. Not a shimmer, not a trick of light that isn't there. Movement. Something large shifting its weight in a space too confined for the movement you are seeing. Bulk. Mass. A shape that has no shape your eye can hold, that occupies the space behind the wall the way water occupies a vessel, the way memory occupies a mind, completely, in a way that makes the container seem incidental. You pull back. The darkness does not follow. The movement stops. The wall is still a wall.

You put your hand on it. The stones are cool. The ambient warmth does not reach through them, or the warmth reaches and the stone holds it and the stone holds it because something on the other side is drawing it away, absorbing it, using it. The wall is not a barrier against entry. The wall is a barrier against whatever is on the other side leaving.

You step back. The wall is still a wall.

You walk on.

The passage opens into something larger, another chamber, and this one holds a pool — the largest body of water you have found, a still dark mirror that fills a depression in the floor, edges crusted white, surface unmarked. The mineral light reaches this one. It falls through a crack in the ceiling that must lead to the surface, to the upper passages, to the world you descended from, and the light falls in a pale column and touches the pool's surface and does not reflect. The water absorbs it. Takes it down. The pool is not reflecting anything. It is not a mirror. It is a mouth. It swallows light the way the walls swallow warmth, the way the fauna swallows sound, taking in and not giving back.

You approach it. The floor around the pool is dry, cracked, powdery — a desert ring around a water source, as though the moisture has been so present for so long that the ground has dried itself out trying to escape it, or as though the pool has been drinking for so long that it has consumed everything around it, the moisture and the damp and the willingness of stone to hold water, leaving only this: dry stone and dark water and a column of light that the dark water slowly takes.

You do not touch it.

You are being watched. Not by the fauna — the fauna watches everything the way water watches the banks that contain it, without intent, without focus, without the quality that makes watching into something. By something else. Something that has decided to attend to you. You do not know how you know this. You do not know when it started. But you are being attended to, in a way the fauna does not attend, with a quality of consideration that suggests preference, suggests evaluation, suggests the thing watching is deciding what to do with what it sees.

You turn.

The far wall of the chamber is close. You did not see the chamber as large when you entered. You see it now as something else — a room, a space, a container with a shape and a size and a far wall that is closer than the far wall should be, as though the space has contracted around you, as though the walls are learning where to be. And on the far wall, in the mineral light: a mark. Not a scratch, not a stain, not a geological feature. A mark. Something made. Something with intention. A shape pressed into the stone that is too regular to be erosion, too deliberate to be accident, too specific to be anything other than a message or a record or a name.

You cannot read it. The shape is not a shape you know. But it was made to be read. By someone. By something. By a hand or a hand-equivalent working stone with hammer-stones and patience, leaving a mark that would outlast the hand that made it, that would outlast the hand-equivalent, that would outlast the body and the species and the world that produced the body and the species, persisting in this place where nothing grows and everything becomes.

You stand before it. The bead in your pocket is warm. The mark on the wall is old. The thing watching you watches. The pool behind you drinks the light. The fauna churns. The warmth holds. The stone goes on.
