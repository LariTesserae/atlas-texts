# Inhabitants

---

The people of the Weeping City have bodies that have learned to weep with it. Their skin is perpetually flushed, soft from moisture, unmarked by the dryness that would kill them elsewhere. They are not uncomfortable—they have never known anything else—but they carry within them a constant shallow perspiration that they lose and replace and lose again, cycling through the water that saturates everything. They drink constantly, small sips at frequent intervals, and their urine runs clear. Their clothes are chosen for breathability, for the capacity to wick and drain, for antimicrobial properties that resist the endless work of the organism skin that grazes every surface. They do not own umbrellas. They have forgotten what protection from rain would mean.

They are shorter than people elsewhere, on average—not from malnutrition but from development in an atmosphere where vertical growth has never been prioritized, where the body's resources flow toward other adaptations. Their lungs are larger, their mucous membranes thicker, their eyes prone to a perpetual mild inflammation that they interpret not as irritation but as acuity. They see well in low light. They hear better than they see, navigating the city's roar with an attention that visitors find exhausting, parsing meaning from frequencies that blur into undifferentiated noise for anyone not raised within the sound.

They move in patterns that follow the water. Their lives are organized around flow—transit routes that parallel the channels, rest stops positioned at collection points, markets that gather where moisture accumulates and people must pause. They are a people who understand circulation, who have internalized the city's logic so thoroughly that they no longer distinguish between their own circulation and the city's, between their own pulse and the pulse of infrastructure beneath their feet. They sleep in shifts. They eat food that has been grown in water, in tanks and vats and the dark wet places where protein cultures multiply in the humidity, flavors developed over generations to approximate the tastes of foods that cannot grow here. They do not miss what they have never known.

Their social structures are fluid—literally. Settlements reorganize as channels shift, communities dispersing and reconverging in new configurations, alliances made and remade as the urban fabric changes around them. There is no fixed center, no permanent hierarchy, only the temporary consolidations that form around points of stability, around the deep pools that have never changed location, around the treatment centers that must always be maintained. They are a people who understand that permanence is illusion, that the only constant is flow. They have made peace with this, or they have died; the ones who remain are those who adapted.

And there are others. There are always others.

---

The fibrous growths are the closest thing to plants, though they are not plants. They emerge from stone in the places where water lingers longest—seeps and weep points and the margins of channels where flow slows enough to leave moisture behind. They are pale, almost translucent, threads so fine that they seem to exist more as absence than presence, as the memory of growth rather than growth itself. In daylight they are nearly invisible, a faint fuzz on stone that might be mineral deposit or might be decay. But when the light dims, when the city's glow softens and the atmospheric haze thickens into something like night, they begin to luminesce—a soft blue-green light that is less than illumination and more than suggestion, a glow that reveals their presence to those who know to look.

They do not photosynthesize. They have no use for light. Instead, they feed on the moisture itself, metabolizing the dissolved minerals that the water carries, processing the trace compounds that the city excretes in its endless circulation. They are filters in the truest sense—not barriers but transformations, turning what passes through them into something else, something that can be used by other things. They grow slowly, by human standards, but they grow; a cluster that exists today will be larger tomorrow, will persist and spread and eventually be replaced by successor clusters that will carry forward whatever pattern they embody. They are not conscious, as far as anyone can tell. They are simply alive, simply continuing, simply present in the way that life is present when it has found a niche and filled it completely.

The organism skin is related to them, or may be related to them—the taxonomic relationships of the Weeping City are subjects of ongoing debate, as the boundaries between species blur in an environment where horizontal gene transfer is constant and the categories that elsewhere divide life have here become permeable. The skin is not fibrous; it is sheet-like, a membrane that forms on surfaces in the low places where moisture accumulates and remains. It grazes the built environment, consuming the accumulations that would otherwise build up—the dust and detritus and the microscopic debris of urban life—and in consuming them it keeps the city clean. It is not symbiosis, exactly; it is not parasitism. It is something else, something that the city's ecology has made room for, a process that has become so integrated into urban function that removal would be more disruptive than presence.

Visitors sometimes panic when they see surfaces rippling, when they realize that the walls are breathing. Residents do not notice. They have never known walls that did not breathe.

---

The crystal clusters are stranger. They grow along water channels with the slow inevitability of frost, forming patterns that are too regular to be random, too varied to be designed. They are silicate, fundamentally—sand and mineral and the dissolved solids of the urban water supply—but they have organized themselves into shapes that have no names, configurations that defy the crystallography that elsewhere governs mineral formation. They grow by accumulating moisture from the air, harvesting the humidity that the city cannot expel, pulling water vapor into their structures and releasing it as liquid, contributing to the very wetness that surrounds them. They are not alive, as far as anyone can determine. They do not metabolize. They do not reproduce. And yet they grow, and they persist, and they have been growing and persisting for so long that the oldest clusters are now architectural features, destinations that people visit the way others visit ancient trees.

In certain districts, the crystal clusters have been incorporated into the city's visual language. They are lit, at night, their facets catching the glow of displays and channels and refracting it into spectra that shift as the light sources shift. They have been given names. They have been made sacred, or commercial, or both—sites of pilgrimage and sites of transaction, places where the boundary between the natural and the constructed has become too uncertain to matter. Children grow up knowing them, knowing their shapes and locations and the stories that have accumulated around them. They are monuments to a process that nobody fully understands.

And then there are the things that are understood even less.

---

The simple fauna exists in the margins—in the deep architecture, in the places where people rarely go, in the water that moves through channels too small to navigate, in the air that hangs motionless in enclosed spaces. They are simple in the way that all successful life is simple: not primitive but pared down, reduced to the essential processes that allow continuation. They eat the organisms that eat the dissolved compounds. They are eaten in turn by slightly more complex organisms, which are eaten by slightly more complex organisms still, creating chains that extend downward and upward and sideways into a ecology that has never been fully mapped. The city's biologists estimate thousands of species. The city's philosophers suspect millions. The city's residents know only that occasionally, in the low places, they see something move—something small, something quick, something that blinks in the dark before disappearing into a crack that closes behind it.

They are not unwelcome. They are simply rare enough to startle, to remind, to suggest that existence continues in more forms than the city knows. A child who sees one remembers it for life. An adult who sees one pauses, briefly, in the way that all people pause when they encounter evidence of life beyond their own.

And there are rumors—always rumors—of larger things. Things that live in the deep pools, in the black water that gathers beneath the foundations where nobody goes. Things that move through the city at night, visible only in the lightning's flash, too large to be fauna and too strange to be people. The city has no myths about them; myths require belief, and belief requires consensus, and consensus has never formed around these reports. But the rumors persist, as rumors do, suggesting that the margins of the Weeping City extend further than anyone has explored, that the life that exists here has not finished becoming whatever it will become.

---

At night, the city breathes. The humidity intensifies, pressing against skin and clothing with a warmth that feels less like weather and more like breath. The moisture catches the light and scatters it, creating a glow that is everywhere and nowhere, a radiance that belongs to no source and illuminates nothing and everything. The people sleep in shifts, their bodies finally still in the wet warmth, their circulation slowing to match the city's own slower pulse. The fibrous growths glow brighter in the darkness, their luminescence the only light in the places where displays have powered down. The crystal clusters catch and scatter and transform the city's glow into something that might be beautiful, that might be strange, that might be both.

And in the margins, in the deep places, in the spaces between the known and the unknown, life continues. Not visibly, not audibly, but persistently, as life has always continued in this place—as life continues everywhere, in the gaps and the cracks and the places where the rules do not quite apply. The Weeping City is alive. Its inhabitants are alive. Its fauna is alive, and its strange growths and stranger crystals are alive in whatever way matters, in whatever way allows continuation. The water moves and the people move and the city moves and nothing stops, nothing ends, nothing is finished becoming.

This is what lives here: a world that has found a way to continue, a ecology that has organized itself around different principles, a beauty that is also a strangeness, a strangeness that is also a beauty. It is not a world that would feel entirely alien to walk through. But it is a world where the categories do not quite fit, where the familiar has become strange and the strange has become home, where life has taken what was offered and made of it something that persists.

The weeping never stops. And what lives here has learned to weep with it.
