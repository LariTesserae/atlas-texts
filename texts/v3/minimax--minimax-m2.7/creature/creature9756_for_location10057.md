The water is the oldest inhabitant. It does not live the way the birds live, not exactly — it has no body to tend, no offspring to feed, no heart to quicken in the cold. But it moves through the stone with something like intention, if intention means following the paths of least resistance through grain and seam. It arrives from below, from deep, from places the air cannot reach, and it emerges into light as thin threads that tremble in any breath of wind. The threads join. They thicken. They fall, and their falling is the pulse of this place, the rhythm beneath all other rhythms, the measure against which every other life calibrates itself.

The insects know this rhythm. They have built their lives around it.

In the small dark pools — no wider than cupped hands, shaded by overhanging rock, catching the mineral richness that the water carries up from the deep — the insects breed. They are not one species but many, a congregation of forms so small they seem more like moving dust than creatures with bodies and purposes. They rise from the pools in clouds that catch light and hold it for a moment, bodies glinting, then settle again on the wet stone. Theirs is a world measured in inches, in the distances between pools, in the thin layer of moisture that clings to every surface. They do not travel far. They do not need to. Everything they require is here: water, mineral, each other.

The birds are larger, and louder, and restless in ways the insects cannot be. They nest in the crevices high above the hollow's floor, in cracks so narrow a hand could not reach, in places the seepage has carved over millennia into hollows just large enough. Their nests are small constructions of moss and fiber, held together by the same dampness that feeds everything, and their eggs — when they have them — are speckled and patient, warmed by bodies that do not seem to notice the thinness of the air. The birds call to one another across the hollow in chains of notes, a back-and-forth that suggests not alarm but conversation, the exchanging of news between neighbors too far apart for sight. They are dark birds, built for altitude, their wings adapted for the constant wind that pours through the southern gap. They rise on thermals that should not exist at this height, catch updrafts that cling to the cliff face, spiral up to hunt insects that cloud the wet stone.

The ferns do not call. They do not move. But they live in the way that patience lives: slow, deliberate, gathering.

They crowd the shaded recesses where water pools longest, their fronds unfurling from roots sunk deep into the gravel, drinking greedily from the seep. In the dry years they retreat — their fronds yellow and curl, their roots contract — but the water persists, and so they return, spreading again across the wet stone, reclaiming what the sun takes. They are not green but something darker, something that has adapted to this lightless place and learned to drink shadow instead. They are the lungs of the hollow, exchanging moisture for the air that the birds breathe, for the insects, for the stone itself.

And the stone lives too, though its living is slow — so slow that a single season, a single year, a single human life, cannot perceive it.

The obsidian drinks the water and gives back darkness, but the water takes something in return: a little of the stone's edge, a little of its mass, grain by grain, seam by seam. Where the water falls longest, the rock has worn smooth. Where it pools longest, the mineral deposits have built up in white veins that look like scar tissue, like the mountain is still healing from some ancient wound. The platforms built by the primitive builders are nearly consumed — their corners crumbled, their surfaces worn — but something remains, and what remains says: someone was here before. Someone sat on these stones. Someone built these rings and pits and carved that nearly-erased face into the wall. Their tools are gone, their bodies are gone, but their labor remains, held in place by the same erosion that slowly unmakes it. The mountain outlasts everything. The water outlasts everything. Even the people.

There is something else here, if you are willing to see it.

The mist. It hangs in the hollow even at midday, not quite fog, not quite cloud, but something between — a permanent exhalation, a held breath. It moves slowly through the space, following currents invisible, carrying moisture from the weeping stone to the ferns to the air to the stone again. It is not alive, but it is present. It fills the silences between drips, softens the harsh edges of obsidian, blurs the line between stone and sky. It is the mountain's dreaming, perhaps, or its constant conversation with itself.

And if you are here — if you have climbed to this impossible place, if you have found the hollow and the water and the small lives that persist in spite of everything — then you are here too. You are the one who rests. You are the one who sits on a worn platform and listens to the insects and the drips and the birds and the wind, and you feel something that might be peace, or might be presence, or might simply be the absence of everything below.

The place does not judge you for coming. It does not welcome you either. It simply holds you, the way the stone holds the water, the way the ferns hold the mist, the way everything here holds everything else in a long chain of motion and exchange and slow, patient living. You breathe the thin air. You listen to the weeping. You rest.

And the water goes on falling, and the insects go on hovering, and the birds go on calling, and the ferns go on drinking, and the stone goes on being slowly undone, and the mountain goes on holding it all together — a place that should be empty and isn't, a place for the weary, a place that asks nothing and gives everything it has.
