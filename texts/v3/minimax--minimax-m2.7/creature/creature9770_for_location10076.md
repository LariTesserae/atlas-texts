What lives here is the architecture itself.

The great structural plants—the towers, the walls, the vaulted canopies—are the dominant life forms, but they are not alone. Within their mass, alongside them, dependent upon them, there are others. The distinction is not taxonomy but behavior: these secondary organisms do not build so much as occupy, exploit, adjust. They are the tenants of a city that was never designed for tenants, and their presence is the only evidence that this place is alive in more than the way stones are alive in the metaphor of geologists.

The first are the hitchhikers.

They arrive as spores on the moist winds, settling into the micro-crevices where growth has not yet sealed itself closed. They are small—too small to see in the thin light, visible only as a darkness in the crevices, a discoloration on the wet surfaces. But they grow. They spread. They are fungi in the broad sense, though they have diversified here into forms that no mycologist would recognize: pale filaments that network through the outer layers of living wood, digesting not the structural tissue but the exudates, the sugars and acids that the living plants bleed through their surfaces in the constant process of growth. They do not harm the architecture. They refine it. Where they spread, the wood is cleaner, more defined, as though they are removing the rough edges of the builders' work. The large structures develop a patina where the hitchhikers have been at work: darker, denser, more coherent. The towers that have been colonized longest are almost black in their lower sections, the fungal mats having thickened and hardened into a second skin over the original growth.

The hitchhikers have their own hitchhikers.

These are smaller still, almost invisible, moving in the thin moisture films that coat every surface. They are not fungal but bacterial, archaeal, something else that does not fit the old categories. They consume the waste products of the fungal mats, and in doing so they generate compounds that the larger plants absorb back through their roots—the living mat you stand on is not merely substrate but exchange medium, a processing system that has evolved to reclaim and recirculate the metabolic products of the entire community. The ground is alive with this secondary commerce: the slow chemistry of decomposition and reconstruction happening everywhere at once, making the air smell of something green and sharp, something that stings the back of the throat if you breathe too deeply.

But there are also the drifters.

These are the plants that do not anchor. They exist as seeds or spores or fragments that catch the moisture currents and settle wherever the humidity is high enough to allow growth. They are the opportunists, the gap-fillers, the plants that arrive in the spaces between the architecture and attempt to establish themselves before the next structural growth closes the opening. They are always small—the largest are the size of your hand—and they are always ephemeral. A drifter will germinate in a crevice, send out a few leaves, perhaps flower once if it can find enough light, and then die, its body incorporated into the growing mass of the architecture it occupied. But in its brief life, it does something the structural plants cannot: it creates texture. The vast walls are not smooth but textured by the ghosts of drifters, by the impressions of their leaves pressed into the yielding surface as the growth closed over them. The architecture remembers its tenants.

And then there are the adjusters.

These are the most difficult to describe because they do not behave like plants at all. They grow, yes, and they photosynthesize, yes, but they move in ways that seem active rather than merely growth-driven. They are vines—the word is inadequate but close—thin tendrils that extend from the main structural masses into the open spaces, seeking, probing, testing. When they find something to grip, they grip. When they find nothing, they retract, wither, fall away. But their behavior is not random. The tendrils are more concentrated in some areas than others, and where they cluster, the configuration of the space changes: a tendril grips a wall, thickens, becomes a support; another grips nearby, and the two fuse into a brace; another extends further into a gap and stops, becoming a beam that was not there before. The adjusters are the maintenance crew. They are the parts of the architecture that move, that respond, that do the work of optimization that the older growths cannot do for themselves. They are slow—so slow that watching them for an hour reveals nothing—but over days and weeks, the shape of the space shifts. A gap becomes a corridor. A corridor becomes a chamber. A chamber develops a ceiling where there was only sky. The adjusters are building the city in real time, one tendril at a time, one grip at a time, one slow decision at a time.

You cannot see them moving. You can only see the evidence of their movement: the new braces, the fused tendrils, the surfaces that have hardened where the moisture has been drawn up and deposited in new patterns. The architecture is full of these adjustments, these small renovations, and when you look at a wall and see a pattern that seems intentional—a curve here, a thickening there, a slight overhang that shades the ground below—you are seeing the accumulated decisions of the adjusters, the record of their slow thought made manifest in living wood.

There is also, in the deeper chambers, something else.

It is not a plant, not a fungus, not a drifter, not an adjuster. It is older than any of these—or rather, it is the precursor to all of them, the original life form from which the others have radiated over the millennia of this place's existence. It exists as a film on the inner surfaces of the oldest structures, a thin dark layer that looks like staining but is in fact a living tissue so primitive that it has no differentiated parts, no specialization, no structure beyond the most basic: a membrane that absorbs, a membrane that grows, a membrane that replicates. It is the ur-plant, the ancestor of the towers and the walls and the vaulted canopies, still present in the interstices of its own descendants, still alive in the places where the light does not reach and the air does not move and the humidity is constant and absolute. It does nothing. It asks for nothing. It simply is, as it has always been, the substrate from which everything else has grown.

You find it if you go deep enough. If you press through the gaps into the oldest chambers, if you look at the surfaces that have never been touched by anything, you will find this dark thin living thing and understand that the architecture is not a structure but a body, and the body is not a body but an ecosystem, and the ecosystem is not an ecosystem but a single ancient organism that has decided, over millions of years, to become a city.

And even now, in the thin light, with the adjusters working and the drifters dying and the hitchhikers spreading their dark patina across the towers, the original film is still growing. Still extending, imperceptibly, into the new spaces. Still being, in the deepest chambers, what it has always been.

The city is alive.

And it is dreaming.
