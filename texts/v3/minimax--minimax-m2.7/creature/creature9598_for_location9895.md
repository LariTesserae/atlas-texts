# What Lives Here

There are people in the Holding, and they are shaped by the damp the way driftwood is shaped by the current—slowly, from all sides at once, until the shape they become is inseparable from what moves through them.

They are people whose lungs have learned the weight of this air. The humidity here is not a condition but a presence, and after a few years the people stop noticing it the way one stops noticing a heartbeat. It is simply what fills them. Children born here cough less than visitors would expect; their small bodies have already accommodated the moisture, taken it in, let it become part of the breath. By the time someone is grown, they carry the damp inside them. Their clothes are never fully dry. Their hair, after the first few days, stops looking wet and simply looks *settled*, the way a branch in a river looks settled—not drowning but participating.

The people of the Holding have learned to read the pumps the way others read weather. They know the rhythm of the machinery's breathing—know when the machines are laboring, when the water table has risen, when a gear is wearing thin because they can feel its failure in the way the settlement shudders, a vibration that travels up through the packed earth and into the bones of anyone standing still long enough to notice. There are families who have maintained particular pumps for generations, father and mother and child and grandchild, each learning the specific voice of their machine, the particular knock or hiss that means *this much pressure, this much work, this much life left in the iron*. When a pump fails, it is not replaced so much as grown around. The people wait. The roots find the gap. The green takes the shape the iron once held.

They are people who measure time in the slow growing of things. A new structure takes a generation to become indistinguishable from the old, and they know this, and they find peace in it. They plant the way a person might set a stone in a garden path—knowing they will not see the moss close over it, hoping their grandchildren will walk that path and never think to ask who laid the stone. The patience of the Holding is not resignation; it is a particular kind of faith, the belief that continuation is its own form of purpose. They do not fight the water. They do not fight the green. They negotiate, as one negotiates with a river—by learning its patterns, building where it allows, yielding where it insists.

There are women who harvest the algae from the pipes, scraping it clean with wide blades, gathering it into baskets that drip blue-green onto their boots. They sell it to the dyers, who mordant it with the iron water that seeps up through the clay, and the cloth that results holds a color that has no name in other places—a green that is also a grey, a blue that is also a rust, the exact shade of something that has lived its whole life between machine and marsh. There are men who work the pumps, not with the pride of engineers but with the familiarity of shepherds, checking pressure and flow, listening for the note that means health, the note that means strain. They are not technicians so much as translators, fluent in the language the iron speaks to the water, the language the roots speak to the stone.

The children are barefoot more than shod. Their feet grow wide and flat in the way that grips wet stone, and they run across the root-bridges without fear because the sway of them has been in their bones since before they could walk. They learn to climb the way other children learn to run—up through the canopy, out along branches that arc over the channels, into the high green light where the pumps' warmth rises and the bananas hang in bunches like something discovered rather than cultivated. They learn which mushrooms fruit on which logs, which frogs hide in which pools, which vines will hold weight and which will slip. They learn the market, the vendors, the smell of fish smoking over ironbark coals. They learn the sound of the settlement at night, when the machines go quiet, when the water moves without being asked.

There are old people in the Holding who sit in rooms where the walls are more root than wood. Their hands rest on surfaces that are neither table nor trunk, and they have stopped caring about the distinction. They have watched the same vine grow thick enough to lean against, watched the same pump become a skeleton and then a skeleton wrapped in green, watched the water rise and fall with the years and the settlement rise and fall with the water, and what they have learned is that endurance is not the same as resistance. It is more like a conversation, a long one, spoken in the language of growth and pressure and the slow giving-way of iron to root. They are people who have made themselves into something the Holding can accommodate. And in return, the Holding has kept them.

Everyone here carries the Holding inside them. Soil under the nails. Rust in the creases of the hands. A smell that clings to wool and hair and the spaces between teeth. When they dream, they dream of water moving through channels, of the sound the pumps make when the water is high, of the way the light comes level and amber through the vine-mesh in the evenings. When they leave—and some do, occasionally, for the roads are not closed, only inconvenient—the smell stays with them for years. It becomes the smell of home in a way that has no translation, no equivalent, no word in any other place. It is simply the smell of wet earth and rust and green growth, all mixed together, and it means *the Holding*, and it means *alive*, and it means *patient, patient, patient, as the root becomes the pipe, as the pipe becomes the root, as the water moves and the people follow, as everything that was made and everything that is wild learns, in time, to grow into the same shape*.
