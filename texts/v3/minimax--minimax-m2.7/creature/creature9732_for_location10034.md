Begin with the water, as everything here begins with the water.

The water carries its own population, a drifting world that does not think of itself as underwater because it knows nothing else. Bacteria form films on submerged surfaces—thin communities of cyanobacteria that tint the undersides of floating mats in shades of rust and copper and the particular green that is not quite plant and not quite mineral but something in between. Where mineral water seeps into channels, these films thicken, and the colors become more emphatic: pale yellows where sulfur-oxidizers dominate, orange-browns where iron-cyclers have found purchase. You do not see these organisms so much as you see their effects—the stain they leave, the residue they create, the slow transformation of everything they touch into something that carries their signature.

Below the surface, in the water column itself, there are larvae. This is where most of the biomass lives, though biomass is not the right word for something that is always becoming, always in motion from one form to another. Mosquito larvae hang at the surface film, their breathing tubes piercing the tension with the casual intimacy of creatures that have always lived this way. Chironomid larvae curve through the water near the bottom, their bodies carrying the red of hemoglobin that lets them breathe where oxygen is scarce. Damselfly larvae cling to submerged roots, their bodies patient and triangular, waiting for the moment when something small enough passes within range of their mask. Crane fly larvae—the leatherjackets—bulge in the mud where it is thick enough to hold them, feeding on organic matter that has settled over seasons, digesting the accumulated death of this place back into living tissue.

The adults that emerge from the water are ephemeral in a different sense: their winged forms are brief punctuations in lives that are mostly spent elsewhere, mostly spent in stages that do not look like the creature the adult will become. Dragonflies patrol the channels in iridescent loops, their nymphs still hunting below in the silt, patient as stones. Mayflies lift from the surface in clouds when conditions align—warm water, still air, a trigger encoded in populations that have no calendar but remember the patterns of their ancestors. They mate, drop their eggs, die, and are gone within hours, leaving only the memory of their falling bodies on the water.

Beetles occupy every niche the water does not fill. Diving beetles move through channels with the efficient leg-motion of swimmers, hunting beneath the surface with the locked-wing air supply that lets them stay submerged longer than seems possible. Water boatmen row through the shallows in their perpetual upside-down way, feeding on algae and biofilm and each other. Ground beetles search the banks and the dry edges of mineral crusts, their armored forms gleaming like fragments of the stained earth, hunting by night for the small creatures that also prefer darkness. Rove beetles colonize the margins where organic matter accumulates—the rotting leaves caught in root lattices, the bodies of things that have died at the water's edge—breaking down what larger predators have missed or refused.

The sound of clicking comes from many sources. Crayfish press themselves into muddy banks during the day, their jointed bodies pressed into hollows they have shaped with their claws, waiting for the dimness that lets them move. At night, they emerge to scuttle along the bottom of channels, their antennae sweeping, their claws held forward in that perpetually defensive posture that speaks to a life lived under threat. Land crabs—larger, slower, armored in the way that says they have survived long enough to need serious protection—move through the ferns along banks where the ground is firm enough to bear their weight. They clip and crush through leaf litter, their presence marked by the geometric cuts they leave in vegetation that has grown across their paths. Where they dig, the soil loosens, and water finds new places to seep.

Snails are everywhere that does not move. They cling to submerged roots, their shells slicked with biofilm. They cluster on the undersides of floating leaves, out of the sun. They glue themselves to the mineral crusts in the shallows, their shells filmed with the same manganese and iron that stains the substrate orange and blue and gray. Some are large—the apple snails that can close their trapdoor against the warm mineral water and wait for conditions to improve; the door snails that hang from vegetation in elongated shells that sway like ornaments when the air moves. Others are small enough to be overlooked: the minute freshwater snails that cluster on the roots of floating mats, their shells no larger than grains of sand, their populations so dense that a single root may host hundreds.

Leeches wait in the mud and the still water, their flattened bodies patient as waiting. They do not hunt so much as advertise—they wave in the current, their small bodies carrying the chemical signature that says blood to any creature that recognizes the meaning. Fish—where there are fish—learn to avoid them. Frogs learn to avoid them. The larger creatures learn to pick their way through habitat where leeches cluster, and the learning lives in their bodies as a kind of caution, a stored knowledge that does not need to be thought.

The frogs own the night here. Not because the day belongs to someone else, but because the night is when their voices come together into something that is more than the sum of its parts—a dense chorus of calls that rises from every pool and channel and patch of saturated growth, each species offering its particular contribution to the whole. Cricket frogs add their clicking to the wet air. Tree frogs—their toe-pads broad and sticky—climb the ferns to call from positions that give their voices distance. Bull frogs, where they exist in the deeper pools, add bass notes that pulse through the humid dark. The calling is not random—it follows the patterns of rainfall, of rising water, of the chemical triggers that tell each population that conditions are right. When the chorus rises, the air itself seems to thicken, to become more alive, and the predator pressure that follows will be correspondingly intense: more eggs means more food for larvae and beetles and things that eat both, and the cycle continues in its patient way.

Snakes come in two categories: water snakes and things that visit the water. The water snakes are common but not often seen—their bodies blend with the coloration of the channels, their movements low and direct, their heads held above the surface with the patience of breath-holders. They feed on fish and frogs and the occasional carrion they find floating past. The others—the vine snakes and tree snakes that move through the low branches, the sand boas that hunt in the mineral flats, the occasional cobra that has wandered in from somewhere drier—these are guests, making use of the abundance without belonging to it in the way the water snakes do. They hunt and are hunted, and the system absorbs them the way it absorbs everything: gently, over time, without particular notice.

Birds arrive in waves. Herons and egrets wade in the shallows, their necks folded against their bodies until something moves within range and the long neck snaps out with the speed of a released spring. Ibis sweep through the vegetation with their curved bills, feeding on the insects and crustaceans that any disturbance turns up. Kingfishers—flashy, impatient, diving with the concentrated purpose of small bodies that carry everything they need to survive—plunge into channels and are gone before the water has time to settle. Rails and crakes move through the dense growth in ways that seem to violate physics—bodies too large for the gaps they pass through, silence maintained where sound should be unavoidable. They nest in the floating mats, raise their chicks in the dense cover, and disappear when the seasons shift enough to make the journey worth attempting again.

The largest predators are patient. Crocodiles—where they exist in this landscape—do not roar or charge or perform any of the dramatic behaviors attributed to them in other places. They drift. They surface with only eyes and nostrils above the waterline, the rest of their body held in the patience of something that has learned to wait for meals that come to them. They eat fish and frogs and the occasional bird or crab that passes too close. They do not need to move quickly because everything else moves slowly here, and the mathematics of encounter works in their favor given enough time.

Things die here, and dying is not a separate event but part of the continuous process that makes this place what it is. Bodies settle into the mud or float in channels until scavengers claim them. Insects finish their brief adult lives and fall to surfaces where other insects will find them. Frogs that have called through one season become food for snakes or birds or the next generation of their own species. The slow chemistry of decay operates on all of it—the bacterial breakdown, the consumption by larvae, the eventual incorporation of what was one thing into the bodies of many things that will also become other things in their turn. There is no waste here. There is no away for anything to go. The system holds all of it, recycles all of it, carries the memory of every life in the form of the lives that follow.

What you see, if you watch long enough, is not the animals but the patterns. The way a channel empties when a heron arrives, the silence that precedes a snake's strike, the sudden aggregation of dragonflies that means something has triggered the emergence. You learn to read the place by its disturbances—the places where the mud has been recently turned, the leaves that have been recently stripped, the water that has been recently still. The animals themselves come and go in the flow, never quite stopping, never quite separate from the system that contains them. You are part of it too, when you stand in this place—you add your body heat to the warmth that rises, your breath to the humid air, your presence to the disturbances that everything else reads and responds to. You are not the observer. You are one pattern among many, watching, being watched, moving through the same slow time that moves through everything here.
