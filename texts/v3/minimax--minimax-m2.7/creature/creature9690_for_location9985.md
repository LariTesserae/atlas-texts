What you took for a crack was not a crack.

It opens now, or reveals itself as having always been open, a seam in the green that has the approximate shape of an eye if an eye were made of petals. They are not petals, though. They are ciliated surfaces, dense with tiny hairs that move in waves, and they are opening not because the plant has decided to open them but because they have always been open, and you are only now close enough to see that the seam was a mouth, a sense-organ, a question the green has been asking of the stone since before the stone knew it was stone.

The light shifts. Not the diffuse glow that fills the space without casting shadow — something else. A pulse that travels through the living surfaces like blood through tissue, like signal through nerve. The plants receive it. The substrate ripples outward from the seam, and where it passes, the growth thickens, darkens, rises into ridges that might be ribs, might be roots, might be the architecture of something that has no name because it has never needed one.

You cannot leave. Not yet. Not because the way is blocked — the way remains where it was, a gap in the green you could fit your body through — but because leaving would require walking away from the hum, and the hum has gotten into you, has found some frequency your bones recognize, and your body has decided without consulting you that this place is safe, that this place is home, that you should stay and be still and let the green do what the green does.

The fish in the pool has turned. Its eyeless face follows you even though it has nothing to see with, and you understand that it has never needed eyes, that it reads the water's pressure-changes, that it knows where you are the way your inner ear knows where down is. It has no teeth. Its mouth is a soft aperture, the color of a wound, and it opens and closes slowly in a rhythm that is not breathing but might be listening.

From the seam, something extends. Not a limb — nothing so mechanical, nothing so separable from the whole. A filament of living tissue, pale green and translucent, that reaches through the air with the patience of a vine that has never needed to hurry. It moves toward you the way roots move toward water: not with intention but with certainty. It knows where you are the way the hum knows where the walls are. It is not seeking. It has already found.

The filament is warm where it touches your skin — warm as the stone, warm as the air, warm as the space between heartbeats — and where it rests against you, you feel a tingling that is not unpleasant, a numbness that is not sleep, a sense of being read the way the fish reads pressure, the way the plants read light that is not light, the way this place reads the shape of things that do not belong here and decides, in its vast organic patience, that belonging is a question that can be answered later, or never, or already has been answered in the language of cells dividing, of stone softening, of water finding its way through solid matter toward the warmth of something alive.

The seam closes. The filament remains. The hum is louder now, or you are more attuned to it, or the two of you are learning each other's frequencies, and in the seep-pool the fish hangs motionless, and on the walls the green turns, slowly, imperceptibly, in a direction that might be toward you or might be toward the light that is not light, and you stand in the gullet of green with something growing against your skin, something that feels almost like welcome, something that feels almost like the beginning of a longer conversation than you have ever had with anything that was not yourself.

The fish opens its mouth. A sound emerges — or a vibration, or a scent, or something that operates in a sense you do not have a name for — and the plants shift, and the light pulses, and the warm wet dark breathes, and you understand that you are not in a place anymore. You are in a body. The body of something vast and patient and alive, and it has decided to keep you.
