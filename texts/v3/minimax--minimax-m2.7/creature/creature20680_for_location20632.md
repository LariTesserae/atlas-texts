# What Lives Here

The plateau holds its water the way a body holds its breath — not taking it in so much as failing to let it out. Moisture rises through the limestone from some deep reservoir that no one has ever named, and in the rising it finds every seam, every crack, every place where pressure has carved its slow passage through stone that is itself slowly dissolving. The water does not fall here. It arrives from below, patient and mineral-rich, and the plateau receives it the way parched earth receives what it has been owed.

In the marsh-basins — those low places where the seepage collects and rests before finding its way onward — the water is brackish and still. Things have adapted to its stillness. Small creatures move through the shallow pools with narrow heads and long tails, their bodies evolved over generations to tolerate the mineral concentration, the coolness, the depth that never quite exceeds what a hand could measure. They are not dramatic animals. They do not leap or call. They persist in water that tastes of stone and time, and when the basins shrink in the dry months they shelter in the mud and wait. The cycle is old enough that their bodies have become architecture — shaped by it, for it, as the city was shaped by the stone it rose from.

The grasses are not grasses so much as survivors. They push up through packed earth and between fitted stones with stems that carry a bluish tint, as though the mineral water has stained them from within. They bend without breaking. They hold their green in the lean seasons by holding it close, by growing slowly, by not reaching for what isn't there. And when the moisture runs stronger — after the long rains that mark the plateau's winter, when the seepage thickens and the walls weep visibly and the streets shine like something newly made — the grasses drink and do not waste what they take. Between them, the bare places speak of earth too packed or too dry for even patience to overcome, and these gaps are more common than the clusters, punctuations in a sentence that is mostly silence.

The shrubs do not compete. They have learned the geography of enough. Their leaves are thick with a waxy film that catches what moisture lingers in the air and keeps it, a small private reservoir in each leaf, and their roots have found the seams where water travels underground and they follow those seams, sometimes visible above, mostly not. The shrubs grow low because wind has taught them to. The plateau wind moves through from the interior with the patience of everything here — not a gust but a current, not a storm but a mode, and anything that reaches above a certain height is worn at until it learns humility or breaks. The ash trees that cling to the ridge-tops and the edges of the marsh-basins have learned. Their trunks lean. Their branches reach only in the direction the wind allows. They are not deformed so much as honest about where they are.

In the higher districts, the cold has a presence. Not hostile — the body adjusts, and the thick stone of the buildings holds warmth enough once fire is lit and bodies gather — but present, a constant partner in every breath. The elevation has thinned the air until the sky is a deeper blue than visitors expect, and the stars, when they emerge from the slow-moving weather systems, arrive in a scatter that feels closer than stars should be. People born here are shaped by that thinness. Their lungs work differently. Their circulation runs closer to the surface. They grow up with a relationship to cold that is structural rather than seasonal, and they carry it with them even when they travel to warmer places below.

The city is the largest living thing on the plateau, if a thing built in layers over centuries can be called singular. It breathes in the sense that it has rhythms — periods of density and quiet, of movement and stillness, of sound and the particular silence that is not empty but only relatively so. During the hours when the streets are full, the city holds so many bodies that the air itself seems to thicken with presence, with voice and footfall and the breathing of draft animals and the creak of wheels on stone and all the small sounds of a dense human settlement going about its business. Then the night deepens, and the movement thins, and the city exhales into something that resembles peace.

The people are not uniform. Generations layer here the way construction layers — older inhabitants in the lower districts where the stone is ancient, younger families pushing upward into newer terraces, the very old sometimes climbing back down to where they were born, returning to origins as the body allows. They have the compactness of people who live in dense quarters — they have learned to share space, to be proximate without intrusion, to maintain privacy through silence and the careful management of sound. They know their neighbors because walls are thin and floors carry footsteps and water runs through channels that pass under buildings, and this knowing has its costs and its gifts. The population is large enough that anonymity exists at a distance — you can be unknown to most — but close enough that you are never truly unknown to someone.

They work the stone. It is not metaphor; the stone is their medium, their material, their sustenance. They cut it from the ridges, shape it with hand tools, lay it in patterns that interlock and endure. The work is generational — a foundation begun by one generation, finished by the next, built upon by a third — and the workers are proud in the way of people who know their work will outlast them. The fitted blocks in the lower walls have held for centuries. They will hold for centuries more. When a building is demolished, it is not demolished so much as absorbed — its stone becomes foundation for what rises on top, its walls incorporated into new walls, its memory carried forward in the grain of the rock. Nothing is wasted. Nothing is truly discarded. The plateau teaches this, with its slow cycles and patient erosion, its water that rises and falls and rises again.

At night, when the city is still, the water is audible. It seeps through walls that have grown damp over centuries, runs through channels that follow routes mapped generations ago, gathers in basins that have always been low places and always will be. The sound is not uniform — it changes with the season, with the weather, with the slow pulse of the water table below — but it is always present, a background hum beneath the silence, the plateau breathing. People sleep to this sound. They dream to it. It is so woven into the experience of the place that absence of it would be a kind of death, an emptiness that nothing else could fill.

The animals that live here have negotiated their terms with the city over long enough time that the negotiations are settled. Birds nest in the wet cliff faces, where the seepage has carved small ledges and the stone stays cool even in summer. Dogs move through the streets with the ease of creatures who have learned the rhythms — they know when to move and when to yield, when the streets will be crowded and when they can cross without hurry. Draft animals are worked but not pushed, their labor measured against centuries of understanding that an exhausted ox is less useful than a rested one. In the marsh-basins, the small creatures do not come into the city, but the city knows they are there, and their presence is part of the landscape's balance — they process the water in ways the people do not, they are part of the cycle that keeps the basins from stagnation, they are necessary in the way that small things are necessary when no one is watching.

The weather moves in systems that have names. The people here have watched the sky long enough to read it — to see the slow build of a system moving in from the interior, to know when the moisture will thicken and the walls will weep more heavily, to plan the work of planting and harvesting and building and resting around patterns that are regular without being rigid. The systems do not interrupt; they are the rhythm. When a system ends and the sky clears, the plateau air takes on a sharpness that makes everything feel closer, more defined, the edges of things more certain. The blue of the sky deepens. The stars brighten. The cold sharpens. And then the next system arrives, slow and inevitable, and the world softens again into mist and damp and the particular gray that makes the stone glow green.

The plateau does not distinguish between what is natural and what is built. The water seeps through ancient stone and through stone laid last season. The wind moves over marsh-basins and over rooftops with equal indifference. The erosion continues beneath the city as it continues in the empty places, eating slowly at the base of cliffs, filling basins with sediment, reshaping the land in ways too slow to see but certain to complete. The city is not an imposition on this landscape; it is an extension of it, another layer of stone, another process, another thing that has grown because the conditions allowed growth. The people are not separate from the plateau any more than the grasses are. They have found their niche in the moisture and the cold and the slow wind, and they have made of that niche a way of being that is as particular and as persistent as the things that grow in the marsh-basins, as the water that rises, as the stone that endures.
