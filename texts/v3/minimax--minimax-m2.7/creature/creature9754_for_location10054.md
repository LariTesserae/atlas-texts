What you begin to understand, after enough days on the plateau, is that the strangeness does not accumulate. It was always already there. The mineral deposits in the channels did not form over the centuries of the city's occupation; they were complete before the first foundation stone was laid, or close enough to complete that the difference does not matter. You can see this if you look at the oldest cuts, the ones that run deepest into the basalt, where the stone is dark and smooth and shows no tool marks from any era. The deposits there are the same shapes as the deposits in the newer channels. The patterns repeat. The city built its drainage to follow what was already draining.

This makes the engineering seem less like engineering and more like listening.

The workers in the filtration stations know this, in a way they do not say. You can see it in how they move through the spaces they have built: the way they touch the pipes with a kind of attentiveness that goes beyond maintenance, the way they pause at certain junctions and let the vibration move through them before moving on. The stations are not efficient by any modern measure. The engineering has been revised so many times that the flow patterns make little logical sense. Water moves through them anyway, or the water does what it was always going to do, and the stations are there to shape that doing into something the city can use.

At night, in the industrial quarter, you can sometimes hear the rats before you see them. They move in the spaces beneath the corrugated metal, in the gaps between brick and concrete, in the channels that were built for water and are now also for rats. Their sound is not quite a sound. It is a texture in the larger texture of the engines, a higher register in the bass that fills everything. You learn not to listen for it specifically. You learn to let it be one more element in the field of noise that you carry with you across the plateau, the way the warm air carries its mineral smell, the way the wet stone carries its faint pulse.

The pulse is easier to feel at night.

In the daylight, the steam plumes and the moving machinery and the light on the pale eastern ground all create a kind of interference that makes the subtler frequencies hard to isolate. But when the factories dim their output and the pumping stations go into their quieter cycle and the conveyor lines go still, the plateau becomes something else. The sound does not disappear; it settles. The bass moves lower, into the body rather than the sternum, and the pulse beneath the pulse becomes more distinct—not louder, exactly, but more present, like something that has been there all along and is now simply being more fully acknowledged.

You stand on the wet stone near the northern edge, where the filtration housings stand white against the dark valley, and you press your hand flat and you wait. The vibration moves through your palm, your wrist, your forearm. It is not mechanical. The engines are mechanical, and their vibration is regular, patterned, the vibration of things that do the same thing over and over. This is not that. This is the vibration of something that is not doing a thing but being a thing, being the thing it is, which is the thing it has always been, which is the movement of water through stone, which is the memory of water, which is the way stone holds what has passed through it and keeps passing through it.

The city persists because the plateau allows it. The plateau allows it because the city fits, because the city's engineers listened well enough to build along the lines the water had already drawn. This is not a symbiosis. There is no mutualism here, no exchange. The plateau does not need the city. The plateau was here before and will be here after, and the city is a brief thickening on its surface, a temporary complexity in the old pattern. But the city can be here for now, and the city can be dense in its center and then stop, and the city can build its pipes aboveground and its rats in the walls and its workers who listen, and all of this can hold together for as long as it holds together.

In the early morning, before the factories start up, the plateau is quiet in a way that is not silence. The birds are not out yet. The insects are not yet active. The rats are still in their walls. But the water is moving. The water is always moving. The mineral smell rises from the wet stone with the warmth of the rising air, and the vibration continues beneath everything, patient and old and complete.
