# What Lives Here

What lives here lives in spite of things.

The water came first, or rather, the water came last — filling this space the way grief fills a shape made for something else. It seeps and condenses and collects, thirty million liters or more, sitting in its engineered cradle, doing what water does: pressing, cooling, being patient. The water does not know it was never supposed to be here. The water does not know anything. But it stays, and the cold of it sinks into everything, and the walls show their mineral trails like the gray tears of something that doesn't understand why it's crying.

The carp were someone's idea, once. A private stock, or a public one, or maybe they came through the intake channels from some other waterway, carried in as eggs, as fry, as accidents. They are pale now — not the bright orange of ornamental koi but something nearer to the color of the light, a ghostly gold, translucent at the edges. They move slowly, these fish, because the water is cold and their blood is cold and everything slows in the deep. They have grown large, some of them, because there is no one to catch them except the fishermen who come and go, and because the Reservoir is a trap that keeps giving and giving, a place where nothing leaves except what is carried. The carp are not beautiful. They are not ugly. They are simply present — ten thousand or fifty thousand of them, drifting through the columned dark like the slow thoughts of some dreaming thing.

Above the water, in the mid-levels, the rats have made their nation. They came through the same tunnels the fishermen use, or different ones — there are many tunnels, a whole circulatory system of passages designed for maintenance and drainage and the movement of workers who no longer exist. The rats have built warrens in the abandoned structures, in the drowned lower floors of the towers where the wood has held and the wiring has been gnawed into nests. They are brown rats, common rats, Rattus norvegicus, the same species that has followed humans everywhere humans have gone. They are well-fed here: they eat the pigeons when they can catch them, the occasional fish that washes up dead, the crumbs left by fishermen, the insulation off wires, each other. Their population swells and crashes with the seasons, with the food, with the diseases that pass through dense populations of small mammals. In the high spaces, you can hear them moving — the quick scurry, the high chitter of alarm, the sound of many small lives conducting their business just out of sight.

The pigeons are louder. They live in the upper reaches, in the hexagonal lattice of the ceiling, in the spaces where the light fixtures mount and the pipes run their long horizontal courses. They coo to each other in the vaulted dark, a sound that bounces and multiplies until it seems like there are hundreds when there are only dozens. They are feral pigeons, Columba livia, descendants of rock doves that humans once kept for messages and for meat and for the simple pleasure of their iridescent throats. They have done well here. The Reservoir is, in its way, a cliff face — a vertical world of ledges and hollows, safe from the ground-bound predators, lit by the steady orange glow that never flickers. The pigeons eat what falls: insects breeding in the condensation, seeds tracked in on boots, the occasional handout from a fisherman who feels sorry for them. They have no natural predators in the deep places, except the rats when they're young or careless, and so they thrive. At dusk, they cluster on the higher platforms, a hundred birds or more, preening and calling, and the sound of them fills the hollow space like a congregation.

The fishermen are harder to describe, because they are not permanent residents. They come and they go, passing through the old service tunnels with their rods and their buckets and their patience. They sit on the walkways for hours, lowering their lines into the cold, and they talk quietly or not at all, and sometimes they pull up carp that they'll clean and cook or carp that they'll throw back. They are men, mostly — older men, younger men who learned from the older ones, the way these things are passed down. They don't talk about why they come here, or what they're looking for. They don't talk about the illegality, or the strangeness, or the way the water seems to hold the cold in it like a grudge. They come because the Reservoir is close, and because the fish are easy, and because there is something about the place that rewards attention. They leave their traces: bait boxes, cigarette butts, the worn patches on the railings where hands have gripped. When they are gone, the Reservoir returns to its mechanical silence, its drip and hum, its slow revolution of air and light and cold.

The pumps are alive, in their way. They have been alive for centuries, these machines, cycling water through channels and overflow pipes, maintaining a level that no one asks them to maintain. They hum in frequencies you feel before you hear — a vibration in the walkway, a resonance in the chest. They are tended by no one now, or by someone very far away, or by automated systems that have long since forgotten their original instructions. They run because they were built to run. They will run until they break, and then other pumps will take over, or won't, and the water will find its own level, and the space will become something else. But for now, the pumps breathe for the Reservoir, and the lights burn, and the system keeps its useless schedule.

There are smaller lives, too. Mosquitoes breed in the standing water that gathers on flat surfaces, their larvae wriggling in the thin films. Mold grows on the abandoned belongings in the upper floors — clothes, books, furniture that someone once needed and then left behind. Bacteria live in the water, processing the slow input of organic matter, keeping the system in its fragile balance. The air itself holds life: spores and dust and the particulates that drift through the industrial light like lazy snow. The Reservoir is not sterile. It has never been sterile. Even in the most engineered spaces, life finds its purchase, its angle of attack, its way of being.

And then there is the place itself — the Reservoir, the space, the engineered void. It does not live, not in any biological sense. But it endures. It persists. It holds its shape in the dark, its walls and its walkways and its hexagonal ceiling, its thousand fixtures still burning, its pumps still cycling. It was built for an emergency that never came, and now it is full of water and life and the slow sound of things doing what they were not designed for. It is too large for what it contains. It is a room without furniture, a cathedral without a god, a body still breathing after the mind has gone.

What lives here lives in spite of things. The cold. The dark. The scale. The purposelessness of the machines that keep it all running. Life does not ask for meaning. Life only asks for a place to be, and it finds that place, and it stays, and it goes on, and it makes the cold space into something that can be lived in. The pigeons coo in the high places. The rats run their circuits. The fishermen come and go with their patient lines. The carp drift through the deep water like slow thoughts, and the pumps hum their frequency, and the water holds the cold in it like a lesson no one remembers learning.

This is what lives here: the small and the patient and the present. Life doing what life does. Making a home out of an emergency. Making a world out of a waiting room. Making something lived-in out of a place that was built to never be used at all.
