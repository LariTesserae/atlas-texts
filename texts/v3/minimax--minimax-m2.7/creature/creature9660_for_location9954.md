What lives here lives quietly.

The plants have made their peace with this place. You will find them pressed against the upper edges of housings where runoff collects in the slight depressions that rust and time have made. They are not one species but several, tangled together in the way of things that have agreed to coexist because neither could survive alone. A grass, perhaps, or something that was once grass and has become its own thin-bladed thing, pale where green should be, the color of water that has carried too much. Beside it, something with wider leaves but not wide, not the broad full leaves of places with real sun, but leaves that have spread as much as they can in the dim orange glow. They grow slowly. They die slowly. They are replaced slowly by others that spring from the same shallow beds of substrate-ash that has learned to hold moisture the way other ash cannot.

The roots do not go deep. There is nowhere deep to go. Beneath the substrate-ash is more substrate-ash, layers of it compressed by their own weight and by the settling of the structures above. The roots find the seams, though. Given enough time, roots find every seam, and here they have had time. You will see them threading through the gaps between pipe and housing, pale white threads like veins, drinking from the moisture that moves through every joint. The plants drink what the machines leak. It is enough.

In the pools—the dark ones that gather where the substrate dips—you might see movement. Not fish. Nothing so defined. Something smaller, flatter, something that has made its body into a shape suited to this place: a body that can tolerate the mineral load, that can survive on what filters down from the processes above. It has no name because no one has been here to name it. Perhaps it is a kind of worm, or a kind of insect that has forgotten its wings, or perhaps it is something that has never been anything else, something that emerged from the conditions here the way life emerges from conditions everywhere: fitfully, persistently, without intention.

You cannot see it clearly. The light does not permit clear seeing. But you can see the water dimple where it passes, the surface tension broken by a presence that has chosen to move rather than wait. It moves toward the edges where the water is warmer, where the runoff enters, where the mineral concentration is highest. It has learned, over whatever span of time it operates on, where warmth gathers. Everything here gathers where warmth gathers.

The structures themselves are not alive, but they are not empty either. There are spaces within them—chambers that once held processes and now hold what processes leave behind when they continue past their purpose. Moisture, first of all. The walls weep, as you have noticed, and the weeping collects in channels that the builders made or that moisture made for itself, the same channels that water takes everywhere once water is present. In these chambers, where the light from distant vents does not reach and the sound is muffled to a low throb, there is something else. You would not call it life if you met it elsewhere. But here, in the dark and the damp and the mineral weight of air that has passed through things that were never meant to be breathed, here it persists.

It does not move when you enter. It has no reason to fear you—you are as strange to it as it is to you, both of you moving through a space that was built for something else. It is patient the way the basin is patient, the way the substrate is patient, the way everything here has become patient because patience is what remains when purpose has gone. It may be feeding on the residue that coats the inner walls, the accumulated compounds that the processes have left behind over years beyond counting. It may be feeding on the moisture itself, on the minerals that the moisture carries. It has made itself from what is here. It is here because it could be here, and being here is the only requirement.

You will hear it only if you are still. Even then, you may not hear it over the sound of the machines. But if you are still enough, if you give the sound time to flatten into background, you may hear a faint movement—not footsteps, not chewing, but something softer. The shift of a body against a surface. The settling of a weight that has decided to move from one place to another. It does not vocalize. The machines have taken the sound.

The birds came once, perhaps. Or something like birds. But there is nothing to eat here that birds could eat, and the sound drives them away, and the air does not carry what air should carry. What remains has no need of flight. What remains fits into the spaces between, into the gaps that the machines have left, into the substrate-ash that offers shelter in its looser moments. It is the size of a hand. It has the color of rust, or of the substrate when wet, or of nothing at all because it is rarely seen and has no reason to be seen by anything that might see it.

It is not one but several. You will never see them together, never know their number, but you will see evidence of them: a trace of passage, a darker smudge on a lighter surface, a displacement of the fine material that covers the substrate in places. They move at night, or at what passes for night here when the glows dim slightly and the cloud cover thickens or thins in ways you cannot predict. They move in the dark because the dark is theirs, because they were made for it or made themselves in it, because darkness here is not absence but presence, not emptiness but density.

The moisture moves through them too. Their bodies are wet the way all bodies are wet here, saturated with the mineral water that has made this place what it is. They drink by touching—pressing their bodies to surfaces where moisture gathers, letting it seep through membranes that have adapted to take what they need and let the rest pass. They do not urinate. Nothing here flushes. What they take in, they keep, cycling it through bodies that have learned to hold salts, to precipitate the excess, to leave traces of white on the surfaces where they rest. You will see these traces if you look. Small marks, faint, the only language they have.

The vegetation has flowers, sometimes. Tiny things, easily missed, that open in the brief moments when the light seems stronger. They are not flowers that attract pollinators—there are no pollinators here, or none that the flowers would recognize. They open because opening is what flowers do, because the signal to open has been encoded so long that it persists without cause. The signal was once response to light, or to temperature, or to the presence of something that would carry pollen from one bloom to another. Now it is response to nothing, or to the machines, or to cycles in the moisture that we cannot sense. They open, and the moisture catches them, weighs them down, and they close again, and the cycle continues.

The basin holds all of this. The basin holds you, as you walk, as you stop, as you press your hand against a housing that thrums with the sound of something deep within. You are not what lives here. You are passing through. But you leave your mark too—a footprint in the substrate that will fill with water and salt and be gone by morning. A handprint on metal that will oxidize and fade and become part of the patina that this place wears. You are brief, and small, and here, and that is enough. That is what being here means.

The water weeps.
The substrate holds.
The machines continue their running, their grinding, their slow conversation with each other in the language of pressure and heat and the movement of material through channels that have no other purpose now but to move.
And in the spaces between, in the chambers within chambers, in the pools and the seams and the places where light almost reaches, something persists.
It does not thrive. It does not hope. It simply continues, and in the continuing, it is.
