There are things that live on the plateau, and they have learned to live slowly.

The insects are the easiest to understand—perhaps because they are the most ancient, the most refined by time into perfect efficiency. They move in waves through the grass, not as individual creatures but as something closer to weather, a shifting density of bodies that parts and reforms around obstacles. At certain hours, the grass seems almost alive with them, each tussock hosting dozens, hundreds, their movements coordinated in ways that have nothing to do with communication as you understand it. They are simply very old, and very patient, and their patterns have had time to become flawless. When you watch them, you might notice that the waves seem to pulse in a rhythm that doesn't quite match the wind. The wind has its own rhythm, and the insects have theirs, and they overlap without ever quite aligning, like two melodies played in different time signatures that somehow harmonize anyway.

The reptiles are less numerous but more visible, basking on the low ridges of exposed stone that break through the soil like bones through thin skin. They are flat, these creatures—flattened by generations of selection into shapes that maximize their contact with warm rock, with warm air, with the thin heat that rises from the plateau's red earth even in the cool hours. Their scales catch light in ways that seem slightly too deliberate, too iridescent, as if they have learned to use the sun's energy more efficiently than any surface should allow. They do not flee when observed. They do not flee at all, in fact—movement seems to cost them something precious, so they simply remain, watching with eyes that hold a patience older than the stones beneath them. Sometimes, in the late afternoon, you might see them gathered in loose clusters on the same rock, stacked three or four deep, their bodies overlapping in arrangements that seem more purposeful than random. You learn not to look too closely at those arrangements either.

The birds come and go, and their presence is the closest the plateau gets to spectacle. Murmurations roll across the sky in formations that seem to follow rules you could almost deduce—until you watch long enough to realize that the rules are changing, that what looked like a response to a predator's shadow is actually something else, a following of some internal map that they share without seeming to communicate. They are not the birds you know. Their shapes are close—close enough that at a distance, in certain light, you might swear you were looking at starlings or swallows or the small brown things that might be antelope-feeders in a wetter place—but their movements are wrong, or perhaps more right, or perhaps right in a way that your eyes cannot parse because they evolved watching things that move differently. When they turn, they turn together in a way that should require communication but doesn't. When they settle, they settle in numbers that seem impossible given the sparse vegetation, as if they are drawing something from the air itself, some nutrient or energy that the plateau provides in abundance despite its apparent poverty. Their cries, when they come, are not calls. They are acknowledgments. Confirmations. The plateau speaking to itself through a mouth it has grown.

And then there are the herd animals, the dark bodies that move in streams across the landscape, their numbers vast enough that you see them in the distance as a shifting darkness, a slow tide that has come in from some direction and will flow out again in the same direction, as if answering some ancient tide-lure. They are quadrupedal, or seem to be, though you would be hard-pressed to count their legs from a distance—they move in ways that suggest more than four, or fewer, or legs that have become something else in the long slow work of adaptation. They do not run. They flow. Their bodies are dark, a darkness that seems to absorb light rather than reflect it, and when the fog comes and the world goes pale, they become silhouettes, outlines, shapes that might be anything. You learn not to approach them. You learn that the space between you and them is not simply distance—it is a territory they understand and you do not, and you would be trespassing in a way that has nothing to do with property or ownership. When they pass through a shallow depression, they do not disturb the water. They are already past before the water has time to ripple. You are not sure whether this is fast or slow. You think it might be neither. You think it might be something the plateau does to time when it needs to.

But the plateau does not need to. The plateau has time, and the strange fauna lives in that time, and the strange fauna is not strange to the plateau—only strange to you, and you are the visitor here, the newcomer, the brief thing that has wandered up from the lower lands with your watcher's eyes and your need to name what you see. The strange fauna does not need your naming. It has its own names, its own categories, and they are old enough to have worn smooth, to have become as natural as the stones.

What are they, then? You could say they are evolved, and you would be right—they have been here long enough to become exactly what the plateau requires of them, no more and no less. You could say they are wrong, and you would also be right, in your way—they do not move like things that evolved on a young world, a world still full of urgency and desperation. They move like things that have won. They have won the time to be patient. They have won the time to be still.

Watch them, if you can bear it. Watch the herd animals pulse in their slow rhythm, that rhythm that has nothing to do with wind or weather or hunger or fear. Watch the shapes that are almost familiar but whose proportions shift when you look away, as if they are only approximations, only attempts at form, things that have not yet decided what they want to be—except that they are decided, they have been decided for millions of years, and what you are seeing is the result of that deciding. Watch the patterns that are too synchronized, too deliberate, and understand that you are seeing something that is not coordinated by communication or instinct or even memory, but by something deeper, something that lives in the land itself, in the iron-red soil, in the shallow depressions that hold water and then don't, in the low ridges of dark stone that feel more geological fault than natural formation. The plateau is patient, and the strange fauna has learned to be patient with it, and their patience is a kind of knowledge, a knowledge of being, of continuing, of persisting through all the small emergencies that would seem so enormous if you were the one living them.

You are not the one living them. You are the one watching. And the watching, on the plateau, is itself a kind of living—a slow communion with something that does not need you, that would continue unchanged if you never came, that will continue unchanged long after you are gone. You are a ripple in the water, and the water has already stilled, and the shape you made is already being worn smooth by time.

There are other things, too, that live on the plateau—things that are not fauna, exactly, or not fauna as you understand it. There are presences that do not have bodies, or have bodies that are not not bodies, that exist in some relationship to matter that is more intimate than inhabitation and less separable than possession. They move through the plateau the way heat moves through stone, the way water moves through soil. They are not visible, but they are visible—not in the sense that they appear when you look, but in the sense that their absence, when they are somewhere else, is noticeable, a thinning, a place where the air feels less heavy with presence. You learn to sense them, if you stay long enough. You learn that the hum of life at scale includes frequencies you cannot hear, wavelengths you cannot see, presences you cannot name but can feel, a pressure at the edge of perception, a weight that is not weight.

These are the oldest inhabitants. The fauna is new by comparison—new, in the way that ten million years is new, in the way that anything that has not yet finished becoming is new. The presences have finished. They are as settled as the stone beneath your feet, as patient as the plateau itself. They do not move, because movement is unnecessary. They do not eat, because eating is unnecessary. They simply are, and their being fills the plateau the way water fills a shallow depression before it soaks away, except that it never soaks away, it stays, it has always stayed, it will always stay until the stone itself is gone and the plateau is sand and the sand is nothing.

You feel them sometimes. Late at night, when the fog has come in and the world is white and formless, when the herd animals have stopped their slow pulse and the reptiles are still and the birds are gone to wherever birds go, you feel the plateau breathing. Not the wind, not the fog, not the rustle of grass or the distant pad of hooves. Something beneath that. Something in the stone itself, in the ancient compressed patience of a landscape that has given up its mountains and settled into its final form. It is not breathing as you breathe. It is breathing as the earth breathes, in cycles too slow to perceive, in rhythms too vast to measure. You are lying on the red soil, and you are warm, and the fog is cool on your face, and you are part of the plateau now, part of the hum, part of the held breath.

You have become part of the strange fauna. Not one of them—you will never be one of them, you cannot be, you are too urgent, too desperate, too full of the small hungers of brief things. But part of them, yes. A ripple in their water. A note in their hum. A brief, bright, impossibly fast flash of something that will be gone before the stone beneath you has time to notice it was ever there.

The plateau does not mourn its brief guests. It does not celebrate them either. It acknowledges them, the way it acknowledges the fog, the way it acknowledges the passing of the herd animals through its shallowest depressions. It acknowledges them with the patience that is the only true patience, the patience of a thing that has nothing left to become.

And the strange fauna continues. The reptiles on their warm stones. The insects in their impossible waves. The birds in their murmurations that write and rewrite the sky. The herd animals in their slow pulse, their dark shapes flowing like something liquid, something patient, something that learned long ago how to wait without wanting.

This is what lives on the plateau. This is what has always lived here, and this is what will live here still when the last visitor has come and gone, when the last structure has melted back into earth, when the last name that was ever given to this place has been forgotten and the plateau remains, unnamed, unnameable, the held breath that never needs to exhale because there is no urgency that requires it.

The plateau is patient.

The strange fauna is patient.

And if you have come here looking for something—looking for answers, or wonder, or the simple shock of the unknown—you will find it, yes. You will find it in the shapes that shift, in the rhythms that don't align, in the herd animals that flow around obstacles you cannot see. But you will not find it as something to overcome. You will find it as something to acknowledge. Something that has earned its strangeness through epochs of persistence. Something that is strange the way a mountain is strange, the way an ocean is strange—not because it means you harm, but because it is too large for your comprehension, too patient for your urgency, too old for your time.

You will stand on the plateau and feel very small.

And the plateau, in its patient way, will feel you.
