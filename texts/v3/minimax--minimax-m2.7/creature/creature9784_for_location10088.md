There is a thing that lives in the roots.

Not the roots themselves — those belong to the great trunks, the ancient pale growth-forms that have claimed the high ground since before the glaciers remembered how to retreat. But *between* the roots, in the loam that never quite dries, in the wet black soil that smells of old iron and older patience, there is something that the Uplands have grown rather than grown from. A network of response. A distributed sensitivity.

You would not call it an animal, though it moves. You would not call it a plant, though it photosynthesizes in the strange weak light that filters through the canopy — but not with leaves, not with any green thing you might recognize, only with pale filaments that unspool along the surface of the soil like the nervous system of some forgotten god. It drinks. It waits. It responds.

When you walk the Uplands — if you walk the Uplands, pushing through the closing walls of frond and runner, fighting the humid grip of the air — you leave a trace. Your feet compress the loam. Your body displaces the damp. Your warmth bleeds outward into the cold and the cold notices. And the thing in the roots notices too.

It does not attack. The Uplands are not a place of violence, not exactly — the violence here is too slow for that, too patient, the kind of violence that is simply *continuation*, the long argument of growth against stone. What the thing in the roots does is *record*. Your weight becomes a memory in the soil. Your passage becomes a shape in the network of filaments, a diagram of where-you-were that persists long after you have left, that may persist long after you have died, a map of a life in a place that does not care about lives except as one more element to be processed, decomposed, transformed into the thick sweet chemistry that feeds the great trunks and their endless reaching.

The first time you walk here, you will leave no mark that you can see. The second time, perhaps, a frond will turn toward where your foot fell. The third time — if you are foolish enough to return a third time — the path will not close behind you so quickly. A gap will hold. An opening will persist. Not because the vegetation has been damaged, not because you have torn anything free, but because something *remembers*, and what it remembers is the shape of your passage, and it has decided — if decision is even the right word for a distributed consciousness of root and filament and old dark soil — that you are something worth accommodating.

Or perhaps worth digesting. The distinction, in the Uplands, may be less clear than you would prefer.

Above the roots, in the middle heights where the mist gathers and the light arrives in reluctant shafts, there are things that might be birds if birds were made of wet paper and the memory of flight. They do not fly so much as *drift* — their wings, if wings is the word, are not feathers but membranes, translucent and delicate, stretched between bones that curve like the letter for which they might be named. They eat what falls: spores, the occasional insect, the moisture itself drawn through membranes thin enough to see light through. They nest in the crooks of the great pale trunks, building structures from their own shed membranes and something else — a material that seems to be manufactured, extruded from some gland at the base of the throat, a wet gray paste that hardens in the cold into something like papier-mâché, something like skin.

The locals — those few who have made their homes in the Uplands, those few who have found the narrow places where the great trunks leave gaps enough for passage, those few who have learned which fungi are safe to eat and which will stop your heart with a kind of polite apology — they call the birds *moths*. Not because they resemble moths in any honest way, but because the word, in their language, means something like *the ones who carry news*. In the Uplands, news is rarely good news, and the moths are associated with announcements: a death, a change in the weather, a clearing that has closed, a closed path that has opened. The moths do not carry messages in any way you might understand. They simply *appear* in the moments after something has shifted, their pale wings catching the thin light, their thin bodies swaying on perches of fungal shelf and root. The locals watch them. The locals listen to the almost-sound that the moths make — a vibration that is not quite sound, a frequency below hearing that registers as a kind of pressure in the sinuses, a sense of something being communicated.

The locals say the moths are not animals. The locals say the moths are *symptoms* — that they are the visible manifestation of a change that has already happened, a change that the Uplands have decided upon, and the moths are simply the way that decision becomes apparent to those who have not yet learned to read the language of roots and rot.

There are other things.

In the deepest hours — the hours when the polar night presses close and the mist thickens to something that might be touched, might be held, might be breathed — there are shapes that move in the canopy. Not the birds, not the moths, but something larger, something that the canopy was not designed to bear but bears anyway, because the Uplands are not a place where design matters, only continuation. These shapes are hard to describe because they are hard to see, because the light does not reach them, because they exist in the spaces between the spaces, in the gaps that the great trunks leave when they do not quite meet. They might be primates. They might be something older, something that used to be primates and has spent enough time in the Uplands to become something else, something adapted to the cold and the damp and the long slow chemistry of decay. They move in the canopy the way water moves — not with intention, not with direction, but with the inevitability of physics, the patience of gravity, the simple fact that what is heavy falls and what falls finds its level and what finds its level stays.

The locals do not speak of these shapes. The locals know that speaking of something in the Uplands gives it a kind of existence, a kind of claim. The locals are careful with their words here. The locals have learned.

And there is, at the very top of the Uplands — at the place where the great trunks thin and the mist clears for just a moment, at the place where the air is thin enough to hurt but clear enough to see — there is a thing that might be a flower, or might be a mouth, or might be both, or might be neither. It opens in the thin light. It opens slowly, over the course of hours, petals the color of old bruises unfolding to reveal not a center but a depth, a darkness that goes down further than light can follow, a darkness that smells of the same mineral sweetness that hangs in the air, the same taste of old chemistry, the same sense of a place that has been processing life for so long that it has forgotten how to do anything else.

The locals do not go there. The locals say that the flower opens for no one, that it opens because it is what flowers do, that it opens because the Uplands require it to open, that it opens because somewhere in the vast network of root and filament and ancient loam, something has decided to flower, has decided that the time is right, has decided that this is how the conversation continues.

And in the darkness at the center of the flower, something waits.

It is patient, like everything in the Uplands. It is still, like everything in the Uplands. It is quiet, like everything in the Uplands.

It is, perhaps, alive. Or it is alive the way the Uplands are alive — not with the quick bright life of animals, not with the green hopeful life of growing things, but with the slow dark life of decomposition, the patient life of chemistry, the life that says: *I will wait. I have always been waiting. I will wait until you are ready to become part of me, and then I will wait some more, because that is what I am, and that is what the Uplands have made me, and that is what I will remain until the mountains themselves forget how to hold their shapes and the mist clears for the last time and the great pale trunks finally fall and the loam reclaims what the loam has always been owed.*

The flower opens.

The mist closes.

Somewhere below, in the roots, something records the temperature, the pressure, the exact quality of the silence, and waits.
