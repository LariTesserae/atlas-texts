# The Creatures of the Basin

Begin with what you can hold: the size of them. Most are small enough to cup in two hands, though their bodies resist that gesture, resist being held. The smallest are the most comprehensible—brief movements in the wet soil, segmented lengths threading through root systems, bodies that might be called worms if the segmentation were simpler, if the ends did not sometimes branch. They are the easiest to look at, and so the eye finds them first, lingers, and moves on only reluctantly to what lies beyond.

The larger creatures are harder. They move through the plants with a fluid predator's ease, and as you watch them—carefully, obliquely, the way the light itself seems to advise—you begin to understand that you cannot watch them. Not for long. Your mind slides off their geometries the way water slides off the weeping stone, finding no purchase, returning to the question of what they are with no more purchase than it started.

Their bodies are organized according to principles that work but that you cannot hold in your mind for long. Surfaces that fold inward and outward at once, or that seem to fold but are in fact continuous, or that the eye insists are folding while the reason insists they cannot. Appendages that emerge in numbers that shift when you are not counting—seven when you began counting, nine by the time you reached the fifth, and by the seventh you have lost the count entirely and are only sure that there were too many, and that they are still moving, and that you cannot say which end is which.

Some of them carry light. Not bioluminescence exactly—the light is sourceless, the same sourceless bright that falls through the air above the basin, and it moves with them in ways that suggest either ownership or imprisonment. A creature with this light is easier to track, easier to follow with the eye, and the eye is grateful for the simplicity. But when the light shifts, when it catches on a surface or bends around an edge, you see what the light reveals: the fine hair along a spine, yes, but the spine is not where a spine should be, and the hair is not quite hair, and the word *spine* comes out of your mouth before you mean it and you understand that you have made a mistake, that you have named this thing as if it were something that could be named, and the creature pauses, turns toward you, and its compound eyes—

Its compound eyes are many. They are arranged in patterns that suggest intention but the intention is not your intention, is not any intention you can participate in, and the eyes do not blink, do not waver, and the creature does not move and the creature does not look away, and the moment extends, and the light finds you too, finds the small wet place where you stand at the edge of what you can hold, and you understand that the creatures have always known you were here, that they have been watching you the way you have been watching them, that the basin holds you all in its wet patience, and that some of the creatures have mouthparts that open and close in patterns you almost understand.

The mouthparts are the worst. Not because they are threatening—you are not certain they could eat you, though the hunting suggests that some of them eat others like themselves—but because they are almost legible. Patterns that seem to be communication, that seem to be language, that move in rhythms your mouth wants to echo. You almost say the words. You almost make the sounds. And then the creature moves, or the light shifts, or a second creature passes between you and the first, and the pattern breaks, and you are left with the shape of the word in your mouth and no way to speak it, and the creatures continue their slow motion through the basin, and the basin continues its erosion, and nothing is named, nothing is spoken, nothing is understood, and everything is seen.

There are thousands of them. You count a hundred. You count a hundred and twenty. Then one of them moves in a way that shows you its underside, its symmetry or lack of symmetry, its organs or their equivalents, and the count is lost, and you are only aware of the number in the way you are aware of the water—always present, always moving, impossible to grasp.

They avoid the structures. The ones built from something that looks almost like bone but rings like glass when struck, the ones that hum at frequencies just below hearing, the ones whose surfaces are warm. The creatures do not approach them. When they move through the basin they flow around the structures like water around stone, and the paths they take are curved, deliberate, and the curvedness is the only evidence of intention that you find in them—that, and the way the ones with the almost-language pause when they are near the structures, their mouthparts moving, and the way the structures hum louder in response, and the way the light falls differently there, brighter and flatter, and the way the structures hold what might be silence or might be attention, and the way the creatures eventually move on, leaving the structures standing in their small clearings, warm and humming and patient.

Some of the creatures are wounded. You see scars that do not heal, missing appendages that do not regenerate, patterns of damage on bodies that should not be able to survive damage. The hunting is not clean. It is slow, iterative, spread across time in ways that suggest long campaigns, old grudges, the patience of things that do not age. You see a creature with a wound in its side, a fold that did not close, a glimpse of inner structure. It moves normally. It moves well. It moves as if the wound is not a wound but a feature, a door left open, an absence that has been there so long it has become architecture.

You do not know what they eat. The hunting suggests predation, and some of them have mouthparts designed for more than the patterns of almost-language. But the water seeps upward through the ancient stone, and the plants grow, and the moisture condenses, and the basin is perpetually wet and perpetually warm, and some of the creatures press their undersides to the wet stone and stay there, and the creatures seem to feed on the basin itself, on the water, on the light, on the warmth that comes from nowhere and everywhere, and the hunting might be something else—not hunger but ceremony, not eating but becoming—and you cannot know, and the not-knowing is the shape of the basin, is the shape of your presence here, is the shape of what you will carry out of this place when you leave, if you leave, if the basin lets you go.

One last thing: they are beautiful. This is the hardest part to hold, the part that stays with you even when everything else has gone soft, gone patient, gone back to the stone. They move through the light and the wet with a grace that has nothing to do with your understanding, that exists in spite of your understanding, and the beauty is not the beauty of forms you can name but the beauty of something that exists completely, that has never needed a name, that is only and entirely itself, and that looks at you with its many shifting eyes, and that does not blink, does not look away, does not leave, and the basin holds it, and the basin holds you, and the light falls, and the water seeps, and the creatures continue, and the erosion continues, and the strange technology hums, and something is always happening, always being eaten or grown or transformed, and the basin is still here, still going, after all this time, and the creatures are still here, and you are still here, and you do not know what you are, not anymore, not in this place where the fauna is too much and the symmetries are wrong and the mouthparts almost speak and the light finds you and the water weeps and the structures hum and nothing is named and everything is seen.
