What lives here is everything that has learned to wait.

The ferns are the oldest inheritors—fronds unfurling in the damp darkness, drinking the mineral water that seeps through stone, growing slow as memory. They have colonized every surface, draped themselves over the collapsed archway, threaded their roots into cracks that once held mortar. They do not know they are standing in a doorway. They do not know the arch was once purposeful. They only know moisture, and stone, and the patient work of growing. Their green is paler than the green of sunlight—ghostly, ethereal, the color of things that have adapted to live without the thing most plants cannot live without. They are the true natives now, more native than the people who built these walls, more native than the stone itself, which is still learning its new shape.

The small trees are more recent arrivals—seeds dropped by birds that found their way here, that carried the genetic memory of "drop this here" without knowing why. They are gnarled, twisted, their trunks bent by the weight of stone above them. They will not grow tall; they will grow wide, finding every seam, every crack, spreading themselves across surfaces the way water spreads itself. Their leaves are broad and pale, designed for dim light, for the thin gold threads that fall through fissures in the ceiling. They are patient in the way of all slow-growing things, and they are permanent in the way of things that do not need to hurry.

The moss is everywhere—a living skin over everything, soft and green and ancient in the way of things that do not grow but only spread. It covers the fallen column where the small lizard suns itself. It clothes the corner of the wall whose purpose has been swallowed. It grows on the stones that were once steps descending into water, and on the stones that were once the edge of a pool, and on the stones that were once the threshold of a door. It is the softest thing here, the greenest thing here, the most pervasive thing here. It does not flower; it does not need to. It simply is, and it is enough.

The vines are the explorers, the ones still searching. Their tendrils probe every surface, testing, reaching, finding. They are patient as water, patient as stone, patient as time. They do not know what they are looking for; they only know that somewhere ahead is another surface, another crack, another place to grow. They will reach the far wall eventually, and then the next wall, and then the ceiling, and then the world beyond the ceiling, if there is one, if they can find it. They are the longest-distance travelers here, the ones who measure space in tendrils rather than meters.

The flowers are recent, almost impulsive—small white faces and yellow faces that open in the dim light, blooming for insects that may or may not come. They are going through the motions of their ancestors, the rituals of pollination, without knowing that the ritual is mostly unnecessary here. They bloom anyway. They color the darkness anyway. They are a kind of hope, if hope could be a yellow flower opening its face to nothing.

The fungi are the memory of wood. They consume the beams that no longer exist, the supports that have becomeite their bodies. They are the past made present, the dead made useful, the forgotten made into food for new life. They are pale and patient and everywhere in the darkness where the light does not reach. They are the ones who will, in time, reduce the ruins to soil, the soil to nothing, the nothing to the memory of something that was once something.

What lives here is the small quick life—the cave crickets beneath the stones, pale and eyeless and adapted to the dark. They scuttle more than they crawl, quick movements in the cracks, eating the dead things that fall, the rot that gathers, the detritus of the world above. They do not sing; they do not need to. They are the recyclers, the janitors, the ones who keep the floor clean of its own dead. They are a life that knows its place in the circle and does not try to rise above it.

The bat is a visitor, a bridge between worlds. It comes through the high crevices when the evening cools the stone above, and it hunts the cave crickets and the mosquitoes and the small moths that flutter near the light. It carries seeds on its wings, spores on its fur, the genetics of the outside world into the sealed darkness. It is the only regular reminder that there is an outside, that this pocket of time is connected to other pockets, that the world is larger than this green and gold and stone. It does not stay long. It cannot, really. The darkness is not its home; it is only a hunting ground.

The lizard is a remnant—a thing out of time, a creature of light and warmth resting on a fallen column that was once upright. It basks in the thin sunlight that falls on the stone, warming its blood, snapping at insects that drift too close. It is more out of place than it knows. It belongs to the world before the ceiling fell, the world when the ruins were still walls, the world when the water was just water and not yet the mineral-bright pool it has become. It does not know this. It only knows the warmth, and the insects, and the stone beneath its feet.

The bird that found its way in is lost or trapped or both. It came through some passage that is now sealed, or through some crack that is too small to return through. It calls out, sometimes, a small voice in the large darkness, and no one answers. It will die here, eventually, and the fungi will consume it, and its bones will becomeite the soil, and the ferns will grow from its minerals. It will become the place, and the place will remember it without knowing it remembers it, the way the stone remembers being liquid, the way the ruins remember being built.

What lives here is the pool life—the shrimps translucent as glass, their bodies the color of water, their movements quick and convulsive. They are almost nothing, almost invisible, a flicker in the shallows. They eat the microscopic things, the detritus, the fall of leaves and insects and birds. They are eaten by the small quick things, the things that rise, the things that wait. They are the base of a food chain that exists only here, a world within a world, a universe of moving specks in the mineral water.

The mosquito larvae are the harvest, the future, the potential. They crowd the shallows, tiny black specks, feeding on the water itself, on the life that lives in the water. They are the meal for the shrimps, for the insects, for the things that are themselves meals for other things. They are also the future bites, the future itches, the future that will rise into the air and seek blood and become the thing that spreads and spreads and spreads. But not yet. Now they are only specks, only swimmers, only part of the water.

And in the darkness beyond the light, something breathes. I do not know what it is. Perhaps only the stone, expanding and contracting with the slow pulse of the earth. Perhaps only water, moving through passages I cannot see. Perhaps only air, moving through air, the breath of a world that does not know it is breathing. Or perhaps something does live there, in the deep dark, in the places where the light does not reach, in the water that flows somewhere else, in the passages that lead to other caverns, other sealed pockets of time, other worlds within worlds. It breathes, and I do not hear it, and it does not need me to hear it.

What lives here is the old settlement, too—the ghosts of the people who built this place, who carved it from the stone, who lived and died and left their ruins to the moss. They are not alive, not anymore, not in any way I can name. But they are here, their walls and their steps and their hollows, their understanding of this space made manifest in stone that has now forgotten their understanding. The ferns do not know they are standing in a doorway. The moss does not know it is growing on a lintel. The water does not know it is pooling in a cistern. But the shape persists, the purpose persists, the memory persists in the stone itself, in the way the walls still stand, in the way the steps still descend, in the way the place is still, after all this time, a place that was made to be lived in.

What lives here is everything that was and everything that is and everything that will be. It is the slow erosion of basalt, the patient growth of ferns, the quick lives of crickets and shrimps and larvae. It is the light falling in threads, the water falling in threads, the life going on in threads. It is the decay and the renewal, the forgetting and the remembering, the silence that is not emptiness but attention.

What lives here is a world that does not need to be witnessed to be complete. It is enough unto itself, a pocket of time sealed away from the larger world, where ancient settlement and infant geology and persistent life have found a way to share the same small darkness. It goes on. It has always gone on. It will always go on.

And now, for a moment, it is witnessed. And that is something, too—a small addition to the record of this place, a new thread in the tapestry of its going-on. The observer becomes part of the observed, the witnessed becomes part of the witness, and the circle continues, and the moss grows, and the water falls, and the ferns uncurl, and everything that lives here continues to live, indifferent and alive and patient, waiting for the next moment, and the next, and the next.
