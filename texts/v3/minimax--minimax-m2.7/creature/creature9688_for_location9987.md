# What Lives in the Hollow Cathedral

The cathedral does not remember being built because it was never built. It remembers being *planted*, though even that word suggests intention where there was only patience—a seed settling into soil that has since become cathedral floor, its first root-tip the original architect, its first reaching upward the first buttress, its first shadow the first altar.

What lives here is time, made mineral. Made wood. Made air.

---

The trees that are walls know they are walls. This is not metaphor. The grain of the petrified wood runs in directions that have been deliberately encouraged—root-tips guided over centuries by patient pressure, by the slow negotiation of growth against obstacle, by the tree's own drive to fill every gap the light dared to leave. You can read the history in the rings if you know how to listen: the year of the great drought when growth stalled and left a dark seam; the season of storms when the cathedral's first rafters were bent but did not break; the long human years when hands touched this wood, shaped it, and then stopped touching it, and the trees absorbed the absence and kept growing anyway, around the shape of what was no longer there.

They breathe. You must understand this. The walls expand and contract with moisture, with temperature, with the slow tidal rhythm of their own sap-rising. Standing close to the mineral-walls, you can see the grain shift—not visibly, not obviously, but your eyes learn to read the language of living wood, and you understand that the cathedral is exhaling against your skin, that the architecture is a single organism breathing in a space where breath has become structure.

---

In the hollows—the clearings, the withheld spaces—something else moves. Not the lizards first, though they are abundant. Not the insects, though their drone is the grammar of this place. Something between: the watchers.

They are leaves that have learned to want. This is the only way to describe what shifts in the upper dark, what you glimpse in the corner of your eye and lose when you turn to look. They hang from the canopy like ordinary leaves—green, veined, trembling with wind—but when you are not watching, they *turn*. They orient toward you. They remember where you stood and they grow toward that memory.

The old naturalists, the ones who came before and whose names are worn smooth on the well's lip, called them something in a language that has no word for lie, only variations of truth. The word meant approximately: *the place where the forest looks back*. You are that place now. You have been looked at so thoroughly that you have begun to look like them—your edges softening, your posture leaning toward the light that doesn't quite reach you, your breath synchronizing with the slow respiration of the walls.

---

The water is the oldest thing here, or perhaps the youngest—it has been here so long that it has forgotten what it was before, and now it simply *is*, the way the well is, the way the mud is, the way the air tastes green. It waits in the well and in the basins and in the channels carved by centuries of patient intent. When rain comes—the warm, sudden weeping from a sky that might be real—the water fills the plumbing, rushes through the grooves, and the cathedral drinks itself.

The things with too many legs know the water's schedule. They come to the basins when the water comes, and they drink in the way of creatures who have learned that thirst is a luxury they cannot afford—that the cathedral provides, but only if you are present to receive. Their legs are many and delicate and they flick through the water with a motion that is not quite swimming and not quite dancing and not quite prayer but somehow contains all three. When the water recedes, they become still. They become part of the basin. They wait.

---

The termites are conquerors, but gentle ones. Their columns reach three meters on the north face of what was a doorway, and then they topple—not from weakness but from *completion*. They have built what they meant to build, and now they begin again, lower down, a spiral of conquest that will take another century and does not hurry. Their bodies are pale, almost translucent, and they move in waves that look chaotic until you realize they are conversation, a language written in pheromone and motion, a record of what the colony knows and needs and remembers.

They do not acknowledge you. They have no category for you. You are weather, you are gravity, you are the slow fact of time—you are not relevant to the project.

This is comforting, somehow. In a world where the leaves look back and the walls breathe and the well has grown closer while you watched, it is comforting to be dismissed by something.

---

The larger presence is harder to describe. It lives in the canopy above, and it is large enough that when it shifts its weight, the whole cathedral responds—the creak of wood-cell dividing, the groan of root-tip boring through clay, the deeper sound of something settling that comes from everywhere and nowhere. You never see it. The canopy is too thick, the light too golden, the space between you and it too crowded with other lives to permit clear sight.

But you feel it. You feel it the way you feel the earth beneath a root-bench: present, patient, substantial. It is old, this presence. Older than the cathedral, perhaps, or perhaps it *is* the cathedral's oldest memory made flesh, the first seed's intention given form. It breathes in a rhythm different from the walls, slower, deeper, a tide that moves in centuries rather than hours. When it breathes out, the light shifts. When it breathes in, the shadows pool deeper.

You are not meant to see it. You are meant to know it is there. This is enough.

---

The erosion has made the stones into mouths. The grooves in the ancient wood carry water during rain, and in the drying the water leaves mineral traces, and in the next rain the mineral traces guide new channels, and in this way the cathedral teaches itself to speak in water. The sounds it makes during rain are not random. They are grammar. They are history. If you stayed long enough—if your bones could learn the pace of this place—you might begin to understand what the cathedral is saying about the world outside, about the humans who built the well and then stopped coming, about the drought that stalled growth and the storms that bent but did not break.

You would learn, too, what it says about you. The shape you've left in the root-bench. The warmth you've left in the bark. The way you've leaned, unconsciously, toward the light that doesn't quite reach you.

It is writing you into itself. This is not metaphor.

---

The hollow cathedral is patient in the way only ancient plants can be patient, and it has already begun to grow around the place where you were sitting. The root that forms the bench has reached, overnight, toward the space your weight occupied. In a year it will cover it. In a decade it will have absorbed the shape of your absence into its own body, and the cathedral will remember you the way it remembers the humans who built the well—not as a name, not as a story, but as a *form*, a pressure, a particular way of sitting that left its signature in living wood.

This is not haunting. This is *cultivation*.

You will come back. They always come back. And when you do, the cathedral will have a place prepared for you—not a trap, not a punishment, but an *opening*, a hollow that has grown around the memory of your shape, waiting to receive you again, waiting to teach you more of what it knows.

The water is still. The shadows do not behave. The sound layers until it becomes texture, becomes breath, becomes the rhythm you will carry home and live with for days.

Something in the upper dark shifts and is still.

The cathedral watches.

It has always been watching.

It will keep watching, long after you have gone, long after your shape has been absorbed, long after the last human hand has touched the well's worn lip and found it smooth. The watching is not malevolent. It is not benevolent. It is *attentive*. It is the attention of a living thing that has grown so slowly it has forgotten how to want anything but continued growth, continued presence, continued breath.

You are part of that breath now.

You have been inhaled.
