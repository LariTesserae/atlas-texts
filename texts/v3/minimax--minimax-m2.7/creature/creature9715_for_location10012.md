What lives here lives by the mist, and the mist comes before light.

You learn this if you watch long enough. The insects are the first to move with it — small dark shapes that emerge from the ash when the moisture reaches the basin floor, moving with a deliberateness that suggests purpose rather than reflex. They drink from the dew that gathers on leaves, from the wet stone, from each other when necessary. Their bodies are made for this: the dense fine hairs that catch and hold droplets, the exoskeletons that darken as moisture enters. They are not numerous in the way of tropical places, where life crowds every surface. They are numerous in the way of deserts, where existence is a series of calculated appearances — here now, gone when the air dries, returned when it returns.

The beetles are the most visible of these. They move along the stone at the basin's edge, where the dry ground meets the first green, and their shells catch the amber light with a faint iridescence — greens and purples that appear only at certain angles, only when the sun is low. They are flat-bodied and quick, designed for wedging into the smallest cracks, and the cracks here are everywhere. The columnar basalt offers lodging by the thousand, and the beetles use it: they emerge in the pre-dawn darkness, mate in the shelter of stone overhangs, return to the same crevice by midmorning and remain there through the heat of the day. Some of them are old — you can tell by the wear on their shells, the small chips and scratches that accumulate over what must be years. A beetle in this basin lives long enough to develop a history.

The lizards are different. They do not wait for the mist. They are creatures of the stone itself, their scales the color of volcanic ash, their bodies flattened to catch the heat that the dark rock holds long after the sun sets. They move along the upper walls in the early morning, positioning themselves in the thin strips of sunlight that reach the eastern face, and they remain there for hours, torpid and receptive. The heat moves through them. Their eyes, clear and gold, track the small movements of insects below without following. They are not hunters in the way of forest lizards, lying in wait. They are receivers. The energy comes to them through the stone and the sun, and they take it, and they are still.

There are at least three species that you can distinguish by size and behavior. The smallest are the wall-runners — no longer than your finger, gray-brown and quick, they move in tight spirals up the columnar faces and disappear into the crevices that honeycomb the rock. They do not come to the basin floor. The middle-sized lizards are the sunners: they claim the flat stone shelves near the rim, and they are territorial, and you learn to recognize individuals by the small chips missing from their tails or the particular shade of their backs. The largest are rare — one, perhaps two that you see over the course of a day, moving along the basin's edge where the terraced walls meet the natural rock. They are perhaps eight inches long, heavy-bodied, and they do not run. They watch.

The birds are harder to see but easier to hear, in the moments when sound exists here. They nest in the upper walls, in the deep crevices that open to the sky, and they fly in pairs or alone, their wings catching the light as they move between the rim and the basin floor. They are small and dark, swifts or swallows adapted to the thin air, and they move with a speed that makes them difficult to follow. In the evenings they circle the rim, their calls faint and high — not songs but signals, contact calls, the small noises that hold a pair together across the distance of the basin. They eat the insects that rise with the mist, and perhaps the small beetles that venture too far from shelter. They are part of the silence not because they do not make sound but because their sound is so small, so quickly absorbed by the stone, that the silence reasserts itself the moment they stop.

Below the visible life there is a life you cannot see. The soil — such as it is — teems with it: microorganisms that fix the nitrogen that the plants need, mites and springtails in densities you would need a magnifier to understand, fungal threads that connect the root systems of the green plants in networks that may be communication, may be nutrition, may be something else entirely that has no name in the language of the beings who once lived here. The fungi are the hidden architecture of the basin. They exist in the deeper soil, in the rotting base of the larger plants, in the shadows that never touch direct sunlight. Some of them are visible — pale fans spread across the underside of dead leaves, the small shelf fungi that emerge from the base of the thicker vines — but most of them are not. They are the basin's memory, breaking down what has died and returning it to what will live, holding the cycle in place across a span of time that the visible creatures cannot comprehend.

The plants themselves are not uniform. You began by seeing them as a mass — the green mat at the center, the dark vines climbing the walls — but as you look longer you begin to see the individuals. There are at least six species of the broad-leaved plants, distinguished by the shape and texture of their leaves, by the color of their stems, by the way they arrange themselves in relation to each other. Some grow in clusters, their leaves overlapping to trap the mist beneath, creating small humid chambers at the base where the insects shelter. Some grow alone, their taproots descending deep, their leaves held rigid to minimize moisture loss. The vines are another matter entirely — they are parasitic in the way of all vines, taking support from the stone, taking light from the sun, taking water from the roots of their hosts, but the taking is so slow, so distributed, that the host plants do not seem to suffer. The relationship is old. Perhaps it has always existed. Perhaps the vines and the broad-leaved plants and the stone walls and the mist are all one system that has simply continued, without beginning, without end.

And then there are the structures of the former inhabitants.

They are not alive, but they participate in the living of this place. The stone walls of the settlement have become part of the basin's fabric: roots thread through the mortar, breaking it down over centuries; lichens colonize the surfaces, pale green and gray and orange; small creatures nest in the abandoned rooms, leaving trails of droppings and shed skins. The flat roofs collect the mist in the pre-dawn hours, and the moisture runs down the walls in small rivulets that the plants at the base drink from. The doorways — empty now, their fiber screens long since disintegrated — channel the wind that occasionally moves through the basin, creating small currents that the insects use. The channels cut into the basin floor, the small basins that once held water, are used by the plants now: their roots follow the old paths, their leaves shade the stone that was once shaped for purposes no longer remembered.

The inhabitants themselves left no bodies, or none that remain. Perhaps they died here, in the basin, and their remains were absorbed by the same processes that break down all organic matter in this place. Perhaps they left, walking up the steep walls to the highland beyond, and did not return. Perhaps there was a final generation that simply did not reproduce, and the basin held them until they were gone, and then continued. The buildings do not speak of catastrophe. They speak of a gradual withdrawal — rooms still furnished with stone benches, storage areas still holding the desiccated remains of what might have been food, sleeping areas with the impressions of bodies pressed into the packed earth over centuries of use. Someone lived here, and then no one did, and the basin absorbed the difference.

What remains is the presence.

You feel it as you stand at the edge, where the ash meets the first green. Not intelligence, exactly — or not intelligence as you know it. A watching quality. The plants are not sentient but they are oriented: their leaves track the sun, their roots descend toward what they need, their growth responds to the mist with an eagerness that suggests awareness, if not thought. The lizards are not aware of you but they are aware of the place: they know the warmth of their territories, the shade of their crevices, the safe paths across the stone. The insects respond to your footsteps with a momentary stillness, then resume their small tasks. The birds note your presence and circle wide. None of them fear you. They have no reason to fear — you are not a predator, you are not prey, you are only a new thing in a place that has seen new things before and will see them again.

The basin breathes.

Not literally — there is no wind, no motion of air that you can feel — but the mist rises and falls, the light moves across the walls, the creatures emerge and shelter and emerge again, and the whole of it has a rhythm that you can sense if you stand still long enough. A cycle that is not your cycle. A time that is not your time. You are in it now, as long as you remain. The basin does not include you — you are too large, too brief, too separate — but it holds you. The walls rise around you. The light falls on you. The silence receives you as it receives everything that enters, without judgment, without welcome, without anything that can be called response.

You are standing in a place that should not hold so much life.

And yet.
