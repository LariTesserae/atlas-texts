The cave crickets arrive first, or perhaps they were always here — the distinction is meaningless in a place where time has lost its teeth. They cluster on the lower walls where the moisture is thickest, their bodies pressed together in configurations that look like accident but are not. Each cricket holds its position with the stillness of something that has found its exact place in a system too perfect for adjustment. Their antennae move, yes — constant, independent, each one a thin whip testing the mineral air for information that does not change. But their bodies do not move. They have settled into the architecture of the cave the way the calcite has settled into its formations: incrementally, inevitably, into a shape that was always waiting for them.

They are translucent in a way that above-ground crickets never are. The light passes through their exoskeletons and illuminates them from within, so that each joint is a small lamp and the body between is a shadow-filled vessel holding that light, containing it, letting it out in measured points. The glow at the joints is faint — less than the fungi, less than the bacteria, but present, constant, part of the same sourceless illumination that fills every corner of this pocket. A cricket's light does not brighten or dim. It simply is, the way the dripping is simply is, the way the moisture on every surface is simply is. The crickets have not adapted to the light. They have been made by it. Their bodies are shaped around the fact of this brightness the way the stalactites are shaped around the fact of this water.

When they call — and they call often, a low resonant thrum that vibrates through the stone and seems to come from nowhere because it has no single source — the sound moves through the cave in waves that are invisible but not imperceptible. You feel them before you hear them, or rather you feel them in a part of your body that does not usually register sound: the sternum, the palms, the soles of the feet where they rest against the mineral floor. The call is not a song in any functional sense. There is no courtship display, no territory claim, no mating signal that varies with circumstance. The crickets call because calling is what they do, the way the water drips because dripping is what the water does, the way the fungi glow because glowing is what the fungi are. The sound is part of the equilibrium. It belongs.

The beetles are different. They move. Their shells catch the light differently than the crickets' bodies — polished, reflective, each one a small mirror tilted at an angle that throws a brief highlight across the formation it crosses. There are many of them, more than you would expect for a space this size, and they do not cluster. Each beetle moves alone, along paths that cross and recross without pattern, or with a pattern so complex that it only resolves from a distance, from the vantage of time-lapse, from the outside view that no creature inside can take. They click against the stone as they go. The sound is faint — you have to be listening for it — but it is everywhere, a constant static that underlies the dripping and the thrum and the sibilance of the pool. It is the sound of small bodies in motion. It is the sound of being busy without purpose, of movement that does not lead anywhere, of existence expressed as transit.

They do not eat, or they do not eat anything you could see. Their mouthparts are adapted in ways that suggest they feed on the mineral surface itself, or on the bacterial film, or on something that exists in the interstices of this sealed world and has no name because no one from above has come to name it. They grow slowly, if they grow at all. Their shells thicken over time, or do not, and either way they are the same beetle, moving the same paths, clicking the same faint click against the same stone that is slowly, slowly building itself around them. A beetle on a column formation crosses a path carved by centuries of mineral accretion and leaves no mark. The stone does not yield to the beetle's weight. The beetle does not try to go around. It crosses, and the stone holds, and both continue.

In the pool, there are things that move without being seen. The water is bright with bacteria — the same pale blue-green luminescence that coats every surface — and whatever lives below moves through that brightness in ways that displace it, leave wakes, disturb the even glow. You cannot see the creatures themselves. You see only the light they part, the faint trails of shadow they draw through the illumination. The trails are everywhere, crossing and recrossing, a constant diagram of motion that covers the pool bed like a map of journeys no one is taking. The creatures are small. They must be small, to live in water this shallow, in a space this limited. But their shadows suggest something else — something that would be small from above, from the world of light and shadow and scale, but here, in this sealed brightness, has grown or shrunk or been shaped into a form that fits the pool exactly, that fills the pool without disturbing it, that moves through it the way a thought moves through a mind: without resistance, without sound, without anything to mark its passage except the wake it leaves behind.

They have no eyes. Eyes would be useless here, where light is everywhere and means nothing, where there is nothing to see that is not already seen, already known, already part of the constant fact of this small room. They have other things: sensitivity to vibration, perhaps, or to chemical gradients, or to the faint current that the dripping creates, the pulse of mineral water entering the pool at a regular interval and displacing the still surface, sending a ripple outward that reaches the edges and returns, a conversation between the water and the stone that the eyeless creatures read like text. They have been shaped by this place. Their bodies are soft, pale, adapted to the temperature that never changes, to the moisture that never varies, to the light that never flickers. They are, in the evolutionary sense, finished. There is no pressure here to change them. The pool is complete. The creatures in it are complete. They are what this place has made, and they will go on being what this place has made until the stone forgets its shape.

The mosses are at the margins of the living world in here, not because they are rare — they are everywhere the moisture is thickest — but because they are barely vegetation at all. They do not photosynthesize, or they do not need to; the bioluminescent glow is enough, or the minerals in the water are enough, or they have found a way to live that does not require the sun's energy at all, that draws sustenance from the stone itself, from the slow seep of mineral water through the interstices, from the bacterial film that coats every surface and provides, perhaps, some form of nourishment that has no name. Their color is not green but the pale non-color of things that have let go of one mode of being and not yet found another. They are the color of the light. They have become the same thing as the illumination that falls on them.

The ferns are further out, clinging to their narrow seam where the crack widens enough to hold a pocket of soil. They are fragile in a way that suggests they are at the edge of viability, one parameter away from not being here at all. The soil is thin — barely enough to anchor roots, barely enough to hold moisture, barely enough to exist. And yet the ferns persist. Their fronds are translucent, the light passing through them the way it passes through the crickets' bodies, and in them too there is a faint luminescence, a glow that is not borrowed from the fungi but generated within the tissue itself, the slow work of adaptation becoming something more than adaptation, becoming transformation. The ferns are becoming luminous. They are learning to be what the crickets and the beetles and the eyeless things in the pool have always been: inhabitants of a world that has its own light, its own time, its own rules.

The roots descend through the hairline fissures, following the moisture, finding their way down through the stone that is still deciding what shape this room will take. They have found the pocket. They have found the drip, the steady rhythm of water that falls without variation, and they are drinking from it, drawing it up through their thin bodies, sending it outward through fronds that glow faintly in the constant blue-green light. The roots do not know they have found something strange. They do not know there is an above, a sun, a world where light comes from a single source and changes with the turning of the planet. They know only this: water, mineral, moisture, the faint chemical signature of a place where life is possible. They are doing what roots do. They are reaching.

And the bacteria are everywhere. They are the base layer, the ground floor, the foundation on which everything else is built. They coat the stone and the water and the mosses and the crickets and the beetles and the creatures in the pool, a film so fine it is almost invisible, present only as a slight luminosity, a glow that seems to come from the stone itself but is in fact a living layer, a community of beings so small and so numerous that they cannot be counted, only sensed, only known by their effect: the light that does not flicker, the brightness that fills every corner, the illumination that has been here since the pocket formed and will be here until the pocket closes.

They do not move. They do not need to. They are the light. They have become so completely what they are that living and shining are the same act, the same fact, the same equilibrium. They are the most finished of all the creatures in this unfinished place: the final form that the cave has reached, the stable state that holds everything else in place. The drips fall, and the bacteria glow. The beetles click, and the bacteria glow. The crickets call, and the bacteria glow. The ferns reach toward the light with their translucent fronds, and the bacteria glow. The light is everywhere, and the bacteria are everywhere, and between them there is no gap, no difference, no space that is not full.

What holds all of this together is not a single force but a series of balances so delicate that they should not hold, should not be possible, should not persist in a space this small, this sealed, this cut off from everything that moves and changes above. And yet they do. The moisture equals the evaporation equals the condensation equals the seepage. The fauna reproduces at exactly the rate the cave can sustain. The light sources are stable, the temperature is constant, the dripping never varies. The beetles click and the crickets call and the eyeless creatures trace their wakes through the bright pool, and none of it disturbs the equilibrium, none of it adds or subtracts from the fact of this place, none of it is anything other than what it is.

The carved marks on the column are almost erased now, the calcite having built up around them over what must be centuries, over what must be millennia. Whoever made them is long gone — not to the surface, not to any place that can be reached from here — gone the way things go when they have finished being what they were, when they have given up their matter to the stone and gone on to whatever comes after matter. The marks are still legible as marks, still unmistakably intentional, but the intention has been lost. They could be a record. They could be a prayer. They could be the scratch of a hand on stone in the dark, marking a way, or marking nothing, or simply needing to make a mark, to leave a sign, to say *I was here, in this place that does not change, I was here and I too was part of the equilibrium, I too was a small and finished creature in a small and finished room*.

The pottery fragments near the pool are smaller now than they were, softened by centuries of mineral seepage, becoming stone themselves. Someone carried them here, down through passages that no longer exist, into a pocket that was just forming, that had just found its shape, and left them behind. They did not come back for them. They did not need them anymore, or they could not carry them back, or they were called away, or they simply moved on, the way water moves on, the way all the creatures of this sealed world move on without moving, holding their places in an equilibrium that does not include departure because it does not include arrival.

The stone someone placed against the wall is almost absorbed now, the calcite having grown up around it, over it, through the gaps in its rough surface, incorporating it into the column, making it part of the slow geology of this small room. It was placed deliberately — the edges are still too regular, too parallel — but the deliberateness has been absorbed too, has become stone, has become part of the fact of this place in the same way the carved marks have become part of the fact, in the same way the pottery has become part of the fact, in the same way everything that has ever been here has become part of the fact, has entered the equilibrium, has taken its place in the slow, dense, luminous, dripping, never-changing work of a cave that is building itself around its own contents and will go on building until the building is finished, until the stalactites meet the stalagmites, until the columns fuse, until the room is full.

And then it will still be here, still be this: dense, bright, damp, cool, crowded with the creatures that have made their home in its constant glow, moved through by the beetles in their endless slow traffic, by the crickets in their resonant thrum, by the eyeless things in the pool tracing their wakes of displaced light, by the roots descending from above, by the bacteria on every surface glowing the same glow they have always glowed. It will still be the pocket that breathes light. It will still be the equilibrium. It will still be the fact.

The light does not change. The water does not vary. The cave breathes its slow mineral breath, and the beings that live within it move in patterns that will not change, will not end, will not arrive anywhere other than where they began. Someone carved marks on the wall. Someone placed a stone. Someone dropped pottery and did not pick it up. And the cave went on being the cave — dense, bright, damp, cool, hidden, packed with its own slow life, never changing, never once considering the difference.
