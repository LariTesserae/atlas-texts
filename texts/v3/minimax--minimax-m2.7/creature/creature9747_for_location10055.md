They are called gatherers, though by whom no one can say. The name has the feel of something once assigned and then simply inherited, passed down through generations of observers who found it more accurate than any alternative and lacked the will to improve upon it. In form they are modular—a central chassis that moves on treads or rails depending on the terrain, around which arrays of specialized implements attach and detach with a precision that speaks to a manufacturing tolerance far beyond what the plateau itself would suggest. The chassis itself is unremarkable: a low, wide, vaguely wedge-shaped body that sits close to the ground, its surfaces dark and matte, its edges worn smooth in places where it has rubbed against rock or been scoured by the passage of dense herds. It is not a thing designed to be beautiful. It is designed to *endure*, and it does.

The implements are the true diversity. Some are broad and flat, designed to sheer across the surface of the ground and separate the living mat from the substrate—lifting moss and root and the thin layer of soil into mechanisms that separate, sort, channel. Others are tall and articulated, extending upward like the neck of some patient heron before terminating in clusters of sensors or tools whose purpose shifts depending on what they are pointed at. Some deploy nets, broad canopies of material so fine it is nearly invisible against the sky until the light catches it, trailing behind as the gatherer advances. Some carry no visible tools at all, their chassis open, waiting, their purpose unclear until the moment they encounter something to process—and then the tools emerge as if they had always been there, had simply been held in reserve. The variety is vast, and no single gatherer carries all of it. Each unit seems to specialize, to have been assigned or to have chosen a particular role in the larger operation, though whether this specialization is programmed or learned or something else entirely is one of the many questions the plateau declines to answer.

They move in loose formations, never quite synchronized but never quite random either. A group will spread across a slope like a hand spreading its fingers, each unit occupying a path that intersects with the others' paths at irregular intervals but avoids direct collision with a nimbleness that seems almost biological. When the terrain demands it—steep pitches, narrow cols, the sharp V-clefts of the young valleys—they compress into tighter formations, the treads or rails clicking and grinding against stone as they adjust their heading in ways that suggest communication without revealing its mechanism. There is sound, certainly: the hum of the engines, the percussion of contact, the occasional sharp exhalation of something pneumatic or thermal. But whether these sounds carry meaning, whether the gatherers are speaking to one another in some language of pressure and frequency, or whether the sounds are simply the inevitable result of their construction and motion—again, the plateau offers no clarification.

The fauna resists classification at every taxonomic level. They are not large by the standards of the herbivores that graze on lower, gentler ground—they are perhaps the size of large dogs, or small deer, their bodies low and dense and built for heat retention in an environment where heat is the scarcest resource. Their coats are thick and layered, with a primary layer of fine underfur and an outer layer of coarser guard hairs that shed water with an efficiency that seems almost engineered. Their limbs are short and powerful, built for stability rather than speed, and their heads are broad and blunt, their mouths equipped with teeth and jaw structures that can handle the tough, slow-growing vegetation of the high altitude with a grinding efficiency. They do not flee from the gatherers. This is perhaps the most unsettling detail, though it takes time to recognize it as such. The herds see the machines. They acknowledge them, in whatever way their neurology permits—heads raise, bodies shift, the mass of them adjusts—but they do not scatter. They do not panic. They move aside when the gatherers pass through them, parting like water around stone, but they move *slowly*, calmly, with the resignation or the acceptance or the indifference of creatures for whom the machines have always been part of the landscape. The calves do not flinch. The old ones do not accelerate. There is a quality of coexistence here, between predator and prey—or between processor and processed, if that word carries less moral weight—that has the feel of something ancient and negotiated.

The gatherers do not kill indiscriminately. This is central to understanding the plateau's operation, and it is easy to miss. The machines sort. They scan. They evaluate. Through mechanisms that involve light, radiation, resonance, or something else entirely, each animal in a herd is assessed, categorized, routed. Most pass through the gatherer's attention unchanged, their bodies deemed unsuitable or unnecessary or simply not yet ready. Some are isolated, separated from the herd by the patient maneuvering of multiple units working in concert, their isolation calm and undramatic. They are led, guided, corralled by gatherers that position themselves with a geometrical precision, drawing a single animal or a small cluster away from the mass and into a secondary formation that moves at a different pace, toward a different destination. What happens to them then—what processing occurs, what substance or material is extracted, what is taken and what is left behind—is not visible from a distance, and the gatherers do not invite closer inspection.

The bodies they leave behind are different. This is the one mark they cannot entirely erase. Animals that have been processed move more slowly afterward, for a time—hours or days, depending on the individual. They feed less. They are less alert, their responses dulled in ways that suggest a neurological effect more than a physical one. Over time they recover, or they do not, and the ones who do not recover are absorbed back into the landscape by the same processes of erosion and regrowth that erase every other trace. The question of what has been taken from them hovers over the plateau like the thin, luminous air. Some have suggested that the machines harvest something from the animals' neural activity—the electrical patterns of their brains, the chemical signatures of their cognition, the quantum configurations of their proteins. Others have suggested that the processing is hormonal, that the machines are extracting or modifying the regulatory substances that govern growth and reproduction and stress response. Others still believe the machines are interested in something simpler and stranger: the pattern of the animals themselves, the informational content of their bodies, the specific and unrepeatable arrangement of molecules that constitutes a living organism at a given moment in time. Whatever it is, they take it in vast quantities, at a rate that seems to match or slightly exceed the natural reproduction of the herds, so that the populations remain stable across years and decades and the plateau continues to hum with its dual sound of life and industry.

The birds are the first indication that the plateau operates on multiple levels simultaneously. They are not large birds—small, generally, with dark plumage and long, narrow wings built for the sustained high-altitude flight that this elevation demands. They follow the herds the way clouds follow weather systems, drifting above and slightly behind, their movements patient and unhurried. They are not parasites, exactly. They do not pester or harry the animals below. They are present, attentive, patient. They descend to feed when the herds have passed through an area, picking through the disturbed moss and soil for whatever has been exposed or dislodged, and they rise again to resume their position above the next formation. They are not numerous in the way the herds are numerous, but they are present everywhere on the plateau, a diffuse second layer of life that interpenetrates the first without competing with it. Whether they are processing something of their own, or whether they are part of the same operation as the gatherers, or whether they are simply another consequence of the plateau's overwhelming density of life, is a question that admits no easy answer. Their presence is simply a fact, as elemental and as unexamined as the wind.

Below the birds, below the herds, below the gatherers, there is the soil. And in the soil, the thing that the plateau is perhaps actually about. It is not visible. It cannot be observed directly. But it is there, in the density of the moss, in the viridity of the sedge, in the sheer improbable abundance of the vegetation that clothes this young, thin, ungenerous geology in a skin of green and gold and gray. Something is being produced here—some material, some substance, some *output*—and the production does not stop. The gatherers harvest it from the fauna. The fauna eats it from the vegetation. The vegetation draws it from the soil and the thin air and the brief water. And the soil contains it, holds it, receives it back from every level of the system as the dead return to the ground and the waste returns to the earth and the machines process and the output flows downward, inward, into whatever reservoir or mechanism or fundament underlies the plateau's industrial heart. The scale is such that the output is invisible precisely because it is everywhere. It is the plateau itself. It is the moss and the sedge and the scrub willow and the stunted, wind-pressed, patient willow that clings to every sheltered hollow with roots that grip the thin soil like fingers clenched against a cliff face. The production is the land. The process is the life. The plateau is not a place where things happen—it is a thing that *is happening*, has been happening, will continue to happen, a machine of existence so vast and so total that it has become indistinguishable from the world it creates.

The cold does not bother them. The gatherers operate in the freezing air as easily as they would in temperate conditions, their mechanisms unaffected by the temperature, their sensors unclouded by the condensation that gathers on every natural surface in the early hours and then burns away as the thin sun climbs. The fauna is built for it, their bodies radiating a warmth that is modest by mammalian standards but constant, a baseline metabolic output that keeps them alive and moving through nights that crack and shatter with frost. The birds maintain their altitude through some combination of physiological adaptation and behavioral strategy, riding thermal columns that rise from the warm bodies of the herds below, converting the heat of the processed and the processing into the lift that carries them through their patient, watchful days.

To stand on the plateau and listen is to hear something that has no analog. The low, constant hum of the gatherers layers over the deeper, less rhythmic sound of the herds—the tramp and shuffle of thousands of feet, the occasional vocalization that rises and falls through the mass like a wave through water. Above it all, the birds add a faint, high, persistent call that is easy to dismiss as wind until you notice that it is too regular, too consistent, too present to be anything so variable as weather. The combination creates a sound that is neither peaceful nor hostile. It is simply *total*. It fills the air the way the life fills the land, leaving no silence, no gap, no moment in which the listener can feel that they have found the bottom of things. The plateau has no bottom. It only has levels, descending through one another in an infinity of presence, each layer complete, each layer dependent, each layer operating according to principles that are legible in their effects but opaque in their cause.

And at night, when the stars emerge with a clarity that the altitude makes inevitable, the plateau goes on. The gatherers continue their circuits, their lights—dim, functional, barely more than indicators of operation—moving across the dark ground like the slow phosphorescence of deep-water creatures. The herds settle where they stand, their bodies lowering, their breath rising in clouds of vapor that the cold catches and holds, so that the herd becomes a landscape of small, warm ghosts moving through the vast, cold dark. The birds descend to roost on the lee sides of ridges, their small bodies huddled against the wind, their presence still present, still processing, still part of the operation even in rest. And somewhere beneath it all, the soil holds what has been given to it, and the vegetation draws upward through the long slow night, and the thing that is being made is made, and the plateau continues, and the thin cold air carries the sound of industry across a land that has never been, and will never be, still.
