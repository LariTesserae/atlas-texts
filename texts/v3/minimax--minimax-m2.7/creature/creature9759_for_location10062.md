Where the main stairway widens into the upper market, there is a woman who repairs harnesses. Her name is Orla, and she has hands the color of wet clay — stained so deep it will never leave, the pigment worked into the lines of her palms like the grain of the wood she mends. She works by the light of a single oil lamp hung from a hook driven into a natural seam in the stone, and she has worked here so long that the ceiling above her bench shows a dark arc of soot from years of accumulated flame. Her chair is low, built for sitting cross-legged on the cold floor the way children sit, and her legs have grown to accept it. She does not stand to greet customers. She waits. If you need a harness, you come to where she is, and she looks up at you with eyes accustomed to close work — small adjustments, precise tension, the difference between a stitch that holds and one that unravels under load.

She is not the oldest person in Brethem, but she is among the oldest presences. She remembers the chamber before it was a market. She remembers when it was a drilling camp with tent-cloth partitions and the sound of picks echoing off walls that still fell to chunks under the bit. Her grandfather dug the cistern channels. Her father expanded the western residential tiers. She has watched the town compress itself, tightening like a fist around the stone it was carved from.

---

The children of Brethem are compact and quiet. Not shy — there is a difference. They have been raised in passages where sound returns, where a shout in a corridor will travel and travel and arrive back altered, diminished, strange. They have learned the economics of noise. They speak in murmurs and signals, in the pressure of a hand on a shoulder, in the particular silence that means *wait here* or *someone is coming*. They play in the wider chambers where the ceiling climbs high enough for running, but even there, even in the pleasure of open space, they move with a kind of economical grace — ducking under timber beams, sidestepping the drainage channels, navigating by instinct the geography of a town that does not change often but when it does, changes completely.

A girl named Sura, eleven or twelve, keeps a pet fungus in a clay pot behind her family's sleeping alcove. It is a pale orange shelf fungus, the kind that grows in the wettest corridors where the water pools longest, and she has trained it to lean toward the east-facing crack where the faintest gray light seeps in each morning. She shows it to the younger children. She calls it *morrowbell* and swears it sways when daylight is near. No one believes her, but no one tells her to stop.

---

The men are broad across the shoulders and short in the legs — a generation of work that has reshaped the body, picks and hammers and buckets passed from father to son until the frame itself becomes a tool. They walk with a particular gait, one foot always slightly turned outward, the hips rolling over stones that are never quite flat. Their faces are gaunt in a way that has nothing to do with hunger — it is the geometry of torchlight, which flatters no one, which carves the bones into prominence and shadows the eyes into depth. When they drink, they drink slowly, holding the cup in both hands the way a child holds something precious, because water here is never entirely trusted and what you hold you hold carefully.

They smell of tallow and mineral salt. Their hair is often damp at the temples, and they keep it short — the damp makes it heavy, and long hair mats against the neck. Their hands are mapped with scars: old cuts from the chisel, burns from the forge if they are smiths, the particular white lines that come from gripping a hand-drill for hours against stone that does not care whether it breaks.

There is a man who runs the cistern collection — Tovo, who is sixty if he is a day and who has spent forty years walking the drainage channels with a pole and a scraper, clearing the sediment that builds up where the flow slows. He knows every channel by feel. He knows which ones silt first, which ones drain clean, which ones need checking after a heavy seep. He has walked the town's veins for so long that the water speaks to him in changes of sound — a shift in pitch, a new gurgle, the absence of a trickle where a trickle should be. He is not a talkative man. He says that the water says enough.

---

The women are quiet in a different register. They have organized the interior life — the cooking fires, the clothing, the storage alcoves carved into every available surface, the careful rotation of supplies that keeps a town of several hundred from the cliff edge of shortage. They keep the calendars of seasons that Brethem does not experience: planting times for the mushroom beds, harvest times for the lichen, the dry months when the seepage drops and the cisterns can be cleaned. They are the ones who remember. The men dig and build and maintain, but the women hold the pattern — the shape of the day's work, the order of meals, the arrangement of sleeping bodies in the shared chambers where families press close together for warmth and for the sound of each other's breathing, which is, in the end, the most intimate sound Brethem produces.

In the evening, in the largest common hall, you will see them sitting with their legs intertwined — a gesture of intimacy and economy both, sharing heat between bodies that are tired, knees drawn up, shoulders touching. They eat from communal pots: thin broths with dried fungus reconstituted in them, flatbreads baked on stone beside the fire, occasionally a rabbit from the upper tunnels. The talk is low. The fire burns. Outside, in the channels, the water goes on moving — the town's pulse, its breath, the reason it exists.

---

There are others. A boy who apprenticed to the light-makers — the ones who render tallow and press oil from seeds and tend the wick-cutting benches — and who now smells perpetually of rendered fat and char. An old man who sits in the market with wooden toys he carves for children, birds and fish and simple shapes that fit in small hands, and who has never had children of his own but whose work is claimed by every child in Brethem. A woman who breeds the pale cave-lice that produce the oil, kept in sealed chambers that hum with their own small life, and who speaks of them with a tenderness others reserve for dogs.

The ones who go above ground carry packs on their backs and come back with news. The valley is green, they say. The sky is wide. The air tastes of nothing. They say this without envy, in the same tone Sura uses to describe morrowbell — a fact of a world that is not theirs, acknowledged and set aside.

At night, the town compresses further. Bodies settle into sleep. The fires burn down to coals. The water continues its patient circulation through stone that is still learning to hold the shape of what was taken from it. And in the deeper passages — the unmapped ones, the ones Tovo has not walked in years — something moves. Not often. Not confirmed. Only the sound of it, when conditions align just so: a scraping, a displacement, a presence larger than a rat and more deliberate than water.

But the people of Brethem are practical. They lock their storehouses. They peg their doors. They keep their knives within reach. And in the morning they rise, and the picks come out again, and the chisels ring against stone, and the town goes on becoming old.
