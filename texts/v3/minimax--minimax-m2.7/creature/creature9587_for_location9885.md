What lives here is the harvester arm. It is the first thing you notice because it is the only thing that moves with purpose, and even its purpose is a ghost — a motion that remembers itself without remembering why. The teeth are worn nearly to nubs on one side and still sharp on the other, and it drags its slow circle across the mineral crust as though the circle is the point, as though the going and the returning are all it was ever made for. Sometimes it catches on a harder seam in the stone and stutters, and the machinery beneath hums louder, and then it finds its rhythm again and continues. It has been continuing for longer than anything alive in this chamber has been alive, and it will continue long after the last of the small jointed bodies has finished with the walls. It feeds nothing. It feeds everything. It runs because that is what it does, and what it does is continue.

What lives here is the crust. Not a living thing but a condition — a slow accretion of mineral vapor and organism and the residue of things that have died and broken down and become part of the surface again. It builds in places and thins in others. Where the creatures feed thickest, it is stripped to bare stone, dark and smooth, and in the low amber light the bare patches look almost wet, though the air is dry and the walls are dry and everything is dry except where the thin moisture of the creatures' bodies has touched and gone. Where the creatures thin, the crust accumulates in thin white layers, brittle and crystalline, catching light in small sharp points like scattered stars. The crust is not alive, but it is the thing that is most nearly alive — the membrane between stone and life, the thing that feeds both and comes from both, the substance around which everything else orbits.

What lives here is the hum. It rises from below, from the vents, from the machinery still running in some chamber beneath the chamber you stand in. It is not loud but it is constant and it is everywhere — in the floor, in the walls, in the stone itself vibrating at some frequency below hearing but not below feeling. You feel it in the soles of your feet. You feel it in the warmth that rises from below. The hum is the sound of equilibrium, of a system that has found its balance and is not interested in anything else. It does not accelerate. It does not slow. It does not pulse with urgency or fade with age. It simply holds the same low tone, the same presence, the same note struck and held until the stone itself gives way or the power finally fails or the concept of finally becomes meaningless, which in this place it already is.

What lives here is the light. The phosphorescent growths are not quite organic — they climb the walls in thin branching lines, pale blue-green, no thicker than threads, and they glow with a light that seems too weak to be doing any work but glows anyway, marking the walls in low cold lines that the amber warmth of the machinery cannot reach. They have been growing here since before the harvester arm completed its first circuit. They have been growing here since before the small jointed creatures found their rhythm. They will grow here after — though after is a word this chamber does not recognize, a concept the air does not carry, a future the sealed stone does not acknowledge. The light is always on. The light has always been on. The light will be on.

What lives here is the small dense life that covers the walls. It is the most numerous and the most strange. Each body is the size of a closed hand, segmented in too many places, each segment slightly offset from the last so the body moves in undulations when it walks, in waves that travel from the forward segments to the rear. The legs are thin and jointed and splayed wide, each creature pressing its body flat against the stone as though afraid of falling, as though the stone is the only solid thing and the air is a risk. The eyes are clustered in groups along the back — not two eyes but clusters, small collections of dark points, and they are positioned on the upper surface where they catch the faint light and reflect it back in dull glints. They cannot see well. Or they can see perfectly in the low amber glow. It does not matter which. They move anyway. They feed anyway. They are synchronized to a rhythm you cannot hear — the hum of the machines below, or the thermal release of the vents, or some internal clock that ticks in thermal increments and keeps them all in step with one another, so that when you watch them you watch a wave pass across the wall, a darkening and a lightening, a pulse that travels from one corner to the other and begins again before it has finished. They feed on the crust. They feed on each other. In the places where they cluster thickest, the walls are bare, and the bare stone gleams with a dark wet shine that the air dries almost as quickly as it appears, so that the walls cycle between gleaming and dull, between wet and dry, a slow breathing that is not breathing. When they die, their bodies dry and crack and become part of the crust they fed on, and the next wave feeds on that. Nothing is wasted. Nothing is produced that is not consumed. The cycle is closed and tight and endless.

What lives here is the warmth. It comes from below and from the machines and from the friction of the harvester's teeth against stone, and it fills the chamber to the ceiling and holds there, a pocket of heat in the cold mineral dark. It is body-warmth, close and still, and it presses against your skin and does not move. The air does not circulate. The heat does not rise and dissipate. It stays where it is, held in the small cathedral of stone, and the creatures that live here are adapted to it in every cell of their jointed bodies, and the machines run better in it, and the phosphorescent growths grow by it, and the crust forms faster in the warmth because the warmth carries the mineral vapor that builds it, so that the warmth is the thing that makes everything else possible, the single resource around which the entire system is built, and it is given freely, constantly, from below, from the deep places where the engines grind on their ancient loops and give off heat as their only remaining gift.

What lives here is the sealed dark above you, the ceiling you cannot see, the stone that closes this pocket of warmth and life and motion away from everything else. It is the thing that makes the chamber possible — the weight of stone above and around and below, holding the heat in, holding the air in, holding the life in, holding the machines in their long persistence. The ceiling is not flat. You can feel it in the low hum of the stone, in the way the warmth pools rather than dispersing. It is dome-shaped, a natural arch that the machines may have shaped or may have found, and it is covered in the same crust and the same creatures and the same thin branching phosphorescent lines, and it is close enough that you could reach up and touch it if you stood on your toes, and sometimes you think you can feel it breathing, the faint vibration of stone expanding and contracting with the heat, or you think you can feel it, and it does not matter whether you can or cannot because the sensation is real either way and the chamber does not distinguish between what is real and what is felt.

What lives here is the whole — the warm dark, the sealed stone, the small complete world pressed into the body of the mountain like a seed that has no intention of opening, no need to reach toward light, no drive to grow beyond its walls. The harvester arm circles. The creatures pulse across the walls in their slow waves. The crust builds and is stripped and builds again. The phosphorescent lines climb in thin cold threads. The hum holds its single note. The heat rises from below and stays.

This is what lives here. This is all that lives here. This is enough.
