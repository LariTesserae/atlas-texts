What lives here is patient, and what lives here is small.

The water does not move like water elsewhere. It lingers. It seeps and settles and waits in its crevices like something considering whether to remain. You might call it the first inhabitant, if you were of a mind to personify mineral flow, if you could find the animism required for such naming. But the water is simply here, has always been here, will be here after whatever else has come and gone. It is the medium in which everything else exists—the lubricant of this pocket world, the reason anything grows at all. It enters the basin through fractures too fine to see and exits the same way, and in between it takes its time. It weeps. The basin earns its name not from sorrow but from persistence, from the unending slow leak of water through stone.

The mosses are the elders. They have been here longer than anything with a heartbeat, and they will remain long after the small creatures have eaten each other into new configurations. The moss does not grow so much as it accumulates—a slow accretion of individual strands, each one a separate organism, each one contributing its own small mass to the collective. Where water runs thin over stone, the moss thickens into cushions, and where the water pools and stills, the moss retreats to the edges, giving way to darker things. There are species here with names in languages you do not speak—*Mnium stellare*, perhaps, or *Hylocomium splendens*—but the names do not matter. What matters is their color, that impossible saturated green, and their texture, which is not quite texture at all but a kind of softness that exists only in dense mats, a yielding that gives when you press but holds its shape when you withdraw. The moss breathes. You cannot hear it, but it takes in what the air offers and releases what the water demands, and this exchange is as vital to the basin as your own respiration is to you.

The ferns are the youth. They unfurl in spring with a urgency that seems almost violent, spirals tightening outward until the fronds shake free and hang limp and green in the damp air. They do not live long—their fronds brown by autumn, their rhizomes retreating into the rock to wait—but in their season they dominate, pushing into spaces the moss has not yet claimed, claiming new territory on the wet stone faces. They are the only things here that reach, even slightly, even briefly. Their reaching is their declaration: *this place will not be stillness only*. In the right light, when a shaft of gray sun finds its way through the aperture above and falls across a patch of ferns, the scene will remind you of something—woodland floor, temperate forest, any other place where ferns grow—but the reminder will fade, because the scale is wrong, the walls are too close, and the light never quite warms.

Between the moss and the ferns, between the stone and the water, there are things you will not see unless you are still.

The springtails cluster on the wet surfaces in numbers that seem impossible for their size. You have to look closely, closer than seems reasonable for a place that offers no obvious reward for attention. But when you look closely, you find them: tiny, pale, the size of commas or periods, scattered across the moss like punctuation left over from some sentence you cannot read. They feed on the decomposing matter, on the fine silt deposited by the seeping water, on each other when starvation demands. They leap—though *leap* is generous, *fling* is more accurate, a sudden violent separation from a surface that can barely hold them—using a forked appendage beneath their abdomen that stores tension like a spring. When they leap, they do not know where they will land. They simply go, trusting the water to slow their fall, trusting the moss to catch them.

The mites are less visible, more numerous. They inhabit the deeper cushions, the places where moss thickens into something approaching depth, where the moisture does not evaporate and the temperature does not vary. They are armored and slow, and they move through the substrate like tanks through soft ground, leaving faint trails in the condensation. They feed on fungal threads, on detritus, on each other in the way of small predators everywhere. Some are pale; some are dark enough to seem black. They do not leap. They do not need to. Their world is inches deep and inches wide, and within it they are giants.

The beetles are the bold ones. They emerge in the evenings, or in the gray perpetual non-day that passes for evening here, and they move with a purpose that seems excessive for creatures of their size. They are small—most of them no larger than your smallest fingernail—but they carry themselves as though they are large, as though the basin is their territory and you are the intruder. Some are glossy, their shells reflecting the dim light like dark mirrors, the same mirrors that the pools make. Others are matte and dull, built for concealment, built to disappear into the lichen that colors the rocks in patches of gray and orange and white. They hunt the mites and the springtails, and they are hunted in turn by things that move faster than they do, though *fast* is relative in a world where the fastest thing is a mite's spring and the slowest thing is the water weeping through stone.

The spiders—you will not see the spiders. Not often. They have made their homes in the crevices where the water seeps deepest, in the cracks that split the limestone faces, in the spaces between stones that are not quite stones anymore but have become something softer, something shaped by the patient movement of mineral through solution. The webs they spin are visible only when the light catches them at the right angle, when a strand catches a stray beam and announces itself. Otherwise, they are invisible—the architecture of capture hidden in the wet dark, waiting for something to stumble into it. The spiders are patient in their way, too. They wait.

There is a beetle, or there was, that you saw once near the far wall—a different species, or perhaps the same species in a different mood. It was climbing a wet stone face where the moss gave way to bare rock, and it was climbing with the determination of something that knows exactly where it is going even if you cannot see the path. You watched it until it reached a point where the rock face vanished into shadow and the beetle followed, and you did not see where it went. You do not know if it found what it was looking for. You do not know if there was anything to find. You do not know if *looking* is what it was doing, or if looking is what you were doing, projecting intention onto a creature whose interior life—if it has one—remains inaccessible.

The sound, though. The sound is a kind of inhabitant.

You feel it before you hear it, as was noted—a vibration through the wet ground, a thrumming that enters through the soles of your feet and builds in the enclosed space until it is something like a voice, something like a heartbeat, something like the respiration of a body too large to see. The sound has no source that you can locate. It comes from below, or from within, or from the stone itself—the pressure of water moving through channels carved before you were born and will continue after you are gone. It is not alive, this sound. It is not conscious, as far as you know, as far as the word *conscious* can apply to a geological process. But it fills the basin, and the basin fills with it, and if you sit still long enough you may find yourself matching your breath to its rhythm, may find your heart following its pulse, may find that you have become synchronized with something that does not know you exist and would not care if it did.

This is the presence here. This is what lives in the Weeping Basin, if you are inclined to ask, if you insist on an answer.

Not the moss, though it is old.
Not the beetles, though they are bold.
Not the mites, though they are many.
Not the spiders, though they are patient.

The pulse. The sound. The deep machinery that makes the water weep and the stone breathe and the basin hold its shape against the larger emptiness that surrounds it. The thing that moves when nothing moves, that speaks when nothing speaks, that continues when there is nothing to continue for.

It does not notice you. It does not need to.
