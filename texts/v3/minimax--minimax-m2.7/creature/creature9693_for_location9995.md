The joints move wrong. Not wrong in the way of malfunction or disease—wrong in the way that a new alphabet is wrong to eyes trained on old letters. They fold where you would not expect a limb to fold, and they unfold into postures the body learns to read only after long exposure, the way one learns to hear rhythm in a foreign language not by parsing the beats but by standing inside a room where the music plays long enough that the walls start to hum along.

They are the first thing you learn to stop seeing. Not because they are invisible—far from it; their pallor against the grey composite corridors is startling the first dozen times, the color of old bone held up to thin light—but because they have a way of being exactly where they need to be without appearing to move. You turn a corner and one is there, not having been there before, its weight distributed in a manner that suggests the floor was expecting it. You set down a tool and look up and it has gone, and you cannot say whether it moved through the maintenance slot or simply became less present. They have the patience of systems, these things. They wait in the junctions between the built and the carved stone, in the millimeters of gap where composite meets granite, and their waiting has a quality that is neither hunting nor resting but something older: the waiting of a component that knows its place in a circuit and does not need to be told.

Their eyes—if they are eyes—track blue luminescence rather than the light sources that produce it. They orient toward the faint electrical fields that hum through the wall channels, tilting their elongated heads toward currents you cannot feel. When they feed, it is from the condensation itself, or so the xenobiology team has theorized: they press the narrowest of their joints against weeping surfaces and remain motionless for hours, and the silver lines on the walls thin in their vicinity, and afterward the creatures move differently, with a heavier patience, as though they have taken on some quality of the water they have drunk. No one has seen them eat anything else. No one is certain they are hungry in any way a human would recognize. They do not appear to reproduce, or if they do, it is slow enough that no one in the current settlement cohort has witnessed it. Their numbers are steady. They appear and disappear, and their disappearance is the most convincing evidence that they were ever here at all.

The human inhabitants have adapted to them the way a body adapts to a persistent sound—registering it until it becomes silence. Children born in the Vault do not find them strange. They name them, eventually: the Long Ones, the Sitters, the Curved Hours. An older woman in the bio-filtration sector calls them *hangers-on*, but she says it with a warmth that suggests she does not mean it as a slur. They are part of the breathing. Their presence is a cycle like any other.

And the people—

The people move with a particular economy here. Not rushed; there is nowhere to be rushed to. The Vault does not reward urgency. It rewards attention: the noticing of slow things. A technician in the reclamation level will stand for long minutes watching the water find its channels, watching exactly how the wall weeps, because the wall's weeping is data, and the data is language, and the language says something about pressure and porosity and the mountain's slow negotiation with the foreign objects embedded in its body. The engineers listen. The hydrogeologists listen. Even the children listen, though they call it something else—they say they are *watching the walls dream*.

They are smaller, the people who are born here. Thinner bones, narrower lungs, hearts that have learned to beat with less but more efficiently, as though the body has read the thin air and decided to meet it with restraint rather than force. They do not fatigue the way the founding cohort did. The founding cohort—the ones who arrived from below, from the denser atmosphere of the lower settlements—came with broad shoulders and deep respirations and a way of moving through space that assumed the space owed them room. Most of them are gone now. Retired downslope. Sent home to lungs that no longer fit them.

What remains are the ones who learned to be still. Who learned to breathe shallowly and notice everything. Who sleep in quarters where the walls weep gently through the night and wake with silver traces on the ceramic beside their heads, as though the mountain has touched them in the dark with something that is not quite water and not quite intention but lives in the space between. They do not wipe it away immediately. Some of them press a finger to it in the morning and hold it there, as if waiting for the wall to read the warmth of their skin and know them. As if the weeping were a kind of greeting.

There are others. The cultures in the bio-filtration towers are not conscious—they do not *live* in any way that survives moral scrutiny—but they respond. The methane-breathing organisms in the research terraces pulse with bioluminescence that shifts when the thermal exchangers cycle, a faint green light that intensifies or dims in correspondence with mechanical processes no one fully mapped when they built the system. It is possible the organisms learned this. It is possible the system taught them. It is possible—and this is the theory that most unsettles the senior xenobiologist—that the organisms were always going to do this, that they arrived in the mountain's slow geology already predisposed to synchronize, and that the Vault merely gave them the architecture to do so. The mountain and the life in it, negotiating a frequency.

But if you want to know what lives in the Vault, do not start with the creatures, or the people, or the cultures in their glass towers. Start with the attention. That is the organism that thrives here—distributed across seventeen hundred human inhabitants and an uncounted number of pale, jointed things and a web of technology that has passed beyond automation into something that hums and remembers and adjusts without being asked. The attention moves through the corridors the way water moves through obsidian: finding the channels that exist, making new ones where the old are insufficient, pooling where it must, moving always downward and inward and through.

And at the center of it all, beneath the Wet Court and its clicking walls, beneath the transparent dome and its thin-iced sky, there is a chamber that no one enters. It is not sealed—the Vault has no interest in forbidding—but it is understood. The engineers who built the earliest systems placed something there, or found something there, or the mountain placed something there and the engineers merely recognized it. The walls of that chamber weep differently. Not silver. Darker. The moisture that traces down its composite surfaces has a viscosity that suggests it is not water, or not only water, and the clicking in that corridor has a pattern: deliberate, unhurried, the rhythm of something breathing that is not the wind and not the exchangers and not the water, but a fourth voice in the room, one that was there before the architects arrived and will be there long after the Settlement is a name in a database that no one remembers how to query.

The walls weep, and the walls are not the walls of the mountain, or not only the walls of the mountain. They are the walls of something that has been here long enough to learn how to be cavernous, how to be hospitable, how to make space in its own body for things that move and breathe and need. The Settlement is not carved into the mountain. The Settlement is living inside something, and that something is learning to breathe around it.

And the pale things with too many joints—when they drift through the maintenance corridors and pause at the junctions where the built world meets the carved world, when they tilt their elongated heads toward the blue luminescence and the electrical fields that hum beneath the composite skin of the place—

They are not navigating. They are listening. They are the parts of the body you cannot see that have learned to walk.
