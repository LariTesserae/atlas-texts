There is a membrane here. Not a thing you see — a thing you learn. The city was not built so much as grown, and what grew it understood that water is not a force to be channeled but a medium to be negotiated with, and so the built environment is porous in ways that take months to understand and years to stop noticing. You walk along a raised walkway at midday and feel nothing unusual. You return at dusk and find the walkway slightly lower, the railings warm in a way that should not be possible without friction, and the ferns that grow along its edges standing at angles that suggest the stone beneath your feet has shifted — not broken, not settled, but *moved*, the way a sleeping body turns without waking.

This is normal. This is the city breathing.

You learn to read the breath. The water table rises and falls in a rhythm the residents call *tide* even though there is no sea within a hundred kilometers, and the tide has a secondary pattern nested inside it — a faster rhythm, a shallower one — that matches the pulse of the luming in the channels. When the luming is slow and even, the buildings are still. When it quickens, the embedded light-veins brighten, and the filament networks in the sediment drink faster, and the air feels thicker, and you realize you have been breathing shallowly without noticing. The residents do not breathe shallowly. They have learned to synchronize. A child raised here does not experience the pulse as stimulus. They experience it as context, the way you experience the awareness of your own heartbeat only when it races.

The oldest residents speak about this synchronization the way you might describe learning to swim in current — not as skill but as adjustment, a physical rewiring that happens without intention. You have seen an outsider spend three days here before the strange thing started happening to her hands. The skin between her fingers, where it had always been dry, began to soften. The lines in her palms deepened. By the end of the first week, her hands looked as though they had been in water for hours, though she had touched none of the channels directly. She left before the second week was over. The residents were not unkind about it. They helped her pack. They walked her to the long boat. One of them said, gently, *your body was deciding.*

What her body was deciding, she may never know, and this is also part of it. The city does not explain itself. It adjusts you or it does not, and the adjustment is not always physical, or not *only* physical, and it is not always completed in any single visit. You have heard — the residents say this with a careful vagueness, the way they say many things — that the city remembers people. That if you leave and come back years later, the buildings you pass will know you. Not the people inside them — the buildings themselves. The paleite in their walls will have a warmth to it that is not about temperature. The filaments in the sediment beneath your feet will drink deeper. The channels you cross will carry their luming a fraction of a second faster, and this difference is so small that only the very old can perceive it, and they perceive it not as data but as greeting.

The deep stores are another membrane. You descend into them through entrances that are not doors — the architecture here does not think in terms of thresholds — but openings, gaps in the wall surface where the paleite simply thins and then is not there, and the dark begins. The oldest residents say the deep stores predate the city. The city grew around them the way a tree grows around a wound: not despite the damage but *because* of it, with the damage as the structuring absence around which new growth organizes. Inside the deep stores the air is different — cooler, denser, carrying a mineral weight that tastes of iron and something else, something the residents have no word for because their language evolved here and so their words are shaped by this place's particular air, and there is no word for a taste that is only found in the deep stores because the language was born in the upper city and the deep stores are a country whose border is breath.

The deep stores are not flooded, not entirely. Some of the lower channels run through them — the water moves at a different pace down here, slower, almost viscous, and the luming is different. Brighter, but not evenly so. It flares and dims in patterns that seem structured, that seem *meant*, though meant by what or for what no one can say. The walls of the deep stores are carved — this is the closest the city comes to obvious art, and even this is not quite art in the way you understand the word. The carvings are channels themselves, secondary waterways cut into the limestone, and they carry water that is darker than the water in the upper channels, water that does not lum, and when you touch it your fingers come away clean but your skin feels strange for hours afterward, tight and faintly warm, as though you have been marked in a way that is not visible.

The residents go into the deep stores sometimes. Not often. Not casually. You learn to recognize the ones who go — they carry themselves differently for a day or two afterward, standing still more than usual, looking at water with a particular kind of attention that is not fear and not wonder but something between the two, something that has no word in your language either. They do not speak about what they find there. They do not speak about what they *don't* find there. They come back quieter, and the quiet is not grief and not contentment. It is the quiet of a conversation that is ongoing, that has always been ongoing, and that you are now more fully part of than you were before.

The buildings that are empty take the quiet differently. You can tell how long a building has been empty not by the decay — nothing decays here — but by the way the plants inside it have changed. The ferns go first: they grow tall, then pale, then collapse into a soft mat that feeds the moss. The vines follow, climbing higher, reaching for light that is always dim, and eventually the vine leaves become something other than what they are on the occupied buildings — thicker, darker, with a texture that feels almost like skin when you touch it, and the spiral trees that grow near empty buildings develop a lean, their crowns tilting toward the open windows as though listening. The deep stores are full of this kind of growth. The oldest sections are entirely taken, not in the way that ruins are overtaken but in the way that a word is overtaken by its meaning — completely, structurally, so that you cannot find the original word beneath the meaning without deliberate effort.

There are animals in the deep stores. You will not see them clearly. They are shapes that move at the edges of the luming, dark and narrow, and they do not flee when you approach but do not approach you either, and the water they move through has a quality to it that makes your eyes water, and your chest tightens, and the pulse from below rises in pitch until it is almost — *almost* — a sound you can name. Then it is gone, and the water is still, and they are gone, and the carved channels in the walls carry their dark water in silence.

The city's children are the place where the membrane is thinnest. Not because they are innocent — innocence is not a concept that translates well here — but because they are still changing, still adjusting, still becoming the thing the city needs them to become, and their bodies carry the evidence of this becoming in ways that are visible if you know how to look. The children who have been here longest have skin that is faintly textured, especially along the forearms and the backs of the hands, a fine pattern of lines that is not quite scarring and not quite scale but shares qualities of both, and their eyes — and this is the detail that unsettles outsiders most — their eyes catch the light in the channels and reflect it differently than the eyes of those who arrived as adults. Not a different color. A different *quality*. As though the light entering their eyes is being sorted somehow, filtered, the way water is filtered through the membranes that separate the freshwater from the denser, older water below.

The adults do not comment on this. The adults have their own versions of it, subtler, deeper, written in places where you cannot see without being invited. You learn not to comment either. You learn this quickly, as you learn the luming, as you learn the pulse. The city is a conversation that has been going on for longer than the residents have been here, and they have grown into it, and you are a visitor in a conversation that does not wait for understanding before continuing.

One evening — you have been here long enough that the seasons have started to feel less like seasons and more like tides, long rhythms with shorter rhythms nested inside them — you sit on a stone ledge beside a channel that is wide and still, and the water reflects nothing because the light has not committed to any direction, and the embedded veins in the building across the channel are pulsing slow, and somewhere beneath the sediment the pulse from below is quiet, and a child walks past on the elevated walkway above you and stops, and looks down, and regards you with an expression that is not curiosity but assessment, and the child says, simply, *It will wait for you to be ready,* and then walks on, and the luming in the channel brightens by exactly the degree that makes the water look like water is supposed to look, and the air is warm and damp and carries the smell of wet stone, and the spiral trees stand in their loose clusters, and their leaves hold the last of the dim light, and nothing moves, and the city is breathing, and the breathing is slow, and there is time.
