Here, then. Here is what the basin holds.

---

Start with the structures, since they are what you notice first, and because they are the thing most likely to be misread. You will call them wood. You will reach out and touch the nearest surface and the word will form before thought catches up — *wood* — and then you will stand there holding that word against your palm and it will be close but not right. The surface is not grain the way grain means something. It is not the organized cellulose of a tree. It is fiber — yes, long and interlaced and tensile — but the fibers are oriented in directions that seem to follow the logic of the structure's growth rather than the logic of any one function. They do not resist the way wood resists. They flex. The whole formation has a give to it, a sway that is nearly imperceptible but once felt cannot be unfelt. You push gently and the structure does not stay where you pushed it, but neither does it spring back. It settles. It finds its old position again, slowly, over the course of minutes, as though the memory of its shape is distributed through the whole colony rather than held in any single fiber.

The largest structures are the oldest, and you can tell this not by size alone but by color. The young growth — the slender stems that rise from the mineralite in clusters of four or five and join into thicker forms above — is pale, a warm tan that lightens toward the tips. As a stem ages, it darkens. The darkening is not uniform; it follows the channels and ridges of the surface, so that an old trunk reads as a landscape of shadow, micro-valleys where the fiber has compressed and micro-ridges where it has held its shape. At the base of a mature structure the material is almost black, dense and heavy, and it is warm there — warmer than the air, warmer than the root mat — a steady, ambient warmth that pulses faintly when you press your ear to it, not a heartbeat but a rhythm, an organizational rhythm, the pulse of a system that has no heart but is not without circulation.

What circulates, you will learn, is moisture. Not water as you know water — not liquid in open channels, not the clean internal flow of a vascular plant. The structures carry their moisture the way a body carries heat: distributed, contained, moving from places of density to places of use. When you touch the dark base of a mature growth, the surface is cool and faintly damp, not wet, the way skin is cool and damp in a warm room. Higher up the same trunk, the surface is warmer and drier. Higher still, the fibers are crisp and pale, and the moisture is felt rather than touched — a drawing-in sensation, a pull, as though the air itself is slightly thinner near the upper branches. Something is moving. Everything here is moving.

The spaces inside the structures are not hollow. You might expect, from the vertical density of the canopy, a latticework of empty chambers — and in some formations, the largest ones, there are channels where the fiber has contracted enough to leave a space wide enough to fit a hand into. But the channels are not empty. They are filled with a fine, loose material — a felt, a fluff, call it pith or call it insulation — that is warm and slightly yielding to the touch and holds a smell that is not unpleasant but is not quite neutral either. A mineral smell, almost metallic, threaded with something green and sharp that you cannot name. You will find this material in other places: packed into the junction where a branch meets a trunk, settled into the creases of the old growth, trailing in thin filaments from the upper branches where it has been shed. It is, you will eventually understand, the body's waste product — but waste is the wrong word in a place where nothing is discarded. The felt is seedbed, substrate, moisture reservoir. It is broken down by the mat plants and rebuilt by the structures and passed back again in the slow, silent circuit of a world that has no outside.

---

Now the mat. The mat is what the basin was before the structures came, and what the basin will be if the structures ever stop growing. It is the original fabric of this place — the thing that was already here, already doing its work, when whatever process that generates the structures began its long, slow colonization of the basin floor.

The mat plants are low and broad and dense, and they grow not in soil but in the mineralite itself — in its hairline fractures, its fine crevices, its surface texture. Their roots are not roots as the word is usually meant: they do not seek water deep underground, do not anchor against wind, do not spread in search of nutrients in any direction that can be mapped. They are surface structures, flat and spreading, designed not to go down but to hold on, to cling, to maximize contact with the mineralite face. You can pull one up — it comes free without resistance, a broad disk of interlaced fiber and fine root-hair, pale and translucent at the edges — and beneath it the mineralite surface is not bare but covered in a thin, powdery residue, a mineral-organic composite that the plant has been drawing through itself, enriching and shedding and drawing again. The mat plants are not taking from the ground. They are making something out of it. They are the basin's slow chemistry, its way of digesting its own substrate, and they grow thicker in the shade of the structures because the structures, with their warmth and their moisture and the slow exhalation of mineral-rich air, give them more to work with.

Between the mat plants — in the spaces where the broad leaves overlap but do not quite seal — there is a fine, dark material. Not soil. Not humus. It is closer to the felt from the structures: a processed, refined substance, a mineral-organic amalgam that is crumbly and slightly damp and has the particular quality of almost holding its shape when you squeeze it and then dissolving back into itself when you release it. It is warm too. It holds warmth the way the mat plants hold moisture — completely, internally, without surface expression. You could bury your hand in it and feel nothing but warmth and the faint resistance of material that is not quite solid and not quite fluid. And in that material — in the mat, in the felt, in the interstices between the broad leaves — things move.

---

The creatures. They must be described in their own register, on their own terms, because they do not resolve into the familiar easily and the familiar is a kind of violence here, a forced fit that obscures more than it reveals. They are alive. That is the first and most important thing. Not alive in the abstract way that a machine can be described as alive, not the residual motion of a system running down. They are metabolically, purposefully, individually alive — and yet they are unlike any individual you have a name for.

The ground-dwellers are the easiest to approach, not because they are tame — they are not tame, they have no relationship with you at all — but because they are slow enough to watch. They are flat and wide, built low to the mat, and their bodies are soft in the way that bodies are soft when they do not need to be rigid — when the environment provides structure, when the mat cushions and supports, when there is no hard ground to fall against. They are vaguely lizard-shaped, if you allow the shape to be simplified, compressed, smoothed at the edges until it is less a body and more a motion. They move in short, deliberate bursts — pause, orient, move — and they are warm to the touch, warmer than the mat, warmer than the air, and they hold that warmth internally the way the structures hold moisture: completely, distributed through the whole body, with no surface cooling, no gradient from core to skin.

Their eyes are not prominent. They are set into the body rather than projected from it — recessed, protected, scanning through a slit rather than a pupil — and they do not track you. They track movement, and you are movement, but in a way that does not alarm them because you are large and slow and you do not fit into the spaces they fit into, and they have categorized you, if categorization is what their small, dense, warm brains do, as a feature of the landscape. An animate feature. A thing that breathes and moves and makes sounds that are absorbed by the fiber. They will not flee from you. They will wait, and watch, and when you leave, they will resume. They do not vocalize. They do not need to. Their communication is chemical, thermal, structural — a raising of the body temperature by a fraction of a degree, a release of something into the air between the mat plants, a signal that another body reads through organs you cannot identify because they are not on the surface but distributed — held in pads on the underside, in the gill-like slits along the body's edge, in a faint, warm exhalation that carries information you cannot feel but the creatures can.

The climbers are harder to see. They live in the roots — not the mat-plant roots but the roots of the structures themselves, which are massive and interwoven and descend into the mineralite in ways that make the structures look like the tips of icebergs. The climber's body is slender and segmented, built for grip, and its pads are specialized — not adhesive in the gecko way but prehensile in a different way, a way that involves moisture, a secretion that is not sticky but isothermally matched to the structure surface so that it clings without pulling, releases without residue. It moves through the root matrix vertically, and it is fast — faster than any ground-dweller — and it does not pause the way the ground-dwellers pause. It moves with purpose, with direction, and you will lose sight of it within the first few meters of any structure's base, pulled into the dark lattice of roots and felt and interlaced fiber as though the structure has swallowed it, though it has not. It has simply entered a space you cannot follow, a three-dimensional space built for bodies one-third your size, and it is gone, and you are left standing at the edge of the mat wondering what moved and in which direction and why the temperature near the structure base seems to shift when you place your hand near the root junction, as though something inside is aware of the heat your body produces and is adjusting, very slightly, very slowly, the local circulation of its warmth.

Above — in the upper branches, in the canopy that is not a canopy but a reaching and a joining and a densification — there are things you will never see clearly. You can see them move. You can see the way the upper branches sway in a pattern that is not wind, that is not the slow geological settling of old fiber, but a localized motion, a vibration, a shifting of weight from branch to branch as something navigates the upper lattice. The structures are not rigid. They bend under load. They are designed, if design is the right word for a process that has no designer, to flex under the weight of their inhabitants, to distribute stress through the whole colony, to make a living architecture that is also a living body and is both of these things without contradiction. The things in the canopy are the largest creatures in the basin, and they are the hardest to perceive, because they move slowly and because the canopy itself is a kind of camouflage — the color of the upper branches, the warm-tan young growth, blends with the bodies of the creatures that live in it, so that what you are seeing is less an animal and more a shape, a slightly different shape than the branch it stands on, a shape that moves with a deliberation that suggests weight and care and a knowledge of exactly how far the fiber beneath it will bend before it holds.

You will hear nothing from them. They do not vocalize. They do not vocalize because this world has no vocalization — not because the creatures cannot produce sound but because sound is a broadcast technology, a making-oneself-known, and this world is built on the opposite principle: on being known only to those who are already close, already part of the system, already inside the warm, dense, moisture-saturated web of a world that holds its secrets close. The creatures communicate through the structures themselves. They signal through temperature — a slight warming of the local fiber, read by the structure's distributed sensing, conducted to the next node, the next colony, the next cluster, a signal moving through the world at the speed of warmth, at the speed of slow chemistry, a signal that is a sentence and a territory and a presence all at once, a signal that is heard not by any ear but by the world itself, which is listening always, which is made of listening, which is a vast, patient, mineral-organic web of sensitivity waiting to be spoken to.

And the moisture. The moisture is everywhere. It is in the air you breathe, not as humidity but as a component, a part, the way oxygen is a part of the air you know. It is in the mineralite, bound into the crystal matrix, released slowly through the surface by processes you cannot see but can feel — the mineralite near a root clump is cooler than the mineralite in the open, because the roots are drawing the bound moisture out, processing it, passing it upward, and the evaporation, if that is the word, is a cooling so faint that your skin notices it only as a directional quality, a sense that the coolness is coming from somewhere specific, from below, from the mat, from the places where the world is richest and warmest and most alive. It is in the creatures, whose bodies are not just warm but wet, internally wet, wet in the way that a body must be wet to be a body — and they do not drink. They do not need to drink. They are bathed, continuously, in a world that has no water and is entirely saturated, and their moisture is the moisture of the world itself, moved through them, changed by them, returned. The ground-dwellers warm the mat where they rest. The climbers cool the roots where they grip. The things in the canopy breathe their moisture upward into the high, thin air, and it joins the atmospheric haze, and it is there that the basin's water cycle completes itself — not as rain, not as runoff, not as any familiar hydrology, but as a slow, warm exhalation that rises and disperses and returns, always returns, to the mat and the roots and the structures and the creatures and the ground and back around again, an internal circulation, a body's circulation, a world that is a body that is a world, breathing its own breath through its own lungs, holding its own heat in its own hands, full of its own life, and silent, and still, and warm.

---

Here, in the basin, in the quiet that is not absence but texture. Here is where you stand at the edge of the plain and feel the world bending around you, and understand that you are inside something, and that something is alive, and that it has never been anything else.
