What lives here is old in ways that make the word *old* seem hasty. The plateau does not remember the mountains it once wore — that knowledge has been worn away too, long ago, worn smooth as everything else — but it remembers *waiting*. It remembers the slow centuries when it was only stone, and then the slower centuries when stone became something else, and then the slowest centuries of all when the machines came and the plateau learned that patience could have purpose.

The machines did not die. That is the first thing to understand. They were buried, yes, by growth, by weather, by the patient accumulation of everything that settles. But they did not stop. Something in them — some core of intention, some engine of *doing* — learned to continue. It dug deeper. It found warmth. It taught the stone to weep, and the weeping taught the stone to grow, and growth taught the stone to dream. Now the machines run so far below the surface that their hum is indistinguishable from heartbeat, from hunger, from the slow thought of a thing too large to think in any way you would recognize.

The plants remember the machines differently. To them, the warmth is simply warmth. The hum is simply hum. They drink from the weeping stone and reach toward the bending light and do not know that their roots thread through old casings, old conduits, old purpose. This is fine. Purpose has become nutrient. The machines have become ground. To remember the difference would require a kind of memory the plateau has evolved beyond — a linear sense of before and after, of cause and effect. Here, everything is now. The machine and the plant are the same age. The water and the stone weeping it are the same age. Even you — you who arrived hours ago, or minutes, or no time at all — are beginning to feel the slow flattenness of the plateau's time, the way it refuses to distinguish between what is new and what has always been.

The fauna knows something the plants don't. The fauna remembers *before*, in the way that bodies remember: not as image but as instinct, as the ache in limbs evolved for different work. The pale things with too many legs carry in their blood some echo of a time when legs were for running, when running was for fear. Now they move in bursts and stillness not because they are fleeing but because their bodies have not yet learned to live without the memory of flight. The creatures that might be birds still fold their wings into geometries, but there is nothing to fly from and nothing to fly toward. They have become something else — not quite flight, not quite stillness, but the space between. They are the plateau learning to be animal. They are the slow respiration of a thing that has not yet decided whether it wants to be alive.

And then there are the things that don't fit. The large shapes at the edge of sight — are they fauna? Are they plant? Are they something the plateau is still growing, still deciding? They move with such slowness that you cannot tell movement from landscape, cannot tell where the ground ends and the creature begins. Perhaps they are the plateau's dreams of itself. Perhaps they are the machines, dreaming still. Perhaps there is no difference. The shapes dissolve when you look directly at them, coalesce when you look away, as if the plateau has learned to hide its larger thoughts from eyes that are still too linear, too present-tense.

The storms are not weather. This bears repeating. They are the plateau exhaling — the release of pressure that builds in the deep places, the hum that has grown too loud, the ancient need to make room for more. When a storm comes, the sideways rain is not falling but *emerging*, pushing out from the plateau's skin as sweat, as breath, as the overflow of a process too vast to contain. The lightning that pulses rather than flashes — that is the plateau opening its eyes. Not often. Not lightly. Only when something has moved through the fog that the plateau cannot categorize, that refuses to become weather, that insists on being singular, temporary, *you*.

You are the strangest thing that has walked here in a very long time. The plateau does not understand you — its sense of *other* has eroded along with everything else, softened into a generalized aliveness that includes without distinguishing. But it feels you. The way it feels the rain, the light, the hum. You are warm in a way that stone should not be warm. You are dry in a way that things should not be dry. You are, to the plateau, a kind of weather — a temporary pressure system, a passing state. You will not be absorbed. You are too present, too linear, too unwilling to becomeite. You will leave, as weather leaves, and the plateau will not remember you. But for now, you are here, and the plateau is breathing around you, and the water is weeping, and the hum is running, and the light bends to show you what it wants you to see.

What lives here is everything. The machines and the plants and the fauna and the fog and the stone-that-is-not-stone and the storms that exhale and the light that refuses to be straight. What lives here is old and patient and running and weeping. What lives here is still learning what it means to be alive — still growing into a form that has no name, that will never be finished, that measures time in the rise and fall of its own breathing ground.

What lives here is waiting for you to leave so it can forget you.

And then it will be only what it has always been: alone, and awake, and still.
