# What Lives in the Rookery

The bats came first, or the cave came first—there is no record, no way to separate them now. They hang in the high chambers where the ceiling narrows to a blade, and when your light finds them they do not startle. They have not startled in generations. Their bodies press together in clusters so dense they become a single organism, a mass of fur and folded wings that breathes as one, and when one stirs the whole colony shivers like a thought passing through a mind. They are *Myotis*—or something close to it, something that has been changing in the dark long enough to become other than what it was. Their eyes are vestigial dots, barely seeds, and they navigate by sound so refined it maps the space in geometries you cannot see. They know the Rookery better than you know your own house. They have been mapping it for ten thousand years, and the map is inside them, written in frequencies.

Below them, between the falling and the floor, the cave crickets have built their civilization. They are *Troglophilus*—adapted, pale, their bodies the color of old bone. They cluster on the walls in patterns that seem random until you look closely: they arrange themselves by moisture gradient, by temperature, by some calculus of microclimate you do not understand. Each one sits in its small territory, rubbing its wings together in the ultrasonic range, adding its voice to the chorus until the combined sound becomes a hiss, a static, a presence that presses against your eardrums like a hand. When you pass too close, they scatter in waves, and the sound changes as the pattern breaks and reforms.

The floor belongs to the isopods. They are *Armadillidium*—or their deep-cave descendants, so pale they are nearly transparent, their pigments long since bleached away by generations of darkness. They do not scatter from your light; they have no eyes to be troubled by it. They simply *are*, stacked in layers two and three deep where the moisture pools, their bodies overlapping like scales, like shingles, like something preparing for a rain that will never come. When you step, they part and close again behind you, and you leave a trail of small disturbances in their world. They are patient. They have been patient since before humans climbed down from trees. They eat the guano that falls from above, the fungal mats that grow at the waterline, the dead that collect in corners. They are the recyclers, the ones who close the loop, and there are more of them than you can count.

The beetles patrol. They are *Duvalius*—cave ground beetles, glossy and black, their bodies armor-plated against the soft violence of this world. They move in lines that cross and recross, following scent-trails invisible to you, carrying information between pools and passages and chambers you have not yet entered. They are the hunters of the mid-levels, taking isopods, taking cave crickets, taking each other when the hunger rises. Their bodies are engineered for endurance; they can go months without eating. They have been crawling these corridors since the lava cooled, and they have learned nothing and forgotten nothing. When you kneel to look at one, it raises its antennae and considers you, and you feel the weight of its attention—small, alien, but attention nonetheless.

And the spiders. They are *Meta*—orb-weavers, their bodies small and brown and unremarkable to look at, but their webs, their webs are another architecture entirely. They have spun in every gap the rock allows, in every corridor and chamber, strings of silk so fine they catch the torch-light and vanish except where they don't, where they catch and hold a thread of gold for a moment before you understand what you're seeing. The webs are not random; they are placed with intention, with knowledge of air currents and prey-flight and the geometry of space. A web in a doorway is not the same as a web in a corner. They have been refined by selection for thousands of generations, and what they have made is a city—connected, overlapping, a net of meaning across the dark.

The fish are the strangest things you will see, and you will not see them clearly. They live in the pools, the black mirrors that collect in the lowest pockets, and they have been in darkness so long that light is not just useless but toxic. Their bodies have become transparent—not in the way of glass, but in the way of absence, of flesh that has forgotten how to hold its own shape. You can see their hearts beating if you are patient, the slow peristalsis of their organs, the eggs in the females, the ghost of what they were when their ancestors washed in from some ancient river. They are *Proteus*—or something like it, something ancient and persistent, and they move in slow circles that never intersect, and they do not blink because they have no eyelids to blink with, and they have been waiting in the dark since before the mountain above them learned to be a mountain.

The fungus is white and soft and it grows where things fall. Bat guano rains down from above, a steady supply of nitrogen, and where it lands the fungus takes hold, spreading in mats that look like something left behind, something abandoned. But it is not abandoned; it is tended by the isopods and the beetles and the slow chemistry of mineral water, and it is patient in a way that patience means nothing because there is no hurry, there has never been any hurry, the Rookery has all the time there is.

The lichen is stranger. It is not lichen as you know it; it is something older, some symbiosis that has been refined in darkness until the pattern it makes on the stone looks like script, like language, like meaning. You will try to read it and fail, but something in you will want to believe it means something, and you will be right to want that. It means something. It means something to the cave. You are only not the intended reader.

And beyond all of this, in the corners your light does not reach, there is movement. There is always movement. The Rookery is not still; it is only still in the way a forest is still when you are looking at one tree and the rest of the forest breathes around you without looking back. Something moves when you are not looking directly. Something is always moving when you are not looking directly. The sound changes; the temperature shifts; your shadow falls wrong on the wall. You feel watched, but that is not the right word. Watched implies eyes, implies intention. Here it is older than that. Here you are *known*—known the way the stone knows the weight it holds, the way the water knows the path down. You have entered something, and it has not been empty since before it was a cave, since the mountain was magma and the bats were something else and the fish had eyes. It has been full of life and the memory of life and the promise of life, and now you are part of that, pressed into the walls by your presence, your warmth, your breath. The Rookery will hold your shape long after you leave. It will hold it in the stone, in the dark, in the frequencies too low for human ears to hear. And when the next ones come—years from now, centuries, longer—they will wonder at the sharpness of this place, and they will not know why it feels like something has been here before, and they will be right not to know, because knowing is not what the Rookery is for. The Rookery is for *holding*. And it holds everything.
