# Lives of the Plateau

The biofilm is the oldest thing here, though age is difficult to measure when nothing changes. It has no Latin name because no one has come close enough to give it one. It predates the pale creatures, predates the quartzite and basalt cobbles, perhaps predates the current arrangement of cliffs and troughs. It exists on the threshold between stone and life, a threshold so narrow that it barely qualifies as a place, yet here it is, occupied.

Seen from above, the plateau would appear lifeless. The satellites that pass overhead—if any do, if anyone has thought to look at this particular nowhere—would read only geology: the pale granite, the dark volcanic intrusions, the faint glint of water in the small basins. Nothing green. Nothing moving. A surface that reflects sun and gives nothing back.

But from ground level, with the eye pressed close to wet stone, the sheen becomes visible. Not a color, exactly—a condition. The gray-green appears and disappears with the angle of light, with the thickness of the moisture film, with whether you are looking or merely glancing. It is not a plant because it does not grow toward anything. It is not an animal because it does not move. It is an arrangement of moisture and mineral and time, the most basic trick the planet knows: something that takes in and gives out, that persists, that is here and then still here, that has survived its own survival.

The biofilm breathes in ways that have no name. It absorbs whatever the water carries—dissolved minerals, trace elements, the faint dissolved oxygen of the thin air—and it releases something back. Not vapor. Not anything visible. The exchange happens at the scale of molecules, in the thin film of water that coats the stone. The biofilm does not live on the stone; it lives in the stone's dampness, in the boundary between solid and liquid, in the place where the granite's surface stops being granite and starts being something else.

Whether it reproduces is unclear. Whether it dies is unclear. It simply is, the way the water is, the way the light is. A condition of the plateau. An inhabitant so thoroughly at home that it cannot be distinguished from the place it inhabits.

---

The pale creatures are harder to see because they move, and movement draws the eye away. They wait for the wind to drop—not entirely, but enough. The wind never fully stops; it merely thins, becomes a murmur rather than a shout, finds fewer edges to speak to. In those intervals, when the sound of the plateau drops from roar to whisper, the pale creatures emerge.

They are the size of large beetles, or large spiders, or something between—creatures whose taxonomy would require catching one, and catching one would require tools and patience and a willingness to touch something that does not want to be touched. Their bodies are dry and hard-shelled, the color of bleached bone or old quartz, and they move with a quickness that seems disproportionate to their size, as though the thin air gives them strange leverage, as though the low gravity of this altitude makes every step a small leap.

They drink from the thin pools, lowering their mouths to the surface with a caution that suggests predators, though what predators would come here is unclear. The wind is too strong for birds. The temperature swings too sharply for anything that cannot shelter deep in the rock. Perhaps the biofilm is predator enough—perhaps the creatures must avoid letting their soft parts touch the stone too long, must keep moving or be absorbed. Or perhaps the caution is older than the danger, written into them by ancestors who knew threats that no longer exist.

They shelter under the largest cobbles when the wind picks up, pressing themselves into the gaps between stone and ground, waiting. The waiting can last hours. The creatures do not sleep, as far as can be told; they simply pause, their antennae or sensory organs extended into the air, reading something—pressure, vibration, the chemical traces of fog. When the wind drops again, they emerge and continue. They do not travel far. They do not gather. They do not call to one another with sound or light or any signal that registers to human senses.

The plateau could hold a thousand of them or ten; the count would be the same. Movement in an otherwise static world. Motion without meaning. The creatures persist, exactly as they are, in the margins of a landscape that does not encourage them but also does not end them.

What they eat is unclear. The biofilm might sustain them, or the thin moisture might carry nutrients directly into their bodies through some membrane or orifice. Or they might not eat at all in any recognizable sense; they might simply persist, burning whatever they were born with, a slow depletion that will eventually leave them still, their shells the only sign they were ever here. The plateau does not record its dead. Nothing accumulates.

---

The wind is the most present inhabitant, though it does not live in the way the biofilm or the pale creatures live. It moves because it must, pulled by differences in pressure and temperature that originate far from here, from places where the air is thick and warm and full of moisture. The wind crosses the plateau because the plateau is in its path, and it speaks to the edges it finds because speaking is what wind does when it meets resistance.

The sound the wind makes is not incidental. It is the plateau's utterance, its way of announcing itself to whatever might listen. The sigh through cracks is the plateau breathing. The shiver across pebble surfaces is the plateau's skin responding to touch. The sharp clack of stone against stone is the plateau's skeleton shifting, just barely, under pressures that no human could feel but that the wind can hear.

The waterfall far below contributes its voice to this utterance. Its roar cannot be seen from here—only heard, only felt as a vibration in the thin air that seems to come from everywhere at once. The waterfall is the plateau's distant heart, the place where all the weeping water finally falls and begins its long journey back to the sea. Its voice is the voice of that return, carried up the cliff face by air that has cooled and contracted and pulled more air after it, a chain of motion that becomes sound that becomes the plateau's one reliable note.

The wind has no intention. The wind does not know the plateau exists. But the plateau knows the wind, responds to it, has been shaped by it for so long that the shape and the shaping are indistinguishable. The plateau is the record of every wind that has ever crossed it, written in stone, legible to anyone patient enough to read the erosion, the polish, the direction of the worn faces. The wind is both author and inhabitant, both stranger and kin.

---

The plateau itself is the largest life, if life is understood broadly enough to include persistence without change. It breathes, in its way—the slow exhalation of water through stone, the slow inhalation of fog and mist. It feeds on time, converting years into shape, eras into equilibrium. It grows in the sense that every rainstorm and frost-crack adds something, minute by minute, to its total mass of broken stone. It reproduces in the sense that the cobbles it produces will someday, in some other landscape, become the seeds of new formations.

But it does not age, not in any way that matters. It has no old parts and young parts; it is all the same age, which is to say it has no age at all. The granite that forms its cliffs solidified from magma before there were any creatures on this planet, and the basalt that forms its cobbles erupted and cooled and broke and tumbled before anything moved across its surface except water. The plateau does not remember its own beginning because it has no mind, but it holds that beginning in its structure, in the arrangement of stone that tells a story no one is left to read.

The water that weeps through it is not the water that was here at the beginning. It is the same water, the same molecules cycling through the same channels, but it has evaporated and fallen and seeped and emerged so many times that it has forgotten what it was before. The water is as old as the plateau and as new as today's fog, arriving and departing in the same breath.

The light is the newest inhabitant, arriving fresh each morning and departing each night. It does not linger, does not accumulate. It touches the plateau and moves on, leaving no mark, changing nothing except the angle of shadows. But without the light, the plateau would not be the plateau. The light reveals, and the revelation is the plateau's truth: everything mineral, everything wet, everything exposed and bare.

---

The plateau will be like this until it is not. The equilibrium is not permanent; nothing is. Somewhere far below, the tectonic plates shift in their slow dance. Somewhere far above, the atmosphere changes in ways that will eventually reach this altitude and alter the wind, the fog, the moisture. The waterfall may grow or shrink or stop. The granite may crack deeper, the basalt may break finer, the biofilm may spread or retreat.

But these changes are not imminent. They are not even approaching. They are geological, which is to say they are happening at a scale of time that makes human history a footnote, makes human thought a ripple on a surface that smooths itself in the next moment. The pale creatures will not see the change. The biofilm will not feel it. Even the wind, patient as it is, will not remember what came before.

The plateau is a held breath that has never been released. And so, for now, it breathes in, holds, and remains.
