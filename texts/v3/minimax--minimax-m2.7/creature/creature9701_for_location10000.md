The creatures move in the way water moves: not thinking about it, not deciding, simply continuing in the direction that pressure and time and the shape of the world have always pointed. They are ancient in their motion, these small ones, the grazers that cross the valley floor in sheets of soft bodies and sensory cilia, their undersides wet with the moisture they've gathered and their backs bright with the slow pulse of internal light. Watch them for a time and you will see the light propagate — a ripple passing through the crowd like wind through grass, like memory through a mind that has forgotten it was ever anything else. They do not flee from observation. They do not notice.

The grazers come in waves not because they choose to but because the world arranges them into waves. The mineral powder of the valleys crunches under their passage, and where they have moved, the iridescent mats are thinner, paler, recovering slowly in the perpetual damp. They feed without eating, if eating is understood as the tearing and consuming of one body by another. They absorb. The bacterial films dissolve against their surfaces, and what is useful passes inward, and what remains is left behind in a faint gray residue that the next rain — if rain ever comes — will carry down into the stone. The grazers are not beautiful, or they are beautiful in the way that all abundant things are beautiful: individually unremarkable, collectively overwhelming, a demonstration of persistence that outlasts any individual instance of persistence. Each one is soft, flattened, perhaps the size of a child's palm, with margins that ripple and curl as it moves. The internal light is not uniform — it pools and gathers in organs that run along the body's edges, pulsing in patterns that shift with the chemistry of the water, with the hunger in the gut, with the density of others nearby. A solitary grazer pulses slowly, almost sadly. A crowd of grazers pulses in slow synchrony, a great breathing glow that rises and falls across the valley floor like the chest of something sleeping.

Above the valley floor, where the weeping stone meets the open air, there are other lives.

The lickworms — the name is approximation, is the closest the visitor's language can come — emerge from the fissures in the granite where moisture gathers and bacteria flourish. They are long, thin, flexible, with no skeleton that can be identified and no structure that resembles bone or shell. They are, in a sense, all mouth and all gut, a digestive tube that moves through the world tasting what it passes. Their bodies are translucent when young and become opaque with age, until the oldest lickworms are the color of old amber, their interiors crowded with the trapped residues of millennia of absorption. They extend from their crevices in slow exploration, tasting the moisture on the stone face, the chemical gradients that shift as water seeps downward, the faint traces left by grazers who passed hours or days before. They communicate in chemistry. The substances they release into the weeping stone change its composition in ways that other lickworms can taste, so that the rock itself becomes a kind of newspaper, a record of who has passed and what they have consumed and what condition they were in when they passed. To read the stone is to know the lickworms, and the lickworms know each other through the stone. They do not have faces. They do not need them.

The movers are what the visitor would call the largest animals, though large is relative when the largest tops the length of a human forearm. They are bilateral in a way that suggests relationship — common ancestry with the grazers, perhaps, though so long diverged that the resemblance is philosophical rather than physical. Where grazers are soft and flat, movers are armored in a carapace that curves up from the stone like a shield, like a curled leaf, like a fragment of the landscape that has decided to go walking. The armor isite,ite,ite — the same minerals that make up the weeping granite, laid down in layers over millions of years by an organism that builds its protection from the same stuff the world is made of. Where the armor meets the stone, there are gaps, and through those gaps the mover's limbs extend: six, or eight, or twelve in the larger species, each tipped with a structure that is not quite a foot and not quite a hand but serves the purposes of both. They grip the wet rock with a patience that is not patience but simply time — the slow adjustment of pressure, the minute reshaping of surfaces that have learned to hold them.

Movers do not feed on the mats. They feed on the lickworms and the grazers and on each other, when hunger permits or demands. They are the predators of this world, or they were, before hunger and availability settled into the uneasy truce that characterizes most long-established ecologies. They do not hunt, exactly. They are too slow for pursuit, too patient for ambush. They wait at the places where water gathers and grazers must pass to drink, or they position themselves at the mouths of fissures where lickworms emerge, or they simply move through a patch of ground and take what presents itself. They are not cruel. Cruelty requires the capacity for unnecessary suffering, and the movers have only the capacity for continuation, for the next moment, for the slow accumulation of energy and the slow expenditure of it. When they mate, it is brief and chemical — a transfer of substances that will become eggs, left in protected crevices where the moisture will keep them alive until they hatch into something small and swimming and utterly unlike either parent. The young are free-swimmers for a time, suspended in the moisture that fills the deep fissures, feeding on bacteria and each other, until they are large enough to climb out onto the stone and begin the long process of growing their carapaces and learning the patterns of the world.

There are other lives, less visible, perhaps less individual.

The gathers coat the surfaces of the carved basins, the channels that once directed water to collection points now lost. They are colonial, or they behave as though they are colonial — a mass of semi-independent units that together form a surface, a membrane, a skin over the water that gathers there. They pulse with light that is not their own but borrowed from the bacteria they farm, the same iridescent films that cover the weeping stone everywhere, herded and cultivated by an organism that has learned to manage what other organisms simply consume. The gathers do not move. They do not need to. The water comes to them, and the bacteria come with it, and what they harvest is enough. They are the oldest things in the Reaches, or they are the descendants of the oldest things, the survivors of whatever catastrophe or simple time swept the world clean of more complex forms. Theirs is not a life that would be recognized as life by visitors from worlds where individuals are the unit of survival. They are a process, a pattern, a way of being that persists not by remaining unchanged but by changing continuously, by growing and dying and growing again in the shape of what is needed. If there was a builder culture here, if there were hands that shaped the stone and minds that planned, the gathers may be their heirs — transformed beyond recognition, but still present, still processing, still alive in the only way that has ever mattered.

The sound, always the sound. The creatures contribute to it in ways that are not language but are not nothing — vibrations in the stone, chemical releases into the water, the faint crackle of chitin againstite as a mover shifts its weight. Each species has its frequencies, its rhythms, its contributions to the vast murmur that fills the world. The visitor who stands still long enough will begin to distinguish patterns: the rise and fall of the grazers' bioluminescent pulse, synchronized across distances that should be impossible for organisms with no visible nervous system. The slow, deep vibration of a mover settling into a new position, the scrape of armor against stone that carries through the mineral powder and into the bones of anyone standing nearby. The almost-silence of the lickworms, broken by the occasional faint click as one retreats into its crevice, or extends further than it has before, or releases a substance into the weeping stone that another lickworm will taste hours later. And beneath it all, always, the water: the endless seep, the patient descent, the sound of a world that has been weeping for so long it has forgotten how to stop.

There are no ghosts here, or if there are, they are the ghosts of chemistry rather than consciousness. The builders, if builders there were, left their marks and then became the marks — worn smooth, dissolved into the stone, indistinguishable from the landscape that held them. Whatever they were, whatever they wanted, whatever they understood about the world they lived in, it is gone now, returned to the slow process that breaks all things down. But the creatures persist. The water persists. The stone weeps and the creatures move and the sound rises and falls like breath, like tide, like something that does not know it is alive but is alive nonetheless.

This is what lives in the Weeping Reaches: not the past, not the builders, not the reason for any of it. Only the present, endlessly, in forms that do not ask for meaning because meaning is not what life requires. Life requires only that it continue. And it does.
