There are herons here who have forgotten they were ever anything else. They stand on the rust-stained railings of what was once a parking garage, or perching on the bones of a traffic light, or motionless at the edge of a submerged awning where the water is shallow enough to see the bottom. They are great blue herons mostly, though there are also smaller egrets with their bright apostrophes of movement. They do not startle. They have been watched for so long by the people who live here that the watching has become mutual, a surveillance that is also a kind of communion. The herons fish. The people watch. Neither seems to find the other remarkable.

In the early morning, when the light is at its thinnest, you can see the herons begin their hunts. They walk slowly through the shallows, their legs bending with a deliberateness that seems almost ceremonial. Their heads move in small, precise increments, tracking something you cannot see. And then—the strike. Faster than thought, faster than the water can record it. A fish, silver and struggling, disappears into the heron's throat, and the heron stands again, the neck settling back into its S-curve, and the hunt begins anew. They do this for hours. They do this every day. They have been doing this since before the city was a city, and they will do this after the last of the concrete has softened into sediment. They do not carry meaning. They are meaning, in the way that water is water, in the way that anything alive continues to be alive.

The people who live here are not sad. You must understand this first. They have had time to be sad—the generations that watched the water rise, that hauled their furniture upstairs, that eventually stopped hauling and started swimming. Sadness was a thing that happened to them. It is not a thing that lives in them. What lives in them now is something else, something that has no name in the language of the drowned but might be called attention, or readiness, or the simple stubbornness of a body that wakes each morning and continues. They tend their rooftop gardens and mend their rope bridges and haul water in jerry cans up endless stairs. They do not speak of leaving because there is nowhere to leave to and no one who would remember them if they went. They are simply here, the way the herons are here, the way the water is here. Present tense, all of it. A city in the grammatical state of now.

At night, sometimes, you see the lights. Not electric lights—there are no electric lights—but small flames, carefully shielded in hurricane globes or glass jars, moving between the buildings on boats so quiet you mistake them at first for will-o'-the-wisps or the phosphorescence of disturbed water. These are the fishers, the ones who go out in the hours when the heat relents and the fish come closer to the surface. They carry torches made from oil-soaked rags wound around sticks. They do not speak. They find their spots by the feel of the current, by the way the water moves around the submerged cars and building foundations, and they wait. The flames move only when the waiting is over, or when they need to move to another spot. In the dark, between the towers, the lights describe slow geometries, the orbits of people who know their place in the drowned world.

There is a woman—I do not know her name—who lives in what was once a hotel. Her floor is the fourteenth, and she has hung curtains in the doorway that open onto the air and close onto a room that smells of old concrete and new growth. She grows tomatoes in planter boxes, which should not be possible in this place of salt and heat, but she does. She is old, or she seems old, though in this climate it is hard to tell where age ends and the weight of experience begins. She sits on her balcony in the evenings and watches the water. She does not wave to the boats that pass below, but she acknowledges them—a lift of the chin, a slight turn of the head—and the boats acknowledge her. An exchange. A grammar of recognition that does not require language.

The frogs are louder than anyone. They have taken the upper floors of the shorter buildings, the ones whose roofs are just barely above the waterline, and their chorus fills the night with a sound that is not quite music and not quite noise but something in between—a persistence, a insistence. They are calling to each other across the dark water, and you imagine they are saying something important, though you will never know what. Their sound is the sound of the place continuing, of biology as its own argument. The frogs do not wonder whether they should be here. They are here, so they call, so they breed, so their tadpoles wriggle in the shallow pools on broken rooftops, so the cycle continues. They are the most successful colonists of this drowned world, after the algae, after the bacteria, after whatever soft and patient thing is slowly dissolving the concrete into a silt that will one day be soil.

And there is something else. You feel it more than you see it. A movement in the deeper water, below the level where light reaches, a shifting of mass that the fish respond to by parting and then reforming. It is not a fish—there are no fish that large, not here. It is not a boat—the boats are all above, in the light. It is something that has found its way into this space the way water finds its way through cracks, patient and certain. You catch glimpses of it sometimes, in the moment before dawn, when the phosphorescence is at its brightest and the water seems almost transparent: a curve of grey, a length too long, a wake that moves upstream from nothing. The people do not speak of it. When you ask, they change the subject, or they say only: *it was here before we were*, which might mean anything or nothing. The herons do not hunt near it. The fish give it wide berth. It is, you suspect, the oldest thing in this city, older than the concrete, older perhaps than the coast itself. It has simply remained, the way the water has remained, the way the dark has remained. A presence without demands. A thing that lives here the way the towers live here, the way the mangroves grip their concrete purchase with roots that have learned to drink salt.

You will not see it clearly. You will not need to. Its existence is enough. It reminds you that this place has dimensions you have not yet explored, depths you have not yet descended to, a strangeness that is still larger than the strangeness you have already encountered. You float in the water between the towers, and you feel the faint current where the tide pulls through, and you understand that you are a visitor in a place that has always been itself. The city was never meant to be an archipelago. But it is one now, and it has had time to learn what that means. The water is warm. The light is weak. The herons stand in their long patience, and the frogs keep their chorus, and the woman on the fourteenth floor tends her impossible tomatoes, and somewhere below you, in the dark, something ancient moves slowly through the drowned streets and does not ask for your understanding. It only asks to be left alone in what is also, now, its home.
