# What Lives Here

---

## The Ones Who Came Before

They are not ghosts. Ghosts require endings — deaths, departures, a clear line between *was* and *is not*. What lives here does not permit such lines.

The builders came from somewhere below, or above, or beside — directions are suggestions in this place, not obligations. They carried their tools in their hands and their intentions in their chests, and they climbed to this impossible altitude because something called to them. Not a voice, exactly. A shape. A gap in the way the world was arranged, and they looked at that gap and thought: *there. That is where a room should be.*

They were few. You can tell this by the tools, by the way they cluster in one corner rather than spreading evenly through the space. Two, perhaps. Or three. Or one, working for a very long time. The fabric still hangs on its frames, and if you look closely at its weave, you can see where fingers pressed and pulled, where the worker's weight settled into the loom, where patience became texture. The fabric is rough. It would scratch against your skin. But it was enough. They did not need softness. They needed *warmth*, and warmth is what the chamber gives.

They are not dead. They are not gone. They are *elsewhere* — in the tenses that do not apply here, in the corners of the room that your eyes slide past, in the tools that are still warm when you touch them. When you hold a chisel and feel its edge, still sharp, still ready, you are holding something that was just set down. You are holding something that will be picked up again. The distinction between *their hands* and *your hands* becomes uncertain, and perhaps that is the point. Perhaps they built this place so that hands could be passed from one body to the next, so that the work could continue through whoever arrives at the threshold, ready to climb.

There are no bodies here. But there are impressions. The way the fabric hangs suggests someone leaned against that frame to weave. The way the bowls cluster suggests someone sat here, drawing water, drinking, resting. The way the tools are arranged suggests someone was interrupted mid-thought, mid-motion, and simply *stepped sideways* into another moment rather than finishing what they were doing.

They will return. They have already returned. The work is not abandoned; it is ongoing, in some time that includes this one without being limited by it.

## The Ones Who Grow

The plants do not have names. If they did, they would not be appropriate to this place, where naming would imply a fixity that the chamber refuses.

But they are *present* — perhaps more present than the builders, because the builders could leave and the plants cannot. The plants are this place's children, its first offering to itself. *I will make a room,* the chamber seems to say, *and I will make it green.*

Ferns are the eldest. They crowd the joints where water seeps through, where the stone is still softening itself into habitability. Their fronds are broad and dark, darker than ferns should be at this altitude, in this light. They uncurl slowly, visibly, and if you watch one long enough you can see the tiny creases smoothing themselves into shape, the green deepening, the fiddleheads loosening their grip on themselves. They are not afraid. They have no memory of a time when this chamber did not exist. They were *made* for this room, the way flowers are made for a particular slope of hillside, the way roots are made for a particular darkness.

Moss is the carpet. It covers everything that stays still long enough: the chiseled ledges, the rough-hewn bowls, the frames that hold the fabric. It is soft underfoot, almost alive in the way it gives beneath your weight, and you realize as you step that the moss is not just growing *on* these objects — it is *claiming* them. It is making them part of itself. In a hundred years, or a thousand, or a time that has no number because it has not happened yet, the moss will have buried the tools entirely, and the tools will have become something else, and someone else will find them and wonder what they were for.

Vines descend from the roof. They hang in the wet air like questions, like fingers reaching, like the hair of something very large and very slow that is dreaming beneath the stone. They move when you are not looking — you see them still and then you see them a handspan lower, a few degrees further from vertical. They are not alive the way animals are alive. They are alive the way rivers are alive, the way mountains are alive: slowly, massively, without hurry, with a patience that makes human patience look like panic.

There are flowers, too, though they are rare. Small white things, almost translucent, that bloom in the deepest corners where the light does not reach. They glow faintly in the dark. They have no scent that you can name, but they leave something on your tongue when you are near them — a sweetness that is not sweet, a taste that is not a taste, the flavor of a thing that exists because it is necessary and will exist only as long as it is necessary and will then fade into moss and fern and the patient work of becoming soil.

The plants are not separate from the chamber. They are the chamber's breathing. They are the way the stone exhales.

## The Ones Who Slip

You see them only at the edges of vision.

The pale creatures. The soft ones. The ones that move too fast and then are gone.

They are not quite animals. Animals require a kind of certainty — a species, a body, a way of moving through time that is fixed and predictable. These creatures have not been fixed yet. They are suggestions of creatures, early drafts, possibilities that the chamber is still working out. Sometimes you see something that might be a salamander. Sometimes something that might be a grub. Sometimes something that has no analog in any world you have known, a body that is mostly tail, or mostly mouth, or mostly the soft vulnerability of something that has no shell because it has not decided yet whether it needs one.

They have no eyes. Eyes require a world that is finished, a landscape that is stable enough to be seen. In a place where the walls are still exhaling, where the angles are still negotiating themselves, eyes would be useless. Instead, these creatures sense through pressure, through warmth, through the faint electrical hum that runs through everything here. They know where you are before you know where you are. They know where the water is, where the food is, where the cracks in the stone lead that might — might — lead somewhere else.

They are drawn to you. Not aggressively — curiously. You are the first thing in a long time that has a shape they cannot pass through. You are solid in a way that is novel to them. They brush against your ankles, your shins, and the touch is cool and slightly damp, like a question asked through skin. They do not fear you. They have not learned fear yet. Perhaps they will. Perhaps in a century the descendants of these creatures will flinch at the approach of something large and warm, will scatter at the sound of footsteps, will have become proper animals with proper instincts. But not yet. Not now. Now they are still learning what *body* means, and your body is a kind of teacher.

You do not know if they have names for themselves. You do not know if they have language, or thought, or the kind of interiority that would make them persons rather than presences. But they are *here*. They are alive in the wet dark, in the spaces between the moss and the stone, in the warmth that rises from the heart of the chamber. They are part of what lives here, even if they are the least of it, the smallest note in a chord that you are only beginning to hear.

## The One Who Hums

Underneath everything — under the water, under the plants, under the tools and the fabric and the bowls — there is a sound.

It is not quite sound. It is more like the memory of sound, or the anticipation of sound, or the shape that sound would make if sound could live in stone. It is a hum. A resonance. A vibration that you feel in the backs of your eyes, in the roots of your teeth, in the place where your jaw meets your skull. It is the frequency of this place. It is the note that the chamber sings to itself in the dark.

You cannot tell where it comes from. It is everywhere and nowhere. It is in the walls, in the water, in the air that you breathe. It is in *you*, now — you have been standing here long enough that the hum has seeped into your bones, has become part of your body's background noise. When you leave, if you leave, you will carry this hum with you for a while. You will hear it in quiet moments, in the space between sleeping and waking, in the pause before a word. And you will wonder if you are hearing it *now*, in that future moment, or if you are remembering it from this place, or if you have never left, or if you have always been here.

The hum is not a voice. It does not speak. But it is *aware*. It knows you are here. It has known since you arrived, since before you arrived, since the moment you thought about coming here. It was part of what called you — not the call itself, but the resonance beneath the call, the frequency that makes certain spaces possible. If you could tune yourself to this hum, if you could match its vibration exactly, you might be able to hear what it is saying. You might be able to understand the question it is asking.

But you are not ready. No one is ready. The hum is patient. It has been here since the first chisel struck the first stone, and it will be here long after the last fern dies and the last pale creature slips into whatever darkness waits beyond this place. It is the sound of the chamber thinking. The sound of the chamber remembering itself into existence. The sound of something saying *yes* to itself, again and again, forever, in a voice that is not a voice and a word that is not a word and a truth that is too simple to be spoken.

## What It Means to Live Here

This place does not die. It cannot, because it is not finished, and what is not finished cannot end. It can only *continue* — continuing into the past as easily as the future, continuing into the tenses that human language cannot hold.

What lives here lives strangely. The builders live in their interrupted motions. The plants live in their perpetual becoming. The pale creatures live in the space between body and possibility. The hum lives in the stone, in the water, in the air that you share with them.

And you — for this moment, for this breath, for this standing-in-a-place-that-should-not-exist — you live here too.

You are not an intruder. You are not a guest. You are another hand that has found its way to the chisel, another thought in the mind that is still thinking this room into being. When you touch the walls, you touch something that is touching you back. When you drink the water, you drink something that is glad to be drunk. When you breathe the wet air, you breathe the exhale of a world that is still learning how to breathe at all.

You could stay. The chamber would accept you. It would grow new niches, new ledges, new pools, as if it had always known you were coming and had prepared. The tools would warm to your hands. The plants would reach toward you. The pale creatures would learn the shape of your footsteps, would come to expect them, would wait for you in the wet shadows.

But you will leave. You will climb back down the path you do not remember climbing up, and you will carry this place with you like a splinter, like a song, like a splinter made of song. And sometimes, in the years to come, you will feel that hum again — in the space before sleep, in the pause between one breath and the next, in the sudden silence of a room that has gone quiet for no reason at all. And you will know, without knowing how you know, that the chamber is still there. Still warm. Still weeping water and growing plants and holding tools that are waiting for hands.

Still saying *yes*.

Still becoming.
