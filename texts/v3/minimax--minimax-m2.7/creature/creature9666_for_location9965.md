You become aware of your own heartbeat, then. It is the loudest thing in the ravine. You had not noticed it before because it was simply there, like the pressure of air or the weight of your clothes, one of those background facts of being alive that the mind quietly subtracts from experience. But now there is nothing else to hear. The beetles do not move. The frogs do not call. The water exhales in its long, smooth breath, and beneath that there is nothing — no wind, no rustle of wings, no scrape of claw on stone. Only your heart, its small insistent rhythm, marking out a time that the ravine has forgotten.

You try to hold still. You want to be kind to this place, to take nothing from it, to leave no mark. But you are breathing, and each breath is a small violence — an intake of air that has been here for centuries, that belongs to this平衡, and you are pulling it into your lungs and filling them with your own warmth and your own pressing blood, and when you exhale you return something different. Carbon dioxide. Heat. The faint chemical signature of animal life, of metabolism, of time. You are a small furnace, burning through the stillness.

And so you begin to feel like an intrusion. Not physically — nothing here seems to react to your presence, no animal startles, no insect takes flight. But philosophically, spatially, in the order of things, you do not belong. You belong to the bright world above, the world where things change, where seasons turn and creatures age and die according to some logic that this place has surpassed. You are the wrong kind of life. You are the life that still believes in tomorrow, in next year, in the slow erosion of your own body toward some conclusion. This ravine has no conclusion. It has only continuation.

You wonder how long you have been standing here. The light has not shifted. The seep has not pulsed. A minute ago or an hour ago — what is the difference? In the world above, a minute and an hour are separated by the movement of the sun, the changing angle of shadows, the slow rotation of the earth through its allotted time. Down here, there is no rotation. There is only the gray light, sourceless and unchanging, and your own counting of breaths, which means nothing. Your breaths could be one hundred or one thousand. The frogs have not moved. The beetles have not shifted. Something in the deep pools — something pale and finless, something that might be a fish or might be something older than fish, something that predates the distinction — has not surfaced, has not quivered, has not acknowledged your presence in any way you can detect.

You think of the word *equilibrium*. It is the word the scientists use, the naturalists, the ones who come here with their instruments and their clipboards and their careful measurements. They measure the pH of the water and the density of the moss and the temperature at three points along the ravine floor, and they find no variation. Nothing varies. They write this down in their notebooks and they nod to each other and they use the word *balance*, and they mean something true but also something incomplete. They have measured the stasis but not its quality. They have counted the creatures and weighed the water and catalogued the species, but they have not felt what it means to stand in a place where nothing wants anything, where nothing reaches for anything, where the sheer density of life is not competition but simply fullness — a cup that cannot hold another drop, a sentence that cannot bear another word.

That is what lives here. Fulfillment. Not happiness — happiness is a feeling, and feelings require time, require the passage from worse to better to worse again. This is something older than feeling. It is the condition of completion, the state of a thing that has reached its final form and will not be changed. The ferns are not growing; they are simply fern. The moss is not spreading; it is simply moss. The frogs are not breeding or hunting or fleeing; they are simply frogs, in their multitudes, each one a small green stillness pressed against the wet stone.

And you are simply you, in your wrongness, your time-bound body, your hungry cells that do not know how to stop consuming. You are the thing that moves in a world of stopped things, and you are beginning to understand that this is not sustainable. Not because anything here is hostile — nothing here is anything — but because the place is complete, and you are not. You are still in process. You are still becoming something, and this place has no becoming left in it.

The drop of water runs down the back of your neck. The pale many-legged thing on the ledge above you does not move. Somewhere in the deep pool, the finless thing waits in a stillness so perfect it has forgotten it is waiting. And you stand in the middle of it all, breathing, your heart marking out its rhythm, its useless, beautiful rhythm, and you feel the weight of your own temporality pressing down on you like the cold wet air.

You will leave. You will climb back up to the light, to the world that changes, to the place where things end. But you will carry this with you — this gray stillness, this sourceless light, this sense of a world that reached its completion so long ago that nothing has happened since. You will try to describe it to others and you will fail, because the words will be full of time, and time is what this place has exhaled. You will say *equilibrium* and *balance* and you will mean something true but not enough, not nearly enough. You will go back to the world of shifting shadows and changing seasons, and some part of you will still be standing in the ravine, still watching the beetles, still listening for a heartbeat that is not your own.

And perhaps that is the strangest thing of all. Perhaps that is what lives here, in the end. Not just the ferns and the frogs and the pale eyeless things. But a kind of memory. A model of what the world might have been, or might become, or might already be beneath the noise of change. A stillness that is not absence but presence. A completion that does not preclude existence but encompasses it, holds it, keeps it.

The ravine does not breathe.

And you begin to understand that you have never quite done so either, until now.
