The water moves. You keep coming back to that, even when you are not looking at it. In the lowest part of the space, where the pools gather and their edges blur into mud, there is a slow current — not visible as flow but present, perceptible if you watch long enough. A leaf caught in the surface will drift, not back and forth, not in circles, but toward a point between two sunk foundations where the concrete has crumbled into the mud and the waterline dips slightly lower than it should. You crouch there. You lower your face toward the surface. The water is dark, opaque with whatever lives in it, and it is moving, almost imperceptibly, inward. Downward. Toward whatever is beneath.

If you put your hand in — and you do, eventually, because the place invites this, makes you complicit in your own unease — the water is cold in a way that goes beyond temperature. It is cold like deep places, like places that have not been touched by sun in longer than memory. It pulls. Not with force, not with any current you can feel against your skin, but with something subtler. Your fingers feel it: the water wants to go somewhere, and it is patient.

There are things in the water. Not fish — the water is too still, too stale in places, though in the moving channels there might be something, some pale creature evolved for darkness that you will never see clearly. But there are other presences. When you lift your hand and shake the water off, the droplets fall into the pool and the surface closes over them without ripple. Something has taken them. Or the water has closed over them. The distinction starts to matter less. You wipe your hand on your trousers and the damp stays longer than it should, clinging to the fabric as if reluctant to leave.

---

The machinery holds its own conversation with the water, and they do not agree.

In one corner, a pump assembly sits on a concrete slab that has sunk unevenly, so that the whole housing tilts at an angle that should prevent operation. But the pump runs. It runs because the shaft is level — someone has shimmed it, added washers and metal scraps beneath the mounting feet until the moving parts sit true even as the body of the machine lists. You see the evidence of this in the pattern of corrosion on the washers themselves, the way they've been chosen and placed and left to slowly fail in their turn. Nothing here is fixed. Everything is compensated.

The reciprocating engine beside it — old, massive, its flywheel scarred with age — drives a belt that connects to two other machines through a system of pulleys overhead. When the engine cycles, the whole assembly shudders, and the belt stretches and jumps on the pulleys with a sound like something swallowing. Then the rhythm settles. The pump draws. The pressure builds. Somewhere, a valve vents, and steam escapes with that brief metallic shriek you've already learned to expect, already learned to wait for. The steam dissipates quickly in the damp air. The sound fades into the underlying pulse, the low throb that fills the space like a heartbeat you cannot locate.

You listen for the heartbeat. You learn to hear it as a single thing, though it is not. It is the sum of many rhythms — the pump's stroke, the engine's cycle, the hum of the overhead wires, the settling of stone under its own weight, the slow crush of things into mud. All of it together makes a sound that is almost silence, that is the sound of something alive in the way that living is not comfortable, not easy, not certain of its own continuation but continuing anyway.

---

The artifacts accumulate. You have been noting them — the metal rings set flush in the platforms, the short lengths of chain, the concrete blocks whose purpose is unclear — but as you move through the space they begin to form a pattern, or the idea of a pattern, or the feeling that there was once a pattern and it has been lost or obscured or never quite existed.

The metal rings are everywhere. Some are small — the size of a fist — set into stone or concrete at irregular intervals. Some are large, bolted to the undersides of platforms where they hang over the water, their eyes downward, their purpose invisible. They do not swing. They do not move. But when you pass beneath them and look up, you cannot shake the sense that they are waiting for something to be hung from them, or that something was hung from them once and the chain or rope or cable is long gone and only the ring remains, fixed in place, holding its shape.

The channels are worse. Shallow grooves cut into stone surfaces, no wider than a hand, no deeper than a finger's first joint. They run in lines that almost connect, that seem to point toward the low place where the water gathers, but they do not meet. They end in flat surfaces or in cracks or in other channels that do not align. They are not drains — there is no slope to carry water, no obvious flow direction. They are not decorative. They are not accidents. They are something else, something that required effort and tools and time and was done anyway, was done and then abandoned or obscured or absorbed back into the place so that its meaning is no longer retrievable.

You find a wall that has no right angle. It is built of old brick, the kind of brick that was fired in local kilns and carries the color of the soil in its body, so that the wall is the color of the mud, the color of the water's edge. The wall should be a rectangle — it has the thickness of a wall, the height of a wall, the feel of a wall — but one section deviates, curves inward, then outward again, so that the inner face is not flat but rippled, not regular but scarred with shallow indentations at roughly hand-sized intervals. Someone made this. Someone carved the indentations on purpose, spaced them carefully, left the marks for no reason you can determine.

You press your hand into one of the indentations. The brick is cold. The indentation is the right size, the right depth, for a hand to fit. You pull your hand back. The cold stays.

---

The light changes when you are not watching. You notice it because you look up — the sky is still gray, still even, still the light of a damp day without drama — and yet the light in the space is different. Sharper, or flatter, or closer. The shadows have moved without your seeing them move. The surfaces that were damp are either drying or wetter, and you cannot tell which, and the difference matters in a way you cannot explain.

In one place, a shaft of light falls through a gap in the overhead pipes and strikes a puddle on a concrete platform. The puddle is still. The light falls on it, and for a moment the water's surface is not dark but silver, bright, almost blinding. Then a cloud passes, or the light shifts, and the water is dark again, opaque, the color of old iron. You did not imagine the brightness. You did not imagine anything.

You begin to look for the gaps. They are everywhere — the pipes and platforms and machinery form a dense canopy overhead, but there are spaces between, channels of light that fall through in narrow columns. Where the light strikes, the surfaces are cleaner. The stone gleams. The water reflects. The rust on metal seems brighter, more alive, more orange than brown. In the dark places between the shafts, surfaces are duller, older, softened by time in a way the lit places are not.

The contrast is everywhere, and it is not quite right. Light should behave differently. Light should spread, should soften, should fill a space rather than marking it. Here, the light marks. It selects. It illuminates only what it touches and leaves the rest to a darkness that feels deliberate, that feels like it has been waiting to be noticed.

---

The tree is the oldest thing here. You knew that before you reached it, felt it in the way the space arranged itself around the knowledge, the way all the paths seemed to lead toward that single point of raised ground. The tree stands on a platform of old masonry — limestone, by the look of it, the kind of stone that remembers water, that holds it in its body, that weeps when the air changes. The platform is barely above the waterline, but it is enough. The tree has roots that grip the stone, that split the stone in places, that have grown into the cracks and pressed them open over decades until the whole platform is mapped with the tree's intention.

The tree itself is not large. It is not impressive in the way that trees can be impressive — it does not rise high, does not spread wide. It is the kind of tree that survives rather than flourishes, that puts its energy into roots and bark and the slow thickening of its trunk rather than into height or canopy. Its leaves are small, tough, dark green with a gray undertone that matches the sky. They do not move in the wind. They wait.

You stand beneath it. The branches reach out over the water, and the roots grip the stone, and the tree holds itself in this place with the same patient persistence as everything else here, the same refusal to be anything other than what it has become. The tree is old. The tree will be here when the machinery fails, when the water rises, when the last human presence has moved on or stopped coming. The tree will hold. The tree is holding.

And the water is moving beneath it, moving toward the place you cannot see, the place beneath the place, the deep pull that you felt with your hand in the water and cannot stop feeling now that your hand is dry. The tree's roots go down into the stone. The stone goes down into the mud. The mud goes down into something else, something that is not mud and not water but the source of both, the place where the water comes from and the place where it returns, the circulation that the machinery interrupts and the machinery cannot stop.

---

You are not alone in this place. You were not alone from the moment you entered, though you did not know it then. The others — those who came before, those who built, those who maintained, those who left — have left their traces in more than the rust and the brick and the worn stone. They have left something in the air. Something in the rhythm of the machinery. Something in the shape of the spaces and the way the light falls and the way the water gathers and the way the tree stands in its one place of solid ground.

You feel it when you stop moving. When you stand still and let the pulse of the machinery fill the air around you. When you listen to the rhythm and wait for the rhythm to resolve into something you can understand. The feeling is not presence. It is not absence. It is the feeling of a space that has been used and used and used until the using has become embedded in its surfaces, until the walls and the floors and the water have absorbed the memory of every hand that touched them and every foot that crossed them and every breath that was drawn in the damp air between the pipes.

The place remembers. The place holds.

And you, standing in it, feeling the cold of the stone through your boots and the weight of the damp on your clothes and the pulse of the old engines in the soles of your feet, you begin to understand that you are not visiting this place. You are not observing it from outside. You are part of it now. The water has touched you. The air has settled on you. The rhythm has entered your body and become your rhythm, and when you leave — if you leave — you will carry the pulse with you, and it will be a long time before you stop hearing it, before the low throb of the machinery fades from your blood and your breath and the slow dark of your dreams.

The place wants to be wetland. The place wants to hold what flows through it. And you, for a little while, are what flows through it now — warm and uncertain and alive with the energy of systems that should have failed long ago but haven't, because the failure is part of the system, because this is a place in its own settled state, and the state includes you now, includes your presence, your attention, your breath.

The water seeps. The stone weeps. The old engines turn.

You stay.
