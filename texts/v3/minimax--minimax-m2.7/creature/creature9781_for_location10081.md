What lives here lives slowly. This is the first thing to understand about the hollow's inhabitants—they have all the time they need, and they take it.

In the dark water of the largest pool, something waits. It has no name, or none that humans have ever given it. It is not fish, not eel, not the kind of thing that leaps or feeds in ways that would make sense to describe. It moves when the water moves, which is to say almost never, and only at night, when the light fails entirely. It is patient the way stone is patient, the way water is patient. It has been in that pool since the pool formed, and it will remain when the pool finally silts over and becomes something else entirely—wet moss, perhaps, or a depression filled with leaves. The other creatures of the hollow give it a wide berth without knowing why. They sense it the way the air senses the coming of weather: a pressure, a stillness that is different from mere stillness.

Around the seepage along the northern face, things breed and swarm in the way that wet stone breeds and swarms. Springsnails cluster in the thin channels where water runs brightest, their shells the color of old copper, no larger than a child's thumbnail. They move slowly, grazing the algae that grows in the thin film over the rock, and when the water pulses with a fresh surge, they hold fast and wait it out. Water pennies flatten themselves against submerged stone, their bodies wide and pale, catching nothing, needing nothing. Caddisfly larvae build their cases from fragments of the hollow's own stone, carrying their shelters with them as they graze, becoming in time small armored creatures that will emerge at dusk and become something winged and brief and then return to the water to begin again.

The ferns host their own world. Springs of small insects move through the fronds, lifting and setting down again in patterns too small to follow. Thrips, perhaps, or springtails, or things that have no common name—creatures that have been here so long in such isolation that they have become particulars of this place alone, adapted to this hollow's moisture and its particular quality of light. They do not travel far. There is no need. The hollow is enough.

In the moss that grows on every surface, things nest. A species of small salamander, unremarkable by day, spends its hours in the cool dark beneath the moss's outer skin. It is four-banded, a deep brown that would be invisible against the stone if it were ever still enough to be seen. It is never still for long. Its life is a series of small movements: emerging at dusk to hunt the insects that also emerge at dusk, retreating before dawn to its burrow of moss and damp. Its eggs, when it lays them, are no larger than a lentil, and it guards them in the way that salamanders guard them—not with any obvious vigilance, but by remaining near, by the simple fact of its presence. The young hatch translucent, legless at first, gaining their limbs slowly in the weeks that follow. They do not disperse far. The hollow raises its young in the hollow. It is a closed system, a room with a door that opens only one way.

Higher up, where the twisting trees take their shapes, there is the noise of small things. A nuthatch works its way down a trunk, probing the bark for insects and their larvae. A winter wren, impossibly small for the richness of its song, moves through the low branches near the hollow's edge, where the ferns begin to thin. They are not the hollow's creatures so much as its visitors—it is the moss and stone and water that are permanent—but they find what they need here, and so they return, and in returning they become part of the place's pattern.

A thrush comes at evening. It does not sing—the hollow's acoustics would swallow the song—but it calls, a thin sound that the darkness makes thinner. It is looking for the snails that cluster near the seepage, the ones with the copper shells. It carries them to a stone it has chosen, a stone worn smooth by centuries of carrying, and it breaks them open, each one, and feeds. The stone is called an anvil stone, though it does not know this. It has been an anvil for longer than birds have been watched. The broken shells accumulate in the moss beside it, small pale fragments, and the moss grows through them, reclaiming even this small violence.

What else? The air itself holds life that has no body, or bodies too small to see—spores and bacteria and the things that ride water droplets, the invisible swarm that makes the air feel thick when the light comes through the canopy. They are the hollow's circulation, its breath, its way of knowing itself. You inhale them without knowing. You exhale them changed.

And there is the cairn.

A hand has been here. One hand, perhaps, or many hands across time—the moss suggests that no one has stood at that stack for a long while, but the stones do not fall, and new stones are not added, and so it remains, held in its small equilibrium. Something about the cairn draws attention. Not birds—they do not care for stones stacked by creatures they do not know. Not the small things that live in moss and water. But something. The way the largest pool is drawn toward it in the geometry of the hollow. The way the ferns grow around it in spirals that are not quite regular, the same spirals that grow near the water, the spirals that are everywhere in the hollow once you learn to see them. As though the cairn is a center, a point the place has decided to hold, the way a hand holds a stone when there is nothing else to hold.

Perhaps the cairn is not evidence of presence so much as it is a presence now, a thing that the hollow has claimed and made its own. The one who stacked those stones has long since gone—back down the slopes, back to the lower country where time moves faster and things change visibly, measurably, in the span of a season. But the stones remain, and what remains is not the person but the act of stacking, the intention, the choice to make a mark. The hollow holds this. The hollow holds everything that has ever been held here—the slow erosion, the patient water, the lives that eat and are eaten in the moss and the dark. It holds the cairn the way it holds the water: without comment, without judgment, with only the patience that is the only truth the hollow knows.

There is something here that is not a creature and not a stone and not water but that is somehow all three. It has no edges. It has no shape. But it is here, in the air, in the silence that is never quite silence, in the way the light falls and the ferns turn and the water pulses through its hidden channels. It is the sense that you are being watched by something that does not have eyes. It is the sense that the hollow has noticed you. It is the sense that it will remember you when you are gone, and that you will not understand what that means, and that this is enough. This is what lives here: not just the small and patient things that make their homes in moss and water, but the hollow itself, awake in its own slow way, holding its contents like breath holds the shape of the lungs that made it.

You will leave. The light will fail. The bird will call its two notes and fall silent, and the silence will fill with drips and hums and the low sound of water doing what water does.

And the hollow will not miss you, because it does not miss. But it will have noticed. It will have held you for a time, and in that holding, you will have become part of its pattern, one more small thing that belongs to the place.

The moss grows. The stone weeps. The water seeps. Everything else is a way of saying the same thing: the hollow lives, in the way that all old things live, slowly, waiting, patient as erosion, patient as the slow turn of light across the wet green depths of the bowl of the world.
