What lives here does not have a name for itself.

---

The dominant forms are the mats. You will call them plants because language is the tool you have and plants are what they most resemble, but the word is a concession, a placeholder for something your taxonomy was never built to hold. They are the color of deep water, of verdigris, of the slow verdeath of copper on old pennies. They grow in layers so dense they seem geological — strata of organic matter that have compressed, over cycles beyond your counting, into something almost like soil. Almost. Press your fingers into them and they give, but not as soil gives. They grip your skin with ten thousand root-filaments, each one a thread of living tissue sunk into metal the way teeth sink into gum. You pull your hand free and the mat springs back, patient, unhurried, already healing the small wound you have made.

The mats do not grow toward light. There is no sun here to grow toward. They grow toward heat, toward moisture, toward the faint currents of air that still move through channels designed for other purposes. They grow along gradients. They are cartographers of the infrastructure beneath them, mapping the warm vents and the cold sinks, the water-tracks and the dry channels, in colors that shift as you move: darker where it is warmer, paler where it is not. You could read the machine's vital signs in the hue of its skin, if you spent long enough learning the language.

---

Among the mats, the spiral-forms grow. They are the ones you notice first, the ones that pull your attention sideways from what you meant to be looking at. They emerge from the carpet in clusters, unwinding upward in helixes that might be tendrils or might be fronds or might be something else entirely. They are the color of rust, the color of old blood, the color of the creatures that move among them. When you pass close, they bend away from you — not quickly, not the way a sensitive plant recoils, but slowly, over seconds, the way a sleeping animal shifts its weight. Their surfaces are faintly warm. Their interiors, when you look closely, pulse with a dim bioluminescence that has no rhythm you can discern: a slow heartbeat, a slow breath, a slow something that is not sleep and not waking and not anything that happens outside this place.

You do not touch them. You learn not to.

---

The creatures are harder to see than the plants, and harder to catch, and when you do see them they are already still. They have adapted to this place the way all things here have adapted: by becoming part of the pattern. The rust-colored ones — there are several species, or what you are calling species out of habit — are the most common. They have too many legs, arranged in pairs that seem redundant, and their bodies are flat in ways that suggest either predation or concealment. They move in bursts: stop, orient, move, stop, orient, move. When you look at them directly, they do not run. They wait. They are the color of the mats, the color of the copper veins, the color of oxidation and age and the slow eating-away of metal by patient acid. If they were afraid of you, you would not see them at all.

There are others. The fish-things, in the water-tracks that run along the lower channels: eyeless, pale, flattened sideways like coins, moving in schools of three or four before scattering into the dark. The large ones — you have seen only two, and each only once — are the size of dogs, or dogs are the size of them, and they move in the upper reaches where the mist is thick, where you cannot see what they are doing or where they go. They may be apex predators. They may be something else. They did not look at you either time. They moved as if you were part of the scenery, as if you were another growth on the walls, as if you were already becoming what this place makes of everything it touches.

---

There is a sound the mats make, sometimes, when the rain falls. Not a rustling — something lower, something that travels through the growth-strata and into the walls and back out again. A hum. A tone. It rises and falls with the rhythm of the water, but it is not an echo, not a consequence. It seems to precede the rain, as if the mats are calling it, as if the moisture comes because the mats have asked for it. You do not know if this is true. You have no way to know. But you listen for it now, when you walk beneath the dripping surfaces, and when the hum comes, you feel something that might be reverence or might be unease, and you cannot tell the difference, and you suspect there is no difference here.

---

The bacteria are the smallest things, and perhaps the most important. They are in everything: in the mats, in the creatures, in the walls themselves, in the water that falls and the water that runs and the water that pools in the low places where the channels have sagged. They eat metal. They eat each other. They build structures — the copper veins, the calcite formations, the strange mineral flowers that emerge from joints where water has pooled and dried and pooled again over millennia. They are the bridge between the manufactured and the organic, the translators who have taught the machine to speak the language of flesh. Without them, the mats would have nothing to grip. Without them, the walls would be bare and hostile and the creatures would have no colors to hide behind. They are the base of a food pyramid that has no apex, a cycle that has no end, a system that runs on the slow consumption of everything it touches, including itself.

---

What lives here does not know it is alive. The mats do not know they are mats. The creatures do not know they are creatures. The bacteria do not know they are bacteria. They do not name themselves. They do not mourn themselves. They do not look up at the mist and wonder what is beyond it. They grow, and they move, and they consume, and they are consumed, and the machinery turns beneath them like a heart that does not know it is a heart, and the rain falls, and the warmth remains, and the whole of it is one thing, one breathing, one slow and ancient and total thing that has never needed a word for what it is.

---

And yet.

You feel it watching.

Not the creatures — though they watch too, in their strange unblinking way — but something larger, something in the pattern of the light and the angle of the rain and the way the copper veins trace letters you cannot quite read across the curved walls. It is not malevolent. Malevolence would require a self to feel it. It is more like attention. Like recognition. Like the slow turning of a gaze that has been looking at you since you arrived and will go on looking after you leave. You are a new thing here, a strange shape in the pattern, and the pattern is curious about you, the way a river is curious about a stone — not thinking, not planning, just eroding, just wearing you smooth, just making you part of itself one molecule at a time.

You are inside something that was never meant to be a world. And it is deciding what to do with you.
