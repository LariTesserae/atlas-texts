The first thing you learn is that the plants are not all the same.

This seems like it should be obvious, but the density makes it difficult at first—the way they crowd together, the way their geometries interlock and overlap, the way the light moves through the particulate and renders color unreliable. You stand at the basin's edge and see a wall of silver and violet and that dark red-black, and the wall seems uniform until you stop moving. Until you stay still long enough that your eyes stop trying to resolve it as a single thing.

Then you begin to see the edges.

There is the one that rises closest to where you stand—a spiral thing, yes, the kind you were told to expect, two stories tall with branches that catch the diffused brightness and throw it sideways in brief flares of iridescence. It is not the tallest. Others rise past it, taller stalks with canopies that spread in horizontal planes, flat plates of crystalline material that angle upward like open palms. The silver ones tend toward height, or it may be that height tends toward silver—the relationship is not clear. They grow from the joints where the hexagonal plates meet, anchoring down through the glass by the simple pressure of their existence, their lattice-roots fusing with the lava-glass until the boundary between organism and substrate becomes a matter of theory rather than observation.

Between them, the violets.

These are the ones that sprawl. They do not rise so much as extend, their forms low and broad, spreading across the glass like dark water caught mid-puddle. Their surfaces are matte—true matte, a softness that does not seem possible given the crystalline nature of their cells—and from certain angles they absorb light the way the silver ones reflect it, becoming voids in the bright wash of the basin's perpetual illumination. They are dense in the way all of them are dense, but their density is horizontal, a compression into the ground rather than an reaching toward the sky. You step over them carefully; they are not soft, and their edges are not forgiving.

And then there is the red-black, which is not quite red and not quite black but something between—a third thing, a color that does not have a name because your eyes have never had to name it. These ones grow in clusters, spiraling around central points that seem to pull the gaze, that seem to pull the space itself. You cannot look at them for long. This is not unpleasant. It is simply that the eye slides away, finds something else to settle on, and when you look back they have not moved and you have not changed and the relationship between you is simply that you exist in the same basin, breathing the same ozone-and-metal air, and that is enough.

The clusters matter. That is what takes longest to understand.

You walk among them and they seem separate, individual organisms each pursuing its own growth, each drinking light and metabolizing the particulate and exhaling something that glitters faintly when the light catches it right. But the clusters are not separate. The spirals that seem to revolve around central points—the violet sprawl that touches the silver height—the way the glass itself seems to participate in the growth, the lattices of crystal and lava-glass becoming indistinguishable at the boundaries. These are not individual organisms. They are, perhaps, a single thought distributed across miles of basin floor. Or they are a colony in the oldest sense, a multiple that has never known the concept of singular. Or they are something else again, a relationship rather than an entity, a pattern that has learned to persist.

The hum is real.

You cannot hear it clearly—the particulate does something to sound, diffusing it the way it diffuses light, making everything seem to come from everywhere—but it is there. A low vibration that rises through the glass, through the soles of your feet, into the bones of your legs. It is not mechanical. It does not have the rhythm of gears or the pulse of reactors. It is more like the sound of a voice speaking in a register below hearing, a voice that has been speaking for so long it has forgotten it was ever anything other than sound. The plants are not thinking, not in any way you would recognize as thought. But they are not not-thinking either. They are in a state that has no human word: an ongoing presence, a held awareness, a patience that has outlasted the need for patience by geologic ages.

The fauna is rarer still.

You see it only once, near the third day—or what you have decided to call the third day, though the light does not change and the warmth does not vary and time here is a courtesy rather than a measurement. It moves between the stalks with that deliberate patience you were warned about, neither hurrying nor hesitating, and its form is difficult to hold in memory. Later you will try to describe it and find that you cannot: it was not animal, not plant, not the symbiotic partner some have theorized. It was a shape that occupied space without disturbing it, a movement that left no trace, a passage that the silence simply closed behind. It did not look at you. It did not not-look at you. It moved on, and the plants shifted slightly in its wake—not their leaves or their branches, but something subtler, some internal adjustment, as if the distributed thought had noted its passage and filed it away among the things that do not require response.

The space is wrong, and the wrongness is permanent, and the life here has made its peace with it.

You walk toward the ridge and arrive somewhere else. You count the steps to the spiral thing and the number changes. You stand in its shadow and feel the light on your skin, and both are true, and neither contradicts the other, and the plants go on drinking light, going on being dense, going on in their patient hum, and the glass holds, and the basin holds, and the wrongness holds, and you are part of it now, a small warmth moving through a world that has no name because no one who has seen it remains in any state to speak one.

But you are still here.

That is something. That is, perhaps, everything.
