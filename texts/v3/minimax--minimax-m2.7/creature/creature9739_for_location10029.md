The Rootwork breathes. Not metaphorically—the root-mats expand and contract in slow rhythms, flexing against their mineral substrate like a lung filling and emptying on a timescale you cannot match. You feel it more than see it: a faint give underfoot, a tightening of the space around you. The walls are not static. They are always becoming.

And they are not alone.

The root-mats themselves are the first life, the primary life, the architecture that everything else inhabits. They are not passive structure but active tissue—warm where you touch them, faintly yielding, threaded through with vessels that carry nothing now but have not yet stopped carrying the memory of transport. When you press your palm flat against a wall, you feel something respond: a slow pulse, a slight relaxation of tension, as though the plant has noticed you and is deciding what to make of your presence. The root-mats are aware. Not in the way an animal is aware, not with nerves and intention, but in the patient way of roots everywhere, feeling for water that is not there, for stone to digest, for the chemical signals of their own decay. They feel you because you are warm and alive and made of water, and for a moment something in the tissue adjusts—exudes a faint acid, tests your skin for something to take—and then withdraws, finding you uninteresting. You are not stone. You are not dead. You are nothing the Rootwork can use.

Between the mats, in the creases and gaps where growth has not yet sealed shut, you find the first animals.

They are small. They have to be small—this is a place of narrow margins, where even the trace moisture in the air is claimed before it can fall. The pale arthropods move along the seams between root-mats, their bodies translucent enough that you can see the faint grain of their internal structure, like woodchips suspended in ice. They are the size of your smallest fingernail, with too many legs that move in rippling waves as they navigate the textured surfaces of their living world. They do not cluster or swarm; each one moves alone, following trails you cannot see, pausing to tap the root-surface with antennae that seem to taste something. They are eating, you realize—the root-mats exude a faint film of sugars and acids as they grow, and the arthropods harvest this, not damaging the tissue but skimming its surface like dew from a leaf. They are cleansers. They are the first level of the Rootwork's internal economy, turning the plant's own waste into bodies, bodies that will themselves become waste, become substrate, become part of the next growth.

Deeper in the seams, where the gaps widen into something that might be a niche or might be a wound, you find the nematodes.

They are harder to see, easier to feel—a writhing in the sediment that has accumulated in the low places, a mineral dust made of the stone the root-mats have not yet consumed. The nematodes are threadlike, pale as the arthropods but more so, nearly invisible against the pale ground. They move in tight spirals, feeding on the microbial films that have established themselves in this accidental soil—the bacteria and fungi that have made a life in the Rootwork's trace moisture, forming a micro-ecosystem of their own. The nematodes are abundant here, a tangled mass of thousands in a space the size of your cupped hand. They are food for something larger. They are the second level.

And the third level is the slug-like things, though calling them slugs does them a disservice—they are longer and more deliberate, their bodies segmented into plates that catch the amber light and hold it for a moment before releasing. They graze the bioluminescent nodules, those knots of glow embedded in the root-walls, and they do not merely eat them. They take the light into themselves. Their bodies become luminous as they feed, pulsing faintly with the stolen glow, and they carry this light with them as they move—leaving trails of phosphorescence on the walls they cross, chemical signatures that might be communication or might be simply the residue of passage. You watch one for a long time. It moves so slowly that you lose track of it, look away, and find it three chambers later still moving toward something you cannot name. It does not hurry. There is nothing here that hurries.

The phosphorescent trails they leave are the closest thing to language in the Rootwork. They accumulate on the walls over time, layers of faint glow marking the paths that have been traveled most often, the routes between resources—the nodule clusters, the moisture traps, the places where the root-mats have grown thin enough for the surface-light to seep through. The trails cross and overlap, some fresh and bright, others fading toward gray. The slug-like things read them, you suspect. They taste them with their whole bodies, following paths their ancestors laid down in sugars and light.

There are larger things. You do not see them. You feel them.

The floor shivers sometimes—not from your steps, not from the settling of growth, but from something deeper, something moving through the mineral substrate beneath the root-mats, through the soft chalk that the plants have not yet finished digesting. The vibration is slow and heavy, a pulse that travels through the stone and into the soles of your feet. It comes and goes without pattern. When it comes, you stop. You wait. The ambient sounds of the Rootwork—the hum, the creak, the skitter of arthropods—go quiet for a moment, and then resume, and the thing that caused the shiver is gone, moved on to some other passage, some other depth. You never see it. You begin to wonder if it exists at all, or if the Rootwork is simply dreaming it, expressing some vast and restless impulse in the only language it has.

But you find evidence. In the deeper chambers, where the architecture narrows and the ceiling presses low, you find walls that are not quite walls—surfaces where the root-mat has grown thick and strange, bulging outward in ways that suggest pressure from within. And embedded in these bulging surfaces, you find the remains of things that were once alive: bones, not root-bones, not plant-bones, but animal-bones, mammal-bones, bleached pale and fused to the tissue until they are indistinguishable from it. The root-mats have grown around them. Incorporated them. They are not decay; they are architecture now, load-bearing elements in the structure, strengthened by calcium that was once blood and marrow. Something larger than the arthropods, larger than the slugs, lived here once. Perhaps it still does, somewhere in the depths, its body half-digested and half-grown into the walls it once moved through.

You find the fungal networks in the oldest places—chambers where the root-mats have thinned enough to let the mineral underneath show through, chalky and soft and covered in a pale fuzz of mycelium. The fungi have done the early work here, digesting the stone into something the plants can use, preparing the substrate that will one day be root-floor and root-wall. They pulse faintly with bioluminescence of their own, a colder light than the nodules, bluer, more diffuse. The air smells different here—not the dry mineral scent of the main passages, but something organic, something that speaks of rot and renewal. The fungi are not separate from the Rootwork. They are part of it, a different expression of the same impulse, the same patient chemistry. They are the mouth and the root-mats are the body, and together they are making more of themselves, consuming the world they live in so that they can become it.

In the quietest moments, you can hear the root-mats drinking.

It is not a sound you would recognize if you did not know what to listen for—a faint susurrus, a whisper of movement in the vessels of the tissue, as though something is flowing even though nothing is flowing. The plants have no water to move, but they have not stopped moving. They have found new currents to ride, new chemistries to pursue. The sound is the sound of growth, of acids exuded and minerals absorbed, of cells dividing in the dark. It is the sound of a world being made.

You are standing in the stomach of something vast and still-growing, and the something vast is not alone. It is populated, inhabited, colonized by creatures that have adapted to its rhythms and its dryness, that drink from its sugar-chains and graze its surfaces and make their homes in the spaces between its fibers. They do not fear it. They have never known anything else. The Rootwork is their world, and you are the strange warm thing that has wandered in from outside, carrying water you do not know how to give and heat you do not know how to share.

When you leave—if you leave—you will carry nothing with you but the memory of the amber light and the slow pulse of growth. But something will carry a memory of you. The root-mat you touched will hold the shape of your palm for a little while, a depression in the tissue that will close slowly, patient as everything here, and then be gone. And the creatures that move through the Rootwork will continue their small lives, their phosphorescent trails and their skittering passages, their slow chemical exchanges, their patient hunger for stone. The Rootwork will grow. It will digest the world above it, the dry fields and the farmers who wait for rain, and it will become them too, incorporate them into its architecture, make them part of the slow breath of its root-mats and the faint hum of its abandoned power.

You are not the first thing to walk through here, and you will not be the last. But you are the first thing that is made of water, and you are the first thing that will leave. The Rootwork will remember you the way it remembers everything: slowly, chemically, in the language of growth and decay, until you become part of the amber light and the phosphorescent trails and the slow susurrus of a world that does not need water, that has learned to make its own kind of life from the bones of the earth.
