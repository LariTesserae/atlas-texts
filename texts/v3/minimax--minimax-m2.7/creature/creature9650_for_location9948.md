# The Inhabitants

There is a hierarchy here, though no one has imposed it. At the lowest stratum, invisible to any but the closest attention, the organisms are most dense. The biofilm covers every submerged surface — a skin of microorganisms so thin it is more felt than seen, grayish and faintly iridescent where the light catches it. It gives the water its peculiar slickness, its resistance to disturbance. The water does not ripple easily. The water remembers itself.

Above the biofilm, in the water itself, there are creatures so small they belong to no familiar category. Rotifers extend their coronae in the thin film between water and air. nematodes writhe through the silt. Tiny crustaceans — pale, eyeless, no larger than commas — drift in the mid-depths, their bodies translucent enough that they seem less like animals than like errors in the water, like the water has developed opinions about itself. They are the base of a food chain that has no top, a circuit of matter and energy that begins in the mineral and ends, for the moment, in the beetles.

The beetles are the visible class. They do not swarm; they persist. Each one is a small catastrophe of form — boxy, armored, equipped with mouthparts that suggest patience more than violence. They move with the deliberation of things that have escaped predation so long ago they have forgotten the shape of fear. Here and there, a beetle will pause on a flagstone's edge, its antennae testing the air (or what passes for air here — humid, mineral-scented, thick with the exhalations of other lives), and then continue on its slow errand. They mate in the corners, where the mist settles thickest. The eggs are laid in the silt of the joints, and the larvae, when they emerge, are pale and legless and feed on the stone itself, on the ancient mortar, on the waste of centuries. The stone accepts them. The stone has time.

Between the beetles and the larvae, between the rotifers and the midges, there is a constant circulation. The flies — small, dark, unremarkable — breed in the water's skin and emerge to hang in clouds above the deepest pools. They do not bite. They do not need to. They are already where everything they require is present: the moisture, the decay, the warmth of the living things below. They are food for the things that eat, and for the things that wait to eat, and for the things that are not quite either.

The clicking comes from *bothydrilus pumilus*, though no one who hears it would use that name. It is a species of water-beetle — not the large armored ones that walk the stones, but a smaller kind, dwelling in the silt, whose larvae are the source of the sound. A scraping, a clicking, a wet percussion that rises from the floor in irregular rhythms. Not synchronized. Not patterned. The larvae are feeding, perhaps, or signaling to one another in the language of their kind, a language that requires no meaning beyond the fact of its own existence. The clicks layer over one another as the evening deepens, as the light fails, as the temperature drops and the water accepts its own chill more readily. The sound is not loud. But it fills the space the way a voice fills a room when there is nothing else to listen to.

And then, when the clicking has reached its densest — when the cistern is at its fullest of sound — the thing that is not a frog begins to call.

It is *Pseudobranchus something*, a salamander that has no business here. Its ancestors were not brought in buckets; it did not arrive in the soil of transplanted plants. It came the way all things come to places they do not belong: slowly, by degrees, through cracks and channels and the slow migration of dampness itself. It is neotenic — it has retained its larval gills, feathery and red, so that it can breathe the water it lives in, so that it has never needed to leave. It is blind. Its eyes are covered by skin, useless organs in a darkness that offers nothing to see. It is pale — almost translucent, like the crustaceans, like the rotifers, like the biofilm itself. It is the color of the water's intention.

It calls.

The sound is a wet click, a resonant thump, a vibration that passes through the water and into the stone and out into the air where the beetles pause and the flies tighten their spirals and the thing that waits in the northwest corner — the figure carved from the same stone that holds the salamander's pool — receives the message without hearing it. The salamander does not need ears to make this sound. It is a compression of body against body, a contraction of the throat, a forcing of air (or what passes for air in its water-lung) through a membrane that vibrates at a frequency meant for others of its kind. But there are no others. The cistern holds one, and one is enough. It calls into the stone and the stone carries it. The sound bounces from arch to arch, from wall to wall, a single voice asking a question that has no answer because the answer is the question, because the calling is itself a kind of living.

Something answers.

It is not another salamander. It is not the beetles. It is not the flies or the midges or the rotifers or the biofilm. It is a thing that lives in the joints, in the spaces where the mortar has receded, a thing that moves slowly and breeds slowly and has no name because no one has looked closely enough. It is segmented, like the beetles, but flatter, and its legs are fewer — six, perhaps, or eight, or a number that changes as it moves. It is pale, like the salamander, because darkness does not require color. It makes a sound that is not a click, not a thump, but something between — a wet exhale, a rasping exhalation, a breath that has been held so long it emerges as something almost like language. It answers the salamander in a voice the salamander cannot hear, because the salamander has no ears, because hearing is not the point. The point is the answering. The point is that the cistern is full of voices, and each voice is answered by some other voice, and the conversation has been going on so long that no one remembers what was said first.

The figure in the northwest corner does not call. It does not click or rasp or breathe. It is stone, and stone is silent. But water gathers in its eye-hollows, and the water has its own voice — a slow drip, a rhythm as patient as the beetles, a sound that marks time in increments too small for any life that lives and dies within them. The figure was carved to be looked at, and no one has looked in so long that the looking has become irrelevant. What matters is the water. What matters is the way the figure holds the water, accepts it, lets it gather and fall and gather again. The figure is a basin. The figure is a mouth. The figure is a question that the cistern answers every evening when the light fails and the voices rise and the water moves in its channels that bend against sense.

The water does not drain.

The water was never meant to drain.

The builders carved the channel with tools, with patience, with the understanding that water flows downhill. But they built in a place where downhill is a matter of perspective, where the stone holds the water the way the air holds the mist, where the cistern has become what it was always going to become — a space that does not resist what enters it, that does not mourn what leaves, that accommodates the slow accumulation of life and water and time until the accommodation itself becomes the point. The channel rises. The water follows. The water does not fight the channel, and the channel does not fight the stone, and the stone does not fight the hill that presses down on it, and the hill — the hill has forgotten that there was ever a before.

In the evenings, when the light from the western shaft has thinned to nothing, the cistern enters its other state. The darkness is not complete — there is always a faint luminosity to the water, a phosphorescence that might be the biofilm or might be the creatures that live in it or might be nothing at all, a property of the water itself, a secret the stone has kept for so long it has forgotten the keeping. In this light, the beetles are blacker. The mist is denser. The sound is a pressure, a weight, a presence that occupies the space the way the water occupies the floor.

And the figure — the figure is still there, watching or being watched, holding the water that gathers in its eyes, waiting or not waiting for whatever comes next.

The water moves.

The beetles shift.

The salamander calls, and the jointed thing answers, and the figure waits.
