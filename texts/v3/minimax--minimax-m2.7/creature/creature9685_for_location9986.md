Among the things that live here, there is a vine that blooms only once, then carries its flower underground for a century before rising again with the bloom held at the center of its new growth, as if remembering what memory tastes like. It is not rare—it is everywhere, threaded through the other growths like a red thread—but its blooming is never witnessed, only the evidence of bloom: the scar where it held, the pale ring of once-petal on root-bark. You learn not to look for the moment. You learn to find the traces.

The fungus does not have a name because naming requires distance, and the fungus is too close to everything. It runs beneath the mat like a second nervous system, and when the pulse in the root-floor beats, it is the fungus that translates—slower beats meaning rain, faster meaning the passage of something large through the upper stories, the kind of large that does not exist anymore outside this place but once did, or perhaps never did, only dreamed itself into shape here where the dreaming could last. The fungus knows the difference. The fungus has opinions about the large things.

There are animals, or the memory of animals, or things that remember being animals before they learned to photosynthesize or parasitize or simply wait. They hang in the upper reaches like ornaments on a tree so vast it has forgotten it is a tree, their bodies motionless for days at a time, their eyes open and seeing nothing, their hearts slowing to matches of the pulse below. When they move, it is sudden and purposeful—a lunge, a stillness, a settling—and the silence they leave behind is not absence but completion, the way silence after a bell is the bell's final note. They eat what falls. They do not fall. They are always here.

In the pools that collect in the cupped leaves—the traps, the learning-traps, the cups that existed before cupping—there lives something that is not quite water and not quite body. It moves against the surface tension in ways that suggest intention without suggesting mind. It might be a single organism spanning all the pools, threaded through the grove in liquid continuity, drinking the condensation, dreaming the hum. It might be the hum's source, or its destination, or the space between, where the note lives when it is not being sung.

The trees—though tree is the wrong word, and everyone who has descended agrees that the wrong word is correct, that calling them trees is a way of admitting defeat—are neither alive nor dead. They are ongoing. They continue. Their bark is not bark but accumulated growth, centuries of the same cells hardening into architecture, and at their centers something still reaches, still believes, still pulls mineral-water up from the fungal network and exhales it as oxygen that the animals breathe and the fungus processes and the vine cycles and the pulse keeps time to. They do not grow in the way growing is understood. They become more themselves.

There is a sound, beneath the hum, that only some visitors hear—a sound like a child's voice, or like speech without language, the kind of sound that communicates before communication was invented. The visitors who hear it stop. They stand very still. They do not report what they heard, or if they report it, they report it wrong, and the grove seems to prefer it that way. The sound is not speech. The sound is the grove practicing, just in case, the shape that would be needed if it ever needed to call something home.
