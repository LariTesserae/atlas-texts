# What Lives Here

---

Begin with the beetles. They have arranged themselves according to a logic you will not decode. At the western edge of the flagstones, where the light fails earliest, four of them move in a line so precise it seems choreographed — each one maintaining the same distance from the one ahead, their legs synchronized, their ridged shells catching what remains of the amber glow with a faint iridescence that appears and vanishes depending on the angle of your attention. They carry nothing. They are not foraging. They are, for reasons that belong only to beetles, moving from one shadow to another, and the only thing that marks their passage is a slight shifting of the dust.

Now the ants: they have been moving all day, but at this hour they accelerate. The files that crossed the roots earlier have redistributed. Some now travel along the grooves cut by ancient runoff, finding in those worn channels a kind of highway, their bodies flowing through the stone's old wounds like blood through capillaries. Others climb. The vines carry colonies upward, and if you watch long enough you will see them disappear into the canopy where the leaves thicken and the moisture collects — not the moisture that comes at night, but a residual dampness that the broad leaves hold and slowly release, so that the upper reaches of the courtyard are always slightly wetter than the ground, always slightly greener, always producing a faint and steady drip that falls onto the stone in a rhythm too irregular to be called a rhythm but regular enough to be felt as such, a presence in the background of everything.

The lizards have their own hours. In the first hours of light — a light that arrives slowly here, filtered and softened by the walls until it is less illumination than suggestion — the lizards are everywhere. They warm themselves on the upper vine-stems where the sun catches in slants through gaps in the stonework above, and their bodies flatten into stillness, and their eyes, fixed on the movement below, calculate distances with a stillness that is not patience but something more mechanical, more certain. They do not blink as often as you would expect. When they move, it is sudden and absolute: a dart, a snap, a return to stillness, and then a bulge traveling down the lizard's throat as the catch is swallowed. After feeding, they descend to the lower walls and find their cracks, and for a time they are inactive, their bodies processing what they have taken, and you will see only the tips of their tails protruding from the stone, slightly twitching.

The birds are harder. They exist mostly as sound and motion — brief flares of flight between the leaf-clusters, small bursts of color that resolve into the green dark before you can fully register what you have seen. But there is one, or perhaps several that share a habit, that comes to the courtyard in the hour before true dark, when the amber light has thickened to something almost amber-gold. This bird — dark-backed, pale-breasted, smaller than your fist — settles on a particular vine that has grown across the eastern wall in a particular way, a natural perch formed by a fork in the stem, and from there it sings. Not the sharp exchange-chatter of the canopy birds, but a single sustained phrase, repeated, repeated, repeated: three notes rising, two falling, a pause, then the same again. The song has no other purpose. The bird is not calling for a mate or warning away a rival. The bird is singing in the last light of the day, in a space that holds the whole world at the size of a room, and the song is the size of that space, and it fills the space entirely.

---

There is a pattern to the small pale flowers. They open at dusk — you have already noted this — but you have not noted that they open in a specific order. The flowers nearest the walls open first, then those in the middle, then those at the edges. The progression takes perhaps an hour, and by the time the last flowers have opened, the first are already beginning to close. By true dark, all are sealed again, their petals folded inward like hands losing their grip on something they cannot hold. The pollinators — small moths, it must be, though you have not seen them — come in the window between opening and closing, when the flowers are at their fullest and their scent is strongest. The scent is faint: green, slightly sweet, with an undertone of something mineral that connects it to the stone, the moisture, the long history of this enclosed air.

The shrubs themselves have been pruned by time, though not by human hands. Their branches have grown and died and fallen and grown again in the same shapes so many times that the natural tendency toward openness has been overwritten by a kind of dense habitual growth. They reach for each other across the narrow space. Where two branches meet, they fuse — the bark grows together, the wood beneath merges, and what was once two separate organisms becomes a single continuous form. The grafts are visible as slight thickenings, as places where the bark is slightly ridged, as knots that tell a story not in language but in growth. The oldest shrubs may have been growing here when the settlement was still occupied. They would have been smaller then, kept in check by harvesting, by browsing, by the attention of people. Now they have taken the space that was cleared for them, and they are only growing, and they will continue until the courtyard can no longer contain them, and then something will give.

---

At night, the courtyard empties of most of its visible life but fills with sound. The insects continue. The moisture arrives. The lizards sleep in their cracks, their bodies cool and still. The birds are absent — you do not know where they go, but they go somewhere, and the canopy is quieter for their absence. The ants do not stop. You can hear them moving in the stone, in the roots, in the walls: a sound so faint it is less heard than felt, a vibration beneath the vibration of the air. And there is another sound, one that is harder to isolate: a slow, deep creak that might be the vines adjusting to the weight of the moisture they have taken in, or the stone contracting as it cools, or something else — a settling that has been going on for centuries, that will continue for centuries more. The courtyard breathes. Not metaphorically: the walls expand and contract by fractions of a millimeter, and the air inside moves in patterns too subtle to see but present, always present, a circulation that brings the moisture to the roots and the roots to the stone and the stone to the light, and the light back to moisture, and so on, in a cycle that is also a kind of patience.

---

In the morning, if you return, you will find new things. A beetle has died at the base of the eastern wall, and something is already working at its shell — a small pale larva, barely visible, entering the body through a joint in the armor. A seedling has emerged overnight: a single pale-green shoot, no thicker than a thread, rising from a crack in the stone between two of the shrubs. It will not survive — the crack is too shallow, the moisture too inconsistent — but it will try. That is what life does here: it tries, it persists, it fails, it tries again, and the trying is not separate from success but is instead the thing that makes success possible, that makes the courtyard a place where the world continues in the only way the world knows how: small, and enclosed, and growing.
