What lives here is patient and still and very, very small.

You would miss them if you looked. Your eyes move over the rock face and register green and grey and brown, the general idea of growth, and your mind calls it moss, calls it lichen, calls it fern, and moves on. But down here, in the damp and the dark, each of those words is a country. Each of them is a world running on a timescale you cannot feel.

The lichen is the oldest thing here. Not in years—the rock is older—but in kind. Lichen does not grow. Lichen arrives and persists. A single patch on this basalt may be a hundred years old, a thin grey-green crust holding the stone together against the spray, and it will be here still when the gorge has widened another meter and the ferns have moved up the walls and you are bones somewhere else entirely. Lichen is time made visible. Lichen is a relationship between fungus and algae that has been negotiating itself for longer than anything else in this place has existed, and it is winning. You can see where it has claimed the rock: the surface dulled, softened, the sharp fracture edges first darkened by moisture and then slowly, slowly rounded by the slow acid of its presence. The rock is being digested. Not by weather. By life.

The moss is younger and hungrier. It grows in low cushions that hold water like sponges, and when you touch it your fingers come away wet even if the rain has stopped. Underneath the green visible part there is a dense mat of rhizoids, threads finer than hair, gripping the stone, pulling minerals from the rock and water from the air and making something out of nothing. The moss does this in the dark. The moss does this with no sun. It has learned to live on diffuse light, on the grey seam of sky far above, on the faint glow that is not quite brightness, and it has made that enough. In the places where water runs thin and steady down the rock face, moss forms a green border, a river channel in miniature, growing where the water goes, receding where it dries.

The ferns are the most recent immigrants. Their ancestors landed here as spores so small you would need a microscope to see them, traveling on wind or on birds or on the feet of animals that no longer come this deep into the gorge. Each frond began as a single cell that split and split and split, and now the frond uncurls in the darkness, and it is already thinking about spores of its own, already preparing the next generation, already ready to claim more of the rock. The ferns grow in the crevices where water collects and soil has built up over years—soil that is really just broken stone and organic matter, a thin dark layer that the roots find and hold. In the deepest part of the gorge, where the spray is constant and the light is lowest, the ferns grow thick and tall, shoulder-high in places, and when the wind moves through them the sound is different from the water: softer, almost alive, a rustling that sounds like breathing.

Beneath all of this, in the thin layer of sediment that gathers in the cracks and pools, there is life you cannot see. Bacteria in the biofilm. Rotifers in the slow-water pools. Tardigrades, if you knew where to look, those eight-legged micro-animals curled in the moss cushions, waiting. They are the ones who have adapted most completely to this place. They can dry out completely and come back. They can freeze solid and survive. They are what life looks like when it decides to live in the hardest possible place and refuses to leave.

The water itself is not alive, but it is where life lives. Every drop that falls through this space carries something: a spore, an egg, a cell, a seed. The water is the delivery system. The water is the source and the medium and the argument. It brings life in and it takes life out and it makes the conditions that allow the slow, patient life on the walls to exist at all.

There is no animal life here. You understand this. No birds will nest in the roots that hang down from above. No fish will rise in the pools where the water calms. The gorge is too new, too raw, too loud. The animals are waiting. They are in the forest above, in the meadow beyond, in the place where the gorge opens out into something wider. They are waiting for this place to become what it will become. They are waiting for the ferns to thicken and the moss to build soil deep enough for roots, waiting for the rock to finish cracking and the water to calm, waiting for a home.

But for now, the moss holds on. The lichen digests the stone. The ferns uncurl. The tardigrades sleep. And the water roars through the new-cut channel, carrying everything it touches toward something that has not happened yet, something that is still being decided by the slow argument between gravity and rock.
