There is water here that has learned slowness. Not the water of rivers or springs, which move with purpose toward the sea. This water seeps and pools and evaporates in its season, and in the spaces between seasons it simply waits. It carries the minerals of the brick in its body, makes itself white with what it has dissolved, and leaves its signature on every surface it touches—the rings, the stains, the slow widening of channels where it has passed a thousand times. The water does not know it is old. It knows only the path that has been worn for it, the path that wears it in return, the exchange that is all there is. Where it pools in the catch basins, it is still enough to be a mirror for the brick above, and if you stood long enough to see yourself reflected in that black circle, you might wonder what the water sees when it looks up through the stone at the place where you are standing. It sees what it always sees: the outline of a throat, the curve of a mouth, the shape of something that holds but does not swallow.

The moss lives here. It has always lived here, or perhaps it has been living here so long that the question of beginning has lost its meaning. The oldest moss is nearest the water, where the channels run, and it has become something you would not recognize if you found it in a garden or a forest—it is darker than moss should be, the deep olive of old copper, and it holds moisture the way a body holds grief, close and heavy. Where it is newer, at the edges of its territories, it is pale, almost luminous, the green of new growth that does not yet know what it will become. Between these two states—the oldest and the youngest, the dark and the pale—there is a gradient that is a record, a calendar written in the language of chlorophyll and calcium. You could read it if you knew how. You could count the years by the shifting of the green from light to dark, the slow advance of the moss into territories it will hold forever once it has claimed them. It does not spread the way plants above the surface spread, with ambition and season, with the urgency of frost coming. It spreads the way stone weathers, the way light moves across a wall, the way a room learns to be a room. Slowly. Without complaint. Without the knowledge that there is any other way to be.

The ferns do not grow as ferns grow elsewhere. They are not the delicate, feathery things of woodland floors, reaching toward patches of sun through the canopy. These are the children of darkness, and they have made their peace with it. Their fronds are dark—almost black, dark as the brick they sprout from, dark as the water that feeds them—and they reach toward the faint light with a patience that is not quite hope. They are reaching, but they do not expect to arrive. They have found a way to live on almost nothing, to grow on the smallest portion of what the water brings them, to unfurl their leaves in a darkness that has no end. When you see them—you will see them, in the cracks and pockets where water collects, in the mortar that has receded enough to hold them—you might think they are struggling. You might think they are reaching for something they have lost. But they have not lost anything. They have only found what their seeds have always known: a life in the low light, a place where the sun does not reach and the damp never ends and the brick weeps steadily, steadily, forever, and there is room enough for roots that ask for nothing more than the privilege of staying.

The algae—or the liverworts, or whatever the slick things are that carpet the channels near the pump—these are the pulse of the place, if the place has a pulse. You will see them when the light catches them right, when the sodium glow from the exit sign finds them and makes them briefly luminous, alive in a way the rest of this place only pretends not to be. They breathe. You can almost see it—the slow vegetable breath the prose speaks of, the expansion and contraction that is not quite respiration and not quite sleep but something in between, something that belongs to a metabolism so slow it has forgotten how to be hurry. They are the floor of this place, the living carpet that nothing walks on, the green beneath the water that carries them and feeds them and asks them for nothing in return. They do not bloom. They do not seed. They simply are, and their being is enough.

The brick is the oldest thing, though it does not know it. The brick was fired in local kilns, shaped by hands that had their own knowledge and their own forgetting, and it was set into these walls by workers who expected the mill to stand for a hundred years, perhaps two, before the stone gave out and the water won. The workers were right about the water. They were wrong about the stone. The stone did not give out. It softened. It wept. It learned to become the thing the water was teaching it to be, grain by grain, year by year, the slow exchange that is all there is. You can see it happening—if you crouch low enough, if the light is right, if you have the patience to watch stone as if it were a living thing, which it is, which it has always been, which it will be until the last grain releases and the water carries it away. The brick weeps, and what it weeps is itself, and the tears are the color of iron and the smell of something older than iron, older than calcium, older than the kilns that made this brick and the hands that set it and the workers who left and the town that fell. The brick remembers everything it is forgetting. That is what weeping is.

The pump is the heartbeat, and the heartbeat is the pump, and if you stand in the center of the chamber you will feel it in the soles of your feet before you hear it in the air—the slow thump that travels through the stone and the water and the moss and the brick, the rhythm that is the closest thing to a living thing that this place contains. It is not alive. It does not think. It does not know that the mill above has closed, that the workers have gone, that the town has emptied or burned or faded into whatever towns fade into when the thing that made them stops making. It knows only the path of its piston, the turn of its flywheel, the cycle of water through pipes that were copper and are now the color of old storms. It clicks on. It clicks off. It clicks on again, and the click is a sound like a heart learning to beat, a sound like a clock that has forgotten what it is counting, a sound like the place itself saying: I am here. I am here. I have been here, and I will be here, and the water will move, and the moss will grow, and the brick will weep, and I will click on and click off and click on again, and nothing outside this place matters, and nothing outside this place knows.

There is a silence here, but it is not empty. The silence is the shape of all that is absent—the animals that do not live here, the insects that do not breed in these waters, the rats that have not found their way down through the tunnels that have become waterways, the bats that do not hang from a ceiling that is too high and too dark and too still. The silence is the weight of all that this place excludes. It is not hostile. It does not push. It simply does not invite, and the not-inviting has its own presence, its own density, its own way of filling the air so that the air becomes something you can feel against your skin, warm and damp and close, a membrane between you and the things that live here, the things that have always lived here, the things that do not know they are alive and do not need to know. The silence has weight, and the weight holds you, and if you let it—if you stop resisting, if you let the warmth and the damp and the stillness become a kind of embrace—you will understand that this place does not want you, but it does not fear you either. It simply does not know you. You are a visitor in a world that has forgotten what visitors are.

There is something in the darkness that is more than the absence of light. The darkness here has texture, has depth, has the quality of a thing that has been accumulating for two centuries in a place where no one comes to stir it up. It pools in the corners, gathers beneath the looms, fills the upper reaches of the chamber where the ceiling is lost and the brick has become something black and strange and patient. It is not like the darkness of night, which has an end. It is like the darkness of sleep, which does not know it is dark, which simply is, and which holds the dreamer gently until the dreamer wakes. The darkness here holds you gently. It does not threaten. It does not hide. It simply is, and it has always been, and it will be, and the ferns reach toward the faint light because they remember something their seeds remember, something that says: there is light somewhere, there is light above, there is the sun, and we are reaching for it even if we never arrive, even if we do not need to arrive, even if the reaching is all there is and all there needs to be.

You could call this place alive, but that is not right. It is not dead either. It is present. It is here. It is the water and the moss and the ferns and the algae and the brick and the pump, and it is the silence, and it is the darkness, and it is the weight of all that has stopped here, all that has found in this throat beneath the mill a place to stay. Two centuries it has been here. Two centuries more it will be here, and the water will move, and the moss will grow, and the brick will weep, and the pump will click on and click off and click on again, and nothing will change, and nothing will end, and nothing will need to be named, because this place does not need names. It only needs to be what it is: the water's path, the moss's place, the pump's rhythm, the brick's slow knowing, the silence that holds, the darkness that embraces, the presence that is here and will be here and has been here, and does not ask you to understand, and does not need you to stay, and does not notice when you leave, because you were never really here at all. You were only passing through. The water does not pass through. The moss does not pass through. The brick weeps and stays. The pump clicks and stays. This is what lives here: the staying. The patient, endless, weeping staying.
