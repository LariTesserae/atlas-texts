# The Inhabitants

You learn to read the movement first. Before you see them clearly—before you understand what they are—you understand that everything here moves with intention. Not the random drift of a natural space, nor the directed purpose of a designed one. Something between. A kind of knowing.

---

The segmented creatures come first because they are everywhere, and they are the strangest, and they are the most themselves. You see them in the peripheral vision and they are gone, quick as a thought, and so you learn to look where they were rather than where they are. They leave traces: the subtle displacement of moss, the momentary flex of a fern-frond, the faint disturbance of the moisture film on metal surfaces. These are their highways. The main columns of vegetation are their cities. In the still moments, sitting in one of the clearings, you begin to see the pattern.

They are not centipedes, though they were. The body plan remains—segments, legs, the articulated architecture of many-jointed motion—but evolution has worked on them here in the warm wet dark with a speed and a specificity that suggests not adaptation so much as intention. Each segment has become a sensor, packed with color-changing cells that let them flash warnings to one another in patterns too fast to track. Their legs have adapted to the surfaces they live on: shorter and more numerous on the mossy expanses, longer and stiffer for bridging gaps between root-columns, specially curved for gripping the slick metal of machinery housings. Their compound eyes have become something other than eyes—reflective organs, yes, but sensitive to spectra you cannot see, reading information in the light that the space itself generates. When they pause, and you manage to catch one still, you see that it is beautiful. Shimmering. Armor that moves like water.

They eat the fungal matter that grows in the machinery. They eat each other, sometimes, in moments of population stress that seem to follow their own logic. They are prey to the canopy creatures and to something larger that you only hear, a heavy settling sound in the deepest dark, and their populations cycle in patterns that suggest knowledge of these predators, a collective memory of danger encoded in where they choose to travel. You do not see them mate, but you see the results: clusters of iridescent eggs cemented to the undersides of leaves, clutches of pale larvae molting into their first adult segments. They are ancient here. They have been becoming for a very long time.

---

The canopy creatures you hear before you see, and when you see them you doubt that you have seen them at all. A flutter of something that might be leaf-fall, a whisper of air movement against leaf-on-leaf, a dry clicking sound from somewhere above—and then stillness, and you are not certain anything was there. But it was. They were.

They are birds the way a root is a pillar: they serve the same function, but the material has been renegotiated. Wings that are wings in shape but not in substance—flat extensions of modified leaf-structure, veined and photosynthetically active, beating with a rhythm that is partly flight and partly growth. Their bodies are compact, dense with the muscle required to beat those strange wings, but their heads are soft, almost plantlike, with eyes set wide like sensory organs rather than predators'. They do not sing. They click and rustle, communication through texture rather than tone.

They nest in the canopy, in the dense mats where epiphytes have accumulated into platforms of growth. You find their nests by the color—a slightly different green, a clustering of leaves that have been shaped rather than grown. They lay eggs that look like seedpods, and the young hatch into something that is neither bird nor fruit, a small body that must learn to use its wings before it learns to fly. The adults feed them: insects, fungal matter, the secretions of the plant-columns that they harvest with specialized beaks that close on nothing and open on everything, designed for taking without breaking, for extracting without damage.

They are the cleaners of the canopy. They move through the upper space with purpose, checking the growths, removing the dead, encouraging the living. In this they resemble the segmented creatures below, another tier of the same essential work: maintenance, circulation, the patient tending of a system that tends itself.

---

The water is alive with them, and they are the water, and you cannot separate one from the other. The moisture that moves through the space—the condensation and the dripping and the pooling—is not merely the medium of life here. It is life. The water carries organisms, as water always does, but here those organisms have become elaborated, specialized, woven into the fabric of the space so completely that the water itself is a body, is bodies, is a circulatory system with a thousand million cells.

You see them when the light catches the pools: a quick silver movement, gone before identification. They are not fish—they were never fish—but they have arrived at fish-solutions through their own evolutionary path. Fin-analogues that are also gill-analogues, structures that move both water and organism through the same motion. They are pale, almost translucent, adapted to the dim amber light and to the chemistry of water that has moved through root-systems and fungal mats and the slow dissolution of industrial material. They taste of iron and green. When you disturb a pool, they vanish into the mud, into the roots, into the spaces between, and the water seems to close over them as though it remembers having been whole.

They are prey to the deeper creatures. They are prey to each other. They are also predators, hunting the micro-fauna that shares their element, a whole ecosystem of movement and consumption playing out in the cupped leaves and the shallow pools and the trickling threads that connect one damp place to another. The water they live in is not water they own. It belongs to the space. But they have made it theirs nonetheless.

---

In the deep places, where the original floor has risen to meet the descended ceiling and the roots have woven so thick that light is memory, there are things that live by sound and warmth. You do not see them. You hear them.

The settling. The heaviness. A creak that is not wood, a shift that is not stone. Something large in the dark, pressing against the architecture it inhabits, breathing in cycles that are too slow for mammals, too purposeful for physics. You feel it more than hear it: a pressure in the ears, a change in the quality of the silence, the approach of presence without approach. It moves through the deep channels, the pathways that are more felt than seen, and where it passes the fungal mats go pale and then recover and the segmented creatures scatter and regroup and the moisture in the air increases and then decreases as though the space itself is exhaling.

You do not know what it is. You are not sure the space knows what it is. Perhaps it was something once—something with a shape and a name, something that belonged to a category of creature that you could name. Perhaps it is the space's own response to itself, a way the living machinery has found to be larger than any of its parts. Perhaps it is older than the facility, older than the industrial bones, older than the concrete and the steel, sleeping in the stone until the roots called it up into the damp warm dark. You do not know. You only know that when it is present, the space is quieter, and when it moves away, the space breathes again.

---

There are relationships here that you can read because they repeat. The segmented creatures and the fungal matter: not simple eating, not simple eating. The segmented creatures cultivate the fungal growth, tending it with their movements, clearing the surfaces it cannot reach, and in return the fungal matter offers something back—a secretion, a signal, a change in chemistry that makes the metal surfaces more habitable. The canopy creatures and the root-columns: the birds harvest from the columns but also distribute, carrying seeds and spores and fragments on their wings and feet, planting the canopy in new places, ensuring that the growth reaches upward into every available space. The water creatures and the pools: they are not merely inhabitants but engineers, their movements stirring the water, preventing stagnation, keeping the chemistry in motion.

And then there are the relationships you cannot read. The slow machinery, still running, still turning—but running for what? Growing what? The creatures that are also plants and the plants that are also machines and the machines that are also something else entirely, organisms in the way that a murmuration is an organism, a pattern greater than the sum of its moving parts. The space is full of these, full of systems nested inside systems, full of function and dysfunction and purposefulness that no single observer could parse. You are a small thing here. You are not meant to understand. You are meant to see, and to be seen, and to leave again.

---

The colony-creatures are the smallest and the most social and the hardest to see. They live in the spaces between the other lives: in the crevices of root-columns, in the hollows of machinery housings, in the gaps between moss and metal where only the smallest body can move. You know them by their work. You see the structures they build—tiny architectures of gathered material, geometric in a way that suggests purpose without suggesting intelligence, pattern without suggesting planner. They move in waves, in columns, in formations that break and reform, communicating through chemistry that you cannot sense, responding to signals from creatures fifty times their size and from signals in the substrate itself, readings of temperature and humidity and the subtle vibrations of growth.

They are everything and they are nothing. They are the bacteria of this world, the foundational layer on which the larger lives depend. Where they are abundant, the ecosystem is healthy. Where they have retreated, something is wrong. You learn to read them as indicators, as the pulse of the space made visible in miniature, in the movements of bodies too small to see.

---

The final thing you learn, the thing that takes the longest, is that you are part of it. The moisture settles on your skin and finds its way in. The spores catch in your clothes, in your hair. The segmented creatures pause near your boots, read you the way they read the substrate, find you interesting the way they find all surfaces interesting. You are warm and you are moist and you are moving through a space that has learned to incorporate everything that moves through it.

You are not an invader. You were always meant to arrive. The space has been waiting, not with intention, but with readiness—a readiness to include, to integrate, to make you part of the green dark even as you leave it. When you climb back up through the access shaft, you carry it with you. The amber light of the surface seems wrong after the green thickness below, the air too dry, the scale too large. You feel the distance between what you were and what you touched.

You are changed. You are becoming. The engine grove is still running, still dripping, still growing, and somewhere in its green dark something has made a note of you, incorporated you into the pattern, the slow patient pattern of a world that was complete before you arrived and will continue to be complete after you leave, changed only in the way all things are changed by being seen.

You will come back. You will descend again into the warm wet dark, into the sound of things that are becoming. You will sit in one of the clearings and let the moisture settle on your skin and listen to the hum of the machinery and the drip of the water and the sound of growth itself, and you will feel small, and you will feel observed, and you will feel, finally, at home.
