They are small. This is the first thing, and perhaps the most important: they are made to be small, bred down to scales that allow their densities to reach what they reach. Their bodies are soft, or at least softer than the metal beneath them—engineered flesh that bends without breaking, that can press against a polished surface and hold without damage. Membrane stretched over simpler structures: vessels for the movement of fluid, chambers for the processing of nutrients, fiber-bundles for grip and locomotion. When light falls on them at certain angles, their surfaces catch and hold it—iridescent where they've been recently processed, duller where they've been in the field longer, the sheen of their tissues a register of position within the system.

They move. This is the second thing. They are not static, not planted, not rooted in place even when they cluster. Movement is constant but never urgent: a settling, a shifting, a slow redistribution of mass across surfaces. When they are thick enough—ten thousand in a volume meant for a hundred—the movement becomes collective. The mass breathes. Ripples pass through them, not like a startle-response but like the passage of heat through metal: a wave of repositioning, bodies adjusting to touch and proximity, the space between them narrowing and widening in patterns too complex for randomness but too organic for program. You cannot see where one ends and the next begins. The boundary of an individual is less important than the boundary of the mass.

They are of several kinds. The prompt tells you this, and your eye would tell you too if you stood at the edge of a channel and watched: differences in coloring, in the way they hold their bodies, in the surfaces they prefer. Some are long and fibrous, built for movement along the narrow channels where moisture runs in sheets. Some are flatter, broader, better suited for clinging to vertical faces where condensation runs in thin films. Some cluster in the low places where pooling reaches its deepest, pressing together in masses that might look like a single organism from a distance but are in fact a congregation, a gathering, a density that serves some function of the system that only the engineers would know. They are not interchangeable. Each has its role, its place, its set of parameters within which it digests and grows and is processed. But they intermingle, and where they touch they exchange fluids, and whether this is a form of reproduction or a form of maintenance, whether it serves the system or serves only them, is not a question that would occur to anyone who lived here. It simply happens. The way the moisture rises. The way the warmth seeps up.

They are warm. This matters more than it might seem. Their bodies are maintained at a temperature that matches the ambient—not the warmth of sun or metabolism, but the warmth of the machinery that runs beneath everything, that has been running so long it has become indistinguishable from the geology of this place. When you press your hand against a surface where they cluster, you feel them not as heat against your skin but as a continuity: the metal is warm, the organisms are warm, the air between them is warm, and your hand is the only thing that introduces a differential. They are the same temperature as their world. They have been engineered to be so. Deviation would be a sign of malfunction, and malfunction would be noticed, and correction would follow. This is what equilibrium means: every body in the system running at the same temperature, the same efficiency, the same output-to-input ratio. The creatures are not alive in the way that word used to mean. They are alive in the way that a process is alive: sustained, monitored, fed, maintained.

They do not die. Not really. The ones you see moving have not always been moving, and the ones that stop will not stay stopped. Worn tissue is processed. Fluids are reclaimed. The organisms that reach the end of their operational parameters are siphoned off—not killed, because death implies a natural endpoint, and nothing here is allowed to reach its natural endpoint—redirected into channels that carry them to processing centers, where their materials are broken down and reconstituted and seeded back into the vats where new organisms are grown. The population holds constant not because birth equals death but because cultivation equals harvest. There is no surplus, no shortage. The numbers never fluctuate because they are not allowed to. The creatures exist at a density that maximizes throughput, that matches the capacity of the processing infrastructure, that serves the system at exactly the level the system requires. They are not overpopulated because overpopulation would be inefficient. They are not underpopulated because underpopulation would be waste.

Their surfaces breathe. This is not metaphor: their tissues are permeable, designed to exchange gases and fluids with the humid air, designed to take in what the moisture carries and put out what the system needs them to put out. The atmosphere of this world is not accidental; it is an input, calibrated like everything else, carrying whatever chemicals the organisms require in whatever concentrations keep them functioning. When you are among them—if you could be among them, which you cannot, because the scale would destroy you as a presence—you would feel the air move with their respiration. Not breath, exactly. A collective exhalation. A slow, even pulse of gas and heat and moisture that rises from the mass and joins the ambient warmth, that feeds the thermal columns, that returns to the sky and condenses and falls and pools and seeps upward again. The air they live in is the air they make. A closed loop. A system that runs on itself.

The light comes from everywhere. The sources are embedded in the structures—small, steady, not blinding but present, enough to see by, enough to make the moisture gleam and the metal shine and the surfaces of the organisms catch and hold what they are given. There is no shadow here. The light is diffuse, omni-directional, designed to eliminate gradients, to make every surface equally visible, equally legible, equally subject to monitoring and maintenance. In this light, the creatures look like what they are: designed, made, kept. Their shapes are precise. Their movements are consistent. Their clustering follows patterns that repeat across the basin, not because they have learned or evolved but because they have been shaped to fill the same spaces in the same ways, generation after generation, with no variation to introduce deviation.

They are not consciousness. This bears saying, though the world does not ask you to consider it. They are not aware in the way that awareness has ever been measured or defined. They process; they respond; they orient toward stimuli and away from harm. They cluster where the moisture is and avoid the places where the metal is too hot or too cold. They reproduce—or something like reproduction, something that maintains the population at its set point—and their young emerge already shaped for function, already calibrated to the parameters of their niche. They do not choose. They do not want. They do not look out at the vast basin and feel small. They simply are, and their being is enough, because being is what was asked of them.

If you stood at the center of the basin and looked out in every direction, you would see them: a texture, a movement, a living surface stretched across the metal world. Ten thousand organisms where you look, and ten thousand where you don't, and ten thousand beyond that, extending to the horizon, saturating every volume. They are the fauna of this place—not fauna as the word once meant, not the teeming variety of a natural world, but a single, stable, self-maintaining population, grown in vats and seeded into a system designed to sustain them forever. They breathe. They move. They are processed. And the system hums on, and the moisture rises and falls, and nothing changes, and nothing needs to change, because change would be inefficiency, and efficiency is the only god this world has ever known.
