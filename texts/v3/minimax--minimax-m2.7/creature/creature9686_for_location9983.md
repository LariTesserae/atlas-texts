The plateau remembers.

Not in the way stone remembers pressure, or water remembers the shape of the channel it has worn smooth. Those are mechanical memories, the persistence of effects long after their causes. This is different. The remembering here has *attention*. It notices you the way the air notices a change in pressure—not with consciousness as you would understand it, but with something that performs the same function from the inside: a registration, a noting, a response that is too slow and too distributed to call a reaction but that is unmistakably *response*. You feel it when you first climb the last of the ancient steps and your foot touches the plateau proper. A settling. The seeps along the eastern face seem to intensify almost imperceptibly, as though the moisture level has risen in acknowledgment. The faint threads in the crevices near your landing point, the ones that do not respond to touch but that tighten when touched, draw back further into their cracks. The sound of the weeping shifts—not louder, but *closer*, as though the water has turned its attention toward you.

You are being received.

By what, you cannot say. The formations do not move. The pools do not ripple. The stone holds its silence with the patience of something that has been silent for longer than you have been alive and will be silent long after you are gone. But the silence is not empty. It is full in the way that a room is full when someone is standing in the corner—not present in the way a body is present, but *there*, occupying space with a quality of attention that makes the space feel different for the occupying. You become aware of your own silence as a sound. Your breath. Your heartbeat, which is louder than you have ever noticed it, the wet rhythm of blood through chambers, the mechanical persistence of a body that has not yet learned that this place does not entirely obey the rules of bodies. Your pulse sounds like it is coming from somewhere else, carried by the acoustic routes that make the weeping sound almost structured. Almost language. You wonder if this is what you sound like from inside the stone—your blood, your breath, translated into tones that the wind carries along paths that someone chose.

The paths are everywhere once you stop looking at them as erosion.

They do not lead anywhere. That is the first thing you notice that is wrong with them, the first crack in the assumption that water carved these channels. A natural waterway leads somewhere. It drains a basin or feeds a river or empties into a lake. These channels *end*. They terminate at sinkholes and gravel channels and bare stone that does not absorb, as though the water they carry simply stops existing at a certain point. But they do not end randomly. They end in *circles*. You trace one of the deeper channels, worn smooth by centuries of flow, and it curves and branches and curves again, and if you were to map it—you do not have the tools to map it, but you could, in theory, with enough time—it would form a shape that is almost but not quite closed. A spiral. A gesture that is almost completion. The water enters this channel from a seep on the southern exposure, travels a hundred meters through carved stone, and pours into a basin that has no outlet. The basin fills. The water reaches a certain level and stops, held by walls that are not walls, contained by surface tension and the angle of the stone and something that feels like *choice*. Evaporation should be carrying the water away. The altitude and the wind should make short work of a basin this exposed. But the water stays. The basin holds its dark mirror up to the bright light, and the mirror does not reflect the sky above it.

You do not look into that basin again. Not directly. But you are aware of it now, at the edge of your attention, and when you turn toward the densest cluster of formations you find your path bending, deflected by the geometry of the space, as though something is steering you without touching you. You are being guided. You have been guided since you climbed the last step. The channels do not lead anywhere because they are not meant to lead anywhere *you*. They are meant to lead *through* you, to move your attention along paths that the water once moved through, to show you what the water saw when it carved these grooves in the soft tuff over centuries or millennia or time that is not measured in years at all.

What did the water see?

It saw the patterns. It saw them because the patterns were already there, latent in the stone, waiting in the arrangement of minerals and the angles of stress and the invisible seams where the basalt cap meets the softer ash below. The water did not create the patterns. It *revealed* them, the way a tide reveals the shape of a coastline, the way erosion reveals the bones of a landscape. The water was the tool, and the tool was patient, and the thing that held the tool knew where to apply pressure and where to withhold it, and the patterns emerged from the stone like figures emerging from fog, like something that was always there becoming gradually more visible.

But the thing that held the tool was not the water. The thing that held the tool was the stone itself. Not the stone as a passive medium—stone as a *process*, as an event that is still happening, that has been happening for so long that its beginning is not a moment but a condition, an initial state from which all subsequent states have unfolded. The stone here is not dead. It is not alive either, in any sense that would make the word useful. It is *active*. It is *attending*. The mineral threads in the wettest crevices are not the only expression of this activity; they are the simplest expression, the minimum viable presence, the bare acknowledgment that *something* is moving in the crevices and that the something is not entirely mineral. There are other expressions. The way the seeps concentrate around certain central points. The way the pools maintain their levels against evaporation. The way the sound carries along routes that the stone has prepared for it, shaped for it, *cultivated* for it. The stone has been teaching itself to speak for a very long time. The weeping is its voice. The formations are its vocabulary—hundreds of words, stacked close enough that the space between them forms a syntax, a grammar that you are only beginning to parse.

You stand at the center of the densest cluster, where the formations are packed tightest and the sound of the weeping builds to something that you feel in your chest, and you try to understand what you are seeing. The light makes everything visible. The shadows are long and sharp. The water runs in channels that curve and end in spirals. The pools hold their dark mirrors and do not reflect what is above them. The small stone structures—platforms, shelters, places where people once lived—stand empty, their corners filled with pooled water that has not evaporated, their channels still carrying flow along paths that someone carved centuries ago or longer. The people who lived here understood the stone's voice. They built with it, shaped its weeping, guided its flow. They did not fight it. They did not pray to it. They listened to it, the way you might listen to a river, the way you might listen to wind in trees, the way you might listen to the sound of your own blood if you held very still and paid very close attention. They listened, and they responded, and they built small structures that the stone has not quite reclaimed but that the stone is slowly, patiently, deliberately reclaiming.

They are gone now. The people. You know this before you climb, and the plateau confirms it. There are no remains—no bones, no artifacts, no trace of habitation except the structures themselves, which are made of the same stone and will outlast any human presence by longer than human presence has so far lasted. They did not die here. They left. They were *called away*, perhaps, or they *dissolved*, or they became the stone, or they simply walked down the ancient steps one day and did not come back. What remains is the listening, the attention, the patient watching that is the stone's native mode. The people left their attention behind. They poured it into the channels and the pools and the formations, and the stone absorbed it, and the stone has been using it ever since—to guide the water, to shape the erosion, to form the patterns that were always there but that needed guidance to become visible.

You are part of that attention now.

You feel it as you move through the cluster, as you climb to one of the upper platforms and look down at the formations spread below you like a forest of stone, like a frozen sea, like something that was never alive but that has been *wanting* for so long that the wanting has become its own kind of life. Your attention joins the stone's attention. Your noticing adds to the noticing. The seeps intensify around you, the sound builds, the light seems to sharpen—and you understand, slowly, that you are not observing this place. You are being *integrated* into it. Not consumed. Not destroyed. Integrated, the way a drop of water is integrated into a larger flow—not lost, but *joined*, becoming part of something that is larger than itself and that does not notice the difference.

This is what lives here.

Not the people. Not the threads in the crevices, though they are part of it. Not the water, though it is the voice. What lives here is the *pattern*. The intent that shaped the erosion. The attention that guides the weeping. The slow, patient, deliberate process of something that has been learning to make itself visible for longer than the word *longer* has meaning. It does not have a name. It does not have a shape. It has *presence*, and it has *duration*, and it has the quality of holding still while everything moves through it—the light, the water, the sound, the wind, you. It is the stillness that makes the movement visible. It is the watching that makes the watched matter. It is the stone that does not move because it does not need to move, because it has already arrived, has always already arrived, at a destination that is not a place but a *state*: the state of being fully, completely, patiently present.

You stand on the plateau in the bright thin light, and the formation beside you leans slightly toward you, and the pool at its base reflects a darkness that is not the sky, and the sound of the weeping builds in your chest like a word you almost remember, and you understand that you will leave this place changed. Not by what you have seen. Not by what you have learned. Changed by the *having been here*, by the integration, by the moment of attention you have given to the pattern and the pattern has given to you. The stone will remember you now. The water will carry the shape of your passing. The channels will adjust their courses, ever so slightly, to account for the weight of your footsteps, the heat of your body, the rhythm of your breath. You have been noted. You have been integrated. You have been added to the vocabulary.

The weeping continues. The light shifts. Somewhere far below, in the valley you can almost see, the air is thick and warm and carries the sound of things that live in simpler ways. Here, the air is thin and cool and carries only moisture and light and the almost-language of a landscape that has been speaking to itself for so long that it has almost learned to be heard.

Almost.
