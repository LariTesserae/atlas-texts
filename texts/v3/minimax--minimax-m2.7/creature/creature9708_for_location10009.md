The bone-beetles do not sleep.

This is what you learn first, before you learn to read their murmurations, before you learn to predict the hours when they will rise in their thousands from the salt-bone formations and fill the amber air with the sound of their passing. They do not sleep, and they do not rest in the way that the word implies—a cessation, a withdrawal from the world. Instead, they slow. They become still in clusters, hundreds of them pressed together in the angles of rock where the heat cannot reach, and in this stillness they continue: their hearts beating, their chitin exchanging gases with the warm stone-vegetation mats they rest upon, their eggs developing in the bodies of the living, the next generation already taking shape within the carapace of the present.

You watch them for a long time, in those first days. You sit on a flat stone near one of these clusters and you watch, and what you see is not rest but a different kind of motion—slower, deeper, the motion of a system in equilibrium rather than a system at rest. The beetles breathe against the stone. The stone breathes against the beetles. Something passes between them that is neither air nor warmth nor food but all three at once, and you understand that you are watching a conversation that has been happening for longer than your kind has had words for such things.

When you rise to leave, the cluster does not startle. Some of them lift their heads—antennae tasting the change in the air, the displacement of your body from the space it occupied—but they do not flee. They have learned, in their long evolution in this basin where the air is thin and the moisture is scarce and every unnecessary movement costs something that cannot be replaced, that not every change is a threat. They have learned to wait. To watch. To hold still and see what the world will do next.

This is a kind of intelligence, though not one that any settler would have recognized. It is the intelligence of patience, of economy, of a life that has learned to need nothing it cannot make for itself. The bone-beetles do not need the settlers. They do not need you. They have been here longer, and they will be here after, and in the meantime they do the only thing that has ever made sense to them: they continue.

The luminous organs watch from the resin-thickets, and you learn them slowly.

At first you think they are separate from the creatures that hold them—a pair of pale spheres set into a crevice in the amber-like surface, glowing faintly in the dim light, and nothing else. But then one of them moves: a slow, deliberate rotation, the sphere turning to follow your passage across the flats, and you realize that the organs are *fixed* in the bodies of something you cannot see. Something that holds itself pressed against the resin-tree's surface, that has colored itself in shades of amber andite anditeiteite, that has learned to be the thing it rests upon.

You learn to see them by looking at the resin-trees' surfaces. By noting where the light catches wrong. By watching for the occasional shift of a form that should not shift—should not be a form at all, only a feature of the tree, only a thickening of the amber extrusion. You learn to see the watchers, and when you see them, you learn not to look directly. You learn to let your gaze rest on the middle distance, on the salt-bones rising from the flats, on the faint movement of the spore-haze in the amber light. You learn to be the thing that does not notice them, and in this way you become the thing they do not fear, and in this way you pass through their territory without incident, and in this way you begin to understand what it means to live in a place where attention is a form of violence.

The watchers are not the basin’s apex predators. You suspect there is no apex here, no single species that sits at the top of a hierarchy of consumption. Instead there is a web of relationships, a dense tangle of small attentions, each creature watching and being watched, each life form a node in a network that has no center. The bone-beetles are watched by the watchers. The stone-vegetation mats are watched by the bone-beetles. The resin-trees are watched by the stone-vegetation mats, or perhaps the stone-vegetation mats grow in response to the resin-trees' weeping, or perhaps the relationship runs in both directions at once, a feedback loop so old that the original inputs have been lost and only the pattern remains.

You cannot see the whole web. You are not meant to. You are a visitor, a brief displacement of air and warmth and sound, and the basin does not expect you to understand it, only to pass through it without doing too much damage. You try to be small. You try to be quiet. You try to learn the rhythms that will let you move through this place without stopping it.

It does not always work. You disturb things. You leave traces—the marks of your passage, the scuff of your boots on the dark basalt, the heat signature of your body in the thin air. The basin notices you, and adjusts, and continues. The bone-beetles shift their patterns. The watchers rotate their organs. The resin-trees slow their weeping, or perhaps they weep faster, responding to some change in the chemistry of the air that you cannot perceive. The basin absorbs you the way it absorbs everything: without malice, without welcome, with the patient indifference of a process that has been happening since before there were processes to happen.

At night, the basin speaks.

The sound is different then—not the click and rustle of the bone-beetles, not the creak and swell of the resin-trees growing, but something deeper, something that rises from the stone itself. You lie on your back on a flat stone near one of the settlements' low walls and you listen, and what you hear is a kind of hum, a vibration that passes through the basalt and into your body and settles in your chest like a second heartbeat.

You do not know what it is. You have theories: the cooling of the stone after the long day's heat, the contraction and expansion of mineral lattices, the slow settling of the earth's crust in this place where the crust is thin and the fires still bank in the deep rock below. Or perhaps it is the collective breathing of the stone-vegetation mats, the exhalation of a million tiny organisms moving through their nightly cycles of repair and growth. Or perhaps it is something else entirely—something the settlers heard, something the basin has always done, a sound that has no name because no one who heard it ever needed to name it.

You listen, and the sound enters you, and you realize that you are changing. Not physically—your body is still the same shape, still the same arrangement of bone andite anditeiteite, still the same collection of chemical processes that will, eventually, run their course. But something else is changing. Something in the way you perceive, in the way you hold the world in your mind. You are learning to hear what the basin is saying, and in learning to hear it, you are becoming something that can hear it, and in becoming something that can hear it, you are no longer quite what you were when you arrived.

This is what the basin does. It does not destroy you and it does not save you. It *changes* you—slowly, patiently, the way it changes everything that stays long enough. The resin-trees grow around the stones that once held them back. The bone-beetles carry the scent of the mats they rest upon. The stone-vegetation mats shift their colors to match the resin-trees they grow beside. Everything in the basin is becoming everything else, a slow interpenetration that has no end and no beginning, only a middle that stretches in all directions at once.

You will not leave this place the same way you entered it. You know this now, and you are not afraid, because fear is just another form of resistance, and the basin does not allow resistance. It allows only continuation—the slow, patient continuation of what is, into what will be, through the body of whatever happens to be present.

You close your eyes. The hum rises through the stone and into your spine and settles there, a new note in the music of your being. Somewhere in the dark, a bone-beetle clicks. Somewhere in the resin-thickets, a watcher turns its eyes toward the stars. Somewhere in the deep rock below, the fires bank and breathe and wait.

The basin holds you, and does not let go, and does not hold on, and continues.
