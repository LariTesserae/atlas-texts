# What Lives in the Underhalls

---

## The People

The people of the Underhalls have lived with damp so long that it has become part of them. Not all of them—those whose families moved down from the surface in the last generation still carry the awkwardness of adaptation, the slight flinch when touching a wall, the habit of lifting their feet slightly when walking as though expecting puddles where there are none. But the third generation, the fourth, those whose grandparents remember only the upper terraces—they have become something else.

They are lean, the people of the deep places. Not from want—there is enough food, and the cultivation terraces provide reliably—but from a kind of ongoing motion. The city does not allow stillness. To live here is to navigate: steps down, steps up, the constant small adjustments of a body learning to read surfaces. The inhabitants move with a particular gait, a slight forward lean, feet finding the edges of stones where the water does not gather, hands trailing walls not for balance but for orientation. A child raised in the third tier and below will navigate the switchbacks in the dark without hesitation. This is not remarkable. This is simply what bodies learn.

They are people who listen. Not to speech—the Underhalls are loud with talk, with the constant murmur of neighbors through thin walls, with the haggling and calling of the market terraces—but to the other sounds. A person born here learns to hear the water table in the pitch of drips, the way the soft tick-tick in a cellar shifts from rapid to slow as the deep channels adjust. They learn to feel weather in the walls: the way damp stone warms before rain, the way the cold from below sharpens when the surface freezes. This is not considered knowledge. It is simply *knowing*, the way one knows the hours of daylight or the taste of bread.

Family structures in the Underhalls tend toward the vertical. The old live in the upper tiers where the air is warmer and the light reaches; the young, who can more easily climb, live in the deep warrens where space is cheaper and easier to claim. The generations orbit each other across the tiers, visiting in the mornings when the light falls on the southern exposure, carrying messages and small gifts through the switchbacks. This is not sentiment. This is infrastructure. The young know what happens in the deep channels; the old know what changes in the weather above. Between them, the information flows, and the city breathes.

---

## The Creatures

The marsh-eels have a season. In spring, when the water table rises and the lower channels run full, they migrate upstream through the drainage system, silver-bodied and purposeful, and the people who catch them know to expect them by the particular sound of their passage—a soft sliding against stone, a displacement that is felt more than heard. They are prized not for their flavor, which is mild and slightly mineral, but for their timing. When the eels come, the worst of the spring floods have passed, and the terraces can be planted. The eels are a calendar, a signal, a conversation between the deep water and the people who have learned to read it.

The damp-welling beetles emerge at dusk. They are the color of old copper, their carapaces iridescent in the low light, and they move in spirals up the weeping walls, hundreds of them moving in patterns that seem random until you watch long enough to see the logic: they are following moisture gradients, climbing toward the fresher seep at higher points, where the water table sits closest to the surface. Children catch them and keep them in small jars, watching them for a day or two before releasing them back onto the walls. Adults consider them neither pest nor pet—they are simply there, one of the moving facts of the city, like the wind or the light.

The bats are louder than they appear. They nest in the upper cornices of the oldest carved halls, and in the evening they pour out in ribbons, their high-pitched calls bouncing off stone in patterns that map the space better than any written chart. The people of the Underhalls do not disturb the bat roosts. The droppings are too valuable—collected, composted, spread on the cultivation terraces where the light is weakest and ordinary fertilizer would wash away. The bats are partners, in the loose way that all cohabitants of the Underhalls are partners: not through agreement, but through the simple fact of shared space.

In the lowest channels, where the water runs slow and cold, there are creatures that have lost their eyes. The cave-crickets are the most visible—pale, slow, surfacing in the deep channels at certain times of year to breed in the dark. But there are others: fish that have faded to translucence, worms that are never seen but whose presence is known by the way the sediment shifts. These creatures are not discussed much. They belong to the deep places, to the hours after midnight when the city is quiet and the water sounds carry everything.

And there are the things in the reed-beds. The ones that slide. The ones whose displacement is heard but not seen. The people of the Underhalls do not speculate much about them—the reed-beds are difficult to access, the bridges too narrow for comfortable lingering, and the hours when the sounds are loudest are the hours before dawn when most people are asleep. But the sounds exist. Soft movements. A rustle that is not wind. Something breathing where nothing should be.

The people of the southern quarter have learned to include them in the texture of the place. The way the water is present, the way the cold rises from below. Something lives in the reed-beds, and it has its own hours, and those hours are not the people's hours. This is acceptable. The Underhalls have room for many rhythms.

---

## The Water

Above all, the water lives. Not as a creature—it has no will that anyone has named—but as a presence, a system, a thing with its own patterns and preferences. It rises in spring and falls in autumn. It seeps through stone in capillaries too fine to see but strong enough to keep every surface damp. It runs in channels that were carved by its own passage over centuries, and it pools in hollows where the old foundations sank below the water table, and it breathes through the walls of every room in the Underhalls, day and night, an exhalation that never stops.

The people of the Underhalls do not think of the water as something separate from themselves. It is the medium in which they live, the condition that shapes their bodies and their houses and their hours. When they dream, the dreams are full of water—not drowning dreams, not flood dreams, but dreams of movement through channels, of coolness against skin, of depths that are not threatening but simply deep. The water is in them. They are in the water. This is not metaphor. This is topology.

In the deepest channels, where no light reaches, the water is black and cold and slow. It moves with a patience that is difficult to convey—the patience of systems that have been running for longer than memory. The people who service the deep channels carry lanterns and wear heavy wool and know the sound of the water's movement as intimately as they know their own heartbeats. They are not afraid. The water is not their enemy. It is simply very old, and very large, and it will outlast them, and this knowledge is not distressing but clarifying. It puts the small concerns of living into proportion. The water has seen generations come and go, seen houses built and rebuilt, seen the beetles spiral up walls ten thousand times. The water remembers nothing, and the water forgives nothing, and the water continues. This is enough.

---

## The Arrangement

What lives in the Underhalls is a negotiation. The people, the creatures, the water, the stone—all of them have needs, and all of them have found ways to coexist, imperfectly, provisionally, in a space that does not have room for conflict. Conflict here would be inefficient. The space is too full, the systems too interdependent. When the drainage channels silt up, the people clear them because the water needs to move and the water moving keeps the marsh-eels healthy and the marsh-eels healthy mean the terraces can be planted and the terraces planted mean food. When the bats roost too thickly in a cornice, the people patch the cracks and wait for the season to turn. When the reed-beds breathe with movements that are not wind, the people of the southern quarter note the sounds and adjust their schedules accordingly, and the sounds adjust to them, and nothing is solved and nothing needs solving.

The Underhalls are full of living. This is the fact that overshadows all other facts. Full of people who have learned the city's rhythms like a language, full of creatures who have made their niches in the margins, full of water that moves through everything like blood through a body. The city is not clean. It is not orderly. It is not quiet. It is alive, in the way that only places can be alive when they have been lived in long enough for the distinction between structure and inhabitant to blur.

Stand in any hollow of the Underhalls at the quiet hours—after midnight, when the last footsteps have faded and the water sounds fill the space—and you will feel it: the fullness. Not empty fullness, not oppressive fullness, but the fullness of a thing that is working. Every surface in use. Every channel flowing. Every creature in its season. The cold rising from below. The damp pressing in from the walls. The light filtering down through gaps and cracks, silver and strange.

Something lives here. Something that is more than the sum of its parts and less than a single thing. Something that breathes.
