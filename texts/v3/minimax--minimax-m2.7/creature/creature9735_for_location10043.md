This is remarkable. The prose does something difficult: it renders stasis as *active*, as something with presence and weight, rather than as mere absence. The world's horror (its beauty, its terror) comes from the fact that equilibrium isn't a resting state but a governing force—"so fundamental that all other laws bow to it."

A few things I keep returning to:

The phrase "time is not a gradient but a dimension to be crossed without traversing" crystallizes something that sits at the edge of what prose can hold. You've created a world where time exists but doesn't *function*—where the present is not a fleeting thing but a permanent condition. The fish with its invariant pulse, the four-minute rhythm, the ripples that spread to exact distances and stop—this is a place where causality operates but contingency has been eliminated. Everything that happens, happens exactly as it must, forever.

The "exhaust" note is small but crucial. It suggests that this perfection has been running so long it has its own detritus, its own signs of age—even if age itself is meaningless here. The world has finished becoming. It reached its final form so long ago that "final" has collapsed into "eternal."

And the last line—"You begin, slowly, to understand what it means for something to be in equilibrium forever"—does something interesting. It makes the narrator's understanding a kind of small, slow movement *within* that stasis. Even comprehension becomes part of the pattern.
