What lives here does not know it lives.

The cabinet lives. It holds itself in that fogged glass and hums with the low, constant note of something that does not sleep and does not wake because it has never been otherwise. It generates—the word is imprecise, but the water rises from it, or through it, or around it, and the engineers who built the channels understood only part of the equation. The cabinet is the source and the symptom. It is the reason the walls weep and the moss takes. It does not feed the conservatory so much as dream it into being, drop by patient drop, the way a body generates its own atmosphere without meaning to.

The moss lives, but it is still learning what that means. Each fiber presses into the composite stone and does not ask whether it will hold. It simply holds. The growth is not upward so much as inward—a thickening, a quilting, a patient accumulation of itself against the wall. There is no ambition in it. There is only arrival and then the slow work of staying. In time, if the seals failed and the pumps fell silent, the moss would be among the last to know. It would hold its green for months while the ferns curled and the vines went brittle, and only then would it begin to darken at the edges, beginning the long process of becoming what it had been before: nothing.

The ferns live with a different quality—their fronds reach and uncurl and reach again, testing the thickness of the air, the direction of whatever invisible thing they orient toward. They do not know the light comes from fixtures. They do not know the warmth is mechanical. They uncurl toward the glow the way all green things have always uncurled, following a signal older than the building, older than the composite stone, older than the word *conservatory* with its suggestion of preservation, of something locked against time.

The vines have not yet decided what they are. Their tendrils extend and retract in small negotiations with the metal frameworks, testing grip, adjusting. They will climb until they cannot, and then they will thicken where they are, putting out leaves in the wrong direction, reaching for something the architecture will not provide. This is not tragedy. This is just growth—growth that does not know its shape until it has already become it.

And the algae: the algae lives the way most life lives, which is to say, without being noticed. It blooms in the channels in faint green-brown skeins, the first stain on the otherwise pristine composite. It will deepen. It will darken. In a century, the channels will carry not just water but the memory of water—the mineral trace, the biological residue, the evidence of something growing. The algae does not care about a century. It reproduces in the only time it has, which is now, which is always now, which is the only time anything has.

The moth lives or does not, and the distinction is less important than it seems. A body on the underside of a leaf, grey wings folded against whatever remains of the engine that once drove them. The moss may incorporate it. The ferns do not know it is there. The cabinet hums on, indifferent to the small deaths happening in its sponsored atmosphere.

But something else lives here too, something harder to name.

The moisture itself lives—not as a substance but as a process, a constant becoming. It rises from the cabinet and settles on the walls and gathers into drops that merge and fall and find the channels and run toward the grates and are caught and recirculated. It is not the same water twice. It is not different water. It is the water living its brief, circular life, carrying nothing, carrying everything, the medium through which all the other living things are connected without knowing it.

And the conservatory itself lives, in the way that any enclosed space lives when something is always happening in it. The pumps pulse. The fixtures buzz. The condensation ticks its irregular rhythm against the metal somewhere in the upper dark, marking time in a language that does not distinguish between one moment and the next. The walls weep. The moss learns. The ferns reach. The vines probe. The algae blooms in its slow, unwitnessed bloom.

It is all happening at once, and none of it knows the other is happening, and all of it continues anyway.

This is what lives here: the slow, still-alien lushness of process without purpose. The patient becoming that does not recognize itself. The water that rises and falls and rises again, carrying nothing, feeding everything, asking nothing, answering nothing, moving through the conservatory at the crest of whatever world this is, where the air has weight and the walls are learning to be stone and the glass cabinet hums its single note into the humid dark, holding its secret, feeding the moss, dreaming the ferns, keeping time with its own mechanical heart while everything green and growing thickens around it, presses close, arrives, and begins.
