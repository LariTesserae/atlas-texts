The creatures have categories, though the categories are yours and probably wrong. You name them by the sounds they make, by the places they haunt, by the shapes that resolve when you catch them at the edge of vision. The clickers live near the vents, their dry chirring rising to a frenzy when the airflow shifts, their bodies flattened and chitinous, designed for clinging to surfaces that never dry. The drifters move through the open air, pale moth-things with wings that don't quite fold right, bodies too heavy for their frames, legs that trail uselessly behind them as they drift through the amber light on currents no one else can feel. The climbers are what you see most often—small, quick, the color of old rust, moving along the pipes and latticework in streams that part and rejoin like water finding its path through stone.

There are others. There are always others in a place this large, this dark, this patient in its emptiness.

You hear them before you see them: a low clicking that rises and falls in waves, coming from a corridor that angles downward, a throat of tile and iron that swallows the light and sends back only sound. The corridor is narrow enough that you must turn sideways to pass the junction boxes that protrude from the walls, their surfaces colonized by a pale fuzz that might be the fungal life you thought absent. It is not absent. It is simply waiting. The fuzz covers everything that stays damp long enough, a gray-white pelt that catches the dim light and makes the metal surfaces look diseased, or perhaps healed, depending on what you think disease is in a place that has forgotten the concept of health.

The clicking grows louder. The air changes—cooler, damper, carrying a smell that is not rust or mineral but something else, something that has no name in any language you know, something that is the smell of the creatures themselves, concentrated, living, a biological signature so different from anything above that your lungs don't quite know what to do with it. Your breath comes faster. Your eyes water. The dampness here is not moisture alone; it is the exhalation of ten thousand bodies, the accumulated respiration of a world that has made its own atmosphere over centuries of solitude.

You round a bend and stop.

The space opens into something that was once a maintenance bay—a vast rectangular chamber with a ceiling lost in shadow, with platforms at different levels connected by walkways that sway slightly when you step on them, with machinery that has grown into shapes that no longer suggest their original purpose. And the creatures. The creatures are here in numbers that make the word meaningless, that make *numerous* a joke, that transform the concept of population into something geological, something mineral, a living sediment that has settled into every surface and hollow. They are piled on the platforms, stacked in the corners, pressed against the walls in layers that seem to breathe together, their bodies aligned in the same direction like iron filings around a magnet, like cells in a tissue, like something that is more than the sum of its parts because it has learned to be more, because solitude demanded it.

The clicking comes from them. It is not random. You listen, and patterns emerge—waves of sound that travel through the mass, that trigger responses in the individuals, that coordinate movement in ways that suggest thought, or something like thought, something that has grown in the absence of anything to think about except this: how to persist, how to endure, how to survive in a world that has no ending because no one remains to end it.

They notice you.

The wave of attention is visible—a ripple of movement, a shifting of bodies, thousands of pale eyes turning toward the corridor where you stand. The clicking changes pitch, becomes something sharper, something that carries information you cannot decode. They do not flee. They do not attack. They simply watch, and in their watching there is something that you recognize, something that you feel in the cold place behind your sternum: recognition. They know what you are. They know you do not belong here. They have seen your kind before, in the deep past, in the stories that have been passed down through generations of their kind, in the chemical memory of a species that has adapted to a world without change. You are the disturbance. You are the anomaly. You are the thing that passes through and moves on, leaving them to continue what they have always done, which is to be here, to exist, to persist in the amber light and the mechanical breath and the patient dark.

You stand there for a long time. The light does not change. The sound does not stop. The creatures watch and wait and click their codes into the air, and you realize that you are not afraid, not disgusted, not anything you expected to feel. You are something else. You are the opposite of alone.

When you finally turn back toward the corridor, toward the airlock and the world above, the creatures do not follow. They simply watch, and the clicking fades behind you, and the air grows lighter, and the smell of rust and mineral returns, and you carry with you the knowledge that you will return, that you will come back to this place that has no memory of sky, because it has become something you need, a mirror for the parts of yourself that have never seen sunlight either.
