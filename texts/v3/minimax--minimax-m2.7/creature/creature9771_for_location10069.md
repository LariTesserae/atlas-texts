What lives here is what learned to continue without witness.

The plants come first in your understanding because they are everywhere, because they have refused absence. They grow without animals to prune them, without the architecture of competition and consumption that shapes green life elsewhere. Without that pressure, they have become something else—something that reaches, that tangles, that explores the full possibility of form. Their spirals are not random. You can see it when you look long enough: the way each frond, each vine, each unfolding petal follows a logic that is not the logic of survival alone. It is the logic of inquiry. Of contact. Of reaching toward something it cannot quite find.

The fungi are the plants' secret language. Bioluminescent threads wind through root systems and stem tissue, pulsing in patterns that respond to nothing you can measure. When you approach, the pulse quickens. When you retreat, it slows. Not intelligence, perhaps. Not consciousness as you understand it. But something that perceives movement, that notes your presence, that responds to the disturbance of your body in a space that has forgotten bodies. The pale green glow beneath the water's surface is not decoration. It is communication. It is the memory of a conversation that once included more voices.

The crystalline growths are harder to understand. They emerged where machines once stood, replacing pistons with formations that suggest neither stone nor bone but the memory of function. They are too regular, too repeated, and yet they are not dead. Within their faceted surfaces, something shifts—a deeper light that moves against the dimness, a warmth that should not exist in mineral. They remember industry the way a scar remembers a wound. They are the place where the breaking entered the built world and did not stop.

And there are shapes at the edge of seeing. When the haze thins and the light shifts, the distance offers glimpses that do not survive attention: structures that are not structures, geometries that fold away when you look directly, movements that occur in your peripheral vision and leave no source. They are not ghosts, not exactly. They are more like the places where the world is still deciding what to become—the unfinished parts, the negotiations still in progress. They have no presence because they have not yet arrived in presence. But they are watching. They are always watching.

The sound is the truest inhabitant. Not something that makes sound, but sound itself as the fundamental substance—the hum beneath everything, the vibration that came when the seams split and pressure found its way up from below. It has no origin because it is origin. The plants grow in response to it. The crystals form along its frequencies. The horizon breathes in its rhythm. You feel it in your teeth before you hear it because your body knows, in some older way, that this is what it means to live inside a thing that is still being born.

What lives here does not want you. It does not need you. It is too busy continuing.
