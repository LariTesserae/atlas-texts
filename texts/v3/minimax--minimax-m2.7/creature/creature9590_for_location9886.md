Some things here do not move so much as *attend*.

The luminous plants are the loudest thing, but they are not loud. Their glow comes and goes in slow waves — not a flicker but a breath, an inhalation of some warmth that passes through the whole network at once, so that every cluster of leaves dims and brightens together, synchronized across a space so large you cannot see its edges. They are not merely alive. They are synchronized, as though sharing a single pulse. You wonder, watching the light rise and fall in that slow tide, whether they are dreaming one dream, or whether the dream belongs to something larger that wears them like skin.

The roots are the first thing that was here. Not the oldest living thing — time means something different in this place, where the rock is still warm — but the most present. They run everywhere. They are the walls and the floor and the ceiling in the places where the ceiling has not yet formed. They drink. You can almost hear it: the faint, persistent sound of absorption, moisture drawn upward through living tissue, passing from root to root in a flow that never ceases and never rushes. The roots know things. Not in the way an animal knows, not with attention or memory, but in the way wood knows — deeply, slowly, through the accumulation of growth rings upon growth rings, each one laid down over centuries of small decisions: *here, more here, less here, now I will reach toward that warmth, now I will thicken against that cold*. A root that runs the length of the wall is an archive. If you could read it, you would read the history of this place, written in density and direction, in every place it chose to grow and every place it chose to yield.

Between the roots, a network of pale threads — fungal, gossamer-thin — links everything to everything. Where the roots drink, the fungi gather. They are the unspoken language of this place, the conversation that happens beneath the threshold of motion. They carry signals the way a nervous system carries signals, but slower, far slower, in chemical gradients that shift over hours or days. When you step near a root-cluster and the light dims for a moment — just barely, just enough to notice — something is passing through the fungal web. A response. A tension. The vault knows you are here. Not all at once, not urgently, but the way a body knows a change in temperature: a gradual registering, a slow adjustment that has already begun before you are aware of it.

The beetles do not know you are here. They are too simple for that. They are small engines of instinct, pale and eyeless, their antennae tasting the air the way their ancestors did when the world above was young. They move with a deliberation that resembles thought but is not thought — each step a chemical decision, each turn a response to some gradient of moisture or temperature that the fungi have already translated into the air itself. They clean the roots. They are necessary in the way that all small necessary things are necessary: invisibly, without recognition.

The moths are different. They have a relationship with the light that the beetles do not. They are drawn upward through the dark spaces, following gradients of luminescence the way water follows gravity, and they arrive at the brightest clusters as though arriving at a destination. They rest there, their wings folded, their bodies slow and heavy, and the light that passes through them takes on a new quality — slightly warmer, slightly gold, as though filtered through something that has lived. They are not feeding. Not exactly. They are *being fed upon*, or perhaps they are contributing something back, some secretion or warmth that the plants require. The relationship is old. It has the quality of something that evolved in mutual patience, each species shaping the other over deep time until neither could exist without the other. You watch one drift toward a cluster of fronds and settle against the soft underside of a leaf, and it looks less like a creature landing on a plant than like a thought returning to a mind.

There is something else here that you do not see. You feel it.

In the deeper hollows, where the root-walls thicken and the glassy rock surfaces through the living floor, there is a weight. Not physical weight — the air is no heavier here — but a presence that displaces the silence rather than filling it. The pools here are darker. The mosses grow in different patterns, less ornamental, more dense, as though clustered around something. Once, near the far edge of what you can see, something long-limbed and the color of wet bark shifted against the root-wall and went still. You could not tell if it was asleep or simply patient. It did not look at you. Its eyes — if it had eyes — were oriented differently, toward the deep architecture of the space rather than its surface. It may have been waiting for something it has been waiting for for longer than you have had a name for waiting. Its presence is the reason the pools here are dark. Not because anything lives in them — or perhaps because something does, something that lives in still water and knows the shape of the roots the way you know the shape of your own hands — but because the space around it is saturated with a patience so old it has become indistinguishable from the stone.

The water pools are their own inhabitants.

Each one is a world. They do not flow, and they do not stagnate. They receive water drop by drop — condensation from the roots, moisture from the air — and they release it in quantities too small to see, seeping downward through the root-mats into the glassy rock beneath. In this slow exchange they do something else: they collect. Dust that is not dust, spores that drift in on air currents from far away, the shed cells of beetles and moths, the exudates of roots and fungi. Over time — a time you cannot measure — each pool becomes a record. A single pool the size of your palm holds more biological information than the air above it holds air. Its stillness is not emptiness. It is fullness arranged in perfect order, each molecule in its place, each particle settled into a pattern that the pool itself has chosen. If you could read a pool, you would know everything that has passed through this place since it formed. If you could read the largest pool, the one that lies somewhere beyond where you can see, you would know things this vault has never told anyone, things the water has been holding quietly for so long that the holding has become its own kind of love.

The artifacts are no longer entirely their own thing. The altar — if altar is the right word — was carved once by hands that understood geometry and purpose. You can see this in the proportions, in the way the stone was chosen and shaped and set in place with intention. But the root-fiber has consumed most of it, not destroying but *completing*, weaving itself through every crevice until the original form is more felt than seen. The pale blossoms that grow from it are not wildflowers. They are deliberate in the way that only cultivated things are deliberate, each one opening at an angle that catches the light and holds it. The altar is still an altar. It is simply an altar that has become a garden, or perhaps a garden that has remembered it is an altar.

The standing stone is simpler and stranger. Wrapped entirely in moss, it is no longer a stone at all but a column of living light — irregular, warm, faintly breathing. The moss does not grow on it. It grows *out of it*, or perhaps *into it*, the boundary between the stone and its covering dissolved by centuries of slow interpenetration. You could not separate them now without destroying both. The stone holds the shape of what it was, and the moss holds the shape of the stone's absence, and together they hold something you do not have a word for, something that was a stone and then a standing thing and then a growing thing and is now neither and all of these at once.

And beneath all of it, woven through all of it, the floor itself.

The root-mats that form the floor are not uniform. They are soft in places and hard in others, and where they thin you can see the glassy rock beneath — still warm, still taking shape, still in that first instant of its existence. The roots do not grow downward so much as *settle*, spreading out beneath themselves in a slow, deliberate exploration of the space below. Some of them are old. Some are very old. Where an old root dies, it becomes part of the floor, its tissue breaking down into something that is neither root nor soil but a third thing — a dark, rich matter that smells of the beginning of things. New roots grow through it. New life rises from the death of what came before. The floor is not a surface. It is a process. It is the vault expressing itself in another language, speaking in the voice of decomposition and renewal, and it has been doing so for so long that the conversation has become the foundation of everything else.

You are standing in it. You have been standing in it this whole time. And it has been holding you — not actively, not with intention, but the way it holds everything: slowly, with patience, with the kind of attention that does not distinguish between a root and a creature and a visitor and a stone. It receives. It integrates. It continues.

That is what lives here.
