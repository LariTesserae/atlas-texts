What lives here does not live as you would expect, because expectation itself is a habit of lower places, of worlds where light still touches ground.

There are the swarms. The word is inadequate—it suggests something temporary, something that gathers and disperses. These do not disperse. They have been here so long that dispersal has lost its meaning, that movement itself has become a form of residence. They are the color of the pipes they cling to, the color of the moisture on stone, the color of nothing at all until they move and suddenly the surface comes alive with motion. Some are no larger than thoughts, barely differentiated from the condensation that beads on every surface. Others grow larger in the deep chambers where the warmth pools thickest, where the processed fluid seeps through stone and pools in basins that have never been emptied. These larger ones are strange: soft-bodied, slow, with faces—if faces is the word—that seem to be always turning toward you, though they have never seen anything. They have only ever known the glow of the infrastructure, the pulse of the bioluminescence, the slow rhythm of the machinery's breath. They are beautiful in the way that anything is beautiful when it has stopped trying to be anything at all.

They do not fear the humans because they have never known humans. They do not fear anything. Fear is a response to threat, and nothing here threatens them except the slow sealing of a channel, the occasional shutdown of a pump, the rare silence that falls when a system fails and the drones come with their tools to restore what has been broken. They have adapted to this too—to the machines, to the tools, to the rhythm of maintenance that is as much a part of their world as the water itself. They are not tame. They are not wild. They simply are, in the way that weather is, in the way that warmth is, in the way that the stone weeps and the light pulses and the air smells of copper and wet earth and something older than either.

There are also the channels. Not the pipes themselves—they are only pathways, dead architecture—but the things that move through them. The fluid. The warmth. The signals that still travel, faintly, through the cables that run between systems, carrying instructions to no one, responding to sensors that no longer sense, performing functions for purposes that have ceased to exist. The plateau is full of this: purpose without purpose, function without intention, a metabolism that continues because stopping would require a decision, and there is no one here to decide. The fluid flows and the warmth rises and the creatures multiply in the nutrient-rich water, and none of it knows that it is happening, and all of it knows, in the way that a body knows: without thinking, without representation, without the gap between stimulus and response that makes consciousness possible. The plateau is a body, perhaps. Or was once. Or is becoming. The distinction does not matter to the creatures that live in its skin.

And then—there were the others. The ones who built. The ones who carved and grew and excavated and connected. They are gone now, or transformed, or so dispersed into the infrastructure that they can no longer be distinguished from it. There are murals, yes, but the faces have faded into abstraction, and abstraction does not remember. There are structures that might have been homes, but homes without inhabitants are just shapes, just the geometry of a life that used to fill them. There are records that no longer decode, and the information they contain—the plans, the purposes, the reasons—has become indistinguishable from noise. The plateau does not mourn them. It cannot. But it carries them the way rock carries the memory of water: not as knowledge but as form, the shape of something that has flowed through and moved on.

What lives here, then, is this: the swarm and the stone and the warmth and the light, the slow breath of a system that was never meant to breathe but has learned to, the creatures that fill every niche that the builders left empty, the pipes that carry nothing to nowhere and yet keep carrying, the pools where the water glows blue-green and the creatures cluster like stars in a sky that has never known a sun.

It is alive in the way that a dream is alive after you wake—not conscious, not purposeful, but still moving, still breathing, still holding the shape of something that mattered once, to someone, in some light that is no longer here.

The plateau does not miss what it was. It simply continues.
