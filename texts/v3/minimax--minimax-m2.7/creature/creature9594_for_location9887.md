What lives here lives slowly.

This is not a matter of temperament or preference but of necessity—the plateau operates on timescales that make speed into a liability, that render urgency into a kind of forgetting. The organisms that have become native to this place have shed the hurried metabolisms of their ancestors, have traded the quick pulse of reproduction and death for something more sustainable, more continuous, more at peace with the long rhythm of a landscape that does not change because change would require effort it no longer needs to make.

The slow creatures move through the green-gold dimness like thoughts through a dreaming mind. They are large, most of them, though size here is a relative thing—their bodies padded with slow-twitch muscle, their bones dense with minerals drawn from the root-mats that feed them. The *ghedra*, as the lowland peoples call them in the few records that survive, are the plateau's most visible inhabitants: quadrupeds with broad, padded feet that leave impressions like soft depressions in the living floor. They stand six hands at the shoulder and carry their weight with the deliberation of things that have learned not to waste motion. Their eyes are wide and dark, pupils contracted to slits that admit only the faintest light, and they move in small family groups through the fungal corridors, pausing often to press their muzzles against the root-walls and taste the moisture that seeps there. They drink the weeping. They are, in some sense, made of it—their bodies maintaining the same slow circulation, the same patient exudation of fluid through tissues that have forgotten how to hurry.

The ghedra do not run. When alarmed, they simply move more purposefully, their heavy bodies displacing the understory with a soft compression of growth that re-knits behind them within hours. They have no predators that require flight—the apex hunters of the plateau are patient enough to match their prey's patience, slow enough to wait out the long silences between moments of hunger. The great *veloth*, a serpentine body that may stretch thirty feet or more through the root-mats, moves with the slowness of a thought becoming memory. It does not strike; it simply arrives, its jaw opening to receive whatever small creature has grown lax in the perpetual dimness, and then it rests for days afterward, digesting at a pace that would shame any creature built for speed.

Between these slow giants, smaller lives persist in their own rhythms. The *breathers*—small, furred things with external cheek-pouches that store the soft fungal masses they harvest—move through the understory in quick, darting bursts that seem almost frantic by the standards of this place. They are the plateau's nervous system, its quick response to disturbance, and their colonies nest in the hollows left by long-fallen trunks, accessible only through passages too narrow for the ghedra to follow. They emerge at the strange non-dawns that pass for morning here, when the weeping intensifies and the bioluminescence dims, and they move in waves through the green dimness, harvesting the fungal mats and carrying them back to feed their young. A single colony may contain hundreds of breathers, and their combined activity creates a soft, continuous sound—a murmuration of small hungers—that rises and falls with the rhythms of the plateau's own breathing.

In the weep-zones, where the water emerges most constantly from the ancient stone, other lives have found their niches. The *drip-feeders* cling to the weeping cliffs in clusters of pale, translucent bodies, their surfaces slick with moisture, their mouths open to receive the slow fall of condensation. They are not insects—the plateau's insects are few, their place taken by smaller arthropods with slower metabolisms—but they serve a similar ecological function, breaking down the organic matter that washes from the cliffs and converting it into forms the root-mats can absorb. They breed slowly, laying clutches of jelly-like eggs in the crevices where water pools and evaporates in endless cycles, and their young spend their first years as microscopic swimmers in the thin films of moisture that coat the stone. The drip-feeders are prey for the *seep-vines*, parasitic growths that extend thin filaments from the root-mats and insert them into the drip-feeders' soft bodies, drawing sustenance at a pace so gradual that the prey does not notice its own diminishment. The seep-vines are in turn consumed by certain fungi that specialize in decomposing their tissue, and the fungi feed the root-mats that produce the seep-vines, and the root-mats are sustained by the drip-feeders that breed in the weep-zones, and the weeping continues, and the cycle perpetuates itself without beginning or end.

The fungi are everywhere, but they are most visible in their luminescent forms—the *pulse-caps* that mark the paths through the understory. They rise from the root-mats in clusters of pale stalks, their caps barely visible in the dimness, but when disturbed—by footfall, by the passage of a ghedra, by the falling of water—they respond with a soft intensification of their glow. Blue-green light ripples outward from the point of disturbance, traveling through connected networks of mycelium that may span miles, creating waves of illumination that pass through the dark like thoughts through a dreaming brain. The pulse-caps do not think, as far as anyone can determine, but they respond, and their responses have the effect of thought—of communication, of collective awareness, of a kind of distributed sensing that encompasses the entire plateau. When something large moves through the understory, the pulse-caps know. When the weeping intensifies, the pulse-caps know. When the old stone shifts in its long patience, the pulse-caps know. They are the plateau's nervous system, its medium of awareness, its way of understanding what happens within its own body.

And there are other fungi, stranger ones, in the deeper layers where light has never reached. The *root-ghosts* are pale, almost transparent, their fruiting bodies emerging from the root-mats like pale fingers from dark water. They do not luminesce; they do not respond to disturbance. They simply exist, growing at a pace so slow that a single specimen may take a human lifetime to produce a cap, feeding on the chemical energy of the root-mats' slow metabolism, converting it into forms that the vegetation can use. The root-ghosts are the plateau's recyclers, its way of reclaiming what would otherwise be lost, its patience made organism. They have no known predators. They have no known competitors. They have only the long slow work of being what they are in a place that has forgotten how to be anything other than itself.

The builders are gone, but their echoes remain. In the root-mats, in the weep-zones, in the patterns of growth that sometimes follow lines too straight to be natural, the plateau carries the memory of hands. The fitted walls that rise from the living ground are colonized now, their stones fused with root and vine until the original masonry is more suggestion than fact, but they still stand, and the vegetation that covers them grows differently—thicker along the edges, thinner in the corners—marking the boundaries of human intention even as it absorbs that intention into its own slow accumulation. The platforms that once held gatherings now hold only the slow pulse of growth, the drip of water, the occasional passage of a ghedra pausing to drink from the weeping that surrounds them. The circles of posts have rotted at their tops, their purpose forgotten, but their bases remain anchored in the root-mass, and sometimes a breather will nest in the hollow between two posts, and the ghost of the circle persists in the shape of its nest.

The plateau does not remember the builders. It has become something new, something that has no need for memory as humans understand it. But it carries them anyway—absorbed, integrated, made part of the slow conversation of organisms that has been ongoing since before there was a word for conversation, since before there was a word for organism, since before there was a word for before. The builders left their marks, and the marks became part of the plateau, and the plateau grew over them, and the growth became stone, and the stone became root, and the root became the floor on which the ghedra walk, and the ghedra walk on the bones of the builders without knowing, without needing to know, without anything but the slow pulse of the pulse-caps to tell them where they are and where they have been and where they will go when the time comes to go.

The weeping continues. The water emerges. The root-mats breathe.

And the plateau endures, patient as stone, alive as a dream, waiting for whatever comes next with the same long stillness with which it has waited for everything that has already come.
