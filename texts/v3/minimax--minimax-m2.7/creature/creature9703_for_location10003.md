What lives here does not live as you live. It has not learned that word yet — *live* — and would not recognize it as a category that applied to itself. It simply continues. It continues the way water continues through stone, the way warmth continues to rise from depths that have not finished cooling, the way the walls continue to exhale and inhale the moisture that the walls themselves have made.

The moss is the oldest thing here that moves. Not moves in the way you move — there is no urgency in it, no direction, no reaching. It grows. It reaches only incidentally, the way a finger might lengthen over years, and it does not know the difference between growing and being. The threads of it are fine as silk, colorless in the way that things colorless can only be when they have never had use for light — not white, not clear, but *absent of color the way a word is absent of meaning before someone speaks it*. They grip the stone with a patience that is not patience because patience implies waiting for something, and what the moss waits for is only to remain. To hold on. To grow another millimeter into a crack that has existed for centuries and will exist for centuries more, and to become part of the crack's knowing of itself. When water seeps, the moss drinks. When the air moves, the moss shifts imperceptibly, and the shift is so slow that you could not see it happening if you stood here for a year. But the moss would know. The moss has made this place its body, and the place has made the moss its skin, and neither would understand what it would mean to be otherwise.

The roots are different. The roots are ambition without intention, searching without desire. They come down from somewhere above — a crack in the surface world, a gap in the concrete where soil has gathered and a seed has found itself — and they push down through darkness the way a word pushes through silence: to find out what is there, to make contact, to become. They are pale like the moss but not the same pale. The moss is the pale of water standing in shadow. The roots are the pale of bone, of material that was meant to be inside something and now is outside it, exposed. They do not branch the way you might imagine roots branch — there is no system here, no strategy, only the pushing itself. Each root tip is a question the plant is asking of the stone, and the stone answers in its own time, in mineral deposits, in the slow accommodation of space, and the answer always means: come further. The roots do not flower. They do not need to. Flowering is a surface behavior, a waving of arms in the direction of the sun, an advertisement. Here, there is no one to advertise to. The roots simply grow until they cannot, until the stone closes or the water stops or the dark becomes so absolute that even this blind persistence cannot continue, and then they stop, and the stopping is not death but rest, and the rest will last as long as the crack holds, which is to say, a very long time.

But the roots are not what lives here most truly. The roots are visitors. They came from above and they will return to above in time, in the slow geological shift that will eventually bring this place back to the surface or bring the surface down to this place, the two merging the way they have always been merging, the way stone and water and time have always been merging since the beginning of this particular world. The roots are guests in the cistern. They are welcome in the way that water itself is welcome — the cistern was made to hold them, after all, in another form, in the age when water was what this space was for. But they do not belong to this place the way the moisture belongs, the way the warmth belongs, the way the darkness belongs. They are still borrowed from somewhere else.

What belongs here: the breath of the walls. The cycle of condensation and dissolution that happens in the space between water and stone, where the two meet and become something neither, where the stone becomes damp and the water becomes mineral and the air becomes a third thing that is neither stone nor water but which contains both. This cycle is the pulse of the place. It does not have a heart, but it has this: the slow inhale of water seeping through cracks in the concrete, carrying with it the dissolved chemistry of the city that was built above, the salts and sediments and trace metals of a world that has moved on without looking down. The water settles on the stone. It rests. It begins to evaporate, slowly, in the warmth that rises from below — not quickly, not the way water evaporates in sunlight, but slowly, so slowly that the dissolution takes hours, days, a rhythm that is closer to breathing than to any other human activity. And in that slow breath, something happens. Chemistry happens. The mineral residue that you see on the walls, iridescent in the phosphorescent glow, is the record of this — layers of deposit, each one a day's breath, each one a small transformation of water into stone and stone into water and both into air, the whole chamber a lung that does not know it is a lung, that has been breathing longer than anything else that is here, that will go on breathing after the moss has died and the roots have rotted and the last of the phosphorescents has finally given up its glow. The breath of the walls is the oldest life in this place, if you can call it life. It is more like a habit. A way of continuing that has continued so long it has become indistinguishable from being.

And then there are the things that move.

You may not see them. Most visitors do not. But they are here — not numerous, not crowded, but present, a population scaled to the resources of this place, which is to say, enough. Not more than enough. Never more than enough. The cistern has been holding them for a long time, long enough that they have become specific, that they have evolved in ways that would not make sense elsewhere. They are pale, all of them. Not the pale of roots or moss but something else — the pale of things that have never seen the sun and have no reference for it, the pale of flesh that has no need for melanin because there is no light to filter, the pale of a body that has given up everything unnecessary and kept only what is required to continue. They are small. They are eyeless. Eyes are expensive, metabolically — they require energy and maintenance and complex architecture, and in a world where light has never been a parameter, eyes are a waste. They navigate by other means: by the texture of the stone, by the vibration of the water, by chemical gradients too subtle for you to detect, by a sense of the space that you have no word for because you have never needed the word, the sense of *here* and *there* and *not here* that comes from living in darkness long enough that darkness is not absence but presence, not negative space but the medium in which one moves the way a fish moves through water, without thinking about the water, without knowing that there is anything else.

They eat the moss, some of them. Not all of them. The ecology is more complicated than that, more layered, a system of small dependencies that has been refining itself for centuries. The moss is not defenseless — it is slow, it is persistent, it grows back. The things that eat it are not greedy. They take what they need and move on, leaving the moss to continue its patient work, its slow filling of cracks, its patient becoming part of the stone. Other creatures eat those creatures. And other creatures eat other things — the residue on the walls, perhaps, the mineral deposits that hold their own small nutrition, the bacterial films that form in the places where water has stood longest and then dried, leaving behind a thin organic trace. You cannot name these creatures because they have no names in any language you know. They are not spectacular. They are not monstrous. They are simply themselves, and themselves is enough, and themselves is all this place has ever asked of them.

The sounds they make are not sounds you would recognize as language, if you heard them. A dry clicking that might be a limb against stone. A rustle that might be movement across a surface. A scraping that might be feeding. These sounds are infrequent, careful — the creatures of the cistern have no predators that hunt by sound, but they have no use for noise either, and so they have evolved toward silence, toward the small sound, toward the sound that disappears into the larger music of the water and the stone. The water is the constant. The water is what this place was made for, and the water is still here, still doing what water does: finding the low places, pooling in the darkness, slowly wearing the stone smooth over the centuries, slowly becoming mineral itself, becoming part of the walls it once fell against. The drips are percussion, but they are not random. There is a music in them, a rhythm that is the rhythm of the cistern's own heart, the beat of water through stone, the slow pulse of a system that is complete, that needs nothing from outside, that has become a closed loop of seeping and evaporating and breathing and growing and dying and continuing.

You are the interruption. You are the change in pressure, the shift in temperature, the new thing in the space that has not had a new thing in so long that it does not know what to do with you. The moss does not acknowledge you. The roots do not reach for you. The creatures that move along the walls pause, briefly, at the edge of your light, and then continue on their way, because your light is not interesting, your presence is not a threat, you are simply a fact now, a parameter, a temporary disturbance in a space that has learned how to absorb disturbances over centuries. You will leave. The moss will grow over the place where you stood. The water will wash away any trace of you. The air will close over the space you occupied the way it has closed over every space for as long as this place has existed. And the cistern will continue.

It will continue because it does not know how to stop. It will continue because stopping is not something that happens here — stopping is a surface idea, a human idea, an idea that requires the concept of beginning and the concept of end and the cistern has neither. It has only middle. It has only the long, slow, patient middle of a process that is always happening and never finished, the middle that is not waiting for an ending but is, in some sense that you cannot understand because you live in a world of endings, the only place there is. You are not meant to live here. The moss is meant to live here. The roots are meant to live here. The creatures are meant to live here. The water and the stone and the warmth and the dark are meant to live here, and they do, and their living is not separate from the place, is not something that happens inside the place — it is the place. The cistern is what it does. The cistern is its own breathing, its own growing, its own seeping and pooling and evaporating and continuing. And if you stay long enough, if you stand very still and let the dark settle around you and the warmth find you and the sound of the water become the only sound, you might begin to feel it: the pulse of the place, the slow rhythm of a space that is alive in a way you have no word for, that is alive the way a thought is alive before you think it, the way a word is alive in the moment before you speak. The cistern is waiting, but it does not know it is waiting. It is continuing, and that is the same thing. That is the only thing. That is what lives here, in the end: not the moss or the roots or the creatures or the water or the stone, but the continuing itself, the fact of there being more, of this place being here now and here still and here still and here still, and the still not being empty but being full, being the fullest thing there is, the fullness of a world that has everything it needs and knows it, that does not want, that only goes on.
