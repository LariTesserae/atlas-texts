Begin with the bodies that press against the walls — the long ones, the ones that stretch from floor to ceiling with no apparent beginning or end. Their bodies are segmented like vertebrae, each section slightly darker than the last, and where they press against stone the heat of them has worn the rock smooth. They do not move often. When they do, the movement passes through them like a wave, a ripple that begins at one end and arrives at the other so slowly you might not believe it started. They have no eyes. They have no need of eyes. Their skin is sensitive enough to feel the stone's slow breathing, to taste the mineral in the air, to track the faint variations in temperature that mark the pathways between hot and not-quite-as-hot. These are the oldest ones. You can tell by the wear of them, by the way the walls have reshaped themselves around their sleeping forms.

Now the smaller ones — the ones that click. They move in clusters, in tight spirals, and when they move together the sound is like rain on something far away, a muted percussion that rises from the stone. Their bodies are armored in overlapping plates, and when they cluster to rest they lock together so completely that you might mistake them for a single creature with many legs. They are the ones who nest in the cracks, whose bodies are thin enough to fit where nothing thicker should. They lay eggs in the salt blooms — the ghost-white places where ancient water touched and went — and the eggs are the size of your smallest nail, and they glow faintly with their own slow heat.

There are creatures that flatten themselves against the floor and move by rippling, by a wave that passes through their body and propels them an inch at a time. Their undersides are textured in ways that catch the faint light your eyes are not good enough to see, patterns that might be sense or might be history. When they pass over each other — and they do, they pass over each other constantly — the friction is silent but the air moves, and you feel it against your skin if you are close enough, a warmth that is not stone-warmth but body-warmth, distributed and shared.

The thermal bodies are the ones you notice last. They are the ones who have become geography. Their backs are landscapes — worn smooth at the peaks, where generations of smaller creatures have rested and shed and died, and cracked at the edges where heat has done its slow work. When one of them moves — and they do move, eventually, after days of stillness — the entire ecosystem shifts. Smaller creatures migrate across its surface like ants across a log. The ones that click move to the cooling edges while the ones that cling move to the warming center, and the long ones adjust themselves along whatever new gradient the movement has created. The thermal bodies have learned to carry. They do not resist. They are what they are, which is mobile heat, and they have made peace with what that means.

There are others — creatures that nest in the machines themselves, in the narrow channels where the factory runs, feeding on whatever the machines produce or reject or leak. Their bodies are translucent in places, soft where they should be hard, and they leave no trace when they die except a faint residue that the stone absorbs. They do not hiss or click. They make no sound anyone has ever heard. Perhaps they are the newest, the most recently arrived, still finding their place in the hierarchy of warmth. Or perhaps they are the oldest and have forgotten how to be noticed.

You could hold this place in your hands, and it would be warm, and the warmth would be alive. The creatures would press against your skin — curious or indifferent, it is hard to tell the difference when everything is so close. Some would scatter. Some would climb. Some would wait, as they have always waited, for the warmth to come to them.

The creatures do not have names. They do not need them. They have only this: the heat, the dark, the slow pulse of stone against stone, the way every surface is held and every body is held in turn. The pocket in the world. The world holding its breath.
