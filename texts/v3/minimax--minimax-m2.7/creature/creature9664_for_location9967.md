The long-limbed thing returns. You see it this time—a species of its own, nothing you have a name for, though it wears the shape of something you might call a weasel stretched long and lean, built for the narrow spaces between root-walls. It moves through the canopy without sound, weight distributed across multiple limbs that grip and release the woven fiber with the ease of something that evolved here, that was shaped by this architecture into its current form. Its eyes are small and dark, its coat the grey of dried moss. It is hunting, though you cannot see what it hunts. The clicking you hear sometimes, the chitinous ticking—it follows that sound, or something like it. The creature knows the paths through the root-mat the way you know the rooms of a house you have lived in all your life.

In the pools—the lowest places where water gathers and freezes in slow sheets—something else lives. You can see it when the light is right: a shimmer at the edge of the ice, a darkness moving in water too cold for your comfort. It is not fish. Fish would not survive here. It is older than fish, perhaps, or parallel—a creature that has solved the problem of cold water and thin oxygen in its own way, a body flattened and slow, gills that function in ice-melt, a lifecycle tied to the freezing and thawing that others flee. It lays its eggs in the silt at the bottom of pools that will freeze solid by midwinter, eggs that wait in stasis until the spring returns. The adults are the size of your palm, unremarkable, the kind of thing you would overlook if you did not know to look. They are old. Their kind has been here since the roots first began to weave, since the moss first caught hold. Perhaps longer. Perhaps the moss came for them.

The fungi are more than decoration. The blue light you see is not purposeless—it is a communication, a chemical language spoken in photons, passed between organisms that share the root-network the way your nerves share signals. The bioluminescent fungi are the nodes of that network, the points where the web thickens, where nutrients flow between species that have learned to trade. The roots do not only carry water. They carry information. They carry the chemical signatures of stress and plenty, of predator approach, of the slow seasonal turn. The fungi translate. The plants listen. Everything here is in conversation, has been in conversation for longer than the stone has been young.

In the deep places—the spaces between root and stone where decay has accumulated into something like soil—worms move. Not earthworms, which prefer warmth, but cold-adapted varieties, pale and slow, their bodies designed for a life measured in decades rather than years. They turn the decay. They make the soil that makes the moss that makes the decay. The cycle is tight, closed, efficient in the way that isolation produces efficiency. Nothing leaves here. Everything is used. The worms are food for things that are food for other things, but the waste returns, and the roots incorporate it, and the process continues.

There are insects, though they are not the insects you know. The clicking comes from a beetle that lives in the root-wood, that bores through fungal-decayed fiber with mandibles adapted to cold, that lays its eggs in galleries it carves with patient precision. Its lifecycle is three years. Its adult form lasts weeks, just long enough to find a mate and continue the line. The rest is spent in darkness, in the living walls, eating and being eaten in turn. There are flies, too—tiny, dark, that emerge in the brief warmth of midday and are gone by evening. They do not bite. They do not need to. They drink condensation, and are drunk in turn by the waiting predators.

The moss itself is an ecosystem. You would call it moss from a distance, but up close it is textured, layered, a community of species that have merged their boundaries into something continuous. There are lichens within the moss, and tiny plants that would be flowers if they ever bloomed, and organisms at the threshold of visibility that you cannot name and cannot quite see. The moss holds water like a sponge, releases it in slow seeps, keeps the creatures that shelter in it damp and alive through the worst of the cold. It is foundation. It is home.

And there are things you never see. The deep-dwellers, the stone-buried ones, creatures that live their lives in the crevices of fresh rock, eating mineral and exhaling something that the roots eventually find useful. The space is full of absence that is not empty, of lives lived in the interstices, of processes occurring at scales too small to register. The cold slows everything. Nothing is in a hurry here. The pace of growth is also the pace of death, the pace of decomposition, the pace of the slow exchange that keeps the system alive.

You are the guest. You have always been the guest. Even now, standing still in the breathing ward, you are an intrusion into a conversation older than your presence, a brief imbalance in a system that will adjust to your absence the way water adjusts to a stone—flowing around, flowing past, the space you occupied filling with something patient and particular and utterly itself.
