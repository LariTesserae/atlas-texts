You begin to walk.

The ground beneath you is uneven in the way that living things are uneven—not the uniform roughness of broken stone but the particular texture of something still deciding what shape it wants to hold. Your boots sink slightly, find purchase, sink again. The sediment here hasn't compacted into permanence. It still has the memory of water moving through it, the spaces between particles still adjusting to the weight of what sits above. You feel the slight shift with each step, the ground breathing you in and out like something between solid and atmosphere.

Up close, you can see that their needles are not just dark but iridescent, holding colors at the edge of perception—blues and greens that shift when you move your head. The trees have grown here in the lee of ridges, their forms shaped entirely by wind, and they have made peace with that shaping. They do not fight the direction the air wants to push them. They lean into it, find their balance, become something that exists because of the force that should have destroyed them.

The water is louder than you expected. You stop when you realize you are walking toward a sound, a persistent whisper that rises and falls with the shifting wind. The cliff face is only a few meters away—gray rock streaked with the dark lines of old seepage—but the water emerging from it is not the expected thin trickle. It is a constant exhalation, a veil of moisture that catches the light and turns the rock behind it into something luminous. The water emerges from countless small openings in the stone, each one barely a crack, and together they create a sound like breath, like language, like the mountain speaking without words. Your hand moves toward the rock without conscious decision, and when your fingers touch the surface, it is warmer than the air. The water is warm. It has traveled up from somewhere deep, carrying heat you cannot explain, and released it here at the place where the rock meets the sky.

You leave your hand there for a long time.

When you turn toward the city, you understand finally why it does not look built. The towers that rise from the plateau are grown from the same stone the plateau is made of—or something so similar that the distinction becomes philosophical rather than material. Their surfaces show the same textures, the same patterns of erosion and deposit, the same marks of water and time. But they are arranged in ways that stone would never arrange itself: the curves that suggest function, the voids that imply shelter, the bridges and channels and spans that speak of purpose. The city is not built on the plateau. It is grown from it, coaxed into being by whatever science or art or magic the people here have mastered.

You can see the membrane corridors more clearly now. They span the gaps between towers like the webs of some vast and careful spider, translucent and slightly iridescent in the thin light. They sway slightly in the wind but hold their shape, and you realize they must flex in response to the same vibrations you feel in the ground. The city is not fighting the instability. It is dancing with it.

The calls of the herd animals grow closer, and you realize you are walking toward the sound of them. They must come here for water, just as you do. They must have learned that the cliff faces that weep are safe places, reliable places, places where the mountain offers what life requires. As you crest a slight rise, you see them at last: dark shapes moving across the wet ground, their forms massive and shaggy, their breath visible in the cold air. There are dozens of them, maybe more, scattered across a shallow depression where the water pools in a way that feels intentional—a natural basin that the city has perhaps encouraged, shaped, made into a gathering place. The animals move slowly, deliberately, and they do not startle at your presence. They have learned that the creatures who emerge from the towers are not hunters. They are part of the same slow becoming.

One of them raises its head as you approach, and its eyes are dark and deep and hold a patience that you recognize. This is a creature that has made its peace with the waiting. It knows that the plateau is still forming, that the rivers have not yet found their final paths, that the mountains are still becoming mountains. It has adapted not to a fixed place but to a process, to the ongoing emergence of the world it lives in. And it passes that patience on to whatever follows it.

The wind picks up again, and the smell comes with it: moisture and stone and ozone, and something beneath those that you cannot name—something that might be the scent of the city thinking, of technology running at the edge of perception, of a civilization that has learned to grow rather than build. The clouds overhead are reshaping themselves again, and a shaft of light breaks through for a moment, illuminating the towers, the herds, the weeping cliff, the wet ground beneath your feet.

In that light, you can see it all clearly: the way the city rises from the plateau, the way the plateau rises from the earth, the way the earth itself is rising still. The mountains on the horizon are sharp-edged and new and they are still becoming, and the city grows with them, and the herds move between them all, and you stand in the middle of it all with your hand still warm from the stone's exhalation, still breathing, still becoming yourself.

The plateau does not notice you.

But you notice it. And in noticing, you become part of its making.
