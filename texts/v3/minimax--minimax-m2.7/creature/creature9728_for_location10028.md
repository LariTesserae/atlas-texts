The people live here the way roots live in stone—not as owners, but as something already too deep to extract. They are of the place now, their bodies adapted in ways that are hard to call genetic because the changes are not written in blood so much as shaped by time and habit and the particular quality of the humid air. Their skin tends toward darker tones where it is exposed, weathered by sun and rain, and paler where it is sheltered—in the arcades, in the deep rooms carved into the terraces, in the market halls where light falls in shafts through old ventilation grates. They are not a single people. That is important. Generations of migration have settled here—descendants of engineers and farmers and priests and outcasts who climbed the long roads or rode the cables and arrived, generation after generation, to find that the mountain had already made room for them.

They build the way the place builds: slowly, from the inside out, in response to what is already there. A family arrives and finds a hollow in the concrete where water collects, and the hollow becomes a basin, and the basin becomes a garden, and the garden becomes a home. The children grow up speaking the water language, the language of gesture and sound that travels across the still channels in the humid air, a language that uses the echo off stone walls and the resonance of copper pipes as part of its grammar. They do not think of this as strange. They do not think of the city as something built at all. It is simply where they were born, and it was already growing, and so they grow with it.

There are those who tend the machines. They are a caste or a guild or simply a way of being that has no clean name—the Keepers, some call them, though not in their own language, which has no word for keeping, only for listening. They learn the hum from childhood, pressing their palms to the vibrating metal, feeling the rhythms of the pumps and the shifts in pressure that mean the water is rising or falling or pooling where it should not. Their hands become rough and dark with mineral deposits, and their ears learn to hear in the hum the small variations that indicate maintenance needs, and their knowledge passes not in books but in proximity, in the years spent working alongside someone who themselves learned from someone else. A Keeper can feel a machine's distress the way a sailor feels weather in the movement of the sea. What they know is not engineering. It is a kind of deep acquaintance. And because no single Keeper understands all the systems—the city is too vast, too layered, too old—maintenance is collective, passed across generations like a song that no one has ever seen written down, only sung.

Children move through the place like water through cracks. They run along the narrow ledges that edge the higher terraces, bare feet finding the grooves worn into the stone by centuries of use, and they jump across gaps where the concrete has cracked and roots have pushed through, and they slide down channels that were made for drainage but have been worn smooth by generations of bare bottoms and bare feet. They know the place the way their parents know it but differently—more intimately, perhaps, because intimacy is something that belongs to those who are still small enough to fit into the spaces that the city keeps forgetting about. There are caves within caves here, hollows in the concrete where rainwater gathers and stagnates and becomes a world of mosquito larvae and tadpoles and slow beetles with shells like polished stone. A child will find such a place and claim it and bring back friends and build, out of scraps of broken pottery and chips of fallen masonry, a civilization that will last until the next rain changes everything.

The old people sit. Not from weakness but from a kind of deliberate settling, a decision to become part of the landscape the way the fountains have become part of the landscape. They find a place—a niche in an arcade wall, a bench beneath a tree whose roots have grown up around the stonework, a platform edge where the view opens to the valley below—and they sit there, day after day, until the moss begins to grow on the parts of them that are still and still. They talk when they want to, mostly not. They watch the water. The younger people bring them food and water and news, and the old people receive these things with the same quiet attention they give to everything else, and sometimes the younger people find this attention uncomfortable, as though the old ones can see something in them that they themselves cannot. Perhaps they can. The old ones have been here long enough that they have become porous to it, their boundaries no longer quite where they began.

There are others. Not human. The city has made room.

The birds came first, or perhaps they were always here—they were certainly here before the terraces, before the mountain was carved and poured and planted, before the first water was channeled upward through copper shafts. They are what the text called them: adapted. The ones that mimic machinery are not a single species but a whole category of behavior, a tendency that different birds have converged on from different evolutionary starting points, each species adding its own flavor to the mimicry—the mechanical click of the rail-bird, the rising steam-whistle call of the tower finch, the deep bass hum of the cistern thrush that nests in the pump shafts and learned long ago to sing the song of the engine beside it. They do not mimic to deceive. They mimic because the city is loud, and the birds are young in it, and their songs are becoming what the city is. Their feathers are sometimes strange—patches of iridescent green where they brush against copper, a rust-colored staining on the breast where they rest against iron. They are part of the city's chemistry now.

The insects are older. The beetles that live in the moss have no interest in the city at all—they live in the moss, and the moss lives in the city, but the distinction matters little to them. They are dark and slow and their shells catch the light in the wet hours, and if you kneel close to a patch of moss on a raining morning you will see them moving through it like thoughts through a mind, purposeful and incomprehensible. There are fireflies on the lower terraces, though the word is wrong—they are not flies but a kind of large soft-bodied beetle that has lost its ability to fly and instead crawls through the canopy at night, trailing a light that is bluish and cold and seems to come from the beetle's despair rather than its joy, if despair can be said to glow. The children catch them in jars and set them on the edges of channels where their light reflects in the slow water, and for a moment the canals blaze with tiny blue flames, and then the children let them go.

The cats are a politics. They arrived as mousers in the granaries and they stayed for the warmth and they multiplied in the niches and the hollows and the dense root tangles, and now they are everywhere—not in the upper terraces, where it is too cold and bright, but below, in the arcades and the canopy streets and the lower levels where the air is thick and warm and full of birdsong. They are large, the cats of this place, their fur grown long and coarse to manage the humidity, their tails thick and their ears tufted with a kind of hair that grows specifically in this city, as though the place is slowly making them over in its own image. They own the lower terraces in the way that nothing owns anything here—which is to say they occupy them, persist in them, have become so much a part of them that to remove them would be to remove a feature, like pulling the vines from the walls. They watch the people with the same patient attention the old people watch the water. They are not tame. They have never been tame. The people who came here centuries ago may have brought them as pets, but the place remade them, the way it remakes everything, and now they are something else: a wild that has been built, a creature that belongs here in the way that stones belong here, in the way the roots belong here, by having been here long enough that belonging is no longer the right word.

The fish are in the channels. They are carp, mostly, or something that used to be carp before the water changed it—the water that rises from the mountain's heart carries minerals in concentrations that affect color and size and behavior over generations, so that the carp in the highest channels are pale and small and quick, startled by shadows, and the carp in the lowest pools are enormous and dark and slow, their scales thick with mineral deposits that catch the light, their eyes clouded with age or with something in the water that is not quite age. The children throw crumbs to them and the fish rise in slow circles and the water closes over the crumbs and the surface calms and the children stay to watch the surface for as long as they can bear to, as though something might surface that is not a fish.

The trees are not one species. They are dozens, hundreds, a library of wood and leaf that has assembled itself from seeds carried by birds, by wind, by water, by the people themselves who planted things for shade and food and beauty and ended up planting a forest. The fig trees are the oldest and the most structural—they have grown so large that their roots have become load-bearing elements, threading through the concrete in ways that engineers in earlier centuries would have found alarming and engineers in later centuries learned to work with. The fig roots are architecture now. The figs themselves hang heavy and purple in the season that the city calls the deep season, when the rain comes daily and the air is so thick it tastes of green, and the people climb the trees with baskets and fill them and carry the fruit down through the terraces in long processions that are part harvest and part ceremony, though no one would call it a ceremony because it is simply what happens, the way rain is what happens, the way the water rises.

And beneath everything, beneath the people and the birds and the cats and the roots and the humming machines, there is the mountain itself. Not as foundation—the place has forgotten its foundations—but as presence. The stone that remains visible in the pale cliffs and the exposed bones of the terraces is not inert. It has been here so long, has been saturated with so much water and so many roots and so much engineered weather, that it has become participant. Moisture beads on its surface. Roots have worked so deeply into it that the distinction between stone and root is theoretical, a question of language rather than substance. The mountain breathes in its own way—not with air but with water, with mineral seeps, with the slow movement of its own interior, the deep pressures and warmth that send water upward through the copper and iron, the same water, perhaps, that has been rising for centuries, cycling through the city and back into the stone, back into the mountain, which gives it back again, which the city takes and uses and returns, an exchange so old it has the rhythm of breath, the rhythm of something dreaming.

You are standing in a garden court on a middle terrace. The concrete beneath your feet is warm from the sun that broke through an hour ago, and the moss is wet and dark and smells of something older than smell, something mineral and green. A bird is singing a song that is almost a mechanical sound, almost a hum, and below you through the designed gaps you can see the next terrace down, its canopy of trees so thick that the streets beneath are only glimpses—a flash of water, a corner of white wall, a figure walking in the shade with a basket on her head. You cannot reach that figure. The paths that would take you there are not the paths you are on, and the architecture here is not a map but a maze, and the maze does not want to be solved. It wants to be walked, slowly, by someone who has no particular place to be.

A cat crosses your path, large and slow and uninterested in you, its tail thick with moisture, its eyes the pale gold of old glass. It disappears into the roots of a fig tree, and the roots accept it the way they accept everything—without comment, without alteration, the way a mind accepts a thought that has always been waiting to arrive.

The water hums in the channels. Somewhere below, a machine turns. Somewhere above, the clouds are rising from the valley like breath.

You stay.
