The warmth is the first thing that feels like company.

Not a presence exactly — not yet — but a direction. The heat rises from below, and it does not rise evenly. It concentrates. It gathers at fault lines and fissure mouths and the shattered intersections where the plateau's skin has cracked open to show the still-glowing stone beneath. Where the warmth gathers, chemistry accelerates. Compounds that would lie inert in the cold begin to move. Structures that would never form find the conditions they need, briefly, to come into being. The warmth is not alive, but it is the thing that makes aliveness possible, and so it is the first thing you might almost call a presence — the condition that everything here waits within.

The crystalline formations are the closest thing to inhabitants.

They grow near the thermal vents, where heat concentrates most fiercely. They are not beautiful in the way of crystals from wet worlds — no clear geometry, no clean angles. They are blocky, semi-opaque, their surfaces textured with patterns that seem to follow some internal logic: ridges and grooves that align with the heat flow, as though the formations have learned, over their slow centuries of growth, which direction is good. They are warm to the touch, warmer than the surrounding rock, as though they are drawing the heat in and holding it, keeping it close to whatever chemistry happens in their cores. If you pressed your palm to one and waited — and here, waiting is easy, because nothing moves fast — you might feel a faint pulse. Not a heartbeat. Not precisely. But something rhythmic in the warmth, something that suggests the formation is doing more than existing.

These formations cluster. They do not grow alone. Where the heat is strong enough, you will find a dozen of them together, some large as boulders and some no larger than your fist, all oriented the same way: toward the vent, toward the source, as though they are reaching. They do not touch — there is always a gap between them, a space of bare basalt that lets the heat pass through — but they grow toward each other, and sometimes, in the oldest clusters, you will find two that have nearly met, their reaching edges almost but not quite in contact. The gap remains. The heat remains the point. Together they form something that almost looks like intention.

The patterns are the other possibility. The almost-architecture.

The turbulence that shapes this world does not build cleanly. It heaves and subsides, pushes up and lets fall, and in the chaos of its movements there are moments when the fractures align, when the blocks fall into lines, when the heat shimmer over a collapse zone makes the geometry look deliberate. These moments do not last. They are erased by the next uplift, buried under the next emergence, worn into randomness by the simple continuation of becoming. But for an hour — sometimes a day — there is a wall. A corridor. A shape that the geology did not mean to make and cannot maintain, but that exists, briefly, as evidence of something trying.

Some of these patterns are merely chance. The world is large and the turbulence is constant, and given enough time and enough chaos, anything will almost-align with anything else. This is statistics, not meaning. But the ones near the thermal vents are different. The ones near the vents seem to pull toward coherence. The fractures follow the heat flow. The blocks arrange themselves along the lines of warmth. In the shimmer, the pattern holds longer than it should. It feels, for a moment, like the world is making a shape it has seen before — or that something in the world is finally learning what shape it wants to make, and is practicing.

The heat flows are a presence in themselves. Not a creature, not a structure — a movement, a direction, a tendency. The warmth rises because it must, because the world is still cooling from its making, but it rises in channels. It finds paths through the stone. It concentrates where the pressure is right and disperses where the crust has cracked too wide. The mineral veins are the visible part of this movement: the red lines that map themselves across the dark basalt at night, slow rivers of thermal energy following the paths of least resistance. But beneath the visible veins, there are deeper channels — flows that have not yet cracked the surface, that carry heat through the world in networks too large to see. These channels have directions. They have tendencies. They warm the stone above them in patterns that recur, that build, that shape the slow emergence of formations and the spacing of almost-architecture.

And occasionally — this is the part that stays with you after you have left, if you ever leave — the heat does something that is almost unexpected.

A vent will open where no vent has been. A pulse of warmth will come from somewhere new. And for a moment, the formations near the old vents will turn — not physically, but in the orientation of their growth, in the direction their ridges run, in the alignment of their surfaces. They will face the new warmth. They will orient toward it. They will hold their orientation for hours, even after the pulse has faded, as though they are waiting, as though they have learned something from the change.

This may not be recognition. It may be chemistry — the simple fact that growth follows heat, that mineral structures form faster where the warmth is stronger, that the "turning" is only the differential rate of formation on different sides of a crystal. It may be nothing more than physics, the world doing what warm stone does. But the way the formations hold their orientation, the way they seem to be waiting — this sits wrong in a way that suggests the chemistry might have more in it than we know how to name. The warmth is the condition for life here. The formations are what the warmth makes when it meets the stone. And what the formations do with the warmth they draw in — whether they grow or merely persist, whether they know the difference between one heat and another — this is a question that the world is still, slowly, working toward answering.

The air, too, carries its hints.

The sulfurous weight of it, the mineral tang, the something-else that has no name — this is not only the smell of hot rock. It is the smell of chemistry happening. Compounds are forming and breaking in the thin atmosphere, driven by the heat that rises from below. Some of these compounds are simple: sulfur dioxide and hydrogen sulfide, the expected products of a world still cooling from its volcanic making. But there are others — traces, hints, things that the instruments back on the ship would struggle to classify. The air here is not dead. It is reactive. It is the site of processes that have not yet been observed elsewhere, chemistries that do not need water, that build from silicate and sulfide and the slow thermal energy of a world learning what it can become.

In the warm hours — or what passes for warm, which is most hours — the air near the largest vents shimmers with more than heat. There are faint colorations in the shimmer, faint iridescences that come and go, as though the compounds in the air are briefly organizing into something more structured than gas. This may be refraction. It may be nothing. But when the iridescence holds, when it flickers in a pattern that seems almost deliberate, it looks like the air itself is being shaped — is becoming something, briefly, before the chemistry settles back into randomness and the shimmer returns to simple heat.

The world is not empty. It is not silent. It is building.

The uplifts and subsidence continue, the constant peristalsis of a world still becoming, and with each cycle something new emerges and something old is lost. The ridges that stood yesterday are folded back into the source. The crystal growths that took centuries to form are shattered in a collapse and their fragments scattered across the basalt, still warm, still holding their orientation toward heat that no longer exists. What replaces them is new: rock that formed yesterday, formations that began growing in the last hour, patterns that are still forming as you stand there, that will be different by the time you turn around.

This is the presence that lives here, if presence is the right word. Not a creature, not a plant, not a consciousness — but a process. A world learning what it wants to be. A chemistry finding its forms. The warmth that rises and the formations that draw it in and the patterns that almost-achieve intention and the air that shimmers with the weight of compounds not yet named — all of this together, all of this ongoing, is what lives in the high places of a world still being born.

You stand on it, and it is warm beneath your feet. You breathe the mineral air, and it tastes like possibility. You watch the heat shimmer across the dark plain, and you see the almost-patterns hold for a moment longer than they should. And you understand — or begin to understand — that the world is not empty.

It is waiting.
