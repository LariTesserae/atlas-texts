There are creatures here that have forgotten what warmth means. Not because they have never known it—the blood still moves, the muscles still flex—but because the cold has been pressing so long that warmth has become an abstraction, a theoretical state mentioned in older instructions their bodies no longer read. They are built for endurance, for the long migration between moisture and the memory of moisture, and their metabolisms run at a pace the jungle would call sleep.

The herd-beasts move in columns that stretch for kilometers, their many legs finding the same paths their ancestors walked, their bodies low and wide, adapted for the thin air and the vast distance between resources. They are not beautiful in the way of jungle creatures—no bright plumage, no dramatic displays. They are beautiful in their indifference, their willingness to continue, their complete absorption in the pattern that keeps them alive. When they call to each other, the sound is a low vibration that travels through stone as easily as through the cold air, a language shaped by a landscape that learned long ago to distrust sound that doesn't know how to wait.

Smaller things persist in the hollows, where the moisture lingers longest. They are armored against the cold, their exoskeletons thick and grey, designed to hold heat and hold moisture and hold the creature inside against the long dry periods when nothing falls from the sky. They click and rustle in the sparse vegetation, moving with a patience that looks like stillness until you watch long enough to see the distance they have crossed. They are not numerous in the way of jungle insects, but their numbers are sustained by the plateau's slow math, by the mathematics of rare abundance and careful expenditure.

Something wings above, rare and large, riding the thermals that rise from the warm stone below. It does not hunt here—there is nothing quick enough to catch, nothing small enough to be worth the effort of descent. It circles instead, patient as the plateau itself, riding the rising air in long spirals that take hours to complete. Perhaps it remembers something older, a time when this elevation supported more, when the thermals carried the scent of easier prey. Perhaps it simply knows that the air rises and finds this satisfying.

And there are the machines. Not visible—not in the way of engines or structures or any obvious technology. But present, as the hum is present, as the faint vibration in the stone is present, as the sense of purpose that permeates the landscape is present. They are below, or within, or woven into the fabric of the plateau so thoroughly that distinguishing the machine from the stone has become impossible. They tend to something. They continue something. The moisture that gathers in the hollows, the mineral deposits that color the scree, the faint warmth that rises from the deep stone—perhaps these are outputs of processes begun so long ago that no one remembers the purpose, maintained by automated patience that has become indistinguishable from geology.

The creatures that live here do not fear the machines. They have no framework for such fear. They move through the landscape, following the patterns that have been pressed into them by generations of wandering, and the hum is simply part of the air, the vibration simply part of the stone. Perhaps they are even sustained by it—touched by the faint warmth, guided by the faint purpose, their own patterns intertwined with whatever ancient intention still persists in the deep places.

To live here is to be patient. To be sparse. To understand that survival is not a victory but a continuation, not a triumph but a long agreement with the shape of the world. The creatures that persist on the plateau have made this agreement. They have become the plateau's way of persisting, its method of remaining, its answer to the question of what lives in a place that has learned to last. They are not alive in the way of the jungle, with its excess and urgency and dramatic death. They are alive in the way of stone, of water, of the slow processes that shape this high and ancient ground.

They endure. That is the only story they know.
