# The Inhabitants

What lives here does not live quickly. Speed is a surface phenomenon, a thing of light and air and the pressure of open sky. Here, where nothing rises and nothing falls, time has thickened into something that cannot be rushed.

---

## The Sheens

The primary inhabitants are also the oldest. They arrived before the water finished rising, or they evolved in the water as it rose, and they have been here ever since—transforming the built environment into something they can inhabit.

They are microbial, mostly. Films that coat stone in colors the surface world has no name for. Pale greens that lean toward gray. Iridescent patches that shift as you move, revealing hues that exist only in the middle distances between one shade and another. Where the stone is smooth, the films are smooth. Where the stone has texture, the films follow it, emphasizing every ancient ridge and groove, every tool-mark the builders left behind.

They glow. Not brightly—they are not trying to be seen—but enough. Enough to make the corridors readable at night that never comes. Enough to mark the edges of things, to trace the outlines of what was built. The glow is constant and equal, without flicker or pulse, a luminescence that has become inseparable from the architecture itself. To touch the stone is to touch light, to run fingers along a surface that is half-living and half-dead, an organism so vast and so slow that a single human lifetime registers as nothing.

The sheens are colonial. Each patch is a confederation of millions, all metabolizing at the same glacial rate, all dying and being replaced in such slow rotation that the community persists long after any individual has ceased. They do not think—thinking requires a nervous system, requires speed—but they respond. They thicken in some places and thin in others, following gradients no human could perceive, marking the stone with patterns that might be meaningful to something, somewhere, at some timescale.

You cannot see them moving. You will never see them moving. But if you return in a century—if you could, if something of you persisted—you might notice that a patch has crept a few centimeters further along a wall. That the luminous boundary has shifted. That the world has grown, imperceptibly, toward whatever the sheens are growing toward.

---

## The Drifters

Above the sheens, above the stone, in the water itself, there is motion.

The drifters are not organisms so much as states of being. They are the accumulated detritus of a dead world—the organic particles that settled when everything living perished, the gradual rain of microscopic death that has been falling since before the water finished filling this place. Some of them might still be technically alive. Some of them might be something else entirely, something that blurs the line between decay and metabolism, between what was once alive and what has found a way to persist in non-living form.

They move with the currents that move with the centuries. A cloud of them might thicken here, thin there, concentrate in the hollows where the water is stiller. They are the medium itself—particles so numerous that they become indistinguishable from the water, so present that the water itself takes on a faint opacity, a quality of being *full* rather than empty.

In the deep places, where no luminescence penetrates, the drifters are the only light. They catch and hold the faint glow of distant sheens, scattering it, diffusing it, turning the water itself into something luminous. To be here is to be inside a soft, gray, endless dawn that has never known a sun.

The drifters do not eat so much as they accumulate. They gather on surfaces, on each other, on the organisms that came before them. They form layers that become geological in their own right, thin deposits that will outlast the stone if given enough time. They are patient in the way that all things here are patient. They do not struggle or strive or compete. They simply are, and are, and are, until the water finally claims them too.

---

## The Settled Ones

Here and there, in the spaces where the architecture has developed a certain kind of complexity, there are organisms that have found a way to stay.

They cling to the stone. They root in the sheens. They build their small lives on the bodies of larger ones, forming symbiotic layers that approach, but never quite achieve, complexity. A shell might attach itself to a wall, and another shell to that shell, and the remains of both might become substrate for a film, and the film might become home to something smaller still, until the wall itself is a community, a city of the microscopic, a teeming monument to the possibility of life in the absence of almost everything life requires.

The settled ones are varied. There are organisms like corals that have forgotten how to be coral—no symmetry, no branching, just slow expansion across surfaces, filling gaps, smoothing edges, becoming part of the architecture. There are organisms like mosses that cling to stone the way mosses once clung to soil, forming velvets that glow faintly at their edges, marking their territories with light. There are organisms that have no surface names at all, forms that exist only here, that have been shaped by the water and the dark and the endless patience into shapes that would not survive elsewhere.

Some of them are mineral. The water has dissolved so much and redeposited so much that the boundary between organism and environment has become meaningless. A coral-like formation might be twenty percent calcium and eighty percent the remains of creatures that died so long ago they have become indistinguishable from the stone they grow upon. A sheen might be more crystalline than biological, a thin shell of mineral deposits that once contained life and now simply contains the memory of life, the shape it made for itself before it was finished.

These are the ones most likely to persist. Life here has learned that hardness is survival. The soft things dissolve. The slow things endure. The organisms that become stone are the ones that will still be here when the water finally finishes its work and there is nothing left but the smooth, the dark, the absolute.

---

## The Edges

Where the built environment meets the open water—the open water that is still full of structures, still close, still corridor and chamber—there are presences that do not fit the categories.

Things that move. Not the drifting of particles, not the slow growth of settled forms, but *movement*: the traversal of space, the expenditure of energy, the selection of one path over another. They are rare. You might swim for an hour and see nothing. You might swim for a day. But they are here, they have always been here, and their rarity makes them more significant than their numbers.

They are small. Most of them. Things the size of fingers, the size of hands, the size of faces. Things that live in the gaps the built world has developed over millennia—cracks in stone, spaces between settled layers, the miniature caverns where erosion has carved out homes. They are dark or pale, depending on their relationship to the light. They are slow by most standards but fast by the standards of everything else that lives here. They hunt the drifters. They graze the sheens. They are the closest thing to predators this world has ever had, though predator is too strong a word—they do not compete or dominate or control. They simply persist, taking what is available, adding themselves to the chain that has no beginning and no end.

Some of them are blind. Some of them have developed senses that have no names—pressure sensitivity, chemical gradients, the faint electrical fields of living tissue. They navigate by means that would be meaningless to a surface creature, perceiving the world through layers of information no human could process. They are adapted to a degree that suggests a long residence, a slow evolution in a place where evolution itself has slowed to geological pace.

They do not come close to you. You are large and warm (relatively) and strange. They keep their distances, their territories, their careful separations. But they watch. They always watch. And sometimes, in the deep dark, where the glow of the sheens is thin and far between, you might feel the weight of their attention—the faint pressure of something that is aware of you, even if it does not understand what you are.

---

## The Deep Ones

Below. Always below.

In the deepest chambers, where the water is oldest and the sediment is thickest, there are things that have learned to live without light at all. They do not glow. They do not need to. They exist in the absolute dark, in the spaces where even the drifters have settled and gone still, and they have become something that is more mineral than biological, more absence than presence, more idea than organism.

You might encounter them and not know it. They have no shape—or they have shapes so large and so slow that shape becomes meaningless. They are like places themselves, like the stone has developed a kind of awareness, like the architecture has grown nervous tissue in the places where erosion has thinned it thinest. They do not move. They do not reproduce. They simply continue, in the way that stones continue, and the way that water continues, and the way that the slow dark itself continues.

They are not alive, perhaps. They are not dead. They are a third state, a state that exists only here, only in the very bottom of the world where everything has settled into final form. They are what remains when life has exhausted itself and found a way to persist in a form that is no longer life and no longer not-life but something in between, something that the water and the dark have shaped over timescales that make the builders seem like children.

You will not see them. You will not know they are there unless you stay long enough, unless you listen to the silence long enough, unless you learn to feel the spaces that are fuller than they should be, the places where the water holds something more than water.

And then, one day, in the deepest place, you might feel it: a presence, vast and patient and utterly still. A thing that has been here since before the water finished rising. A thing that will be here when the water finishes its work.

You will not know its name. It has none. But you will know that you are not alone, that you have never been alone, that in the very bottom of the world there is something that is aware of you, and that has been aware of you all along.

---

## The World as a Whole

And so it is: a world of accumulations, of layer upon layer of life that has become environment that has become life again. A world where the built and the grown and the settled have collapsed into each other until the categories no longer apply. A world where nothing is born and nothing dies so much as it transforms, where the water is the only constant, where the slow dark is the only horizon.

What lives here lives without hurry. What lives here lives without end. The sheens spread. The drifters drift. The settled ones settle deeper into their slow permanence. The edges persist in their tiny motions, their microscopic hunts, their careful territories.

And the deep ones endure.

This is the end-state: a world that has become its own ecology, its own history, its own continuation. Not empty, never empty, but full in a way that is hard to perceive—a fullness of absence, of potential, of patience. A fullness that does not demand to be seen.

What lives here is patient.

What lives here is enough.
