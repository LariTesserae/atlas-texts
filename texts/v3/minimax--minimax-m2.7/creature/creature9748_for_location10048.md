# Inhabitants of the Weeping Quarter

They are a people shaped by water and compression. The narrowness of their passages has bent them toward a certain crouch, a forward lean as they move between walls. Their hands know the textures here: the cool damp of dressed stone, the splinter-prone grain of timber frames swollen with moisture, the iron of door-rings gone orange with rust and exposure. They have learned to read the water's moods — when it will rise in the morning channels, when it will find new paths through the paving, when the stones will weep their heaviest and the air will thicken with a coolness that finds its way past any cloak.

Their faces carry a particular pallor, not sickly but river-touched, as though the moisture has worked its way beneath the skin. Those who leave speak of a tightness in the chest for weeks afterward, a feeling of having been too open, too exposed to dry air. In the Quarter itself, the damp becomes normal. Bodies adjust. Clothing rots slower than it should here, or perhaps rots in its own way — going soft, going gray, absorbing the salts that rise with the water and deposit themselves on every surface. The residents' garments share this quality: heavy, salt-stiffened, never quite dry.

They are a people who have learned not to look up. The sky is scarce in the Weeping Quarter — glimpsed only in certain angles between buildings, a thin bright thing that seems too far away to be real. The light instead pools where it pools, gathers on certain stones, and the residents have learned which corners to avoid when they do not wish to be seen. This is not hiding, exactly. It is an accommodation. They have become sensitive to the moods of illumination, these people, knowing which doorways darken at noon, which puddles hold reflections of other skies. A few claim the ability to read the water's movements — to know, by the taste of the air, whether the evening will bring a rise or a pause.

Their rituals are small and damp-adapted. Offerings left on thresholds: a pinch of salt, a folded paper, a small stone from a particular quarry. These things disappear, taken by the water or the air or something else. The residents do not speak of where the offerings go. They speak only of the act, the leaving, the small gesture of acknowledgment toward the place that holds them.

Children, when they are born, are introduced to the water early — held to the rising channels at dawn, marked with the black wet that seeps between stones. This is blessing or binding, depending on who you ask. The children grow quiet here, learning the value of narrow spaces, the danger of open sound. Their voices do not carry as others' do. Their feet know the channels' paths as their parents' feet knew them. They play in the puddles that gather in the settlement's lowest corners, and their games involve the small translucent swimmers that no one has named — catching them, releasing them, inventing rules that change each season.

The elders move least. They have earned their stillness through years of learning the place's rhythms, and now they sit in the damp-adapted corners of their homes and let the water's murmur fill the silences between their thoughts. Some of them speak of a memory that is not their own — a sense that the stones carry something, that the fresh fracture-lines of recent erosion have exposed surfaces still young enough to hold the strangeness that once filled this hollow. They do not agree on what that strangeness was. The water, they say, knows. The water remembers.

There are also, perhaps, others.

The light has its residents too — or so the people believe, though they would not use that word. Certain corners of the Weeping Quarter are not empty. Something occupies them, or allows itself to be seen there, or simply is the reason the light pools and gathers rather than passing through. The residents have learned to acknowledge these presences without interaction: a nod toward a doorway that darkens at noon, a murmured word when passing a puddle whose reflections show a sky elsewhere. They do not speak of these things directly. They do not need to. The acknowledgment is inherited, passed from parent to child like the taste of salt on the tongue, like the knowledge of which stones weep and which hold their dryness.

And deeper still, in the water itself — the tiny swimmers, the larvae or not-larvae, the creatures visible only in certain light — there may be something that pays attention. The residents leave offerings small enough to fit in a cupped palm. They have never watched to see what takes them. Some things are better unobserved.

The Weeping Quarter holds all of this: the people who have bent themselves to its shape, the light that has learned to gather in certain places, the water that moves and remembers, the small lives in the channels, the strangeness that has not yet weathered smooth. Its inhabitants do not rule it. They inhabit it, as a body inhabits its own limbs — with constant negotiation, constant accommodation, constant small adjustments that become, over time, a way of being that can only exist here, in this damp-soft, sharp-edged, weeping place where the land still remembers its new shape and the water still rises through the stones at dawn.
