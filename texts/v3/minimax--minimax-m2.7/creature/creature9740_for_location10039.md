What lives here knows how to persist.

The people have the particular posture of those who have learned patience not as a virtue but as a survival strategy—shoulders curved forward against the damp, eyes adapted to the gray, hands that reach for things without looking because looking takes time they cannot spare. They are a people who have made accommodations. Their lungs are acclimated to the copper-tinged fog, their sinuses thick with decades of moisture, their skin perpetually flushed from the damp cold that never quite becomes cold enough to bite but never quite becomes warmth either. They carry umbrellas not as precaution but as habit, fingers worn smooth on the handles. They have learned to sleep through the sounds of the city settling, the groans of foundations adjusting, the particular silence that comes after the machinery goes quiet for a moment before resuming.

They are not unkind. They are simply occupied. They conduct their economies in doorways, trade in small goods and smaller secrets, marry and argue and raise children who will grow into the same postures, the same habits, the same particular relationship with the fog that settles against their lungs like something they swallowed and never quite digested. The children are wiry, quick, adapted early to the uneven streets and the art of walking without looking. They play in courtyards where the stones are slick and the light is always failing, and they learn the city the way children everywhere learn their own bodies: through collision, through repetition, through the slow accumulation of knowing what hurts and what doesn't.

The rats are the city's oldest residents, or near enough. They have forgotten what it was to fear human footsteps. They move through the gutters with the confidence of creatures who have outlasted many empires, their eyes bright and knowing in the fog, their fur thick and occasionally patchy with the particular mange that comes from living close to industry. They are not aggressive, exactly, but they are unaccommodating. They will not move from your path. They expect you to move from theirs, and usually, you do.

The gulls are newcomers by comparison, though they have been here long enough now that their cries have become part of the city's particular silence. They wheel above the rooftops in tight formations, their white bodies stark against the gray, and their voices carry in ways that human voices do not—sharp, demanding, insistent on being heard. They are scavengers, in the way all creatures here are scavengers, finding sustenance in the city's overflow.

And then there are the others. The ones in the river. The ones that move in the drainage channels when the water is low. No one has given them a name, or if they have, the name has been forgotten. They are smooth and dark and they have learned the rhythms of the city's pulse well enough to move when no one is watching. They are patient in ways that the people are not, slow-moving, deliberate, adapted now to the particular chemistry of what runs off from the factories. Some of them are quite large. Most people prefer not to think about how large.

The plants live in defiance. This is their defining characteristic: a stubborn insistence on growth in conditions that should not permit it. The ferns crowd every shaded corner, their fronds heavy with moisture that drips in a kind of perpetual rain. The ivy climbs with a determination that seems almost purposeful, as if the plant has decided that the buildings will not be allowed to fall, and so it binds them together, root by root, tendril by tendril, holding the old structures upright through sheer organic will. The trees in the squares are monuments to adaptation—their bark black with mold, their leaves dark and waxy and perpetually beaded with water, their roots crackling through pavement to find what sustenance they can in the fractured earth below. And the rooftop gardens: these are the work of people who understand that growing things must be insisted upon, that soil here is not given but taken, that the plants that survive are those that have been taught to survive. The flowers are sometimes wrong in ways that take a moment to place—colors that don't quite correspond to known species, petals that catch the light at angles that seem deliberate. But they bloom. They persist.

What else? The buildings themselves, perhaps. Some of the older ones have developed a kind of sentience, or something adjacent to it. Doors that open onto walls, that have always opened onto walls, that the people have long since stopped trying to explain. Windows that look out onto other windows that look out onto windows, an infinite regression of glass and fog. Staircases that arrive somewhere other than where they began. The people navigate these oddities without comment, the way they navigate the rats and the fog and the ground that shifts beneath their feet. It is simply the city. It is simply how things are.

There are shapes in the fog that might be nothing, that probably are nothing. But they are there, at the edges of vision, at the corners of eyes that have learned not to look directly. They move when the light changes. They do not move when addressed. They are part of the city's texture now, woven into its particular strangeness so thoroughly that to remove them would be to remove something essential.

And yet, for all this—for the damp and the instability and the strangeness that accumulates like sediment—they stay. They raise children who will stay. They plant gardens on rooftops and repair cracks in walls and learn the city's rhythms as deeply as they have learned the rhythms of their own breathing. The city is not kind, exactly, and it is not welcoming, and it does not make promises about tomorrow. But it is theirs. They have made it theirs, through the accumulation of ten thousand small acts of habitation, of persistence, of refusal to be anything other than what they are.

This is what lives here: a people who have become inseparable from their place, who have grown into the city's shape so completely that they could no more leave than the ivy could stop climbing, than the river-creatures could stop breathing the particular chemistry of the water, than the fog could stop pooling in the valley each evening like something poured and settling.

They live here. They have always lived here. They will go on living here, in the damp and the gray and the strangeness, because this is what home means when home is a city that hasn't quite finished becoming itself.
