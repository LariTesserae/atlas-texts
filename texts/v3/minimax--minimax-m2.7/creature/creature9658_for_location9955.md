The creatures are always almost something you have seen before.

The schools that turn in the mid-water column — you will call them fish, and for a while they will let you, and then you will notice the way each body segments into three distinct lengths along its spine, or the way the leading edge of each fin is not membrane but a bristling array of fine, translucent filaments that trail behind like threads of silk, and you will stop calling them fish. They are something that learned the same solution from a different direction, a world away from anything that ever swam under sun. You will watch them pivot, ten thousand bodies tilting at once, and you will feel the displacement in your chest — a pressure shift, subtle and massive, the whole water column reorganizing itself around an absence, around the shape of a place they have chosen not to go. They do not flee. They simply agree, collectively, not to enter a space. This is not the same thing as fear. It may be older than fear.

Among the basalt columns where the mats grow thickest, the smaller forms are easiest to lose. They are the size of your thumbnail and they move in patterns that have the rhythms of language — not speech, not signal, but something closer to the way a phrase has a shape, a rising and a falling, a local density that resolves and recurs. You will not understand it. No one would. But you will feel it as a structure, a scaffolding of intention even if you cannot name the intention. Some of them are bioluminescent, but not in the way you expect. They do not glow steadily. They pulse in pairs — a faint blue-green light that catches for a moment and then goes dark, and then another pair, a few meters away, and the light travels through the swarm like a thought being thought, a wave of activation rippling through ten thousand small bodies in a direction that seems to mean something. You will watch this for a long time. You will not figure out what it means. You will begin to suspect it means nothing, or that it means everything, and that the distinction stops mattering, deep in the basin, where meaning and function become the same word spoken in different registers.

The pale mats deserve their own language. They are not plant, not bacterial, not fungal — or they are all three, a living membrane that shifts composition from zone to zone across the submerged stone. Where the water moves fastest, near the inflows where rain spills over the rim in braided threads, the mat is thinner, more translucent, threaded with veins of copper-green that carry something — nutrients, or electrons, or information — in slow, visible rivers. Where the water stills, near the deeps, the mat thickens into something you might almost call flesh, a pale and glistening surface that yields when a filter-feeder pushes through it, closing slowly behind like skin remembering a wound. You will put your hand in it once. The temperature is the same as the water — eight degrees, always eight degrees — but the texture is not. It is warm, faintly, almost imperceptibly warm, and it grips your fingers with a gentleness that is not passive. You will pull your hand back. It will take longer than you expect.

The filter-feeders are the most difficult to look away from. They move on six jointed limbs — you will count them, you will count them again, and still you will feel the wrongness of the number, the way six feels like a lie your hands are telling, because the body attached to those limbs is long and sinuous and segmented into perhaps forty visible portions, each of which could, in the right light, be mistaken for a separate organism. They feed by extending one limb at a time in long, deliberate sweeps, and the motion is patient, methodical, something you might call agricultural if the word did not feel so presumptuous. They do not strain the water so much as parse it, separating the dense from the thin with an efficiency that borders on the surgical. Behind them, the mat is not disturbed. It has been fed, or groomed, or marked. You cannot tell which. You begin to suspect these are not distinct categories here.

The predators are easier to describe and harder to hold in the mind. They are long — a meter, sometimes two — and their carapaces are not shells but something more like armor plate, dark and mineralized, ridged in patterns that cast micro-shadows across the water when they pass through the light. Their clicking is not random. It is directional. When one turns its head — and it is a head, undeniably, with what might be sensory pits arranged in rows — the click sharpens, reflects, and returns, and you understand that you are listening to a sonar, a language of pressure waves that maps the water in real time and reads the shapes of everything in it. Including you. You are a shape in that map now, and you feel the mapping as a kind of exposure, a moment when the distance between you and the basin decreases, and then the predator moves on, following some other signal, and you are again only a warm, dense thing drifting through a world that is deciding, slowly and without urgency, what to do with you.

There are larger things.

You feel them before you see them, if they are things at all — a displacement, a darkening, a shift in the clicking itself as the basin's dominant frequencies change, as if a different instrument has entered the orchestra and the rest of the musicians are adjusting. They are not always visible. They may be deep, below the reach of the gray-green light, or they may be slow, so slow that you mistake their movement for the movement of water, for the long undulation of currents against new stone. But the water carries their signature. The mats near their passage are flattened, pressed into the stone like grass underfoot. The filter-feeders in those zones extend their limbs less frequently, less urgently, a slow pulsing that might be resting or might be something else entirely. And the clicking in those zones has a new layer — a low, resonant thrum that you feel in your molars, that you feel in the thin bone behind your ear, that you feel in the slow pulse of your own blood being pushed through vessels that have not yet understood how to be afraid.

Nothing in the basin is afraid of you.

This is the thing you will keep returning to. Every creature you see is aware of you — the pressure-sense, the chemical traces, the returning echoes of their own sound — and none of them responds with anything that resembles fear. They do not flee. They do not hide. The schools part around you or do not part, according to some calculation you cannot follow. The filter-feeders continue their slow sweeps at distances that should make them startle and do not. The predators click once, twice, and continue their patrol, their sensory pits oriented toward you with what you might call curiosity if you were willing to anthropomorphize, or what you might call indifference if you were not paying attention, and what you eventually settle on calling something for which you do not have a word, something that is neither curious nor indifferent but simply complete — a knowing that has already factored you in, already accounted for your presence in whatever vast, slow system of calculation this place runs on. You are known. You are not feared. You are not welcome. You are something else, something the basin is processing the way it processes everything — slowly, thoroughly, without urgency, across a duration that will outlast your presence by so much that your presence is already negligible, already folded into the pattern, already a thread in the mat, a trace in the water, a faint warmth dissolving into the eight-degree dark.

The rain above is the one thing that does not belong to the basin.

It falls into the water and joins it, and the boundary dissolves, and the basin grows by one measureless increment, and the rain continues, and the water holds its temperature, and the stone holds its shape, and the creatures continue their slow industry, and the clicking goes on, and you hang in the water with your lungs full of air that tastes of mineral and rain, and you understand — not with your mind but with your body, with the part of you that is still water, still stone, still the soft animal thing that learned to call this world home before it learned to call anything else — you understand that you are inside something. Not an ecosystem. Not a place. Something with the weight of intention, with the patience of geology, with the slow, relentless turning of its attention, and that something is looking at you the way all living things look at light: steadily, hungrily, and without any expectation of an answer.
