The grazers came first in the morning, their silhouettes appearing over the rim of a dry channel like something surfacing. They moved in loose groups of three or four, though 'group' was not quite the word — they were simply going the same direction, acknowledging each other with the low tolerance of animals that share a world rather than a purpose. They were tall at the shoulder, with legs longer than they needed to be and a body held at an angle that suggested perpetual readiness, as though they might need to run from something that had not yet arrived. Their coats were the color of the stone: pale, dust-colored, marked with darker lines along the spine and legs that might have been shadow or might have been pattern. They were not afraid. Fear required a history of predation close enough to have taught it, and these animals had been alive long enough to have learned only the mathematics of distance — how far to stand from the water's edge, how far to let another body come before the conversation became something else. They moved with a slowness that was not lethargy. It was the pace of creatures who had calculated the world and found it sufficient.

The grazers knew the springs the way memory knows a face: not as information but as recognition. Each animal carried a private map of the oasis-patches, updated seasonally by the slow drift of water through stone. They moved between them in arcs that varied by season, by the age of the animal, by the particular calculus each body had learned. The young ran the tighter circuits, testing the boundaries, feeling the pull of each water-site like a string pulled taut. The old moved in wider loops, conserving, taking the longer paths because the shorter ones had begun to feel too exposed. The distance between patches was not a problem to be solved — it was simply the shape of the world, and they fit it the way water fits a vessel.

Their food was the scrub that clustered at the edges of the springs: the thorny shrubs with their yellow flowers, bitter and tough, and the coarser grasses that grew where enough dust had gathered. The grazers did not eat much. They did not need to. The sparse vegetation held what it held, and the animals took it in small portions, moving as they fed, never staying long enough to deplete. This was not restraint. It was simply what happened when hunger was mild and the next meal was always a known distance away.

The predators moved differently. They were low and wide, built for the long game rather than the sprint. Their bodies were dense with muscle in a way that suggested not strength but stamina — the kind of force that could be applied and reapplied over kilometers, until the mathematics of pursuit collapsed in favor of the mathematics of capture. They were the color of old bone, nearly white, with eyes set wide on the head for the peripheral vision that mattered when you were tracking something that moved in every direction. They did not hunt in packs. The territory was too large, the resources too scattered, for the economy of cooperation. Each animal held its own ground, a circuit measured not in kilometers but in the positions of the water-patches and the spaces between.

The predators knew the grazers' patterns the way the grazers knew the water. They knew which channels the herds preferred, which patches of scrub offered cover for the slow approach, which ridgelines allowed a view long enough to track a group's movement for hours. They did not ambush, exactly. They persistence-hunted, following at the distances that mattered — close enough to maintain contact, far enough to avoid alarm. The grazers knew they were there, and the grazers did not run. Running was expensive, and the predators were not yet close enough to make it worth the cost. The negotiation happened in distance, in the slow adjustment of positions, in the moments when a grazer would lift its head and watch and the predator would slow and the world would hold its breath before resuming its ordinary pace. Sometimes the negotiation ended in the mathematics of exhaustion. Sometimes it ended in the mathematics of patience: the predator turning away, moving to a position where the next interaction might go differently.

The insects were everywhere in the heat. They rode the thermal columns above the scrub patches, spiraling up on the rising air, too small to see clearly against the washed sky but present — a shimmer, a disturbance in the stillness that resolved into motion when attention was paid. They were not nuisance insects, not the kind that bit or swarmed. They were the kind that simply were: the machinery of the plateau, moving pollen and decay and small lives from one patch to the next. Their presence was most noticeable at dusk, when the air cooled and the thermals collapsed and they descended in slow spirals back to ground. Then the scrub patches would seem briefly alive with their motion, a hum that was not quite sound, before they settled and the night settled with them.

The vultures were less a presence than a fact. They held still on the air the way held still is its own kind of motion — banking in the faintest thermals, wings spread wide and motionless, heads turning with a patient attention that made them feel watched. They did not hunt. They waited for the others to finish, for the mathematics of pursuit and capture to resolve into a result worth following. They knew the predators the way the predators knew the grazers: they watched them, tracked their movements, followed at the distances that indicated a meal might be coming. The vultures were patient in a way that felt ancient. They had been doing this longer than anything else on the plateau, and they carried that history in the stillness of their flight.

Between the animals, between the water-patches, the plateau held itself. The stone held the channels where water had been, the memory of flow preserved in the braided shapes that no rain would fill again. The dust moved in the wind, accumulating in the low places, building soil in the places where soil could hold. The thin soil gathered what the air carried: spores, seeds, the detritus of lives that had passed through. The yellow-flowered shrubs grew from this accumulation, stubborn and thorny, taking what the rock allowed and giving back what the grazers needed. The trees grew less frequently, and when they grew they grew alone — wide-crowned and deep-rooted, a presence that mattered more than its size suggested. The trees were old. Some of them had been old when the structures arrived, or when the structures were already old. Their bark was pale, their wood was dense, and their roots went down into the stone in ways that made the plateau feel less like a surface and more like a beginning.

The structures were not alive, but they were not empty either. The dark glass pillars hummed with a frequency that lived below hearing, in the place where sound became sensation — a vibration in the teeth, in the bones of the chest, in the inner ear where balance lives. The pillars stood in shallow depressions worn into the stone, as though they had settled into place over time, as though the plateau had made room for them. Their surfaces were iridescent in the way that oil on water is iridescent, shifting as the light shifted, holding colors that were not quite in the spectrum the eyes expected. The pillars did nothing visible. They simply stood, and hummed, and were.

The low walls followed no geometry the mind could follow — not circle, not line, not any shape that had a name. They were built from local stone, cream and rose-gray like the plateau itself, but fitted together with a precision that made them feel placed rather than assembled. The joints were seamless. The walls were low enough to step over, high enough to notice, and their paths curved in ways that led nowhere and everywhere. Some of the walls enclosed nothing. Some enclosed springs, or scrub patches, or the space where nothing grew. The enclosures were not traps. They were simply — contained. Held. The walls remembered a purpose that had worn away, but the holding remained.

The basin beside the largest spring was the most unsettling of the structures because it was the most finished. It was carved from a single piece of something that resisted classification: not stone, not metal, not any material the plateau offered. It was heavy, dense, cool to the touch even in the warm air. Its inner surface was smooth except for the patterns too fine to see — lines and whorls and geometries that emerged only when the light hit them at the exact angle that the plateau's noon sun hit nothing else. The patterns moved when the light moved, or seemed to, as though they were looking for something. The basin held water that seeped up from below, clear and cold and tasting of nothing, and it held the light when the sun dropped low, and it held the eye in a way that was difficult to break.

In the evening, when the amber light came, the structures that could glow did glow. The pillars flickered with a light too faint to see by, a pulse that matched the hum that lived below hearing. The basin caught the last of the sun and held it, reflecting it back transformed. The walls threw no shadows because there was no light left to cast them, but the sense of them remained — a presence, a holding. The glow was not for anyone. It was simply what happened when the plateau entered its other state, the state where presence was enough.

The strange zones in the eastern section were avoided. The animals knew this without knowing it: a knowledge in the body, in the way the grazers' paths curved and the predators' territories ended short. The haze that gathered on certain mornings was thin, barely visible, but the animals felt it the way weather is felt — in the way the air changed against the skin, in the way the light bent in directions it should not. In those zones, the channels seemed deeper than they were, and the shadows pointed wrong, and the silence was a different kind of silence — the kind that held something instead of nothing. The springs there were still, not seeping, and the scrub that grew near them was paler, thinner, as though something was drawing from it at a rate it could not sustain. The structures in those zones did not glow at dusk. They absorbed light instead, holding it and not giving it back.

The pulse came from below, or seemed to. It was felt more than heard — a rhythm that lived in the stone itself, in the deep places where warmth might or might not be coming from a source. It was slow, slower than a heartbeat, the pace of something vast and patient. The animals did not react to it. It had always been there, or had been there long enough that nothing alive remembered its beginning. The pulse was the plateau's vital sign, perhaps, or the structures' communication, or something older than either. It asked nothing. It simply continued, and would continue, and the plateau would erode around it, and the stars would turn above it, and the pulse would remain — unhurried, unanswered, present.

What lived here was the plateau itself, and everything it held: the grazers and the predators and the insects and the birds, the shrubs and the trees and the dust, the water in its hidden courses, the structures in their patient waiting. What lived here was also the attention — the quality of presence that the plateau offered, the sense of being in a place that knew you were there and did not mind. The plateau did not watch the way a predator watched. It watched the way stone watches, which was not watching at all but was not not-watching either. It was the watching of something that had been still long enough to have become a kind of witness, that held the shape of everything that moved across it without moving itself. The grazers moved through it. The predators moved through it. The structures stood in it, and the pulse rose through it, and the light fell on it and left and came back and left again.

And there were others, sometimes — the ones who came from the river basin below, climbing the long approach that the plateau had not made easy. They were few, and they did not stay long. They came with water and little else, following paths that the plateau did not offer, seeking the structures or the springs or simply the view. They walked the way the grazers walked: slowly, carefully, attending to the distances. They touched the pillars and felt the hum and did not understand it. They looked into the basin and saw the fine patterns and could not read them. They slept under the wide trees and woke before dawn and stood in the pale light and felt the plateau watching them, and they left before the heat of midday, and they did not come back.

The plateau did not miss them. It did not remember them. It held the shape of their passage for a while — a footprint in thin soil, a disturbance in dust — and then the wind moved, and the shape was gone, and the plateau was as it had been: present, patient, watching in whatever way stone watches. The structures hummed. The pulse rose. The water seeped where it seeped, and the grazers moved between the patches, and the predators followed at the distances that mattered, and the plateau continued: not stable, not unstable, simply ongoing, the way the world is ongoing when no one is there to witness it differently.
