There is a frog here — many frogs, actually, several species whose calls layer over one another in the warm hours, whose choruses rise and fall like breath. When the afternoon heat reaches its peak and the humidity seems to thicken to something you could almost hold in your hands, they fall silent for a time, and in that silence you become aware of the insects: a constant, airless hum that seems less like sound than like the texture of the place itself, the vibration of all that small life going about its business. Then the frogs resume, and the birds begin their evening stirrings, and the layered noise of the basin becomes itself again — not loud, not quiet, only present, the way a room full of quiet people is full.

The birds are modest in their movements, modest in their numbers. Not the great murmurations of more dramatic places — not starlings or passenger pigeons or the dense clouds that wheel above marshes and coasts. Here the birds move in small, loose groups through the scattered trees: perhaps a family of warblers working the branches, their sharp chips barely audible above the other sounds, or a line of egrets moving through the wetter patches in that slow, deliberate way egrets have, as if they are thinking about each step before committing to it. Doves come and go, their soft cooing a constant undertone. Herons visit the deeper pools at dawn, standing with the patience of the landscape itself, their stillness broken only by the violent swiftness of a strike.

The turtles come out to warm themselves on the edges of the shallowest pools — snappers and painted turtles, their shells dark with algae, their eyes alert in their ancient, patient way. They slide into the water at the slightest disturbance, but if you are still enough, they forget you and return to their basking. There is a water snake I imagine here, striped and unhurried, moving through the vegetation at the pool edges with the confidence of a creature that has never needed to fear very much. It moves like the water itself — like the seepage, like the whole slow system — without urgency, without hurry.

In the evenings, something larger passes through. Not often, not daily, but the tracks are there in the soft soil, and sometimes, if you sit long enough in the blue hour before true dark, you might see it: a deer perhaps, or a feral pig, or something that has adapted its movements to the flatness and the cover. It crosses the basin slowly, pausing at the pools, its ears swiveling at the small sounds of the chorus that never quite stops. It drinks, and moves on, and the sounds resume behind it, and the darkness comes down like a gentle hand.

Everything that lives here is small, or at least modest. The trees are not impressive. The pools are not dramatic. Even the sounds — the frogs, the insects, the birds — are small individually, only abundant in aggregate. But that abundance is the point, the lesson of this place: that life fills what can be filled, that even the most ancient, patient landscape hosts its full measure of living things, each of them doing what living things do, each of them passing through its own small life in the time it takes the sun to move a little way across the sky. The frogs sing. The insects hum. The birds move through in their loose, murmuring groups, and the turtles bask, and somewhere in the warm dark something is hunting or resting or simply enduring.

This is what the land holds: not the dramatic concentrations of the fertile delta or the wilderness, not the spectacle of migration, but the steady, widespread presence of creatures who have found their place here and are keeping it, who need nothing more than what the basin offers and who take it without flourish, without drama, in the same unhurried spirit with which the land itself endures.
