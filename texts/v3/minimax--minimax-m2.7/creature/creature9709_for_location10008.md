The light finds them, mostly. Not the fauna — they move in the shadows at the edges, quick and dark and gone before you can name them — but the air-plants, the rootless clusters, they are creatures of the glow. They have learned to live where the luminescence meets the weeping, where the stone releases its moisture and the surfaces release their cold light, and they have grown accordingly: thin, translucent, their structures more membrane than stem. Press a fingertip to one and it does not feel like plant matter. It feels like skin. It feels like something that might flinch.

They do not bloom in any season you would recognize. They cycle on their own time, their own indicators — perhaps mineral concentration in the condensation, perhaps the angle of the light as the embedded fields pulse through their patterns. When they cycle, the clusters do not flower. They *clear*. The pale tissue becomes suddenly, startlingly transparent, and for a day or two you can see through them to the stone behind, to the faint movement of water in the channels beneath. Then the opacity returns, slowly, as if they are refilling with whatever they need from the air. The fauna notices this too. They come to drink during the clears, when the plants are at their thinnest, as if the pooling water below tastes different then, or as if they are drawn to the vulnerability, the brief nakedness of the place.

The small warm-blooded things — you hear them most in the deep hours, or what pass for hours here, when the field pulses slow and the water cycles toward its low ebb. They are the color of the stone in motion, the dark grey of vesicular basalt given briefly animation. Their bodies are dense and quick, limbs adapted for grip rather than stride, and their movement through the chamber is less walking than flowing, one surface to the next, following the channels and conduits like roads they know by heart. They are silent when they move, but they are not silent when they rest. They make a sound at dusk and at the deep hour: a thin, high vibration, barely above the resonance of the air itself. It is not song. It is not communication, or not communication as you would name it. It is the sound of a metabolism running too hot in a place that should not require heat, and the sound carries further than it should, and it is answered. Not by another of their kind — by the walls. By the conduits. By the slow response of the luminescent compounds as the fields shift almost imperceptibly to match the frequency.

You are not the first to enter this place. They know this. The way they keep their distance is not fear, exactly — it is something closer to assessment. They have seen things enter before, and they have watched those things either leave or be absorbed, and they have incorporated this knowledge into their own behavior, into the particular distance they keep from any warmth that breathes oxygen and moves with purpose. They drink from the channels with their bodies pressed flat against the stone, minimizing the surface exposed to the air, as if they know that exposure is what the place notices. As if they have learned, over generations, to be small.

What they eat is still unclear. They do not feed on the air-plants, not directly — you have watched them pass through clusters without pausing, without the quick glance and turn that would indicate interest or rejection. Perhaps they eat what the water carries, the mineral traces and the microscopic life in the pools, filtered through structures too small to see. Perhaps they eat the light itself, in some fashion you lack the biology to name. Perhaps they eat the resonance, the signal in the air, drawing energy from frequencies that pass through stone and flesh alike. The second and third possibilities feel less like speculation and more like observation, the longer you spend here. They vibrate at a frequency that is not their own. They are calibrated, somehow, to the pulse of the chamber itself.

The pools are not empty. You have seen what lives in them only once, and imperfectly: something thin and slow and very large, moving through the water in the basin-shaped floor like a thought moving through a mind. It was there, and then it was not, and the water closed over its passage with a smoothness that suggested depth, that suggested it had gone somewhere you could not follow. The mineral traces on the stone around the pools are older than the fauna, older perhaps than the erosion you can see. They record the presence of something that came before, that perhaps still comes, that uses the chamber the way a body uses a bloodstream — quickly, invisibly, with purpose distributed across the whole.

The built elements are not built in any sense you understand. They grow, or they accumulate, or they are grown and accumulated by processes that have no operator. The dark glass platforms adjust their surfaces in response to weight, to temperature, to the mineral content of the water that sometimes sprays across them in the stronger flows. They are hospitable, these platforms. The fauna nests on them, sometimes, when the conditions are right — when the humidity is high and the light dims to something that feels almost like night. They bring things to the platforms: water, minerals, the shed membranes of the air-plants after the clears. They arrange these things in patterns that are not random and not ritual and not yet architecture. They are, perhaps, offering something. They are, perhaps, feeding something. The platforms grow slightly after these offerings, or rearrange, or simply hold still in a way that suggests satisfaction. The difference between technology and organism has worn smooth here, just as the stone has worn smooth, just as the water has worn its channels into the floor. What lives here is the boundary between the two, and it has become a place where the distinction no longer matters.
