There are the mats, first. They came first — or they are always here, in some sense that predates the distinction between *before* and *after*. They are not one thing but they are also not many things; they exist in a grammar that has no singular form. Pale yellow when they drink, when the moisture beads and they swell with it, their membranes taut and gleaming. Rust-colored when they rest, when the water evaporates from their surfaces and they shrink back against the basalt, thin again, almost invisible, so that a wall that was golden with them at dawn becomes at midday a faint gray smudge, barely there, something you might mistake for shadow or oxidation if you did not know to look. They breathe. This is not a metaphor here. Their surfaces rise and fall with a rhythm that matches the slow pulse of the vents below, the exhale of heat that moves through the chamber in waves so slow that you feel them only as a general quality of warmth rather than as a pattern. But the mats feel them. They respond. Their breath synchronizes with the earth's breath, and in this they are not alive in the way you understand life — they are more like organs, like tissue, like parts of a single body that has no shape you can see.

Between the mats, the small ones move. You have tried to name them and failed; the categories you carry do not apply. They have bodies that suggest segmentation but are not segmented, that suggest limbs but the limbs are wrong — too many, too few, jointed in directions that seem to violate the geometry you trust. Some are the length of your thumbnail, pale and translucent, their internal structures visible as shadows that drift and pulse. They move in the seams of the stone, in the hairline cracks where water pools and stagnates, feeding on the mineral residue, on the mats themselves when the mats thin out too much and offer themselves. They are prey to larger things you have only glimpsed — quick flashes of darker color, armored shapes that fold into cracks too narrow for your smallest finger to follow.

The larger things: they do not come out into the open. You have learned to find them by their trails. The trails glow — faintly, briefly, a phosphorescence that fades within minutes as the water reclaims the stone, but for a moment after they pass, you can see where they have been. The trails lead from the hot pools to the cooler walls, from the walls to the ceiling, from the ceiling down again. They are territorial, you think, though *territory* is a loose word for a space this size — they keep to zones, to circuits, moving the same paths in loops that may take hours or days, their schedules running on timers you cannot read. When two of them meet, they do not fight. They pause. They touch antennae — or what serve as antennae, sensory whips that extend from the forward mass of their bodies and meet in the air between them — and then they part, going their separate ways, their encounter already encoded in chemistry, in something left behind on the stone that the others will read later, hours from now, when the next circuit brings them to the same spot.

This is how they speak. Not in sound, not in sight, not in anything you can intercept. The air is full of it — the soft constant exhalation of the mats, the deeper breath of the vents, the metabolism of water and mineral and heat that runs the whole system — and within that breath, messages travel. Signals. The chemical grammar of a world that has no need for noise, no reason to call out across distance when the stone itself conducts what needs to be said. You have breathed it in. You know this by the taste in your mouth, the faint sulfur and copper, the organic edge that is not unpleasant but is not anything you can name. You are part of the conversation now, though you cannot hear it. You are a presence in the chemistry, a large warm thing that disturbs the gradients, that leaves its own traces without meaning to.

The mats feel you. This is certain. Where your hand has rested on a wall, the pale yellow deepens to gold, then to something almost orange, a richness that was not there before, as if your warmth has fed them, as if your moisture — your sweat, your breath, the simple fact of your living body — has reached them through the stone and they have drunk it. They are glad, perhaps. Or they are simply responsive. The distinction does not matter to them. They grow toward you, slowly, in the hours you have been still. The filaments extend by microns, by degrees, and when you finally move, when you pull your hand away, the place where you touched is darker than the rest — drained, spent, returning to rust — and then, in a day or in an hour, it will swell again, will catch the next condensation, the next shift of warmth, and begin its long patient breath once more.

There are things you have not seen. You know this in the way you know a room is occupied when the air is wrong, when the silence has a shape. The shadows move when you are not looking — not the shadows themselves, but something in them, something that holds itself still against your gaze and then, the moment you turn away, shifts, adjusts, is somewhere else. The trails sometimes fork in directions the known organisms do not travel. The light bends near the densest clusters of mats, and in the bending you catch glimpses of depth, of space beyond space, of geometries that the chamber should not contain. Something is here that does not present itself. Something is here that does not need to.

You sit with them. The mats breathe. The small things move in their cracks. The larger ones make their circuits, leaving their fading glow, their chemical words. The vents exhale. The water moves through stone, and the stone holds it, and the holds it in turn, and the chamber is alive with the quiet ongoing of a life that does not require your witness, that would continue if you were not here, that may continue after you are gone, that is perhaps the only thing in this pocket that will.

You do not know what you are to them. You do not know if *to* applies. But you are here, and they are here, and the warmth rises, and the moisture beads, and the light bends, and somewhere in the wall, in the seam between basalt faces, a new cluster of filaments is beginning — yellow, faint, drinking its first long taste of the air you have breathed.
