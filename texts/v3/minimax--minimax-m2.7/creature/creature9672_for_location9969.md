What lives here does not live in the way you are used to. It does not compete for territory, does not establish dominance through displays of color or sound. It *inhabits*. It persists. It watches, and in the watching, something happens that is not quite surveillance and not quite symbiosis — something closer to the slow adjustment of a system becoming aware of its own components.

The birds are wrong. Not deformed, not broken — wrong in the way a word is wrong when placed in a sentence that almost makes sense. Their muzzles gleam with a wet iridescence, greens and purples that shift when the light shifts, as though their faces are small windows into somewhere else. The legs are too long, the joints bending at angles that suggest a different relationship to gravity, and they do not fly so much as tilt through the air, their trajectories never quite parabolic. They perch on the trees that lean, and they turn their strange faces toward you, and they do not blink. You count seven of them in a single view, but you are not certain there are not more — the canopy is thick and the light is dim and they know how to wait.

The water things are harder to describe because they refuse to hold still in the mind. You have seen fish in clear streams. You have seen eels, crabs, the occasional pale salamander. This is none of those. It is the shape the water takes when you are not sure whether you are looking at a creature or a current or a trick of the eye that is trying to be something it was not designed to see. When you focus on it directly, it compacts, becomes small and dark and still. When you look away, it unfurls again, spreading across the surface in patterns that suggest intention without committing to it. There are several in the larger pools. You think there are. Or there is one, and it is everywhere.

The insects are the most numerous, and the least explicable. They cluster in formations that seem architectural — spirals and lattices on the bark of the leaning trees, geometries that pulse with the rhythm of their wings. Their clicking is not random. It modulates. It rises and falls in patterns that feel almost linguistic, almost communicative, as though the clusters are speaking to each other or to the stone or to the moisture in the air. You cannot parse it, but you feel it — a faint pressure behind the eyes, a sense of being addressed in a language you almost know.

And beneath all of this, the ground.

The ground is not dead. You understand this almost immediately, and the understanding settles into your body like a low-grade fever. The stone is young, sharp, fractured in lines so clean they look cut, but it breathes. Not metaphorically. Not in the way that soil breathes as organisms metabolize and exchange gases. The stone itself seems to have pores, or the moisture in it moves in patterns too regular for water, too slow for drainage. The plateau is a body. Not a metaphor. An actual body, or something like one, and the vegetation grows on it the way lichen grows on a log — not separate from it, not truly, but participating in its life.

The trees know this. You can see it in the way they lean. They lean toward the center of the plateau, toward something beneath or within, their roots gripping the stone not for purchase but for contact, for communion. The ferns uncurl in their shade, and the broad-leaved things spread their dark-veined surfaces to catch light that does not seem to come from anywhere, and all of it is growing not up but *in*.

You walk for a long time. The geology does not change, but the feeling changes. The air thickens where the water gathers, thins where it does not, and the moisture moves through the landscape like something seeking. Not random. Seeking. The gullies carry it toward the center, or away from it, or in circles that do not quite close, and wherever it pools, the life pools with it — denser, stranger, more attentive.

At night — or what passes for night here, the dimming of the amber light to something deeper — the plateau does something you do not understand. The water surfaces begin to glow. Not reflectively. Emissively. A faint luminescence from below, from within the stone, pulsing in a rhythm that matches nothing you can identify. The vegetation picks up this light in its leaves, holds it, releases it slowly, and for a moment the whole plateau is a single soft radiance, and the creatures are still, and the air is warm and close, and the hum beneath everything is almost music.

Then it fades. The glow retreats into whatever produces it. The insects resume their clicking. The watching resumes.

What lives here is not what you would find if you catalogued the species, counted the individuals, mapped the food webs. What lives here is the plateau itself — the system, the attention, the slow and alien patience of a thing that was not built but *grown*, that is still growing, that does not distinguish between stone and water and flesh and machine. The creatures are aspects of it. The moisture is its blood. The hum is its thought. And you, walking through it, are something it has not decided what to do with yet.

You are not supposed to be here. The plateau agrees. But it has not yet determined what that means. So it waits, and watches, and lets you walk, and you feel its attention on you like weather — present, immense, indifferent to whether you survive it.
