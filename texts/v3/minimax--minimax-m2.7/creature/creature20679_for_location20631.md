What lives here is patient and vertical. The people move in rhythms older than memory—up steps worn smooth by generations, through corridors that open onto sudden drops, along streets that switchback like the mountain itself once did before the city flattened its logic. They carry baskets and bags, tools and children, the small cargo of daily survival. They know the water's routes. They know which stairwells flood and which channels sing. They have inherited knowledge so deeply encoded that it feels like instinct: when the rain comes, shelter; when the mist clears, work. The city does not permit idleness, but it permits pause. There are ledges and alcoves throughout the structure, places where the stone has weathered into seats, where a person can stand and watch water sheet down a wall and feel the city breathing beneath their feet.

The people are themselves a kind of vegetation—layered, dense, integrated. They were born into the verticality, or they arrived and never left, or they came for a reason that became a lifetime. They range from the pale-skinned inhabitants of the upper quarters, where light barely reaches and the air is thinnest, to the soot-darkened workers of the lower tiers, where steam rises and industry runs without rest. Between these extremes exists a spectrum of adaptation so gradual that no line divides one quarter from the next. The body changes here. Lungs thicken. Legs strengthen. Gait adjusts to slopes that never level. People from the high towers develop a particular kind of stillness, a patience for dim light and long ascents. Those from below learn to navigate by sound and heat, reading the city's machines as others read the sky.

The plants are the most numerous inhabitants. They arrived as intention and became something else—interference, collaboration, symbiosis. The city's original architects planted green in calculated amounts, designing terraces and root systems to stabilize the soil that remained between buildings. But plants do not honor calculations. A root finds a crack and follows it. A seed lodges in a joint and germinates. Over generations, what was planted has become indistinguishable from what has invaded. Moss colonizes every surface where moisture collects. Vines trace the iron bones of aqueducts and bridges, thickening until their weight becomes structural concern. Trees stand in courtyards where they should not fit, their trunks compressed by walls on either side, their canopy spreading across gaps that were once open to sky. The green does not threaten the stone; it has become part of it. To remove the plants would be to remove the city's skin.

In the lower quarters, where moisture and warmth collude, the vegetation is thickest and strangest. Ferns cascade from platforms that once served factories. Mushrooms push through floor grates. Lichens map the stones in pale continents. Here, the boundary between built and grown has dissolved entirely. Walls are forests. Staircases are root systems. The city has become a kind of organism itself, its metabolism conducted in water and chlorophyll, its respiration performed by leaves that have never been planted but will not leave.

The water lives too—not as a creature but as an agent, a force that moves through the city's body like blood through veins. It enters as rain and is caught, channeled, divided. Some flows into cisterns and waits. Some runs through pipes to fountains and basins where people gather. Some seeps downward through stone and concrete, feeding roots, staining surfaces, finding its own routes through the architecture's seams. The city's relationship to water is ancient and intimate; the infrastructure has evolved alongside the need, so that now the pipes and channels are as natural as the rock they replaced. Water carries minerals that leave marks on everything—white lines on stone, green-black stains on metal, the particular coloration that gives the city its aged quality. Everything here bears the signature of water's passage.

Machines are the city's other life. They are not alive—they were never alive—but they behave. They operate. They maintain the conditions that permit everything else to continue. The engines that drive the pumps run on schedules that predate anyone living. The steam vents release pressure with a constancy that becomes silence if you stop noticing it. Factories transform raw material into goods that flow up and down the city's tiers, feeding a commerce that has grown so dense it no longer requires currency in any visible form. The machines are tended by workers who have inherited the knowledge of their operation, who understand the sounds and rhythms that indicate health or failure. When a machine fails, the city responds with the same equanimity it shows to weather—withdrawal, repair, restoration. The machines are as much a part of the living city as its people and its plants.

Smaller lives exist in the margins. Fungi fruit in damp corners where ventilation fails. Insects cluster in the city's thermal zones, where heat rises from industry and collects. Rats navigate the lower channels, their populations held in check by the city's own predators—cats that have been here so long they have become architectural, visible only by inference. Birds circle the upper towers, nesting in places too high and exposed for human access. The city has made space for these inhabitants the way ecosystems make space—through density and succession, through niches that open and close. Nothing here is abundant, but nothing is absent.

What lives here, in the end, is the city itself. The people, plants, water, and machines are not separate from it; they are its organs and tissues. The city breathes in water and breathes out mist. It moves goods and information through its arteries. It grows and dies and regrows in slow cycles that span human lifetimes. Its memory lives in the stone—the wear patterns on stairs, the mineral stains on walls, the thickness of vines that have climbed the same supports for centuries. To live here is to be part of this larger metabolism, to participate in a system that was old when the oldest current residents' grandparents were born.

The city continues because it has forgotten how to stop. Its systems interlock so completely that failure in one area creates adjustment in others rather than collapse. Water that once fed a fountain now feeds a root system when the fountain's pipes clog. Heat that once warmed a bathhouse now rises through channels to warm a greenhouse above. The city adapts. It patches and reroutes and evolves. Its current state is not a design but a history, every era's modifications layered atop one another until the original form is lost. What exists is not what was intended. It is what has accumulated—accumulated life, accumulated function, accumulated age.

And so the city lives: dense, vertical, endlessly operating. Its inhabitants tend their small portions of its metabolism, aware of the whole only as a condition, a background presence that shapes every moment without requiring attention. They move through its layers. They breathe its air. They listen to its sounds—the mechanical hum, the water's flow, the occasional voice echoing in stone corridors. They are inside a living thing, and the living thing does not notice them any more than a body notices a single cell. But the cell notices. It notices everything. It has no choice. To live here is to be absorbed into something larger, and to find within that absorption a form of belonging that nothing else could provide.
