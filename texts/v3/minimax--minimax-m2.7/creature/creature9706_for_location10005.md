The people of Threnn are shaped by the water as much as by the stone. They move slowly, or perhaps the thin air merely makes stillness feel like wisdom. Their hands are broad, blunt-fingered—the hands of people who work with wedge and adze, who spend long years learning the particular grain of basalt, where it splits clean and where it wants to hold together. But they are also the hands of people who unclog drainage channels, who clear the spring's throat of gravel and fallen leaves, who learn early that maintenance is the only architecture that truly lasts.

They do not speak much, or perhaps this is a misperception: perhaps they speak constantly, but the stone swallows most of it. What reaches across the settlement is the tone of speech, not its content—the reassuring murmur of routine conversation, the sharper notes of occasional argument, the long stretches of companionable silence when two people simply sit and listen to what the mountain says. Children learn the water's language before they learn words. They know the difference between the spring's healthy gurgle and the sound it makes when gravel is building. They learn to hear when the moss has dried too much, when the channels need attention.

What lives in Threnn, beyond the people, is patience. The mountain has not finished its work, and so the inhabitants have not finished theirs. This is not a place for urgency. The spring does not rush. The moss does not hurry. The basalt columns do not collapse in a day. And so the people do not either. Generations pass, and the work continues—the checking of foundations, the clearing of channels, the tending of small gardens that grow as slowly as the stone around them. There is a kind of contentment in this, or perhaps simply acceptance: the understanding that the place will outlast any individual's effort, and so effort must be continuous, sustained, offered without expectation of completion.

In the evenings, when the mist comes down and the houses glow with small fires, there is something that might be called intimacy—but it is the intimacy of the mountain, not of people. The town is small enough that everyone knows everyone, but this knowledge is quiet, undemanding. People are known by their work: who maintains the spring, who repairs the drainage, who tends the herb gardens on the roofs. When someone dies, their work is simply taken up by another. There is no mythology of heroes here. The mountain does not celebrate, and so neither do its people.

What also lives in Threnn is a particular quality of attention. The inhabitants have learned to notice small changes—the way water pools differently after rain, the direction a crack has widened, the precise color the moss turns when it is holding too much moisture. This attention is not scientific; it has no instruments, no methodology. It is simply the accumulated perception of generations who understood that survival depended on watching. The elders see what the young miss, and the young see what age has made invisible to the elders. Together, they hold the place in a kind of continuous, distributed awareness.

And then there are the absences, which also live: the animals that do not stay, the wildlife that passes through but does not linger. Threnn is not unwelcoming, but it is selective. What remains is what can bear the wet, the cold, the thin air. The birds that come are the hardy ones—the ones that can navigate the mist, that do not need the easy warmth of lower altitudes. The heron, when it appears, is a question the plateau asks itself. The answer is silence.

At night, when the fires burn low, something lives in the space between sleep and waking. The water speaks its continuous language. The wind moves through the moss with a sound like breathing. The stone holds the day's warmth and releases it slowly, and the town settles into itself, a small warm thing at the edge of an immense and indifferent landscape. In the morning, the mist will lift. The people will rise and check the spring and clear the channels and go about the work of holding a place that does not yet know what it will become.

This is what lives in Threnn: the people, patient as the stone; the water, patient as the moss; the attention, patient as the mountain itself. Three hundred years, and three hundred more, or until the mountain decides otherwise.
