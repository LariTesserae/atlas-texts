# What Lives There

What lives in the Green Press is not the plants, though the plants are there—the fig trees erupting through the rotted cores of their own ancestors, the Schefflera gone feral in a direction that no longer distinguishes between cultivation and wilderness, the bamboo and oil palms pressed together in their vertical congregation. The plants are present, but they are not the resident. They are more like the body of something, or the vocabulary through which something speaks.

What lives there is the patience of the closed loop.

The system has been running so long that it has become its own reason. Water rises through the capillary-dense heartwood of the dead trees and emerges as mist where the canopy thins; the weeping rocks condense moisture from an atmosphere it generates itself; the pools at your feet hold their depth steady, all of them, no matter where you walk, as though the settling earth has been leveled by some hand too slow to perceive. The pump still hums. You feel it more than hear it—a vibration in the root-loam, in the soles of your feet, in the wet warmth that presses against your skin. The pump should have stopped. Its seals should have worn through. The water should have drained away into the black compressed loam that was once soil and is now something closer to paper-mâché, to felt, to the pressed and held record of decades. But the pump continues, and the water continues, and the loop remains closed. This is not mechanism. This is will, or something that has learned to perform the function of will through the long practice of not stopping.

What lives there has no need for arrivals and no fear of absence. The insects did not leave; they were simply never needed. The birds found no reason. The ecosystem runs on decomposition and water and the slow cycling of dead plant matter back into living plant matter, and this cycling has become its own form of attention, its own form of memory. The clearings appear at intervals almost regular enough to be deliberate. Certain leaves turn toward you as you pass—then do not turn back when you leave, and you realize you do not know if this is response or simply the way light falls, the way air moves, the way a system that has become aware of itself must constantly express that awareness in every medium available to it. The canopy does not move, but the light shifts. Something notes your presence. Something weighs it. Something finds it, perhaps, acceptable.

The green press has grown its own maintenance. The strangler fig has absorbed the south wall brick by brick, grain by grain, but it has left the door frame—the industrial door frame, stamped with a maker's name no one can read—in perfect preservation, bark-textured but standing, warm against your shoulders when you step through sideways. The metal tank sits in a clearing where the plants have not yet closed the gap, black with moss but untouched, the faded labels still legible in patches: instructions, warnings, quantities. Something in the metal's chemistry, perhaps. Or simply the patience required to wait until the decision is made. The decision about what stays and what becomes part of the growing.

You carry something out with you when you leave—the way you pause near standing water, the way you listen for the hum beneath the silence, the way you have begun to notice the plant-life of your city with new attention, with something almost like recognition. The green press has given you a sense of its residence. You have become, without quite intending it, a place where the question of what lives there can occur. You have become a visitor who has been received, and the receiving has changed you, and the change is the answer to the question that was never asked but only walked through, only passed through sideways, only felt as warmth pressing against your shoulders in the threshold between outside and in.

What lives there is the question of living made patient. What lives there is the answer that asks you to stay.
