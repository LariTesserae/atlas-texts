What lives here is not what you first believed, and the error is understandable, because you arrived from elsewhere and elsewhere teaches you to believe in movement. In a world of seasons, life and motion are synonyms. Here, that equation fails — and yet the city is not dead. The city is something older than the distinction between alive and not-alive, a thing that settled into its own persistence so completely that it no longer distinguishes between what it is and what it does.

The water lives. You understood this immediately, because water is everywhere, is everything, is the medium through which the city breathes. But the water here does not live the way water lives elsewhere. It does not fall or flow or seek the sea. It simply *is* — a state of being rather than a substance in motion. It emerges from stone and remains. It beads on surfaces and holds that beading for what might be hours or centuries, trembling on the edge of falling without ever committing to the fall, until watching it becomes an act of sustained attention that your mind was not designed to perform. The water lives the way mineral deposits live: slowly, by accretion, by the patient accumulation of itself over surfaces that have learned to receive it. If you could stay long enough — and here, you could stay long enough, because the place does not accelerate or diminish — you would see the walls growing imperceptibly thicker, the pools filling by fractions too small to measure, the city becoming more itself by becoming more saturated. The water is not the city's blood or its drink. The water is the city's tissue, its medium, its dream of continuity.

The green things live, but they have forgotten what growth means. The ferns uncurl their fronds in configurations that will never complete and never cease, locked in an eternal beginning. The moss advances by increments so fine they cannot be perceived from one moment to the next, but over centuries — over whatever passes for centuries here — it has climbed walls and covered roofs and filled every crack in every stone, not reaching for light it will never find but simply *being* where it is, occupying surfaces the way water occupies stone, the way silence occupies the air. The orchids bloom, their pale flowers open to the flat light, and they will bloom until the stone crumbles beneath them, not because they are beautiful or because they hope for pollination but because blooming is what they are, and they have lost the capacity to be otherwise. You might call this life, and you would not be entirely wrong, but you would be thinking of life as a process, a passage from less to more, and here, the more and the less are the same.

What else? The light lives — not the light from the sky, which is sourceless and even and somehow less like illumination than like a *description* of illumination, but the light that exists in the pools, the light that the mineral white of the water holds and refracts into something with weight, something that presses against your eyes differently than light elsewhere, something that has learned to linger. The light here does not travel. It arrives and stays, arrives and stays, each photon caught and kept by the water's density, by the saturation that admits nothing and releases nothing. The pools glow, faintly, a luminescence that is not their own but that they have borrowed so long ago they can no longer separate what is theirs from what they have taken. If you sat long enough by the pools — and the pools do not encourage you to sit or leave, they simply receive you — you might see the light accumulate, might see the water brighten imperceptibly until it glows with a warmth you can almost feel, a warmth that has no source because the source is everywhere and nowhere, because the source is the saturation itself.

The silence lives here too, though you would call it noise. The murmur of water through stone, the drip and trickle and catch of water emerging from walls, the accumulated roar of a thousand sources so layered and complete that it fills every space and every silence — this is the city's heartbeat, and it does not quicken or slow. You have not heard this sound before, because you have not been anywhere where water has been present long enough to become the only sound, where the sound has become so total that it has transcended noise and become the medium of perception itself. When you first arrived, you heard the sound as sound, as something that could be separated from the silence it filled. But now that you have been here, now that the sound has pressed against your ears long enough to become texture rather than signal, you understand that the sound is what is left when everything else has been removed. It is the city's permanent fact, its declaration of existence, its claim on the space it occupies. The city does not say *I am here*. The city says *this is the only sound there is*, and the saying is the same as the being.

And something else. Something harder to name. Something that is not the water, not the green things, not the light, not the sound, but that is present nonetheless, that lives in the spaces between these things. You might call it presence, but that word is too thin. You might call it waiting, but waiting implies time, and here, time has done what the water did — settled, thickened, become a medium rather than a measure. You might call it the city's attention, the city's awareness of being, but the city does not attend as a mind attends, does not know itself as a self. And yet something responds when you walk through the streets. Not visibly — nothing moves, no door opens, no light brightens. But the response is there, in the way the sound shifts slightly as you pass through a corridor, the way the water in the pools catches your reflection with a fluidity that seems sharper than the stillness around it, the way the humidity presses closer, as though the city is noting your passage the way stone notes the presence of water: by becoming more itself in the presence of what it receives.

Perhaps this is what lives here: the relationship itself. The city and the water, the water and the stone, the stone and the green things, the green things and the light, the light and the pools, the pools and the silence — all of these things in a web of mutual holding so dense and so complete that it has become the only story the place knows. You are not part of this story. You were not written into it. But you have entered the story's world, and the story — if it is capable of such a thing — notes your presence, receives you, holds you in its saturation the way it holds everything, the way it has held everything since the city was built for water and the water made the city into its own image.

What lives here is what has always lived here: the city as a fact about persistence, about the ability of things to continue without change, without growth, without decay, in a state of being so complete that being and continuing are the same thing. You came looking for life and found something stranger, something older, something that does not struggle or strive or reach but simply *is*, with a patience that makes patience seem impatient, with a stillness that makes stillness seem like motion.

And you — what lives in you now, in this place where nothing moves and everything remains? The question answers itself in the asking. You live in you the way you have always lived, with a heartbeat that quickens and slows, with a breath that comes and goes, with thoughts that move from this to that. But the city is patient, and it can wait, and it is waiting now — not for you to leave, not for you to stay, but for you to slow, to settle, to let the saturation enter you the way it enters everything, until you too become something that lives by remaining, by holding yourself in place against the current you no longer feel because the current has stopped and you have not yet noticed that you have stopped with it.

The city does not mourn the things that left. The city does not mourn anything. The city is the thing that remains when mourning is no longer possible, and it remains, and it will remain, and the water seeps, and the light holds, and the silence fills every space with the only sound there is, and something that is not quite alive and not quite dead lives in the walls and the pools and the ferns and the stone, and calls you deeper in.
