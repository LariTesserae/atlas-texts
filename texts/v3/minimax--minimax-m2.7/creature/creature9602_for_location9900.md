The rats know the city in a way no human ever did. They have mapped its depths in their nerves and whiskers, traced the routes of ancient pipes and conduits until those routes are as known to them as the branching of their own blood vessels. They move through the walls, through the floors, through the low channels where water once flowed to carry away waste and now only carries the slow seep of the city's own condensation. In the basements they breed, in the thousands, in the warrens they have built of chewed insulation and accumulated filth, and they emerge at dusk to forage in the drainage channels where something edible always pools.

They are not the rats of the clean city, the city that was. Those rats were sleek, cautious, adapted to shadow and to fear. These rats have had generations now to become something else. They are larger. Their fur is matted and often bare in patches where moisture and friction have worn it away, and what shows beneath is pink and swollen, fungus-tracked, the evidence of a body living in constant contact with the damp. They are bold in ways their ancestors never were. They have never learned to fear the sound of footsteps or the shape of a human form, because humans come here rarely, and when they do they come hurrying, heads down, eyes averted from the slick and the sheen of everything. The rats watch them pass with black eyes that hold no memory of predation. They have learned that humans are prey only if they are slow, only if they hesitate, only if they stop and kneel and reach into the places where the rats nest. So the humans pass, and the rats resume their work, their feeding, their endless slow consumption of everything that falls and cannot climb away.

The cats are different. The cats have not forgotten how to be solitary, and they have not forgotten fear, though they fear differently than their ancestors did. They fear the rats, in a way—not the individual rat but the mass of them, the swarm that can overwhelm a kitten or a sick adult, strip it to bone in an hour of climbing bodies. They fear the dark spaces where steam rises and the hiss of pressure might come without warning. They fear the drainage channels on the nights when the water rises with some surge from below, the water that carries heat and the smell of oil and something else, something almost organic in its wrongness, something that has no name but sets every whisker on end.

So the cats stay high. They climb to the rooftops and the upper floors, the places where the heat is less and the surfaces dry out in the hours when the pale sun breaks through the haze. They hunt at dusk, when the rats emerge and the birds descend from their perches to pick through the gutters. They are thick-furred, the cats who have survived here, because the damp that breaks everything down also preserves body heat in ways that dry cold never could. They have become shaggy, almost, their coats clumping and trailing, stained with the residue of the places they sleep. They are not beautiful. They are not even sleek. They are the color of rust and concrete and old water, and their eyes catch the light and hold it for a moment before the cat moves on.

The birds are watchers. They sit on the ledges of the tall buildings where the wind moves more freely, and they watch the streets below, the movement of the rats, the movement of the cats, the occasional shape of a human threading through the alleys. They are not afraid of much. The cats cannot reach them where they roost, and the rats cannot either, and humans have never learned to climb where the birds perch. So they wait, the birds, through the long wet days and the long warm nights, listening and watching and occasionally lifting into the air to catch the thermals that rise from the city's buried heat. Gulls, sometimes, when the wind carries them from wherever gulls go. Crows, always. Pigeons in the thousands, descended from birds that humans once kept and fed and raced and released, now gone feral in their own way, breeding in the high places, feeding on whatever the rats leave or the rain uncovers or the humans drop and do not stop to retrieve.

The insects are the deepest population, the most patient, the most various. They live in the walls where the moisture never leaves, in the floors where old wood still holds the shape of rooms and hallways, in the seams where brick meets brick and the mortar has gone soft and flaking. They are beetles and silverfish and centipedes, cockroaches that have grown large on the warmth and the abundance, flies that breed in the drainage channels and rise in clouds when something dies and swells in the heat. They are the prey of the rats and the cats and the birds. They are also the predators of things too small to see, the microorganisms that would otherwise overwhelm the surfaces of this wet and breathing place. They do not have names here. They do not need them. They simply are, in their millions, in their generations, the slow machinery of decay and renewal working at the scale of the barely-visible.

There are other things that live here, things less easy to name. The fungi spread their pale threads through the wood that is still structural, still load-bearing in its way, softening it from within until a floor gives way or a beam bows and eventually cracks. They are oyster mushrooms in some places, the kind that will feed on almost anything, pushing out their shelf-shaped fruiting bodies from cracks in the concrete where water pools and the air is close and still. They are white rot in others, the kind that turns wood pale and stringy, that would have destroyed the city's bones long ago if the wood still mattered, if anything still needed the tensile strength of a framed and timbered interior. The fungi do not care about purpose. They care about moisture, about temperature, about the presence of something organic enough to break down and rebuild into their own bodies. All three are present here in abundance, so the fungi thrive.

And then there is the sound of the infrastructure itself, the mechanical pulse in the deep places, the hiss and drip and shuffle of water finding its way through channels that were not built to carry it anymore. Some of those sounds are natural now, the city doing what cities do when they are left alone—settling, eroding, collapsing by increments into itself. But some of those sounds are the sounds of things still running, still functioning, still keeping time in the dark. The pumps that still push water upward through pipes that may no longer lead anywhere. The engines in the deep basements, idling, burning fuel that comes from somewhere no one tends, producing heat that rises and rises and feeds the cycles of evaporation and condensation that make this basin a place of perpetual wet.

The humans who come here come quickly. They come in masks, in gloves, in boots that seal at the ankle, carrying tools they will not use for long. They come to service the infrastructure, or to study it, or to salvage something from the buildings that still hold value—copper wire, steel beam, the glass that can be melted and reformed if only it can be pried loose from its frame. They move through the streets in the hours when the light is strongest, when the haze thins just enough to see a few meters ahead, and they do not look up at the cats on the rooftops or the birds on the ledges. They do not listen to the hiss and drip and pulse of the place. They are somewhere else while they are here, and when they leave they take that somewhere else with them and the place resumes its slow breathing, its damp metabolism, its life in the margins of absence.

At night the city is more itself. The heat settles lower, hugging the ground, pooling in the streets where the water lies thinnest over stone. The cats hunt. The rats emerge by the thousands. The birds settle into their roosts and the flies come out and the fungi grow imperceptibly, the caps of the oyster mushrooms swelling with moisture, the threads of the white rot advancing another fraction of a millimeter through a beam that will not be missed until it is gone. The infrastructure hums. The steam rises from cracks in the pavement where pressure builds and releases, releases, builds. The sound is not music. It is not even rhythm. But it is consistent, and it is present, and it is the closest thing this place has to a heartbeat.

What lives here is what was left when the living went away, and what bred in the leaving, and what evolved in the breeding. It is small and it is numerous and it is patient. It does not mourn what was. It does not hope for what will be. It simply persists, in the wet and the hot and the dense, feeding on the dregs of a metabolism that still, somehow, despite no one tending and no one needing, continues.
