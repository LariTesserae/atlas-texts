The people of the Dripping are not a demonstrative people, which is not to say they are cold. They are simply calibrated to a place that rewards stillness over gesture, attention over assertion. Their faces, by the third generation, begin to develop the particular stillness of the plateau itself — a patience in the eyes, an economy of expression that reads, to outsiders, as reserve. The children born here learn early to read the moisture: the weight of the fog, the particular darkness of certain stones, the way the condensation forms first on the iron grates before it touches the flagstones. They learn to predict the water table's rhythms by the behavior of the ferns in the deep quarter, which brighten or dim in advance of the rise and fall, so that the city's children grow up reading the plateau's intentions the way others read weather or season.

They are a people shaped by verticality. The body learns the stairs as a grammar — the thigh-strength required to descend from the residential tier to the deep quarter and back, the particular balance required on streets that are carved into incline, the habit of looking up and down simultaneously when navigating the commercial terrace, where the level above overhangs the level below and the light arrives at an angle the stone has been wearing smooth for centuries. The people of the Dripping have strong calves and careful feet and a relationship to gravity that is both intimate and unthinking, like breathing. They carry loads against their chests rather than on their backs, hugging packages close as they descend, because the stairs punish the rear-heavy posture. They have learned not to run on the upper streets, where the flagstones are worn to a polish by generations of careful feet but offer no purchase for the hurried.

Their industry is not the restless industry of lower cities. The smelters and the weavers and the forge-keepers work with a slowness that is sometimes mistaken for laziness by those who do not understand the place. But the slowness is a form of respect — for the stone that receives their labor, for the water that powers their looms, for the heat that must be husbanded because the altitude makes every resource feel provisional. The furnace-keeper on the smelting terrace tends his fire across a span of years, learning its moods, its needs, the particular quality of draft that the plateau's geometry produces on different mornings. He does not control the fire so much as conduct it, and the fire does not burn the way fires burn in other places — it smolders, it waits, it makes the iron soft rather than molten, and the iron that emerges from the smelting terrace's working has a patience in it that the iron of lower forges does not possess.

The people do not speak of the deep quarter's lowest level, but they live in its shadow. Every family in the Dripping has a name that stretches back at least to the founding, and every family has at least one member who descends, once a year, to the level where the walls are grown and the pools are black. The descent is not ritual exactly — there is no ceremony, no observed protocol. It is simply something that is done, the way one maintains a grave. They go alone or in pairs, in the hour before the evening fog settles, carrying nothing, descending through the industrial terraces and past the old mining tunnels and into the lightless reach where the cave-salamanders live their slow lives in the standing pools. What they do there is their own. Some sit. Some sleep. Some simply stand and breathe the air that smells of iron and something older, until the hour passes and they ascend, and the next day they return to their work as though nothing has happened.

The children who grow up here do not find this strange. They are taught, early, that the plateau is not merely substrate — that it is the thing itself, the presence beneath and within and around them, patient and cool and unfinished. They are taught to walk gently on the flagstones because the flagstones remember every footfall, and to speak softly in the deep quarter because the stone amplifies sound in ways that are not merely acoustic, and to leave offerings in the channel-pools — a coin, a twist of copper wire, a fragment of whatever they have made that day — because the water takes what is given and gives back what is needed, and this exchange is the oldest contract the city has.

The cliff-rats have lived on the plateau longer than the city. They are the color of the stone at dusk — grey and faintly blue, as though the iron in the granite has migrated into their fur — and their eyes are large and dark and see a spectrum the human eye cannot name. They are not pets and not pests; they are neighbors, in the full and unhurried sense of the word. The people of the deep quarter leave food scraps in the channel-pools for them, not as feeding but as acknowledgment, and the cliff-rats take what they need and leave the rest, and both species have arranged themselves around this arrangement so thoroughly that neither notices the other anymore, the way a hand does not notice the glove it wears.

The mountain foxes are wilder. They come to the plateau's western margin in the dry season, when the scrub below fails and the moisture that seeps from the plateau's sides offers the only available water. They are thin, these foxes — thinner than their lowland cousins, their fur denser, their movements more deliberate. They do not enter the city. They watch it from the gullies, where the erosion has carved pathways they know by heart, and in the hour before dawn you can see them sitting on the pale stone, their ears tracking the sounds of the city waking: the first aqueduct hum, the settling of stone, the distant ring of the eastern bell struck once, gently, to mark the hour. They are not part of the city's economy, but they are part of its audience — witnesses to the slow making of something they do not understand but have learned to attend.

The ravens are different. The ravens understand. They have nested in the northern buttress for so long that their droppings have altered the color of the stone — a dark stain that spreads across the face like a slow bruise, marking the places where they have roosted for generations. They are large, these ravens, and their voices carry in the thin air with a clarity that would be beautiful if it were not so relentless. They watch the city with an intelligence that is not human but is not alien either; it is the intelligence of a creature that has made its accommodation with the plateau and understands, in its own way, what the plateau is doing. When the water table rises and the ferns brighten and the condensation falls heavy from every surface, the ravens grow quiet, as though listening. When it falls, they are louder, their calls ringing off the stone in patterns that sound, if you listen long enough, like a language. The people of the Dripping do not try to learn it. They have learned only this: when the ravens go silent, something is shifting in the deep.

And there are the salamanders, in the lowest pools, in the lightless reach where the walls are grown and not built. They are the oldest residents of the Dripping that anyone can name, though *resident* is the wrong word — they are more like features of the place, the way the channels are features, the way the fog is a feature, the way the plateau itself is a feature of the world's slow grammar. They do not age as other creatures age. They do not seem to move as other creatures move. They simply are, in the black water, in the iron air, in the presence of something that has been here since before the plateau rose, since before the stone was stone, since before the word *here* had meaning. The people who descend to sit with them do not speak of what they find there. The children who grow up hearing about the salamanders do not ask what they are. There are mysteries the Dripping has chosen to keep, not out of fear but out of respect — the way one does not ask a mountain its name, or demand of the fog that it explain itself.

What lives in the Dripping is a city, and a plateau, and a people who have learned to live between them. What lives in the Dripping is a patience that precedes human time and will outlast it. What lives in the Dripping is the slow, wet, iron-dark saying of something the plateau has been trying to articulate since the violence that raised it finally, at last, subsided into silence. The people of the Dripping are the words it has found so far. The cliff-rats are older words. The ravens are punctuation. The foxes are a grammar no one speaks but everyone hears. And the salamanders, in the lowest pools, in the grown walls, in the dark that has no bottom — the salamanders are what the plateau calls itself, in the language it uses when no one is listening, when the fog has settled and the bells have faded and the stone has gone quiet with the particular silence of something that is still, after everything, only beginning.
