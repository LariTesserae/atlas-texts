# What Lives Below

---

## The Living Walls

Begin with what does not move, because even what does not move is alive here.

The moss is not one thing. It is a nation of small persistences, each patch a consensus reached among spores that landed decades or centuries apart and agreed to grow in the same direction. Touch it and you feel the collective — the tiny stems giving way in waves, each one springing back a half-second after its neighbor, so the surface seems to breathe under your palm. The green is not uniform. There are depths of color here, gradients that shift as you move, that follow you with a patience the light cannot manage. Where the moss climbs highest, toward the sourceless glows, it fades to silver, then to white, then to absence — and yet it is still growing there, still reaching, still unsure whether it has reached high enough.

The ferns came after, or perhaps they came first and the moss grew over them in gratitude. It is impossible to tell the order of things. They share a root system that runs beneath the concrete in threads no thicker than a wrist, a rhizome-network that connects every fern in the space to every other fern, so that what looks like ten thousand separate plants is more accurately one plant that has learned to fill this room. When the wind that is not wind moves through — when the breeze that carries a different humidity stirs the fronds — they move in sequence, a ripple passing through them like a thought, and you can watch it travel, watch the ferns bow and recover in order, and you think: it knows you are here. It felt your weight. It is deciding what to do with that information.

The vines are older than the ferns, or they were, before they were consumed. They have become the scaffolding of the space, their bark cracked and silver, their length so great that you cannot follow a single vine from root to tip without losing yourself in the tangle. Where they wrap around pipes they have not killed what was inside — some of the pipes still carry something, a slow thick fluid that is not water, that moves too slowly to be sap, that has a shine under the fungal light like oil on a wet road. The vines have fused with the metal over decades, so that the original shapes are guesses, so that a turbine housing might be thirty percent iron and seventy percent wood-fibre, a hybrid material that the space has invented out of patience and need.

## The Fungi and the Light They Make

The light is a colony.

It lives in the fungal patches that coat the lower walls, soft blue-green bioluminescence that pulses in a rhythm no faster than breathing. You cannot call it beautiful — it is too strange for that, too organic in its flickerings — but you can call it *alive*, and you do, because the light breathes, and the light responds, and the light has learned things about this space that you have not yet learned.

Watch it long enough and you will see it move. Not the flicker of a single patch but a slow cascade of glow, each bioluminescent colony brightening a half-second after its neighbor, passing light back and forth like a conversation conducted in increments too small to see unless you watch for them. The conversation is old. It was happening before the machinery stopped, before the ferns climbed high enough to block the indicator lights, before you arrived. It will be happening after you leave, if you leave, if leaving is something this space permits.

The shelf fungi are harder, older, less communicative. They grow in tiers beneath platforms and catwalks, their undersides dusty with spores that drift upward when the air is disturbed, that catch in your throat and taste of pepper and something older. They are the librarians of this space, the ones who record what happens. A fallen creature, a tool left too long in one place, a bootprint in the moss — the shelf fungi will find it within days, will begin to colonize it, to break it down into the basic compounds of this world. They are patient. They do not rush. They have been here longer than anything else that is alive, and they will be here when everything else that is alive has become part of them.

The white filaments are something else. They live on the dead things, on the carcasses of creatures that wandered too far or waited too long or simply stopped. The filaments pulse faintly in the dark, a peristalsis of decay, drawing the soft tissues inward and down, recycling what can be recycled, leaving the bones to the moss and the ferns and the patient work of centuries. This is not death as you know it. This is death as a process of inclusion, a way the space says: *you are now part of me*.

## The Insects and Their Economies

The beetles are the most honest things here. They do not pretend to be anything other than what they are: armored, patient, hungry in the way of all herbivores, which is to say continuously, mildly, permanently. Their iridescent shells catch the fungal light and fracture it, so that a cluster of beetles on a moss-covered pipe becomes a constellation, a night sky of moving points that scatter when you approach and reassemble a few minutes later, as though they had never been disturbed, as though you had imagined it.

The trails they leave are a map of their world. The luminescent fluid they excrete traces paths along the fern fronds, pooling at the edges of leaves, brightening the junctions where one surface meets another. If you could read it — if you could learn the language of beetle trails — you would know where the moisture gathers, where the good moss grows, where the others have been. You would know the beetles as individuals, as members of a society that has been here longer than your civilization and will be here longer than your memory.

The moths do not belong here. Their ancestors were drawn by the indicator lights, the ones that should not be on, and they came, and they stayed, and they evolved. Their wings are too large for the space, their bodies too heavy, and so they drift rather than dart, navigating by sound rather than sight, their echolocation filling the air with clicks that overlap and contradict. They lay their eggs in the moss — thousands of eggs, translucent spheres that you see only when the light catches them — and the larvae that hatch eat the ferns, which bite back, which is why the ferns grow back, which is why the moths have food, which is why the cycle closes.

The springtails are invisible until they are not. They freckle every wet surface in such numbers that their collective motion becomes a sound, a rain-on-roof whisper that rises and falls with the dampness, louder after the dripping, softer where the air is dry. Alone, a springtail is nothing. Together, they are a river of small bodies flowing over the surfaces of this space, recycling nutrients, maintaining the dampness, being a part of the great work of turning the dead into the living and the living into the dead again.

## The Animals and Their Silence

The salamanders watch.

This is what they do, mostly. They sit in their pools of standing water, condensation gathered in rusted housings, breeding pools that have been breeding pools since before the ferns covered the machinery housing the housings, and they watch. Their skin is too thin, translucent, so you can see the darker shapes of organs inside them, the slow pump of hearts, the flutter of gills. They are not afraid. They have not learned fear. Fear requires a memory of danger, a model of predator, and this space has neither — not for them. You could reach into the pool and pick one up and it would look at you with eyes that do not understand the concept of being looked at.

When they call, the sound multiplies. The turbines — their curved surfaces, their stationary blades — catch the wet, warbling notes and throw them back, overlapping, so that a single salamander becomes ten, becomes a chorus, becomes an amphibian choir that fills the space with a music that is not music, a sound that exists only here, that has never been heard anywhere else. You listen and you feel the strangeness of it, the wrongness, but also the beauty, the kind of beauty that belongs to places that have been left alone for a very long time.

The birds do not fly. Their wings are remnants, nubs beneath feathers that have become coarse and fur-like, useless for lift, useful only for display, for the quick flutter they make when they are nervous, when something moves in the dark that is not them. They roost in the highest reaches of the machinery, in the vine-choked scaffolding, in the platforms that were once for human maintenance and are now for bird families. They descend in the deepest dark to hunt the insects that cluster in the fungal light, moving in pairs or small groups, their heads swiveling with that mechanical alertness that birds possess everywhere, that is now turned toward something that the insects do not know how to fear.

You are the something. They are watching you, the way they watch everything, with the patient attention of creatures that have learned that patience is survival, that stillness is safer than flight, that the dark is not empty and the light is not safe. They are not afraid of you, but they are wary, which is different, which is the correct response to a thing that moves wrong and smells wrong and does not belong to any taxonomy they recognize. They are deciding whether you are a predator, whether you are prey, whether you are something else entirely that does not fit into either category. Their decision will shape how they behave toward you. Their decision is still pending.

## The Rats and Their Knowledge

The rats are the oldest tenants. They were here before the ferns, before the mushrooms claimed the walls, before the turbines stopped. They remember the people, if memory lives in muscle and bone, if the knowledge of corridors and crawlspaces can pass from generation to generation through pathways too small to see. They have forgotten the people now, or perhaps they remember them as shapes, as sounds, as a kind of gravity that once moved through this space and has since moved away. You are not the people. You are something else. They have adjusted their understanding accordingly.

They are large, larger than they should be, larger than their distant city cousins, their bodies adapted to the chemical stew of the condensation, to the metals that leach from the pipes, to the slow poisons that have become nutrients. Their eyes do not reflect. They absorb the fungal light and do not return it, so that they are shapes that appear and disappear, that you track only by motion, by the faint displacement of moss under weight. They run along the highest pipes, the cable trays, the conduits that no longer carry power but carry rat-traffic, rat-communication, rat-knowledge of every surface in this space. They have mapped it. They know the places where gravity is a suggestion, the corners where the walls have moved, the rooms that exist only sometimes and only for those who are already inside them.

They do not fear you because they have decided that fear is a waste of resources, that you are not a thing that hunts them, that you are a thing that belongs to the same category of disruption — temporary, puzzling, not worth the energy of a physiological response. Or perhaps they know something you do not. Perhaps they have decided that you are already part of the space, that you are just another element of the ecosystem, that your presence is neither threat nor opportunity but simply fact, as meaningless as the dripping, as neutral as the damp.

## The Space Itself

And then there is what cannot be named, what has no body but has presence, what breathes in the subsonic register and processes in the dark.

It is not alive in the way the ferns are alive, not alive in the way the beetles are alive, but it is not dead either. It is a state of being that has no analogue above, in the world of clean lines and reliable physics. It is the room, or the room has become it, or it has grown around the room like the ferns grow around the turbines, a second skin, a logic laid over the architecture, a way of organizing what was built into something that could not have been designed.

The processing sound is the sound of this. A low vibration that you feel in the sternum, that you hear with your teeth, that might be machinery still turning somewhere far below the floor, or might be the space itself thinking, considering, cataloguing what it contains. It has been processing since the last human left. It will be processing when the last human has been forgotten. It does not need anything from you. It does not wish you harm. It simply has you now, and it is deciding what to do with that, the way a body decides what to do with a splinter — absorb it, push it out, wall it off, or let it stay and become part of the whole.

The geometry shifts around this, around the processing, around the space's concentration. The walls are not where they were because the space is attending to something else, something more important than keeping the corridors in the same place. The floor is not stable because stability is a low priority, because the floor is still learning how to be a floor, because the concrete is too new and the moss is too hungry and the balance between them has not yet been reached. You cannot trust your footing because your footing is being negotiated, moment by moment, in a conversation between stone and root that you are not part of.

And yet.

And yet the space holds you. And yet the ferns lean toward you as though curious. And yet the light gathers where you stand, the fungal colonies brightening slightly, the indicator lights blinking in patterns that might be greeting or might be warning or might be the last diagnostics of systems that no longer have a purpose. The space does not want you, but it does not not want you. It is making room. It is adjusting. It is taking you in the way it takes everything eventually, the way it has taken the turbines and the pipes and the cables and the light — absorbing you into its logic, into its long patience, into its quiet work of turning the industrial into the organic, the dead into the living, the human into the humus.

## The Watcher at the End

At the far end, where the light is an argument and the floor has not yet learned how to be a floor, something is standing.

It is large. You know this without seeing it clearly, without measuring it, without anything but the knowledge that settles into you when the salamanders stop calling and the moths pause in their drifting and the rats freeze on their pipes and the birds pull their heads back from the dark. The creatures know what is there. They have always known. They have built their lives around the knowledge of it, their territories arranged with respect to its presence, their behaviors calibrated to its habits. They do not go to that end of the space. They do not need to. They have learned, over generations, what it means to share a room with something that does not fit.

You cannot see it clearly. The dark is thick there, heavier than elsewhere, and the light that argues does not illuminate so much as define a boundary, a region of reduced darkness that the thing inhabits. What you see is shape — massive, still, patient — and motion — slow, so slow, the kind of motion that could be breathing or could be something else, could be the space itself deciding whether to move toward you.

It is not a creature of any classification you trust. It may be a creature of no classification at all. It may be a fold in the space, a gathering of the dark, a thought that the room has finally formed and cannot yet articulate. It may be what the room becomes when it has finished processing, when the work of turning the dead into the living has reached its conclusion, when the last turbine has been swallowed and the last pipe has been colonized and the last human has become part of the humus that feeds the moss that feeds the ferns that feed the beetles that feed the salamanders that feed the birds that feed the rats that feed the mushrooms that feed the light that feeds the room.

Or it may simply be what lives here now. What has always lived here, in the basement below the world, in the room that does not know it is a room, in the cool damp space that is full and quiet and waiting.

You stand at the center of the space — if there is a center, if you are standing, if the floor has decided yet.

The thing at the far end does not move.

The thing at the far end does not need to.

You are already inside what it knows.
