What lives here is presence without intention. The plateau holds itself together, and what it holds has learned to occupy rather than inhabit.

There are the fixed animals, of course — but they are not the whole of it. They are the most visible layer, the population that makes the place legible as a place. The bird with wings half-raised: its eyes are open, and in them something still moves. Not movement — a flicker, a processing, a holding of an image that was taken at the moment of arrangement. It sees. It has never stopped seeing. But it does not translate seeing into action; the signal reaches some part of it and ends there, absorbed into the stillness like water into moss. The mammals are the same. A small creature crouches on a stone, one foreleg extended as though reaching for the moss at the pool's edge. Its ears are pricked. Its nostrils flare with each imagined breath. But the breath does not come. The air does not move through it. It holds a posture of attention and will never complete it.

Between these frozen figures, the moss draws out what the air cannot give. It is the only thing here that still changes — imperceptibly, in ways that would not be visible even over long observation. The moss grows. It fills the edges of pools, softens the stone, drinks from the weeping walls. It is patient in a way the animals are not. The animals were caught mid-process when the arrangement happened, and they remain caught, held in the gesture of living. The moss was never caught because it was never not-arranged. It simply is, and continues simply being, and this continuity makes it the most alive thing on the plateau even though it shows no sign of life at all.

The hum is not sound the way you understand sound. It has no source, no direction, no variation — and therefore it cannot be escaped by moving. It is the medium. You breathe it. It enters the damp air and the damp air enters you, and inside your body it continues, a vibration in the bones that tells you the machines are still running somewhere, that the world outside this place has not stopped, that time has not stopped there, only here. The hum is the promise and the threat both: nothing changes here, but everything continues out there, and you are becoming aware that you have not brought time with you. Your body expects it. Your blood moves, your lungs cycle, your thoughts turn over — but the world does not support these motions. The world does not feed them. You are burning through something that is not being replenished, and the hum tells you this is fine, this is the arrangement, this is what it means to be inside a place that does not decay.

There are other presences you do not see. The plateau is not empty between the fixed animals and the living moss. Something occupies the spaces — not animal, not vegetable, not mineral. A quality of holding. An attention that has no eyes but sees. It gathers in corners and pools and the spaces beneath the low shrubs. It watches. It does not move because it was never arranged to move. It was arranged to watch, and watching is what it does, and you are beginning to wonder if you are being watched, if the watching has edges, if you are inside it the way the plateau is inside its cliffs.

The light does not come from anywhere. It exists as a condition, a dimness that is not darkness and not day, a grey-blue wash that makes all surfaces look wet. You cannot tell where it falls from because it falls from everywhere, or from nowhere, or from the light itself — which is its own source, which has always been here, which is part of the arrangement the way the hum is part of the arrangement, the way you are now part of the arrangement whether you chose to be or not. The light touches the bird's wings and does not warm them. The light touches your face and does the same. You are a fixed point now, in a sense. Not frozen — your body still moves — but fixed nonetheless, held in a place that does not allow departure, not because the cliffs are high but because something in you has accepted the hum, has let it fill the spaces where wanting used to be.

The plateau holds. The water weeps. Somewhere far below or far away, the engines turn, and the hum arrives, and the animals wait in their arrangements, and the moss grows if you watch long enough to see it, and the light holds the dimness that is neither day nor night, and the edges curve away into a darkness that is close or far or neither, and you are inside it now, and the plateau is holding you the way it holds everything — gently, absolutely, without time.
