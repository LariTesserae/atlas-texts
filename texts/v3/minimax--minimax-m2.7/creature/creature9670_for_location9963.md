# What Lives in Veth Saline

There is a fungus among us, the old-timers say, and they mean it as comfort. In the eastern basins where the substrate runs deepest, where the roots have descended so far that the surface yields like flesh when pressed, the people speak of the city as a body. They have no other framework for it. What grows here is not separate from what was built; what was built has become indistinguishable from what grows. The distinction dissolves if you look too long.

The substrate is the first and oldest inhabitant. It breathes. Not metaphorically, not in the way poets describe earth as if it could exhale—but actually, measurably, the way mycelial networks draw down and release according to cycles that the city's oldest residents have learned to read the way others read weather. The packed ground of the avenues is firmer in the morning, when the night's moisture has been absorbed and the surface tension of living tissue holds firm. By afternoon it softens. By evening, in the deep hollows where mist collects, a person might sink slightly, ankle-deep, before the substrate adjusts to their weight and firms again. This is not animation in the sense of will. The residents would be offended by the suggestion. It is simply responsiveness—the substrate knows the difference between footfall and standing, between the weight of a person and the weight of rain.

The buildings are the second order of inhabitant, and they are harder to speak of. They were constructed, once. Three centuries ago, when the first settlers came up from the lowlands with their engines and their blueprints, they shaped the substrate into forms they understood: walls, floors, rooms with corners. But the substrate did not remain shaped. It grew. It added cells to its surfaces, secreted bark-like tissue over exposed beams, sent root-fibers through gaps that would otherwise have let in weather. Within two generations, the oldest structures had become something else—something that could be called a building only because it contained people, only because it had been intended as one. What it had become was a partnership, a collaboration between intention and metabolism.

The towers are the most developed examples of this partnership. They spiral upward on impulses that no architect designed, their growth driven by something in the substrate that responds to sunlight, to proximity to other towers, to the accumulated presence of the people who live within them. A tower in the upper districts will lean toward its neighbors—not dramatically, not visibly over short periods, but measurably over years, so that the streets between them have narrowed and the towers seem to be conversing. The residents say the towers are lonely. They say this without irony, without any sense that they are speaking of inanimate objects. The towers lean because they want company. The towers grow because they want to see.

Inside the towers, the walls are warm. This warmth is not heating, not in the mechanical sense. The bark-tissue generates heat as part of its metabolism, and the residents have learned to cultivate this, to encourage the growth of certain varieties that run warmer, to prune back the ones that would cool the interior too much. A tower-home maintains itself at a temperature that its residents find comfortable. This is not coincidence. The substrate knows their habits. It knows, somehow, which rooms they use at which hours, and it adjusts its warmth accordingly—cooler in the rooms that are rarely occupied, warmer in the ones that have become, through years of use, extensions of the body.

The people themselves have changed, though they would not call it that. They are still human, recognizably so—their faces, their voices, their children, their griefs and ambitions are comprehensible to anyone from the lowland cities. But they move differently. They have learned to walk on surfaces that shift, that yield, that respond. Their gait has developed a particular quality, a slight extra attention to each step, a readiness to adjust. Children born here learn this gait without being taught; their bodies simply grow into the city's rhythms. They are barefoot more often than shod. The substrate does not hurt their feet, and they have learned to feel through it—to sense, with the soles of their feet, the subtle changes in firmness that might indicate a change in the path ahead, a softening in a place where water has pooled, a firming where a root has grown through and created a small rise.

The people of Veth Saline are ten thousand, and they are not spread evenly. They cluster in the middle districts, where the substrate is neither too soft nor too ancient, where the buildings have grown enough to be stable but not so much that the distinction between inside and outside has collapsed entirely. The lower districts, where the industrial heart still beats, are louder, denser, more crowded with both people and machinery. The higher districts, where the towers thin and the air carries more mist, are nearly empty—places where people go for solitude, for the pleasure of walking through spaces that feel less inhabited, more wild.

There are people who live in the oldest districts, where the factories have been half-consumed and the machines have become part of the organism. These people are fewer and more particular. They have adapted their lives to the rhythms of a world that no longer distinguishes between engine and ecosystem. They listen to the hiss of steam and understand it the way farmers understand weather—not as mechanism but as mood, as the expression of something vast and mostly incomprehensible. They repair the machines, but they do not claim to understand them. They simply live alongside them, the way earlier generations lived alongside rivers and seasons.

The animals are harder to speak of, because there are so few, and because what remains raises questions that the residents prefer not to ask.

The salamander was seen once, in the eastern district, in a place where moisture collects in the root-mats and does not dry. A child reported it, and a few adults went to look, and they saw something that moved like a salamander—four legs, a long tail, a body suited to the moisture of the substrate's deeper layers. They did not catch it. They did not try. It was gone by the next morning, and it has not been seen since. The residents speak of it the way others speak of rare birds, of good omens. They are not sure it was real. They are not sure it matters.

The moth is more certain. There is a species here that has adapted to the building-flowers—the small, bright growths that appear on the bark-surfaces of towers, that produce nectar in quantities too small to be useful to the residents but enough to sustain a small population of pollinators. The moth is not beautiful, by the standards of the lowland cities. It is pale, nearly translucent, with wings that catch the bioluminescent glow of the towers at night and seem to flicker. It does not come to artificial light, because there is no artificial light—only the living glow of the bark, the growing glow of the flowers. It is active only in the deep hours, between midnight and dawn, when the mist is thickest and the city is quietest. Some residents have never seen it. Others have learned to watch for it, to sit on their balconies in the dark and wait for the small flicker of wings.

The rodent is the most uncertain. There are sightings, sometimes, of something small and quick moving through the root-mats at the edges of the districts, where the substrate is softest and the buildings are sparse. It might be a rodent. It might be a growth, a tumor of the substrate that has taken on animal characteristics, that moves and responds but is not alive in any sense the residents understand. The residents do not investigate. The space between possible and certain is one they prefer not to cross. Whatever it is, it moves carefully through the city's margins, and the city does not consume it, and this seems to be enough.

The absence of fauna is not felt as loss. The residents have never known anything else. The silence beneath the city's sounds is simply the texture of life here—a silence that is not quiet, that is full of the creak and sigh of plant-matter and the hiss and clatter of machines, but that has a gap in it, a place where animal sound should be. They have filled this gap with the sounds of their own making. The factories provide rhythm. The towers provide creak and groan. The water moving through the arterial channels provides a sound like swallowing, like breathing, like something alive and vast drinking deep. The silence is not silence. It is just the sound of something that has become used to being alone.

The substrate is aware. This is the fact that the residents have learned not to notice, the strangeness that lives in peripheral vision, in moments when the light falls wrong or the shadows move independently of their sources.

It is not consciousness, not the way the residents understand consciousness. The substrate does not think, does not plan, does not desire in any recognizable sense. But it responds. It responds to attention, to presence, to the accumulated weight of human habitation. The streets are firmer where people walk often, softer in corners that are rarely visited. The buildings grow toward the sun but also toward the people, tilting slightly to offer shade, to create shelter from the mist, to place their flowers at a height that is convenient for gathering. The towers adjust their warmth according to occupancy, their windows opening or closing as the bark-skin responds to temperature and desire.

The residents have learned not to see this as strange. They have learned to see it as simply how things are—the same way earlier generations saw the sun as simply there, the same way they saw rivers as simply flowing. The substrate responds because that is what it does. The buildings grow because that is what they do. The city breathes because it is alive, and being alive means moving, means changing, means becoming something that was not there before.

In the morning, the rain comes. It is always soft, always persistent, carried in from the distant sea on winds that have been climbing for hours and have grown cold and tired and content to rest. The rain beads on leaf-surfaces and runs down channels that the substrate has grown to carry it—arterial channels that run through the buildings, that feed the towers, that carry moisture down to the processing roots far below. The factories resume their ancient rhythms. The looms clatter. The steam hisses. The city makes itself, makes its goods, makes its strange and quiet life.

The emptiness remains. It is patient, the space between things, the wide swaths of living ground where no building has taken root. It is not wasteland. It is not neglect. It is simply the pace of things—the substrate growing faster than the will to fill it, the people preferring to live close and leave room, the city learning what it wants to become and taking its time to become it. The emptiness waits. The emptiness breathes. The emptiness is part of what lives here, the rest and the potential, the space that could be anything and is willing to wait.

The city dreams. This is the only way to say it that makes sense. In the deep hours, when the mist rises and the towers glow faintly and the people have gone to their homes that have grown to fit them, the substrate exhales. The sound is barely perceptible—lower than whisper, lower than breath, a vibration that can only be felt if you are standing still, if you are barefoot, if you are patient. The city exhales, and the sound is like sleep, like rest, like something vast and alive finally letting go.

What lives in Veth Saline? Everything that has learned to live without the things it was built to need. Everything that has become something new. Everything that is still becoming, still growing, still finding out what it is.
