The grass moves in waves that aren't wind.

This is the first thing that unsettles you, though you can't say exactly why. There's no breeze. The air sits still and damp around you, heavy with moisture that hasn't decided to fall. And yet the grass bends and rises in a slow undulation that passes through the meadow like breath through a body. It travels from somewhere to somewhere, this wave, and you cannot locate its source or destination. The grass simply moves, and then it doesn't, and then it moves again, and the rhythm has nothing to do with anything you can measure.

The plateau breathes, and the grass is its surface.

---

The drone fills everything. It has no gaps, no silences, no moments of rest. Insects beyond counting move through the air and the grass and the spaces between, their wings carrying a frequency that settles into your chest before it reaches your ears. You stop hearing them as individuals. They become a tone, a vibration, a sound that is also a texture, something you feel in your teeth and the backs of your eyes. The plateau has a heartbeat made of wings. The insects are the blood. The grass is the skin. And you are—

What are you?

A visitor. A temporary thickness in the air. A small warmth in all that damp.

---

In the tall grass, movement. Large forms pressing through, their bodies hidden by vegetation that reaches above their shoulders or backs or whatever passes for shoulders in the creatures that have become native to this place. You hear them before you see them: the rustle, the low calls, the heavy breathing of something vast moving through a channel it has worn into the plant mass over generations of following water and green. You never see them clearly. The grass closes behind them, or the scale is too large, or they move through the vegetation the way fish move through kelp, present but never fully visible.

You learn to read the herds by their silences. When the insect drone thickens and then thins, when the birdsong shifts from one pattern to another, when the grass waves in those strange interior rhythms—something large has passed nearby. The small voices go quiet. The system pauses. Then the noise returns, and the presence is gone, moved on to some other channel, some other part of the vast plateau that continues in all directions without meaningful boundary.

---

The plateau is not one thing. This takes time to understand.

It is water and the paths water follows, ancient channels carved so long ago that human time means nothing to them. It is the moss that coats every surface, soft as something that has never known dryness, pulling moisture from air that never quite releases it. It is the grass that rises above your head and sways in waves that have nothing to do with wind. It is the insects and the birds and the mammals and the flowering plants whose names you do not know, each one a presence in a system that is larger than all of them combined.

But it is also a presence itself. You begin to feel this the longer you stay.

The way the vegetation pulses with rhythms that sync to something you cannot name. The way the insect chorus swells and fades according to a clock that doesn't match light or temperature or any external variable you can identify. The way the herds move through patterns that feel less like following resources and more like following a grammar, a set of rules that exist below the threshold of individual awareness.

The plateau is what lives here. The creatures are how it lives.

---

The early ones knew this.

They came, these tool-users, with their hands and their stone and their small shelters built from wood that has long since become indistinguishable from the general vegetation. They left marks in the rock—symbols whose meanings have weathered away, structures whose purposes have gone soft in all that damp. They were here for a while, or for generations, and then they were not. The plateau did not reject them. The plateau did not absorb them violently. The plateau simply continued, and in continuing, incorporated them into its pattern.

The remnants are faint. A foundation half-consumed. A ring of stones arranged with a symmetry that no longer means anything. A mark scratched into rock, its purpose dissolved by centuries of wet air and moss. These are not ruins. Ruins imply collapse, imply something that once stood and now lies broken. These are traces, smudges, suggestions of presence in a place that has not stopped being present.

What happened to them? The question doesn't quite make sense here. The plateau takes everything in eventually. The water follows its channels and the vegetation follows the water and the creatures follow the vegetation, and if you stay long enough—

You begin to wonder if the question of "what happened to them" is the wrong question. Perhaps there was no happening. Perhaps the early ones became the plateau the way the grass became the substrate, the way the moss became the stone, the way everything here has become something other than what it started as. Perhaps "human" was always a temporary state, a wave that passed through and then became the medium itself.

---

The birds carry it further.

Their calls weave through the drone of insects, frequencies layering over frequencies until the air is thick with signal. Some of them you learn to recognize—the sharp alert of something small warning its neighbors, the deep call of something larger establishing presence, the sudden chorus that rises when the heat builds past a threshold and everything with lungs decides to make itself known. But most of them you cannot track. The sounds come from everywhere, from nowhere, from a direction that shifts when you turn toward it.

The birds are not the plateau. They are not absorbed into it the way the early ones may have been. They are wild, individual, each one a creature with its own purposes and fears and hungers. But they move through the plateau like thoughts through a mind, and when you listen long enough, you begin to hear patterns. Response and response, call and answer, a grammar of presence that is also a grammar of place. The birds speak the plateau the way the plateau speaks through the grass and the insects and the slow pulse of water through ancient channels.

You are not part of this conversation. But you begin to understand that the conversation exists, that it has been happening for longer than you can measure, that it will continue after you leave or are absorbed or simply become another breath in the plateau's long exhalation.

---

The heat builds through the morning, and you learn to read it in your body before you read it in the air.

Your lungs thicken. Your skin tightens with moisture that has no source and no destination. The damp settles into your clothes and your hair and the spaces between your fingers, and you stop trying to stay dry. Dryness is not a category that applies here. You are wet, the same way the moss is wet, the same way the air is wet, the same way everything that lives on this plateau is wet as a condition of existence.

And then the rain comes, and you learn that what you thought was wet was merely damp.

The storms roll in from the south with a weight that feels continental, fronts that have traveled far enough to build their own weather, their own momentum, their own sense of purpose. When they arrive, the rain does not fall—it descends, sheets of water that turn channels into rivers and meadows into ponds and your body into something that has forgotten what dry meant. The plateau drinks. The plateau fills. The plateau shows you what it looks like when a place has been thirsty for exactly this long, when the water arrives in quantities large enough to satisfy a hunger that has been accumulating for millennia.

You shelter where you can. The plateau offers no caves, no overhangs, nothing that was shaped for your protection. You stand in the tall grass and let the rain find you, and you realize that you are being marked by this, absorbed by this, the way the moss marks the stone and the grass marks the earth and the water marks the channels that have been waiting for exactly this water since before anything here had a name.

---

What lives here is not separate from where it lives.

You came expecting to observe, to document, to understand a place from the outside. The plateau has other plans. The damp gets into your lungs and stays. The drone gets into your hearing and becomes the silence you hear when you try to sleep. The waves in the grass get into your sense of time and stretch it, thin it, until you are no longer sure whether minutes or hours have passed, whether the rhythm you feel is external or something your body has begun to generate on its own.

The plateau breathes, and you are breathing with it.

This is the uncanny thing, the thing that makes the place strange in a way that is not alien but is also not quite familiar. You are being incorporated. Not violently, not quickly, not in a way you can resist or even identify. But the plateau is taking you in, the same way it took in the early ones, the same way it takes in every presence that stays long enough to stop being separate. You are becoming part of the pattern.

And the pattern is vast, and patient, and has been continuing without interruption for longer than you can imagine.
