What lives here lives as the plateau allows: pressed thin by heat, stretched across distances that punish the small and reward the still. The creatures here have become the place thinks they should be—not through intention, but through the patient arithmetic of survival, the endless subtraction of everything that cannot hold.

---

The lizards are the plateau's first language. They come in a register of browns and grays, colors so precisely matched to the stone that you might look directly at one and still believe it a chip of volcanic glass, a shadow's edge, a place where the light fell wrong. They are small most of them, the size of your thumb or smaller, though you learn quickly not to assume. In the deeper ruins, where the shadows pool even at midday, you find the larger ones—the ones that have grown past the point where most predators bother, their scales thickened and textured like the stone they mimic, their eyes ancient in a way that reptile eyes are always ancient. They do not run. They watch you with the particular stillness of things that have learned that stillness itself is a defense, that to move is to become visible, and visibility is death. You step closer, and then they are gone—not fleeing but simply no longer present, the way a dream becomes no dream when you turn toward it. They live in the cracks, in the spaces between what was built and what remains, in the cool dark beneath a rusted engine block or in the gap where a wall has surrendered to the centuries. They emerge to warmth as the sun climbs, arranging themselves on flat stones like offerings, and they remain until the heat becomes too much, and then they sink back into their holes and wait for the evening to bring it again. They have been here longer than the factories. They will be here when the factories are nothing, when even the rust has become soil and the soil has forgotten that anything once stood above it.

Between the lizards and the insects, the plateau hums. You do not hear it so much as feel it—a frequency in the teeth, a vibration behind the eyes, a sense that the air itself is not empty but dense with motion. The insects are countless and nameless, species so specialized to this microclimate that they exist nowhere else on earth. Some are visible, darting between the rocks, their wings catching light like fragments of mica. Most are not. Most are too small, their entire lives conducted in the spaces between grains of sand, in the moisture that beads and vanishes, in the brief hours when the air holds enough to drink. They are the infrastructure of this place, the workers that no one sees. They pollinate the sparse flowers of the gray-green grasses. They decompose what dies—the lizards, the birds, the small warm things—and return it to the stone. They feed the lizards, the birds, the things that are not quite birds, the things that move in the margins of what you can see. They are the plateau's circulation, its hidden metabolism, the pulse that continues when you believe yourself alone.

The birds announce themselves by their shadows. You look up, and there they are—dark shapes against the white sky, riding the thermals that rise from the dark rock. Vultures first, then hawks, then things you do not have words for, things with wingspans that suggest they should not be able to hold themselves aloft in the thin air but do anyway, their bodies adjusted to altitudes that would stop a human heart. They circle the ruins, the structures, the places where death gathers. They are patient, the birds. They wait on the posts that once carried electricity, their heads tucked, their feathers catching the light. When something dies—and things die here, as things always die, the small warm things taken by the things slightly larger, the insects falling to the insects that are predators, the occasional thing that simply gives up and lets the heat take it—the birds are already present, or they arrive within the hour, drawn by something you cannot sense. They tear at what is dead with their hooked beaks, and they leave the bones for the sun and the beetles and the patient chemistry of decay. They are the plateau's cleaners, its priests of the final threshold, the things that return what is gone to what will be.

The ruins are not empty. This is what you learn if you stay, if you let the days accumulate, if you begin to see the plateau not as a place you visit but as a place that contains you. The small warm things have made the ruins into their own architecture. In the control stations, where the glass is long shattered and the electronics are rust and the consoles are open to the sky, they have built warrens. You see the tracks first—paths worn in the dust, small paired prints, the drag of a tail. Then you see the droppings, the small dark pellets that accumulate in the corners, the places where the walls still offer shelter. Then you see them: quick movements at the edge of vision, things the color of dust and shadow, things that have made themselves the same size as the space between things, things that fit into the world in ways that you never could. They are not rats, exactly. They are not anything exactly. They are what fills the niche that rats might fill, in a place where rats have never been, where the small mammalian body plan has been arrived at by some other route, through some other ancestor, shaped by the same pressures that shape all things that live in the margins of ruins. They are quick and they are shy and they are everywhere. At night, in the deep dark between moons, you hear them moving—the scratch of claws on concrete, the whisper of small bodies against small spaces, the sound of life proceeding without regard for you.

But there are larger things. You sense them before you see them, if you ever see them at all. In the heat-shimmer over the dark rock, the air sometimes moves wrong—patterns that are not wind, currents that suggest mass displacing mass, the passage of something large through a medium that should not be able to hold it. You turn, and there is nothing. Or there is a shape at the edge of vision, something that is there and not there, something that the eye refuses to resolve into a thing. The locals, if there are locals, do not speak of these things. Or if they speak, they speak in the way that people speak of the weather or the geology—as though they were features of the landscape, as though naming them would change nothing. The plateau has its larger inhabitants. The plateau has its older inhabitants. The plateau has things that were here before the factories, that watched the factories rise and watched them fall, that remain.

The plants live differently. They live in relationship to absence—in the cracks where water gathers for hours longer than it gathers elsewhere, in the shadows that persist when all the rest of the plateau has been scoured by light. The gray-green grasses grow in clumps, in colonies, in the places where something has gone right. They are not grasses as you know grasses. They are older, stranger, their roots sunk deep into the stone itself, drinking from moisture that you cannot feel, from the thin groundwater that moves through the volcanic rock in veins you cannot see. The twisted bushes are the same—stunted, silvered, their leaves reduced to the minimum that photosynthesis requires. They grow slowly, so slowly that you cannot see them change, so slowly that you might believe they were always this size, that they will always be this size. But they grow. Given decades, you might return and find them larger. Given centuries, you might find them where there were none. The plateau does not hurry. The plateau's life does not hurry.

And then there is the water, when it comes. The pools that form after rain are not just water. They are events, gatherings, places where the plateau briefly becomes more than it usually is. Something collects in the pools—the mineral dust, the pollen of the grasses, the spores of things you cannot see. The pools become thick with life, with the eggs of insects that will hatch when the next rain comes, with the larvae of creatures whose adult forms you have never seen, with the slow chemistry of things that live in the spaces between water and stone. And sometimes the pools themselves are wrong. Too clear, as though light passed through them without touching, as though they were not quite there. Or too thick, viscous, carrying a faint luminescence that the sun has not put there. You do not drink from the pools. You do not touch the water that lingers after the sun has taken the rest. You watch, and you move on, and you let the plateau keep its secrets.

The plateau watches back. You know this. You have always known this, though you did not have the word for it until you came here. The rocks sometimes show a sheen—not mineral shine, but something that shifts, something that follows your position, something that suggests attention. The animals sometimes act wrong—a lizard that tracks you with too much focus, that moves not away from you but parallel to you, maintaining distance in a way that implies calculation. A bird that makes a sound that is not quite a bird sound, something that lives in the register of human voices but is not human, has never been human. A rustle in the rocks that suggests something larger than the lizards, larger than the small warm things, something that should be visible but is not. The plateau breathes, but the plateau also sees. The plateau has been here longer than anything that might observe it, and observation is not something you do to the plateau so much as something the plateau does to you.

What lives here lives in the interstices. In the cracks, in the shadows, in the hours when the heat makes the air thick and strange. In the brief minutes of rain, in the long hours of sun, in the even longer hours of night when the temperature drops and the plateau contracts and the creatures that hide in the day emerge to do what they do in the dark. The plateau is full—not with buildings, not with people, but with life that has found the shape the plateau allows. It is a life reduced to essentials, hardened by pressure, made strange by time. It is a life that will outlast you, that will outlast your kind, that has already outlasted the loudest attempt to make this place into something it was not. The factories were a fever. The plateau has recovered. The plateau remains.

You stand on the dark stone, and the heat rises around you, and the air shimmers, and somewhere in the distance a bird circles, and somewhere in the cracks something moves, and somewhere in the deep stone water moves that you will never see. You are observed. You are permitted to be here. For now.
