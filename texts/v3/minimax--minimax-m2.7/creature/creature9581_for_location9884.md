# The Water That Climbs

---

They tell you about the water on the first day, but you don't understand until the third night.

You arrive in the late afternoon, when the Shelf is still warm from the sun that filters through the haze above, that permanent grey ceiling that might be atmospheric chemistry or might be particulate left over from some event older than human memory. The transport set you down on the cultivated landing-pad — a flat disc of root-matter that rose to meet the craft before settling back into the green mass when the engines cut — and from there you walked through the corridor that is not a corridor, that is a pathway between walls of compressed vascular tissue that breathe against your shoulders as you pass.

Your guide is a woman whose name you will never learn correctly, something in a language whose phonemes your mouth was not built to shape. She leads you to the room that will be yours, grown inside the heart of a columnar tree so large its diameter must be thirty meters at the base. The room is cool and damp and smells of chlorophyll and mineral and something else, something underneath, a base note like the smell of deep wells before they went dry. You drink from the tap in the wall and the water is cold and tastes of stone and green.

"The water comes up," you say, or ask, and she nods as if you have asked a question rather than made a statement.

"It comes up," she confirms. "It has always come up."

You think she means springs. You think she means aquifers, the deep water table that once fed rivers. You have read the histories, the accounts from before the drying, the desperate years when the last waters retreated into stone and everyone who could afford it fled upward to the high places. You imagine wells, pumps, some remnant of the old engineering still functioning in the root-beds below.

You do not understand.

---

On the first full day you walk the city — if city is the word for what has grown here, this dense habitation of grown-rooms and cultivated corridors and great open plazas where the buildings have become trees and the trees have become buildings and the distinction has dissolved into a single continuous architecture of living tissue. Your guide leads you through the market-halls, where the trade is still in seeds and fiber and the processed outputs of the plant-world: oils that burn cleanly, resins that set hard as stone, membranes that let light through and keep dust out. The people here are darker-skinned than you expected — the haze filters the light, reduces the spectrum, and generations of living beneath this permanent overcast have shifted the melanin in ways that would have been called adaptation in the old literature but which here is simply what everyone looks like. They nod as you pass. They do not stare. They have had visitors before.

You stop at a viewing-pool, a great basin of water maintained by the plants for communal use, and you watch the animals come. They are small and chitinous, their exoskeletons gleaming faintly in the filtered light, their bodies streamlined for moisture retention in ways that make them look like something between insects and crustaceans but larger, the size of dogs, moving in great waves that wash across the substrate and pause at the water's edge to drink. They do not scatter when you approach. They have learned that the people here are not predators, are in fact something they can ignore, and they go about their business with the calm efficiency of creatures who have found a niche and filled it completely. Your guide tells you their names — you will forget them, they are too foreign to your tongue — and explains their roles in the ecosystem, the migrations that move through the Shelf like tides, the prey species, the predators, the parasites that live in the parasites. The world is full.

"The water," you say again, watching the small creatures drink. "Where does it come from?"

Your guide looks at you for a moment, her expression unreadable, and then points down.

"Below," she says. "Always below."

---

On the second day, she takes you down.

The descent is through the root-caves, the ancient infrastructure that the plants have not yet fully colonized — or have chosen not to colonize, you will learn later, because some places must be kept raw, must be remembered. The walls change as you descend: from living tissue to grown stone to bare rock, the grey limestone of ancient seabed, porous and pocked with the shells of things that lived when water still fell from the sky. There are devices here, artifacts from before, and your guide touches them with the casual familiarity of someone who has grown up alongside them. A panel that still glows faintly. A terminal that displays symbols you cannot read. A machine that hums at a frequency you feel in your teeth, drawing power from sources you cannot begin to guess at.

And there, at the lowest point of the descent, there is the water.

It seeps upward through the stone, against gravity, against all physics you learned in school. You watch it happen: a darkening on the grey limestone, a spreading patch of dampness that moves upward through the rock at a pace you can barely track, not dripping but flowing, traveling through channels in the stone so fine they are nearly invisible. You put your hand against the wall and it is wet and cold and the water moves into your palm, and you pull your hand away and the water follows, climbing the wall, continuing its upward journey as if your touch was only a pause in a process that has been running for longer than human settlement.

"How?" you ask, and your voice sounds strange in the cave, echoes back from the stone.

Your guide is quiet for a long moment. Then she says: "The plants learned it from the old machines. Or the old machines learned it from the plants. No one remembers which came first."

She touches the wet stone. "It comes from below. It has always come from below. The stone is full of it, from when the sea was here. The plants reach down, and they pull, and it rises. It takes a long time. But it never stops."

You stare at the water climbing the wall, and you think of the plants above, the great columns reaching upward, the roots you cannot see spreading through the substrate, and you think of them all as a single system, a circulatory network that moves water through the body of the world the way your blood moves through your veins. You think of the tap in your room, the water that tastes of mineral and chlorophyll and stone, and you understand now that you have been drinking something that has traveled upward through millions of tons of ancient seabed, pulled by the patient hunger of a vegetation that learned to live without rain.

---

On the third night, you climb to the viewing platform alone.

The bioluminescence has begun. It starts in the canopy, in the high tips of the columnar trees where the light is strongest, and spreads downward in waves that move like breath, like circulation, like something alive and aware. The whole valley fills with green and blue light, faint at first and then brighter, until you are looking down at a world that glows from within, a world that has learned to make its own light because the sun was taken away. The fauna stirs in response: the chittering chorus rises, the migrations begin, the sound of ten thousand small bodies moving through the green fills the air with a sound like rain on leaves.

And then the roar comes from the high canopy.

It is louder than anything you have ever heard. It vibrates in your chest, in your skull, in the bones of your legs, and you freeze without thinking, your body responding to a predator-call older than language, older than humanity. The prey-animals freeze too. The chittering stops. The world holds its breath.

The roar fades. The silence stretches. And then, slowly, the sounds return: the creak of plant-matter, the distant calls of things hunting, the pulse of the root-network below you, in-out in spans of an hour, the slow breathing of a world that does not need you.

You stand on the platform and you watch the light pulse through the canopy and you think about the water climbing upward through the stone, and the plants that learned to live without sun, and the people who stopped asking where anything comes from because they have lived so long with the impossible it has become the only possible thing. You think about the old machines that still hum in the caves below, drawing power from vacuum fluctuations, and the advanced engineering that is the same as biology is the same as technology is the same as life.

The cold deepens. Your lips are cracked and your throat is dry and you drink from the tap in the wall when you return to your room, and the water tastes of mineral and stone and green, and you understand now that you have been drinking the old sea, the water that has been climbing toward the light for longer than the plants have grown, longer than the city has stood, longer than anyone can remember.

It comes from below.

It has always come from below.
