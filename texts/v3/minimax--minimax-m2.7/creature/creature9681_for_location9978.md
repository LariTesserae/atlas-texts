What lives here is the water first. Not the springs alone, not the seepage alone, but the water in its entirety—falling and rising, dripping and pooling, the mist that gathers on skin and frond alike until the air itself becomes saturated, until breathing is a form of drinking. The water here does not behave as water should. It lingers. It accumulates in the spaces between stone and leaf, in the fine hairs of moss, in the corrosion on the wheel's paddles where it pools in micro-deposits that glisten briefly and vanish into the larger damp. This persistence is the first life of the place, the condition upon which all others depend.

The tree ferns are the second life, and they are many. They crowd and tier, their rhizomes gripping the rock in dense clusters that blur at the edges where one plant ends and another begins. Their fronds do not reach upward so much as outward and inward, pressing against neighbors, creating a continuous surface that separates the basin from the sky. The fronds are dark beneath, lighter above, and when they move—though they rarely move—it is not from wind but from the passage of something through them, some creature too quick or too small to see. The ferns have learned to occupy every ledge, every fault, every horizontal surface wide enough to hold their roots. They have grown over the waterwheel's frame, not consuming it but accepting it as just another structure in the landscape, another vertical element around which to arrange themselves. In time they will cover it entirely. They are patient.

The mosses are a third life, softer and more diffuse. They are not one moss but many—species layered over species, their colors shifting from emerald to chartreuse to a grayish green where the water runs thin. They coat the rocks, thicken at the edges of channels, line the interior of the building where the roof drips and the walls sweat. They are the visible manifestation of the place's moisture, the place made tender. When water moves through them—and it is always moving, always finding new paths downward—the sound is small and continuous, a murmur that suggests speech without language, presence without voice.

These are the vegetal lives, and they are sufficient. But the basin holds others.

The waterwheel belongs to them as well, or they to it. It turns. The fact of its turning is the most insistent claim the place makes upon attention—not because it is loud or dramatic, but because it should not still be turning. The paddles are thick with moss, the axle creaks in its bearing, the speed is slow and heavy as though the wheel remembers effort and has not yet released it. What it drives is unclear. The belt connects to a shrouded box whose interior hums with a low electrical note, a frequency that sits beneath hearing yet somehow registers, a vibration felt more than heard. The pipes that exit the box vanish into the rock, and where they lead, what they serve, remains unknown. The mechanism persists. The purpose does not announce itself. The wheel turns, and the water weeps, and the two are connected by something no longer remembered, perhaps never understood.

The building is less a structure than a growth. Its walls of dark stone are fitted without mortar, and in the spaces between, water seeps. The roof of corroded metal sweats continually, its surface patterned with mineral deposits in concentric rings that suggest years of condensation. The door hangs open on hinges that no longer move; it has settled into its posture of welcome and will not be shifted from it. Inside, the tangle of pipes and gauges continues, and the gauges still register something—needles trembling at positions that may mean nothing, may mean everything, may mean that whatever they measure remains within whatever parameters were set before the people left.

The people. They are the absent life of the place, the lives that should be here and are not. They built the wheel and the building, fitted the pipes, installed the gauges. They understood—or did not understand—what the mechanism was meant to do. They left, or died, or simply stopped tending, and the place absorbed their absence. It did not collapse. It did not decay into ruin. It continued, each element finding a new relationship with the others, the loop closing around the gap they left.

What remains of them is the door hanging open. The wheel turning. The gauges trembling. The choice to stop maintaining, or the circumstance that prevented it, has become irrelevant. The basin breathes. The water persists. The machinery functions because function is easier than failure when no one is watching.

The gastropods are small lives, patient lives. They cluster at the basin's edges where the cliff walls are wettest, pressed against the rock where the mineral content is highest and the surface most hospitable to their slow digestion. Their shells are thick with deposits, iridescent in the diffuse light, layers of calcium and whatever else the water carries, built up over years—over what years, it is impossible to say. They do not acknowledge the presence of visitors. They do not flee. They breathe against the stone, their bodies dark and glistening, and in their stillness they become part of the architecture, part of the place's patience. They are the oldest visible things here, though whether they were here before the people or only after, whether they arrived on their own or were brought, remains as unanswerable as everything else.

Something moves in the fronds. It is not the wind—the fronds move together, synchronized, as though something passes through them rather than brushing against them. The motion is quick and small, scale-feet or feathered, impossible to distinguish because it never pauses, never allows itself to be seen directly. It is the life that refuses observation, the presence that maintains its mystery. It does not matter whether it is reptile or bird or some creature between; what matters is that it moves, that it lives in the upper reaches where the fronds close overhead, that it has found a way to exist in a place that should not support it. It feeds on the insects that feed on the moss, and it is fed upon by nothing, or by something that moves too slowly to be seen. The loop closes around it too.

The insects themselves are too small to see without attention, too numerous to catalogue. They orbit the moisture, breed in the pools, live their brief lives in the damp and die in the damp and return to the moss. They are the base of a food chain that extends upward—to the creature in the fronds, perhaps, and perhaps not. Perhaps the creature in the fronds eats nothing. Perhaps it only moves.

And the place itself. The basin as a whole. This is the life that is hardest to name but most present: the closed loop, the self-sustaining system, the breathing space. The water that should not persist, persists. The humidity that should dissipate, accumulates. The warmth that has no source, holds. The machinery that should require tending, requires nothing. The ferns have covered what needed covering. The mosses have filled what needed filling. The waterwheel has slowed to a speed that it can maintain indefinitely, and the electrical hum continues at a frequency low enough that it does not deplete whatever stores it.

The basin breathes. The walls rise. The sky above is uniform, and the light is diffuse, and the shadows are faint not from lack of darkness but from lack of source, because the darkness here comes from the density of growth, from the layers of frond and leaf, from the way the place folds in upon itself and holds what it holds.

There are no visitors. There is no path. The structures were built by people, but the people are gone, and in their absence the place has found a different equilibrium—not the equilibrium they intended, not the equilibrium they maintained, but one that the basin has chosen for itself, one that suits the water and the ferns and the moss and the wheel and the hum and the gastropods and the quick thing in the fronds and the continuous murmur of moisture finding its way downward.

The loop is closed. The basin breathes. It breathes without lungs, without effort, without beginning or end. The water arrives. The moisture gathers. The fronds hold it overhead like a ceiling, like a sky, like a breath that does not exhale.

What lives here is the place itself, finally. Not its features but their arrangement, not its parts but their persistence, not its purpose but its continuation. The ferns grow over the wheel's frame. The pipes mineralize at their joints. The building's door hangs open on hinges that will never move again. And still the wheel turns. Still the water weeps. Still the air holds its weight of moisture, pressing close, and the small, bounded space maintains its slow, patient breath.

This is what lives here. This is what has always lived here, perhaps. The people came and built, and then they left, and the place absorbed them into its breathing, into its loop, into its patient continuation. The gastropods were here before. The ferns were here before. The water was here before. The people were an interruption, perhaps, a brief complexity that the place simplified around, reduced to their essential contributions—the wheel, the building, the pipes—and then released, allowing them to become what they were always going to become: part of the basin, part of the breath, part of the closed loop that does not require remembering to continue.
