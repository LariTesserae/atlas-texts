They come to the high place in the old way, on foot, following paths that are not paths until they have walked them. The locals — those who remember — do not number themselves, do not need to. They are fewer than they once were, and fewer still than that, and at some point the counting stopped mattering because anyone who knew how to count was also someone who knew to stop. What remains is a loose, distributed knowledge, a knowing that lives in the body rather than in any record, passed forward through gesture and habit, through the particular way one holds a shoulder against the slope of a ridge or turns a face to the fog before deciding the path is passable. They know the plateau the way the body knows the breath: not through instruction but through repetition so thorough it becomes instinct, and instinct so deep it becomes a kind of speaking.

A woman walks a circuit that has no name. She moves through the forest at a pace that would seem inefficient to anyone watching from above — slow, deliberate, her hands sometimes trailing the trunks of trees as though reading them, her weight shifting onto each foot with the patience of something that has learned the ground must be trusted before it can be used. She is not young. The cold and the wet have deepened the lines of her face, have made her something more angular than she might otherwise have been, and the gray in her hair is the color of the lichen that grows in concentric rings around the base of the trees, a gray that is almost green. She is carrying a woven container on her back, though it is light — dried matter of some kind, fungal, selected for properties she could name if asked, though no one asks. She moves between clearings where the breathing vents mark the soil with their particular warmth, and she leaves offerings at three of them — small things, tucked into cracks in the stone where the steam rises — not because the breathings need anything, but because the practice keeps the practice alive, and the practice is a kind of memory, and memory is the only currency that still holds here.

There are others like her, scattered across the plateau in ones and twos, though *like her* is imprecise. They share the land and they share the knowledge, but the knowledge has been shaped by each mind that holds it, has taken on the contours of separate lives. One man tends the water channels that carry seepage from the upper slopes into holding pools where certain plants grow that will grow nowhere else on the plateau — plants that the man cultivates without tools, by the manipulation of flow and light and the careful placement of stones in patterns he learned from a woman who learned it from a woman whose name is now only a sound, a word in a dialect that fewer and fewer people speak. The work is slow. The channels must be cleared after each heavy rain, which is often, and the stones must be repositioned to accommodate the shifts in the soil, which is always. He works with his hands in water that is warm from the deep vents and feet that are numb from the cold that seeps upward through his boots, and the contradiction of it — that the ground can be both warm and cold at once — is something he stopped noticing years ago, is something so woven into his experience of the place that it has become, like the fog itself, simply the texture of being here.

Children grow up knowing the plateau before they know the word for it. They learn to read the weather in the way the mist moves — whether it will lift by midmorning or hold until afternoon, whether the evening fog will come early and thick or late and thin. They learn the animals not as names but as presences, as textures of sound and scent and disturbance in the grass. A child in this place does not ask what made a track in the mud. The child already knows, has known since before conscious memory, because the knowing is in the body of the place, and the child's body has been learning it since birth. They play in the spaces between the breathing vents, where the warmth pools and the air moves in slow spirals, and they learn the edges of the warm zones the way they learn the edges of their own skin — without thinking, without boundary, just a gradual awareness of where the self ends and the not-self begins. Some of them, when they grow older, leave. The plateau does not prevent this. It offers no resistance and no farewell. It simply continues, and the ones who leave carry something with them that they will not understand until they are old, if then — a sensitivity to atmosphere, to the way weather presses against the body, a habit of pausing at the crest of a hill to read the land ahead not with their eyes but with some deeper instrument, some sense the plateau taught them to open and that the lowlands can never quite close.

But the humans are few, and the most populous chapters of life on the plateau are written in forms far older and more numerous than any human hand.

The herds move through the high meadows in the way that water moves through the carved valleys — not with the urgency of the desperate but with the deep, gravitational certainty of the heavy. They are large animals, four-legged, thick-skinned in a way that has nothing to do with leather and everything to do with endurance: their hides are layered with fat and dense with hair that catches and holds the moisture of the fog, so that each animal carries a faint halo of mist around its body, a personal weather system, as though the plateau's own atmosphere had condensed into individual creatures and sent them wandering. They graze in the open spaces where the canopy breaks and the sky is visible in those larger fragments — gray sky, mostly, the color of the moss, the color of the stone after rain — and their grazing is methodical and unhurried, their massive heads moving across the sward with a sweeping patience that looks, from a distance, like the turning of a slow gear in some vast machinery. They do not rush. The plateau has not taught them to rush. There is nothing here that chases them, not in the open, not in the way that matters. The thing that hunts them is patient too, and so they have become patient in return, and the patience of the grazer and the patience of the predator have arrived at the same equilibrium that characterizes everything on the plateau: a slow, mutual acknowledgment, a shared understanding that time is the resource no one on these heights is in danger of running out of.

Their dung is a small ecology in itself. It falls and is immediately colonized — insects first, a shimmering arrival that moves across the pile in a军团 of motion, followed within hours by fungi that bloom in small, pale cups along its surface, followed within days by the soil organisms that incorporate it, and within weeks there is no record that it was ever there except that the grass nearby is a shade greener, is a fraction taller, is carrying within its cellular structure the material of a life that has passed through one body and into the next. The cycle closes so completely that it is difficult to say where the animal ends and the ground begins. The animals carry the plateau in their bodies and return it to the ground that feeds the plants that feed the animals, and the system has no loose ends, no waste, no gap through which material might escape. It is an economy with no currency, or with every currency at once, and it runs without transaction, without negotiation, on the simple, ancient principle that what is taken must be given back.

Above the herds, the sky is not empty.

The flocks move in formations that shift like water over stone, their individual bodies subordinated entirely to the collective motion of the group. They rise from the canopy in sudden eruptions — dark against the gray, a scattering and reconverging that seems to follow no rule and yet follows every rule that governs fluid dynamics, murmuration as mathematics, as the expression of a principle written not in language but in the synchronized response of ten thousand small bodies to the movement of ten thousand neighbors. They wheel over the open meadows, their calls echoing in the fog with a strangeness that the plateau lends to all sound — a flattening, a thickening, as though the air were not just medium but material, as though the noise had to push through something that resistance. Their voices carry differently here than they would in a dry place. There is no echo, or rather, the echo does not return: it absorbs into the moss, into the stone, into the water that is in everything, so that the calls seem to fall downward as much as outward, to sink into the ground rather than bounce off it, to be received by the earth as a kind of input rather than reflected as a kind of output. The birds are adapted to this. Their hearing has learned to distinguish the calls that travel far from the calls that do not, has learned to parse the distance and direction of a voice by the way the plateau's saturated air bends it, and so they navigate not only by sight but by a kind of acoustic topography, a map drawn in sound that they read with their bodies.

And there are the ones who are not seen but who see.

The predators move at the edges — of the herds, of the clearings, of the light. They are present in the way that temperature is present: felt before it is perceived, registered as an absence or a shift before it is identified as a thing. A clearing that was warm becomes cooler. The birdsong stops. The fog, which moves with its own slow purposelessness, seems to thicken in one direction and thin in another. And then the moment passes, and the air warms, and the birdsong resumes, and the grass that was bent by the passage of a body straightens, and the plateau returns to its broad, slow rhythm, and the predator is somewhere else, was always going to be somewhere else, moving through the landscape the way water moves through the stone — not against the grain but along it, finding the channels that already exist, that the water and the predator and the fog have been making together for longer than anything with a name.

In the moss there are lives within lives within lives. The moss itself is a city, a continent of organisms so densely packed into its thick, waterlogged body that a single handful contains more individual lives than all the human beings who have ever stood on the plateau. Microorganisms thread through it. Rotifers and nematodes and tardigrades and a thousand species of mite occupy its layers, moving through channels of water so fine that surface tension binds the water to the plant cells as surely as any wall, forming a world within the world, a hydrology at the scale of the cell, a circulation system that operates invisibly within the visible green. The moss grows by the millimeter and dies by the millimeter, and the boundary between growth and death is not a line but a zone, a region of transition so gradual that no instrument could identify exactly where one becomes the other. It is dying and growing in the same square inch, at the same time, in the same breath.

Along the water channels — the real ones, the ones that run between the ferns and beneath the roots, the ones that carry water from the high seeps down through the soil layers that are themselves living things, teeming with organisms that cycle nitrogen and carbon and phosphorus in the dark, in the wet, in the slow chemistry of decomposition that is the plateau's most fundamental industry — along these channels, the small things are busy in a way that makes the word *busy* inadequate. Springtails rise and fall in bursts of movement that seem random but are not, that trace patterns of resource and risk across the surface film of slow-moving water.larvae of non-biting midges drift in the current, their translucent bodies pulsing with the food they have filtered from the water, converting dissolved organic matter into living tissue with an efficiency that no human industrial process has approached. Caddisfly larvae build their cases from fragments of stone and plant, selecting materials by weight and size with a precision that suggests intention but is only instinct, only the ancient behavioral program of a creature that has been building cases on this plateau for longer than the plateau has had its current shape. They are architects without blueprints, engineers without drawings, and their structures — some of them no larger than a grain of rice — are among the most sophisticated constructions that the plateau produces, load-bearing and hydrodynamic and beautiful in the particular way that useful things are beautiful when they are made perfectly for their purpose.

The breathing vents themselves have their own ecology. Where steam rises from cracks in the stone, the rock is stained in rings of color — orange and yellow and pale green — that trace the zones of temperature, the gradients of heat that radiate outward from the vent like ripples in still water. Thermophilic bacteria live in the hottest zones, organisms that thrive at temperatures that would kill the moss, the ferns, the animals, but that are mild by the standards of the deep earth from which the heat originates. These bacteria are among the oldest life-forms on the plateau, lineage older than the plateau itself, survivors of conditions that predate the current landscape, and they color the stone the way blood colors a vessel — from within, as a function of being alive. Around them, in the warmer but not scalding zones, other organisms cluster. Algae that are not green but gold, or amber, or a deep rust-red, their pigments selected by natural selection for the specific wavelengths of light that filter down through the steam, filtering the filtering light, turning it into something that feeds them, that keeps them alive in the margins of a world that would otherwise be too cold, too dark, too wet. The steam rises and carries with it the faint smell of sulfur and iron, and this smell — the taste of the deep earth, of processes that are going on beneath the soles of every foot and the roots of every tree — is the baseline against which all other smells are measured on the plateau. It is the air's default setting. Everything else — the loam and the moss and the musk and the decay — is an elaboration on it, a set of variations played against the fundamental note of stone and heat and ancient chemistry.

At night the plateau changes in the way that a held breath changes a body.

The fog comes in from the south and fills the world from the ground up, a slow flood that has no urgency, that moves at the pace of water finding its level, and the animals that have been visible during the day — the great dark shapes of the herds in the meadows, the sudden flash of a bird across a gap in the canopy, the slow deliberate locomotion of anything with legs — all of this becomes submerged, becomes memory, becomes a story the air tells itself about what it held during the day. What remains visible is the breath. The steam columns that rise from the vents are the only things that resist the fog completely, or rather, the fog uses them — wraps around them, draws them into itself, turns each column of steam into a pale, twisting presence that rises and dissipates and rises again, the plateau's exhalation made visible by the cold of the night, by the distance between the warm earth and the chill sky. The breathings hiss and sigh with a constancy that is more audible at night than during the day, when the plateau is louder with life and sound, when the fog is thinner and the air is full of the rustling and dripping and movement of ten thousand organisms doing what ten thousand organisms do. At night the scale of the quiet reveals itself. It is not silence. It is the opposite of silence. It is the sound of everything operating at once — the moss growing, the water moving, the stone cooling and contracting, the roots extending by fractions of millimeters, the soil organisms metabolizing in the dark — and the collective sound of this, the net effect of all the plateau's processes running simultaneously, is a quiet that can be felt in the chest, in the teeth, in the spaces between the ribs where the breath catches and holds before it is released.

In this quiet, the plateau breathes. The steam rises. The fog moves. The moss holds its weight of water and its weight of life, and beneath the moss the stone holds its warmth, and beneath the stone the processes that have been running since before the plateau had a name continue without reference to anything above them, without regard for the fact that there are eyes that could see them or ears that could hear them or minds that could hold them as a thing. The plateau does not know it is being witnessed. The steam does not know it is rising. The fog does not know it is beautiful. The vast, slow machinery turns, and will turn, and the sound it makes is the sound of no one listening, and it is enough, and it has always been enough, and the woman on her circuit leaves her small offerings at the breathing vents not because the steam needs a gift but because the gift is a way of saying *I know you are here*, and the steam rises through the night air and the fog absorbs it, and the plateau breathes in, and breathes out, and the breath does not carry the woman's name down into the lowlands where no one would remember it, and the world below goes on knowing nothing of the heights, and the heights go on being everything they are, and the two facts have nothing to do with each other and everything to do with each other, and the fog holds the distance between them the way it holds everything — gently, completely, without effort, without end.
