The animals are the first thing you might not see.

They move at the periphery of the threshold — not hidden, not elusive in the way a deer is elusive, watching from the tree line. Simply *thin*, in the way that a signal is thin when it almost doesn't come through. You might catch a motion from the corner of your eye: something small, low to the ground, crossing the mineral field with a quick, uncertain gait. It is the size of a cat, or perhaps a very large mouse. It has too many legs, or not enough. You will not be certain which, because when you turn to look directly it is already gone, or was never there, or was always there and you have only just begun to perceive it.

There are two. You have seen them both, though not at the same time, and this may be important or may be nothing. The first was in the shadow of a building whose doorway opened onto a room that was also an outside. It did not startle. It did not freeze. It simply continued — across the shadow, into a crease between the wall and the mineral ground — and was gone the way a word is gone after you have finished saying it. The second was out in the open, in the wide mineral field beyond the city's edge, and it moved in a direction that the wind was not moving, or perhaps it moved in the direction of the wind and the wind was moving in two directions at once, which you had not noticed until you watched it.

These animals do not have faces. You are almost certain of this. Not because their faces are obscured or turned away, but because the concept of *face* does not fully apply to them — they are animals in the way that a draft is an animal, that a stain on a wall is an animal. They exist in the 1.4 register: present but uncertain, alive but reluctant. You have not seen them eat. You have not seen them rest. They do not appear to be dying and they do not appear to be thriving. They appear to be *continuing*, which is not the same thing.

Perhaps they are not animals at all. Perhaps they are the plateau's attempt at life — an involuntary twitch in the tissue of a place that has almost stopped being a place. They would be the most honest expression of this plateau's vitality: small, strange, insufficient, and stubbornly, pointlessly *here*.

***

The light lives here too. You must understand this.

It is not the sun. The sun is present — a pale disc behind the haze, too high and too far to give warmth — and it does not behave the way light should. It does not stream. It does not angle. It *hovers*, a diffuse suggestion of illumination that lands on the mineral field and sits there, neither absorbed nor reflected, as though the light is thinking about what to do next and has not yet decided.

When the light brightens — and it does brighten, in slow cycles that have no relationship to the sun's position — it is not the light changing. It is the air. The thin air cycles through states, thins and thickens in some pattern you cannot perceive but can feel, and this cycling is what changes the quality of the light from *barely there* to *almost something*. You have learned to tell time by this — not by the sun's arc, which is meaningless here, but by the slow breath of the atmosphere, the in-and-out of whatever process is keeping this plateau suspended in its own dimness.

The shadows are the worst part. They are not shadows at all, or they are shadows that have remembered that shadows are supposed to behave and have decided, instead, to behave otherwise. You will see a shadow on a wall — a building's shadow, cast by that pale non-illumination — and it will be correct for a time, and then it will begin to thicken at the edges, pooling like liquid, and then it will thin again, and then you will look away, and when you look back the shadow is somewhere it was not. Not nearby. Not adjacent. *Somewhere else*, on a wall that should cast its own shadow, a shadow that is instead somewhere else doing something that shadows do not do.

You stop being frightened of this after a while. You stop being frightened of most things here after a while, because fear requires a sense that the world will behave in ways you can predict and fail to avoid. The world here has set that aside. It is difficult to be afraid of something that is not even sure what direction it is facing.

***

The wind is the city's only voice.

It comes from no particular direction, or from all directions, or from the place where directions have gone to stop meaning anything. It moves through the empty streets — those avenues wide enough for processions, those plazas where a thousand people might once have gathered — and it makes sounds that are almost language. Not quite. Not words. But the shape of words, the rhythm of speech heard through a wall, the sense that meaning is being attempted just beyond the edge of comprehension.

In the narrowest streets, where the buildings lean toward each other and their upper stories nearly touch, the wind hums. It finds the gaps in the stone — the spaces where mortar has not crumbled but has *faded*, has relaxed from the concept of *holding together* — and it passes through and the stone answers. Not with sound, exactly. With a change in the quality of the silence. The silence in those streets is a different silence: thicker, more particular, the silence of a room that has been lived in for a very long time and has absorbed the texture of voices.

You press your ear to the wall in one of these streets and hear something that might be an echo of a word. The syllable is worn, rounded, repeated so many times in its original use that it has been sanded down to a single tone. You do not know what it meant. You are not sure the city knows anymore. But it is still saying it. That is the dynamic, the 2.5 out of 3 — the city is still *saying* something, even if it no longer knows what.

***

The mineral ground is not dead.

This is important. The ground is not dead in the way that a stone is dead, not absent in the way that a vacuum is absent. It is *complete* — finished, self-contained, needing nothing — and there is a difference. A dead thing was once alive and is not now. A complete thing never needed life to begin with. The mineral field beneath you is the texture of a thing that was always mineral and has simply had the courtesy to solidify.

But it remembers being liquid.

You see this in the formations where it crystallizes — not salt, not silicate, something that catches the pale light and holds it the way a held breath holds air. The fracturing is geometric but not rigid; the angles suggest flow, the way a frozen wave suggests the movement that preceded it. In places, the mineral has formed into shapes that are almost architectural — low arches, raised ridges — as though the ground is trying to build itself into a city of its own, a city beneath the city, made of the same substance the first city was made from and has now returned to.

You kneel and put your hand flat against it. The surface is smooth — not polished-smooth but *worn* smooth, the way a stone in a riverbed is smooth, except there is no river and there has been no river. The smoothness is the smoothness of time, and time here is heavy and slow and has nothing else to do. The ground accepts your hand. It does not warm to your touch — it cannot, it has forgotten the relationship between warmth and presence — but it does not push you away. It is patient. It has been patient for a very long time.

When you lift your hand, the impression of your palm lingers for a moment in the mineral, a shallow depression that fades as you watch. The ground has already decided it was never there.

***

There is something below.

Not beneath — *below*, which is a different relationship in this place, where depth and direction have loosened from their meanings. You feel it in the soles of your feet before you know it is there: a vibration, low and deep, felt in the bones rather than heard, a frequency that is less like sound and more like the earth remembering that it is thinking.

It is slow. Whatever it is, it is slow in the way that geological time is slow, the kind of slow that makes a single thought take centuries. It is not tectonic. Tectonic forces are mechanical, bound by cause and effect, by pressure and release. This is not mechanical. This is the plateau's own thought, its own slow dreaming, rising from whatever is left of the infrastructure of meaning beneath the stone.

Perhaps it is the dissolution process itself — the chemical or metaphysical reaction by which this place has been slowly forgetting how to be a place — and what you feel is the rhythm of that forgetting, the pulse of a world actively losing its worldliness. Perhaps it is older. Perhaps there was something here before the city, something that the city was built on top of or out of, and it is still there, still moving, slower than the city by orders of magnitude, like a thought being thought so slowly that each individual thought takes longer than a civilization.

You stand very still. The vibration passes through you. You feel it in your sternum, your jaw, the backs of your eyes. It is not unpleasant. It is not pleasant. It is the vibration of a place that is still, against all odds, *active*. And for a moment, standing on the dry mineral ground in the dim light with the wind sighing through the empty streets and the shadows doing their slow wrong things on the walls, you feel what the plateau is feeling: not distress, not peace, but the vast, patient, uncomprehending effort of something that is still trying to be.

***

The city itself. The buildings. The walls and windows and rooms.

They are here. They stand. But standing is not what it used to be. Standing here means *persisting in the decision to be vertical*, and some of them have begun to question this decision. You walk past a building whose upper floors are horizontal, lying flat against the sky as though they have lain down, and the ground floor is still upright, and this is not ruin and it is not construction and it is not a fall. It is a *change of mind*. The building has decided, slowly, over decades or centuries, that vertical was optional.

You step inside one of the rooms that is still a room. The walls are smooth — smoothed not by hands but by time, which has the same effect and none of the intention. The floor is patterned, or you think it is, or you want it to be, because a floor should have a pattern and your mind is filling in what the world has left blank. The room is empty of furniture, of debris, of any evidence that a life was once lived here. But it is not empty of *presence*. The walls hold a texture that is not texture — a sense of *having been shaped*, of stone that remembers being shaped, of matter that was once touched by intent and has not yet finished forgetting that touch.

You put your hand on the wall. The stone is cool. It does not warm to your hand. The relationship between body and stone — between heat and presence, between pressure and response — has been filed down by time to a whisper. But the wall *accepts* your hand. That is the word. It receives your touch the way a word receives its speaker: briefly, without change, carrying you for a moment and then letting you go.

***

And the dissolution — the dissolution is alive. You must know this.

It is not a force. It is not a process in the mechanical sense. It is the weather of this place, the climate, the atmosphere of unbecoming that saturates everything. It is 2.9 out of 3, which means it is almost the whole story. The dissolution is the thing that lives here most honestly — not the animals, not the wind, not the slow thought below. The dissolution itself. It moves through the stone and the mineral and the air and the light, not destroying, not healing, but *editing*. Going back through the world's draft and crossing things out. Not because they are wrong. Because they are tired. Because being a thing is an effort that the plateau has been making for longer than you can fathom and is now, gently, beginning to set down.

You feel it when you stay too long. The edges of your own thinking soften. The word *road* begins to feel optional. The relationship between *here* and *there* loosens. You start to understand, in the way that understanding happens here — slowly, without language, in the bones — why the buildings have horizontal roofs and the streets curve when you are not looking at them and the shadows move against the light. It is not malfunction. It is the plateau *resting*. It is the city letting its structure relax, holding itself together less tightly, allowing the possibility that *holding together* was always a choice and choices can be unmade.

This is what lives here: the unbecoming itself. Not a death. Not a birth. The pause between, stretched out over eons, in which a world is still present but no longer certain. And in that pause, in that vast and quiet gap between being and having been, something persists — a vibration in the ground, a wind through empty streets, a faint almost-sound that might be a word, might be a heartbeat, might be the last steady thought of a plateau that has not yet finished being a place and has not yet decided whether it wants to.

It breathes. Very slowly. You have been listening for an hour and you are only just beginning to hear it.
