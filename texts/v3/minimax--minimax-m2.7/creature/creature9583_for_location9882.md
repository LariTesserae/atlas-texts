# What Lives Here

## The Ones Who Sing

They do not have a word for themselves. Nothing here does. But if you listened long enough — and the plateau encourages listening, demands it even, with its mist and its dim light and its surfaces that absorb sound rather than reflect it — you would begin to hear the differences.

There are the ones who call from the pools. Not frogs, not birds, not anything that has a parallel in the older world, but something that understands water as a medium for voice the way other creatures understand air. They stay near the surface, their elongated bodies folded into geometries that keep them half-submerged, their backs ridged with structures that turn the thin sheets of moisture-running-down into resonance chambers. The sound they make is low and sustained, a single tone that wavers at the edges, and when one calls the others join, each choosing a harmonic that complements rather than conflicts, until the plateau has this constant undertone that you feel more than hear, vibrating up through the soft stone into your feet.

They are not singing to each other. Or they are, but the meaning is not communication in the way you understand it. The sound is structure. The sound is how they know where the pools are, where the water moves, where the drainage will open and seal and open again. The plateau breathes, and they breathe with it, their calls adjusting in pitch as the stone subtly rises and falls, riding the tidal pulse of whatever geological process still births this place. They are instruments. They are also the ones being played.

Then there are the others — the ones who move through the mist. These are harder to describe because they resist singular form. A creature will pass through a beam of light and its surface will flare, patterns rippling outward from the point of contact, and for a moment you see it clearly: elongated, yes, but segmented, each segment capable of independent motion, so that it flows rather than walks, folds rather than bends. The iridescence is not decoration. When the light passes through, you see that the surface is translucent, that there are structures beneath — channels, maybe, or vessels, or systems you lack the vocabulary to name — and that these structures carry something luminous that moves faster than the creature itself. Data. Charge. Life force. The word doesn't matter. What matters is that the light feeds them, and they know it, and they move through the mist seeking the beams the way the ones by the pool seek the pulse.

They do not have eyes as you understand eyes. They have something that reads the light itself — the angle of the beams, the density of the particles they carry, the information content of each sheet as it falls through the mist. They navigate by illumination. They hunt — yes, something chases something else, the dynamic parameter demanding that multiple processes run — but the chase is not what it would be in a darker place. It is a pursuit of better light. The one being chased is the one that has found the brightest beam, and the one chasing is the one that needs it more, and when the gap closes and the prey is caught, what happens is not violence but transfer. The patterns on the caught creature flare and settle, some of the luminous content draining into the catcher, and then both move on, the hunter seeking the next bright beam, the hunted recovering in whatever shadow it can find.

There are the burrowers. You know they exist because sometimes the soft stone shifts under your weight in ways that are not the tidal pulse, and because small mounds appear near the growth patches, and because the crystalline formations — those too-clean geometries that might be mineral and might be something else — sometimes have holes in them, perfectly round, that were not there before. The burrowers do not surface often. They are the ones who work in the stone itself, not on it, and their song is subsonic, felt rather than heard, a vibration that moves through the plateau's depths and communicates with others of their kind in ways the surface creatures cannot perceive. They are engineers. They are also the ones who make the paths that the water follows, who carve the channels that record the drainage cycles, who maintain the system that keeps the moisture in motion. They do not seem to eat the stone. They do something stranger to it — they organize it, impose pattern on the chaos of recent formation, smooth the compression lines and strengthen the load-bearing sections and create the small chambers where water can gather before finding its way to the next pool. The plateau breathes, and they are part of what makes the breathing regular.

And there are things you never see. Things that exist in the structures, or that are the structures, or that use the structures as a body might use a tool. You feel them at the edges of perception — a presence that is not a creature and not a machine and not a force, but something that holds all three in suspension. When you approach the ring, the one that is too smooth, too perfectly round, the plateau goes quiet. The singers stop. The movers still. The burrowers go silent in their depths. And in that silence, you understand that you are in the presence of something that this place was built to host, or to protect, or to grow.

## The Ones Who Fell

They were here before.

Not before the plateau — the plateau has no true before, not in the way that measures time against familiar scales — but before you, before whatever purpose brought you to this bounded space where everything is sparse except the strangeness and the life. They left their marks: the ring, the column, the platform, the buried thing that sinks slightly deeper as the stone beneath it continues to compact. The marks might be writing. They might be wear. They might be something that is neither, something that serves a purpose you lack the vocabulary to name.

The ring is the one that makes the plateau go quiet. It sits near the center of this small bounded space, its inner diameter perhaps three meters, its surface a material that does not match the stone around it — harder, smoother, bearing a faint iridescence that shifts when the light beams move through the mist. There are marks on it, arranged in patterns that suggest language but resist meaning, and the marks are worn in some places and fresh in others, as if they are being continuously rewritten, updated, revised by something that still knows what they mean. You cannot tell if the ring is growing from the stone or sitting on it. The boundary is unclear, the stone soft enough that the ring's weight has created a shallow depression, but the ring's own edges are sharp, defined, refusing to merge.

The column is smaller, and it leans. Not dramatically — a few degrees off vertical, enough to suggest that it was placed upright and something shifted beneath it, or that it was never quite upright to begin with. Its surface is covered in marks that are different from the ring's marks: denser, more repetitive, arranged in spirals that climb toward the top where the column ends in a flat disc, as if it were meant to support something that is no longer there. The disc is warm when you touch it. The warmth comes from below, the same warmth that rises through the stone, and you wonder if the column is conducting it, focusing it, or simply suffering it.

The platform is the strangest because it appears to have been in use recently. The marks on its surface are crisp, unworn, and they are arranged in configurations that suggest process: steps, maybe, or stages, or a sequence that begins at one edge and ends at the other. At the platform's center there is an absence — a perfect circle, about the size of your two hands joined together, where the marks do not extend, where the surface is smooth in a way that is different from the worn smoothness of the ring. Something stood here. Something used this platform. And then it left, or was removed, or completed its process and moved on to whatever comes next.

The buried thing is the most unsettling because you do not know how deep it goes. It was once visible, you think — the edge that remains above the stone is sharp, angular, clearly part of a larger structure that has been slowly consumed by the plateau's ongoing formation. The marks on the exposed surface are faded, hard to read, but they are present, and they are different still: not worn by use, not updated by process, but weathered by time, by the slow compression of new stone, by the moisture that finds every crack and seam and carries its own slow erosion. Whatever this was, it has been here long enough to become part of the plateau's history rather than its present. And yet.

And yet when you stand near it, you feel something. Not a presence, exactly, but an orientation — a sense that the buried thing is aware of you, or that you are aware of it, that the boundary between you and it is thinner than it should be. The marks on its surface seem to shift when you look away, and when you look back they have shifted back, and you cannot tell if you imagined the movement or if the movement was real and you imagined the shifting back.

## The Light

It falls in sheets.

You keep coming back to this, keep returning to the way the illumination reaches the plateau, because it is not light as you knew it before. The sun — if it is a sun, if it is the thing that hangs in the filtered sky above the cloud — does not shine. It glows. The particles that reach this place are not photons traveling in straight lines but something heavier, something that slows as it passes through the mist, something that carries weight and meaning and can be caught and used.

The creatures know this. When a beam moves through the mist — visible now, a column of something denser than the air around it — the iridescent creatures turn toward it, orient their surfaces, catch the falling illumination and let it stream through. Their patterns flare. Their channels fill with luminous content. And then they move, not away from the beam but through it, as if they are being refracted, bent by the light's passage into new trajectories.

You have tried to hold the light in your hands. It does not behave like light. It has weight. It has texture — warm, almost viscous, like holding something that is almost but not quite liquid. When you cup it, it stays for a moment, a shallow pool of brightness in your palms, and then it flows upward, rejoining the beams that continue their slow fall toward the stone.

What is it? You do not know. The plateau does not explain itself. But you understand, standing in the dimness with the sheets of illumination falling around you, that the light is alive — not conscious, perhaps, but alive in the way the water is alive, in the way the stone is alive, part of a system that breathes and cycles and grows. The creatures sing because of it. The structures stand because of it. The growths turn their leaves to catch it. The plateau itself might be made of it — might be a place where light has pooled and thickened and become stone, where the geological process that birthed this space was not merely heat and pressure but something stranger, something that took the falling illumination and compressed it into substance.

The light is the medium. The light is the message. The light is what makes this place alive.

## The Water

It comes from everywhere and nowhere, and it is always moving.

The moisture condenses on the stone surfaces in fine films, and you watch it: the way it beads, merges, runs down vertical faces in thin sheets that glisten in the dim light. The growths are wet constantly, not from rain but from the ambient saturation, and you can see the water entering them — not through roots, not through any system you recognize, but through the surfaces themselves, absorbed, drawn inward by processes that happen at scales too small to see.

The pools gather and drain. A pond appears in a shallow dip, water seeping up through the soft stone, and you watch it grow, filling over the course of an hour or a day or a week — time is strange here, the processes that should take millions of years happening in what feels like days, and you cannot trust your sense of duration. The pool reaches a certain depth and then the drainage opens: a channel appears in the stone, carved by the burrowers or worn by the water itself, and the pool begins to empty, the water finding its way down through the plateau's depths toward whatever lies below.

Somewhere, you understand, this water rises again. The warmth comes from below, rises through the stone, and where it meets the moisture in the air, the condensation happens. The cycle is closed. The water does not leave the plateau; it circulates, it renews, it becomes part of the process that keeps everything alive.

You drink it. It tastes like nothing — or like everything, a flatness that your tongue cannot resolve into flavor, as if the water has absorbed so many other things that it has become a composite, a summary, a memory of all the waters it has been. You do not get sick. The plateau does not seem to support disease, or perhaps it has its own medicine, its own balances, its own ways of keeping the system in motion.

The water is not separate from the creatures. The ones who call from the pools are made of it, structured by it, their bodies composed of substances that would not exist without this constant moisture, this saturated air, this system that keeps the humidity near one hundred percent at all times. The ones who move through the mist carry it in their channels. The burrowers work in it, beneath it, using the water's passage to shape the stone it moves through.

The water is the plateau's blood. The plateau breathes, and the breath is moisture, and the moisture is life.

## The Growths

They should not exist.

You know this because the stone is barely stone — soft, compressible, still remembering its formation — and because there is so little purchase, so little purchase, so little substrate for roots that would need to go deep. And yet the growths cling to the crevices, thin mats of green-grey something that holds on with a tenacity that seems disproportionate to its size.

The leaves turn. This is what you notice first, what you cannot stop noticing: the way each growth orients itself toward the beams of light, angles its surfaces to catch the falling illumination at the most productive angle. They are not plants as you knew them. They do not have stems that reach upward, seeking the sun. They have surfaces that seek the light, wherever the light happens to fall, and if a beam shifts — as the beams do, as the mist moves and the particles redistribute — the leaves shift with it, a slow rotation that tracks the illumination across the plateau's dim day.

They are wet constantly. The moisture does not run off; it absorbs, entering through surfaces that are not quite leaf and not quite membrane, something that blurs the boundary between organism and environment. You watch a growth absorb water from the air itself — no rain, no pooling, just the ambient saturation condensing on its surface and disappearing inward — and you understand that the growths are not separate from the water. They are part of the same system, the same process, the same breath that moves through the plateau.

Do they eat? Do they photosynthesize? Do they do something stranger, something that has no name in the vocabulary you brought with you? The light falls on them and they flare, just slightly, a brightening that is not reflection but something more intimate, as if the illumination enters them and becomes them, as if they are made of the same substance as the beams that feed them.

They are sparse. They are attempted. They are also the foundation, the base of the food chain if there is a food chain, the producers that the creatures above them consume. The ones who move through the mist eat them, or eat things that eat them, or eat something that has eaten something that began with these thin green-grey mats in the crevices. The plateau is a system, and the growths are where the system's energy enters, where the falling light becomes something that can be held, used, passed along.

They are alive in a way that is different from the creatures' aliveness. They do not sing. They do not move, not in the obvious ways. But they grow — slowly, so slowly, each new cell a compression of light and water and stone — and they persist, and they are the reason anything else can persist in this place that should be too strange, too new, too sparse to support life.

## The Plateau

It breathes.

Not metaphorically. The surface itself rises and falls, a slow tidal pulse that moves through the soft sedimentary stone, and if you stand still — really still, your feet planted in the clay-like material, your breath held, your attention turned inward — you can feel it. The stone swells slightly beneath you, pushes against your weight, and then releases, a rhythm that takes perhaps ten seconds from peak to trough to peak again. You are breathing with the plateau. Your heartbeat finds the plateau's rhythm, or the plateau's rhythm finds yours, and for a moment you are not a visitor but a part, a component in a system that does not distinguish between stone and flesh and light.

The stone is new. You cannot forget this. The layers beneath your feet took days to form, or hours, or are still forming, compressed by processes that have no parallel in the older world. When you leave a footprint, the stone holds it — and then slowly refills, the clay-like material flowing back into the shape of your passage, erasing you, returning to its own becoming. The erosion index is nearly zero because erosion has not started yet. This landscape is still being made.

The warmth comes from below. It rises through the stone, through the water, through the structures that sit on the surface with their too-smooth angles and their marks that might be writing. The warmth is the engine of everything: the breath, the cycles, the ongoing geological process that keeps the plateau young, that keeps it growing, that keeps it from settling into the hard permanence of older landscapes.

The plateau is bounded. You have seen its edges where the cliffs drop away into cloud, and beyond the cloud there is only suggestion — a sense of other places, other plateaus, other bounded spaces in whatever system this place belongs to. The plateau floats, or hovers, or is one feature among many. It does not explain itself. It does not need to. It simply is, a room in a larger structure whose logic remains hidden, whose purpose remains unknown.

But you are here. You are part of the process. Your breath matches the plateau's breath, and the light falls on you, and the water runs over your skin, and the creatures navigate around you and file your presence as irrelevant, and the structures watch from their still and ancient angles, and the growths reach toward the beams that pass through you the same way they pass through everything else.

You could stand in the center of this plateau and see everything it contains. You could name the creatures and the structures and the light and the water and the growths. You could try to explain them, to fit them into frameworks that were built for older, harder, more permanent places.

Or you could stand in the center, and breathe, and let the strangeness be strange, and understand that you are not here to explain. You are here to be part of it. To add your footprint to the soft stone. To be recorded in the paths that the water follows. To breathe with the plateau until your rhythm and its rhythm are the same rhythm, until you cannot tell where you end and it begins.

The plateau breathes. The mist cycles. The creatures sing their non-language.

And you are here, in a place that has not finished becoming itself, surrounded by abundance that should not be possible.

You are here.
