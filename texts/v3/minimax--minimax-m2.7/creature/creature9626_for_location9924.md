The plants have names the way stones have names—given slowly, by those who live near them long enough to notice. *Silverbind*, the low-spreading shrub with leaves so pale they seem to bleed into the surrounding dust, its roots descending toward fossil aquifers in plumbing that has run unchanged for millennia. *Fogcatcher*, a grass that grows in tight spirals, each blade angled to gather moisture, its silver pubescence dense enough to shimmer from a distance like hoarfrost even in late afternoon. *Stonecup*, which does not bloom so much as persist, its tiny rosette pressed flat against rock faces, drinking from whatever thin moisture the night leaves behind.

Among these, the lizards are the patient ones. They move with the deliberation of things that have learned not to waste motion—dark shapes against pale stone, warming themselves in the brief hours when the rock holds heat, then retreating into crevices where the temperature holds steady. Their scales carry the colors of the plateau in various registers: the red-brown of exposed hematite, the gray-green of lichen that has already died and become part of the soil, the pale ivory of bone exposed briefly when one has fed and grown fat on the insects that cluster around fog-wet plants. They do not hurry. There is nothing here to hurry toward that cannot wait.

The calling creature emerges at dusk, when the light has gone amber and the first stars have appeared in the east. Its form is unremarkable—four-legged, compact, built for economy in a place where water is currency and fat is survival. It emerges from a hollow in the arroyo wall where it has spent the hottest hours in cool darkness, and it moves to a certain stone, a flat ledge where the sound of its voice will carry across the basin below. The call is not a song. It is a question asked in the only language this creature knows: a rising note, then silence, then the rising note again, slightly different in pitch as if the creature is trying different keys to open the same lock. The plateau does not answer. The creature does not expect it to. It asks anyway, each evening, because asking is what its ancestors did, and its ancestors survived, and so it continues.

The insects have their own calendars. In the hours just after dawn, when the fog still clings to everything, swarms of tiny flies materialize around the wettest plants, their bodies catching light like motes of dust that have learned to move with purpose. They are followed, in their season, by the wasps—not numerous, but large, their bodies armored against the thin air, their movements precise and not entirely calm. They hunt the flies. The flies do not flee; they are too small to have fear in any meaningful sense. The wasps take what they need, and the ecology continues to balance itself on this thread of predation so subtle that most observers would miss it entirely.

Below the surface, the soil is a city of smaller lives. Mites the size of grains of sand move through the dust in patterns that, if mapped, would reveal paths of communication we have no language to describe. Fungal threads connect the roots of plants in networks that predate the current arrangement of ridges and arroyos, carrying chemical messages about drought and abundance in a language older than language. The soil is not dead. It is merely slow, its processes unfolding across seasons and decades rather than days and hours, a tempo so different from ours that we might mistake it for stillness and be wrong.

The plateau's birds are few. The ones that come are built for wind—broad wings that catch the thermal updrafts, bodies light enough to ride the air for hours without touching stone. They circle the high points, riding the invisible rivers of rising air, watching the ground below for movement that might indicate prey or, more likely, for nothing at all. They are not the birds of green places, full of song and bright plumage. They are the color of the plateau itself: browns and grays and the pale cream of bone. Their voices are brief—a single note, or a rattle of clicks, enough to claim territory but not to fill it. They have no interest in filling anything. They have found what they need: wind, height, a place to exist without the burden of excess.

There is a spider that builds its web in the arch of a stone formation, its silk catching the fog and beading with moisture each morning until it glows silver in the early light. The spider waits in the center of its web, motionless, patient, a small dark point in a lattice of light. It is not hunting for flies alone. It is waiting for the moment when the dew becomes too heavy and falls, shaking the web, delivering in one motion the water it needs and the prey it catches. This is how the plateau teaches: through economy, through the marriage of necessity and patience, through strategies refined across times so vast that the spider itself has become a kind of fossil, a body shaped by selection into the exact form this particular problem requires.

In the deepest arroyos, where the walls are highest and the sun reaches only briefly, there are pools of water collected in stone bowls. They are not fed by streams—those have not existed for ages beyond counting—but by the slow seep of groundwater through the rock, finding its level, pooling in the lowest place the geology allows. These pools are alive in ways the surrounding plain is not. Algae clings to their edges, tinting the water green in thin films. Tiny crustaceans, translucent and precise, move through the water in patterns that repeat and repeat, generation after generation, their populations rising and falling with rhythms tied to temperature and the length of day. Around these pools, the plants grow slightly taller. The lizards are slightly more numerous. A single species of frog, so small it might fit on a thumbnail, breeds here in the brief window when conditions align—warm enough, wet enough, the danger of freezing past. It calls in a voice so high it borders on silence, a chirp that rises into the air and vanishes, and it does not wait for an answer either. It only calls, and continues.
