# Inhabitants

---

There are people who were born here and people who came. The born know nothing else. The born have never experienced a foundation that did not sway, never stood on ground that remained where they left it. They walk the catwalks with a particular gait—slightly forward-leaning, weight distributed for the give of tensile cable, ready for the subtle translation that comes with wind or temperature shift or the slow negotiation of geology beneath. When they visit other cities, they find the stillness of true ground unsettling. They wait for the floor to move. It never does.

The newcomers arrive by cable-car from the lowland stations, hauled up the face of the Massif in baskets that sway more than they should. Most come for the wages, which are high because the work is dangerous and the foundries are always shorthanded. Some come for reasons they don't articulate, drawn by reports of the sound, of the mineral, of a place where things are still being decided. They arrive expecting hardship, expecting strangeness. They do not expect to stay.

Most of them stay.

---

The foundry workers are the first to change.

The resonant frequency is not audible in the ordinary sense—it operates below hearing, in the register of pressure and bone. New workers describe it as a fullness in the chest, a sense of being inside a sound rather than beside one. After a month, this sensation resolves into tolerance: the frequency becomes invisible, unnoticeable, as much a part of experience as heartbeat. After a year, the tolerance deepens into something else. The workers stop describing the sound and start describing what it says.

It speaks in textures rather than words. A worker might describe the morning shift as *slippery*, or the maintenance period as *cracked*, or a good extraction as *full*. They develop a vocabulary of feeling that outsiders cannot parse. The foundries keep dictionaries of these terms, laminated cards posted near the resonant chambers, but the cards are understood to be approximations. The real language is lived.

The dependency comes after. The body acclimates to the frequency so completely that silence becomes painful—not loud in the way of noise but loud in the way of absence, a pressure differential that the nervous system reads as wrong. The workers must return to the chambers during their off-hours, sitting in the outer rings where the sound is faint but present, just to feel right. Some take jobs in the ventilation systems, the hot-air returns, anything to stay near the thrum.

After longer, something else. The workers who have been here longest—they describe it variously as *settling*, *joining*, *remembering*. They say the frequency is not something being made by the machinery. They say the machinery is reminding the rock what it sounds like. They say the kalite knows this, that it is drawn to the resonance because resonance is what it wants to become. They say the sound is trying to remember what it used to be, and they want to help.

No one who has worked the foundries for more than a decade has ever left Kaldenvast.

---

The children run in packs through the middle districts, the zones where the suspension is oldest and most proven, where generations of engineering have created something that approximates stability. They play on the catwalks and in the nets, games that involve a great deal of falling safely, tumbling into meshes that catch and release. They climb the cables that adults are wary of, shimmy up the tensile rigs to places where the city thins into scaffolding and sky.

They catch climbing-scorpions and keep them in jars, watching the pale arthropods move through angles that seem to require more joints than they possess. The scorpions are docile in the way of things too strange to be aggressive, amenable to handling, content to taste whatever is offered—skin, cloth, wood, the rubber of boot-soles. The children have learned not to let them taste the same spot twice. The numbness accumulates.

The children know things that adults have forgotten. They know which cables sing when the wind is right, which corners are darker than they should be, which rooms in the Sump Quarters have plants growing inward instead of outward. They know where the deep-dwellers emerge most often, which intersections to avoid in the quiet hours, how to make enough noise to feel safe without disturbing the neighbors. They have maps of the city that exist nowhere else—inventories of the impossible, passed down in whispers and dares.

When a child disappears, the city does not panic. The children know this. They have their own rituals of mourning, their own ways of marking absence, because absence here is not always permanent. Some children wander into the deep tunnels and come back changed, missing pieces, speaking of corridors that were not there before. Some do not come back at all, and even their absence has a shape, a presence, a sense of not-quite-gone.

---

The deep-dwellers are not understood.

This is not a failure of science. Science is present in Kaldenvast, rigorous and adaptive, the kind of science that must account for physics that have not yet fully committed. The expeditions have been mounted, the surveys conducted, the specimens examined. What emerges is only the negative space of knowledge: what they are not, what they cannot be, what they do not do.

They are pale. This is consistent. The pallor is not the white of absence but the white of protection, of shielding from a spectrum that does not reach where they live. Their joints are too many—not dramatically so, not grotesquely, but the count does not match any taxonomy, and the articulation moves in planes that seem to exist at angles to the rest of the anatomy. They are the size of large dogs, or sometimes small humans, and their proportions shift when observed directly, as if the measurement itself is unstable.

They move slowly. This is the most disturbing thing. The slowness is not infirmity but deliberateness, a pace that suggests they have no reason to hurry, that time here is not the same as time up above. They emerge from the tunnels singly, always singly, never in pairs or groups. They move through the lower districts, which are already emptying as the population migrates upward, and they do not interact with anything they encounter. They do not eat. They do not drink. They do not rest.

The expeditions have mapped sections of the deep tunnel system, corridors and chambers that predate the city's suspension, voids in the rock that were always there or that formed later or that may not exist yet in any stable sense. The deep-dwellers are encountered in these spaces, but they are not encountered in numbers. They are not encountered at rest. They are not encountered doing anything that suggests purpose. They are simply present, moving through, as if traversing rather than inhabiting.

Some workers say the foundries call to them. Some say the deep-dwellers come up when the resonance is silent, drawn by the same absence that drives the workers to return. Some say they are looking for something they lost, or something they have not yet lost, or something that exists only in the gap between what was and what will be.

No one has ever seen one die. No one has ever seen one born.

---

The engineers are a quiet breed.

They move through the city's suspension systems with a particular care, adjusting cable tensions, inspecting anchor-points, monitoring the friction-fitted joints for signs of slippage. They carry instruments that measure imperceptible shifts—the millimeter-scale movements of geology that, over time, become meters, become the relocation of entire cliff faces. They are the ones who notice when the face you grew up next to has moved to the east. They have been noticing for generations.

The engineering is not precise in the way of mature cities. There is no standard, no code, no guarantee. There is only the accumulated knowledge of what holds, transmitted from masters to apprentices in the language of feel and sound—the way a cable should hum when plucked, the particular groan that indicates trouble, the silence that means danger. The engineers learn to read the city's suspension the way sailors read the sea, with attention to patterns that are not quite patterns, rhythms that are not quite rhythms.

They do not trust the cables completely. They trust them enough. This is the philosophy of Kaldenvast, shared by everyone who lives here: enough trust to function, enough suspicion to survive. The cables are inspected constantly, replaced preemptively, watched for signs of metal fatigue and cable-stretch and anchor-shift. The engineering is good. The engineering is not perfect. The engineering does not need to be perfect. It only needs to hold long enough for the geology to decide what it wants to be.

The engineers say this is the secret of the city, the thing outsiders never understand. Kaldenvast is not defying the geology. It is negotiating with it. The suspension is not a triumph over instability but an accommodation to it, a way of staying present while the ground beneath makes up its mind. The city will fall eventually, or the rock will settle, or both will come to terms. Until then, the engineers adjust the cables, and the foundries thrum, and the people walk the catwalks and trust the nets and do not look down.

---

The birds are wrong in the wingbeats.

They circle the foundry stacks in the hours when the haze thins, pale shapes against the grey, their flight pattern asymmetric and unsettling. The vibration they produce registers below hearing, a thrum that some workers describe as familiar, as the sound beneath the sound. No one has ever seen one land. No one has ever found a nest, a roost, a place where they go when they are not circling. They exist only in motion, only in the air above the city, as if flight is their only state, as if landing would require them to become something other than what they are.

The birds are not afraid of people. They circle close sometimes, near enough to feel the vibration of their wingbeats in the chest, near enough to see the wrongness of the feathers—too many joints in the wing-bones, too many primary feathers, wings that seem to fold in directions that don't correspond to any anatomy. They are beautiful in the way that only wrong things can be beautiful, a beauty that makes the observer uneasy, that makes the body want to look away even as the eyes insist on looking.

The ornithologists—there are ornithologists, because there are scientists of every kind in Kaldenvast, all of them studying phenomena that refuse to behave—have theories. The birds are mutations, adapted to the chemical composition of the foundry haze. The birds are not birds at all, but some other thing that has chosen this shape. The birds are visitors, arriving from somewhere else, somewhere where wingbeats work differently, where flight is not what it is here.

The ornithologists do not agree. The birds continue to circle. The theories remain theories.

---

The people who have come back from the deep tunnels are the hardest to describe.

They are present, mostly. They live in the middle districts, walk the catwalks, work the jobs they had before. But there are gaps—patches of skin that have changed texture, smooth and pale where there should be roughness, warm where there should be cool. Gaps in memory, whole days missing, conversations that never happened. Gaps in presence, moments where they seem to be elsewhere, eyes focusing on something that is not there.

They do not speak of what they encountered. They say there was nothing to encounter. They say the tunnels are empty, that the deep-dwellers are not there when the expeditions arrive, that the corridors go on forever or end abruptly or loop back or open into spaces that should not fit in the rock. They say time works differently. They say they walked for hours and came back in minutes, or walked for minutes and came back in days. They say the sound is different down there—not the foundry's thrum but something older, something that is not sound at all but the memory of sound, the echo of a frequency that existed before the rock knew itself.

The patches of changed skin do not heal. They spread slowly, over years, a paleness that advances by millimeters. The affected workers say they don't mind. They say the changed skin feels the same, functions the same, only feels different in the moments when they press their palm to their chest and notice that the temperature is wrong, that something beneath the surface is not quite what it was.

They are still here. They are still working. They say they are still themselves.

---

The moss does not care about any of this.

The moss grows. The moss covers. The moss is the wrong color—teal-grey, an adaptation to light that arrives in fragments, to moisture that never stops, to surfaces that have been damp for a century and will be damp for a century more. The moss is patient in the way of plants, the way of geology, the way of Kaldenvast itself. It grows on wood that should have rotted long ago. It grows on stone that should be too mineral-poor to support life. It grows on the cables and the catwalks and the nets and the walls of the Sump Quarters, a thick mat that muffles sound, that holds moisture like a sponge, that makes every surface soft and damp and alive.

The moss is not beautiful. It is not ugly. It simply is, filling niches that exist because the city exists, persisting because persistence is what plants do, because life finds ways, because in Kaldenvast there are so many ways to find. The ferns sprout from wall-sockets. The roots infiltrate the floorboards. The wrong-colored vegetation advances imperceptibly, taking over corners that were not guarded, covering surfaces that were once clear.

The residents do not fight it. The moss is not a problem to be solved. The moss is simply there, part of the accommodation, another living thing making its home in the city's suspension. The residents learn to live with it the way they learn to live with everything else: with attention, with patience, with the understanding that some things cannot be fixed, only joined.

---

In the end, what lives in Kaldenvast is the city itself.

The people and the fauna and the foundries and the moss, all of it together, all of it suspended, all of it negotiating with a geology that has not yet decided what it is. The city is alive in the way that all inhabited places are alive—a system of systems, a negotiation between the human and the inhuman, a way of being present that changes everything it touches. The residents do not conquer the Massif. They do not ignore it. They live within it, from it, alongside it, their lives threaded through the living rock the same as the cables, the same as the water, the same as the sound.

Kaldenvast should not be here. But here it is, and here it will remain, for as long as the suspension holds, for as long as the foundries thrum, for as long as the geology continues its slow negotiation with itself. The people who live here know this. They have made their peace with uncertainty. They hang their lives from cables threaded through stone that was once miles underground, and they trust the engineering, and they do not look down.

This is home. This is the only home they know. And in Kaldenvast, that is enough.
