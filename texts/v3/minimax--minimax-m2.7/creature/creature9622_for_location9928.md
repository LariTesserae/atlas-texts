What lives here is patient, and most of it is still deciding.

The bird is not one bird. This you learn slowly, or all at once and then spend time uncertain which. It crosses the gap in the canopy high above the basin floor, and its call comes back to you from what sounds like the same distance in every direction — from the green walls that rise in the south, from somewhere below the rim where the water is still learning to fall, from close enough that the voice should have a source but does not. The bird is the same color as the light here: a dull metallic, a thing that has absorbed the gold that falls through the water-heavy leaves and kept it for itself. When it perches — if it perches, in the moments when the silence that is not silence falls so total you can hear your own pulse — it holds its head at an angle that suggests listening, but there is nothing to listen to except the not-quite-silence, and that was already there.

The fish are the ones you notice most because they do not move. You have time to look. They hang in the still water of the deep pools, not swimming, not hovering — *holding*, as if the water itself bears their weight, as if gravity has made some exception that only applies below the surface. Their colors are the colors of the light here after it has passed through water and been changed: a silver that is also a gold, a red that is so dark it is almost black, a blue that has no business being bright enough to see from the bank but is. They do not flee when you crouch at the pool's edge. They turn, slowly, to face you — all of them, if there are many, or the single one that looks like many — and their eyes hold yours, and you understand after a moment that they are not looking back. They are waiting. The water that will not settle keeps them suspended, and they have accepted this, or they do not know anything else.

In the deep moss, where the pads of the fern fronds have accumulated and begun their slow work of becoming soil, something small moves. You catch it at the edge of vision: a flicker, a tremor in the green. It is the color of the dark underside of leaves. It has many legs, or it has two and they are very fast. It pauses when you pause, and this is not fear, because nothing in the basin is afraid — there is nothing here that eats, nothing here that hunts, nothing that needs to flee. It pauses the way a thought pauses: mid-formation, caught on the edge of becoming something else. You watch it become still, become part of the moss, become indistinguishable from the place that made it, and you look away, and when you look back it is somewhere else entirely, and you cannot track its passage because the moss is deep and the shadows lie at angles that do not match the light.

There are sounds you cannot explain. Not the water — the water is everywhere, and you have already learned not to listen for its source. These are other sounds. A tone, low and resonant, that rises from somewhere beneath the stone when the air is very still. A clicking, irregular, that might be the shells of things you cannot see, closing and opening in the wet. A sound like breathing that is not yours, that is too slow to be yours, that comes from the canopy or the water or the deep moss and is gone before you can place it. These sounds do not echo. They simply appear, fully formed, in the silence that is not silence, and they end, and the silence reclaims the space they occupied, and you are not sure you heard them at all.

What lives here is young. This is the truest thing you can say. The bird is newly fledged or newly arrived; its plumage has not yet learned to catch the light in the way the local light demands. The fish are breeding — you see this eventually, in the deeper pools, the clusters of bright things that cling to the undersides of overhanging roots — and their young are the wrong color, too bright, too visible, the color of places where predators have not yet learned to look. The thing in the moss is molting, or laying eggs, or doing whatever the young of its kind do when they are still learning the shape of themselves. Everything is beginning. Everything is in the first draft of itself.

And the place itself — the plateau, the basin, the water that falls and does not fall, the light that pools and does not pool — the place is also a kind of living thing. It breathes. You do not feel it on your skin, but you feel it in the way the silence swells and contracts, in the way the water levels in the pools rise and fall by imperceptible degrees, in the way the moss seems to lean toward you when you are still and away from you when you move. It is aware of you. It is aware of everything. It is learning what it is by watching what lives in it, and the living is what teaches it, and the living is so abundant and so raw and so strange that the learning will take longer than your lifetime, longer than the lifetime of the bird, the fish, the small quick thing in the moss.

You will leave. You know this. You came from somewhere else and you will go back, across the rim, down the channels of young stone, to the world that has decided what it is. But for now you are here, and the place is watching you, and you are becoming part of its learning, and the water falls from the rim in wide curtains that catch the light and hold it, and you understand, finally, that they are not hanging. They are waiting. They are waiting to fall, and they will, when the channels are carved deep enough, when the stone wears smooth, when the world has had time to forget its own becoming. But not yet. Not yet.

The basin holds you. The moss is soft beneath your weight. The light is wrong and the silence is not and the water has not yet decided, and you are what lives here now, for a little while, until you become what the place needs you to be.
