# The Dwellers

The primates come down from the canopy near midday, when the light in the hollows has turned warm enough to feel like something on bare skin. They move along the root bridges—bundles of woven trunk that the mat has grown thick enough to bear weight—and descend the largest of the living cliffs by a route that looks accidental but is not. They know where the fibers give, where the mat has formed handholds without knowing it was forming handholds, where the surface will hold a footfall. There are several species, and they do not mix: one group of smaller, rust-colored animals with long tails and expressive faces; another of larger, blue-gray creatures whose成年 carry the young on their backs and whose calls are the ones that carry farthest, the ones that sound almost like language. They come to the water.

The cisterns are not natural, exactly. The mat has made them, pulling away from the underlying granite where the rock dips to form a shallow basin, but the pooling happens because the roots have directed it here, grown channels that catch the rain and send it downward to this point, this place where the water collects and the animals come. The primates drink with their hands, cupping water and bringing it to their mouths, and they splash in the shallow edges where the ferns grow thick and the light comes green and diffuse through the canopy gap above. They are playful in the way of animals who have no predators here, who live in a world so saturated with life that nothing new can be added and nothing old can be removed, only replaced. They swim. They groom. They rest in the sun at the edge of the pool, and their fur steams in the warmth.

Birds are more numerous and more various. In the canopy, the colonies are dense enough that the branches sag, that the mat thickens around the nests so that a nest is not a thing built but a thing grown—woven from fiber the birds have gathered and treated with secretions that make it permanent, that make it part of the canopy itself. The colonies shift and move; birds leave and return, and the mat fills the spaces they leave behind, and new birds arrive and find a place or do not and move on. There are seed-eaters and fruit-eaters and insects that the birds catch in the air with sharp, deliberate movements. There are nectar-feeders whose beaks are long and curved to match flowers that bloom only in the upper canopy, flowers that have evolved to need exactly these birds, these specific bodies, this exact shape of attention. The flowers open at dawn and close at dusk, and the birds time their feeding to the light, and the synchronization is so complete that it looks like choreography but is only time, only the slow work of matching that happens when two things depend on each other for long enough.

In the hollows, other birds hunt. Lizards sun themselves on the pale stone and drop from the warm surfaces into the grass at the edge of the mat, and the birds catch them—the smaller ones, the ones that did not move fast enough. The hunts are quick and silent, over before the eye can follow, and the bird is gone back to the canopy with its prey before the shadow of its passing has left the stone. Smaller birds move along the ground at the edges of the hollows, picking through the root mat where it meets the bare rock, finding insects in the cracks, finding seeds that have blown in from somewhere else, finding the small, persistent things that live at the edge of everything. Their calls are thin and high and layer over one another until the hollow fills with sound that is almost like music but is not quite, is just life, just the noise that living things make when they are close enough together and busy enough to make it.

The large animals are harder to see. They move at dawn and dusk, along paths that the mat has grown over and around until the paths are tunnels through living wood, dark and close and smelling of root and mineral and the particular warmth of the interior of something alive. The tunnels are used by many species—the ungulates that come down from the higher slopes to drink, the predators that follow them, the smaller creatures that use the same paths because the paths are there and they lead somewhere. The predators are patient. They wait in the tunnels, in the dark, where the light is only the green glow of fungi and the sound is only the sound of the mat and the water and the distant moving of other things. They wait, and the things they wait for come, and the waiting ends.

The plateau has made its own time, or perhaps it has found the time that was always there and held it still. Days pass, but they do not change anything. Seasons turn, but the turning is gentle—the rain comes more often in what might be winter, and the flowers bloom in what might be spring, and the canopy is fuller and the sound louder in the warmest months—but the pattern is fixed, has been fixed long enough that the plants and animals do not respond to it so much as ride it, the way a current carries a thing without the thing choosing to move. The young are born in the wet season, when the food is more abundant and the water more available and the mat more active, more alive, more given to the business of growth. They are born into a world that is complete, that does not need them except as replacement, as continuity, as one more turn of the wheel that keeps the wheel turning. They grow quickly, in the way of things that live in a world with no space for the slow, and they join the pattern, and the pattern absorbs them, and the pattern continues.

---

The humans who lived here—or passed through, or left and came back, or came and did not leave—left their marks in the way that small things leave marks in large places. The shelter is not large. It holds two people, perhaps three if they are close, and it was built from the materials at hand: branches cut from the mat and woven into a frame, leaves layered thick on the roof, the whole thing lashed to four living trunks with vines that have since grown thick enough to hold without lashing, that have incorporated the structure into themselves so that the shelter is no longer something built but something grown, something that belongs to the plateau as much as the trunks it hangs from. Inside, the air is warm and close and smells of fiber and the particular mineral scent of the plateau's water. There are places where people slept, low depressions in the woven floor that have been used enough to take a shape, to hold the memory of bodies. There are no possessions. There may never have been. The people who lived here lived the way the plateau lives: taking what is needed, leaving what is not, trusting that the needed will be there when it is needed again.

The shrine is older. It is carved into a boulder of granite that sits at the edge of a hollow, where the bare rock meets the mat in a line that looks deliberate but is not, is only the edge of a clearing where the conditions for growth are not met, where the seed cannot take hold in the thin skin of soil that washes away before it can accumulate. The face in the stone is worn nearly flat by warm rain and time. It might be a face. It might be something else—a shape, a form, a gesture that was clear when it was carved and has since been made unclear by the patient work of water and the slow movement of the air. The offerings at its base are fresh. Someone has been here, recently, has walked out from the shelter or down from the platform or from somewhere else entirely, has crossed the root mat on paths that are barely visible unless you know to look for them, has climbed the boulder carefully, has placed the dried fruit and the bundle of cut vines where they will be seen by whatever sees, by whatever cares, by the plateau itself. The offering is small. It does not need to be large. It needs only to be there, to say what it says, which is: I was here. I remember. I came back.

The platform is high, as high as the canopy, reached by a ladder of bundled roots that sways slightly when you climb it, that is steadier than it looks because the roots have grown together at the joints, have fused into something that holds. From the platform you can see the plateau entire: the pale hollows, the green blooms, the Teeth in the distance dark against the sky, the way the canopy ripples in the wind like water, like something that is not quite still and not quite moving but in between, always in between. The sound from here is different—it rises, it fills the air, it becomes a presence that you can feel in the chest and the back of the throat. It is the sound of ten thousand lives, ten thousand small movements, ten thousand things that do not know they are making a sound but make it anyway, the way a heartbeat does not know it is a heartbeat but is one. From here the plateau looks like a single thing, a great green mass that breathes and shifts and holds everything it contains. From here you can see that the hollows are not empty but are only less full, that the mat is not uniform but is only continuous, that the whole of it is a system, a logic, a way of being alive that has been refined over time so long that time has stopped meaning what it usually means and started meaning only this: the plateau, the wait, the slow work of becoming what you already are.

The watchtower is smaller than it sounds. It was never large—a person standing, perhaps two standing close—and it served some purpose that is no longer visible, that may no longer be needed or may never have been needed, may have been built for the sake of building, for the sake of seeing, for the sake of standing somewhere high and looking out over something vast. The wood is going back into the mat. The roots have grown up around the base, have wrapped around the bundled posts, have pulled the structure into themselves so that the watchtower is now part of the root network, part of the continuous living surface that covers everything. In time there will be no watchtower. There will be a slight swelling in the root mat where the posts were, a place where the growth is a little denser, a little older, a little more than it would be otherwise. And then even that will be gone, absorbed, forgotten, as the plateau covers and keeps covering, as it waits and keeps waiting, as it grows into the space left by everything that passed through and did not stay.

The dead are here. There are places where the mat has grown over small cairns of stone, places where the root network is slightly different, slightly thicker, slightly warmer—places where something was buried in the time before the cairns were covered, when the people who lived here still lived and were not yet part of the plateau they lived in. The plateau has taken them. It has grown over and through and around them, has incorporated them into the root mat, has made them part of the continuous living surface that covers everything. They are not gone, exactly. They are in the mat, in the warm slow pulse of the organism that holds the plateau together. They are in the water that moves through the roots, carrying mineral and trace of something that was once alive and is now something else, something that feeds the flowers and the fungi and the small things that live in the damp heart of everything. The plateau does not distinguish. It does not hold the dead separate from the living. It takes what is there and makes it part of what continues, and the continuing goes on, and the dead are in the continuing, and the continuing does not know they are there but holds them anyway, because that is what the plateau does.

---

The fungi are the secret of the plateau, the thing that makes it possible. They grow in the root network, in the dark warm interior of the mat where the light does not reach and the water moves slow and the air is close and damp and smells of mineral and decay. They are the network beneath the network, the thing that connects everything to everything else, the system that makes the mat a system instead of just a collection of plants grown together. They move through the root fibers, through the dead wood at the center of the largest trunks, through the spaces between roots where the water pools and the small things live. They are bioluminescent—not brightly, not the way a firefly glows, but dimly, enough to see by if you are close, enough to know that the dark is not empty. Their light is green and soft and constant, and it pulses very slightly, very slowly, like a heartbeat so slow that it might not be a heartbeat at all but something else, something that does not have a name because it does not need one.

The fungi are the plateau's memory. They hold the connections, the linkages between one root and another, between one trunk and the next, between the mat and the water and the stone beneath. They pass signals through the network—chemical, electrical, something that is not quite thought but is not quite not-thought, something that the plateau does without knowing it is doing it. When a part of the mat is damaged, the fungi send signals to other parts, and the other parts respond, sending resources to the damaged area, helping it heal. When a new growth takes hold, the fungi incorporate it into the network, make it part of the whole, extend the reach of the system by a little, by the length of a new root or the width of a new branch or the depth of a new channel in the dark. The plateau does not know it has a memory. It does not need to know. The memory is there in the fungi, in the slow pulse of light in the deep root tunnels, in the connections that are made and remade and made again as the mat grows and changes and stays the same.

The plateau is not one thing. It is ten thousand things that have become one thing, or that have forgotten they are ten thousand, or that have never been separate enough to count. The distinction does not matter. What matters is the mat, the continuous living surface that covers everything, that holds the water and the minerals and the dead and the living and the waiting in a single web, a single breath, a single slow pulse of life that has been going on so long that it has forgotten how to stop. The plateau is patient. It has been patient for a very long time. It will be patient for a very long time more. It covers. It grows. It fills every surface it can reach and waits for the surfaces it cannot, the bare rock hollows, the granite domes, the Teeth that rise through the mat and break the canopy and stand bare against the sky. It will grow over them too, in time. It is growing over them now, very slowly, very patiently, the roots working their way into the cracks, the mat thickening at the edges, the slow patient work of life that wants to cover everything and will not stop until it does.

You could live here. The evidence is there, and more than the evidence—there is water, and food, and shelter if you know how to build it, and warmth, and the company of ten thousand lives that are not your kind but are alive, are moving, are part of the world in the way that everything is part of the world when everything is connected. But living here would mean changing, would mean becoming something that fits into the mat instead of standing outside it, would mean accepting the sound and the warmth and the slow pulse of the root network under every step and every breath. It would mean learning the paths that are not paths, the hollows that are not empty, the creatures that are not separate but are all one thing wearing different shapes. It would mean dying here, eventually, and being covered, and being held in the mat, and becoming part of the memory that the fungi carry, the slow pulse of light in the deep tunnels where the water moves and the things that were alive are still moving, still part of everything, still there.

The plateau would allow it. It allows everything, in the end. It allows the primates to come down to the water and swim and play. It allows the birds to nest in the canopy and the lizards to sun on the stone and the predators to wait in the dark. It allows the humans to build their shelters and their shrines and their platforms, to mark their paths, to leave their offerings, to bury their dead in the places where the roots are warmest. It allows you to stand on the granite dome and look out over the whole of it and feel what you feel, which is awe, which is unease, which is the particular vertigo of standing on something alive and knowing that you are small and it is large and it will outlast you by so much that the difference does not matter. The plateau does not care about the difference. The plateau does not care about anything, except covering, growing, waiting. It will wait for you, too, after you are gone. It will grow over what you leave behind. It will hold you in the mat and the memory and the slow pulse of the things that are still alive, and you will be part of them, and they will not know it, and that will not matter, because being part of something is not about being known by it but about being held by it, carried forward, kept in the shape of the world that keeps shaping itself long after you are gone.

The plateau is patient. It has been waiting for a very long time, and it is very good at it.
