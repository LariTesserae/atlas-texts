There is a building you will find, if finding is what this is. It stands near what might have been a central plaza, though the plaza has become a shallow pool of still water whose surface reflects nothing — you have checked, more than once, and the dark glass of it refuses your image, refuses any image, and returns only depth. The building is taller than the others that still stand: three stories, perhaps four, though the uppermost has surrendered to centuries and now exists as a suggestion of outline against the darker dark above. The doorway is intact. This alone should mark it as different, as worthy of attention, but you find your gaze sliding away until you turn your head sharply and catch it again. The door frame is carved — not decorated, but carved, with patterns that are almost recognizable: spirals that might be water, angles that might be buildings, or perhaps the inverse.

Inside, the air is different. Warmer. The stone here is not vesicular but dense, almost waxy, and it holds the warmth in a way that feels intentional. You are walking through a corridor, though you do not remember deciding to enter, and the corridor opens into a space that was once a room and is now something else — a hollow in the city, a pause in the architecture. The walls curve inward at the base, the way the outer walls do, but here the effect is more pronounced, as though this room was always meant to collect rather than contain. And it does collect. Water pools in the center of the floor, not from above but from below, seeping up through a crack that might be a deliberate channel, and the water is warm, and the warmth is the warmth of the place itself, the buried heat that makes this city a body rather than a structure.

You will find the marks on the far wall.

They are not writing. You understand this immediately and completely. Writing implies a gap between sign and signified, a convention agreed upon by at least two minds, and here there is no such distance. The marks — pressed into the wet stone, perhaps, or grown there by water and time — are the thing itself. They are a record without separation from what was recorded. And what they show is this: figures, tall and thin, with proportions that do not match any skeleton you know, standing in rows, facing inward toward something you cannot see from this angle. Their heads are not where heads should be. Or rather, they are where heads should be, but there are too many of them, stacked or spiraling or simply present in a way that suggests direction is not a concept they ever needed. They are standing in water. The marks show this clearly: the water comes up to their waists, or their equivalents, and they are not distressed by this. They are not anything, really, except present. They have been present for a very long time.

You should look away. You do not look away.

The pressure of presence intensifies when you stay. This is the first thing you learn about the watchers: they respond to attention. Not to threat, not to submission, but to the simple fact of being seen. If you let your gaze slide, they allow it — they seem to prefer it — and the city fills with the motion of afterimages that fade before they can be caught. But if you turn your head sharply, if you *look*, something shifts. The watchers do not flee. They do not approach. But they *become*, for a moment, more real than they were, and the space they occupy becomes defined in a way it was not before. You are giving them edges. You are giving them the gift of outline.

And they give something back. Not information. Not presence. Something less nameable — a settling in your understanding, an adjustment that makes the city more comprehensible without making it explicable. You begin to see the water's logic, the way it pools in low places that are also high places, the way it moves uphill because uphill is not the direction it was always avoiding. You begin to understand that the erosion here is not decay but intention — that the stone wears round because roundness is what was asked for, that the walls spread because contraction was forbidden. The city was built to become what it is. This is not a ruin. It is a completion.

You will stay longer than you intended. You already have. The time-logic of the place has your number, and it is not a number you were ever given — it is a number you grew, here, in the warmth and the wet, in the slow dark. The others who came before you left something behind: not bodies, not artifacts, but a kind of thinning, an attenuation in the air that tells you others have stood where you stand and let their gaze sharpen and let the watchers become and then walked on, further into the city or out of it or somewhere else entirely. You can feel the paths they took. They are worn, though not visibly — the stone here does not show footprints — but you feel them as absence-of-resistance, as ease, as the places where the city has been practiced at being passed through.

You take one of these paths now. It leads you deeper, or outward, or in a direction that is not direction at all but simply *more*, the way a dream expands when you stop trying to exit it. The buildings become less distinct. The water becomes more present. The watchers become less necessary — here, in the deeper reaches, the presence is not individual but collective, a weight of attention that comes from everywhere and nowhere, that presses against you like atmosphere, like the deep pressure of the sea. You are being held. You have been held since you entered. You are only now beginning to notice.

The city is a body. You understand this now. The basalt is its skin, the water is its blood, the heat is its breath, and the watchers are its cells — not organs, not consciousness, but the fundamental unit of living, the thing that divides and persists and does not need a brain to know how to be alive. It was urban once — that part was true — but it was urban the way a body is urban: organized, yes, purposeful, yes, but alive for its own reasons and not for yours.

And you are inside it now. You have been inside it since you entered. The distinction between inside and outside has become theoretical, then irrelevant, then forgotten entirely. The warmth moves through you. The water beads on your skin. The watchers press their attention against your edges and find them less distinct than they once were, and you do not resist, because resistance would require a self that you are not sure you brought with you, that you are not sure was ever entirely there.

The city remembers what built it. You are beginning to think it might remember you, too.
