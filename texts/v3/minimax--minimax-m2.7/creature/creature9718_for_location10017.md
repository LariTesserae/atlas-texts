They come to you in time. You do not go to them.

The first thing you learn is the pressure — not against your body but against your sense of where things are. You have been oriented by edges your whole life: the rim of a cup, the line where wall meets ceiling, the particular distance at which a face becomes readable. Here, there are no edges. There is only the gradual, the gradient, the place where one density becomes another so slowly that the becoming is itself the only landmark. You learn to read the world by its hesitations. Where the water grows thicker with plant-matter. Where it thins toward something like openness. Where the plant-surface yields to your weight and where it holds. These are the coordinates of this place, and the creatures that live here navigate by the same ones.

The large ones move seldom. You know they are there because the plant-mass shudders — a tremor so slow it takes minutes to pass through, like a groan in deep wood, like the settling of a thing too large to hurry. When they move, it is with a patience that is not virtue but nature: they have nowhere to be that they are not already, and the distance between any two points in this world is so vast that velocity has lost its meaning. They are shaped for the pressing-through of plant-matter dense as packed earth, their bodies long and flexible, segmented in ways that suggest joints but move more like hinges in water, like the slow unfolding of a frond. You cannot see them. You know them by the way the substrate bends around their passage, by the faint taste of displaced chemistry that lingers after they have gone. Some of them have not moved in so long that the plant-matter has grown over their surfaces, has claimed them as ground, and they are walking surfaces now, indistinguishable from the rest, carrying the weight of whatever walks above them. They do not die in the way other creatures die. They become.

Between the great ones and the small ones there is a middle order that you might call the workers, if the word did not imply intention. They are the ones that keep the world cycling — feeding on the plant-matter, breaking it down, passing it along in chemical form to other creatures that need it, to the plants themselves in forms the plants can absorb. They move with purpose, if purpose can be distinguished from compulsion. They cluster where the substrate is thickest, where the ancient tissue still holds its slow vitality, and they work at it with bodies that are less bodies than processes — soft, formless, clinging, digesting outward from a center that is not a center but a place where the chemistry happens to be most concentrated.
