# What Lives in the Drowned Grove

The people first, though they would not put themselves first. They would point to the water, or the roots, or the long memory of the trees before they would point to themselves.

They are quiet people in the way that the grove is quiet—not silent, but still, listening. Their voices carry differently here. The sound moves in three dimensions, bounces off leaf and pooling surface and root-curl, arrives at the ear from strange angles. They have learned to speak softly and let the grove carry their words. A shout would break something. They know this in their bodies.

Their skin is pale, adapted to shadow. When they must go to the edges of the grove, where the light comes through unbroken and the water spills into its long fall, they wear woven hoods and go in pairs. They return brown from these journeys, and the others look at them with something like wonder, the way you might look at someone who has visited another country. The sun is a foreign thing here. It exists, but as rumor.

They are short and wiry, the ones you've seen, with hands that are always moving—testing, reaching, adjusting. The grove requires constant attention. A root will grow where you don't want it. A pooling place will silt over. A connection between wood and wood will weaken, and if you don't notice, it will fail, and someone will fall into the dark water below, and you will hear them call out, and you will not be able to reach them before the current takes them into the deeps where the fathoms go. This has happened. You can see it in the faces of the older ones, the way their eyes move to the dark places between platforms and stay there.

They marry late, or not at all. Children are raised by the community. This is not kindness alone—it is necessity. The water does not forgive distraction. A parent watching a child is a parent not watching the walkways, not checking the root-mats, not listening for the telltale creak that means a bridge is aging past its strength. So the children move through the grove in groups, supervised by those whose eyes are still sharp and whose hands still steady, and the children learn to walk the wet wood before they can speak full sentences, and the children learn to swim in the shallows before they can name all the frogs.

The frogs are everywhere in the deep places. You see them first as stillness—a patch of brown or green on a leaf, a ripple that was not yours. Then your eyes adjust, and you see they are numerous beyond counting. They come in sizes from thumbnail to fist, in colors that mirror the wood and the water and the moss. They do not flee from you. They have never needed to flee from anything. The people do not eat them, and the people have taught their children not to eat them, and this teaching goes back so far that it has become instinct, and the frogs have become instinct too—a part of the grove's metabolism, processing what falls, turning waste into life, their small bodies linking the water to the wood to the air in ways the people understand but cannot quite explain.

The birds are louder but harder to see. They live in the canopy where the light filters green and gold, and they move fast, and they do not trust the visitors. The people have made spaces for them—hollows where root meets root, just large enough for a nest, just hidden enough from the rain. The birds know these spaces. They return to them. Some of them have been returning longer than any human memory. The people tell you the birds were here first, and they believe it, and the birds do not argue.

There are things in the water that you do not see. The people know they are there because the water moves sometimes when it should not, and because their hooks come back damaged, and because sometimes a frog disappears from a leaf it was resting on and the leaf is undisturbed and the water below is still. The people do not speak of these things often. When they do, they speak softly, and they do not swim in the deep places after dark. The water goes far down. The fathoms are not empty. This is known.

And the grove itself—the trees, the roots, the patient architecture of living wood—that is the oldest living thing, and perhaps the only true living thing, and the people are merely the most recent of its tenants. The trees do not think as you understand thinking. But they grow toward what they need. They seal what is cut. They remember. The oldest root-bridges are not merely shaped by human hands—they are the result of a collaboration so long that neither party remembers where it began. The people asked. The trees listened. The wood shaped itself.

You could say the trees live here, and you would be right, but you would be saying something incomplete. The trees do not live in the grove. They are the grove. And the grove is not a place but a process, a way of being wet and growing and reaching toward light that filters through a thousand small gaps. It is older than the people, older than the plateau it grows on, older perhaps than the mountain it sits upon. It will be here after the people are gone, after the walkways have rotted and the hearths have grown over, after the last voice has called out over the water and no one has answered. The grove does not need the people. But the people need the grove, and for now, the grove permits them.

This is what lives here: the water in its thousand forms, moving always, gathering and falling and gathering again. The roots, the bridges, the living architecture that is also road. The frogs in the dark places, processing what falls. The birds in the high green light, catching what rises. The small things between—lizards and rodents and insects filling the air with their chord of buzz and trill. The people who learned to ask instead of take, who learned to listen to wood as if it could answer, who built their lives around the truth that this place was here first and will be here last.

And something else, in the deep water, moving slow. Waiting. It has no name that the people speak aloud. But it is part of the design.
