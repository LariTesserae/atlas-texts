# The Inhabitants

The spiders came first. Or perhaps they were always here—the first tenants, the original settlers of this damp cathedral. Their webs are the first thing you notice when you enter, not because they are large, though some are, but because they are everywhere: strung across channels where water passes beneath the light, stretched between building corners and fern clusters, draped across the gaps between stones where moss has grown thick enough to hold a thread. They are patient creatures. They sit at the centers of their webs, legs folded close, and wait for the midges that rise from the water's edge. When the light catches their silk at certain angles—morning, when the sun finds its way through the canopy, or late afternoon, when the angle is low—they become visible all at once: a thousand silver geometries suspended in the damp air. There are more of them than there should be. Their populations seem out of proportion with the insects available, as if they are feeding on something else, or as if the silence itself has given them permission to multiply beyond the usual checks. They do not move often. When they do, it is with a deliberation that is almost ceremonial—a leg lifting, a body turning, a repositioning that takes seconds and seems to require no urgency. The damp suits them. The moisture keeps their silk strong, their bodies sleek. They are dark, most of them—dark bodies, dark legs, dark as the wet stone—but there are others, paler species that live in the deeper shadows of the buildings, pale as bone, pale as the fungal growths that appear on old timber in the wettest seasons.

The insects are the next layer. They rise in clouds at the edges of light, where sunlit water meets shadow, where the damp air holds enough warmth to call them forth. Midges first, always midges, tiny and numerous, their bodies close enough to the water to seem almost amphibious. Then gnats, with their erratic flights, their paths unpredictable as smoke. Then things with longer wings and iridescent bodies—damselflies, perhaps, though they move differently here, less skittering, more hovering, as if each flight were considered before it was taken. They do not sing. That is the strangeness of it: midges in such numbers should produce a drone, a hum of living bodies in motion, but this cloud is silent, or so close to silent that the ear strains to find it and finds only the faintest whisper, a sound like memory rather than presence. The insects move through the air as if the air itself were thick, as if they must work to push through it, and perhaps they do—the moisture-laden air of the glen is denser than dry air, and their small wings must labor against it. They cluster at the light. They cluster at the water. They cluster at the edges of the webs, and the spiders wait.

The birds came later, or so the people say, though no one remembers a time when they were not here. They nest in the canopy in such density that the branches bow under them, and you can see the nests if you look up: dozens, hundreds, woven into every fork and gap where a structure will hold. They are songbirds, most of them—the species that would elsewhere fill the morning with calls and trills and territorial declarations—but here they do not sing. They do not call. They watch, from their nests, from the branches, from the stones that project from the cliff walls, and their silence is the loudest thing in the glen. You learn to read them by their postures instead of their voices: a cocking of the head means alarm; a flattening of the body means threat; a particular angle of the tail means that something moves below that has not moved before. They have learned, over generations, to communicate in the language of the body, to say with a wing what the voice would waste. Some of them still attempt song, in the breeding season, or when the light falls in certain ways—traces of sound, fragments of melody that die before they complete themselves, as if the song itself cannot survive in this place. The ones that survive are the quiet ones. Their eggs are pale, speckled, indistinguishable from the stones they rest upon. Their chicks are silent in the nest, and when they fledge, they join the watching.

The small mammals are the hardest to see. They live in the spaces between, in the runs they have worn through the fern-beds, in the burrows beneath the stone walls, in the hollows of old trees. You know they are there by their signs: the tracks in mud, the droppings on stones, the particular way the ferns bend where they pass. There are mice here, and voles, and something larger—a creature with a long body and short legs that moves like a whisper through the undergrowth. They are healthy, all of them, their coats sleek and full, their eyes bright with the particular alertness of prey that knows itself watched. But they do not make sounds. They do not chirp or squeak or chatter. When they fight, it is silent. When they breed, it is silent. When they die—and they do die, the predators see to that—it is silent too, a small body gone without a voice. They have learned, as the birds have learned, as the insects have learned, that sound is a danger in this place, that the quiet is a resource that must not be spent. Their eyes are large, adapted to the dim light, and their ears are larger still, pricked to catch the faintest vibrations, the smallest sounds that other creatures might not notice. They listen more than they move. When they do move, it is quickly, purposefully, a blur of grey-brown fur from one cover to the next, and then stillness.

The predators are patient. They have learned the grammar of this silence. There is a long-bodied thing that lives among the rocks—a serpentine creature with scales that match the granite, eyes that are bright and cold and unblinking. It waits on rock shelves where water drips, where the light is uncertain, where prey must pass. It does not strike often, but when it strikes, it is done before the sound of it can travel, a motion so quick and clean that the air barely stirs. There is a bird-of-prey, pale-breasted, that perches on dead branches and watches the canopy for movement. It is the largest predator in the glen, its wings broad, its talons sharp, its eyes better than any other's at catching the small movements that the quiet creatures cannot quite suppress. It does not cry out when it hunts. It does not screech its victory. It takes its prey and it flies, and the flight is soundless, and the prey does not have time to scream. There are others—the things that live in the water, eel-like and patient, waiting in the deep pools where light does not reach. The things that live in the walls, small and sharp-toothed, that come out only at night and leave their kills where the moss will grow over them. All of them are silent. All of them are still. They have learned that stillness is the beginning of the hunt, and silence is its completion.

The people are the newest layer, or the oldest, depending on how you count. They came, the elders say, because the water was here, because the walls gave shelter, because the bounded space meant safety from the larger world. They found the glen already full—already dense, already silent, already holding more life than any place they had known. They did not understand it. They do not understand it now. But they have adapted, as all things here adapt, to the conditions of their living. Their voices are muted, as the prompt said, but it is more than that: they have learned to speak in the frequencies that do not carry, to use their breath rather than their throats, to say with a touch what words would waste. Their footsteps are soft because the stone is soft with moss, and because they have learned to place them carefully, and because the silence itself seems to swallow sound before it can be made. Their children are born quiet. Their old ones die quietly. The hearth-pit smoke rises in a column that does not waver, does not bend, rises as if the air above it is too still to disturb. They have built their structures low, dark-eaved, hugging the ground, because height means exposure, and exposure means visibility, and visibility in a place this dense with watching is a kind of death. They do not look up often. They look at the water instead, at the channels, at the pools where the surface is still enough to reflect a face. They find their reflections there, and the reflections are silent, and the silence is enough.

The web holds them all. That is what the oldest among them say, the ones who still remember the old stories: the web holds them. The spiders have built it, thread by thread, generation by generation, a structure so vast and so fine that it spans not just the glen but the air itself, the moisture, the silence. Everything that moves here moves through the web, and the web catches what would otherwise be lost—the sound of a footstep, the call of a bird, the chirp of a mouse. The web holds it. The web takes it. And in exchange, the web gives silence, and the silence gives life, and the life gives density, and the density gives more to the web. It is an economy, a circulation, a system so old that no one remembers its beginning. The spiders tend it. The insects feed it. The birds and mammals and predators are caught in it, and in being caught, become part of it. And the people, the newest tenants, have learned to walk carefully through its strands, to speak softly within its reaches, to live as if the web is not there even as they feel it above them, around them, holding them as it holds all the rest.

There is something at the center. The oldest stories say so. Something that breathes at the heart of the glen, something that the water feeds and the silence nourishes and the web connects. You cannot see it. You cannot hear it, not exactly, not with the ear. But you feel it, when you stand in the damp air and let the quiet settle around you: a presence, a weight, a patience that is older than the stone and will outlast it. The predators watch for it. The birds watch for it. The spiders build toward it. And the people, who have learned to read the silences of this place, know better than to look for it directly. They tend their water channels. They maintain their walls. They eat their meals in the communal hall, quietly, always quietly, and they do not ask what breathes at the center, because the asking itself would be a sound, and the sound would be taken, and what would be given in return is not something they wish to know.

The glen holds what it holds. The bounded space contains. And within that containment, life goes on—abundant, dense, watchful, silent—waiting for something that has not yet arrived, or perhaps has always been here, or perhaps is the glen itself, breathing in and breathing out, holding and held, the silence made flesh and the flesh made silence, until no one can tell where one ends and the other begins.
