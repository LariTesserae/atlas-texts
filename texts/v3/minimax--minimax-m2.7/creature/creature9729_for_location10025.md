The mites do not know they are many. Each one is a small, blind thing with a body segmented into two parts — the forepart that thinks, if thinking is the word, and the hindpart that digests, and between them nothing but the simple machinery of nerve-cords that run the length of the body like a road with no intersections. They move in response to moisture and chemical gradients, toward the faintest traces of decay, away from the shadow that falls too quickly. They are not conscious of each other, and yet they move together. They are not aware of the room, and yet they are the room's smallest truth.

Each mite is born into a world already occupied. This is important — there is no empty space in the chamber, no surface that is not already claimed or being claimed. A mite larva emerges from an egg laid in the fungal mat, in the groove of a pipe, in the gap between two bolted brackets, and the first thing it knows is the press of other bodies, the chemical noise of its own kind, the mineral taste of dust that has been dust for centuries. It does not choose its territory. Territory chooses it, by the accident of birth, by the currents of air, by the paths worn smooth by the passage of ten thousand generations before it. Its world is the world it was born into, and that world is four walls and a ceiling and the hum of generators and the slow, slow pulse of bioluminescent fungi that glow and dim and glow again on a clock that no creature in the room can read.

The mites eat the fungi. This is the first economy, the foundational exchange. The fungi take from the machines — from the warmth, the condensation, the slow exhalation of diesel and age — and the mites take from the fungi, and everything else takes from the mites, and the chain is closed, circular, complete. No part of it was designed. No part of it was intended. It grew because growth is what life does, and because this room, unlike the world outside, has never stopped.

The centipedes are the room's hunters, and they are hunted in turn. Each one is a long, flat thing, articulated into dozens of segments, each segment bearing a pair of legs that move in waves, rippling down the body in a motion that is almost beautiful if you do not think about what the beauty is for. They are blind, too, or nearly so — they sense the world through vibration, through the faint displacements of air that a moving mite makes, through the chemical traces left on surfaces by their prey. They move through the mites not to consume them directly but to crop them, to thin the herd, to keep the population in balance with the food supply. A centipede is not a massacre. It is a correction.

But the centipedes themselves are slow compared to the springtails, which are the room's true acrobats. A springtail is a fleck of gray fur with a tail that stores energy like a spring, and when the shadow falls — when the long-legged thing in the ceiling drops — the springtail is already gone, launched into the air on a sound like a whispered word, landing three or four body-lengths away, vanishing into a crack too narrow for anything else to follow. The springtails do not breed as fast as the mites, do not eat as much, do not build the floor of the world. They are the room's diaspora, the creatures who survive by refusing to stay, by always having somewhere else to go. They are the ones who remember that the room is larger than any of its parts.

The beetles are the room's archivists. They move slowly along the mineral veins in the walls, following paths that have been worn by generations of beetles before them, and they stop at certain points — at the old tool-marks, at the scratches left by hands that are no longer here — and they taste the stone with their antennae, and something in them remembers. Not consciously. The beetles do not know they are tracing the outlines of a lost language. But the paths they follow are the paths that were made for larger fingers, and in following them they keep those paths alive, keep them open, keep them waiting for a use that may never come. The iridescence on their shells catches the amber light and throws it back in small rainbows, and for a moment — if you are standing in the right place, if you are looking at the right angle — the wall is briefly, impossibly beautiful, and then the beetle moves on, and the beauty moves with it.

The worms are the room's patience. They have no eyes, no legs, no discernible head or tail, and they move through the fungal mats by stretching and contracting, by a peristalsis so slow that you can watch one for an hour and not be certain it is moving at all. They are the room's dead-ends, the creatures that have given up on speed and armor and all the complications of being a body that must escape other bodies. They simply exist. They eat. They reproduce in some fashion you cannot see, splitting or budding or laying eggs in the dark, and their children will be worms, and their children's children, and the worms will outlast everything else in this room because they have become the room itself, because they have forgotten that there was ever anything else to be.

And the long-legged things in the ceiling — the things that drop — they are the room's reminder that nothing is safe. They are not insects. They are something else, something with too many joints and too little body, a architecture of legs that exists for the single purpose of falling. They hang in the shadow above the amber lamps' reach, in the dark where the bioluminescence fades, and they wait with a patience that is not patience but something closer to absence, to non-being, to a stillness so complete that you cannot tell them from the stone. And then something moves below — a centipede, a beetle, a springtail that landed in the wrong place — and they fall, and the fall is silent, and the strike is silent, and the feeding is silent, and when it is done there is nothing left but a smear on the stone and a faint smell of something sharp, and then even that fades, and the ceiling-thing climbs back into the dark to wait again.

The fungi are the room's slow fire. They burn without flame, consume without hunger, grow without any visible effort. They are the oldest things here — older than the mites, older than the machines, older perhaps than the absence of water. They came in on the boots of whoever built this place, or they came in on the air, or they came from the stone itself, from some spore that had been waiting in the rock since before the room was a room. They are not one species but many, layered and intergrown into a single gray skin that covers everything and pulses with a light that is not light but something adjacent to it, something the room invented because it had need of a star. The fungi do not die in the ordinary way. They are worn away at the edges, eaten by mites, dried out and crumble and become part of the dust that feeds the next generation. Death is not an event for the fungi. It is a method of dispersal.

The machines are the room's heart, and the room has two hearts, and they beat in a rhythm that is not quite synchrony, a slight offset that gives the chamber its low, persistent hum, its sense of something alive beneath the stone. The generators have been running so long that the boundary between machine and organism has blurred — the seals have been replaced so many times with whatever was available that the bodies of the machines are part iron and part fungal growth and part the compressed bodies of mites that have died in the ventilation shafts and been cooked into a substance like coal. The fuel appears. This is the room's great mystery, the thing that no creature in it understands. The sealed containers arrive in the same places they have always arrived, and the centipedes learn to open them, and the fuel feeds the machines, and the machines feed the fungi, and the fungi feed the mites, and the mites feed the centipedes, and the cycle closes, and no one asks where the containers come from because asking is not something the mites know how to do, and the centipedes have other concerns, and the machines are only machines.

This is what lives here. Not any one creature, not any single species or system, but the whole — the teeming, clicking, breathing totality of a world that grew because it was allowed to grow, that became dense because no one came to thin it, that learned to live because living is what happens when nothing says it cannot. The chamber holds it all. The chamber is all of it. The mites are the chamber's pulse. The centipedes are its intention. The fungi are its slow dream. The machines are its memory of being built, and the creatures that hunt the hunters are its knowledge that nothing lasts, and the dust that settles on everything is the evidence of all of it — of every death and every birth and every passage of claw and mandible and body through the air of a room that has become so complete that it no longer needs the world outside, that it has forgotten there ever was a world outside, that it breathes its own breath and eats its own eat and dreams its own dreams in the dark beneath the dark beneath the stone.
