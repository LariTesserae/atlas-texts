What lives here is patient, or it is quick. Those are the two rhythms the plateau permits, and the life that has settled here has learned one or the other.

In the shallow depressions where rain gathers, there are creatures that emerge only when the water does. They emerge and they feed and they breed and they seed the mud with their eggs or their larvae or their spores, and then the water vanishes and they vanish with it, in whatever form they have evolved for waiting—the egg that can survive desiccation, the cyst that hardens into the stone itself, the seed-case that splits only when the next rain comes, perhaps months hence. These are the quick lives, and they are numerous in the way of things that bet everything on timing: clouds that gather, rain that falls, a pool that forms for a few hours, a window that opens and closes in the same afternoon. In that window, the water comes alive. Tiny things—insects with armor that glints in the light, larvae that wiggle in the shallows, creatures so small you might mistake them for debris. But they are not debris. They are the harvest that draws larger things, the link in a chain that stretches from the ephemeral pools up through the food web and into the gorges and across the plateaus where the larger predators wait.

The larger predators are the ones who have learned patience. There is a bird that lives on this plateau—though "lives" is perhaps too active a word for what it does, which is mostly to wait. It is pale, the color of old bone, with a wingspan that catches the turbulent air and holds it, riding the gusts without effort. It perches on the highest points of exposed rock and it watches. The wind tears at its feathers and it does not move. Below, in the water-pockets, the quick lives breed and hatch and feed, and the bird watches, and when something moves in a pattern it recognizes—a flash of iridescence, a sudden skittering, the tremor of something small crossing stone—the bird drops. It drops like a stone itself, folding its wings and falling toward the earth with a silence that seems impossible given the wind, and then it rises again with something struggling in its beak, and it is gone before you can be certain it was ever there. This bird has no name that you know. It has no need for names. It has the plateau and the wind and the patience that the plateau teaches.

The gorges are where the life is thickest, and the gorges are where you cannot go—not here, not in this writing, not yet, because to enter the gorges is to enter a different world, a world of shade and dripping and the slow accumulation of moisture in the deep places where the sun does not reach. Down there, the walls glisten. Down there, the air is thick with the smell of wet stone and the green smell of things that grow in the absence of light. Fungi spread across the rock faces in pale fans, edible-looking but not, perhaps—nothing here is what it seems. Mosses thick as carpets. Vines that hang from the gorge walls in curtains, their roots invisible, anchored somewhere in the ancient stone, drinking from water that seeps through the rock itself, patient water, water that has taken centuries to filter down through the layers of the plateau and emerge here in the deep shade. And in that water, in those slow drips and persistent seeps, there is life that has never seen the sky. Creatures with no eyes. Creatures with too many eyes. Creatures that move in the film of water on stone and know nothing of air, nothing of wind, nothing of the vast turbulent sky that presses down from above. They are the citizens of a hidden kingdom, and they are as complex and as complete as anything that lives on the plateau surface, perhaps more so, because they have found a way to persist in a place where the world above cannot reach them.

On the plateau surface itself, in the bare stone centers where almost nothing grows, there are things that look like nothing. You might walk across such a stone and see nothing, and that is the point—they have made themselves invisible, or nearly so, in the way of prey that cannot afford to be seen. There is a lizard here, small, that matches the color of the rock so precisely you will not see it until it moves, and by then it is already gone, a flicker of motion in the corner of your eye as it disappears into a crack. It has evolved to be stone, to be crack, to be nothing until motion becomes necessary. And then there are the insects, the small and silent workers who do not need your attention, who do not need anyone's attention, who simply are: beetles that move across the rock in straight lines for reasons known only to them, ants that have found a food source you cannot see and form lines that flow toward it and away from it in patterns that might be communication or might be something older, some chemical memory written into their bodies before they were born. They are the infrastructure of this place, the ones who do the work that keeps the system running, and they are everywhere, if you look, and they are nowhere, if you do not.

There is also, in the deep gorges, something you might call a sound, though sound is not quite right. It is more like a vibration that the air carries, a low humming that seems to come from the stone itself, and it changes with the weather, with the temperature, with the pressure. Some say it is the wind threading through the channels. Some say it is water moving in underground rivers that have never been mapped. Some say it is the mountain dreaming. You do not know what to believe, but you know that when you stand very still, when the wind dies for a moment and the rain is not falling and the birds have gone elsewhere, you can hear it: that hum, that pulse, that sense that the land itself is alive in a way that is not metaphor, not symbol, not anything you can name, but is simply, undeniably, true. The plateau breathes. The drowned summits remember the heights they used to be. And the life that lives here—whether quick or patient, whether visible or hidden, whether it has a name or not—is part of that breath, that pulse, that slow dreaming of stone and water and sky that has been going on since before you came and will go on long after you leave.
