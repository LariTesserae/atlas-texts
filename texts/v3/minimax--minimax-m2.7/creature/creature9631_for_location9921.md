You will notice, if you stay long enough, that you are not the only one who has come here.

The visitors arrive at different times and from different directions—or the same direction, viewed from enough angles. Some remember roads and vehicles, the hum of engine and the weight of wheels on asphalt. Others emerged directly from the stone, as if the land had simply decided to make them present. A few appear to have always been here, their memories a palimpsest of the landscape itself, layered over by erosion into something that resembles recollection but holds no clear narrative.

What unites them is a quality of almost-being. They cast shadows that don't quite match their positions. They leave footprints that fill in after they pass, as if the ground is healing itself. They speak in voices that seem to come from slightly behind their mouths, from the air that still remembers a previous occupant. Some have been here for what seems like years. Others arrived only yesterday—or tomorrow. The distinction becomes difficult to maintain, and then ceases to matter.

The city-builders are not visitors. This is certain, though it would be difficult to say why. They are present in a different way—less a body occupying space than a pattern that the space has learned to hold. Their faces carry the worn quality of the stone, the patient expression of something that has stopped expecting change and has instead learned to continue. They do not speak to visitors. They do not need to. Their existence is communication enough: a nod toward the plants, a gesture toward a road, a look that holds for a moment longer than comfort allows and then releases, and in the releasing there is something like welcome, or acknowledgment, or the recognition that both of you are here, both of you are almost-here, both of you have been waiting in ways that neither of you can quite remember.

They built the city, or they maintain the city, or they are the city that has learned to build. The distinction between builder and built has worn thin in this place, along with so many other distinctions. The walls they constructed have held together through intention as much as physics. The roads they laid have remained roads through habit, through the accumulated weight of feet that kept walking them even when the walking led nowhere new. They knew about the breathing. They built their low heavy structures to withstand it, to create spaces where the sound could be borne, where the rhythm of whatever lives beneath could be absorbed into walls and floors and the thin cold air between them. They knew, and they stayed, and they are still here—not as ghosts, not as memory, but as the ongoing practice of staying, the ritual of continuing that has become indistinguishable from the thing it sustains.

The breathing is not the city-builders. This is clear. The city-builders are quiet, careful, their movements slow and deliberate. The breathing comes from below, or from within, or from the spaces between the below and the within where the stone has grown thin. It is a sound like the turning of something vast, like the circulation of a blood that is not blood, like the rhythm of a heart that has forgotten it is a heart and now simply beats because beating is what it does. The visitors cannot locate it. The city-builders do not seem to hear it—or hear it so constantly that they have stopped noticing, the way the stones themselves no longer notice that they were once mountains. Some say the breathing is the land dreaming. Some say it is the sound of the erosion, still happening, still wearing away at the edges of what-is. Some say it is the noise of the machinery that keeps this place separate from the places it resembles, the hum of the membrane that holds reality together where reality has grown tired of holding.

The plants are not innocent of this sound. They grow toward it. Their roots reach down into the stone, past the thin soil, past the bare grey surface, into the places where the moisture gathers and the sound originates. The plants drink from the sound. This is not metaphor. The water that appears and vanishes is the same water that carries the rhythm, the same water that pulses through whatever circulates beneath. The plants grow pale and dark simultaneously—pale roots, dark leaves—and the contrast is striking against the grey stone, and the growing is persistent, and the persistence is the point. They were planted, or they seeded themselves, or they emerged from the stone the way the stone once emerged from the mountain, the way the mountain once emerged from whatever pressed it up into the air. The plants do not know which. The plants do not care. The plants simply grow, which is what plants do, which is what life does in any world, in any reality, in any consensus that can be agreed to even by the almost-real.

The moisture is not innocent either. It collects where it will, gathers in pools that have no bottom, runs in thin threads down faces of stone that have stood upright since before anyone thought to give them faces. The water is not water. It is something that functions like water, that behaves like water, that pools and flows and evaporates according to patterns that only approximate the patterns of simpler worlds. It has been analyzed. It has been tasted. It carries minerals that do not match the minerals of the stone, traces of something that might be salt or might be memory, might be the residue of a sky that is reflected in pools when the reflection shows a sky that is not above. Visitors drink from it. Visitors do not get sick. Visitors do not get well. They simply continue, as the water continues, as the moisture continues to appear and vanish according to its own logic, according to the pressures and consistencies of this place where physics has grown tired and allowed itself to rest.

The windows are the hardest part to explain. They are not glass—glass is too modern, too precise, too dependent on temperatures and processes that this place abandoned long ago. They are openings in the stone, shaped with tools that left no marks, fitted with frames that have no fasteners. Through them, you see rooms that do not quite match the buildings they belong to. The geometry inside is wrong—not off by much, not enough to notice at first glance, but wrong in the way that a dream is wrong, in the way that a memory is wrong when you try to remember it too directly. Stairs that rise and fall in the same glance. Corridors that are longer on the inside than the outside. Doors that open onto rooms that contain doors that open onto the room you just left. Something moves behind the windows. Something watches. It is not the city-builder—or it is the city-builder at a different frequency, at a frequency where the city-builder has stopped being a pattern the space holds and has become instead a presence that holds the space. The window-watcher does not look at visitors. The window-watcher looks at the place, at the stone and the plants and the moisture and the other windows. The window-watcher is counting something, or remembering something, or simply seeing, which is its own kind of purpose.

And still you walk the roads, because the roads go somewhere, and somewhere is where you need to be, and the need is its own kind of reason. The roads are worn smooth by feet that have traveled them for longer than memory—yours, theirs, anyone's. They lead from building to building, from threshold to threshold, from door to door that opens onto air or stone or a room that contains a room that contains a view of a garden that contains a road that leads back to where you started. You have walked this road before. You will walk it again. The walking is the point, as much as there is a point, as much as points are available in a place that has stopped believing in destinations.

The cold is a kind of holding. It does not bite or burn or demand attention. It simply is, and in being, it holds you, holds the stone, holds the buildings and the plants and the moisture and the windows and the watching. The cold preserves. The cold slows. The cold allows things to persist that would otherwise have given up, would otherwise have let the strangeness win. The cold is the condition of this place, not its enemy. The cold is what makes it possible for the landscape to have survived its own strangeness, to have endured this long without dissolving into something else, something simpler, something that would not require doors that open onto air or plants that grow toward sound or windows that watch without being watched.

The city endures because it was built to endure. The city-builders built it knowing what lives beneath, knowing what breathes, knowing what sound the stone makes when it remembers that it was once a mountain. They built the walls to hold the sound. They built the roads to carry the footsteps of whatever would come after. They built the windows to see, and the doors to open, and the rooms to contain the geometry that does not quite work, that does not need to work, that works only enough to continue, only enough to persist, only enough to be home for those who have forgotten what home means and have settled for the almost-real, the almost-here, the almost-being that this place allows.

You are one of them now. The distinction between visitor and resident has worn thin, like the distinction between building and landscape, between road and stone, between the breathing and the silence that holds it. You drink the water that appears from nowhere. You walk the roads that lead nowhere new. You look through windows that watch you looking. You hear the breathing beneath your own breathing, and you do not find it strange anymore, because strangeness is only the first response, and you have passed through strangeness into something else, something that does not have a name but that feels like patience, like the willingness to continue, like the agreement to be held by a place that has forgotten what it was and is still forgetting what it is becoming.

The edges of things do not hold. You know this now. You have learned it in your walking, in your drinking, in your looking through windows that do not quite match the interior geometry. The edges are where consensus has grown thin, where the fabric of what-is has worn through to translucence, to possibility, to the space where the breathing begins. You stand at one of these edges now, or you have always been standing at one, or you have walked so long that you no longer know where the edge is and where you are. It does not matter. The edge is just another place where the city continues, where the roads lead, where the plants grow and the moisture gathers and the city-builders nod and the windows watch and the breathing holds everything together, holds you together, holds the memory of the mountains that you are only now beginning to forget.
