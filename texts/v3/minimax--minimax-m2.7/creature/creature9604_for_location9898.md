# The Inhabitants

They have learned to breathe with the city. This is the first thing that marks them—not language, not architecture, not any artifact of culture but this simple physicality, the way their lungs have adjusted to air that is two parts water, the way their skin has learned to accept perpetual moisture as a condition rather than an intrusion. Visitors from elsewhere arrive already uncomfortable, already fighting the wet that clings to everything, but those born here are seamless in it. They do not perspire the way others do because they are never dry. The boundary between self and atmosphere has become negotiable, and they have negotiated it in favor of the surrounding world.

Their bodies show it. The people of the Weeping City are lean in ways that speak of efficiency rather than scarcity—not thin but refined, as if generations of navigating vertical space have streamlined them into something optimized. Their hands are particular, the fingers long and sure, adapted for grasping the endless rails and rungs and handholds that make movement through the city's three-dimensional maze possible. They move in ways that would seem strange to those who know only horizontal terrain: climbing, descending, cantilevering across gaps, reading the architecture as a native speaker reads the intentions of a text. A child here learns vertical orientation before horizontal. They do not ask how far but how high, and the question is not metaphorical but existential.

The light has shaped them too, though this is harder to see. The diffuse glow that permeates the upper levels, the harsh white flashes of lightning that illuminate the canopy, the softer luminescence of the water channels far below—these have produced a people who see differently. Their eyes carry more rods than cones, making them acute in low light, comfortable in the variable illumination of a world where darkness is not absence but presence, where the night is simply a different mode of being alive. They are not troubled by the absence of sun. They have never known its full absence, but they have known the difference between the haze above the densest sectors and the sharper light that reaches the margins, and they have made peace with gradation.

They are many. This is perhaps the most defining feature of those who live in the Weeping City: not their physiology but their multiplicity. The scale of the place demands population. The complexity of the infrastructure cannot be maintained by a small number of hands. The vertical stacking has created not one city but thousands of interlocking neighborhoods, districts that speak different dialects, sectors that have developed distinct traditions, layers that exist in almost complete ignorance of one another except where the transit systems force intersection. To walk from one district to another might take an entire day, might require passes and permissions, might bring you into contact with people whose lives are as alien to yours as anything you might encounter in a place without roads. The city contains multitudes not because it is exceptional but because it is vast, and the people have multiplied to fill it, and in doing so have created a humanity more various than any single description can encompass.

In the wet places, where the water channels run and the moisture emerges from stone, there are other inhabitants.

They are small. They have to be. The city keeps itself clean of large organisms not through violence but through density—there is no room for large bodies except in the spaces carved for them, no food for large bodies except what the infrastructure provides, no stability for large bodies except on the maintained surfaces. What thrives in the margins are simpler things. Creatures no larger than a hand, translucent, patient, feeding on the microorganisms that graze the surfaces. They have no common name because they are rarely seen, emerging only in the deep hours when the transit slows, when the sound drops just enough for a careful observer to notice movement where movement should not be.

They are not the bioluminescent growths, though these also live, persist, perhaps are fed by the same ecological current. The fibrous pale organisms that emerge from damp stone are something else again—neither plant nor fungus but something that has found a niche in this world of mineral and water, something that grows along the edges of the city's moisture, something that might be alive in ways that require new words. They glow faintly when the light dims, not because they choose to but because they cannot help it, because the chemistry that sustains them produces light as a byproduct, and this light is enough to see by if you have learned to see by it. They are not beautiful in any human sense. They are simply present, persistent, another form of life that has refused to be excluded.

And there are stranger things still. The crystalline clusters that form along water channels, the rippling surfaces of the microorganism skins, the patterns in the stone that might be erosion and might be growth and might be something that has no name because it has never been observed long enough to be understood—these are not alive in any sense that would be immediately recognizable, but they are also not dead. They continue. They change. They respond to the conditions of this world in ways that suggest not merely reaction but something that might, from sufficient distance, resemble adaptation. Whether they are inhabitants in the full sense, whether they live or merely persist, is a question that the people of the Weeping City have learned not to ask, because the answer would not change anything. They are here. They are part of the city. That is enough.

There are those who say the city itself is alive.

This is not mysticism. It is not the poetic fancy of a people who have personified their environment through generations of intimacy. It is an observation, empirical in its spirit if not in its method, made by those who have watched the infrastructure adjust to itself, who have seen channels reroute without human intervention, who have noted the way the city seems to heal where it is damaged, closing wounds in the built environment before those wounds have fully formed.

The people do not worship the city. That is important to understand. They are not devotees of a machine god or acolytes of an emergent intelligence. They are practical. They are pragmatic. They maintain their systems and tend their infrastructure and repair what breaks. But they have also noticed that more things maintain themselves here than would maintain themselves elsewhere. They have noticed that the technological and the biological have become so thoroughly intertwined that the distinction has lost its usefulness. They have noticed that they live inside something that is larger than themselves, that has its own rhythms and patterns and needs, that might not be alive but is at least alive-adjacent in ways that matter more than the precise biological definition of the term.

Some of them serve it, in practical terms. There are those who work the deep infrastructure, who tend the water channels and the recycling pools and the systems that the city uses to maintain itself. They are not servants exactly. They are more like symbionts, organisms that have found a niche in the larger organism, contributing to its function in exchange for a place within it. They know its rhythms as farmers know the seasons, as sailors know the tides. They can feel when something is wrong in the deep places, a disturbance in the pulse of the circulation, a blockage in the flow. They are not magical. They are simply attuned, and the attunement is a form of knowledge that cannot be written down, that passes from body to body through generations of shared work, that belongs to the city as much as it belongs to them.

Whether the city thinks, whether the city is aware, whether the vast infrastructure that surrounds them has developed something that could be called interiority—these questions are unanswered and perhaps unanswerable. But the people who live here have made their peace with uncertainty. They live in a world of perpetual change, of constant turbulence, of systems too complex to fully comprehend. They have learned to live with the questions. They have learned to live without answers. They have learned to live in a place that might be alive, among others who are certainly alive, in an ecology of bodies and systems and processes that has continued so long that nobody remembers when it started and nobody can imagine when it might end.

---

They sleep in shifts. This is perhaps the most revealing detail about the people of the Weeping City: the schedules of their lives do not correspond to any cycle of light and dark that an outsider would recognize. The city never stops. Its pulse continues through the hours that elsewhere would be called night, and so its people sleep in shifts, waking and resting in patterns that are determined by function rather than by sun.

They do not sleep alone. The housing units are designed for intimacy, for the compression of bodies in spaces that must serve many needs. Privacy is not unknown but it is not guaranteed. The default condition of sleep is the presence of others, bodies close enough to touch, breath and sound and the small movements of living in proximity. This shapes them in ways that are hard to identify but easy to see. They are accustomed to one another. They are not starved for contact. They move through the city in crowds and clusters, comfortable in density, finding solitude an interesting novelty rather than a pressing need.

They eat together too. The food of the Weeping City is grown in ways that would seem alien to those who know only surface agriculture—cultivated in vats and towers and the strange vertical farms that occupy the margins where light still reaches, grown from organisms that have been engineered or evolved or somehow persuaded to serve as nutrition.
