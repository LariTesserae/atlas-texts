What lives here is not one thing but a condition—the condition of a place that has not ended. The water is the most obvious inhabitant, but to call it water is to give it a name it no longer needs. It was water when it came in through the burst mains and over the broken embankments, when it followed the logic of gravity and pressure and open doors. It is something else now. It holds its shape the way a held breath holds its shape, and it moves with intention—not the intention of a creature but the intention of a force that has been in one place long enough to develop preferences. It pools in the corners of flooded rooms and lets the surface still when there is no wind, and it does not flow the way water flows when water is going somewhere. It is here. It has been here. It is where things settle.

The machinery is older than the flooding and younger than the buildings and it runs in the space between the two, in the dark below the waterline where someone once built for permanence and got it. The pumps were designed to push water out, to reject it, to maintain the boundary between wet and dry that this district was built to cross. But the pumps have no memory and no instructions and no one to give them instructions, and so they run. They pull water from the lower channels and push it through the old pipes, and the water goes somewhere—it must go somewhere—but it comes back. It always comes back. The pumps do not know this. The pumps do not know anything. But they have become the heartbeat of this place, and the place has learned to count on it, and the low thrum in the walls and the water and the air is not a sound but a pulse, and the pulse means the place is alive, and the place is alive.

There are the fungal things. They should not be where they are—none of them should be where they are—but they are, and they are the most honest residents, the ones who most clearly belong to this place as it is rather than the place it was. They grow on the lower walls in shelves and clusters, and they are gray and pale and they do not reach for light because they have forgotten that light exists, or they never knew, and in the darkness they are not pale but a deep and luminous white, and they are soft, and if you touched one it would feel like something that had never been touched because nothing here is touched, nothing here is handled, nothing here is held. They grow on wood that is not quite wood anymore and on brick that has been softened by years of wet, and they grow in the mouths of pipes where the water has pulled back just enough to let them take hold, and they are fruiting, they are always fruiting, and the spores they release are so fine and so light that they hang in the still air above the water in a way that suggests breath, that suggests the place breathing out, and if you breathe in you taste them but they do not taste like anything, they taste like the absence of taste, they taste like water that has been water too long.

And there are the pale things. You have seen one or thought you have, and thinking you have is close enough because they are the kind of thing that exists in the space between seeing and not seeing, in the space where the eye invents what the mind expects. They are long and slow and they move through the water the way a thought moves through a dream, without urgency, without hesitation, and they are pale the way roots are pale, the way things that grow in darkness are pale, and they have no eyes because eyes are for seeing and they do not need to see. They know the water. They know the space between the buildings, the channels and corridors that were once streets, and they have learned the architecture in the way that water learns architecture—by filling it, by pressing against it, by being patient in the way that only water can be patient and that these things have inherited. They feed on the fungal growths or on what the fungal growths feed on or on the water itself filtered through a process you cannot imagine, and they do not flee from you because you are not a threat and you are not prey and you are barely a presence—you are a ripple, a disturbance, a wrongness in their still medium, and they move away from you not in fear but in the way a current moves away from an obstacle, reflexively, impersonally, already forgetting.

The willows are changing. You can see it in the bark, in the way it glistens when there is no rain, and you can see it in the way they lean—not toward the light because there is no light to lean toward but toward each other, and toward the old pumping station, and toward something beneath the water that they have learned to root toward the way other roots learn to grow toward water. Their roots are exposed where the soil has washed away, and the roots are white and thick and they grip the submerged concrete like fingers gripping something in the dark, and they do not let go. They will not let go. Something in them has decided that this is where they are and this is where they stay and the water that should kill them has instead taught them a new way to breathe.

And there is the district itself—the buildings, the streets, the infrastructure—as something alive, or as something that has always been alive and is only now being seen for what it is. The buildings do not breathe but they hold, and what they hold has weight, and the weight is in the walls. The walls remember people. Not consciously—they are not haunted, nothing here remembers consciously—but the way stone remembers the hand that shaped it, the way wood remembers the forest it came from. You can feel it when you press your palm against the wet brick: something in the material that is not material, a record of warmth and sound and the small daily actions of people who are not here and who left something behind that is not their fault and is not a ghost but is an absence that has weight, that has presence, that makes the air in the upper windows of the buildings feel thicker than it should, that makes the sealed doors feel watched.

What lives here is the threshold. That is the truest thing you can say about it. This is a place that was always going to be here because here is where wet and dry were always going to meet, where the machinery and the natural world were always going to push against each other, where the sky and the water were always going to become the same gray thing, and when the water came and the people left, the threshold remained, and on the threshold something has grown that is not water and is not land and is not machinery and is not nature, that is the condition of all three at once, and it is patient, and it is growing, and the gray light above is not gray because of winter—it is gray because this is the color of the world before it decides what it is, and it has not decided, and it will not decide, and you are standing in the middle of that not-deciding, waist-deep, and the cold is not an absence of warmth but a presence of something else, something that has been here since the water came and has been growing in it, feeding on it, becoming it, and when you ask what lives here you are asking the wrong question because living is not what this is doing. Living is what it was doing. This is what comes after living stops and the machinery keeps running.
