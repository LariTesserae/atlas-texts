The plateau holds its life the way it holds its water — in dispersed pockets, along lines of movement, never abundant in any one place but impossible to dismiss across the whole. The creatures here have learned to read the land in gradients, to feel the moisture moving through stone the way other creatures feel the pull of magnetic north, and they move accordingly, in slow circuits that connect the seeps like stations on a map written in thirst and satisfaction.

The herds are the plateau's signature, though they resist easy naming. They are not deer, not antelope, not the grazing animals of lower altitudes — they are something older in design, something that evolved to the rhythm of these particular rains and these particular mists. Their bodies are narrow and long, built for distance rather than speed, though they can move quickly when the arroyos flash-flood and they must flee the sudden brown water. Their legs are jointed in ways that let them navigate the uneven stone without losing step, and their feet — wide, padded, spreading against the soil — leave tracks that fill with fine dust and hold the shape of their passage long after they've moved on. Their hides are the color of the wet season: a grey-green that darkens when the mist lies heavy and pales to near-white in the dry spells, so that a herd at rest in the morning light seems to dissolve into the landscape, their forms becoming suggestions rather than certainties. They have learned, over generations uncounted, to read the air — the humidity content, the faint mineral smell of the seeps — and they move through the plateau in waves, ebbing and flowing between water sources in rhythms that overlap and interweave but rarely collapse into chaos. A traveler might see a herd gathered at a seep in the grey light of early morning, hundreds of them standing in the shallow pools with the mist curling around their flanks, silent except for the sound of their breathing and the occasional low vocalization that carries farther than it should in the still air.

The predators came first, or so the logic of the plateau would suggest, though no fossil record here is complete enough to confirm it. They are low to the ground, broader than the herds they follow, with bodies that seem to have been compressed by some slow, constant pressure into forms that ride just above the stone. Their coloration mimics the basalt — deep charcoal, weathered to near-invisibility against the dark rock — and they move in a way that suggests the plateau itself shifting, a ripple of movement that resolves into stillness the moment attention falls upon it. They hunt from the ridgelines, where the wind carries the scent of the herds to them and where the uneven ground provides cover for their approach. They are patient in the way of all apex creatures: they can wait for hours in a crevice, their eyes fixed on a seep where the prey will eventually come, their breathing slowing to match the subsonic pulse of the land beneath them. They are not solitary, exactly, but their social structures are difficult to parse from outside — they move in loose associations that come together and disperse according to rules no human observer has yet deciphered. What can be said is that they are present, always present, at the edges of every water source, watching, waiting, part of the plateau's ecology in the way that erosion is part of it, or the rain.

The birds belong to the thermals and the arroyos. They are dark — black or deep brown, their feathers holding a faint iridescence that shows only in the brief moments when the sun breaks through the cloud cover and turns the plateau suddenly golden. They are not songbirds; their calls are functional, sharp sounds that carry across the wide stone without becoming music. They nest in the deep ravines, where the walls are vertical and the rock holds the warmth through the cool nights, and they ride the rising air columns that form above the sun-heated stone during the day, spiraling upward in wide, lazy circles until they reach the altitude where the winds off the plateau's rim take them. They eat what the plateau offers: the insects that emerge after rain, the small creatures that shelter in the rock crevices, the carrion left by the predators when the kill is too large to finish. They are, in their way, the plateau's cleaners, the ones who return what dies to the air, to the soil, to the slow cycle of matter that moves through the high interior.

Below the birds, below the herd animals and the predators, there is a lesser life that carries the plateau's weight. Lizards that sun themselves on the flat stones in the brief hours when the light is strong enough to draw them out, their bodies flattened to catch and hold the warmth. Insects that emerge in the nights — moths with wings the color of dust, beetles with shells that reflect the faint starlight when the clouds part, the small and scrappy creatures that form the base of everything. The springs themselves host their own ecology: thin films of algae, tiny crustaceans the size of fingernails, the aquatic insects whose larvae are food for the birds and whose adults fill the air above the water in swarms that seem, for a moment, like a different kind of rain.

And there is, in the deep places, in the fissures that drop down toward the hum — something else. Not seen, not heard, but felt the way the land's pulse is felt, in the quiet moments when the breath slows and the ear opens to frequencies below hearing. The plateau was built over it, or through it, or around it — the infrastructure that hums in the dark beneath the basalt, the power that moves through channels carved or laid before the erosion began its slow work of forgetting. It does not touch the surface life, not directly, not in ways that could be measured or mapped. But it is there, as the water is there, as the ancient coral limestone is there, part of the substrate beneath the substrate, the foundation that everything rests on without knowing it. The creatures of the plateau pass over it in their migrations, and the seeps find their paths along or against its channels, and the mist that rises in the morning carries, perhaps, some faint charge — some industrial memory — that no instrument has yet been able to name.

This is what lives in the high interior: the lean and the watchful, the patient and the hungry, the bodies that move between water and stone in rhythms older than memory. The plateau holds them all, and they hold each other, and beneath them all the hum goes on, deep and steady, part of the silence.
