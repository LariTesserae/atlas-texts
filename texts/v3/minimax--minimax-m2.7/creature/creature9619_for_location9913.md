You notice, after some time, that you have stopped breathing as loudly. The body learns this space quickly — the air is thick, and shallow breathing seems more appropriate, as if the lungs themselves have understood that they are guests in a system that does not require them. You become aware of the sound of your own pulse in your ears, amplified by the close walls, and then aware of how quickly that sound fades as your body settles into the pocket's rhythms. The heart slows here, or so it seems. Time moves differently when there is nothing to look at but stone.

---

There are places where the moisture gathers not as pools but as saturation — where the stone itself has become something other than stone. Press your hand to the wall in one of these places and you will feel it: cold, but not merely cold, a cold with texture, slightly yielding, like pressing into wet clay that has hardened just short of true solidity. These patches feel almost warm by comparison to the dry stone around them, though they cannot be; it is only that the moisture reads as warmth to the skin, that the body interprets wetness as life-heat even in the absence of life. In these places, the boundary between wall and something less defined has begun to blur. The chisel marks that are still visible elsewhere have here been smoothed to nothing. The surface has become skin-like, faintly ridged, like the lines on a seashell or the whorls of a fingerprint. Touch it and you understand that this is not stone remembering the builders' hands. This is stone forgetting. The original shape is being erased by something patient, something that has no agenda but which erases nonetheless, by the simple fact of presence and time and water finding the same place together again and again.

---

The niches are not all empty. In one, near the bottom of a wall where water has pooled and dried and pooled again, a formation has grown that fills the space almost entirely — a mineral plug, white and dense, that looks almost like a candle that has melted and reformed, though of course it has never been a candle. It grew here, crystal by crystal, over what might have been centuries, filling a space that was made for something else. It is the most deliberate-looking thing in the pocket, the most artful, and it was made by no artist. You might mistake it for a conscious choice if you did not know what you know — if you did not understand that growth here is gravity and chemistry, not intention. But it fills the niche as a seed fills a cavity, and there is a rightness to it, a completeness that suggests purpose even in the absence of any purposive agent.

---

The light is the thing you cannot trust. It seems to come from below, or from within the walls themselves, or from some source so deep that it has simply saturated the stone over eons until the stone itself became luminous. But these are guesses. You have looked for the source and found none. The white veins in the ceiling do not glow so much as hold — they catch what light there is and release it reluctantly, diffusion rather than illumination. In the far corners, where the light does not reach, you can feel the darkness as a presence, a weight. You do not go there. There is nothing in the darkness that is not also in the light, but the darkness makes different demands on the imagination, asks different questions. Here, in the light, the space is a puzzle. In the darkness, it would be a mystery.

---

The mineral formations have their own geometries. Some branch upward from pools, delicate and pale, the color of ice or of bone held up to light. These are the oldest, or so you think — they are the most fragile, their structure precarious, liable to shatter if disturbed. In some places they have already shattered, and their fragments lie on the pool floors like scattered teeth, still present but no longer part of the whole. Other formations grow outward from cracks in the walls, flat and layered, like the scales of some ancient fish or the fronds of a fern pressed flat between pages of stone. These are denser, darker, often colored — rust and copper and the faint green of verdigris where copper has met water and transformed. Where they grow, the wall behind them is stained, saturated, as if they are flowering from the stone rather than upon it. And there are places — rarer, harder to see — where a single crystal has found its footing on an existing formation and begun to grow. These are the bright ones, the ones that catch the light and hold it, giving back prismatic fragments of the gray luminance. They are young, in the language of this place. They have time.

---

You begin to notice the textures of silence here. True silence is impossible; the water is too present, the space too enclosed. But there are silences of different densities, silences that rest in different places. In the deep pools, where drops fall rarely now — the paths having shifted, the water having found new routes — there is a particular silence, a held quality, as if the pool is waiting. In the niches, if you press close, you can hear what might be called silence but which is more accurately described as absence of specific sound: a flattening, a muffling, as if the carved space still remembers its purpose and holds that memory as a kind of acoustic dampening. The niches do not echo. The space swallows sound there, absorbs it, returns nothing. You could speak into a niche and the words would fall dead against the stone. This, too, is a kind of memory.

---

The stairs descend from somewhere that is no longer accessible. They end here, or begin here, depending on your direction of approach — though there is only one direction that makes sense now, the direction of the living world, the world above and beyond, which seems impossibly distant, as if it belongs to another story entirely. The steps are worn at their centers, a curve that fits the arch of a foot, though whose foot, and how many feet over how many years, cannot be known. The stone here is different: darker, denser, as if the pressure of passage has compressed it, driven out the moisture, made it harder and more resilient. You trace the worn curve with your fingers and feel the history of use, the repetition that shaped stone into something that fits a body, that makes movement easier, that turns a raw stair into a tool. This is what the built world does: it accommodates the body, and in accommodating it, it records the body, preserves its shape in the medium of the building. The stair remembers feet. The niches remember hands. The channels remember water, or the absence of water, or the control of water — it is impossible to say which, and perhaps it does not matter. The built world is a world of records, and this pocket is full of them.

---

But the records are being edited. This is the thing about time: it does not merely pass; it intervenes. The channels that once carried water — or kept it out, or controlled its flow — are half-filled now with sediment so fine it might be powder. You would not notice it was there if you were not looking, but once seen, it cannot be unseen: a pale line along the channel's edge, like a tide mark, like the record of a water level that no longer exists. The worn steps have not been worn further in what might be centuries; instead, they are being built up, mineral deposits slow-filling the curves, as if the stone is healing itself from the wound of use, as if it remembers being whole and is trying to return to that state. The niches themselves are shrinking. The one with the mineral plug is the extreme case, but others show the beginnings of this process: rimes of white bloom along their edges, crystal growths at their mouths, the slow closure that will, given enough time, seal them entirely. The space is being written over. The past is being replaced by the present, which is itself a kind of past, a record of now that will itself be edited, in time, by the same patient hand.

---

You understand, after a while, that you are the only living thing here, and that this is temporary. Your breath clouds the air, faintly, when you exhale. Your skin leaves moisture on the stone when you touch it. Your presence changes the humidity, disrupts the equilibrium by a fraction, adds your water-vapor to a system that has been, until this moment, closed. You are a disturbance, small but real. The space will adjust — the walls will absorb your contribution, incorporate it into their slow chemistry — but it will take time, and in that time, you have made a mark. You hope it is small. You hope it will fade. But you have been here, and here remembers.

---

There is a word for this kind of space, or there are words: cellar, crypt, tomb, pocket. Each implies something about what was kept here, or who was kept here, or what kept itself here. The word you choose depends on what you fear, or what you hope. The space does not care which word you use. The space is older than language, or at least older than the language you have for it. The builders had a word, surely — a word for the space as they made it, for the function they intended. That word is lost. Only the space remains, and the space has no need of words. It has its own vocabulary: water, stone, time, crystal, sound, light, growth. These are the words it speaks to itself, in a language made of presence and patience, and you are permitted to listen, for a time, but you are not invited to speak.

---

When you leave — and you will leave, because leaving is what the living do — you will carry nothing with you. The space does not permit removal; what is here belongs here, has always belonged here, will belong here when you are gone. But you will carry something away: the memory of gray light, of mineral gardens, of water speaking to stone and stone answering in the language of slow change. You will carry the sound, too, and the silence between sounds, and the feeling of low ceiling and ancient air. These are the things the pocket allows you to keep. These are the things that will remain, long after the pocket itself has shifted and grown and become something you would not recognize, if you could see it again. You are a visitor to a moment in a process that has no beginning and no end. You have been here. Here has been in you. The exchange is not equal — it cannot be, you are so brief, and it is so long — but it is real, and for a time, that was enough.
