# What Lives There

The people who have built here have built with the knowledge that the plateau does not care what they make. This is not a cruelty in the stone—it is simply a fact, like the fact of the water or the fact of the wind, and the people have made their peace with it in the way that farmers make their peace with soil that will grow what it will grow, no more. They build because building is what their hands know how to do, and they build carefully because careless building would fall, and they build with the stone that the plateau offers because to import stone from elsewhere would be an insult to the negotiation that has already been completed.

They are not a people who speak of change. Their oldest stories do not concern origins or migrations or the discovery of this high place. Their oldest stories concern the way the light falls on the basins at different hours, the way the blue flowers close before rain, the way the wind shifts when winter tightens its hold. These are not metaphors for anything. They are the stories themselves, and the people tell them with the same attention they bring to all things—with the attention of those who have learned that attention is enough.

A child raised here learns the plateau the way a root learns soil: not through study but through the slow accretion of presence, through the body's knowledge of where the ground is cold in morning, where the moss is thick enough to kneel, where the water sounds different because the basin is deeper. The child does not ask why these things are so. The question would be understood, but it would be understood as a strange question, the kind of question that assumes the world might be otherwise. The world is not otherwise. The world is this.

The people are neither tall nor short, neither pale nor dark, neither thin nor broad in any particular way that would distinguish them from people in other places. This is because they have not needed to distinguish themselves. There has been no pressure from the land to select for any particular trait, no drought or flood or scarcity that would thin the population toward one shape of survival. They have simply continued, in the way that the birds have continued, in the way that the flowers have continued, and so they have continued as they are, which is as they have always been, which is as they will be when their grandchildren's grandchildren walk the same paths and kneel at the same basins and tell the same stories to children who will not need them to be different.

They build their fires in the evening when the stone begins to lose the warmth it has gathered through the day. The smoke rises and thins and joins whatever the smoke becomes when it leaves the plateau, and they do not think about this, because there is no need to think about what does not affect the burning. In the morning they wake when the light reaches the particular angle that triggers waking, and they rise, and they begin the work that is before them, and the work is always there because the work is never finished and never needs to be finished because finishing is not the point. The point is the continuing, and the continuing is enough.

---

Among the birds there is one that does not sing. It is brown and small and quick, and it moves through the city with the same confidence as the others but without their music, as if it has decided that what it has to say cannot be said in sound. It builds its nest in the same place each year, in a crack between two stones that have been there since the crack first appeared in the young basalt, and it raises its young with the efficiency of an animal that knows exactly what is required, and when the young have grown they do not stay or leave but simply are, in the way that all creatures here simply are, occupying the space that the equilibrium has made for them.

The birds that do sing—the ones with the clear notes that rise in the morning and fall at dusk—they sing to the wind as much as to each other. The wind does not hear. The wind is not a creature that hears. But the birds sing anyway, and perhaps the singing is not for anyone, and perhaps that is its own kind of communication, the kind that does not require a listener, the kind that is complete in the making.

The insects are too small for the human eye to see their faces, but if you could see them you would see that each one is perfect. Not perfect in the way of an ideal form, but perfect in the way of a key in a lock, of a hand in a glove, of something that fits so completely into its place that the fitting itself becomes invisible. A particular beetle emerges from the soil when the soil reaches a certain temperature, and it finds a particular plant, and it eats a particular amount, and it lays its eggs in a particular location, and it dies, and the eggs hatch, and the next generation does the same, and the doing has no variation because variation would be a kind of error, and there is no error here because error has never been necessary.

The small creatures—the ones with fur, the ones that scurry along walls and pause at the edges of basins and blink at the light—are not tame, not in the way that animals elsewhere become tame through generations of contact with humans. They are simply unafraid, not because they have learned that humans are harmless but because fear has never been a useful response to this particular environment. The plateau has no predators that hunt by stealth. The plateau has no sudden movements, no shadows that mean danger, no urgency that would be answered by flight. So the creatures do not flee. They regard. They assess. They determine that you are not a threat, or that you are not anything at all, and they return to their business, which is the business of eating and drinking and continuing, which is all the business there is.

---

There is a woman who tends the basins. This is not her only work, and it is not a duty that was assigned to her, but she has made it her work because she noticed, some years ago, that the water did not drain as quickly when the dead leaves were cleared, and so she clears the dead leaves, and the water drains more slowly, and she does not know why this is so, and she does not need to know why, and she continues, and the water continues, and between them there is a conversation that has no words and no need for words.

There is a man who cuts the timber from the valley below. He does not cut more than the forest can replace, because his father taught him the signs, and his father's father taught his father, and the signs are simple: cut here when the tree is old enough, and do not cut there because the roots hold the soil that holds the water, and if you take the water, there will be nothing left to take. He knows this the way he knows his own name, not as information but as fact, the kind of fact that does not require belief.

There are children who play in the spaces between the buildings, where the moss is thick and the stones are worn smooth by generations of small feet. They play games that have names and rules and outcomes, and the outcomes are not predetermined, but the games are the same games that their parents played, and their parents' parents played, because the plateau has not changed, and so the play has not needed to change, and the changing of the games is not a loss but simply a different kind of continuing, the kind where the shape remains and the details shift and no one notices the shift because the shift is not the point.

---

At night the plateau holds its breath. The wind does not stop—it never stops—but it softens, becomes a whisper rather than a voice, a presence rather than a force. The water in the basins continues its small speaking, but the speaking is for no one now, or for those who are asleep and cannot hear it, or for something else that is not human and does not need ears. The smoke from the chimneys thins and rises and is gone. The birds settle into their places, some in the trees of the valley, some in the cracks of the walls, some in the open air with their wings tucked and their eyes closed. The insects retreat into the soil or the moss or the cracks where the cold cannot reach. The small creatures find their burrows or their nests or their corners of the city where they have always slept.

The people sleep. They do not dream differently than their ancestors dreamed, or if they do, the difference is not remembered, and if it is remembered, it is not important. What matters is the sleep itself, the body's surrender to the night, the slow breath that rises and falls in the darkness, the warmth that gathers under blankets or furs or simply the body's own heat, and the morning that comes as it has always come, with the light reaching the angle that triggers waking, with the birds beginning their songs, with the smoke rising from the chimneys as the fires are rekindled, with the man walking down to the valley and the woman clearing the basins and the children running through the spaces where the moss has grown to exactly the height it has always grown.

The plateau does not notice them. The plateau does not notice anything, not in the way that noticing requires awareness, not in the way that awareness requires a self that can be aware. But they are here, as the water is here, as the moss is here, as the wind is here, as the stone is here. They are part of what lives, and what lives here is the plateau, and the plateau is what lives, and the living is the same as the stone and the same as the water and the same as the centuries that pass without passing, that continue without changing, that are always now in the way that still water is always still, in the way that moss is always climbing, in the way that the wind is always sliding across the young stone with its sound that is no sound until it meets something that can hear.

And now there is something that can hear.

Now there is you.
