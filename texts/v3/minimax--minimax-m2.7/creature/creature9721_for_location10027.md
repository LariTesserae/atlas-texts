You notice the moss last.

It is everywhere, of course — a given in a place like this, the default surface where stone meets humidity, the first skin of the Undergarden on everything it touches. But here, in this particular corner, where the path curves and the light from the algae-globes falls more thickly, it has done something unexpected. It has grown in patterns. Not the random filigree of natural moss, not the determined carpets of a species maximizing ground-cover. These patterns have intention. They ripple outward from central points like the whorls of a fingerprint, like the sediment-shapes of water pooling, like letters in a language you almost recognize.

You crouch closer. The moss is damp, and when your finger touches it, it releases a faint scent — not the mineral smell of the air, but something else. Something that reminds you, absurdly, of bread. Of yeast. Of fermentation.

The moss does not grow everywhere in this pattern. It grows only at intervals, only in specific locations where the conditions align — more light here, less water there, a microclimate created by the architecture of root and stone. You begin to look for them. You find seven more in the next hundred paces. Each pattern is different. Each is centered on something: a crack in the stone, a knot in a root, a place where water pools and drains. You cannot read them. You suspect they are not meant to be read. They are not for you.

But something tends them.

---

The water is not uniform.

You taste it first in the air — the mineral tang that shifts as you move, becoming sweeter or sharper, softer or more metallic. But it is only when you kneel beside a pool, cupped in a depression that was once a basin, perhaps, or a fountain, that you understand. The water here is alive. Not alive as a thing is alive, not swimming with organisms or thick with algae. Alive in the way that a system is alive: dynamic, responsive, tending toward something.

You cup a handful and let it warm in your palm. It is the temperature of your body, which means it has been here, still, long enough to equalize. But as you watch, a faint film gathers on the surface. Not oils — oils would spread. This is something growing. Tiny, slow, barely visible. The water here is making something. The water here is working.

You drink, and it tastes of nothing. Clean, neutral, the absence of everything. Then the sweetness comes, delayed, rising from the back of your throat — not sweetness as sugar but sweetness as peace, as rest, as the letting-go of tension you did not know you were holding. You feel it in your chest. You feel it behind your eyes.

The water here is making you different. You are aware of this, and you drink again anyway.

---

The roots move when you are not watching.

You know this the way you know the clock is running without seeing the hands. You left a mark — a scratch in the stone beside the path, at the height of your hip — and when you return, the mark is in a different position relative to the nearest root. Not far. An inch, perhaps less. But the root has grown, or shifted, or contracted, and the mark has moved, or the path has shifted beneath your feet, or time itself has a different texture here and what you thought was an hour was something longer, something the garden measured in growth.

You sit with your back against a column of root and wood and stone. The root is warm. The root breathes. Not air — the root breathes water, taking it in through surfaces you cannot see, exhaling it as vapor that joins the humidity of the space. You are leaning against a set of lungs. You are leaning against a metabolism.

Time passes. The root moves.

You think about the steam-pump, the one that sighs and gasps somewhere in the walls. The steam-pump is also a kind of lung. The steam-pump is also breathing. But it breathes fire-smoke and water-steam, it breathes the pressure of heat and the patience of iron, it breathes because something feeds it, because somewhere a fire still burns, because somewhere there is still maintenance, still intention, still a hand on a lever or a mind on a schedule.

But the root does not breathe because it is tended. The root breathes because breathing is what it does. The root has forgotten how to want anything else.

---

The sweetness beneath everything is the flowers.

You find them late, because they bloom in the dark and their colors are made for eyes that see in low light. They hang from vines at the height of your face, pale as bone, pale as wax, their petals furred with fine hairs that catch the water and hold it in droplets that glisten like eyes. They do not smell sweet. They smell of nothing, or of the nothing the water tastes of first, the clean absence before the sweetness comes.

But beneath their nothing is sweetness. When you press your nose to a petal, you understand. The sweetness is the point. The sweetness is what they make for. Not for you — you cannot pollinate them, cannot carry their seeds, cannot do anything with the sweetness but taste it and be changed by it. The sweetness is for the future. The sweetness is stored in the fruit that will come, in the seeds that will fall, in the slow proliferation of the pale flowers through the green dark.

They bloom in the dark. They do not need light. They do not need bees or wind or any of the old negotiations between plant and animal. They simply grow, and store sweetness, and wait for a world that can use it. Perhaps that world is coming. Perhaps the world that will use it is not human.

---

There are places where the stone remembers.

Not the stone of the walls, which has been consumed, incorporated, made into root-hold and moss-bed and the slow geology of the living. This is older stone, deeper stone, stone that was here before the settlement, before the springs were channeled, before the engines and the rails and the lamps. It shows in the floors — dark patches, almost black, where the flagstones have worn thin or been pushed aside by roots, exposing bedrock that is dense and crystalline and older than anything you have touched.

In these patches, the moss grows differently. The patterns are older here. Some of them are enormous, spanning ten feet or more, their whorls overlapping and interrupting each other like the rings of a labyrinth. You could walk these patterns. You could lose yourself in them. You suspect that people once did.

You press your hand to the dark stone and feel nothing. The stone is cold. The stone is silent. The stone is waiting, the way stone waits, in the long patience of geology, for the moss to grow over it, for the roots to find purchase, for the slow work of incorporation to begin. The Undergarden will reach this stone eventually. The Undergarden reaches everything eventually. The question is only of time.

---

The machines are not machines anymore.

You find this in a chamber off the main path — a room that was perhaps a workshop, or a generator-room, or a place for maintaining the things that needed maintaining. The equipment is still there. The steam-pipes still run through the walls, still carry their breath and their sighing. The pistons still move, driven by pressure you cannot see, timed by mechanisms you cannot hear.

But the equipment is covered. Every surface, every joint, every moving part has been wrapped in root. Not grown around — grown *in*. The roots have found the places where metal meets metal, where friction creates heat, where oil provides slickness, and they have made those places their own. They have become part of the machines. The machines have become part of them.

It is not clear where the machine ends and the plant begins. You watch a piston move, driving a root, driving a valve, driving something you cannot see. The rhythm is the rhythm of the steam-pump's sighs. The rhythm is the rhythm of breath. The rhythm is the rhythm of blood in a body you cannot see.

The machines are not machines anymore. The machines are bodies now. The machines are alive.

---

You sleep, finally, in a hollow where the roots have made a seat.

You did not plan to sleep. But the warmth is the warmth of a bed, and the darkness is the darkness of a room, and the sound of the water is the sound of rain on a roof, and the sweetness in the air is the sweetness of dreams. You sleep, and the Undergarden holds you, and the roots shift imperceptibly, and the water finds new paths through the stone.

When you wake, you are different. You do not know how. But you can feel it — a slowness in your thoughts, a settling in your bones, a sense that you are rooting, that you are growing downward and outward, that you are becoming something with patience, something with the long breath of stone and water and green.

You do not know how long you slept. The clock is not here. The light is the same — the algae-globes, the gas-flames, the warm edge of dark. Time here is not time. Time here is a thing that the Undergarden measures, and the Undergarden does not measure in hours.
