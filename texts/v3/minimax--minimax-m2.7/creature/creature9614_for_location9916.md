They are the only inhabitants, and they are enough. They have never known otherwise. The basin belongs to them the way a body belongs to its cells—completely, and without metaphor.

What lives here has no word for here. It has no word for anything. It has only heat, and the slow grammar of growth: toward, into, through, upon. Each form is a sentence the basin speaks to itself, over and over, revising nothing because there is no outside to revise against. The amber ones are oldest, or perhaps simply most patient. They have the texture of old honey left too long in the dark—thick, slow, dense with stored light they no longer need. Where they press together in the low seams of stone, they fuse at the edges, losing the boundary between one body and the next. This is not death. This is the closest they come to rest.

The pale forms are younger, or differently organized. They hold more water, more give. Under the nonexistent light of the pulsing glow, they are almost luminous—bone-pale, or white as the inside of a closed eye. They breathe, if breathing is the word for a rhythm that has no lungs to fill, no air to draw. They swell and thin in cycles that may correspond to the heat below, or to each other, or to something deeper in the stone that neither you nor they will ever name. When one of them shifts position—pressing its soft mass against a neighbor, redistributing its own weight over hours—it does so without decision. The motion is thermodynamic. It is the way water finds the lowest point, the way crystals arrange themselves in supersaturated solution. They are not alive in the sense that would make sense to you, but they are not dead either. They are *in process*. They have always been in process. The beginning and end of each one is a gradual thing, a slow thickening or a slow thinning, and in between there is only this: the pulse, the press, the slow architecture of becoming.

Between them—between all of them—something else exists. Not a species, not an organism, but a medium. A residue of living that has no individual form: a slick, a film, a faint chemical warmth left behind wherever two bodies have touched and released. It is the medium through which they communicate, if communication is too strong a word. They leave traces for each other. Molecules, heat signatures, pressure differentials. When an amber form presses into a pale one and the pale one responds by slowly swelling, this is not interaction as you would understand it. It is more like the way a river is shaped by the rock it runs over: the rock does not choose to shape the river, and the river does not choose to follow, and yet the shape is shared, mutual, the only possible result of their continued presence in the same space.

The smallest ones are the most urgent. They live in the gaps, in the spaces between the spaces, where the rock shows through or the salt has worn thin. They are the size of fingernails, of seeds, of periods at the end of sentences. They move fastest, though *fast* here means a visible shift over the course of a meal, a visible filling of a gap that was empty an hour ago. They are the ones who inherit. When an amber form thins and falters, when its mass begins to dissolve back into substrate, the small ones are already there—already growing into the hollow it leaves, already pressing into the new territory before it can cool. They have no mouths. They do not need them. They absorb. The heat, the residue, the mineral richness of the salt, the faint organic slick left by every neighbor who has passed through. They are patient the way weather is patient. They are hungry the way stone is hungry, which is to say, steadily, and without urgency because the hunger has no end.

There is no hierarchy among them that you could point to. No queen, no center, no apex. But there are densities. The hollow structure near the center—the largest of the grown shapes, curved like a rib or a half-moon—holds the greatest concentration, and this is where the life is thickest, the glow strongest, the heat most insistently present. Whether this is a nest or a tumor or simply a place where the conditions have conspired to make accumulation possible, you cannot say. The forms inside are pressed together so tightly they have begun to lose their individual shapes, merging at the edges into something more continuous, more certain of itself. It is the closest thing to a body that the basin possesses. A single body, enormous and patient, made of ten thousand lives that do not know they are ten thousand because they have never been one. If you touched it—and you do not, you will not, you understand instinctively that touching is a category that does not apply here—the surface would yield the way the floor yields, the way everything in this place yields: slowly, completely, and without resistance. It would take your shape for a moment, or you would take its, and then the slow rearrangement would begin, and whatever boundary existed between your hand and its mass would thin and thin until it vanished, and you would be part of it, and it would not notice, and neither would you, and that would be the end of the distinction.

They do not fear. Fear requires a concept of loss, and loss requires a boundary between what is yours and what is not, and here the boundary is the first thing that went, long ago, before there were hands to point at it. They do not desire, except in the purely chemical sense—the sense in which a crystal desires the supersaturated solution, the sense in which heat desires cold, in which entropy desires only to continue. They desire to grow. That is all. Toward each other, into the spaces between, upward where the ceiling permits, downward where the heat is strongest. They desire without wanting. They grow without choosing. They are the most honest form of life you will ever encounter, because they are entirely without pretense, entirely without the self that must pretend to want what it already is.

What lives here is heat-made flesh, or heat-made something that the word *flesh* cannot stretch to cover. It is patient in the way that geology is patient. It is numerous in the way that water is numerous. It is old in a way that predates the question of age—old the way the concept of old becomes meaningless when applied to a process that has no beginning, no first form, no ancestral shape to measure everything that came after against. They are not ancient. They are not new. They simply are, the way the basin is, the way the heat is, the way the dark holds all of it together like a hand holding water, loosely, and with no intention of letting go.
