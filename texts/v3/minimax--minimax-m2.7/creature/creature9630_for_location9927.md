# What Lives in Veth Karan

It lives in the texture of things before it lives in the shape of them. In the condensation on a pipe joint at dawn, in the way a wall holds humidity a half-second longer than the air around it, in the particular quality of rust that forms on certain steels—not the orange-red of decay but something darker, almost burgundy, as if the metal were bleeding. To understand what lives in Veth Karan you must understand that life here has learned to mean something different than it means elsewhere. It means: whatever persists in the space between heat and water, whatever can hold on when the world exhales and inhales and gives nothing freely.

Start with the people. They are not many, by the scale of the land they occupy—and the land insists on scale, forces a person to reckon with it, because to cross from one structure to another is to walk through a silence so complete it becomes a kind of pressure, a weight against the ears. The people of Veth Karan have thickened skins, not metaphorically but literally: generations of living in low humidity punctuated by sudden saturation have produced a population whose skin holds moisture differently, whose lips crack less easily and whose eyes water more readily, as if the body itself were learning to weep on behalf of the stones. They are quiet people, though not by temperament. It is the city that quiets them. There is no reason to raise one's voice between structures when the distance between listeners is measured in kilometers of bare rock. There is no reason to speak quickly when the water takes its time, when the machines pulse and breathe and take their time, when the whole system moves at a pace that would drive a faster people to madness. They have learned slowness. They have made it into a practice and the practice into a kind of grace.

The people cluster. They cannot help it. The emptiness between structures is not merely geographic but ontological—it is a void in which the assumptions of ordinary habitation do not apply. In the nodes, the inhabited zones, the density is surprising: narrow corridors of living space stacked three and four levels high, ventilation shafts threading between buildings like shared lungs, shared water channels that run through kitchens and bathrooms and open-air bathing pools on the upper levels where the condensation is thickest. To live in Veth Karan is to live in the topology of its water, and the people have internalized this geography so thoroughly that they navigate the city as much by sound—the drip and hush of channels through walls—as by sight. A child growing up here learns the rhythm of the pipes the way a child elsewhere learns the rhythm of a parent's heartbeat.

They are industrial people, in the way that word once meant something: they maintain, they extract, they monitor. The machinery that pulls moisture from the atmosphere is not a technology they invented so much as one they inherited, a knowledge passed down in maintenance logs and apprenticeship and the specific, intimate knowledge a person develops for a machine they have spent twenty years keeping alive. Each moisture extractor has a keeper, and each keeper has their own ways—the particular angle they adjust the intake baffles, the specific rhythm of the cleaning cycles, the private understanding of when a machine is *sick* versus merely difficult. The machines are not loved, exactly, but they are known. They are known the way landscape is known, with the body more than the mind, with the recognition that what you are tending is not a tool but a system larger than yourself, older than yourself, and that the relationship is not one of mastery but of stewardship.

The water itself is a kind of inhabitant. Not alive in any way that science from elsewhere would recognize, but alive in the way that patterns are alive, in the way that a current is alive while it lasts. It arrives as condensation, as dew, as the patient exhalation of stone, and then it moves—through channels carved by hand and by time, through pipes of iron and ceramic and repurposed steam line, down from the high structures to the low ones and outward toward collection basins that have no visible purpose. The water does not ask where it is going. The people do not always know. There are channels that appear on no map, routes that the city's oldest residents have never walked, whole branches of the water system that exist in a kind of historical superposition—still functioning, still flowing, but forgotten, or remembered only in fragments. To walk the deep channels is to understand that the city's plumbing is not a single system but a palimpsest, layer upon layer, the old channels still carrying water that has been running through them for centuries, and the new channels threading alongside them or through them or in some places straight through the abandoned routes, carrying different water, or perhaps the same water, or perhaps no water at all, just the memory of flow.

And there are the others. Not the people, not the water, not the machines—though the machines have their own strange life, the way all sustained systems eventually do.

The fungal threads are the quietest. They live in the deep places, in the cracks where moisture seeps and settles and does not evaporate because the stone holds it, because the architecture of heat and elevation creates microclimates beneath the surface where water can exist in small, stable congregations. The threads are pale—off-white, faintly luminous in total darkness, the way certain fungi are—and they feed on moisture the way no organism elsewhere does, not consuming the water but *processing* it, moving it through their filaments in slow chemical rhythms that take days to complete a single cycle. A biologist from elsewhere might call them decomposers, might argue about taxonomy, but here taxonomy breaks down. These fungi are not decomposing anything. They are doing something stranger: they are refining the water, or perhaps the water is refining itself through them, the way a river polishes a stone, the way a system under pressure eventually produces something that could not have been predicted from its components. In the deepest channels, where no light has ever reached and the humidity is constant, the fungal mats form patterns that are not random—too regular to be coincidence, too irregular to be designed. Some of the older residents claim these patterns are words. They have not agreed on what they say.

The creatures are harder to speak of because they are harder to see. They have adapted to the logic of the condensation economy: small things that can exist on moisture alone or nearly so, that drink from the weeping rocks and are sustained by dew that forms and disappears in a single hour. These are the size of large insects or small mammals—unclassifiable, taxonomically homeless, surviving in niches so narrow that their existence seems more like a fact of physics than biology. They move in the dawn, when the condensation is thickest, and they are silent when they move. You might see the evidence of their passage before you see them: a trail of moisture disturbed on a dark stone surface, a pattern of small prints in a layer of dew. They are gone before you can look closely. The larger things that eat them—and here *larger* means only somewhat larger, not predator so much as consumer—move at dusk and in the early hours of darkness, and they are more still, more watchful, more given to long periods of immobility interrupted by sudden motion. They are not dangerous to people, or they have learned not to be, which amounts to the same thing. Their eyes are pale, almost translucent, adapted to dim light and to the flicker of amber lamp-glow across bare rock. They are patient in a way that mirrors the land's patience, and they are difficult to distinguish from the stones themselves when they choose to be still.

There is something else, or there is the sense of something else, which in a place like Veth Karan may be the same thing. The wrongness mentioned in the description is not an intruder. It is not something that has arrived from outside. It is, if anything, the oldest resident—older than the people, older than the machines, perhaps older than the water itself, or at least older than the water's current path through the world. It is the sense that the condensation is not merely a phenomenon but an intention. That the weeping of the stones is not merely physics but *grief*, or something that grief is the nearest human word for, and that beneath the city, beneath the channels and the fungal mats and the deep basalt, something is holding its breath.

It is not a god. The people of Veth Karan would not use that word, or any word. It is more like a condition—a way the air thickens at certain hours, a quality in the condensation that is almost but not quite distinguishable from its chemical composition, a pattern in the flow of water through the deepest channels that repeats with a periodicity too long to measure by human time but short enough to be felt, if a person stays long enough, if a person sits in the damp corridors of a low structure at two in the morning and listens to the pipes and the silence and the faint, almost inaudible sound of water moving through routes that may or may not exist.

It is the kind of thing that accumulates. It is the kind of thing that, the longer you stay, the more you notice, until one day you realize you have stopped noticing it and started *expecting* it, and by then you are already part of it, already moving through the city with a sense of its rhythms that you cannot explain because the rhythms themselves are not explained, are not explained, and the water keeps moving, and the stones keep weeping, and the fungal threads in the deep channels keep polishing something that no one has ever seen all the way through, and the amber lights of the inhabited zones keep burning at night, and between them the darkness holds its shape, and the heat keeps bending the light, and the world of Veth Karan continues not in defiance of its own strangeness but in full, patient accommodation of it, the way a body accommodates a heartbeat, the way a stone accommodates the weight of centuries—

the way water, against all reason, in the hottest air, finds its way down.
