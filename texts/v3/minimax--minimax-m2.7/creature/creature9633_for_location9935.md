The creatures that patrol the corridors between clusters are few, but they are the grammar by which the plateau speaks.

You hear them before you understand them — not the high chatter of the small fauna, not the collective murmur of the clusters, but something deeper, a sustained call that bends around the growth clusters and arrives from a direction that keeps shifting. The call is not loud, not in the way loudness usually means, but it is present, a frequency that seems to move through stone as much as air, and when it comes you feel it in the thin soil beneath your feet before you hear it, a vibration that says something large is moving through a place you might be standing in an hour from now.

The corridor-hunters, if you need a name. The plateau's ecology demands them: creatures that can cross the void, that carry the warmth of their bodies through the cold for hours, that have learned to read the acoustic geography well enough to navigate by sound alone. They are solitary. The territory a single hunter marks is measured in the distance a call will carry before it fades — a day's walk, sometimes two, the corridors between clusters divided into domains that overlap at their edges but never quite coincide. You learn to recognize the calls of specific individuals over time, the way the pitch and rhythm and timbre of the sustained call differs from one hunter to the next, the way a call that has been silent for a long period means the territory has changed hands.

They are not predators in any simple sense. The small fauna are too dispersed, too hidden in the clusters, too fast in their movements — the energy cost of hunting them across the void would be greater than the return. What the hunters do is patrol: they move through the corridors, calling at the boundaries, marking their presence in the acoustic geography of the plateau, and in doing so they define the paths that are safe to travel. They eat what they find — the small creatures that risk the crossings between clusters, the pools where the water draws a different kind of life, the carrion that the brief warm season leaves behind. But the hunting is secondary to the patrol, and the patrol is its own purpose, a behavior that evolved not for sustenance but for something that might be territory or might be simply the need to move through a space too large to stay still in.

In the deep cold, the hunters go silent.

This is when the plateau is most dangerous — not because the hunters are a threat, but because their silence means the acoustic geography is unoccupied, the normal signals absent, and you must navigate by the clusters and the channels and the memory of calls that are not being made. The silence of the hunters is not dormancy, exactly. They move still, slower, their metabolism tuned to the cold in ways that let them survive without the food they would need in warmer conditions. But the calls stop, and the corridors are empty of sound, and the only things moving through them are the wind and the cold and whatever has need of crossing in the worst weather.

You have seen one.

Not often, not clearly — the hunters are built for the corridors, not the open spaces, and they avoid the clusters where the density is highest and the light is strongest. But once, in the last hour of a cold night when the glow of a cluster was close enough to see and the sky had begun its slow lightening toward something that was not quite dawn, a shape moved through the corridor ahead of you. It was large — larger than you expected, the body carried low and long, the limbs thick for the weight they bore, the surfaces seeming to absorb light rather than reflect it. You could not see it clearly. The thin air and the angle of the non-light conspired against detail. But you saw it move, the unhurried pace of something that knows it will cross the corridor before the call it makes returns to it, and then it was gone, a darker shape against the dark, and the corridor was silent again.

It did not look at you. It did not stop. The crossing took minutes that felt longer, and in those minutes you understood something about the plateau that you had only sensed before: that the vastness is not empty, that the quiet is not peace, that the motion you cannot see is as real as the motion you can. The hunter moved through its territory, and the territory was the corridor, and the corridor was the space between places, and the space between places was what the plateau mostly was.

You walked on. The cluster grew brighter as you approached. Behind you the corridor was still silent, and would remain so until the cold deepened or the warm season came or the hunter decided that the territory needed its presence made audible again. The plateau held you and the hunter and the cluster and the silence, and the holding was not cruel and was not kind and was not anything you had a name for yet. The cold was always the same temperature. You kept walking.
