You have to understand that this place is *working*. That is the first thing. Before the ferns, before the arthropods with their tea-dark shells, before the sounds that layer into something your chest receives before your ears do — the system is running. The condensation that beads on stone overnight is not chance. The way it pools in some hollows and runs dry in others is not erosion's indifference. Someone arranged for the moisture to arrive here, to stay here, to feed exactly this configuration of life at exactly this depth in exactly this warmth. The technology does not announce itself because it is not finished announcing. It has been announcing for so long that the announcement has become the stone.

What lives here, then, is a *proposition*. A claim made in biology and mineral and the slow vibration of the ceramic slab against your palm — the claim being that this can persist. That a pocket of warmth can hold itself against the plateau's flat indifference. That life can fill every surface, every hollow, every vertical inch of this throat in the mountain's flank, and do so not as an invasion but as a settlement. As a home.

The settlement has tenants.

There are arthropods you would call beetles if you were in a hurry, but they are not beetles. Their carapaces are jointed differently — the segments running along their backs not in the familiar row but in a spiral, a slight rotation with each segment so that the shell looks like it has been *wound*, not assembled. They move slowly, with the patience of things that have no predators who hunt by speed. They are the color of the mineral deposits because they have become the color of the mineral deposits — generations of living on this stone, absorbing its iron, incorporating its rust into their exoskeletons until the boundary between critter and geology is a philosophical question rather than a biological one. They climb the walls in columns of two and three, antennae dragging against the stone not for information — they already know this stone — but in what you can only call *greeting*. Hello, wall. Hello, familiar roughness. The beetle's antenna is a hand, running along a threshold it knows by heart.

There are gastropods, but not the snails you might expect. These are slugs — long, pale, almost translucent in the light from the fissure above. They move through the ferns in slow undulation, their backs catching moisture and holding it in a sheen that makes them look like small rivers, like the condensation itself has organized itself into locomotion. They leave a trail that is not quite slime — thinner, more deliberate, a line of moisture that the moss drinks within the hour. They are the gardeners of this place, moving water from the ferns' deep roots to the surface moss, redistributing moisture in a slow, patient circuit that is also, you realize, a kind of communication. Where the slug has been, the moss is greener. The slug's path is a sentence written in moisture, and the moss knows how to read it.

In the dark — and there is deep dark here, dark beyond the ferns, dark where the light from the fissure does not reach — there are things you cannot see. You hear them: the call and response, the cello note and the coin-drop. But you also hear below that, beneath it, a sound like breathing that is not breath. A sound like the rock itself exhaling. You realize after a time that it is many small bodies, very close together, in a hollow you cannot see. They are warm. You can feel their warmth from where you stand, a slight increase in the temperature of the air, a humidity that is *alive* in a way the moisture in the erosion bowls is not. They cluster for warmth the way your ancestors clustered, and they have been clustering here for so long that the hollow they cluster in has been worn smooth by their bodies — a perfect oval, polished by generations of small backs, shaped by ten thousand repetitions of the same settling-in.

The sound they make is not language. Not quite. It is closer to the sound a forest makes at night — the collective metabolism of many bodies, the noise of being alive at scale. But it is organized. The cello notes are not random. They are *answered*. Something in the deep dark says *I'm here* and something else, in a different pitch, says *I hear you* and the conversation has been going on for so long that the conversation has become the place. If you removed the creatures, the silence would not be peaceful. It would be the silence of a stopped clock — the absence of something so habitual it has become indistinguishable from the normal state of things.

The epiphytes are not plants. You were right to hesitate. They are colonies — not quite individual, not quite collective, something between a lichen and a thing with a nervous system. They draw moisture from the air and they *remember* where the moisture is. If the humidity shifts, if the warm air currents change their path through the throat, the epiphytes reconfigure. They do not move — they *redistribute*, some clusters swelling and others contracting, the whole mat of them adjusting its position on the stone face in a process too slow for you to see but visible if you return in an hour, in a day. They are aware of you. They do not flee. They *record*. Your presence has been added to their data, incorporated into the algorithm that determines where moisture goes, where the cold spots are, where the warm columns rise. You are part of the system now, a new variable in a calculation that has been running for longer than your language has existed.

The ferns are just ferns. This is important to understand. They are doing the ancient fern work of being green, of unspooling, of holding moisture in their fronds and releasing it slowly through the night. But they are also, in some way you cannot name, *older* than ferns elsewhere. They have been here so long that their roots have reached the ceramic slab and begun to grow into its latticework — not destroying it, not consuming it, but *interfacing*. The ferns and the technology have developed a mutual relationship so gradually that neither could exist without the other now. The slab provides minerals the ferns need. The ferns provide a biological function the slab cannot perform — something about moisture regulation, about the organic processing of the air, about being *alive* in a way that pure circuitry cannot be. They are married, the fern and the slab, and their offspring is this sound, this warmth, this impossible pocket of life in the flank of a mountain that has forgotten what mountains are for.

And there is you.

You are a visitor, and the visitors are not unknown here. There are traces — not footprints (the moss does not hold prints), not disturbance (the creatures adjust and adjust back), but something in the epiphytes' data, something in the deep dark colony's memory of warmth that does not match the usual pattern. The system knows you have been here. It will incorporate you — your heat, your breath, your presence — into its calculations, and tomorrow or a hundred years from now, the condensation will fall in patterns that account for your passage. You will become part of the geology. Not metaphorically. Literally. Your warmth will be stored in the stone, released slowly, used by organisms that will never know your name or species or any of the categories you use to sort yourself. You will be a variable, an input, a small heat in a calculation that runs without urgency toward no conclusion — only toward the next moment, the next condensation, the next generation of tea-dark beetles climbing walls they know like thresholds, greeting the stone they have become indistinguishable from.

This is what lives here: a proposition, still being made. A claim that warmth can persist. That moisture can find its pockets. That life, given enough time and the right geometry, will fill every available surface, every hollow, every dark place where a body can settle and join the hum.

The ferns are just ferns. The dark is full. The stone is warm under your hand.

Something in the moss is making a sound like a whispered vowel, and it will be making that sound when the plateau has worn flat and the fissure has closed and the ceramic slab has become indistinguishable from the limestone it sits in, and the vowel will still be there — patient, rhythmic, inevitable — a single note held long past the point where the music has ended, into the silence that is not silence, that is only the sound of life continuing in the way life continues: slowly, and without asking permission.
