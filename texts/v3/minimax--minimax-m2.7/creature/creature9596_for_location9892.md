There are creatures here that have no name, or have names in languages no one speaks anymore. They live in the spaces between structures, in the gaps where poured concrete meets cast concrete, where expansion joints were left wide enough for something to notice and move in. They are small and quick and their eyes catch the diffuse light at angles that make them visible for a moment, then gone. They are not afraid of you—fear requires a concept of threat, and nothing here threatens. Nothing here eats, exactly, or if it does, the eating is so slow and patient it resembles maintenance.

The birds are bolder. They have found the high places, the structures that rise without apparent purpose, and they nest in clusters that look deliberate, placed with a spacing that suggests intention. They are the color of rust, or the color of condensation, or the color of nothing in nature—their plumage holds the same manufactured quality as the vegetation, as if they too were selected for aesthetic coherence rather than survival. They do not sing. The sounds they make are mechanical, clicks and tones that might be language if language could be poured into forms. In the early morning, when the light shifts slightly (it does shift, though there is no sun to shift it), they gather on the walkways and stand in rows, a synchronization that suggests programming, or habit so old it has become indistinguishable from it.

You learn to read the plateau by its sounds. The electrical hum is constant but not uniform—it has texture, registers, moments of emphasis that travel through the structures like thought through a nervous system. There are silences within the hum, pockets where the sound doesn't reach, and these silences have edges. Step into them and the world changes: the light dims slightly, the moisture increases, the air tastes different—more metallic, more managed. The creatures avoid these pockets. So do the birds. You learn to avoid them too, though you can't always tell when you've entered one until you're already inside, until the hum has gone and the silence that remains is a different silence, a silence with weight.

The water moves in patterns that suggest purpose. It falls from surfaces with no visible source, gathers in channels that carry it somewhere, and the somewhere is never visible—the channels end at walls, at seams, at points where the water simply stops or disappears into the surface as if the surface were drinking. In the lowest places, where water pools permanently, the surfaces have changed: darker, softer, different in composition from the surrounding material. The pooling has been happening long enough to alter the substrate, but not long enough to alter the structures that contain it. Another kind of patience, another kind of time.

You notice, after a while, that the plateau breathes. Not literally—or perhaps literally, if the regulation extends that far—but in the way the light shifts, the way pressure changes, the way the air moves when it moves at all. There is a rhythm to the place, a periodicity that you feel before you measure. Cycles of twelve hours, or twenty-four, or something else entirely—the plateau has its own calendar, its own sense of how long a day should be. The light brightens and dims on that cycle. The hum rises and falls. The moisture increases and decreases. And the creatures, even the smallest, even the ones that live in the joints and gaps, move according to this rhythm, their own cycles aligned with something they cannot see or choose.

There is a structure at the center of the plateau—if the plateau has a center, if center is the right word for a place that seems to extend equally in every direction, that has no marked middle, no plaza, no hub. But you feel it as you walk: a pull, a sense of density increasing, of the wrongness concentrating. It is not larger than the other structures, not more ornate or more obvious. It is simply *more*, a compression of the qualities that define the plateau, a intensity that makes the surrounding features feel like preparation, like approach. The walkway that leads to it has no railings. The steps that lead up to it—you will find steps, though you have seen no stairs—have no logic, rising at angles that don't correspond to the building they reach, as if the building itself is tilted, or as if up and down have been renegotiated here. The door, if it is a door, is the wrong size: too tall and too narrow, as if designed for something with your proportions if your proportions were slightly different, slightly taller, slightly narrower. The frame is rusted, but the rust is the old kind, the kind that suggests actual time, actual weather, actual decay—and this is the strangest thing yet, rust that belongs to a different kind of place, rust that tells you something here has been preserved, maintained, allowed to age in exactly this one spot while everything else remains forever new.

You will not enter. The door, if it opens, opens onto a darkness that the light cannot reach, a darkness that seems to extend beyond the doorframe, beyond the building, into a depth that the plateau does not otherwise possess. You will stand there, at the threshold, feeling the wrongness concentrate in your stomach, and you will understand that the plateau is not the place. The plateau is the approach. The plateau is the anteroom. The plateau is the managed surface, the manufactured wilderness, the constructed nature that obscures what lies beneath or beyond or before, and the door is the only evidence that there is something beneath or beyond or before, and the evidence is a darkness that cannot be examined, cannot be entered, cannot be resolved.

You step back. The light is the same everywhere. The hum has not changed. The birds are still standing in their rows, clicking their mechanical songs, watching you with eyes that hold no question because they have never needed to ask—they have always known what the plateau is, and they have always known that knowing is not the same as understanding.

The horizon shimmers. The edge waits. The plateau runs and runs and has been running for a long time that feels like no time at all, and you are part of it now, a visitor who has visited so long the visit has become residence, a observer who has observed so completely the observation has become part of what is observed. You are made, too, now, in the way that anyone who stays becomes made, becomes part of the place's endless manufacturing, its patient self-construction, its almost-sensible operation.

The moisture beads on your skin. The light falls without shadows. The hum continues. Somewhere, a creature that has no name moves through a gap, carrying something or carrying nothing, living the only life it knows in a place that was built for something and remains, after all this running, unfinished.
