# The Pika

The pika does not hurry, except when it must. In the still moments between gusts, it emerges from the rock crevice where the wind cannot reach, sits on the stone with its rounded ears flush against its head, and surveys the plateau with eyes that have no white — only the dark wet shine of something watching. It is the size of a man's fist, compact and dense, its fur the color of dried grass or the lichen it lives among, so that when it freezes on the stone it becomes difficult to distinguish from the aggregate of the place itself. This is not camouflage in the deliberate sense; it is simply that the pika has become a variation on a theme, one more texture among textures, its small body the latest expression of a surface that has been forming since before the pika existed as a species.

It moves in bursts. When the wind drops — when the roar thins to a whistle, then a hush, then a silence so total it seems to press against the ears — the pika springs into motion. It runs along the base of the rocks, low to the ground where the air is slower, where the gusts are broken by the formations that have shaped themselves against millennia of current. It does not run the way a dog runs, or even the way a rabbit runs; it is more like a small engine, a compact turbine of muscle and bone, its legs a blur that covers the stone in a sequence of rapid hops. It is going somewhere specific. It has a purpose that is not play.

The purpose is the haypile.

Every pika maintains one. It is the architecture of its life — a midden of gathered vegetation accumulated against the winter, when the plateau will be buried in ice and the wind will howl in a register that is not the steady roar but something more desperate, more alive with cold. The pika cannot hibernate. Its body cannot store enough fat to survive the months in torpor, and so it does what no other creature here does: it plans. It cuts the sparse grass with its teeth, the tufts that have found purchase in the cracks, the dried stalks of plants that have already given what life they had, and it carries them back to the nest in its mouth, one or two at a time, adding them to the pile, mixing them with whatever the wind has brought down from higher up — dried flowers, shed feathers, fragments of matter that have no use except as volume, except as insulation, except as the material of survival.

The haypile grows slowly. This is understood. The pika does not seem to know that it is building toward something; it only knows the present act, the cutting, the carrying, the adding. It has been doing this since the first warm days of what the calendar calls summer, and it will continue until the first frost silvers the stones, until the pools freeze over and the lizards are gone and the insects are only memories in the egg cases that will not hatch until the warmth returns. When the winter comes, the pika will emerge from its burrow — not into the wind, but into the snow that will seal the opening, into a darkness that will be warmer than the air above — and it will eat from the haypile. It will eat and it will wait and it will survive, or it will not, and the distinction between the two outcomes is measured in the quality of the pile, in the density of its construction, in the care with which it was assembled in the months when assembly was possible.

There is violence in this place, but it is not obvious. The hawk circles above, riding the current with its wings set in the rigid V of patience, and when a pika moves between the rocks in a moment of inattention — when a gust has just passed and the pika has judged the silence long enough — the hawk folds its wings and drops. The strike is a sound you do not hear until it is over, a rush and a impact, and then the hawk rises with something small and struggling in its talons, carrying it away to the ledge where it will eat without competition, where the wind will not disturb its meal. The pika population does not grow. It holds steady, a balance maintained by the hawks, the cold, the accidents of terrain. Some years there are more; some years there are fewer. The plateau does not record this. It only continues.

And the pikas continue too, in their small lives measured by the haypile, by the crevice, by the moments between gusts when movement is possible. They call to each other — a sharp, staccato sound, a note that is almost a bark, almost a chirp, a sound so high and brief that the wind swallows most of it before it can travel far. The calls serve different purposes: warning, greeting, territory. A pika owns the rocks it occupies, owns the path between its nest and its feeding grounds, and it will defend this territory not with violence but with presence, with the sound of its voice in the spaces where sound can carry. When another pika approaches too close, the owner of the territory sits on the highest stone it can find, the stone that the wind reaches first, and it calls. The call says: this is mine. The call says: go. Often the intruder goes. Sometimes it does not, and then there is a brief confrontation — a chase, a flash of small bodies moving through the rocks — and then the order is reestablished, the boundaries redrawn in the language of the place, which is not language at all but motion and sound and the accumulation of small decisions made in the wind.

You can watch them for hours, if you are willing to be still. The stillness is the condition. The pikas have learned to read movement the way they read the wind — as a signal, as a threat, as a fact that demands response. When you sit motionless on a stone at the edge of a pika territory, they will eventually decide you are not movement but formation, not predator but landscape, a new shape in the arrangement of the world. They will go about their business. They will cut the grass and carry it and call to each other and sit on the stones in the brief stills, and you will be there, watching, a presence that does not belong but is tolerated, that does not fit but is permitted by the patience of creatures who have no energy to waste on anything that is not immediate.

This is what the plateau teaches, if you let it. The immediate is all there is. The pika lives in a window so narrow that there is no room for tomorrow except in the haypile, no room for yesterday except in the worn path between the rocks. It is a creature of the present tense, a small engine running on the fuel of now, and its success is measured not in what it accumulates but in what it remains: alive, present, able to emerge again when the wind drops and the silence comes and the stone is still warm from the sun that does not warm. The pika has found the thin margin where life is possible, and it has claimed it, and it holds it the way the plateau holds still — not by force, but by persistence, by the simple refusal to be anything other than what it is.
