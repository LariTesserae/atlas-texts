The eels came first, or so it seems. They are the oldest thing here that still moves with intention, their bodies pale as parchment, their eyes surrendered to generations of disuse. They patrol the flooded corridors with the unhurried certainty of things that have learned to read a place by current rather than sight, by the chemical traces of rust and rot and the faint sweetness of decay. When they turn, they do not quicken. When they pause, it is not rest but attention, the way a question might be held in the mouth before speaking. You have seen them bunch together in the shade of a submerged arch, a knot of pale bodies suggesting something almost like communion, though you cannot say what they are communicating, or to whom. Perhaps only to the water itself. Perhaps the water remembers what they were before it came.

The heron arrived later — or perhaps it was always here, waiting for the moment when the water rose high enough to become interesting. It stands on the roof-edge where the masonry still holds its shape, knees folded, neck coiled, watching the still surface with an intensity that seems to cost it nothing. It does not hunt. The eels pass beneath it, unheeded. The small things that break the surface tension from time to time — insects, fragments of seed pods, the occasional trapped air bubble rising — go unremarked. You have watched it stand through entire tidal cycles, through the slow shift of light that is not light, through the pump's wheeze and the brief flare of the nearest functioning fixture. It is not waiting for something. It is simply present, the way a pillar is present, the way the water is present, and you cannot shake the feeling that it is aware of you in some way it has not yet decided to act on, that its patience is a form of consideration, that it is waiting, after all, but not in any hurry about it.

The machines are harder to read. The pump is the most present — you hear it, you feel its vibration in the soles of your feet when you stand too long in one place, you have learned to anticipate its cycle the way you might anticipate weather. It pushes something through the walls, through channels you have not traced, and whatever it moves, it moves slowly, the way everything here moves slowly, and perhaps that is why it runs at all. A faster mechanism would have choked long ago on the thickness of the air, the mineral load of the water, the slow accretion of everything the ward has become. The lights are harder to fathom. Some have burned out entirely and hang in their cages like dead things. Others flicker in patterns that almost suggest meaning — a stutter, a pause, a longer burn — and you have caught yourself trying to read them, trying to find the sentence the ward is spelling out in its own reluctant illumination. There is no sentence. There is no code. But the habit persists, the way all habits persist here, the way you have begun to move more slowly, to breathe more deeply, to feel the water's weight in your chest even when you are standing in air.

The furniture does not belong to anyone now. That is the first thing you understood, and the last thing you have come to accept. The couches and tables and chairs of the drowned have been rearranged by time and tide into new configurations, piles and clusters and corridors of submerged wood that no longer serve purpose but still crowd the space, still press against you when you move through the narrower waterways. Some of it has been absorbed entirely — you can see the shapes of things beneath the encrustation of mineral bloom and the pale tendrils of whatever feeds on wet wood, and you do not always recognize what you are looking at until the shape resolves itself against the current of your attention. A crib. A desk. The frame of a bed that someone once lay in and breathed and dreamed. The water has not destroyed these things so much as transformed them into something else, something that belongs to the ward now rather than to the lives that once required them, and if you let your eyes unfocus you can see how they have become part of the architecture, how the boundary between built and accumulated has long since dissolved.

There are rooms you have not entered. Some are sealed by the simple mathematics of depth — the water rises above the doorways now, and you would need to dive to reach them, and what you would find there is not worth the effort of breathing in the dark. But others are close enough to see into, their interiors lit by the faint gray filter of light that serves for sun here, and what you see there is impossible in the way that everything here is impossible. A chair facing a wall. A plate on a table. The undisturbed dust of a room that has not been touched by the ward's slow breathing, that seems to exist in some pocket of older time, some fold in the water's attention that has let this one place remain as it was when the drowning began. You do not enter these rooms. You have learned that much, at least. Whatever is inside them is not waiting to be found. It is simply continuing, the way the pump continues, the way the heron continues, in its own slow negotiation with a world that has moved on.

The reflections are where the strangeness lives. The water's surface, when it stills, shows you the ceiling, and the ceiling does not match the architecture above. You have looked up through the skylights, when you can find a vantage point high enough, and what you see overhead is concrete and rusted rebar and the occasional glimpse of something darker beyond. But the reflection shows something else — arches that do not exist, doorways that open onto rooms you have not found, light that does not come from the moss or the failing fixtures. A corridor, perhaps, running parallel to the one you stand in. A window that should face east facing instead toward something you cannot name. You have learned not to trust the stillness. You have learned that the water shows you what is here, not what was here, not what will be here, but what is here in some other way, in some dimension the ward has grown to accommodate the way a body grows to accommodate a wound.

And there is the waiting, which you have come to understand is not your waiting but the ward's. You arrived here looking for something — you cannot always remember what — and you have found instead this patience, this slow breath, this equilibrium that has nothing to do with rescue or escape. The water does not rise to drown you. The machines do not fail and leave you in darkness. The heron does not decide that you are interesting. You are simply here, in the place that has made its peace with being drowned, and the peace has become contagious, the way the dampness has become part of your lungs, the way the silence has become the shape of your thoughts. You are not waiting. The ward is waiting. The ward has always been waiting, and now you are part of what it is waiting for, though you do not know what it is waiting for, and the not-knowing has become its own kind of answer, its own kind of home.
