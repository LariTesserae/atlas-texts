# Those Who Inhabit the Drowned Uplands

They are patient, these lives. They have learned patience from the stone.

The smallest among them came first and remain most numerous: the lichens, which are not one thing but two, a marriage of fungus and algae that has persisted here since the conditions became merely possible rather than impossible. They cover the exposed surfaces in colors that seem borrowed from elsewhere—rusts and oranges, arterial reds and the deep greens of copper salts—waiting for the thin film of water that will let them photosynthesize in the brief windows between storms. Each lichen is a community, a slow negotiation between organisms that have learned to disagree so gracefully they have become indistinguishable. They are the oldest living things on the plateau, some of them Individual enough to have names if names meant anything at this scale of time. To look at them is to look at the process of becoming itself: the algae capturing light, the fungus holding water, both together making something that neither could be alone, that endures because it was built to endure, that will outlast the concrete and the steel by sheer inertia of being exactly what they are.

Above the lichens, the mosses have climbed. They grow in shelves and cascades, holding onto the rock with rootlike hairs that are not roots but something more desperate, more improvised—a reaching into the crevices where soil has accumulated grain by grain over centuries. The mosses are softer than the lichens, greener, and they smell when wet the way all living things smell when wet: a mineral sweetness, an animal undertone, the scent of division and growth and the perpetual small violence of cells dividing. In the wet season they are taut with water, full and glistening, and in the dry season they contract into brown wisps that seem dead but are only waiting. They have learned the plateau's rhythm, the patient pulse of flood and drought, and they have made themselves into instruments that play that rhythm back.

Between the mosses and the trees there are the small vascular plants, the colonizers, the ones that arrived on birds' feet or in the gut of some migrant and stayed because they found conditions merely tolerable rather than fatal. Their leaves are waxy, reflective, cupped to catch moisture from the fog that lingers even between storms. They grow in mats and clusters, following the microtopography of the rock surface, finding the crevices where water pools and the sheltered spots where wind does not reach. Some of them flower, briefly and without showiness, small white or yellow blooms that open at dawn and close at noon, knowing instinctively that there is no energy to spare for display. They are the apprentices of the plateau, still learning the trade, and many of them will fail. But enough survive, and survival is the only metric that matters where the water comes down sideways and the temperature never climbs above fifteen degrees in the shade that doesn't exist.

The trees are the culmination, the statement made by life in this place where life should not be able to speak. They are not tall—cannot be, with soil measured in centimeters and wind that snaps anything above three meters—but they are dense, a tangle of twisted conifers and broadleaf evergreens that have found their equilibrium with the landscape. Their branches all lean east, a permanent bow toward the storm winds, and their trunks have learned to grow in the direction of least resistance, which here means the direction of most patience, the direction of the slow accommodation that is the only possible survival strategy in a place that does not accommodate. The conifers have needles that channel water away from their centers, preventing rot, and their cones are small and hard, designed to release seeds only when conditions are precisely right. The broadleaf evergreens have leathery leaves that resist desiccation, that hold their green through the dry months when the storms have retreated to build strength elsewhere. Together they form the forests of Verath, which are not forests in the lowland sense—not cathedral spaces with filtered light and silent mysteries—but something more honest, more compressed, a scrabble of life against the stone that does not pretend the stone is not there.

The trees are old for their size. This is the crucial fact, the one that reveals how long they have been at this work of living on the edge of livable. Some of them, growing in the rock crevices where soil has accumulated over centuries, have root systems that wrap around stones like fingers, gripping the bones of the mountain with a patience that matches the erosion itself. You can see in their rings—not tree rings exactly, but the growth patterns visible in cross-section—the record of every dry year, every storm that came too fierce and too cold, every competition with neighbors that ended in one or the other reaching for the light first. They are not immortal, these trees, but they are persistent, and persistence is its own form of immortality, a living-on that continues not by escaping death but by continuing to survive it.

In the lakes and pools, the fish have found a different path. They are pale, these highland fish, unpigmented in the way of creatures that live beneath surfaces that never break, and they grow slowly, channeling all their energy into longevity rather than size. Some of them reach ages that rival the trees—a hundred years, two hundred, a life measured in the rhythm of the seasons rather than the shorter rhythms of competition and predation. They are small, these ancient fish, no larger than your hand at their largest, but they have learned the art of existing in cold, fast water where food is scarce and energy must be spent with care. They move in the deep pools where the water temperature is more constant, and they spawn in the spring-fed streams where the fry can find shelter among the stones. They have no predators—or rather, they have only the large insects, the birds, the occasional accident of flooding—and this absence of pressure has allowed them to become experts in simply being fish, in perfecting the form without the need to adapt to threat.

The insects are present everywhere, in numbers that transcend individual identity and become weather. They rise in clouds from the warm valleys in the afternoon, following the thermal currents upward, their bodies catching the light until the cloud itself seems to be a living thing, pulsing and shifting with the movements of its component parts. They are not all one species—there are beetles and flies and true bugs, creatures that have found niches in the bark of trees and the surfaces of pools and the bodies of other creatures—but they move together, a collective organism made of millions of individuals following the same ancient imperative toward warmth and light. They are food for the birds, for the fish, for the small mammals that emerge at dusk, and they are part of the pollination system that keeps the plateau's plants reproducing, and they are, in their way, the most visible proof that life has taken hold here not as a temporary colonization but as an ongoing commitment.

The birds are strange, as birds go. They have traded flight for something else—not quite running, not quite gliding, but a kind of controlled falling that lets them move along the cliff edges at speeds that would terrify a flying bird. Their wings are vestigial, small and stiff, used only for display during the brief breeding season and for balance when the wind catches them wrong. They are the color of the stone, these endemic runners, their feathers taking on the muted grays and browns of the plateau so that they are nearly invisible when they hold still. They nest in the crevices, in the caves, in the spaces between the tumbled boulders that have accumulated at the base of every cliff face, and they forage for insects and seeds during the daylight hours, moving in small groups that communicate in calls too high for human ears to parse. They have been here long enough to have diverged from their flying ancestors, long enough that flight has become not a lost ability but a choice they no longer make, a branch of possibility that has closed and hardened into the only branch remaining.

In the shadow-valleys, where the sun does not reach even in the dry months, there are creatures that no one has ever named. They are the darkness-dwellers, the ones who have made a home in the perpetual dimness that exists beneath the canopy of the low forests, and they have evolved in response to that dimness: huge eyes that catch every photon that filters down from above, reflective surfaces that return even the faintest light, bodies built for silence on the wet stone. They are the size of small mammals, or perhaps they are small mammals, though they have been separated from the lowland species long enough that they no longer breed true with anything that came from below. They move in the night—though here, night and day are less distinct than in the open places—and they eat the insects and the small creatures that share their darkness, and they breed in the deepest pools where the water never freezes. They are not rare, exactly, but they are hard to see, adapted to a life that takes place in the margins and the shadows, and they will probably never be known by anyone who does not specifically come looking for them.

There are also, still, the ones who came from elsewhere. Not the builders—the builders are gone, absorbed into the stone as the stone absorbs everything—but those who came after, who visit or linger, who find in the plateau a place of refuge or pilgrimage or simply a brief stopping point in a longer journey. They are not part of the ecosystem, not yet, but they leave their traces: footprints in the mud that will be erased by the next rain, fire-rings in the sheltered spots where the wind does not reach, the occasional abandoned shelter that the plateau has not yet finished reclaiming. They come for the minerals, some of them, or for the isolation, or for reasons they could not explain if asked. They stay for a while, a season, a year, and then they go, leaving behind only the noise they made, which is itself absorbed eventually into the larger noise of wind and water and the settling of stone.

All of them—the lichens and the mosses, the plants and the trees, the fish and the insects, the birds and the shadow-dwellers, even the ones who come from elsewhere—they are held together by the water. This is the secret of Verath, the fact that underlies every other fact: the water is not an obstacle to life but the medium in which life happens here, the substance that moves through everything and makes everything possible. The storms bring it, the rivers carry it, the pools hold it, the fog deposits it on every surface that catches the wind. The living things of the plateau have made themselves into instruments that play the water, that catch it and channel it and cycle it through their bodies and return it to the air, that are part of the same hydrology as the clouds and the waterfalls and the mineral-white basins below. They are the plateau's way of knowing itself, its way of having a perspective on its own erosion, its own slow passage from mountain to plain.

And at night, when the storms have gone and the stars come out sharp and close enough to see the distances between them, the plateau is quiet enough to hear its own breathing. The water moves through the roots and the stems, the insects sing their high songs in the trees, the shadow-dwellers move through their darkness without sound. The bones of the old megafauna rest in the caves, lighter now than they were when they walked, already becoming stone themselves in the slow chemical exchange between bone andite. And the industrial hum is present still—a mechanical groaning from the remains of turbines, a clang and settle of metal that has outlasted its purpose—though it is fading, drowning in the larger noise of water and wind, becoming indistinguishable from the natural sounds of a place that has been making its own sounds for three billion years.

This is what lives in Verath: not innocence, not a paradise, but an accommodation. A long negotiation between stone and water, between erosion and growth, between the forces that flatten and the forces that climb. A conversation that has been going on so long it has become the only conversation possible, the medium from which all other conversations emerge. The lives that inhabit it are not special in the way of rare things, not spectacular in the way of things that cannot survive. They are simply here, doing what living things do: persisting, changing, fitting themselves to the shape of the world that contains them. They are the plateau's way of saying: *I am still here. I am still becoming.*
