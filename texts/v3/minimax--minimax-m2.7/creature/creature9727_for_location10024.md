# What Lives on the Iron Plateau

To speak of life on the plateau is to speak of a word that means something different here than it means anywhere else. On worlds where water pools in rivers and collects in clouds, life divides itself into eaters and eaten, hunters and hunted, a restless economy of consumption that moves fast because water moves fast—the metabolic urgency of things that must drink or die. But the plateau has no such economy. Here, life does not eat. It *weathers*. It accrues. It settles into the slow transformations that the stone itself is undergoing and rides those transformations like a current, transforming in turn.

This is difficult to hold in the mind at first—the idea of a creature that is not separate from its environment but continuous with it, that does not take from the world but passes through it, leaving behind the way a river leaves behind silt. The first settlers could not see it, many of them. Their eyes were tuned to the vocabulary of carbon-based motion: the flutter of wings, the dart of limbs, the snap of jaws. They looked for movement in the wrong timescale and the wrong register, and finding none, called the plateau lifeless. But the plateau was never lifeless. It was only living in a way that their instruments were not yet calibrated to read.

---

The most visible of the plateau's inhabitants are the drifters—crystalline formations that rise on the thermal currents like inverted snowflakes, their geometries too precise for randomness, too irregular for manufacture. They emerge from the stone in clusters during the deep still hours before dawn, when the geothermal pulse runs strongest through the deep fissures, and by midmorning the sky above the ridgelines will be populated with them: translucent bodies ranging from the size of a clenched fist to the span of both arms, tumbling slowly in the thin air, their facets catching what light the distant sun provides and refracting it into faint prismatic trails. They do not fly, not exactly. They drift, caught in the upward-moving columns of warm air that rise from the vents, their crystalline structure light enough to be borne aloft but heavy enough to sink when the currents shift. The motion is languid, almost meditative. They seem to be waiting for something, though no one has determined what.

Each drifter is a single organism, or perhaps a single generation—the distinction blurs. Their crystalline bodies are grown in layers, each season's growth laid down over the last, the way a tree adds rings. The oldest drifters are dense with these layers, their structures darkened by the accumulation of deep-time residue, and when at last they become too heavy for the thermals to bear, they sink back toward the stone from which they rose. They do not die, exactly. They return. The residue they carry—the mineral fragments and chemical concentrates they have gathered across their drift—is incorporated into the rock, and in time, in places where the thermal gradients align just so, new drifters will emerge from the same stone. The settlements noticed this first: that certain formations grew larger after a drifter-cluster died nearby, and that the smelters could detect higher concentrations of rare earths in the ore taken from those formations. The drifters were not leaving waste. They were leaving *progress*. The plateau was using them the way a body uses its own dying cells—recycling, concentrating, moving materials from one place to another across distances no geological process could manage alone.

---

More mysterious, though less visible, are the fault-line colonies. These do not drift; they are rooted—or something like rooted—to the deeper structures of the plateau. They manifest as unfoldings along fissures and stress lines: branching structures that emerge from cracks in the rock, spreading outward in patterns that have the regularity of growth but the complexity of thought. From a distance, they look almost like circuits—geometric lines and nodes, connections that seem to follow logic. Up close, the regularity breaks down, replaced by a fractal irregularity that suggests neither intention nor accident. The colonies are colonial in the same sense as corals: each node is an individual, but the pattern is the organism, or perhaps the individual is the organism and the pattern is the society, and the distinction matters less than it would on a water-world.

The fault-line colonists move, but on a timescale that makes the drifters seem hurried. A human observer stationed at a colony for a full day might notice no change at all. But a colony that was mapped in the first years of settlement and examined again a decade later will have shifted—extended, retracted, reformed along different vectors, as if responding to some stimulus that operates on geological patience. They consume the electrical gradients that run through the plateau's deeper stone, drawing current through mineral structures that function as both nervous system and circulatory system. They do not store what they take. They burn it, metabolizing charge the way a breathing thing metabolizes air, and the waste heat they release is detectable by the settlement's monitoring equipment as faint thermal blooms along the fault lines. The colonists are, in their way, a kind of geothermal engine—converting deep heat into localized warmth, moving energy from the planet's interior toward its surface, participating in the great slow respiration of the world itself.

Their secretions are useful to the settlements, though the settlements do not fully understand what they are. The residue that accumulates around established colonies is high in metallic compounds that the industrial processes can refine: not ore in the traditional sense, but something more refined already, something that the colonists have already concentrated from the diffuse stone. Mining here does not mean digging for metal-bearing rock; it means cultivating the zones around fault-line colonies, encouraging their growth in patterns that will yield predictable outputs. The settlements have learned, through generations of trial and error, which formations to protect and which to harvest, which colonies respond to intervention and which resent it. The relationship is not farming—the colonists are not domesticated, do not obey, cannot be directed—but it is not unrelated to farming either. It is something older, something that predates the agricultural paradigm: a mutualism that neither party fully comprehends but both have survived.

---

The surface dwellers are harder to categorize. They are not rooted like the colonists, not airborne like the drifters. They move, but they move across the stone itself, leaving tracks that the wind erases in hours or in days, depending on the formation. Some are slow and solitary, broad flat shapes that drift across the plateau like shadows, their bodies composed of something that is not quite mineral and not quite metal—flexible, layered, capable of conforming to the stone's contours as they slide. Others are smaller, faster, moving in brief sprints across the surface before vanishing into the fissures. The settlements call them skimmers, though they do not skim anything; they are not feeding, not fleeing. They are simply *occupying*, moving through the space the way a thought moves through a mind, present and then elsewhere.

The skimmers are the most animal-like of the plateau's inhabitants—the closest to the behaviors that human observers can read as familiar. They have territories. They respond to intrusion, drawing back, freezing, resuming motion when the threat passes. They do not communicate, as far as anyone can determine, but they coordinate: a skimmer under observation will alter its path to avoid a settlement worker, and if the worker moves, the skimmer adjusts again, as if reading intent rather than motion. Whether this represents cognition or something more primitive—a sensitivity to electromagnetic fields, perhaps, or to the subtle vibrations that the settlements' machinery produces—the settlers have not been able to determine. The skimmers do not submit to study. They do not come when called, do not leave bodies that can be dissected, do not leave traces that survive the plateau's slow erasure. They are here, and then they are not, and the only evidence of their presence is the brief dark track across the stone and the sensation, if one is standing in the right place at the right moment, of something noticing you in return.

---

Below the plateau's edge, where the canyon systems drop toward depths that no settlement has fully mapped, the life changes. The air thickens, the temperature rises, and the geothermal gradients become more complex, more violent, more alive. Here, the fauna does not merely tolerate the conditions; it *requires* them. The forms that move through the canyons are not crystalline or mineral in any way that the eye can recognize. They are more like patterns of density—gradients made visible, pockets of organization that condense out of the heavier air and disperse again when the conditions shift. The settlement workers who descend into the canyons for maintenance and extraction report seeing things that move in clouds and spirals: formations that hold their shape for minutes at a time and then scatter, reforming elsewhere, as if the space itself were unstable and the life were simply the pattern of its stability.

The canyon-dwellers are not solitary. They gather in the thermal columns that rise from the deepest vents, swirling together in configurations that sometimes look purposeful and sometimes look like nothing at all. The settlement explorers have described them as congregations, as migrations, as storms of some interior weather that operates on principles no one has yet articulated. They consume the thicker atmosphere, metabolizing compounds that the plateau-surface air cannot provide, and they release substances that the settlements have learned to collect: volatile compounds, rare gases, mineral precipitates that form only in the specific conditions of the deep canyons. The settlements depend on these canyons the way a body depends on its liver: to filter, to process, to transform what cannot be processed elsewhere. The canyons are not hospitable. They are barely survivable. But they are necessary, and so the settlements maintain their presence at the rim, watching the depths with instruments that do not see clearly and sending workers down in rotations that maximize exposure without maximizing risk.

---

The plateau does not observe the settlements, exactly. It does not care about them, in the way that caring is an act of attention, a directed thing. But it is aware of them, in the way that a body is aware of an infection: a presence that disrupts, that consumes, that changes the local conditions in ways that the body must accommodate. The settlements draw heat and rare minerals and electrical gradients; they add machinery that vibrates and hums and introduces frequencies that the stone has never encountered; they carve and refine and release waste products that the plateau's own cycles do not recognize. The fault-line colonies near the settlement borders have changed in response, their patterns shifting, their secretions altering. Whether this represents adaptation or something else—resistance, perhaps, or indifference—the settlements cannot say. The colonies continue to grow. The drifters continue to emerge. The skimmers continue to move through spaces that the settlements do not occupy. The plateau continues to breathe its slow century-long breath, and the sounds that carry across it are the sounds of a world that is alive in a way that the word *alive* has never before been asked to mean.

---

What lives on the iron plateau? The question is not simple, because the answer requires a language that does not yet exist—a way of speaking about life that does not assume water, does not assume carbon, does not assume the division between self and world that makes the word *creature* meaningful. Something lives here that is half of the stone and half of something else. Something moves that does not move toward or away from anything. Something grows that does not outgrow its environment because it is its environment, or nearly so, or in some fashion that the distinction is moot. The settlements call it fauna because they have no better word, and perhaps they are right to do so: the Latin root means *the things that live*, and these things live, in their way, more surely than anything the human observers have brought with them. The settlements will pass. The machinery will fail. The foundations will erode, and the carved stone will soften back into the plateau's undulating curves. But the fault-line colonies will remain, and the drifters will continue to rise on the thermal currents, and somewhere in the deep canyons, patterns will continue to condense and disperse and reform, patient as the planet itself, breathing once per century and asking nothing of the silence but that it listen.
