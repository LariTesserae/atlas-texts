The people are difficult to see as individuals.

This is not because they are small or few—the settlement is populous enough—but because they move through a density that refuses the eye's segmentation. They pass through tunnels and between walls and beneath overhangs in a continuous flow that blurs boundaries between person and person, between person and structure. One learns to look for movement rather than form, for the way light catches on damp skin, for the particular rhythm of bare feet on weeping stone.

When they do resolve, briefly, into discrete figures, they are compact: not short so much as *condensed*, as if the pressure of the close-built city has compressed them into efficient shapes. Their skin carries the sheen of constant moisture, darkened where it contacts the weeping rock, softened along the palms and soles where nothing dry has ever worn. Many bear the mineral residue of the water—pale traces along forearms, streaking down calves, faint as old bruises where the seep has run and dried against their bodies. Their hair, when worn long, hangs heavy with damp; their clothes, whatever material they are, cling and settle against them in the warm air.

They move quickly but not carelessly. There is a precision to their navigation—this foot to that stone, this shoulder to that threshold—learned from a childhood spent in passages where the fauna is everywhere and the ground is never stable. They do not step over the creatures so much as flow around them, their feet finding the spaces between the skittering bodies with an unconscious grace that suggests long practice. The insects and mollusks and translucent clustered things move aside for them too, or perhaps they move *with* them; one cannot always say where the human motion ends and the animal motion begins.

They build with their hands, and the evidence is everywhere.

The fitted stone bears the marks of patient selection—each piece chosen and placed, not cut but found, carried, positioned. The timber, where it appears, is dark with moisture and age, fitted into channels in the basalt with wedges of reed and packed mud. The reed itself grows somewhere nearby, perhaps in the low places where the pooling water gathers, and it appears throughout the settlement in woven walls, in door-hangings, in the frames around low thresholds. The people have developed techniques that do not require metal or machinery: lashing, weaving, the compression of damp material against stone until it dries and holds. The structures are not crude but *simple*—this is not a people who lack sophistication but one that has refined its craft to match its materials and its light.

They have also refined their bodies.

Small adaptations mark the generations: the webbing between fingers, perhaps, or the way the nostrils can close against the humid air's constant moisture. Eyes that function well in dim light, having learned to read the shapes that move in the settlement's deep places. Lungs that process air thick with mineral salt, having never known anything else. They are not changed from some ancestral form so much as *confirmed* by their environment, every generation's survivors expressing the same useful traits until these traits simply become the people themselves.

Children move differently.

They are faster, more chaotic, their movements lacking the precise economy of the adults. They run through the tunnels and climb the low walls and splash through the pools where the fauna clusters thickest, and the adults watch them with an expression that is difficult to read—tolerance, perhaps, or recognition, or something older and less named. The children have not yet learned the settlement's rhythms, and so they make their own: bursts of noise, sudden stops, the delighted discovery of some creature's particular behavior. They will learn. The sound of the settlement will teach them.

The old move differently too.

Slower, more deliberate, their bodies carrying the mineral traces deeper in the skin, their joints stiffened by decades of damp and cool and the particular way the weeping rock demands a certain stance. They sit in the low doorways where the humidity gathers thickest, and their hands rest on their knees with a patience that is not resignation but something closer to belonging. They have watched the fauna long enough to know its patterns. They have watched the water long enough to know where it will seep and where it will pool and where the stone will crack next. The old carry this knowledge in their bodies, and it will leave with them.

The sound teaches them.

The fauna is the first teacher. Its calls and scrapes and pulses have patterns if one waits long enough: seasonal variations in the insect populations, the nocturnal shifts of the skittering things, the times when the translucent creatures gather and disperse along the weeping stone. The old have learned to read these patterns, to know from the particular pitch of the hum whether rain approaches from the east or the fauna is breeding in the deep tunnels. They have learned this the way one learns a language, slowly, through immersion, through failure and correction and eventual fluency.

The water is the second teacher. It teaches in a slower register—the way the seep changes with the deep pressure, the way the pools gather and evaporate and gather again, the way the basalt learns to crack along lines of least resistance. The old have watched the stone for decades, have seen new fractures appear and old ones widen, have learned which outcroppings weep and which have dried and which will weep again when the humidity rises. This knowledge is less portable than the fauna's patterns but no less real.

The settlement itself is the third teacher. It teaches in the settling of timber, in the creak of reed, in the way the fitted stone bears weight and shifts and bears weight again. The old know which walls lean and have always leaned, which tunnels flood in the wet season, which thresholds require a particular step. They have felt the settlement's pulse for so long that they have become part of its rhythm, their heartbeats calibrated to the drip and skitter and hum.

And the new ones.

They arrive sometimes, from elsewhere—if elsewhere exists, if the basalt plains give way to some other ground. They come to the settlement's edge and they stop, as you have stopped, held by the sound, and the people watch them from the doorways and the tunnels with an expression that is not quite welcome and not quite wariness. The new ones do not move correctly. They step on the fauna, slip on the weeping stone, freeze at the sound's constancy. They are too loud and too careful and too slow. They have not yet learned that the settlement does not change, that the equilibrium will not shift to accommodate their presence, that they must change instead.

Some stay.

These ones learn slowly, their bodies gradually accepting the moisture, their eyes slowly adjusting to the dim light, their ears finally isolating one sound from the other. They learn to move through the tunnels without thinking, to sleep through the hum, to build with the hand-tools in the way of the people. Their children, if they have children, will be compact and damp-skinned, will flow around the fauna, will carry the mineral traces in their flesh. The settlement will absorb them the way it absorbs everything: steadily, without ceremony, in the way that water seeps up between the basalt plates and remains.

And the fauna.

One must speak of the fauna, though the language falters.

They are many, and they are everywhere, and they are not one thing but many things, and they have no common name because the people have not needed one. They simply *are*, the way the moisture is, the way the sound is. One can speak of types—the insects that skitter in coordinated patterns, their legs catching the light as they move together along the weeping stone; the mollusks that have adapted to damp air, their shells filmed with moisture, their bodies extended in the humidity's embrace; the translucent creatures that cluster in the shade of overhangs, their forms shifting as they pulse with some internal rhythm; the small things that hum and do not move and do not cease. But these categories blur at the edges, and the fauna does not observe them.

The insects are the most visible. They move across the settlement's surfaces in densities that seem impossible for their size—the ground appears to shift with their passage, to become briefly alive and mobile and then still again when they pass. They are not uniform; their configurations vary across the settlement's depth, as if the different humidity levels and light conditions have produced local varieties, distinct populations that do not intermix. Some are dark, almost black, adapted to the deep tunnels. Some are iridescent, catching light where it falls on the weeping stone. Some are small enough to slip between the fitted stones, living in the gaps, their presence felt rather than seen.

The mollusks are slower. They cling to the vertical surfaces—the walls, the outcroppings, the undersides of overhangs—and they pulse with a rhythm that is difficult to distinguish from the settlement's own pulse. They extend their bodies into the humid air when the moisture gathers, and they retract when it disperses, and they have found, in this, a way of living that does not require them to move. They are eaten, sometimes, by the people, their shells broken against the weeping stone, their bodies pulled free and eaten raw or dried in the warm air. This is not their purpose—they have no purpose—but it is their fate, and they have accepted it.

The translucent creatures are the strangest. They cluster in the shade, their forms so clear that one can see the structures behind them through their bodies, and yet they are not invisible—they catch the light in ways that make them seem to pulse, to shift, to exist in a frequency just adjacent to the visible. They do not skitter or crawl but rather *flow*, their movements liquid, their bodies adapting to every surface they touch. They have no obvious organs, no visible mouths or limbs, and yet they move with apparent purpose, gathering in certain places, dispersing at certain times. The people do not eat them, or do not often eat them, and the insects do not seem to hunt them, and their role in the settlement's ecology is unclear but their presence is constant.

The humming things are the least visible and perhaps the most numerous.

They live in the cracks, in the gaps, in the spaces between the basalt plates where the water seeps up from below. They do not move in any way the eye can track; they simply *are*, their bodies tuned to some frequency that produces sound without apparent motion. When the ear focuses on them, they resolve into a low background hum, omnipresent, indistinguishable from the settlement's own sound. One can hear them only by not listening, by allowing the ear to accept the sound as a single continuous voice and then, briefly, to separate.

They are alive in a way that is difficult to name.

The people know this, even if they do not say it. They build around the fauna, with the fauna, perhaps for the fauna, though *for* is perhaps too deliberate a word. The structures include spaces that seem designed for the creatures—narrow channels that lead from the weeping stone to low hollows where the translucent things gather, platforms of damp reed where the mollusks cling, thresholds worn smooth by the passage of countless insects. The people have not made these spaces intentionally, or perhaps they have made them so often that the intention has become habit and the habit has become instinct. The settlement is not human-alone. It never was.

You will learn this, if you stay.

You will learn to hear the hum as a single voice and then as a chorus, to separate the drip of one outcropping from the drip of another, to hear the particular creak that means the timber is settling and the particular silence that means the water has risen. You will learn to move through the tunnels without looking, your body remembering the path that your eyes have not quite seen. You will learn to sleep through the sound, to wake in it, to find the silence on the other side of the basalt plains and feel it as absence rather than peace.

Your skin will darken where it contacts the weeping rock. Your hands will soften where they touch the damp timber. The mineral traces will appear in your flesh, faint at first and then deeper, and your children, if you have children, will carry them deeper still.

And one day you will stop at the settlement's edge, and the new arrivals will look at you, and you will watch them with an expression that is not quite welcome and not quite wariness, and you will know that they do not move correctly, that they are too loud and too careful and too slow, that they have not yet learned that the settlement does not change.

You will not explain. The sound will teach them, as it taught you, as it teaches everyone.

The sound does not change. The settlement remains. The fauna skitters and pulses and hums, and the water seeps and pools and weeps, and the people move through it all with their compact bodies and their damp skin and their knowledge of the deep places, and the world holds its wetness like a held breath.

It does not exhale. It does not need to. This is equilibrium: the way all things have found their place, and remain in it, and will remain, and will remain, and will remain.
