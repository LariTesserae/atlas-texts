What lives here lives slowly. Not the slowness of patience, which implies waiting for something, but the slowness of chemistry, of processes that have no reason to accelerate because they have already become what they are meant to be.

The vegetation lives. This is the easiest thing to say, and the hardest to understand. The stalks drink from the air, drawing sulfur compounds and mineral vapor through surfaces that gleam like wounds in the light. A stalk might take centuries to reach its full height, and when it dies, it does not fall as organic matter would fall—it simply stops adding, becomesiteiteiteite inert, and remains standing, a gray scaffold for the next generation climbing its spine. They pulse with their bioluminescence not because they need to, not because light serves some function in their economy, but because the chemistry that sustains them produces light as a byproduct, and producing it costs nothing, and so they produce it endlessly, in the same rhythm, for the same reasons a fire burns.

The towers live. This is harder to speak of, because their life is mechanical but not without grace. Within their gray forms, channels carry something—it is not electricity, not exactly, but it serves the same function, a charge that moves through black wires and buried channels and feeds the circular structures sunk into the salt like seeds. The towers were built, once, by something that had hands or had designs or had purposes that have since eroded away. But what they were built to do, they still do. They maintain. They sustain. They hum their hum at the same frequency as the vegetation, and it may be that this is coincidence, the natural resonance of mineral systems under chemical stress, or it may be that the towers were designed to speak to the growth, to maintain some balance, some ratio of mineral life to machine presence, that has long since exceeded any intention. They do not know they are maintaining. They do not know they are maintaining nothing but themselves. But they maintain.

It is the frequency of equilibrium, the sound a world makes when it has achieved what it sought and no longer remembers seeking it. A person standing here might feel the hum before they hear it, a pressure in the chest, a sense of being in the presence of something vast that has chosen to be quiet. The hum is the one shared breath of everything here, the one common note struck by vegetation and tower and salt anditeiteiteiteiteiteiteite atmosphere. It is the sound of a world that has finished becoming itself.

And the emptiness lives too, in its way. The vast spaces between the thickets, the distances that a person might walk for an hour without seeing anything new—these are not dead. They are the pause between notes. They are the silence that makes the hum possible, the negative space that defines the shape of what is present. In an ecosystem of water and carbon, emptiness is absence, the mere absence of life. But here, where life is mineral and chemistry and slow charge, emptiness is something else. It is the condition of possibility. It is the room in which the hum can be heard.

What lives here has no knowledge of its own living. The vegetation does not know it grows. The towers do not know they maintain. The hum does not know it hums. And the emptiness does not know it waits, though if it did wait, it would wait for something to come and find the waiting place, to stand in the silence and feel the hum and understand, for one vertiginous moment, what it means to be alive in a place where life is made of stone and air and the slow turning of mechanisms that have forgotten why they turn.

Perhaps that is what they were built for. Perhaps that is what the hum is for.
