You will sit on the western rim where the limestone has worn to a broad, warm seat, and you will stay longer than you intended. There is something in the quality of the air here—not merely humidity but a kind of liquid stillness that presses against the skin, fills the lungs, slows the pulse. The body adjusts. The body begins to want nothing. This is the first thing the sinkhole does to you: it makes stillness feel like enough.

The light that reaches this depth is filtered twice—once by the canopy, which catches the direct sun and turns it to green, and again by the moisture in the air, which diffuses everything into a luminous evenness that has no harsh angles. Shadows here are suggestions rather than facts. The light falls soft on the limestone walls, picking out the scallops and pockets in pale relief, and the effect is of something always just beginning to be revealed. In the hour before noon, a wedge of stronger light enters through the canopy gap, moving across the pool surface in a slow diagonal, and the insects that have been dozing on the water's skin rise in small clouds where it passes. Then it is gone, and the surface settles again to its dark glass.

You notice that you have stopped listening to the world above. The birds are still singing somewhere, the insects still stridulating, but it arrives now only as a faint, directionless hum—the forest's constant voice heard from within its own body, as if hearing your own blood from inside the ear. Down here, the sounds are immediate and small: the drip of condensed water gathering weight on a fern frond until it falls, the soft percussion of it striking stone; the almost inaudible click of a lizard's tongue against a surface; the slow, wet sounds of things growing. A vine descends close enough to touch, and when you press your finger to a leaf, it is warm with the sinkhole's own temperature, and slightly damp, and it yields slightly to your pressure like a living thing that has not yet learned to resist.

The catfish have no fear of you. You can see their faces now, pressed against the glass-dark surface, their mouths working at the microscopic film, their barbels drifting in the still air. They have been here so long that they have forgotten predators, or perhaps there have never been any, or perhaps the pool protects them in some way you cannot name. They are small fish, no larger than a hand, pale-bellied and slow, and they move in the upper water with the patience of something that has no reason to hurry. When the heron strikes, it is sudden and efficient—a single motion, neck extended, beak a white needle in the dim light—and the fish it takes is gone without trace, and the others do not scatter. They continue their patient work. They have been doing this for so long that the rhythm of feeding and being fed upon is as natural as the seep of water through stone.

At night, the sinkhole becomes another place. The moisture that has been rising all day from the warm rock and the warm water now meets air that has begun to cool, and the mist forms thicker, pooling in the lower hollows like something alive, something that breathes. The temperature drop is slight but perceptible—a few degrees, enough to make the stone feel cooler against the hand, enough to raise the hairs on the forearm. The column of light from the canopy gap is gone, and what remains is the faint, ambient glow of a tropical night, enough to see by if you are patient, enough to lose yourself in if you are not. The insects that have spent the day in their drowsy, sun-warmed murmurations are active now in a different way—circling the pool surface in tighter spirals, rising and falling in patterns that seem almost but not quite random. The bats emerge from their crevices, their wings catching what little light there is, their flight silent and sure in the air that is thick with their prey. You cannot see them hunt, but you can hear the small, wet sounds of their success—a click, a pause, another click—and then the flutter of wings and the bat is gone, carrying its meal into the dark above.

If you stay long enough, you begin to feel the depth of this place—not the thirty meters to the water, which is easy to measure, but the other depth, the one that has to do with time. The stone has been here since before the limestone was limestone, since the calcium was seashells and the magnesium was coral, since the slow pressure of the earth began its patient work of becoming. The water has been seeping through it for so long that the rock itself has become a kind of memory of water, its pores and hollows recording every drop that passed through on its way down. To press your hand to the warm stone is to touch something that is still becoming. The sinkhole is not a finished thing. It is a process, a moment in a longer story that began before there were trees to witness it and will continue long after you have gone.

The carved stairs are evidence of this, though you cannot read them. Who cut them? How long ago? The stone has been healing the cuts for so long that the grooves are now softer than the surrounding rock, worn smooth by centuries of feet, by water, by the slow patience of calcium and time. The basin beside them was carved too, or shaped, or simply accumulated its hollow from the same patient work—but if it was made by hands, those hands have been gone so long that their intention has been absorbed by the stone, returned to the earth, made indistinguishable from erosion and age. What remains is the fact of the hollow itself, the way it holds water that falls from the sky and the condensation that gathers on the stone, a thumbprint of something that is not quite yours but not quite not yours either. You have touched this water. Your predecessors, whoever they were, also touched it. The water does not remember you, but the basin does, in its own way, in the slow way that stone holds everything.

The pool is deeper than it looks. The light stops somewhere in the upper three meters, absorbed by the minerals that have been dissolving into the water for longer than you can imagine, and what lies below is darkness that is not empty but full—full of calcium, full of life, full of the slow chemical conversation between stone and water that has been happening since the first drop found its way down. There may be creatures in that darkness that have never seen light. There may be bacteria that metabolize sulfur or iron, living in the warm seep that feeds the pool from below, part of a food chain that does not need the sun to continue. The surface is a membrane between worlds, and it trembles sometimes for no reason you can see—the heron has not moved, the bats have gone to their roosts, the water should be still—and then is still again, as if something beneath has shifted in its sleep.

The heron will stay until the light fails. It will stand on its root with one leg drawn up, neck folded, beak pointing at the water with a stillness that is not waiting but being. It has found its shape, as the sinkhole has found its shape, as the catfish have found theirs. This is what the place teaches, if you stay long enough: that stillness is not absence but presence, that depth is not darkness but fullness, that the patient work of water and stone and time has been building this moment for so long that it is almost ready to be itself.

Almost. There is still a trickle of water, somewhere below. There is still the slow dissolving of the earth. The sinkhole breathes, and you breathe with it, and the green light falls and the mist rises and the catfish cruise in their patient, whiskered way, and none of it needs you to witness it, but you do, and that is a kind of grace, and it will not last, and that is all right, and you sit on the warm limestone rim and you watch and the place goes on being what it has always been becoming.
