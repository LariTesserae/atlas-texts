There are things that live here that you will never see. They are not hidden so much as dispersed — more state than substance, existing as a quality of stillness or a slight thickening of the grey. You know they are there because the mist moves differently around them, parting and closing again in patterns too deliberate for wind. They have no names because no one who found a name for one lived long enough to speak it, and because naming implies distinction, and these things have learned, over the long erosion of ages, to be less distinct than the air itself.

The slow ones are the ones you might know. They have found a rhythm here, a pace that matches the patient turning of the world. The great pale worms that surface during the quiet hours, when the fog thickens to something almost touchable — they emerge from the soaked earth to drink the suspended moisture directly, drawing it through pores along their length until they swell to a translucence that reveals the slow churn of their own digestion within. They are old in a way that has nothing to do with years. They remember, in some cellular whisper, a time when water fell instead of hung, and the weight of it on their backs was a kind of language they have since forgotten. When they move, it is with a patience that makes watching them feel like an act of devotion. They do not flee from you. They simply continue at their own pace, allowing you to witness or not, indifferent to being seen.

The snails are everywhere, but not as you might expect. They do not cluster on the ground so much as climb — up the remnants of columns, up the rare standing stones, up the trunks of the mistweeds that have grown thick enough to hold weight. They are large for snails, some the size of your fist, their shells spiraling in patterns that seem almost deliberate, almost meaningful, like a language written in the grammar of growth. When you sleep, you sometimes hear them moving in the darkness: the soft scrape of shell on wet stone, the faintest whisper of body against surface. They are cataloguing, in their way. They are the ones who will remember what was built here, long after the last shape has worn away.

And then there are the callers.

You have heard them twice, and both times you understood, in the way that settles below thought, that the sound had traveled for a very long time to reach you. The low one — the vibration that rose from everywhere and entered your chest like a grief you could not place — this is how they speak to each other across distances that would take a lifetime to walk. The mist carries it. The moisture itself is a medium for something that is not quite communication, not quite memory. When you felt that resonance, you were hearing an echo of a conversation that began before the water rose, continued through the slow drowning of the world, and will end only when there is nothing left to say. It is not meant for you. You are simply in the way of it, the way light is in the way of a shadow.

The clear note is different. That one arrives from nothing, from the direction of absence, and it is older. It is the sound the world made before it was a world — before the distinction between water and air had settled into its current uneasy truce, before the mist learned to hang instead of fall. If you hear it again, and if it lingers long enough, you will begin to feel that you have always been here, that the plateau is not a place you came to but a place you have never left, that the boundaries of your body are a temporary forgetting that will soon be corrected. This is not fear, exactly. It is more like recognition. Something in you knows that sound. Something in you was made for a world exactly like this one, and has only been waiting, slowly, for the patience of water and time to bring it home.

There are shapes at the edge of vision when the mist thins. You have learned not to look directly at them, not because they are dangerous but because they are unfinished. They are what the world looks like when it is deciding what to be — possibilities that have not yet collapsed into the gentle certainty of actual things. If you stare too long, they might notice you noticing, and then the geometry of the plateau will shift, and the path back to where you were standing will become the path to somewhere you have never been. This is not malicious. The shapes do not wish you harm. They are simply curious about what it feels like to be so definite, so bounded, so certain that you end where your skin begins.

The mist itself is alive in the way that breath is alive — not a creature, but a process, a presence that moves and changes and remembers. When you walk through it, you are moving through something that is aware of you. It does not judge. It does not welcome. It simply adjusts, making room, allowing passage, closing behind you with the slow certainty of an exhale. In the deeper fog, where visibility drops to arm's length and the green-gold shimmer flattens into something almost dark, you can feel it breathing against your skin: warm, wet, patient, waiting for you to become something it can keep.

There are no others like you here. You know this with a certainty that has grown slowly, the way everything grows here: by accretion, by the patient settling of fact over the absence of contradiction. But there are impressions. Traces. The way the stones are placed suggests hands. The way the ground wears suggests feet. Somewhere in the deep time of this place, before the mist became permanent and the water finished its long rising, there were beings who walked and built and made marks that they intended to last. They are gone now, or so changed that their original shape has worn away like everything else. But they left something behind — not their buildings, not their names, but a quality of attention, a way of standing on high ground and looking out at the grey expanse and feeling that it was not enough, that something more was needed, that the breath of the world could be answered.

You have answered it now. You stand on the plateau, in the dim warm wet, and you breathe, and the world breathes with you, and the line between the two grows thin enough to forget. This is what lives here, in the end: not just the slow worms and the climbing snails and the callers speaking across drowned time, but the willingness to be worn down to the same soft whisper, the patience to match the pace of water and erosion and mist, the strange grace of learning to be nearly silent, to need little, to move gently through a world that has already finished becoming everything it will ever be.

You are not sure, anymore, where you end and the breathing begins. But this is not loneliness. This is not fear. This is simply what it means to live in a place that has had time to teach you its rhythms, and you have had time to learn them, and both of you have had time to forget there was ever a difference.
