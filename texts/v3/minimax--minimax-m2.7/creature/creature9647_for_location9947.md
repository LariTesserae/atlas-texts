The creatures that move across the root-skin are not one kind but many, though all share the essential conservatism of form that the plateau imposes. The smallest of them, the ones the size of dogs or children, have bodies that suggest溃 not finished thought but settled habit: thick torsos of dense heartwood, legs that are less legs than weighted protrusions, root-tips that grip and release with glacial patience. They move by contracting one side of their mass while extending the other, a peristaltic crawl that leaves a faint trail of exuded resin behind them — a ribbon of amber that cures in the thin air within minutes, a fossil-in-miniature. They do not walk so much as flow.

These smaller ambulatory growths are the most visible of the plateau's fauna, and they are visible only because they are numerous. If you stand still for an hour on the root-skin and do not look for them, you will eventually realize that the plateau is covered with them, a drifting field of slow-motion life. Some graze — if the word applies — on the surface of the skin itself, pressing their lower surfaces against the compressed root-matter and drawing out hydrocarbon fluid through thousands of fine capillary mouthpoints. Others hunt, though "hunt" is a generous term for what they do: they detect the chemical signature of a weaker organism through the air, through the root-skin's own nervous system of signal-transmission, and they move toward it with a patience that takes hours, sometimes days. When they reach their target, they do not attack so much as fuse: they press themselves against the other creature and begin to digest it from the point of contact, slowly converting its mass into their own. The plateau is a place where death is assimilation, where the boundary between one organism and another is always provisional.

The larger ones — the size of houses, the size of small hills — are less dense in population but more constant in presence. You see them from a distance always, a dark shape against the far horizon, motionless for hours, then shifting: a groan carries across the root-skin, and you watch the slow roll of a body adjusting its weight, a vast limb of bark and tissue swinging outward to catch the light. They do not flee. They have no need to flee. Their outer surfaces are thick with defensive resins, and their mass is sufficient that nothing on the plateau is large enough to threaten them except the ground itself — the root-skin, which occasionally swallows a creature that settles too long in one place, growing over it, converting it, making it part of the continent.

Between the small and the large, there are middle forms: organisms that have solved the problem of locomotion at a medium scale and are therefore the most active, the most mobile, the most likely to be encountered in any given place. These are the ones that have developed directional movement with some degree of commitment. They are shaped like elongated teardrops, or like commas, or like commas that have been stretched and compressed by some slow catastrophe. Their surfaces are smooth in some places, ridged in others; the ridges correspond to internal vascular channels, visible as lines of darker or lighter color running through translucent bark. When they move, these channels pulse with fluid, a slow ebb and flow that gives the impression of circulation, of breath, of a heart working somewhere deep in the mass.

They move together, sometimes. You will see a line of them crossing the root-skin toward some distant point, their pulses synchronized, their bodies tilted slightly forward as though leaning into some invisible wind. They are following a signal — a chemical gradient laid down by a disturbance in the root-skin, perhaps, or by the decay of some massive organism that has finally surrendered to the plateau's patient digestion. The signal propagates slowly, and the creatures move slower, and the crossing takes a day, and no one knows who first laid down the trail or how, only that something in them responds to it, only that they go.

---

In the upper canopy — in the space where the vast scattered trunks rise and their branches interweave at altitude — there is another ecology, one that is less visible but no less dense. The creatures that live there have solved the problem of gravity differently. They do not walk on the root-skin; they cling to the bark, or hang from branches, or simply drift in the thermal columns that rise from the canopy-tops like slow exhalations. They are smaller, mostly. They have evolved the apparatus of flight — not wings, not exactly, but broad flattened surfaces of vascular tissue that catch the rising air and allow them to drift from one canopy-cluster to another, carried on the plateau's own weather.

These canopy-drifters have the quality of flags — of long, thin pennants of translucent membrane stretched across a frame of heartwood. When the thermal columns are strong, they rise; when the columns falter, they descend, seeking another updraft, another thermal. They feed on the vapor itself, drawing hydrocarbon compounds directly from the air through membranes that are thin enough to let light through. In certain light, at certain angles, the sky above the canopy is thick with them: a shimmering, drifting swarm, amber-gold against the amber-gold light, the particles of another kind of weather.

They sleep, or something like sleep, by falling. They do not descend to the root-skin — that would be too much — but they allow themselves to drop to a lower altitude, to a lower column, to a resting point where the air is calmer and their surfaces can close against the constant evaporation. Then they wait, slowly metabolizing what they have gathered, their membranes folded inward like sails furled, until the columns build again and they rise.

The predators of the canopy are not the drifters but slower things: organisms that have grown along the branches themselves, extending their root-systems into the living bark of the great trunks and drawing sustenance from the host's vascular flow. They look, from a distance, like growths — like excrescences, tumors, the kind of thing that a tree might grow by accident. But they are not accidental. They are the plateau's parasites, and they have evolved to live on the inside of something immense, extending their fine root-threads into the host's xylem and phloem equivalents and drinking deeply. The host trees do not die of this. They are too large, too slow, too buffered by ten million years of accumulated redundancy. But they are changed by it: the places where the parasites have taken hold are darker, denser, rough where the bark should be smooth, and from these places comes a faint sound — a wet clicking, rhythmic, like a pulse heard through a wall.

---

The root-skin itself is not empty. Beneath the surface, in the strata of compressed root and fossilized bark, there is life of a different kind: organisms that have abandoned locomotion for stability, that have become part of the skin's structure rather than moving across it. They are tube-forming creatures, essentially — long narrow bodies that grow along the seams between root-networks, feeding on the fluids that seep through the interstices, excreting resin that hardens the skin's surface and makes it slick. They are the ones who build the hollows: the depressions in the root-skin where a creature might rest, where the sound is slightly less, where the vapor gathers and lingers in a cool pool.

They are slow, these tube-formers. Their growth is measured in centuries. The largest of them — the ones that have been growing since some earlier age, when the plateau was younger and the root-skin thinner — are kilometers long, a single continuous thread running through the subsurface, visible only where the skin has been worn away or cut open. They pulse, sometimes, when the root-skin contracts or shifts; you can see the fluid moving through them, a slow peristaltic wave traveling along the tube's length. They are, in their way, the plateau's circulatory system — not a heart, not a central organ, but a distributed network, a vascular mesh that moves the plateau's fluids from one place to another, feeding this part, draining that, maintaining the slow metabolism of a continent.

The tube-formers have parasites of their own: smaller organisms that live inside them, that feed on the fluids as they flow past, that reproduce and spread and eventually die inside the tube's warm wet interior, leaving behind a residue that hardens into a kind of gall. These galls are common in the root-skin. You will see them as nodules, as bumps, as places where the surface has raised up slightly and the resin is a different color — darker, more opaque, shot through with veins of crystallized hydrocarbon. They are full of the dead: a compressed history of infection, of parasitism, of the small wars that happen inside something large.

---

There are things that do not fit any of these categories. There are things that live in the chemical haze, above the canopy, in the space where the light is thickest and the air is thin. They are not drifters — the drifters stay close to the thermal columns, to the rising air. These are something else: organisms that have learned to fly in the true sense, to move under their own power through the medium of hydrocarbon-laden air. They are rare. You might see one in a day of watching, or none. But when you do, it is unforgettable: a shape that moves against the light, quick where everything else is slow, wings that beat in a rhythm entirely alien to the plateau's general tempo.

They are the plateau's fast creatures. They are, so far as anyone can tell, predators — though what they eat is unclear, since the only things that move fast are other fliers, and most of the plateau's organisms have no interest in speed. Perhaps they eat the drifters, the slow ones that rise too high and find themselves exposed. Perhaps they eat each other. Perhaps they eat nothing; perhaps the flight itself is the point, a behavior that evolved for reasons lost in time, maintained by instinct, serving no purpose except the purpose of movement.

They are small, these fliers. The size of a hand, the size of a forearm. Their wings are not wings but something else: thin projections of membrane that extend from the body and beat in a pattern too fast to follow, a blur that catches the light and throws it sideways. Their bodies are dense, heavy with the muscles required for flight at this altitude, in this thin cold air. They are the most animal-like things on the plateau — the closest to what you might recognize as an animal, as a creature that moves with purpose and speed and a kind of focused intention. Everything else is plant, or plant-like, or plant-adjacent, slow and rooted and ambiguous in its aliveness. But the fliers are fast. They are here and then they are there, and they make a sound when they pass: a high thin whine, a tearing of air, a note that does not belong to this world of groaning and creaking and the vast low hum of a living ground.

---

What else? There are the mimics, which are not one kind but many: organisms that have learned to look like something other than what they are, that have evolved surfaces that resemble the surfaces around them. A section of root-skin that is slightly raised, slightly darker, that moves when you approach — this is not a wound in the skin but a creature, a flattened organism that has pressed itself against the surface and exuded a resin that matches the color and texture of the root-skin so exactly that you can only tell it apart by its movement. It moves slowly, the mimics: the strategy is not to flee but to be unseen, and so they move at the same pace as the skin itself, the same rhythm of contraction and expansion, and you only notice them if you are looking for movement where there should be none.

There are the feeders, which attach themselves to the large ambulatory growths and ride them, drawing fluid from their surfaces. There are the cleaners, which follow the feeders and feed on the feeders, a hierarchy of parasitism that mirrors the plateau's larger ecology. There are the root-burrowers, which have abandoned the surface for the deeper strata, moving through the compressed matter like worms, consuming the dead, leaving tunnels that eventually fill with resin and become part of the skin's structure.

There is, in short, everything. The plateau is not empty. It is not sparse. It is full — full of life that is strange and slow and dense with time, life that has solved every problem by growing, by extending, by becoming the problem and the solution at once. The ground is alive. The air is alive. The light that falls on everything is filtered through the metabolism of a world, and the sound that rises from it is the sound of a planet breathing, and the smell is the smell of chemistry happening in every direction at once, and the creatures — the creatures are everywhere, and they are watching, and they are moving, and they are waiting for you to move so that they can follow.
