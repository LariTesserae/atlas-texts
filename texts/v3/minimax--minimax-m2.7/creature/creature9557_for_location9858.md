There is a creature that lives in the roots.

Not one creature but many: a colonial organism, or perhaps something that began as many and became one, a mass of pale filaments that interweave through the root-lattice like thought through a brain. It moves when you watch—not toward you, not away, but *in response*, a slow peristalsis of itself that sends ripples through the connected roots. The roots, you realize, are not entirely dead. They carry something. They are veins. And this thing in them is the heart, or the blood, or both.

It does not have a name you could speak.

When the condensation drips particularly heavily, when the humidity peaks in the small hours when no light reaches, it extends something—a tendril, a probe, a question—and touches the stone. Tests it. Retracts. You would not call it intelligence, but you might call it *attention*. It is paying attention to the world it lives inside. It is measuring.

There are other things that pay attention back.

The moss is not moss. You will have suspected this. It moves too much, too purposefully, for moss. Under the velvet surface there are bodies—or the suggestion of bodies—packeted and patient, waiting in the damp for their moment. They are the size of seeds, the size of flakes of old skin. They have been here long enough that their ancestors stopped remembering anything else. They have no eyes because eyes are waste here, no resource to spend on light that doesn't come. They have instead a sensitivity to pressure, to chemical gradient, to the faintest vibration in the stone. They know when you enter by the displacement you cause, the air you move, the warmth you bring. They know and they wait, and when you leave they will resume their slow business, their patient consumption of the walls, their patient becoming.

What do they become?

That is the question the courtyard is always asking itself.

The fronds that reach up from the window-slit are not fronds. You know this now. They are arms, or they are branches, or they are the same thing under a different name. They curl at their ends because they have reached as far as they can toward the light-memory and found nothing, and so they stop, and hang, and wait for the light to return or for them to find another way. They are fern-like, or vine-like, or perhaps something older than both, a plant-form that found this particular darkness and decided it was home, that made itself into this shape because this shape is what darkness asks for. They are patient in the way only sessile life can be patient: they will wait until the stone fails, until the wall crumbles, until the light comes or doesn't, and either way they will have been here, they will have done this, they will have made themselves into this.

In the upper reaches, where the fronds are thickest and the air is coldest, there is a nest.

You cannot see it clearly. It is built of stems—cut stems, stripped and woven, carried here by something that has hands or beak or the chemical precision of mandibles. The nest is old. Its builders may be dead, or they may be something else now, or they may be the creatures that stir in the roots and the moss. The nest holds shape. It holds presence. Inside it there is warmth—not much, but some, a metabolism's worth of heat gathered in a cup of woven stems and insulated by condensation, and in that warmth, in that pocket of not-quite-cold, something lives.

The birds, if birds they are, are eyeless.

They did not lose their eyes gradually, over generations. They lost them quickly, in a few generations, because eyes are expensive and darkness is absolute and what you don't need becomes what you cannot afford. They navigate by sound now—by the soft, continuous clicking they make, a sonar so refined it reads the shape of the courtyard, the distance to the root-lattice, the position of the dripping, the location of the nest's entrance. They click and they listen and they know where everything is in a space no larger than a cupped pair of hands.

They raise young. The young are pink and blind and utterly helpless, and the parents feed them on what the courtyard offers: insects drawn to the moisture, bits of moss pulled loose and carried up, perhaps the bodies of the small things that live in the roots when those bodies are discarded, moulted, no longer needed. The young eat and grow and eventually their eyes fade and their clicking begins and they join the courtyard's conversation, the endless soft chatter of the dark-adapted, the community of the blind.

You have been standing here too long.

The damp has settled into your clothes. Your breath mists. Your eyes have adjusted—not to light, because there is no light, but to the faintest variations in darkness, the shapes of things that move without light, the suggestion of form where form has become habit. You can see, now, the way the roots move when nothing else does. You can see the pulse of the colonial thing, the slow heartbeat of the courtyard's core. You can see where the moss is thickest, where the bodies are densest, where the decay is fastest and the growth most urgent.

The cold is not uncomfortable anymore.

Something has happened—not to the courtyard, but to your sense of it. You have stopped being a visitor. You have stopped being an intrusion. You are simply present now, another body in the hollow, another source of warmth and breath and motion for the creatures to sense and catalogue and incorporate into their understanding. You are part of the system. You have entered the process.

The walls are waiting. The moss is waiting. The roots are waiting. The blind things in the nest and the pale things in the stone and the slow colonial heart that pulses in the root-lattice are all waiting for something, and you cannot tell if it is you or for you or neither, if you have been seen or merely sensed, if your presence here means anything to the courtyard or if meaning is a thing this place left behind long ago.

But you are here.

And the courtyard is full.

And the cold settles in like patience, and the damp gathers like memory, and somewhere beneath the roots, where the water pools and the darkness is total, something is moving toward something else, something is feeding on something, something is dying and something is growing, and the whole system breathes in its strange ecology, its alien patience, its endless slow becoming.

You could stay.

You could hold this place in your mind and let it hold you, and you could be substrate too, eventually, and your bones could become stone and your stone could become root and your roots could become home for things that have no eyes and no name and no need for anything but this: this cold, this damp, this dark, this life.
