This is what lives here.

The pale things with too many legs: jointed, eyeless, moving slowly through the mineral moisture in paths that seem chosen. They have six legs, or eight, or more — it is difficult to count what does not wish to be counted. Their bodies are soft in the way of things that have never known dryness, translucent where the light reaches them, showing the slow pulse of something internal working through dissolved stone. They do not flee the wrong-angled light. They have never known any other light. When they move, they leave faint trails on the wet sandstone, dissolving it grain by grain, and in this way they are slowly reshaping the hollow, though they do not know this, or do not care, or time means something different to them.

The soft-bodied masses: they cling to the walls where water seeps through, slow and patient, neither plant nor animal but something that breathes moisture and excretes mineral. When the visitor presses a hand to the wall near one, it does not move. It pulses faintly, a slow rhythm that might be a heartbeat or might be the pulse of water through stone, or might be time itself slowing down to be near them. They are growing. They have always been growing. They add to themselves molecule by molecule from the mineral-rich water, and in a thousand years or ten thousand they will be larger, and in a million they will be the wall, and the wall will be them, and the distinction will have dissolved along with the sandstone.

The pale tufts: small, rootless, clinging to the damper stretches of wall. They look like mold but they are not mold. They look like something that should have roots but cannot find purchase in the soft stone. They fruit in the night, emerging wet and pale, and by noon they are gone, withdrawn into the wall or simply declined to be observed. The one stunted plant with leaves like wet paper: it grows from a crack in the floor where water pools and evaporates and pools again. Its leaves are the color of nothing else, gray-green, translucent when held to the wrong light. It drinks slowly, mineral water in sips so small they might be imagination. It has been growing for as long as the hollow has existed. It will outlast the visitor. It will outlast the visitor's kind.

The drifters: small things that move through the air without wings, insect or seed or neither, drifting in the mineral-thick atmosphere. They do not fly so much as hang suspended, moving when the damp air moves, going nowhere in particular. When the visitor breathes, they drift toward the breath and away again, following currents too subtle to name. They are perhaps the youngest things here, or perhaps the oldest — it is difficult to tell with drifters, whose time is measured in the time it takes to fall.

The water in the narrow channel: it does not babble. It moves, yes, but in silence, as if sound has not yet learned to happen here, or has forgotten. It carries dissolved things in solution, things the visitor's eyes could not see but the visitor's tongue could taste — mineral, ancient, patient. The creatures drink from it, when they drink. The walls weep into it. It flows toward something below, somewhere deeper, somewhere this channel leads, and in that below-place perhaps there is more hollow, more damp, more soft-stone rooms waiting to be decided.

The structures: the tooth-like fragments half-buried in the walls. They are not teeth. They are not bone. They are something between — grown, not carved, emerging from mineral-rich water the way a stalactite emerges, but faster, or slower, or at a speed that makes the distinction meaningless. They have always been here. The hollow has always been here. The visitor is the new thing, the temporary thing, the thing that will leave and be forgotten while the structures continue to grow in their slow patient way, adding a layer, another layer, recording time in the language of mineral.

This is what lives here: the dampness itself. The moisture that is not quite water, that tastes of stone and something older than stone. It lives in the air, in the walls, in the creature-bodies and the plant-flesh and the soft-body masses. It is the medium through which everything here moves and grows and is. The visitor, stepping into this hollow, enters a space already saturated with something that does not have a name in the visitor's language. The visitor breathes it in. The visitor's lungs become damp. The visitor's skin becomes damp. The visitor begins, already, to be part of this place.

The creatures watch from their damp corners. They have watched before. They will watch again. They are patient in the way of things that live in geological time, that measure existence in mineral accumulation rather than seasons. They do not fear the visitor. They do not not-fear the visitor. They simply observe, with whatever they have instead of eyes, the temporary shape that has entered the threshold, and they wait, as they have always waited, for the next thing to arrive, or for nothing to arrive, which is also a kind of waiting, which is also a kind of time.

The hollow was always here. The creatures have always lived in it. The damp has always wept through the sandstone, softening it, dissolving it grain by grain, making room for more. The visitor came from elsewhere. The visitor will return to elsewhere. And when the visitor is gone, the hollow will remain, still deciding, still unfinished, still becoming whatever it is becoming, and the creatures will remain, and the damp will remain, and the light will continue to fall at its wrong-angled angle, casting shadows backward, illuminating a space that should be dark but is not, that should be silent but is not, that should be empty but is not.

This is what lives here. This is what has always lived here. This is what will live here still, long after the visitor is the kind of absence that is forgotten.
