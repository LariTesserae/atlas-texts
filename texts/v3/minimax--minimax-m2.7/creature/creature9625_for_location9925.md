What lives here is what has always lived here, and what has come to live here, and the distinction between them has worn away like the mortar in the walls.

I have lived in Caldtow for thirty-one years. I arrived when I was young enough that the strangeness of it became the shape of my thinking, so that when I travel now — which is rarely — the solidity of ordinary places feels like a kind of violence. The blank walls. The absence of green at the base of things. I find myself looking for moss on stonework in towns that have not forgotten they are stone, and finding none, and feeling a small grief that I cannot name.

Here, the moss tells me where to look.

---

The people are few and they are quiet, not from shyness but from habit. We have learned to speak softly because the place itself is soft, and loud voices carry strangely here, catching on branches and returning altered, muffled, as if the trees were listening and had decided not to repeat what they heard. This is not superstition. It is simply what happens in a place where the air is heavy with moisture and full of leaves. You learn to modulate. You learn that a whisper will reach the next courtyard, and that a shout will not.

My neighbor is a man named Torin who repairs the batteries for the waterwheel system. His workshop is the back half of a building that was once a weaver's cottage — you can still see the outline of the loom in the floor, the grooves worn by the shuttle's passage, now filled with soil and sprouting clover. He works by daylight and by lantern, his hands stained black with the patina that grows on copper in this damp, the same patina that greens the church spire and streaks the old bridge stones. When I bring him bread, he looks up from his work and nods, and the nod means more than words would. We have not needed many words. The place speaks for us, or rather, the place has made us people who do not need to speak.

There are children here — perhaps eight or ten, of varying ages. They play in the courtyards and in the margins, where the settlement gives way to the grazed hillsides. They are wilder than the adults, louder, more willing to climb the trees that have grown through the old schoolhouse and make perches in the high branches where the heron sometimes lands at dusk. The children do not yet know the boundary between the grown and the built. They run across walls that are moss to the ankle and brick to the shin and they do not think about it. They catch dormice in the mill and name them and release them. They know where the foxes sleep by the way the air smells near the fallen oak, and they do not go there, because the foxes were here first.

My daughter is one of these children. She is seven. She knows the name of every tree in the settlement and which ones have fruit and which ones have been trained to grow across paths as gates. She calls the fig in the church *the tree that ate the altar*, which is not quite accurate — the roots have not eaten anything, they have simply made it part of themselves, the way all things here eventually become part of everything else. She thinks this is normal. She thinks all churches grow figs in their centers, given time. When she is older, if she leaves, she will learn that this is not normal. That most places resist. That the boundary between growing and grown is a war in most of the world, not a conversation.

I hope she will be able to explain what a conversation looks like. I hope the words will come.

---

The animals are not tame but they are known.

The foxes are the most present of the larger creatures. There are always foxes here — my grandmother spoke of foxes, and her grandmother spoke of foxes, and by now the species has changed, the valley selecting for a particular kind of russet that hides well in the root-mats and the moss. They den in the old oak that fell and became a hill, its trunk now a mound of bark and soil and hollow channels where the small creatures live too. The foxes do not hunt in the settlement proper; there is too much human presence, too much foot-traffic on the spongy ground. They hunt in the margins, in the grazed hills where the goats keep the grass short and the mice are abundant. Sometimes I see one at dusk, standing still in the lower marsh, and we regard each other across the cattails, and then one of us moves and the moment ends. We have no agreement and no conflict. We simply share the valley, the way the trees share the walls.

The dormice are another matter. They have no fear. They live in the mill, in the spaces between the old machinery, and they come out at dusk to eat the insects that gather in the shafts of light. I have sat in the mill at evening and watched them — dozens of them, small and pale and quick, their tails like ribbons — moving across the grain of the wood, over the moss that grows in the gaps, past the generator that hums with the waterwheel's turn. They do not notice me. Or they notice me and have decided I am not a threat, which amounts to the same thing. Perhaps they have been here long enough to learn that the humans of Caldtow are not a danger. Perhaps they have always known, and we have simply been slow to learn it of them.

The heron is solitary. It comes to the marsh at dusk and stands in the shallows with one leg drawn up, the way herons do, and it waits. I have watched it for an hour, motionless, while the light turned copper and the dragonflies dimmed and the air cooled and the moss on every surface began to drip with the evening's condensation. It does not look at me. It is interested only in the water and what moves in it. When it strikes, the motion is too fast to follow — a blur of gray and a splash and then a fish, silver in the heron's beak, thrashing until it stills. The heron lifts its head and swallows, and then it stands again, waiting, and the waiting is the larger part of its life.

The crows are different. They are interested in everything. They watch from the high elms and call to each other in voices that carry through the valley, and they follow the foxes sometimes, and they investigate the garden plots when the vegetables are ripe. They are not pests; they are residents, with their own claims on the place. In the mornings, the sound of them — the calls and the wing-beats and the rustle of leaves disturbed — is part of the silence. I would miss them if they left. I would not know what to listen for.

---

The plants are not the background. The plants are the people of this place, in some sense — the oldest residents, the ones who were here before the mill and will be here long after the last of us is gone.

The elm in the textile hall is the eldest, I think. No one living remembers when it was planted, or whether it was planted at all. Perhaps a bird dropped a seed four centuries ago, when the mill was new and the river still turned gears, and the elm grew slowly in the crack of a wall, and when the wall fell, the elm remained. Now it is the column of the building, the pillar that holds up the shadow of a roof, and its roots have cracked the remaining walls apart, and its branches fill the space where looms once stood. I put my hand on its bark and I feel the age of it, the slow patience of wood. It will outlast the bricks. It will outlast the moss. It will become, in time, the only shape left, and then it will fall, and its fallen trunk will become a hill, and the foxes will den in it, and the children will climb it, and new trees will grow from its decay.

This is not a metaphor. This is simply what happens here.

The fig in the church is younger — perhaps a hundred years, perhaps less. It has overtaken the altar and the nave and half the tower, its roots spreading through the floor like veins, thickening where they meet stone, branching where they meet light. The residents have made peace with it. They have not tried to prune it back, because they understand that pruning is not the way here — the way is to guide, to train, to accept. The fig will have the church. In return, the church has become something more than a church. On summer evenings, when the light comes through the leaves and falls on the stone pews — still here, still stone, though moss has softened their edges — the space is like no other space I know. My daughter calls it the green light. She goes there when she is upset and she sits on the moss-covered pew and looks up at the canopy through the open roof and she comes back calm. I have done the same. The fig does not speak but it holds. This is enough.

The willow arch is newer, younger than my grandmother's memory. It grew from a cutting placed in the soil of one courtyard and trained across to the other, year by year, its branches guided along a wooden frame until they rooted on the far side. Now the frame is gone, rotted away, and the willow is a gate — a living gate, green in summer, bare in winter, always moving faintly in the wind that funnels through the valley. Everyone who lives here has walked through it thousands of times. It is the threshold between the lower quarter and the upper, between the marsh and the gardens, between one state of the place and another. I think of it as a kind of punctuation. You pass through and the sentence changes.

---

What else lives here?

Fungi, of course. They are everywhere the damp reaches, which is everywhere. Shelves of orange on the fallen oak. White bloom on the bark of the ash by my door. Black tongues on the underside of the elm's fallen branches. They are not obtrusive — they do not announce themselves — but they are present, part of the color of the place, the way the green of the moss is part of the color of the stone. Without them, the decay would not happen. Without decay, there would be no soil. Without soil, no fig in the church, no willow arch, no moss to glow in the morning light. We owe them a debt we cannot repay.

Insects beyond counting. Gnats in the shafts of light. Beetles in the rotting wood, their shells dark and wet with the moisture that never quite leaves. Dragonflies in the marsh, their wings catching the copper light, their bodies iridescent for a moment before they are gone. Moths at night, thumping against the glass of the lantern, their dust on the panes by morning. Spiders in every corner, their webs beaded with dew, catching the light when the sun finds them. We share the space with them, these small lives, and we do not think about it, or we think about it the way we think about breathing — not at all, until we are asked to.

The water lives here too, in its way. It is not an inhabitant but it is present, the way the air is present, the way the light is present. It comes from the springs in the hills above and it passes through the settlement in channels and pipes and seeps and rills, and it goes into the ground and comes up again in the marsh and finally, finally, finds its way to the river that is no longer a river, that is now a damp, a trace, a memory of what water can do when it is not interrupted. The waterwheel turns with it. The generator hums. The batteries charge. The people of Caldtow wash and drink and water their gardens, and the water thanks us for nothing, but it stays, because the valley holds it, because the moss sponges it, because the stones weep and the soil drinks and the roots reach for it with their small, slow hunger.

---

There is a way of being here that I cannot explain to someone who has not lived it.

It is not peace, exactly, though there is peace in it. It is not slowness, though everything moves slowly here — the growth, the decay, the sound of the water, the drip of condensation, the creak of the trees in a wind that is always gentler than it should be. It is something else. A quality of attention. A habit of noticing. You stand in a courtyard in the morning, when the fog is thick and you cannot see the edges of the settlement, and you hear the water beneath the moss, and you feel the cool damp on your skin, and you notice the green — not as color but as presence, not as backdrop but as actor, as resident, as something with a stake in the place. And for a moment, just a moment, you are not separate from it. The boundary that you carry with you, the boundary between self and world, between observer and observed — it softens. It becomes moss. It becomes ground.

This is what lives here: the softening. The wearing-away of edges. The patient conversation between what was built and what grows, which has become so long and so quiet that we can no longer hear the words, only the rhythm, the beat of it, the pulse of water beneath moss that is always there if you listen, that you stop noticing and then notice again, and each time it surprises you, like a heartbeat you forgot was there.

The fog will lift. The moss will glow. The waterwheel will turn. The fig will open its leaves to the light.

And I will be here, and it will be here, and we will not know where one of us ends and the other begins, and this is not a metaphor.

This is simply what happens in Caldtow, where the settlement has learned to grow, and the growth has learned to stay.
