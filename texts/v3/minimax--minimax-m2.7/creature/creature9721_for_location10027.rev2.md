The plants have names here, or had names once. The settlers called the great root-trees *welcomes*, because their branches reached inward toward any light source, pulling it closer, cradling it. Now the lights are the gas-jets behind their algae, and the welcomes still strain toward them, their leaves turned broad and pale, their bark smoothed by centuries of reaching. You can press your palm against a welcome's trunk and feel the slow pulse of growth beneath — not a heartbeat, nothing so rhythmic, but a pressure, a presence, the weight of a thing that has been alive for longer than you will ever be.

Between the welcomes, the runners have woven themselves. These are the vines, the *paths*, as the old settlers called them, because you can still walk where they tell you to walk. The runners grow fast in the wet, putting down roots every few feet, anchoring themselves to stone and to each other. They have flowers — small, white, scentless — that open at dusk or in the presence of carbon dioxide, a slow blooming that no one sees because there is no one to see it. The flowers produce something like fruit, small pale pods, but the pods are dry, and nothing eats them, and they fall and rot and become the next generation of runners, growing in the same paths, reinforcing the same routes. A garden that maintains itself, that tends itself, that has forgotten why it makes these shapes and simply continues.

Along the walls, the moss has become something else. Where it would be moss on the surface of the world — a thin green skin, easily scraped away — here it has thickened, has differentiated. It has become a felt, a leather, a skin over the stone that breathes. You can see, if you look closely, that the moss has layers: the outer edge pale and damp, the middle green-black and dense, the inner edge where it touches stone turned white and brittle, dissolving slowly into the rock. The stone here is not stone so much as a record, layers of its own history compressed into something solid, and the moss is reading it, is translating it, is returning it to soil one atom at a time.

In the pools — the places where water gathers, where the weeping walls have carved small basins in the stone — there are things that are not plants. They are not animals either. The settlers might have called them algae, but they are more than algae: they are colonies, they are communities, they are decisions. A pool might be green, or it might be silver, or it might be a red so deep it looks like blood. The color changes over days, over weeks, following patterns you cannot parse but that feel, when you watch them, like language. The water here has memory, or intention, or both. It gathers in the places where it is needed. It withdraws from places where the roots have grown too thick, where the stone no longer needs its softness. The water is not alive the way the plants are alive, but it is not dead either. It is the medium, the condition, the argument that everything else is having with itself.

And there is the rot.

The Undergarden is not clean. Beneath the green, beneath the living architecture, there is a stratum of decomposition that has its own presence. Fungi have made themselves here in forms that the surface world would not recognize. They do not fruit in the open; they fruit in the dark, in the hollows of dead roots, in the spaces where a welcome has died back and left a cathedral of fiber. Their caps are small and pale, almost colorless, their gills lined with spores that drift upward in the still air and settle on the stones and wait. The fungi are not separate from the plants. The plants are not separate from the fungi. The roots of the welcomes are threaded with mycelium, the runners are anchored by fungal networks, the moss is more fungus than plant. Everything here grows together, feeds together, becomes each other in slow cycles of death and absorption that have no beginning and no end.

But something else lives here. Something you feel before you see.

In the deep places — the corners where the steam-pump's breath does not reach, the roots where no light has ever fallen — there is a darkness that is not the absence of light but the presence of something else. It moves. It is patient. It is the oldest thing here, perhaps older than the settlement, perhaps the reason the settlement was built in this place. The old records, the ones you read before descending, spoke of "the gardener" — a presence the first settlers found here, a presence they thought they understood and shaped and used. They were wrong. The gardener is still here. It is the water. It is the roots. It is the slow grammar of growth and decay that writes and rewrites the Undergarden in every century. It does not think, or if it thinks, it thinks in centuries. It does not love, or if it loves, it loves stone and darkness and the patient pulse of water through rock. When you walk through the paths, you are walking through its body. When you breathe the warm damp air, you are breathing its breath.

It does not notice you. You are too small, too brief, too temporary to register.

But you are here. And that is enough. The Undergarden has room for you, too.
