# The People of Still Water

They move through the town the way water moves through it—slowly, with patience, following the paths that have been worn smooth by generations before. There is no hurry in Still Water, and so there is no hurry in the people. They walk at a pace that lets them notice the seeps and the damp places, that lets them feel the cool air and hear the bell before it sounds. A visitor might mistake this for lethargy, but it is something else: a recognition that time here does not demand haste. The plateau has its own rhythm, and the people have learned to walk within it.

They are not numerous. You would not count more than a few hundred if you gathered them in the market square, and the square would not feel crowded. But they are enough. They fill the streets with presence without filling them with noise. When they speak, it is quietly; when they laugh, it is without the sharpness of urgency. Their voices carry differently in this thin air—thin as the light, clear as the sound of the bell—so that a conversation across a courtyard is easy, but one across a street is an effort. They have learned to be near one another, to gather in the spaces that are small, to let their bodies rather than their voices do the work of being together.

The faces are weathered in the way that stone is weathered—not worn away, but shaped. Lines form around the eyes from squinting against the light that reflects off the plateau. The skin carries a particular quality of dryness, as if the air has drawn something out of it over years, leaving behind a quiet resilience. Hair turns gray early here, and stays gray, and those who are old are not ashamed of their age. There is no rush toward youth. The elders sit in the doorways in the afternoon, watching the shadows move across the paving stones, and they are part of the architecture of the place as much as the walls themselves. Children grow up in their company, learning the town's rhythms before they learn its words, and the boundary between the generations is permeable—a child might spend a morning with an old man who remembers when the fountain still ran, hearing a story she will tell in her own old age.

They have the hands of people who work with stone and earth and water. The joints are thick from gripping tools; the fingers are scarred in small ways from chisels and knives and the iron nails that hold the beams. Calluses form where the skin meets the loom and the spindle, where the palms press against the handles of the shovels that clear the drainage channels each spring. These hands are patient. They do not rush. A mason might spend a day on a single stone, fitting it to its neighbor, and consider the work complete. A weaver might pull threads through the loom one by one, watching the pattern emerge with the slowness of a season. The work is not spectacle; it is practice, and practice is enough.

The women of Still Water carry water from the deep well at the town's heart, and they have done so for generations. The well is ancient—older than any living memory, older than the walls that ring the town—and its water rises from somewhere far below the plateau, cold and mineral-tasting, the same water that seeps in the joints and beads on the stone. The women walk bent under the weight of what they carry, but they do not hurry. They move along the drainage channels where the footing is sure, where generations have walked before them, and they greet one another with a nod or a word, and the word is enough. There is a ritual to this—water from the well, drawn at dawn, carried to the houses where it will be used for cooking and cleaning and the small ceremonies of the body—but it is not performed as ritual. It is simply life, the way breathing is life, the way the bell at midday is life.

The men work the terraces above the town, where the soil is thin and the wind is steady. They grow what will grow: hardy grains, root vegetables, the small hardy greens that push up through the cold soil. They have built the terraces over centuries, stone by stone, each generation adding a little higher, and the walls of those terraces are as old as anything in the town below. The work is constant and quiet—the turning of soil, the clearing of stones, the mending of walls that the winter freeze has cracked. They do not speak much while they work. The wind speaks enough. But when they return at dusk, they carry the smell of the earth with them, and that smell mixes with the smoke that rises from the chimneys, and the town takes on its evening quality, its closing-in, its readiness for the night.

The smith works near the southern gate, where the road from the lowlands arrives. His forge glows in the early morning and again at dusk, and the sound of his hammer carries through the streets like a heartbeat. He makes what is needed: nails, hinges, the small iron fittings that hold the wooden beams to the stone. He does not make art. He makes endurance. The things he makes will outlast him, will be used by hands not yet born, and this is not a burden to him but a simple fact. The anvil knows his weight. The forge knows his fire. He works until he cannot work, and then his son takes up the hammer, or his daughter, or the child of a neighbor who has no smith of her own. The craft passes as the terraces pass, as the walls pass, as the water from the well passes from hand to hand. There is no urgency in the passing. There is only the passing itself, and the hands that receive what is given.

The bell is rung by whoever tends the temple. This is not a priestly office, exactly—there is no hierarchy of spirit here, no theology that demands its elaboration. The temple is plain stone, its door open in the day and closed at night, and those who enter do so quietly, without ceremony. They light a candle, or they sit in the silence, or they simply stand and leave. But the bell is rung. This is fixed. At dawn, a figure climbs the stairs to the tower—sometimes a woman, sometimes a man, sometimes a child who has been given this task by an elder—and pulls the rope, and the sound rolls out across the plateau, carrying to the terraces and the fields and the road beyond the gate. The bell does not ask who rings it. It only waits, and speaks when spoken, and the people of Still Water hear it, and begin their day.

Children are few. This is the nature of a town that does not grow, that holds itself in balance. But children are cherished—not with the frenzy of elsewhere, but with a steady attention, a watchfulness. They are allowed to run in the streets, to climb the low walls, to sit by the well and drop pebbles into its darkness and listen for the echo. They learn the bell before they learn to read, learn the paths before they learn to write, learn the sound of their own voices in the market square where a conversation three streets away is still audible if the wind is right. They grow slowly in Still Water, as everything grows slowly here. They do not hurry toward adulthood, and when they reach it, they do not hurry toward age. Time is the one thing the town does not lack.

And there are those who come. The gates open, and sometimes strangers arrive—travelers from the lowlands, merchants with carts, someone seeking a place that does not change. The people of Still Water do not turn them away, but they do not rush to embrace them either. They watch. They let the stranger walk the streets, let them feel the seep and the damp and the cool, let them hear the bell and see the stone. Some strangers leave within a day, finding the quiet oppressive, the climate too harsh, the pace too slow. But some stay. They find a place in a household, or a space in a street, and they learn the work, and they learn the bell, and after a year or a decade, they are no longer strangers. They are part of the permanence. They have become Still Water.

---

What lives in Still Water is a people shaped by the place that shaped them. They are not large, not loud, not in a hurry. They are patient and practical and quiet. They have learned to fit themselves to the stone, to the damp, to the cold that never quite rises to warmth. They are not content exactly—there is no striving here, no reaching—but they are settled, rooted, held. When an outsider looks at them, they may seem slow, may seem still, may seem like people who have stopped. But they have not stopped. They have only found a pace that matches the stone, a rhythm that matches the bell, a way of being that does not need to change because it has already become what it was meant to be. The town is a fixed point, and so are they. They are the human part of the plateau's permanence, the voices that answer the bell, the hands that carry the water and mend the walls and tend the terraces. They live in Still Water, and Still Water lives in them.
