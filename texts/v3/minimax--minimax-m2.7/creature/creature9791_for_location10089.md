The birds have names you will never learn. You find them later in books, or you don't, because the names come from elsewhere and the birds are from here, and what a name does to a thing that lives in weeping stone and rising steam is not capture but diminish. The largest ones—they are not eagles but something smaller, darker, with a wingspan built for riding the valley's breath and not much else—keep to the cliff edges. They nest in crevices high above the marsh, in places the water hasn't reached yet, their entrances screened by curtains of moss so thick it hangs like fur. You find one nest by accident: a bowl of woven reeds, darker than the surrounding moss, and inside it three eggs the color of dried blood. The adult bird watches you from a nearby ledge, head turned sideways so one eye keeps you in focus, and does not move, does not call, does not need you to go. It simply waits for you to finish being what you are and return to whatever you were doing before you climbed eight thousand feet to interrupt the machinery of its body.

Lower, in the scrub that clings to the cliff faces, small birds move in quick flurries—tits and wrens and things between, their plumage the color of wet stone, their calls a high thin noise the mist drinks. They do not fly so much as launch themselves from perch to perch, never staying long, their bodies metabolizing the warmth with an efficiency that makes them constant motion in a place that otherwise moves slowly. They eat insects that are themselves eating things smaller, and so on down into the silt, where the rot begins and the bacteria begin and the whole wet chain of being feeds itself without assistance. The small birds do not sing so much as tick—a percussive, mechanical sound, like something testing whether the air is still there.

The marsh holds its own population. The creature with the dark head—if you are patient, if you find a position downwind and wait—the creature with the dark head surfaces again and again, and each time you see more. It is an amphibian, or something that used to be one, its lineage so ancient the word *amphibian* may no longer apply. It is large, the size of a dog's head or larger, and its skin is the color of the water itself—green-black, mottled with the purple of algae, so well camouflaged that it becomes visible only through motion, through the displacement it causes. It surfaces to breathe, a process so slow you can watch the air intake: the nostrils breaking the surface, the pause, the slow deflation, the submerging that seems to take longer than the surfacing did. It is patient in the way the water is patient, the way the stone is patient. It has been breathing this same air for longer than you have been anything.

Around the pools, things you cannot see move in the water. The surface-skins of algae tremble when nothing trembles them, and if you look closely—kneeling at the edge, your face close enough to see the condensation on your own skin reflected in the dark—you see the insects. They are not on the surface but just beneath it, suspended in the first inch of water, their legs pressed against the tension of the surface from below. They are the size of fingernails, their bodies jeweled with iridescence that catches what light reaches this deep, and they pulse there, dozens of them, hundreds, suspended in a medium that is their air and their ocean and their entire world. They do not swim. They simply exist in the boundary, and the boundary holds them, and they have no reason to leave it.

Larger things move through the marsh at night. You hear them—the splash that is not a bird, the slow displacement that parts the reeds and does not hurry to close again. You never see them clearly, only their evidence: paths through the vegetation, flattened where a body has pressed through, muds that hold the imprint of something heavy. The paths converge on the water, where the warmth is greatest, and you realize these creatures come to feed, or to warm themselves, or to do something you cannot imagine. Their prints are broad, five-toed, and the claws are long—the marks they leave are deep in the silt, deep enough that water does not fill them in for hours. Whatever they are, they are heavy. Their coats, when they pass close enough for you to glimpse, are dark and thick and matted with moisture, and their eyes—you see one eye, once, a glint of amber in the darkness—catch the light the way a cat's catches it, reflecting back what little there is.

In the cliffs, there are creatures that never come down. You find their sign everywhere: droppings on ledges, scratches on stone, the worn paths where generations have traveled the same route so many times the rock polishes smooth. They are small—you hear them more than see them, the chittering in the crevices, the quick scuttle of something startled. They are the valley's permanent residents, adapted to its warmth, its wet, its constant damp. Their fur is slick, almost oily, and their eyes are reduced, unnecessary in the darkness of the rock. They navigate by sound and by the heat-sense that has replaced their sight, and they live in colonies so dense you can smell them before you see them—musk and warmth and the mineral tang of the stone they live in. They are not rodents, not quite, though they fill a similar niche. They have been here long enough to have become something else, something the valley has shaped from the raw material of mammal into a form that fits its needs exactly. They do not look up when you pass. They do not know you are there.

The steam vents have their own fauna. Around the hottest vents—the ones that pulse with the deepest rhythm, the hum you feel in your teeth—nothing lives directly. The stone is too hot for flesh, too alkaline, too changed by the chemistry of heat. But a foot away, in the ring of altered ground where the heat begins to moderate, life returns. Mosses first, then lichens in impossible colors—yellows and oranges and a green so bright it seems to glow—and then small creatures that eat the mosses, and then larger creatures that eat those, until the vent becomes the center of a small economy, a warm circle of being in a cold world. The rhythm of the vent controls the rhythm of these lives: when the output swells, the animals retreat to the outer rings; when it falls, they press closer. The vent does not know it is feeding them. The animals do not know they are responding to a pulse from below. But the coordination is perfect, has been perfect for so long that no one remembers it being otherwise.

What you notice, finally, is the absence of urgency. The creatures here do not rush. The birds circle without destination. The marsh-creature surfaces and submerges at its own pace. The things in the night move through the reeds with a slowness that is not caution but preference. The heat has made time irrelevant, or rather has made it so abundant that nothing needs to grasp at it. Everything here has been doing the same thing for so long that the doing has become its own reward, has become the point, has replaced whatever purpose brought them here in the first place. The birds' ancestors learned to ride these thermals, and now they ride them because riding them is what they do. The amphibian's ancestors learned to breathe this warm wet air, and now breathing is all there is. The machines are running, and the machines are the creatures, and the creatures are the valley, and no one is keeping score.

You are the only one counting. The only one waiting for something to happen. The only one who arrived and will leave and considers these arrivals and departures events rather than the small, insignificant motions they are. The valley does not know you are here. It will not know when you are gone. The water weeps the same way whether you watch or not. The steam rises the same way whether you breathe it or not. The creatures move through their ancient patterns, and when you are gone, those patterns continue, and continue, and do not even notice the space where you used to stand.
