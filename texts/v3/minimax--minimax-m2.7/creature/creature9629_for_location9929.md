What lives here is patient and small. The seeds arrived without intention — carried by wind that had crossed distances long enough to exhaust whatever purpose once moved it, deposited in the thin seams where two planes meet and water gathers long enough to pause before moving on. They took hold in the only way available to them: slowly, deeply, following the seam downward as far as the seam allowed, which is not far, but it is enough. The roots are thin and wiry and they do not seek. They simply follow the faintest gradient of moisture through the composite, through the microscopic spaces between manufactured particles, finding the water that weeps upward from somewhere in the infrastructure's depths. It is not a generous life. The plants that result are stunted and sparse, holding their small leaves close to the ground to conserve what little they have, turning their faces away from the worst of the heat. But they persist. Season by season, pulse by pulse of moisture, they hold their place on a surface that was never meant to host them. They are the only evidence that this place is not entirely finished, that something has arrived and chosen to remain.

What lives here also lives between the pulses. In the hours when the moisture comes and the composite weeps and every channel fills with shallow, still water that glows copper under the light, something stirs in the pooling. It is not visible. It does not rise to the surface or leave tracks in the soft mud. But it is there — a thin film of microbial life, or something like it, cycling through the water as it gathers and evaporates. When the pools shrink and the composite dries, what lives there contracts into the seams, into the microscopic spaces between particles, and waits. It has no name. It does not move in any way that can be seen. But it is present, and it is patient, and it has been here longer than the plants, or perhaps it arrived with them, or perhaps the plants carry it with them now, root to leaf, a partnership of small-scale persistence.

What lives here is the infrastructure itself. The channels were cut to move water, but they do more than move. They hold. They weep. They release moisture in pulses that suggest a rhythm, a logic, a slow heartbeat deep beneath the composite planes. The reservoirs sealed into the structure are not inert. They cycle. They respond to heat and pressure and the slow changes in temperature that mark the passage of time here — time that was not designed into the system but that the system has learned anyway. When the moisture comes, it comes because the infrastructure has decided it is time, and when it stops, it stops because the balance has tipped, and somewhere in the deep geometry of sealed channels and engineered passages, water finds its way to places the original builders may not have mapped. The hum is not mechanical. It is the sound of this process — the faint, constant vibration of water moving through material, of heat conducting through composite, of a system that was meant to be static but that has become something else. The hum is a life of a kind. It does not think. It does not feel. But it breathes, in its own way, and it has not stopped.

The structures live too. They rise from the composite in simple forms — walls, platforms, columns — and they cast their shadows with perfect clarity because nothing softens them. The material they are made from is the same as the plain, or nearly the same, but they were shaped differently, placed differently, given a form that suggests intention even if the intention has been lost. What they were meant for is not known and may never have been known. But they stand. They endure the heat and the pulses of moisture and the long, slow process of erosion that has barely begun. They cast their shadows and they wait. Perhaps they are waiting for something to return. Perhaps they are waiting for nothing. But waiting is a form of living, and they have been doing it longer than anything else here.

There is life in the erosion too, though it is not the kind that moves. The composite is learning to soften. The edges that were crisp are beginning to round. The places where water crosses the same path repeatedly have developed a slight gloss, a faint shine, as though the material is learning to be wet, to hold moisture longer, to become something other than what it was designed to be. This is slow life — the life of change, of transformation, of a surface that was made to be permanent and that is slowly discovering it is not. The water does not carve here the way it carves in natural landscapes. It only touches, and touches again, and over time the touching becomes something else. The planet is still learning how to wear itself down. But it is learning. And learning is a kind of life.

What lives here is also the wind. It is not alive in the way a plant is alive, or the way the hum is alive, but it is present and it acts and it shapes. It crosses the planes without obstacle, uninterrupted for kilometers in every direction, and it carries dust — not much, but some, enough to leave a faint residue in the low channels where water has already been. The dust settles in layers so thin they are almost invisible, but they accumulate. They become soil, of a kind, in the places where water pools long enough to keep them damp. And in those places, the seeds find purchase more easily. The wind has carried them all — the dust, the seeds, the microbial life that stirs in the pooling water — and the wind will continue to carry them, moving them from place to place across a surface that has no natural contours to stop them. The wind is not alive, but it is a force, and forces can be a kind of life too.

The moisture lives. This is not a small thing. The water that arrives in pulses, that beads on every surface, that fills the channels with shallow sheets that glow copper — it is alive in the way that water is alive wherever it moves and changes and gathers and releases. It arrives from deep in the infrastructure, from condensation channels and sealed reservoirs, and it does not arrive accidentally. There is a rhythm to it, a pulse that suggests a cycle, and the cycle suggests a system, and the system suggests a kind of intention even if the intention is not conscious. The water comes because the world was built to return water to its surface, or because the world has learned to do so on its own. Either way, the moisture is here, and it moves, and it sustains what lives.

There are no animals. But there are shadows, and shadows move with the sun, and at night the shadows lengthen and then disappear, and in the morning they return. There are the stars, which do not live but which are present, indifferent, offering nothing and asking nothing. There is the heat, which presses down without pause, which shapes everything, which makes the moisture possible and the persistence of the plants possible and the slow learning of the composite possible. The heat is a presence. It is not alive, but it is here, and it has been here long enough that everything else has adapted to it or died or found a way to hold on in the thin seams where water lingers.

What lives here is small and patient and unassuming. It does not announce itself. It does not move in ways that can be easily seen. But it persists, and it adapts, and it grows, in the places where the pulses of moisture have created the conditions for growth. The infrastructure hums its slow breath. The composite softens at its edges. The structures wait. The vegetation holds on in the seams. And beneath it all, in the microscopic spaces between particles, in the shallow pools that gather and evaporate, in the deep channels that move water from somewhere to somewhere else, something is alive — not as a single thing but as a condition, a process, a way of continuing.

This is what lives here.
