There is a rat that lives in the channels. Not a city rat, not a barn rat — something older, fur the color of the stone when the stone is wet, when the fog has pressed itself into every crevice and the plateau is darker than it usually allows itself to be. Its body is compact, almost spherical, and it moves through the channels with the certainty of a thing that has been doing this for longer than anyone remembers. It drinks from the thin trickle that runs along the channel's shallowest groove, where the water gathers after the fog has given what it can, and it does not hurry. Nothing here hurries.

The rat does not fear the people. It has learned the schedule of the settlement — the hours when the channels are quiet, the hours when feet pass close, the hours when water is being drawn and the flow becomes busy and complicated. It knows the sound of the channel-sweepers, the soft whisk of their brooms against stone, and it knows the different pressure of those brooms — the one who pushes debris toward the gutters rather than away, the one who works from the left side and leaves a corner damp, where the water gathers in a small dark pool that the rat finds useful. It has learned the settlement the way the settlement has learned the plateau: slowly, through repetition, through the accumulation of small adaptations that add up, over generations, to something that looks like knowledge but is really just survival made habitual.

In the morning, before the sun clears the eastern ridge, the vines bloom. They are pale — not white, not yellow, but something in between, a color that seems to glow from within rather than reflect from without. They open in the dark, at the moment when the fog is thickest and the air is so wet that a breath drawn too sharply will fill your lungs with cold rather than air. The blossoms last perhaps an hour. They are already curling at their edges by the time the light comes, their petals contracting in the thin dry air that follows the fog's retreat, their brief life collapsed into a brown twist that the wind takes or that clings to the stone depending on where it falls. The people call these vines by a name that translates roughly to *the ones who do not stay*, and they do not pick the flowers. They are not beautiful in the way that beauty is usually understood here. They are more like a kind of testimony — a record of something happening, witnessed by no one, witnessed by the plateau itself which does not witness in the way that consciousness witnesses but which holds the event all the same, folded into the stone like a fossil of light.

The birds are loud in the early part of the day. They nest in the crevices of the old cliffs, the ones that are not part of the settlement but adjacent to it, the places where the plateau's ancient eroded face offers crevices deep enough for shelter, high enough for threat to pass below. The hawks are the most visible — they ride the wind that pours over the southern ridge and use it like a river, tilting into it, letting it carry them along the plateau's edge, their wings locked, their shadows falling across the stone fields in long dark ellipses that travel faster than the birds themselves. They hunt by the channels sometimes, though the channels belong to the rat, and the hawks and the rat have reached an understanding that is not peace but simply the absence of active conflict: the rat stays low and still when the hawk is near, and the hawk has learned that the rat is not worth the effort of a dive, that the effort of climbing back to the right altitude after a low hunt costs more than the meal would provide. The hawks eat what they can take easily. They are practical in the way that all creatures here are practical — not through any philosophy of restraint, but because the plateau demands it.

Below the hawks, in the smaller air close to the stone, there are other birds. Small things, brown and grey and quick, that move through the settlement in pairs or singly, never in flocks. They eat the insects, which are always there — narrow-bodied, dark-shelled, patient in the way that insects are patient, their bodies adapted to lose water slowly, to survive in air that would desiccate a creature with less discipline. The insects live in the gardens mostly, in the spaces between the plants where the soil is dark and holds moisture a little longer than the surrounding stone. They do not swarm. They do not need to. There are always enough of them, which is to say there are never many, but their presence is constant enough that the settlement has organized itself around them — around the sound of their humming, which is audible only when the wind dies, and around the fact that they are food for the birds, which are food for something else, which is food for something else still, in a chain that the people understand without having ever articulated it.

At night, the thing comes. The people have a name for it, but the name is not a name — it is more like a description, a long phrase that means roughly *the one that belongs to the dark side of the wall*. It moves through the settlement in the hours between the last fire being lit and the first fire being rekindled, when the settlement is neither awake nor asleep but somewhere in the grey country between. It is the size of a small dog, though it is not a dog, and its fur is the color of the night-stone, dark and dense and designed for warmth in an environment where warmth is always escaping. Its legs are long for its body — not long in the way of a gazelle, but long in the way of something that moves through spaces that are shaped for movement, through the gaps between walls, through the channels when they are dry, through the narrow paths between buildings that are barely wide enough for a human hand. It eats what it finds. The eggs from the cliff-nests, when it can reach them, which it can in the parts of the cliff that are closest to the settlement, where the birds have learned to nest higher than the creature can climb, so that the eggs are safe and the creature must find other food and the hawks and the small brown birds and the insect world remain in a balance that no one intended and no one controls.

The people and the creature have an understanding that is older than language. They do not see each other as threats because they have never been threats to each other, and the plateau has trained them both in the economy of not wasting energy on conflict. The creature does not enter buildings. It is not afraid of the people — it has heard them, smelled them, felt the vibrations of their footsteps through the stone for so many generations that it has learned to place them in a category that is separate from the categories of food and threat, a category that is simply *known*. And the people do not hunt it because it does not harm them, because it keeps the rodent population in check, because it has always been here and to hunt it would be to hunt something that belongs to the plateau as surely as the stone belongs to the plateau, as surely as the fog belongs to the plateau.

The trees are the slowest things. They grow in the courtyards, in the deeper ravines, in the places where the walls have settled and cracked and created pockets of soil that collect and hold what little water falls. They are not large. They will never be large. Their wood is dense, dark, slow-grown, the rings pressed together so tightly that the grain is nearly invisible. They bend in the wind not because they are weak but because they have learned that resistance is futile and adaptation is the only strategy that works. Their roots are deep — deeper than anyone has ever mapped, deeper than the stone's surface would suggest is possible, reaching through cracks and fissures into the hidden places where water moves in quantities too small for anyone to see but too persistent for anything to stop. The trees are not beautiful. They are not interesting. They are simply there, decade after decade, their branches growing longer and more horizontal, their trunks thickening in the wrong direction, their leaves tough and small and designed for a life of wanting.

Between the trees and the ground there is a layer of things that are not quite plants and not quite soil — mosses that cling to the north-facing walls where the sun does not reach, lichens that spread across the old stone in pale orange and grey-green maps, films of life so thin and so slow that they are only visible when you are standing very close and the light is coming from the right direction. They do not bloom. They do not seed in any way that can be seen. They simply persist, growing by fractions, their presence a record of moisture and time and the patient accumulation of biological matter in a place where biological matter is the rarest commodity.

In the gardens, the plants are what the people have made them. Not through genetic engineering in any modern sense, but through the simple, brutal practice of saving seed from the plants that survived, year after year, cycle after cycle, until the survivors were recognizable as something distinct from their lowland ancestors. The leaves are waxy. The roots are deep. The plants grow slowly and fruit sparingly and they are tough enough to survive a week of dry wind and tender enough to respond when the fog comes thick and the channels fill and the world briefly becomes something other than what it usually is. The people tend them in the early morning, before the wind picks up, their hands quick and precise, their movements shaped by generations of work in air that makes you breathless. They do not speak much while they work. The work does not require speaking. The work requires breath, and breath is the one thing the plateau gives only reluctantly.

And there are the people themselves, who are also part of this plateau, who have adapted in the ways that adaptation happens — broad chests for the thin air, slow speech because fast speech uses more oxygen, skin that is permanently weathered, eyes that squint not from weakness but from habit, hands that know stone the way hands know wood in other places, that know the weight of a stone and the fit of one stone against another without needing to think about it. They are not numerous. They do not need to be. The plateau does not support numbers; it supports sufficiency, and sufficiency is what they have achieved, in the way that a bird achieves it, in the way that the rat in the channel achieves it, in the way that the vines achieve it in the pre-dawn dark, opening for an hour and folding back and trusting that the plateau will be here tomorrow, and the day after, and the day after that, and that the fog will rise again, as it always does, from the unseen distant sea, patient as stone, climbing the broken tooth of the plateau to give what little it has to give.
