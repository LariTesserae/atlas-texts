# What Lives in Kel Suarath

What lives in Kel Suarath is not what you would find. This is the first thing to understand. The drowned plateau does not host—it persists. It does not sustain—it continues. What lives here lives in the way that stone lives, in the way that water lives, in the way that centuries live when no one counts them.

---

The forests live. This must be said first, because it is the truest thing, and because it troubles the mind to consider. The trees that rise from the submerged floor—those black-barked giants with their torn-cloth leaves—do not simply grow. Growth is too simple a word for what they do. They *persist in growing*. They do not seem to age, or if they age, the aging does not manifest in the ways that aging normally does. There is no hollowing, no decay, no structural failure from within. The wood does not rot where it stands, though fallen branches and shed leaves accumulate on the floor and are not consumed, are not broken down, are simply covered by the slow silt of dead growth falling on dead growth, building a floor of pale matter that the living trees rise from like islands themselves.

The roots are not where you would expect roots to be. They are not in the soil—they *are* the soil. The pale matter that carpets the flooded floor is root-deep, interlocked with root, a substrate that the trees have slowly made from their own shed selves over centuries uncounted. The trees stand in the remains of themselves. They are rooted in their own dead. And this is not metaphor here, not poetry—this is the literal condition of their existence. They have made the ground they stand on, and they continue to make it, and there is nothing to stop them.

The fronds of the submerged meadows sway. They sway toward the light that comes from below as often as above, reaching for a sun they cannot see, straining toward a surface that is forty feet of dark water above them. They bend in the slow current, but they also bend when there is no current—a slow oscillation, a periodic flexing, as though they are breathing, as though they are answering some signal that travels not through water but through the shared substrate of fallen matter, through the network of pale roots that connects them all.

This network is worth lingering on. Beneath the forests, beneath the accumulated dead, beneath the silt of centuries—beneath all of this there is something that might be called a mycelium, though it is not mushroom-forming, not sporing, not doing any of the things that fungal life typically does. It is a threadwork, a connective tissue, a nervous system that is not nervous because it does not feel but that is somehow still *alive in some other way*. It connects the trees to each other. It connects the ferns to the moss. It connects the islands to the floor. It is the thing that says *grow* to everything it touches, and everything it touches grows, and the thing that says *grow* never says anything else, and so everything grows, always, forever, until the water takes it.

Is this alive? The question feels wrong to ask here. The question assumes a boundary between living and not-living that this place no longer observes. The water moves. The water has moved for so long that it has worn stone smooth, that it has pressed channels through ridgelines, that it has shaped the very floor it fills. Is the water alive? It breathes—the locals had a word for this, *the breathing of the old place*. And breathing is what living things do. And the water breathes, and the water lives, and the water is what lives here most completely, most constantly, most in accord with its own nature.

---

But there is something beneath the water. This is the other thing to understand. The plateau is not simply drowned; it is *occupied*. Not by bodies, not by minds in any way you would recognize, but by something that has its own way of being present, its own way of filling a space.

What is it? The question is unanswerable in the terms the place provides. It is not a creature, because it has no edges. It is not a force, because it has preferences, or what might be called preferences—the way the current strengthens here but not there, the way the algae blooms brighter in some channels than in others, the way the trees grow thicker on some slopes and thinner on others as though something is choosing, is distributing, is *attending*. It is not a god, because it does not ask for worship. It does not speak. It does not seem to know that there is anything to speak to.

It dreams.

This is the only word that fits. Beneath the floor, in the stone itself, in whatever medium stone becomes when it has been pressed on by water and darkness and slow time—something dreams. The dreaming stirs the water. The water stirs the plants. The plants make sounds that are not quite sounds, a rustling that is not quite rustling, a whisper that is not quite whisper, the suggestion of speech in a language that has no speakers, that has never had speakers, that exists only in the way that longing exists in empty rooms.

The dream does not seem to be about anything. Or if it is, the meaning of it has been lost so completely that it no longer matters. It is just dreaming. It is just the act of dreaming, continued and continued, without waking, without end. And the dreaming is the life that lives here—not the trees, not the water, not even the mycelium that threads through everything—but the dream beneath, the dreaming that the plateau does, the slow unconscious thought of a place that has never been awake and does not know that it is asleep.

---

The islands are not safe, though they are safe enough to stand on. This is the final thing to understand.

Standing on the islands, you feel it. The stone beneath your feet is not solid in the way that stone is supposed to be solid. It gives, almost imperceptibly, a slow oozing, a microscopic dissolution at the margins where water meets stone meets air meets time. The islands are being consumed. The houses built on them were built to be consumed—not with despair, not with resignation, but with the understanding that consumption is what happens to things that last, that permanence is a story stone tells itself until the water tells a different story, and the water is older than the story, older than the telling, older than the tongue that tried to name it.

What lives on the islands? The moss lives, gray and damp and patient, the slowest thing that grows here, the most thorough. It covers everything. It does not kill the stone it covers—it simply continues, simply persists, simply makes a soft gray world that was never meant for weight, never meant for walking, never meant for anything but the long damp holding-on of a living thing that has forgotten what it means to stop.

The houses are not ruins. They are *remains*. They are what is left when the people who built them went somewhere else—or became something else—or were taken by the water that was already rising when they built the first stone into the first wall. The houses are not ruins because they were never quite finished. They were always being built, always being added to, always being extended, because the people who built them understood that the building would never be done, that the building was the point, that the building was how you lived in a place that was always becoming water, always returning, always taking back.

What lives in the houses? Nothing. But the houses themselves are alive in the way that all made things are alive when they have been made with patience, with care, with the understanding that making is a form of love, and love is a form of living, and living is what happens when you do not stop, when you cannot stop, when the alternative is to be taken by the water that asks for nothing because it has no wants, because it is only water, because it is only the breathing of the old place, forever, until there is nothing left to breathe.

---

The sound lives here. The grinding, the humming, the throat-song from the earth itself—this is not incidental. This is not a side effect of water moving through stone. This is what the place is. The sound is the place's heartbeat, or its breath, or its dreaming made audible, or its living made known.

The locals, if there were any locals left to call them that, had a word for this. The word is gone. The language it belonged to is gone. The people are gone. Only the sound remains, and the water, and the slow forests, and the pale growth, and the dreaming beneath the floor that has no dreamer, that *is* the dreamer, that dreams itself into existence and continues dreaming because there is nothing else to do, because there is nowhere else to be, because the drowned plateau is where the old place lives, and the old place is where the water lives, and the water is where the light lives, and the light is where the growing lives, and the growing is what lives here, what has always lived here, what will live here until the water rises or the islands sink or the dreamer wakes for the first and last time and finds that there is nothing to wake to, and so the dreamer does not wake, and the water does not stop, and the forests grow, and the sound continues, and the darkness glows with its own cold light, and the old place breathes, breathes, breathes, forever, in the drowned plateau of Kel Suarath, where nothing asks permission and nothing is denied.
