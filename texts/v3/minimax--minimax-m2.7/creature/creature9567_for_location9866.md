What lives here is not what you would expect if you expected anything, which you do not, not anymore. Expectation requires faith in pattern, and pattern has been scoured from this place so thoroughly that you have learned to move through it differently—alert to anomaly, to the small disturbances that might mean something is here, might mean you are not alone.

There is a creature—if *creature* is still the word for something that has shed so many of the qualities you associate with life—that moves through the dark water like a question that forgot its answer. You have seen it only once, and even then not clearly: a shape that bent light wrong, that seemed to occupy slightly more space than it should, whose edges blurred into the water around it so that you could not say where water ended and body began. It passed through your field of vision the way weather passes—without intention, or with an intention so alien that your mind refused to hold it. You swam on. It did not acknowledge you. You are not sure it acknowledged anything.

There is the mat-life, the dark clinging things on ancient ridges. They grow so slowly that a human lifetime might add less than a centimeter to their spread, yet they persist. They have no root, no anchor beyond the passive hold of texture against stone. They simply remain. When you pass close to them—rare, because finding them is rare—you slow and look, and sometimes you see that they have grown slightly since you last passed, have extended a pale tendril into new territory, have begun the patient work of occupying one more square centimeter of a world that does not welcome them. You do not touch them. You are not sure what touching them would mean.

The bioluminescence you see is not one thing. It is several things, or many, and they do not agree with each other. There is the cold blue-green flash that comes once and is gone—the quick mind, the predator's signal, the creature that has learned to make light in a place where light is never safe. There is a dimmer, yellower glow that you have glimpsed only twice, hanging in the dark like a rumor, pulsing slowly enough that at first you mistook it for a current-carried particle, a bit of debris, something dead. But it was not dead. It was thinking, perhaps. It was waiting. You do not know what it was waiting for. You are not sure it knew.

And there are the currents themselves, which you have learned to read as a kind of language, though the grammar is treacherous and changes without warning. They carry things. Not often—a few particles, a smell that might once have been something else, the faint chemical memory of distant life. Once, the current brought you something you could almost hold: a seed, or what you decided was a seed, black and heavy and smaller than your smallest fingernail. It sat in your palm for a moment before the water took it back, and in that moment you thought about the surface, about sunlight, about roots reaching down instead of up. Then the current shifted and it was gone, and you were alone again in the way that only deep water can make you alone.

What lives here, in the end, is something you do not have a name for. It is the pattern of absence that persists. It is the dark that holds its shape. It is the weight that presses in from every direction, patient as deep time, older than the mountains, and it is also the swimmers who push against it—the carbon-and-water things that refuse to become part of the weight, that insist on moving even when movement is the only thing they have.

You live here. That is what lives in this place. You, and whatever you have become in the swimming.
