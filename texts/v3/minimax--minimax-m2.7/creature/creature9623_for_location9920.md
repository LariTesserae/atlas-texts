The lakes harbor creatures that breed in darkness. In the shallow margins where water meets stone, one observes the *pleATING*s—unnamed by any formal taxonomy, but so called for the sound they produce when surface tension breaks beneath their mass: a thin, drawn-out exhalation, like air escaping a sealed jar. They are flat, roughly the size of a human hand, their bodies composed almost entirely of viscous membrane stretched over what may be organs or may be cavities of standing water drawn from the lake itself. When disturbed, a pleating will fold inward upon itself, compressing to a fraction of its resting diameter, then slowly reinflate over several minutes. One has never observed one die. They simply cease to be found in locations where they were once common, and appear, eventually, in locations where they were not.

Below the pleatings, in water too turbid for surface observation, the *rooted swimmers* move. These are creature-forms best described as fish-shaped, though their appendages—where they possess any—vary wildly from specimen to specimen. Some bear filamentous protrusions along their lateral lines; others have what appear to be vestigial limbs, jointed and grasping, that they use to pull themselves along the lakebed when swimming proves insufficient. What unites them is their behavior: at regular intervals, rooted swimmers will cease forward motion and descend, pressing themselves against the limestone bottom, remaining motionless for hours. During these periods, their surfaces undergo a visible change—the water held within their tissues grows cloudy, then opaque, then begins to emit a faint luminescence, pale blue and sourceless. Old specimens are said to glow constantly. Young ones do not glow at all. No one has satisfactorily explained the mechanism, though the locals—who are few and wary—claim the creatures are "charging."

The lakes' apex predators are the *drowners*, though this name is local and perhaps misleading. A drowner is not an aquatic creature; it is amphibious, or perhaps amphibious is insufficient—something that moves between media without preference. They are large, four-limbed, covered in a mucus that gleams wetly under fungal light and never appears to dry. Their faces are broad and flat, with eyes set so far apart that a drowner seems to be looking at two separate panoramas simultaneously. When they hunt, they do so with patience rather than speed, remaining motionless at water's edge or beneath overhangs of saturated moss, waiting. The drowners take large prey—creatures the size of small dogs, though those are not dogs, never dogs—but they do not kill with violence. They wait until their target is close, then exhale. The exhalation is not air. It is a mist, fine and pervasive, that settles on the prey's hide and seems to slow it, to thicken its movements, to make each step a negotiation with resistance that was not there before. A creature touched by drowner breath moves as though underwater, and the drowner follows, unhurried, until the prey collapses from exhaustion or simply stands still, unable to proceed, until the drowner closes the distance.

One has not determined whether the drowner's breath is a toxin or a modification of the humid medium itself. One suspects the latter. The plateau's air is already a medium; perhaps the drowners simply intensify its properties, rendering the environment less traversable until only they can navigate it freely.

In the fern forests, the canopy trembles with the passage of *squealers*. These are small creatures, social, roughly the mass of a terrestrial rabbit, though their morphology is entirely unlike any lagomorph. They are cylindrical, their bodies segmented, their legs—if the word applies—arrayed in radial patterns along their lower thirds, giving them an appearance of perpetual readiness to move in any direction without turning. Squealers travel in groups of forty to sixty, moving through the fern canopy rather than beneath it, their many legs hooking the broad leaves and pulling them into motion. The sound they produce is not a squeal, despite the name. It is a rapid clicking, almost mechanical, that serves as both communication and, one suspects, sonar. Squealers navigate by echolocation through the dense fern growth, and they navigate well—their coordinated movement through the canopy resembles nothing so much as schooling fish, each individual's trajectory responding instantaneously to the movements of its neighbors, the group flowing around obstacles without apparent communication.

When a squealer population grows too large for its territory, a division occurs. The group splits along lines that are not geographic but temporal—half the squealers continue their existing pattern while the other half begin a new one, identical in structure but offset by several hours. The two groups remain in proximity, passing through one another, their clicking calls overlapping in dense polyrhythm. It is not clear whether the squealers recognize the distinction between their own temporal cohort and others, or whether they simply do not collide through some mechanism of mutual spatial accommodation. One has never observed a collision. One does not wish to.

Beneath the ferns, in the root layer where light fails entirely, the *filament-bearers* persist. They are creatures of minimal mass—soft-bodied, translucent, their internal structures visible as dark threads or vessels suspended in what appears to be largely water. They attach themselves to the roots of the ferns or to the limestone substrate and wait, extending filaments outward, sometimes several meters in length, that serve as both sensory organs and prey-capture mechanisms. A filament-bearer waits until its filaments register movement, then contracts rapidly, drawing whatever has touched the filaments into its central mass, where it is held and slowly absorbed. The process takes hours. The filament-bearers do not kill quickly. They do not seem to need to.

In the open karst regions, where the stone pavement stretches between islands of vegetation, one finds the *shallow-burrowers*. These are the plateau's ungulates, insofar as any terrestrial analog applies, though they are diggers first and walkers second. A shallow-burrower spends most of its time below the stone surface, in tunnels that penetrate no more than a few centimeters into the limestone—but that limestone is soft, and the burrowers are persistent, and over generations they create networks that weaken the stone above, so that karst pavement that appears solid may in fact be a thin crust over years of tunneling. When one walks the karst, one walks above them. When the crust fails—as it fails sometimes, without warning—the shallow-burrowers scatter, their tunnels filling with water that was not there before, the depression becoming a pool within hours, colonized by pleatings within days. The shallow-burrowers do not seem to mind. They simply begin again elsewhere.

They emerge at dusk, if dusk is the word for it—the plateau's light cycles do not correspond to any sun-based schedule, but something shifts in the light balance after several hours of particular activity, and the shallow-burrowers surface then, grazing on the moss that coats every stone, their broad flat mouths rasping across the surface in continuous motion. They are hunted during these emergences. The *long-necks* wait at the edges of the karst, where the stone meets vegetation, their bodies held motionless while their heads—elongated, sinuous, supported by cervical vertebrae that seem excessive in number—track the burrowers' movements with predatory focus. A long-neck does not chase. It strikes, once, from a distance, its neck uncoiling with a speed that seems to violate the creature's apparent mass, its jaw closing on a shallow-burrower before the prey has fully registered the attack. Then the long-neck withdraws to the vegetation's edge and digests, slowly, over a period that may extend for days.

The long-necks are not the largest things on the plateau. In the caves, where the industrial machinery continues its ancient operations, one hears movement that suggests creatures of considerable size—thumps and scrapes and the wet displacement of mass against stone. These cave-dwellers are rarely seen. They emerge at irregular intervals, often following periods of heavy rain or fog, their tracks visible in the soft mud of cave mouths but their bodies observed only at a distance, when they stand at the threshold between the lit and the dark, regarding the plateau above. They are quadrupedal, or appear to be; their proportions are wrong for any quadruped one could name, with forelimbs that seem too long and hindquarters that seem compressed, as though the creature were meant for a different gravitational context entirely. They do not flee when observed. They regard. They breathe. Then they withdraw into the dark, and one hears them for hours afterward, moving deeper into the cave systems, toward the sources of heat and mechanical noise, where the machines run and the fungal light intensifies and the air grows thick with moisture and something else, something with a chemical signature that might be sulfur or might be something older.

The insects are not a single phenomenon but a category so vast and varied that any attempt at comprehensive description fails. One can speak of the *cloud-makers*, whose populations rise in afternoon fog—when afternoon is approximated by whatever internal rhythm governs such things—and whose collective bodies thicken the air until navigation becomes a matter of pushing through, of feeling the legs and wings and carapaces brush against one's skin in continuous contact. One can speak of the *deep-dwellers*, who inhabit the cave systems exclusively and whose forms are adapted for total darkness: eyeless, pale, their sensory apparatus oriented toward vibration and chemical signal. One can speak of the *waterwalkers*, who persist on the surfaces of pools and streams, their legs distributing their mass so precisely that they do not break surface tension, moving across water as though it were stone. They are prey for many things and predators of smaller things still; they are a constant presence, a background texture against which the larger fauna moves.

The drone they produce together is not uniform. It varies by location, by time, by the composition of the fog. Near the processing facilities, the drone acquires a mechanical quality, as though the insects are timing their movements to the rhythm of the machinery. In the fern forests, the drone is higher, more chaotic, the sound of many small wings generating interference patterns that produce something almost musical. Over the open karst, the drone thins and disperses, becoming almost inaudible, a reminder that even the plateau's insects are tied to moisture, to vegetation, to the medium that sustains everything here and without which they could not persist.

One has not mentioned the *bleeders*. One hesitates to. They are found near water—in the streams, at the margins of lakes, in the sinkholes where water vanishes into the earth. They are not predatory in any conventional sense. They do not chase or ambush or trap. They land, and they feed, and they leave, and the mark they leave is not a wound but a slow leakage, a thin seepage of fluid from the body of whatever they have fed upon that continues long after the bleeder has departed. A human touched by a bleeder will find fluid weeping from pores and minor cuts for hours afterward, the body apparently unable to staunch the flow, the bleeding so gradual that it does not constitute a medical emergency but so persistent that it cannot be ignored. The bleeder does not appear to feed on blood. It appears to feed on something else—some property of the body's boundary, some quality of containment—that the body then struggles to restore.

Whether this is a wound or an alteration of the boundary itself, one cannot say. One suspects the latter. The plateau blurs boundaries. The water is not quite water. The air is not quite air. The creatures persist at the edge of categories, their behaviors almost correct, their anatomies almost recognizable, their ecosystems almost coherent. One inhabits this place the way one inhabits a dream: with the constant sense that the rules are different, that one's ordinary competencies will not serve, that the landscape is aware of one's presence and is waiting, patiently, for the moment when familiarity fails and something else takes its place.

The plateau breathes.

The water drips.

The machines run.
