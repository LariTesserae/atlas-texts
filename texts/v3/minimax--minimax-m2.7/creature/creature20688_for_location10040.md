# The Inhabitants of the Underplain

## I. The Clickers

You notice them first by sound. Before your eyes adjust, before the bioluminescence resolves into shapes, the clicking finds you — that dry percussion against stone, like someone tapping on the bowl's walls with fingernails worn to horn. They are the most visible of the Underplain's creatures, though *visible* is a generous word for the attention they permit.

They are not foxes, not exactly. They are not forearm-length or fist-sized either, though they fit comfortably into those measurements when they hold still. What they are is *armored* — jointed and gleaming faintly at those same joints, where the bioluminescence pools in their bodies like oil in the creases of old machinery. Their shells are the color of old copper turned green, or the color of mineral deposits that time has left behind. When they move, they move slowly, deliberately, each motion a small negotiation with gravity and stone. They click because they cannot help it. Their legs are shaped for it, built for rasping against the floor's texture, and in the dense air of the Underplain, the sound carries differently than it would on the surface — it fills rather than fades.

A Clicker does not startle. This is important. A Clicker has never startled. Its ancestors forgot how, generations ago, when nothing in the bowl ever came fast enough to warrant the response. When you descend into their territory, they will turn their faceted eyes toward you — if eyes is the right word for organs that gather light rather than project it — and they will assess. They will click once, twice, a kind of census-taking, and then they will continue their business. They have business. They are always busy in the way that water is busy, the way that erosion is busy: slowly, inevitably, with no sense of urgency because urgency would change nothing.

They do not sleep, as far as anyone can tell. They do not seem to rest. They simply move through their lives at the pace that the bowl has taught them is the only pace worth keeping.

## II. The Hum-Bees (Those Who Breathe in Frequencies)

If the Clickers are the Underplain's percussion section, then the Hum-Bees are its strings — not literally, of course, but in effect. Their breathing fills the bowl with a sound that is less heard than felt, a pressure in the chest cavity that registers before the ears can name it.

They are smaller than the Clickers, smaller than your smallest finger, and their bodies are soft in the way that larvae are soft before they harden. They cluster. This is their primary behavior: clustering in the crevices of the ancient structures, in the gaps between stone blocks that the long settling has left behind, in the crooks where the pale fronds meet the substrate. They cluster and they breathe and their breathing hums.

Each cluster is a kind of organism in its own way — a mass of small bodies, each one pushing air through membranes that vibrate at frequencies just below hearing, so that the effect is not a sound but a presence. Stand near a cluster and you feel it in your teeth, in the plates of your skull. The hum is warm. Not warm like sunlight, but warm like body heat, like the thermal memory of blood. It is the closest thing to comfort that the Underplain offers, and it is offered without intention, without awareness that comfort exists as a category.

The Hum-Bees do not sting. They have no need of defense. They are too small to eat, too slow to flee, and they have existed in this place long enough that the concept of predator has worn down to something theoretical, something that might apply to some creature somewhere but not here, not in the bowl, not in their crevices.

## III. The Pale Frond-Keepers

The plants do not have a name in any language, but if they did, it might be something like *frond-keeper* — a term that acknowledges both the plant itself and the small life it sustains.

The fronds are the most lush things in the Underplain, though lush is a relative word. They are waxy and heavy, saturated with oils that the plants have been producing for so long that the processes feel geological rather than biological. The oils would burn, yes, the prompt said this, but they have forgotten how to burn. Fire is not a concept the Underplain recognizes. The fronds uncurl over seasons — not the seasons of the surface, but internal rhythms, cycles of growth and rest that the plants measure in increments too vast for any animal to perceive.

What the plants keep, they keep quietly. In the crooks where their fronds meet the substrate stone, in the small valleys their root-systems have carved into the bowl's floor, there are communities. Hum-Bees cluster in their shelter. Clickers rest in their shade. Things smaller still — things that are more idea than organism, things that are more movement than matter — find purchase in the slow chemistry of the fronds' surface oils.

The relationship is not symbiosis exactly. It is older than symbiosis. It is simply *togetherness*, a word that implies no contract and no awareness of the contract's terms. The plants grow. The animals live in the growing. Neither asks anything of the other because asking is a behavior that requires a kind of temporal hierarchy — a before and after, a cause and effect — that the Underplain has worn smooth.

## IV. The Deep Ones

Below the Clickers, below the Hum-Bees, below the visible spectrum of the bowl's life, there are the Deep Ones. You do not see them. You feel them — a pressure change in the air when you stand too long in one place, a shifting in the mineral weight that suggests something vast has adjusted its attention.

They are not creatures exactly. They are more like processes, like the bowl itself deciding to notice you. Their movements take centuries. Their thoughts — if they think — operate in spans that make the Clickers look like mayflies. When a Deep One moves, the buildings shift slightly in their foundations. When a Deep One breathes, the hum of the Hum-Bees stutters for a moment before resuming. They are the reason the bowl does not feel abandoned. They are the presence behind the presence, the company that the buildings knew they were keeping when they built their faint glow into their crevices.

You will not see a Deep One. The closest you will come is a sense of being watched by something that has been watching since before watching was a concept, and will continue watching until watching becomes meaningless. They are the Underplain's conscience, though conscience is a word that implies moral weight, and the Underplain has no use for morality. They are simply *there*, holding the bowl's shape in whatever way they hold shape, keeping the great breathing going through whatever means deep time has taught them.

## V. The Sound of Living

It is the sound that stays with you, in the end.

Not the Clickers alone, not the Hum-Bees alone, but all of it together — the rasping and the humming and the slow shifting of the Deep Ones in their depths. It fills the bowl like water fills a vessel, like silence fills a room when no one is left to break it. It is not peaceful. Peace is a surface word, a daylight word. It is something else: *fullness*, perhaps. *Completion*. A sense that this place has everything it needs and has never needed to look for more.

The creatures do not sing. They do not communicate in any way you would recognize as language. But they make sounds, constant sounds, sounds that have been filling this bowl since long before the first Clicker's ancestor crawled out of some mineral pore and thought *here, this is here, this is where I will live*. And the sounds have learned each other, have grown into each other, have become a single sound that is both conversation and chorus, both presence and absence.

When you stand in the bowl and listen — truly listen, with the patience the stone walls have — you begin to hear it. The hum of the Underplain. The sound of life that does not know it is alive, doing what life does: continuing. Being full. Being itself, without apology, without explanation, without anything except the simple vast fact of its own existence.

This is what lives here.

This is what has always lived here.

This is what will be living here long after the last surface word for *here* has worn down to its mineral memory and settled into the bowl's floor, waiting.
