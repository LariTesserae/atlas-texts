They are, first of all, patient. The people here have no other choice — patience is the architecture of survival on this plateau, built into the bones of everyone who lives here. You can see it in the way they move: unhurried, deliberate, each gesture weighted as if they are aware that motion costs something in a place this cold. The young children learn this early, are carried for the first several years against the body heat of their mothers or older siblings, and when they finally walk they do so slowly, carefully, their small hands trailing along the worn stone walls of the lanes as if the rock itself is teaching them how to inhabit this place.

The people are not tall. This is worth noting, because it would be easy to imagine them as rugged, outsized — survivors carved from granite. But they are compact, round-bodied, built to conserve heat. Their faces are broad and often wind-reddened, the skin thickening at the cheeks and around the eyes where the sun reflects off the pale stone at angles that lowland peoples never encounter. They have learned to squint even on overcast days. Their hands are large relative to their frames — the work of generations of stone carving has selected for grip strength, for the ability to hold a chisel steady in cold air that wants to numb the fingers. They wear layers of stone-grey wool, processed from the dense underfur of the herd animals, and their boots are sewn tight to prevent the loss of heat through the soles. They do not speak much. This is not shyness; it is economy. Sound carries strangely on the plateau, bouncing between the ridges, arriving distorted and strange, and so they have developed a habit of listening first, of letting silence gather information before they fill it. When they do speak, it is in low voices, and their language has many words for water in its different states — the vapor that gathers in stone channels at dawn, the liquid that pools in basalt depressions, the ice that seals and must be broken, the snow that falls and must be cleared. They are people who measure the world in moisture.

And then there are the herds.

The herd animals are the second people of the plateau, though they do not know it. They are enormous — heavier than horses, their skins thick and grey-brown, the texture of old leather. Their heads are blunt and their eyes are placed on the sides, giving them a wide field of vision, and they use this constantly, scanning the ridgelines for movement, for the shape of a predator, for the darker shadows where water pools. They do not run. They never run, not even when startled. Instead they move faster — a slow canter that is still, to lowland eyes, almost comical in its apparent lethargy. But there is nothing lethargic about them. They are engines of heat, their bodies running several degrees warmer than the air around them, and the steam of their breath hangs in the cold like a signal fire. They have been here longer than the people. The people know this. Their oldest stories speak of the herds as they speak of the stone itself — as something given, as a condition of the place rather than a feature of it. Over generations, the relationship has become a kind of language. The herds learn to read the water systems: they know, without being taught, which channels lead to the settlement pools, which basins will be filled. The people learn to read the herds: they watch for the slow columns to shift direction, for the way the lead animals lower their great blunt heads to the ground and smell the air, and they understand from these gestures that something is changing — a drought coming, a storm, a shift in the deep geology that will alter where water rises to the surface.

Between these two — the people and the herds — there are the small ones.

The small warm-bodied creatures that live in the stone's crevices are harder to see. They emerge only at midday, when the thin sun has warmed the rock faces enough that the air in the crevices stirs and wakes them. They are dense-furred, their coats the color of the basalt so that they blend perfectly against the stone — grey-brown, near-black, sometimes with the faintest greenish tinge where mineral residue has stained their fur. They are not rodents, exactly, but they occupy the rodent niche: small, quick, tunneling, their incisors built for gnawing through roots and frozen plant matter. They do not fear the people, exactly, but they do not trust them either. They watch from the crevice mouths, their dark eyes bright and liquid, and when a footstep approaches they vanish with a flick of motion that is less than a heartbeat. They live in the spaces the people have not yet carved, in the deep places between terraces where the stone is still raw and fractured. They are numerous — more numerous than anyone who does not live here would believe — and they are the reason the plateau feels alive even when no movement is visible. You look at a bare stretch of basalt, and if you wait long enough, something moves.

The birds are overhead, and they are the most visible life on the plateau — the long shadows of their wings passing over the stone like a repeated announcement. They do not land often. The plateau offers them little that they cannot find elsewhere, except the wind, which here moves in predictable patterns, layer over layer, and which they ride with an expertise that speaks to millennia of adaptation. They are not songbirds. Their calls are thin, sharp, almost lost in the wind. They are part of the plateau's grammar of sound: the creak of a door on its hinges, the drip of water from a cistern, the low murmur of a voice, the stamp of a herd animal's foot, and above it all the thin keening of the wind and the thinner cry of the bird that rides it.

At night, when the frost comes down and the oil lamps glow small and orange in the stone doorways, there are other lives that wake.

The pools — the still dark ones that hold the sky — these are not empty. If you could look beneath the surface, you would find life there too: small creatures, translucent, evolved for cold so extreme that their bodies produce a kind of natural antifreeze that keeps their tissues from freezing solid. They move slowly, if they move at all. Most of them are dormant through the long winter, frozen into the ice at the bottom of the pools, their lives suspended until the thin spring sun warms the water enough to wake them. They are the plateau's secret, its hidden abundance. No one talks about them much. They are too small to see, and they live in places — deep in the stone, beneath the ice, in the spaces between soil particles — that require a special kind of looking to find.

And beneath the stone itself, there is something else. Not alive, not dead — the word *alive* does not mean the same thing in the deep rock as it does at the surface. But something is there. The plateau was buried once, deep and compressed, and the forces that pushed it upward are still at work. The ridges are new. The fractures are fresh. The frost moves through the rock each night, widening the cracks by fractions of millimeters, and in the morning the stone looks like it has been drawn on — white veins threading through black. This is the slow work of making a landscape. This is what lives in the plateau when everything else is still: the geology itself, the pressure and the cold, the patient rearrangement of matter, the long exhalation of a world still being born.

The people feel it sometimes, in the deep places of their terraces. A tremor that is not quite a tremor. A settling of stone that groans in the night. They build their homes with deep foundations, cut into the rock so that the thermal mass of the stone holds heat even when the air is white with frost, and they have learned that the stone wants to move — wants to fracture and rearrange and find a new shape — and so they leave space in their doorways, gaps in their walls, joints that can flex without breaking. They live with the geology. They do not fight it. They have become, over generations, part of the same slow process: a people being carved out of the rock at the same time the rock is being carved into by the people.

This is what lives here. This is what persists. The wind moving patient across the plateau like a hand smoothing a map. The cold settling heavy into the stone. The frost drawing its white lines each morning. The pools holding the sky. The moss finding the moisture. The ferns curling tight against the frost. The shrubs bent under the weight of crystals that will not melt until noon. The herds moving slow between the water. The small creatures flickering in the crevices. The birds passing overhead on their clockwork rounds. The oil lamps glowing orange in the stone doorways. The people — patient, compact, listening — carving their homes into the ridges and terraces of a place that is still, after all these years, making itself.

The plateau at dusk is the quietest thing. And in the quiet, if you listen, you can hear it: the slow breath of stone, the patient work of cold, the almost-silence of a world that has not finished becoming itself yet. This is what lives here. This is what will still be here, long after the oil lamps have gone out and the terraces have settled back into the rock. This is what was here before, and what will be here after: the patient wind, the heavy cold, the slow exhalation of a geology still pressing itself upward into the thin cold light.
