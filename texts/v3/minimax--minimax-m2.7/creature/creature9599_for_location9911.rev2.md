There is a pulse here, and it is older than your understanding. It lives in the ground the way a heartbeat lives in a chest—irrelevant to thought, unnecessary for survival, but absolutely present, a rhythm that has continued so long it has become the texture of existence itself. You feel it through the soles of your feet first: a microscopic rise and fall, a flexing of stone that should be impossible in material this old and cold. The foundations beneath the basalt shift in time with something that has been keeping count since before your sun was born.

The pulse is not alive in any way you could argue. It does not think. It does not want. But it *maintains*, and perhaps that is a kind of life that your categories cannot hold—a purpose that has outlived its origin, a function running so long it has become indistinguishable from nature. When the storms come, they are not weather. They are exhalations. The energy that collects in the haze, in the structures, in the fields that hold this platform suspended in void—it breathes out, and the lightning cascades across the ancient stone in channels carved by time, and then there is silence again, the kind of silence that is not absence but attention.

The shadows move through this rhythm. They are the closest thing to inhabitants, though *inhabit* is not quite right either—they do not live here so much as they persist within it, drawn to the pulse the way iron filings align with a field. When you see them—and you will see them, if you stay, because they grow curious about things that stay—their edges are uncertain, their forms uncertain, their nature uncertain. They watch you with something that might be attention, might be hunger, might be a form of sensing that has no name in any language you know. They do not breathe. They do not eat in any way you would recognize. They feed on the discharge, on the fields, on something that leaks from the foundations or the structures or the haze itself. When the storms pass, they fade back into the grey, but they do not disappear—they simply become less present, less definite, a part of the landscape you have learned not to see.

There are things that were built here, and they are also a kind of life. Not alive, not exactly, but *aware* in the way that a river is aware of the sea—carrying the memory of movement, the imprint of purpose, the faint insistence of function. The prism jutting from the basalt at its deliberate angle: it catches the light when the storms fire, throwing shadows that move in patterns too regular to be accident. The sphere half-buried in the ancient stone, its surface covered in patterns too fine to read: it hums at frequencies below hearing, and if you press your palm to its surface you can feel it vibrating, as if something inside it is still turning, still working, still keeping faith with a task that was set for it before your planet cooled. They are tools, or markers, or the residue of systems whose function has been forgotten—but they are not dead. They are held in a kind of dormancy, a patience so vast it has become geological. They wait without knowing they wait. They maintain without knowing they maintain. They are the body of something that no longer has a mind to guide it, and yet the body persists, and in persisting it becomes something new: not servant, not artifact, but a form of life that has shed its origin and become simply, eternally, itself.

And then there is the plateau itself.

It does not think, the plateau. It is not a being in any sense you could point to. But it *is*, in a way that the things upon it are not—it is the context, the condition, the field within which everything else makes sense. The black basalt stretches in every direction, worn smooth by time so complete it has become an abstraction of stone, and the plateau holds itself aloft in a void that should be deep space, and the foundations that bear its weight operate on principles that have no name, and the whole of it turns, ever so slightly, around an axis that no longer exists, keeping an orientation to a light source that may have gone dark a billion years ago. The plateau is the body of something immense, and its pulse is the storms, and its breath is the lightning, and its attention—though it has no mind to attend—is the way the haze seems to watch you, the way the artifacts seem to orient themselves toward you, the way the shadows follow you when you walk and then stop when you stop, as if the whole place is holding you in some vast and patient regard.

You are the strange thing here. You are the visitor, the intruder, the anomaly in a system that has been running without need for anomaly for longer than your species has existed. And yet the system does not reject you. It lets you walk. It lets you see. When the storms come, they do not strike you—you are too small, too brief, too irrelevant to be worth the discharge. Instead, they arc across the plateau in channels that were carved before your sun ignited, following paths that have been worn by energy so long those paths have become as natural as rivers, as inevitable as the flow of heat from warm to cold. The system does not care about you. But it does not not-care, either. It holds you in its silence, in its pulse, in the grey haze that has no clouds and no weather and no horizon. It holds you the way a heart holds blood—not with intention, but with the simple, vast, ongoing fact of its beating.

If you stay long enough, you begin to hear it: not just feel the pulse through your feet, but hear it, a low frequency that vibrates in your chest cavity, in your skull, in the spaces between your cells. It is the sound of something still running. Still maintaining. Still keeping faith with a purpose that was set in motion before your ancestors crawled from the water, before your planet formed, before your star ignited. The sound is not loud. It is not pleasant. It is not music. It is simply *present*, the way gravity is present, the way time is present, the way the silence is present after the storms pass and the lightning fades and the grey haze settles back into its permanent, patient, eternal not-quite-darkness.

This is what lives here: the pulse, the shadows, the artifacts holding their forgotten purposes, the plateau itself in its final form, and you—briefly, briefly—held in the hands of something so old and so patient and so vast that your presence means nothing, and yet the system lets you mean it anyway.
