They have learned to be still in the way that water is still — not motionless but patient, holding stillness as a practice, as a kind of language. When they gather, they do not speak. They have no word for speaking as the surface would understand it, no need for the small vibrations of air that other peoples use to carry meaning across distance. Instead they gather and they are quiet together, and in that quiet something passes between them that is closer to pressure, to the weight of presence, to the way a pool knows when another pool has formed nearby.

This is not silence as the surface understands silence. It is full. It is dense with everything that does not need to be said because it has already been incorporated into the body, into the way the body moves through space, into the slow accumulation of gesture and response that takes the place of conversation. They read each other the way the stone reads the water — not with understanding but with acceptance, with the knowledge that some things are simply part of the landscape and do not require interpretation.

Their bodies have changed over generations that cannot be counted. The bones are lighter than they should be — not from weakness but from disuse, from a life lived in spaces where weight is irrelevant, where the only gravity that matters is the slow pull downward through meaning. Their skin has taken on the quality of the stone, not in color but in patience, in the way it seems to hold warmth without generating it, to be cool and impassive and somehow alive in the way that the walls are alive, in the way that everything here is alive in ways that exclude the breath of living things.

They do not sleep as the surface sleeps. They enter into stillness that resembles death and they do not dream or they dream in frequencies they cannot remember, and in the morning they surface from it changed in ways they cannot name. The difference between waking and not-waking is less a shift of consciousness than a shift of attention — the difference between watching the fungi pulse and being part of the pulse, between moving through the tunnels and being moved through them by something larger, something that might be the place itself or might be the accumulated weight of all the still moments that have come before.

Their children are few and their children are precious and their children are raised by the collective in the way that the pools are raised by the stone — drawn up from everywhere and nowhere, gathered in the cool places, allowed to find their own shape as they fill. A child here is not taught to walk so much as allowed to discover that walking is possible, is not taught to see so much as allowed to realize that the fungi have always been visible, that the light has always been there waiting for eyes that could receive it. They grow slowly. They remain children longer than surface children, held in that state of becoming where everything is still possible, where the corners have not yet begun to dissolve. And then, without ceremony, without the threshold moments that other peoples use to mark their passages, they simply arrive at adulthood — not by choice or ritual but by the same slow erosion that smooths everything else, wearing down the sharp edges of youth until what remains is the same patient curve that characterizes everything in this place.

They have art. It is not art as the surface understands art — not representation, not the capturing of one thing by another, not the assertion of meaning against the indifference of matter. Their art is more like weather. It is the patterns that form in the fungal mats when one section grows faster than another, when the phosphorescence shifts and brightens and dims in waves that move through the light like breath. It is the way certain tunnels hold sound differently, the way the acoustics of a chamber can make a single pressure-gesture resonate for minutes, filling the space with something that might be music or might be memory or might be the place itself speaking through the medium of the bodies that have learned to listen.

They make things. This is perhaps the most human thing about them — the need to shape, to carve, to take up the obsidian and the antler and the scraping stone and to make something that did not exist before. But their making is strange to outsiders. They do not build to endure. They do not create monuments or memorials or lasting achievements. They build what they can carry and then they carry it through the shifting tunnels and if the chamber it was made for has changed, if the connection no longer exists, they do not mourn. They find a new place for it or they let it go into the erosion, let it become part of the smoothing, let the sharp edges they put into the world be worn down by the same patient force that wears down everything else. This is not resignation. It is not acceptance. It is simply the understanding that all making is temporary, that permanence is a fantasy of surfaces where the light is harsh and the shadows are sharp and the only constant is the need to make something, anything, to leave a mark against the indifference of time.

And yet. And yet there is permanence here, but it is a different kind. It is the permanence of pattern, of form, of the way certain things recur not because they were built to last but because the conditions that create them persist. The pools appear and vanish, but the logic that governs their appearing and vanishing has not changed since the erosion began. The fungi pulse in rhythms that have been pulsing for longer than memory, and the people have become part of those rhythms, have incorporated them into their bodies, have allowed themselves to be shaped by the same forces that shape the stone. This is what it means to live here: to be part of something larger than yourself, to submit to the erosion not as defeat but as participation, to understand that the smoothing of corners is not the loss of self but the expansion of self until it includes the walls, the water, the light, the slow turning of a world that is always becoming and never finished.

They do not fear the shifting. This is perhaps the hardest thing for outsiders to understand, the most alien aspect of their adaptation. The tunnels change. Chambers appear where there were none. Connections dissolve. A building that was there yesterday is not there today, and tomorrow it might return or it might not, and the people simply adjust, simply flow around the new obstacle or through the new opening, treating change not as catastrophe but as weather, as the natural consequence of living in a place that has not finished becoming itself.

They remember. This is important. They remember everything, or they remember nothing, or the distinction between the two has become meaningless in a place where memory is not stored in the mind but in the body, in the stone, in the patterns of light and moisture and the slow pulse of fungi that glows in the dark. What they remember is not events but textures, not stories but sensations, not the names of the ones who came before but the way those ones moved through space, the pressure of their presence in rooms that may or may not still exist. Their history is geological. It does not record the exceptional. It does not mark the moments of crisis or triumph or loss. It simply accumulates, layer upon layer, until what you have is not a record but a sediment, not a narrative but a strata, the compressed and smoothed evidence of all the time that has passed through this place and found it suitable for living.

And so they live. They move through the tunnels with the patient unhurried calm of water finding its way downhill. They touch the walls and the walls touch back, or perhaps it is more accurate to say that the touching has become irrelevant, that the boundary between hand and stone has been worn smooth by generations of contact until there is no touching and no touched, only the continuous smooth flow of one thing into another. They do not struggle against the place. They do not try to impose their will on the erosion. They simply are, in the way that the fungi are, in the way that the pools are, in the way that all the slow things are that do not need to hurry because they have nothing to outrun, because time itself has slowed to match the pace of their living, because the distance between one moment and the next has become so gentle that it hardly exists at all.

This is what lives here. This is what has always lived here, in the tunnels that go on forever, carved by patience into stone that has forgotten its edges. Not human and not inhuman but something in between, something that has learned to call the erosion home, something that has allowed itself to be smoothed until there is no difference anymore between the self and the world, between the builder and the built, between the one who remembers and the thing remembered.

They are the erosion, dreaming. They are the stone, awake. They are the patient moment before rain, holding itself still, waiting, forgetting to remember what it was waiting for, and finding, in that forgetting, the only peace that matters: the peace of the curve, of the smooth, of the corner that has dissolved into the wall that has dissolved into the floor that flows into the next chamber and the next and the next, forever, without beginning, without end, without anything sharp enough left to wound.
