The lizards are the largest predators, though *predator* implies a hunt, and what they do is closer to claiming. They claim the elevated ridges and the hollows where the made-ground pools into shallow bowls. They claim the vertical surfaces too — the walls rise smooth and featureless for the first body-length, then begin their textures: ridges, flutes, shallow niches that serve as territories. A lizard will establish itself at a niche and remain there for what might be days or weeks, long after its kind have moved on elsewhere, holding ground that offers nothing but position. Their carapaces catch the sourceless light in ways that seem to exceed the available luminance, iridescence that blooms without apparent cause, colors arriving before their sources would allow. When they run — which is rarely, and then only across flat stretches when something larger passes through — the click of their undersides against the mineral surface is precise, metronomic, each footfall a single note in a pattern too complex to be random.

The beetles are easier to read. They live in the scoring. The makers' hand-tools left their marks across every surface, and the beetles have made these marks into architecture: colonies that trace the striations like cities along river-canyons, each groove housing thousands, the clicking rhythms the sound of their collective respiration. They are not synchronized — the colony breathes in waves, a pulse that moves through the scored lines like a slow heartbeat felt through fingertips pressed to a throat. When you pass close enough to feel the vibration in your palms, you can almost believe the beetles are communicating something. Statistics, perhaps. Counts. The population of the colony at this moment, the temperature of the substrate, the phase of whatever cycle governs this place. But the information, if it is information, has no audience. The beetles speak only to the grooves, and the grooves hold.

The flocking creatures are harder to observe. They move through the canopy in sheets, their bodies so thin and their movements so coordinated that at first you take them for shadows — a cloud passing through, a shift in the diffuse light. But the shadows move against the light's direction, and when the flock turns, the turn is too sharp, too simultaneous, a single thought distributed across hundreds of bodies. They feed on something in the air: spores, perhaps, or the humidity that weeps from the seams. They do not land. They do not rest. They move in slow circuits around the perimeter of the made-world, and their murmurations shift in configuration — elongate, compress, split and rejoin — with a grammar that suggests meaning, though the meaning is not for you. You are large and slow and you belong to a different order of time. The flock does not acknowledge you.

The plants are oldest. This is their world now, though they did not build it. They were placed here, perhaps — seeds or spores or root-cuttings carried in by whatever hands tended the system in its early centuries — and they have made themselves at home in ways the makers may not have intended. The fronds have grown thick over the channels, creating a false floor that conceals the engineered waterways beneath. The broad waxy leaves have merged where they touch, their edges fusing, so that whole sections of the interior have become continuous green surfaces, membrane rather than vegetation. The tendrils have found the seams and followed them down, threading into depths where the air is different — more humid, older, carrying a mineral tang that suggests the foundations are not as inert as they appear. Some of the plants have gone blind: leaves bleached white, photosynthesis abandoned in favor of something else, roots extended into the substrate's veins to drink whatever flows there. They are the elders. They have been here longest.

And there are smaller things. Fungi in the hollows, pale and translucent, pulsing faintly with moisture they have drawn from nowhere. Mites in the car's no-under-surfaces, moving in lines that never intersect. Something like a tardigrade in the channels, ancient and patient, adapted to whatever the made-world provides — dehydration, warmth, the slow hum. The scale of this place rewards attention to the small. Walk the perimeter and you will pass through microhabitats that repeat but do not quite: the same pattern of scoring, the same density of beetle-colony, the same thickness of canopy, but something will be different each time — the angle of a frond, the depth of a hollow, the color of a lizard's carapace in the sourceless light. You will walk for an hour and arrive back at your starting point having seen only the surface of what there is to see.

The hum has a texture now that you are learning to distinguish. It is not uniform. There are layers within it — a low constant note, a vibration beneath that, something almost like a voice if you hold still long enough and listen in the way the place seems to ask you to listen. The makers are not here. You understand this. But the made-world remembers them, and the hum is the memory of effort: hands against mineral, tools dragging, the patient repetition of work across generations. The builders are gone, but the building continues, and the hum is the sound of continuation.
