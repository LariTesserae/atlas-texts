What lives here is the wetness itself—not as a force or element, but as something that persists without purpose. The condensation gathers in the small hours before dawn, pooling on ledges and in the shallow channels that run between structures like the traces of arteries long since emptied. It does not ask to exist. It does not nourish anything. It simply emerges from the stone's hidden pores, clings to surfaces for an hour or two, and returns again to darker places. This is the closest thing to breath Kel-Veth possesses.

There is a presence in the silence. Not sound—you understand this immediately, within minutes of climbing the plateau's winding approaches—but its inverse. The architecture has forgotten noise the way a body forgets a limb that was removed long ago. There are places where the air feels thicker, where you might imagine sound once concentrated and then faded: the thresholds of vast doorways, the flat circles that might have been plazas, the throat-like passages between chambers that widen and narrow like viscera. If echoes could fossilize, they would look like this.

Some travelers speak of figures in the outer districts—shapes that move at the edge of vision, gray against gray, present only when the light angles wrong and casts the world in its most pitiless contrast. They are never seen clearly. They do not approach. They may be the residue of those who built this place, worn thin by time into something that no longer remembers it was once solid. Or they may be nothing at all: the eye, desperate for content in a landscape that offers none, inventing companions from the shifting lattices of shadow and moisture.

The channels hold something. You feel it before you understand it—a pull in the water's slow movement, a directionality that suggests destination rather than mere gravity. The condensation that drips from higher terraces does not simply fall; it descends with intention, gathering into threads that join other threads, joining into rivulets, joining into the thin persistent streams that carve patient paths toward the plateau's eastern edge. Where they go, no one climbs far enough to know. The drains that swallow them plunge into darkness so complete it seems less like a hole in the world than a decision the world has made to stop looking.

Perhaps the water is the true inhabitant. It enters the city at altitude, drawn from clouds that wreath the plateau's summit like a crown it never asked to wear. It moves through the architecture—through channels and seams and the capillary networks that web every wall—and it leaves again, descending toward depths that have not been visited since the terraces were new. It does not care for what it passes through. It does not leave marks or stains or the mineral traces that water usually claims. It is clean in the way that emptiness is clean: because nothing has been allowed to stick.

In the deepest chambers, the ones that require descending through passages too narrow for comfort, there are surfaces that glisten differently. Not with condensation—with something older. A moisture that does not dry, that returns as quickly as it is wiped, as if the stone itself is exhaling something too faint to taste but present enough to see. Some say the builders are still here, dissolved into the fabric of their creation, indistinguishable from the weeping they engineered but unable to stop, unable to become anything else. This is a comforting thought, perhaps. Or perhaps it is simply what the silence suggests to minds that cannot bear the alternative: that this place was made by hands, or by whatever served hands, and that those hands are gone, and the weeping is not grief but architecture—the city persisting in the only language it was taught, the only word it knows how to say.

What lives here is the waiting. The condensation gathers and falls and gathers again, and the gray light moves across the sharp planes of the facades, and somewhere in the channels the water runs toward a destination no one will ever reach. This is not life as you understand it. But it is not death either. It is something between—a rhythm without a heart, a motion without a mover, a city that continues because continuing is all it remembers how to do.
