# Preparation for the Pocket

You are going somewhere small. That is the first thing to understand, and perhaps the hardest. Your instincts as a traveler will pull toward scale — toward the idea that a place worth visiting must have horizon lines, must have distance, must offer the comfort of space to retreat into if things go wrong. This place has none of that. The ceiling is close enough to feel like a lid. The walls press inward. You will be held.

That feeling of enclosure is not a threat. It is the environment. The moment you accept it — the moment you stop waiting for the walls to open up and give you room — is the moment you will be able to inhabit it. You will still feel the tightness. You will simply stop fighting it. And in that surrender you will find that the space is, in its own way, generous. There is enough here. Enough warmth. Enough dark. Enough of everything necessary for the creatures that live here and have always lived here, long before you arrived, long before whatever machines came and threaded themselves into the stone.

You are visiting their home. Remember that. You are a visitor in a space that has been lived in, claimed, layered with bodies on bodies for a duration you cannot easily comprehend. Your job is to visit gently and leave.

---

## On Heat

The warmth is the first thing you will notice, and it will not let you forget it. This is not the warmth of sunlight, which you can step away from. This is the warmth of stone that is still becoming stone — still holding the memory of its molten origin, still bleeding heat into the air through long, slow exhalations. The walls are not hot enough to burn you. Not yet. But they are hot enough that if you lean against them, you will feel the slow cooking begin, a pressure that builds not in seconds but in minutes, a heat that works its way past your clothing and into your skin and, if you let it, into your tissues.

Carry something to sit on. A simple pad, an insulating layer — anything that creates a barrier between your body and the rock. You will want to rest. You will want to sit. And you will not want to sit on the floor, because the floor is where the thermal gradients live, the slow migration paths the creatures use, and sitting there means entering their territory at their level, which is a statement you may not intend to make. Something to sit on is not luxury. It is basic recognition of the environment's temperature.

Hydration becomes different here. The warmth is not aggressive, but it is persistent, and your body will lose water in ways that are easy to miss. You will not sweat the way you would in desert sun. The evaporation will be quieter, the loss more gradual, and it will be easy to mistake the mineral taste in the air for the taste of water on your tongue. It is not. The mineral taste is the stone. The dryness you feel is real. Drink before you think you need to. Carry more water than you expect to use.

---

## On Darkness

There is no light here. Not the faint light of a overcast sky, not the remembered light of a door left open somewhere in another universe. There is no light. The dark is not absence — it is presence, a weight that fills the space and makes every surface guessable. You will learn the space by touch and by the faint thermal shifts, the places where heat concentrates and the places where it dissipates. Your hands will become your primary sense within minutes.

Bring a light. Not a bright light — the creatures here have thermal senses, and a sudden flare of brightness will register to them as a change in temperature, a disruption, something new moving through their space. Something dim and steady will serve you better: a small, warm glow that you can move with, that does not announce you so much as it asks permission to be present. Think of it as a greeting rather than an intrusion.

When your light is on, you will see the creatures. When it is off, you will hear them.

---

## On the Creatures

They are the reason you came. They are also the thing most likely to go wrong.

Dense is the word used, and it is accurate, but density here means something different than it might in other contexts. In a city, density means many people in one place. Here, density means that every surface is claimed. Walls, ceiling, floor — each one supporting life, layered three and four deep in some places, one creature sleeping on another creature sleeping on the stone. They do not compete for space. They have solved the problem of how to share it, arrived at an arrangement so old that it has become architectural, structural, as fundamental to this place as the rock itself.

You cannot avoid them. Do not try. You will brush against one. You will step on the trail of another. These are not insults. They are facts of navigation. The creatures do not take offense at your presence any more than the stone takes offense at the heat. You are large. You are warm. You move through their space in ways they can sense but cannot control. What matters is not that you touch them — you will — but how you move, and what you do when you realize you have.

Move slowly. This is the single most important behavioral instruction. The creatures are slow. Their lives are measured in thermal gradients, in the slow drift from one warm patch to another, in the long patience of gestation and shedding and growth. Fast movement is not part of their world. Fast movement, to them, means predator, means danger, means something that enters at a speed their slow world does not accommodate. When you move quickly, you make yourself a threat. When you move slowly, you make yourself a presence — just another warm thing in a space full of warm things, moving through at a pace that makes sense.

If you touch one — if you brush a limb, or disturb a resting body, or find yourself suddenly in contact with something that was not there a moment ago — do not jerk away. They do not jerk. They cycle. They adjust. Hold still. Let the creature register your presence. Let it move or not move on its own schedule. Your instinct will be to pull back, to remove yourself from the contact, and this instinct is wrong. Pulling back fast is an aggressive act. Pulling back slowly is a conversation. Take three slow seconds to withdraw, and the creature will settle, will forget you, will return to the slow drift of its life.

You will not be able to see all of them. Some will be too small. Some will be too still. Some will be pressed so flat against the stone that they are more texture than body. Your light will show you the large ones, the active ones, the ones that move when you are near. Do not let the ones you cannot see convince you that you are alone. You are never alone here. You are always observed. You are always part of the density. Accept this and you will be fine. Fight it and you will spend your visit in a state of low-grade panic that will make everything harder.

## On Sound

The sound is structural. You will feel it before you hear it — a low vibration in the bones, in the chest, in the part of you that registers pressure rather than pitch. It is not loud. It does not demand anything. It is simply always there, the way the warmth is always there, the way the dark is always there.

This sound is not the creatures. Or rather, it is not only the creatures. It is the machines — the industrial hum that threads through the stone, embedded so deeply that the walls themselves seem to vibrate with it. You will not be able to tell where it comes from. It will seem to come from everywhere. It will become background, then absence, then background again, depending on your attention. Do not try to locate its source. There is no source. There is only the fact of it, the way there is a fact of warmth.

Your ears will adjust. After a few hours, you will stop noticing it unless something changes — unless a machine cycles, or a creature moves, or you move close to a section of wall where the embedded infrastructure runs thicker and the hum is louder. These changes will be small. The soundscape will feel stable. It is not. It is alive in its own way, slow and structural and constant.

---

## On Time

Everything here is slow. The erosion is slow. The cooling is slow. The creatures' lives are slow, measured in thermal gradients and slow migration and the long patience of shared space. The machines are slow — not inefficient, but operating on a timescale that has nothing to do with urgency.

You will be slow too, if you are wise. This is not a place to rush. There is nothing to rush toward and nothing to rush away from. There is only the space, and its warmth, and its dark, and the slow cycling of life on every surface. You will enter that rhythm whether you intend to or not. Your heartbeat will slow. Your thoughts will slow. You will find yourself waiting without knowing what you are waiting for, and then you will realize you are simply inhabiting the place, the way the creatures do, the way the machines do — holding, persisting, being present in the warmth.

Do not fight the slowness. It will not hurt you. It is simply what this place is. If you stay long enough, it is what you will become too.

---

## What to Bring

- **Water**. More than you think. The warmth will take it from you in ways you will not notice until you are thirsty, and by then it will be too late.
- **A sitting pad**. Insulated. Something that creates a barrier between you and the stone.
- **A light**. Dim. Warm-colored. Something you can carry and move with, not something that floods the space.
- **Patience**. You will not need to bring it. It will come to you, once you stop trying to leave.

---

## What to Leave

- **Urgency**. Nothing here requires it.
- **Expectations of space**. The walls will not move for you. Move toward them and you will find they are closer. Move away and you will find they are still there. The space is not yours to shape. It is yours to hold.
- **Fear of the dark**. It is not your enemy. It is the room.

---

## The Ending

You will leave the way you entered — through whatever passage brought you, whatever crack or gap or threshold separates this pocket from whatever lies beyond. The creatures will not see you go. Or they will, and it will not matter. You are not the first visitor and you will not be the last, and to them you will be just another warm shape that moved slowly and left no trace.

The stone will still be cooling. The machines will still be humming. The creatures will still be layered on creatures on stone, cycling through their slow lives in the warmth that has no name and no origin, only presence. You will carry the memory of it with you — the close walls, the mineral taste, the slow pulse of everything holding. And you will understand, in a way that is difficult to articulate, that some spaces do not need to be large to hold everything. That some warmth does not need to be bright to be felt.

That the pocket held you, and you held it back, and for a time, in the dark, you were enough.
