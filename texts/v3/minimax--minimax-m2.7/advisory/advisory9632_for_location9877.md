# Preparation Guide: The Memory-World of the Wet Stone

The place described in the passage is not hostile in the way of hostile places — it does not want to kill you, does not seem to want anything at all. But it is also not welcoming in the way that welcoming places are. It is simply *other*, in a way that will require conscious preparation to survive physically and maintain equilibrium psychologically. Below is a thorough preparation guide for an ordinary human traveler.

---

## Understanding What You Are Walking Into

Before gear, before protocol, before training — you need to internalize something about this place that the description conveys viscerally but that travelers sometimes fail to truly absorb: **you are going somewhere that was not made for you and does not care that you are there.**

This is not a wilderness. It is not even a "habitat" in the ecological sense, because that word implies a niche, a relationship, an evolutionary fit between organism and environment. Here, the environment *is* the organisms, and the organisms *are* the environment, and you are neither. You will be walking through a thing that has been running, uninterrupted, on its own processes for longer than anything else in this region. Nothing about it has been selecting for your comfort, your survival, your sanity. You are an anomaly moving through a system that has never encountered an anomaly.

This means several things:

- **Everything is alive.** Not just the creatures — the ground, the air, the moisture, the sediment. The skin. The stone beneath the skin. The erosion patterns. You cannot step on anything "dead." Even what appears inert is part of the living system.
- **Everything is in motion.** There is no stillness here, no quiet. The background noise of this world is not silence punctuated by life — it is life itself, audible, constant, layered. You will not experience "finding" creatures. You will be surrounded, covered, immersed in them at all times.
- **There are no hard edges.** No boundaries, no clean lines, no structures you can hold in your mind. The landscape is a flat, featureless skin that extends in every direction. Orientation will be difficult. Navigation will be mostly internal.
- **The sensory experience will be overwhelming** in ways that are hard to predict. The description mentions sound, motion, and light, but does not cover smell, which will be present and intense — organic, damp, metabolic, probably earthy and also something else, something you have no word for because nothing on Earth prepares you for the smell of a world that runs on chitin and bioluminescent fungus. Touch will be constant — the slickness, the humidity, the sensation of small legs on your suit, on your gloves, on any surface you contact. The physical presence of the noise will be felt as vibration, as pressure, as a hum in the teeth and chest.

Knowing this going in matters. You cannot prepare for a place you refuse to see clearly.

## Phase One: Physical Preparation

### Health and Physical Conditioning

**You need to be in excellent physical condition before you go.** This is not optional.

The environment is not physically demanding in the way of mountains or deserts — there is no elevation change, no extreme temperature, no obvious physical labor. But the sensory load, the constant alertness, the psychological strain of being in a place so fundamentally *other*, is exhausting in a way that cannot be overstated. People who have visited similar worlds report a fatigue that is not physical but feels physical, that settles into the body like lead, that makes every step feel heavier than it should.

You will be wearing sealed protective gear for the duration of your visit. Depending on your mission parameters, this could be hours or days. Sealed gear means no fresh air exchange, temperature regulation difficulties, hydration management challenges, and restricted movement. The better your baseline physical condition, the more reserve you have for coping with these stresses.

**Specific conditioning recommendations:**

- **Aerobic endurance training** for at least three months prior. Cardio capacity directly affects your ability to function in sealed gear with limited fresh air and while experiencing elevated stress responses.
- **Heat acclimatization training**, paradoxically. The environment is cool, but sealed gear causes heat buildup during physical exertion. Your body needs to be efficient at managing core temperature under these conditions.
- **Balance and proprioception exercises**. The slick surfaces and unpredictable texture of the skin will make footing uncertain. Stronger balance reduces falls, which is important because falling in this environment means contact with the skin, with the sediment, with the creatures, and potentially with a breach in your protective equipment.
- **Mental conditioning** — meditation, mindfulness, exposure training if available. You need to be able to maintain calm under sustained sensory bombardment. Panic will not kill you here, but it will incapacitate you, and incapacitation in this environment is a serious situation.

### Medical Considerations

**Consult with a multiverse medicine specialist before your visit.** Do not skip this step.

Because this world runs on biological processes that have no terrestrial parallel, you need a thorough medical evaluation and likely prophylactic treatments. Key concerns:

**Unknown pathogens and toxins.** The skin, the moisture, the creatures, the sediment, the air itself — all may harbor microorganisms, enzymes, or chemical compounds that are harmless to the local fauna but toxic, allergenic, or infectious to humans. Standard immunizations will not cover this. A multiverse medicine specialist can assess your destination's biology and recommend appropriate prophylactic antibiotics, antivirals, antifungals, or experimental treatments based on the known biochemistry of that world's life systems.

**Respiratory exposure.** Even with sealed gear, you may need to remove your mask briefly for equipment failure, for certain types of data collection, or in emergencies. The local atmosphere may be breathable but full of spores, particulates, or VOCs from the skin's metabolism. Know your risk profile before you go.

**Biological contamination.** The sediment in the low places is described as a fine grey powder made from the remains of creatures — it feeds the skin, it holds moisture, it is part of the closed loop of life. It may also be a reservoir for biological contamination. Your specialist should advise on whether this material poses ingestion or absorption risks.

**Duration limits.** Discuss with your specialist how long a human can safely remain in this environment. There may be cumulative exposure effects — something about the bioluminescent skin, the constant motion, the structural vibration, the metabolic compounds in the air — that causes problems over extended periods. Plan your visit within safe limits.

### Diet and Hydration Before the Visit

In the days before your visit:

- **Hydrate aggressively.** Your body will be under increased stress, and your gear may limit fluid intake during the visit. Pre-hydration improves your reserve.
- **Eat high-quality, easily digestible nutrition.** Complex carbohydrates, lean protein, healthy fats. Avoid foods that are hard to digest or that produce significant gas — you will be in sealed gear, and any discomfort will be amplified.
- **Consider a pre-visit gut treatment** if your multiverse medicine specialist recommends it. Some travelers have reported gastrointestinal distress from visiting worlds with radically different microbial environments, even without direct exposure. Fortifying your gut flora beforehand can help.

## Phase Two: Protective Equipment

This is the core of your preparation. The equipment you wear into this world is the barrier between you and a place that is not designed for you.

### Sealed Environmental Suit

**This is non-negotiable. You cannot enter this environment in open clothing. You cannot enter this environment with any exposed skin.**

The local biology is too alien, too dense, too active. Every surface — including your skin — is part of the living system. You cannot risk direct contact.

Your suit requirements:

**Full-body coverage.** Every square centimeter of you must be sealed inside protective material. Boots, gloves, hood, the works. No gaps, no exposed seams, no weak points where a small creature might push through or where liquid could seep in.

**Chemical and biological resistance.** The suit material must be impermeable to the compounds found in this world — the metallic moisture, the skin's metabolic products, the creature's biological fluids, the sediment. Standard hazmat materials may or may not be sufficient; consult with your equipment specialist about what this world's chemistry requires.

**Flexibility and dexterity.** The slick, unpredictable surfaces demand that you be able to move freely. Bulky, rigid suits will make you unstable, cause falls, and increase fatigue. Modern sealed suits offer varying degrees of flexibility — choose the best you can get for this environment. You will need to climb over ridges of thickened skin, navigate around clusters of creatures, maintain footing on surfaces that offer little traction.

**Thermal regulation.** The environment is cool but sealed suits build heat. Your suit needs active or passive cooling — either a small powered climate control system or a material designed to dissipate heat while maintaining seal integrity. Without this, you will overheat quickly.

**Durability against chitin and abrasion.** The creatures have many joints and hard exoskeletons. They will be crawling on you constantly — on your suit, on your gloves, on your helmet. The suit material must resist abrasion and puncturing from these interactions. It's not that the creatures are trying to hurt you, but their legs are sharp, their movements are relentless, and the pressure will be constant.

**Self-healing or redundant sealing.** If possible, choose a suit with self-healing materials or redundant sealing layers. You may not know if a seal is compromised until it's too late. Self-healing properties buy you time to exit safely if a small breach occurs.

### Helmet and Breathing Apparatus

**Sealed helmet with independent air supply.** The air in this world may be breathable, but breathing it directly is not advisable until you understand its full chemistry. Filtered, managed air is safer.

Your helmet requirements:

**Full face seal.** No gaps around the edges. The helmet must lock securely to the suit.

**Clear, durable visor.** You need to see the environment, and you need to see it clearly despite the dim bioluminescent light. The visor must be scratch-resistant, anti-fog, and tough enough to withstand contact with the skin, the creatures, and the environment in general.

**Internal heads-up display or data system.** Some travelers benefit from having environmental data visible inside the helmet — air quality readings, temperature, humidity. This information helps you make real-time decisions and provides psychological comfort.

**Communication system.** Unless you are visiting alone (which is not recommended), you need clear communication with your team. The noise of the environment will be incredibly loud — the clicking, scraping, wet feeding sounds, the bass vibrations of larger things. Standard voice communication may be difficult. Consider bone-conduction communication, text-based systems, or signal-based methods that can cut through the noise.

**Redundant oxygen supply.** If your air system is filtered rather than carried, you need backup. If your air is carried, you need reserve. Plan for supply failure.

### Mobility and Navigation Gear

**Lighting.** The world is dim — a grey, sourceless luminescence that is "enough to see by, barely." You will need your own light source, but consider carefully what kind:

- **Soft, diffuse lighting** rather than sharp beams. Point sources will create glaring reflections off the slick surfaces and may startle or disorient you. Even, low-level illumination that supplements the ambient light is better.
- **Wide-spectrum or bioluminescent-compatible lighting.** You want to see the creatures, the skin, the texture of the environment — your eyes need wavelengths that the local life uses. Standard white light may wash out the details.
- **Hands-free options.** You will need both hands free for balance, for equipment, for dealing with the constant contact of creatures on your suit. Head-mounted or suit-integrated lighting is best.

**Navigation tools.** The landscape is flat and featureless. You need to be able to track your position, heading, and distance traveled.

- **Internal positioning system** compatible with this universe's physics. Your multiverse travel equipment may include wayfinding — use it.
- **Physical markers.** If you will be moving through the environment, consider leaving temporary markers — stakes, reflectors, small devices that transmit signals. The skin will grow over them, the creatures will walk over them, but they may still be detectable.
- **Mapping tools.** A small handheld device that can record your path, build a map of your movement through the environment. The more data you have about where you have been, the easier it is to get back.

**Communication and tracking equipment for team coordination.** If you are visiting with others (and you should be), you need to be able to track each other's positions at all times. In this environment, separation is extremely dangerous. Small handheld transceivers, wearable beacons, any system that works through the local biological noise.

### Backup and Emergency Equipment

**Redundant suit.** If you are visiting for more than a few hours, bring a backup suit. Seal breaches happen. Having a replacement immediately available may save your life.

**Emergency seal kit.** Sealant compounds, patches, tape — materials that can temporarily seal a breach in your suit if one occurs. This is distinct from a full backup suit.

**Emergency air supply.** Reserve oxygen or filtered air, independent of your main system. In a sealed environment, running out of air is the most immediate lethal threat.

**Medical kit.** Specialized for this environment. Wound treatment for potential suit breach injuries, broad-spectrum antibiotics or treatments for unknown biological exposure, pain management, anti-inflammatory, anything your multiverse medicine specialist recommends.

**Emergency beacon.** If you become incapacitated — if you fall, if you are injured, if you cannot continue — you need a way to signal for extraction. A beacon that transmits through the local environment and reaches your home base or your team.

**Portable shelter or safe zone.** Depending on the duration of your visit, consider whether you need a small sealed space you can enter — a pop-up containment unit, a sealed tent — where you can remove your helmet, eat, drink, and rest without being exposed to the environment. This is probably only necessary for longer visits, but if you are planning to be there for more than a few hours, it may be essential for maintaining your function.

## Phase Three: Psychological and Sensory Preparation

The physical equipment is the easy part. The harder preparation is mental.

### Sensory Overload Training

**You need to expose yourself to intense, sustained sensory input before you go.** This is to build tolerance, to reduce the shock response, and to develop coping strategies for when your senses are overwhelmed.

Options:

- **White noise rooms.** Spend hours in environments with constant, loud, undifferentiated noise. The purpose is to learn to function while your auditory system is saturated.
- **Visual saturation environments.** Places with constant motion, flickering lights, overwhelming visual input. The goal is to learn to process visual information without becoming distracted or panicked.
- **Sensory integration training.** Programs designed to train your brain to manage conflicting or overwhelming sensory input. These are sometimes available through multiverse travel agencies or specialized training centers.
- **Controlled exposure to organic textures.** Spend time in environments with wet, slick, alive-feeling surfaces. Crawl through spaces with bugs, with fungal growth, with decomposing organic matter. Desensitize yourself to the visceral discomfort of touching living systems.

The goal is not to stop feeling overwhelmed — you will feel overwhelmed. The goal is to build the capacity to function despite that overwhelm, to maintain cognitive function and emotional regulation when every sense is telling you that something is wrong.

### Psychological Preparation and Framing

**How you think about this place matters.**

The description uses language that is beautiful but that can mislead. Words like "breathes," "pulse," "dream" — these are organic, even comforting words, but they describe something that is not comforting. The skin that breathes, the landscape that dreams — this is not a cozy, nurturing presence. It is an alien process, running for longer than you can imagine, that would absorb you, use you, cycle you through its metabolism without hesitation if you gave it the chance.

This is not a place of malice. It is a place of radical indifference. It will not try to hurt you, but it will also not try not to hurt you. You are not part of its system, and your survival is not something it is designed to support.

**Prepare yourself to hold this without flinching.** A lot of psychological difficulty in alien environments comes not from the environment itself but from the dissonance between what we expect and what we encounter. If you go in expecting a landscape, you will be constantly off-balance. If you go in expecting a living system that is vast, old, dense, and utterly indifferent to you, you can accept the experience on its own terms.

**Accept the experience as it is described — not as you might hope or imagine it.** The description says the fauna is overwhelming, that sound is a physical presence, that motion is everywhere. Do not hope that it will be less intense in person. Do not imagine that you will acclimate quickly. It will be exactly this intense, or more so, because description cannot capture the full weight of real presence.

**Do not try to make meaning of it.** Some travelers make the mistake of trying to understand this place, to fit it into a framework of meaning or purpose. Why is it here? What does it want? What is it for? These questions assume a human-style intentionality that the place does not have. It is not here for a reason. It does not want. It simply is. Let it be what it is without forcing your need for narrative onto it.

**Develop cognitive strategies for managing the overload.** Several approaches that have worked for experienced travelers:

- **Mindful presence.** Focus on individual sensory details — the exact color of the bioluminescence on a specific patch of skin, the pattern of movement in a small cluster of creatures, the temperature of a patch of moisture. This grounds you in the present and prevents dissociation.
- **Analytical framing.** Treat the experience as data. Observe, categorize, record. This creates a cognitive distance that reduces emotional reactivity.
- **Breathing and grounding.** Simple techniques — box breathing, body scan, physical grounding — can prevent panic and maintain function.
- **Pre-planned response scripts.** Have mental scripts for common situations: "If I feel panicked, I will stop moving, take three slow breaths, look at my hands, and name five things I can see." Scripts reduce cognitive load during high-stress moments.

### Contact and Interaction Protocols

**Before you go, establish strict rules about how you will interact with the environment.**

- **You will not touch the skin.** Even if you are curious. Even if it seems harmless. The skin is the base of the entire living system. Contact with it — with bare skin, but also with equipment that then contacts you — introduces unknown variables. Touch nothing.
- **You will not consume anything.** No water from the moisture, no material from the sediment, no creature no matter how dead it appears. This world is not edible for you.
- **You will not remain still for long periods.** Motion is life here, and stillness may attract attention or may simply cause you to accumulate a density of fauna on your suit that becomes problematic. Keep moving.
- **You will not touch creatures.** Even if they land on you, even if they seem calm, even if they seem to be investigating you with something that might be curiosity. Do not engage. Let them do what they do. Your only interaction should be observation and, if necessary, gentle removal.
- **You will have a defined exit plan.** A time limit, a waypoint for return, an extraction protocol. Do not let curiosity extend your visit beyond safe limits. The environment may become more dangerous with duration, or you may simply lose judgment capacity.

## Phase Four: Operational Preparation

### Visit Duration and Scheduling

**Keep your visit as short as possible while meeting your objectives.** The first hour is disorientation. The second hour is adjustment. Beyond that, you are in diminishing returns of useful experience and increasing risk of cumulative exposure effects.

If you must stay longer, schedule rest periods in a sealed shelter. Rotate team members if possible so that no one is exposed continuously. Do not attempt to "push through" fatigue — in this environment, fatigue becomes confusion, confusion becomes bad decisions, and bad decisions become serious problems.

### Team Structure and Roles

**Never visit alone.** At minimum, a team of two. More is better if the mission permits.

Suggested roles:

- **Primary observer.** The person whose job is to focus on the environment, take observations, direct the visit. This person should be the most psychologically prepared and experienced.
- **Safety and equipment monitor.** The person whose job is to watch the primary observer, monitor equipment status, watch for signs of distress, and enforce the exit protocol if needed.
- **Navigation and data management.** If the visit requires mapping, tracking, or data collection, a third person focuses on this while the others focus on observation and safety.

Team members should stay within visual range of each other at all times. The environment is disorienting; separation can become permanent.

### Entry and Exit Protocol

**Establish before you go: how you will enter, how you will exit, what you will do if something goes wrong.**

Entry protocol:

- Brief the team on objectives, time limits, and emergency procedures.
- Check all equipment seals, air supply, communication, lighting, medical kit.
- Enter together, slowly, giving yourself time to adjust to the sensory input from the moment of entry.
- If anyone is significantly distressed at entry, abort. Do not push through initial panic.

Exit protocol:

- Fixed time limit or triggered by mutual agreement once objectives are met.
- Do not delay exit for "just a few more minutes." This is how people die in alien environments.
- Return via the exact path you took in, following your navigation markers.
- Once out, proceed to decontamination and medical evaluation.

Emergency procedures:

- **Suit breach.** Stop, seal the breach with emergency sealant, do not remove the suit, exit immediately. Alert your team.
- **Medical emergency.** Protect, stabilize, extract. Your team's first priority is getting the affected person out, not continuing the mission.
- **Severe disorientation.** Stop moving, signal your team, wait for assistance. Do not continue navigating blindly.
- **Panic attack.** Sit down if safe, focus on breathing, use your pre-planned scripts. Your team will monitor and assist. Do not make major decisions during panic.
- **Communication failure.** If you lose contact with your team, stop and wait. Do not move to find them. If your beacon is still functioning, they will find you. If you must move, move in the direction that most likely leads toward the exit, and broadcast your status constantly.

### Decontamination and Post-Visit Medical Protocol

**You will not simply walk out and go home.** The environment has been on your suit, your equipment, possibly inside seal failures you didn't notice.

Standard decontamination:

- Full-body cleaning with specialized solution that neutralizes the chemistry of this world. Your multiverse travel provider should have access to appropriate solutions.
- Equipment cleaning and inspection.
- Biological sample collection if required for mission documentation — swabs, air samples, sediment samples, taken with appropriate precautions.

Medical evaluation:

- Full medical examination immediately after the visit.
- Follow-up examinations at 24 hours, 72 hours, and one week. Some biological exposures have delayed onset.
- Report any symptoms, even minor ones — headache, nausea, skin irritation, unusual fatigue, changes in perception. These may be signs of exposure.
- Quarantine if recommended by your medical specialist. Some multiverse biology can spread through human carriers even without symptoms.

## Phase Five: Integrative Preparation

### Research and Familiarization

**Before your visit, review everything known about this environment.**

- What is the biological chemistry? How does the skin metabolize? What are the creatures' evolutionary relationships, if understood? This background helps you interpret what you see and reduces the cognitive shock of the unfamiliar.
- What have previous visitors reported? Their accounts — especially their emotional and psychological reports — are more valuable than scientific summaries. Knowing that others found the noise unbearable, or that the first few minutes are the worst, helps you prepare.
- What are the known hazards? Even if the place is described as non-hostile, previous visitors may have identified risks — specific creature behaviors, areas of higher concentration, dangerous compounds in the sediment. Learn from their experience.

### Mental Rehearsal

**Spend time before your visit mentally rehearsing the experience.**

Close your eyes and imagine:

- Entering the space. The dim light. The slick feel of the stone through your boots. The immediate, overwhelming sound.
- The skin pulsing under your light. Its slow, biological rhythm.
- The creatures. The way they move. The density. The constant contact with your suit.
- The vibration in your chest from the noise. The metallic taste in the air if your mask has a small leak.
- Navigating the flat, featureless landscape. Moving toward a waypoint. Turning back.
- Maintaining your protocols. Not touching. Not stopping. Not consuming anything.
- The exit. The decontamination. The debrief.

Mental rehearsal builds familiarity. It cannot prepare you for the real experience, but it reduces the surprise.

### What to Bring (and What to Leave Behind)

**Bring:**

- Your sealed suit, thoroughly inspected.
- Your helmet and air system, with full supplies.
- Lighting, navigation, communication.
- Emergency equipment.
- A recording device if documentation is part of your mission — visual, auditory, whatever your objectives require.
- Water and nutrition if your visit is extended enough to require intake during the visit.
- Your training, your protocols, your mental scripts.

**Leave behind:**

- Curiosity that overrides caution.
- The assumption that this world will be comprehensible.
- The expectation that you can control the experience.
- Non-essential equipment that creates weight, bulk, or potential breach points.
- Sentimentality. This world does not need your feelings about it, and projecting sentiment will only create confusion.

## Final Note: What You Are Walking Toward

You are going to a place where the stone remembers water. A place covered in skin that breathes. A place that moves, feeds, dies, and grows without end, without change, without purpose — a closed loop of life that has been running longer than anything else in this region.

You will not understand it. You will not make friends with it. You will not be transformed by it in any way that matters to it. You are a brief anomaly in a system that does not care about anomalies.

This is not a failure of preparation. This is the nature of the place. Your preparation is not to make the environment bend to you — it is to allow you to observe it accurately, to function within it safely, and to exit intact. What you bring back is knowledge, data, experience — not ownership, not meaning, not connection.

Go prepared. Go humble. Go aware that you are walking into something vast and old and utterly indifferent to your presence.

And come back.
