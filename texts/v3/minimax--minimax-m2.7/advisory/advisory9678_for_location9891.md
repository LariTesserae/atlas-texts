# Travel Advisory: The Drowned Grove

## Preparing for a Visit to the Arboreal Waterworld of the High Plateau

---

## The Truth About the Water

Let us begin with the thing you cannot prepare for until you are inside it: *the water is everything.* Not as flood, not as river, but as atmosphere—suspended in the air, pooled in every depression, sheeting across every surface in quantities that would seem impossible if you tried to calculate the volume against the size of the space. The mist alone contains more water than most lowland storms.

When the grove-people say "dry," they mean something different than you do. They mean "the water is not actively running down my collar." They mean "my clothing has reached a state of dampness that is merely uncomfortable rather than immediately dangerous." You must recalibrate your entire relationship with moisture before you arrive, or the first hour will feel like drowning in air.

Your body will adjust. The human physiology is more adaptable than it credits itself, and within two days the sensation of wet clothing against skin will fade to background noise. What will not fade is the way water finds every failure in your equipment, every gap in your seals, every moment of inattention. *Assume wetness. Plan for wetness. Make peace with wetness.*

---

## Clothing: The Architecture of Damp

### The Inner Layer

Begin with what touches your skin. You need fabrics that move moisture away from your body while retaining enough dampness that they do not become crisp and uncomfortable. Silk performs better than you might expect in this environment—it wicks well, dries moderately fast, and develops a comfortable weight when damp. Wool is functional but heavy when saturated, and the constant moisture inhibits its natural odor-resisting properties. Synthetic athletic fabrics are adequate but will develop a persistent smell that no amount of rinsing will eliminate; the grove's humidity preserves everything, including the evidence of your exertions.

Avoid cotton in all its forms. Cotton that is wet is cold, heavy, and slow to dry. In the grove's constant dampness, cotton never stops being wet.

### The Outer Layer

Your primary concern is not cold—it is *persistent saturation.* The temperature holds in that maddening range just above cool, the kind of morning that never commits to becoming afternoon. You will not hypothermize from weather. You will become uncomfortable from the cumulative effect of hours of damp, days of damp, the way your clothing never fully dries, the way you can wring water from your collar and not be surprised.

A loose outer layer of woven plant fiber serves better than synthetic waterproofing. The grove-people's garments are woven from fibers cultivated here specifically for this purpose—materials that accept moisture without losing structure, that do not delaminate or peel when kept perpetually damp. If you cannot acquire such fabric, loose-woven linen or hemp approaches the right qualities. The weave should have gaps. Let the water pass through rather than pool against you.

You will not stay dry. The goal is to stay *functional.*

### Footwear

This is where most visitors fail. The platforms are grown, not built—their surfaces undulate with the logic of living wood, and their edges roll rather than cut. Every step is on a curve. Every surface that is not a deliberate walkway is slick with moisture, with the excretion of organisms that have made their peace with wetness, with the slow seep of water through the root-mats.

Your footwear must grip curves. It must conform to surfaces that change elevation by millimeters rather than inches. It must drain water rather than trap it. And it must do this while remaining soft enough that you can feel the surface beneath you, because feeling the surface is how you navigate—your feet read the grain, the texture, the subtle tilts that tell you which way a path turns.

Minimalist footwear with aggressive tread performs better than hiking boots. Water sandals with good footbed traction can work. The grove-people go barefoot on the most-used paths because the living wood is soft and warm and the water does not trouble them—but their feet have developed calluses and proprioception over generations that yours have not. Protect yourself accordingly, but do not insulate yourself from the surface.

Bring extra footwear. Rotate. Allow pieces to dry between wearings, knowing that "dry" in this context means "damp but not actively dripping."

### Hand Protection

You will use your hands constantly—to steady yourself on root-railings that are slick with condensation, to grip branches when the path narrows, to catch yourself when you slip (and you *will* slip). Gloves that retain grip when wet are worth their weight in gold. Leather loses traction when saturated. Synthetic rubbers maintain it. The grove-people wear no gloves at all, but their hands have grown tough and certain in ways that cannot be simulated in a single visit.

Bring multiple pairs. You will lose one to a deep pool. You will tear another on a protruding knot. Plan for losses.

## Gear and Equipment

### Carriage

The grove's paths are three-dimensional in the way that lowland paths are not. A bridge is also a ladder. A walkway curves upward into a ramp. To move between levels, you may need to climb, or swim, or take a boat through a channel that threads between root-columns. A standard backpack will snag on everything. A bag that hangs low on your back will catch on overhead branches, will throw you off balance on narrow paths, will drag in water when you crouch under a low-hanging root.

Bags that ride high and close, that distribute weight across your chest as well as your back, that have no loose straps or dangling buckles, serve better. The grove-people carry things in pouches at their belts, in baskets lashed to root-hooks they have trained into their structures. You can improvise attachment points once you understand the grove's vocabulary of shapes, but for your first visit, a chest-harness bag allows you to move through any configuration of space without your cargo becoming a liability.

### Watercraft

If you are wise, you will acquire a small boat before you enter in earnest. The grove's paths often go vertical when you wish to go horizontal, and water remains the most efficient medium for transport. The boats here are shallow-drafted, wide, carved from the soft wood of the grove-trees and sealed with resins that the trees produce when prompted. They are paddled rather than rowed—the motion of oars catches on overhanging roots—and poled in the deeper channels where the root-columns grow thick.

A collapsible canoe or inflatable raft serves well enough for initial exploration. If you bring something rigid, bring padding; the wood of the grove is not forgiving to impacts, and you will scrape hull against root more often than you intend.

### Tools

Bring a knife. The wood of the grove-trees is soft, almost spongy, and it takes an edge beautifully. You can carve a handhold, a notch, a marker. The trees will grow around what you cut, eventually incorporating your marks into their own growth—a temporary addition to the living architecture. You cannot permanently alter this place, only briefly interrupt its pattern.

Bring light. The grove filters sunlight through a thousand gaps, creating conditions that alternate between bright and shadowed in ways that confuse depth perception. A headlamp with red-light capability (to preserve night vision and avoid disturbing creatures) serves better than a handheld light, since you will need both hands for climbing, for paddling, for catching yourself when you slip.

Bring something to contain fire. The grove-people keep small hearths, contained in stone grown into the larger root structures, but the smoke from open flame disperses poorly in the humidity and the still air of the interior. If you must have fire, contain it. If you cannot contain it, do without.

## The Sound

You cannot prepare for the sound. It defeats your expectations before you cross the threshold.

Begin with this: the grove is never quiet. Even in what the residents call the "still hours"—the deep part of night when most activity ceases—the grove produces noise that would be called "constant" by any reasonable measure. Water drips from leaf to leaf, accumulating in pools that overflow into other pools, moving always downward through the architecture of root and soil. Insects chorus in frequencies that layer into a single chord that rises and falls with the temperature, the light, the hour. Creatures call to one another across the three-dimensional space—birds above, frogs below, things in between that have no names in your language.

On top of this, the grove adds its own noise. Wood creaks as it bears weight. Roots shift in their slow growth. Water moves through channels you cannot see, depositing and withdrawing, carrying the sound of its passage through the living architecture. The grove-people speak in voices pitched to carry through the noise, consonants that punch, vowels that linger. They shout often. They sing sometimes—long phrases that the grove seems to absorb and reflect, throwing back transformed, so that a single voice becomes a chorus, a chorus becomes an environment.

You will not sleep as you sleep in quiet places. Your body will learn to sleep in noise, but it takes time, and the first nights will be thin and strange. Bring whatever ritual enables your sleep—auditory masking, chemical aids if you use them, anything—and accept that you will lose sleep until your nervous system stops treating the sound as a threat.

The silence of the deep pools, where the water goes still and dark, is the closest thing to quiet the grove offers. Even there, you hear your own heartbeat.

## The Physical Reality

### Altitude

The grove sits high. Water cascades downward through its structure and off its edges into a fall you hear but do not see—a distant white noise beneath everything else. The air carries the thinness of elevation, a quality that takes your breath when you first arrive and never quite loses its edge. You will acclimate. Within a week, you will forget what sea-level breathing felt like.

But the first days are work. Your lungs labor. Your heart beats faster for the same effort. Your sleep is disrupted by your body's adjustment. Move slowly. Rest often. Do not attempt the grove's more ambitious paths until your body has found its new baseline. Altitude sickness is not a risk here—your destination is not so high—but your body will tell you that something has changed, and you must listen.

### Three-Dimensional Navigation

The grove is not a flat place, and its paths do not obey the assumptions that flat places encode into their visitors. A destination that appears close may be above you, below you, or reachable only by water. Paths that seem to end continue around roots, through hollows, up ladders grown from trained branches. The geography is spatial rather than planar—you navigate through a volume, not across a surface.

Carry a simple chart if you can, but understand that the grove's residents do not use maps. They use *knowledge*—a familiarity with the space that comes from generations of moving through it, from learning its logic by living it. You will not achieve this knowledge in a single visit, but you can begin to feel the logic: the way water moves downward, the way light filters through gaps, the way the most-used paths show wear. The grove reveals itself to those who pay attention.

Swimming is likely. The channels between platforms are often navigable only by boat, but sometimes boats are not convenient and you will be asked to swim. The water is cool but not dangerous. The deep pools are dark and their bottoms are unknown, but the creatures that live in them are not aggressive toward beings who do not threaten them. Swim steadily, do not thrash, and you will arrive where you need to go.

### The Wetness Itself

After a sufficient time, you will stop noticing the wetness. This is the grove's gift to those who stay: the cessation of the struggle, the acceptance of saturation as a condition of existence rather than a temporary state to be escaped. Before that gift arrives, however, the wetness is everything. Your skin softens and wrinkles. Your clothing gains weight. Your hair mats against your skull. Water finds your ears, your nose, the spaces between your fingers.

The grove-people manage this through practice. They have developed postures that shed water, movements that distribute it rather than accumulate it. They do not fight the wetness; they move with it. Watch how they hold their bodies. Notice where the water pools when they sit, how they position themselves when they sleep, which gestures are natural and which are attempts to escape an escape that cannot be achieved.

You cannot out-wick dampness. You can only accept it.

## The Grove's Inhabitants

### The People

Sparse, as described, but not unfriendly. The grove permits visitors, and the people extend the grove's permission. But they do not go out of their way to welcome you. They are busy—with the tending of the roots, the harvesting of the water, the maintenance of the systems that generations of their ancestors built. You are interesting enough to observe, unusual enough to warrant attention, but not significant enough to interrupt their work.

Approach with patience. Wait to be addressed. Offer what you have that might be useful—this world's currency is function, not exchange, and the grove-people value things that serve. A tool well-made. A technique learned elsewhere. A willingness to work without immediate reward. They will see these things and adjust their estimation of you accordingly.

They will feed you, if you ask. They will shelter you, if you need it. They will teach you, if you demonstrate that you can learn. But they will not pursue your company. The emptiness of the grove is part of its design, and they have made peace with solitude. To impose yourself on them would be a kind of violence. Let them come to you, at their pace.

### The Creatures

The frogs in the deep pools are your first introduction to the grove's fauna. They are small and watchful, their eyes catching light, their skins patterned in greens and browns that disappear against the water and the wood. They do not flee. They do not approach. They wait, as the grove waits, as everything here seems to wait.

The birds above are louder and more curious. They call to one another in phrases that almost resolve into language, that seem to carry information you cannot decode. They nest in the angles where root meets root, in hollows that the grove-people have shaped over generations to accommodate them. They are not tame, but they are not frightened. They have lived alongside humans long enough to understand that fear is not required.

The insects are the constant. They fill the air with their chord, rising and falling with the temperature, the light, the hour. They do not bite—or rather, the ones that bite do so in ways that are more curious than aggressive, exploratory rather than feeding. You will develop a sense for which creatures notice you and which do not. Do not swat at the grove's insects; they are part of the design.

The water itself holds things. You will not see them clearly—the pools are dark, their depths uncertain—but you will sense movement, the displacement of water by bodies you cannot identify. They are not dangerous to you. They have their place in the ecology, and you are too large to be prey.

## What to Expect Upon Arrival

Your first hour in the grove will be disorientation. The light will seem dim and then bright, depending on the angle of the gaps above. The sound will seem overwhelming and then, suddenly, normal—as if your hearing has adjusted and there was never any other state. The wetness will seem like a crisis and then, within minutes, like a condition you have always known.

You will slip. The wood is not what your feet expect, and your weight will shift without warning, and you will reach for something to catch yourself and find only water-slicked root beneath your hand. You will curse, or gasp, or simply fall. The grove-people will not laugh. They will not even look. They have seen it before. They have done it themselves.

You will ask for something—a place to sleep, a direction to go, a person to speak with—and your question will be answered with a patience that is not warmth but not coldness either. You will be given what you need. You will not be given more.

You will feel, in your chest, the way the place holds you at a distance. This is not hostility. This is *permission.* The grove permits you. The grove's people permit you. You are temporary, and the grove knows this, and your presence here is a brief interruption in something that has been continuing longer than you have existed as a species.

Accept the distance. Walk within it. Let the water find you and pass through you and teach you, in its slow way, that saturation is not drowning. It is only the beginning of a different relationship with moisture, with sound, with the slow patience of things that grow.

---

## Final Notes

Bring no technology you cannot bear to lose. The humidity will find every seal, every battery compartment, every vulnerable point. Bring no paper that is not waterproofed. Bring no cotton. Bring no expectations of dryness.

Bring curiosity, and patience, and a willingness to fall.

The grove is not made for you. But it permits you, and that permission is not nothing. Walk gently. Learn the paths. Let the water teach you.

*Safe travels.*
