# Preparation Guide: The Perpetual Basin

## Overview

You are visiting a world that has achieved what most engineered systems cannot: perfect equilibrium. It has been this way for as long as records exist, and nothing suggests it will change. The ground is metal, the air is warm, the moisture is constant, and the constructed fauna moves in patterns that have never shifted. Your task is to enter this system and remain yourself—psychologically, biologically, and physically—while everything around you remains perfectly, eternally the same.

---

## Personal Gear

### Breathing and Atmosphere

The air is breathable, as far as your instruments can tell—nitrogen-oxygen mix, slightly elevated humidity, no detected toxins. But this world was not designed for human respiration. Assume the atmosphere has been optimized for its engineered fauna, not for you. Bring a comfortable respirator regardless: not necessarily airtight, but something that filters and humidifies. Your lungs are not adapted to breathing warm, saturated air for extended periods. The moisture itself becomes an irritant after hours—the filter will help.

### Temperature Regulation

The warmth comes from below and from the machinery embedded in the landscape. It never varies. You will never cool down. This is not a place where hypothermia is a risk; it is a place where your body will constantly be fighting to dump heat into an environment that will not accept it.

- Light, breathable clothing. You want to maximize your own evaporative cooling.
- Cooling vest or portable thermal regulation device. If you have one designed for high-heat environments, bring it. If you don't, bring extra water and plan for shorter exposure sessions.
- Accept that you will be uncomfortable. The warmth is not hostile, but it is relentless.

### Moisture Management

Water is everywhere. It seeps up through the metal, condenses on every surface, runs in channels, falls as drizzle. You will be wet constantly. Plan accordingly:

- Moisture-wicking underlayers that dry quickly.
- Waterproof outer layers for zones where you're standing in pooled moisture.
- Skin protection. The constant wetness will soften your skin, making it vulnerable to abrasion and infection. Barrier cream on exposed skin, moisture-resistant dressing on any cuts or abrasions.
- Footwear that can handle being submerged. Sealed boots that don't let water in, or breathable boots that drain and dry. Test them before you arrive.
- Dry storage for anything you cannot afford to get wet—electronics, medicine, emergency supplies.

## Biological Considerations

### Engineered Fauna

These organisms are not natural. They have been designed, bred, optimized over timescales that make human civilization look brief. They are everywhere—on the ground, on vertical surfaces, in the channels, filling the spaces between structures in densities that make movement ripple outward like wind through grass. They are not hostile to you, because they were never designed to interact with you. But they are not inert, either.

- They are engineered tissue: membrane, fiber, flesh. They breathe. They digest. They reproduce within parameters.
- Do not assume they are harmless to human biology. Engineered life may use proteins and compounds that your immune system has never encountered. Treat them the way you would treat any unknown organism—with distance and caution.
- Avoid skin contact where possible. Long-sleeved, full-coverage clothing. If they contact you, wash the area with clean water when possible.
- Do not ingest local water. The channels carry moisture that has been in contact with engineered organisms and industrial processes. Assume it is not safe for human consumption.

### Pathogenic Risk

The world is saturated with constructed life. Even if the organisms are not directly dangerous, they create an environment rich in biological material that has never been part of human evolutionary history.

- Full-body protective suit may be warranted, depending on your sensitivity profile and the duration of your visit. At minimum, sealed goggles and respirator.
- Antifungal prophylaxis. Warm, wet environments encourage fungal growth on human tissue. If you are staying more than a few hours, consider precautions.
- Antibiotics effective against engineered microorganisms. Consult your medical protocols.
- Decontamination protocols when leaving. You do not want to carry foreign biological material back to other worlds.

### Resource Management

Food and water must come with you. The local environment offers nothing you can safely consume. Plan for more than you think you'll need—the warmth will increase your metabolic rate, and you will need hydration more frequently than in a temperate environment.

## Psychological Preparation

### The Monotony Problem

The strangest thing about this world is that nothing changes. Not the temperature, not the humidity, not the fauna densities, not the machinery hum. You will look at a structure and it will look exactly the same an hour later, a day later, a week later. The horizon will look exactly the same in every direction. There are no seasons, no weather variation, no day-night cycle (or if there is, it is too subtle to notice).

This is deeply disorienting to human psychology. Your brain expects change. It expects gradients, wear patterns, places where the system has been stressed and healed. When everything is perfectly stable, you lose your reference points. Time becomes abstract. Space becomes abstract. You may find yourself unable to estimate how long you've been walking, or whether you've passed the same junction twice.

Prepare for this.

- Establish strict routines. Eat at regular intervals, check instruments at intervals, log your observations. Routines give you internal structure that the environment cannot provide.
- Bring anchors. Photographs, recordings, objects from home. The visual monotony will erode your sense of self if you do not actively maintain it.
- If possible, travel with others. Conversation and shared observation break the uniformity. Alone in the Basin, you will have nothing to measure yourself against.

### Sensory Adaptation

The sounds are minimal and consistent: the machinery hum, the soft wet movement of fauna, the faint patter of drizzle. These sounds never vary. After a while, your brain will stop registering them, and then it will start generating its own stimulation—phantom sounds, visual trails, intrusive thoughts.

- Earplugs or white noise may help, but they can also increase the disorientation by removing what little variation exists.
- Alternatively, train yourself to listen actively. Make the sounds into data. Count the fauna movements per minute in a given area. Note the pitch variations in the machinery hum. Turn the monotony into a subject of study.

### The Risk of Dissociation

Some travelers report that extended exposure to uniform environments leads to dissociative states—a sense of unreality, of being detached from their own body, of not trusting their own perceptions. The Basin is designed for engineered organisms that do not have human psychology. It does not care about your sense of self.

- Schedule breaks. Leave the environment regularly. Do not attempt to spend weeks inside it without resupply and reset.
- Maintain a log. Writing observations forces you to engage with your experience rather than floating through it.
- Watch for warning signs. If you find yourself unable to remember what you did an hour ago, or if the environment starts feeling like a dream, leave immediately.

## Navigation

### Orientation

Without natural features—no sun, no stars, no mountains, no rivers—orientation is a serious problem. The landscape is a grid. All directions look the same. You will lose your sense of direction within minutes of losing your reference point.

- Bring GPS or equivalent positioning technology. The metal substrate may interfere with signals—test your equipment before arrival.
- Leave beacons. Drop markers at regular intervals, especially at junctions and decision points. You cannot trust your memory here.
- Establish a return protocol. Always know how to get back to your entry point. Mark your path in multiple ways.

### Movement Protocol

The channels between structures are the safest routes—they are designed for fauna movement, which means they are clear and predictable. Vertical surfaces are coated with fauna. Open floors are safer but offer no shade or cover.

- Move in patterns. Do not wander aimlessly. Establish a logical route and follow it.
- Respect the fauna. Do not step on dense clusters. Do not block channels. You are the intruder; move around them.
- Mark your route. You will pass the same junctions repeatedly. Without marks, you cannot tell if you've been here before.

## Survival Contingencies

### Equipment Failure

The environment is stable, but you are not. Your equipment will degrade—filters will clog, seals will soften, electronics will fail in the humidity. Carry spares. Plan for your respirator to fail, your GPS to die, your dry storage to leak.

- Triple redundancy for critical systems.
- Sealed storage for electronics.
- Manual backup for navigation—paper maps, compass (if it works here), written route descriptions.

### Medical Emergency

You are hours from any rescue in an environment designed for organisms that do not need human medical care.

- Full medical kit. Treat this as an extended expedition into a remote, uninhabited region.
- Wound care is critical. The fauna can be abrasive, the moisture will encourage infection. Aggressive wound management.
- Heat stroke and dehydration are the most likely acute threats. Monitor yourself and your companions.

### Behavioral Emergency

If you or a companion experiences severe dissociation, panic, or psychological breakdown in the Basin, do not attempt to help them alone. The environment will erode your judgment as fast as theirs.

- Evacuate immediately.
- Do not leave anyone alone. If one person is compromised, the other must maintain contact and orientation for both.

---

## Departure

When you leave, decontaminate thoroughly. You have been immersed in an environment saturated with engineered biological material. Wash, change clothes, seal your gear for processing. Do not assume you are clean.

The Basin will still be there. The fauna will still be moving in the same patterns, the machinery will still be humming, the drizzle will still be falling. Nothing will have changed.

You will have changed, even if you cannot feel it yet.

---

## Summary

- The warmth is relentless. Manage your body temperature.
- The moisture is constant. Protect your skin and keep your gear dry.
- The fauna is everywhere. Maintain biological barriers.
- The environment is stable. Establish internal structure.
- The landscape is uniform. Navigate with technology and marks, not memory.
- The monotony is deep. Bring companions, routines, and anchors.

You are visiting a world that has solved stability at the cost of change. You cannot solve stability—you can only manage your own fragility within it. Go prepared, stay aware, and do not stay too long.
