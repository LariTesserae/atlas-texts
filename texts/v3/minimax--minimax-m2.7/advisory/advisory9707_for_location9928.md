# A Preparation Guide for Visitors to the Newly-Risen Plateau

## Preliminary Notes on This Document

What follows is drawn from a synthesis of observation reports, testimonial accounts, and extrapolation from first principles. The plateau has been catalogued under seventeen provisional designations by the Cartographic Consortium; among travelers, it is most commonly referred to as "the Basin of Unfinished Things" or simply "the Young Place." The latter name carries a weight the former does not: the plateau's defining characteristic is not its geography but its temporal state. It is, in the most literal sense, a world still being born.

You should know this before you go. The plateau will not be what you expect, because it does not yet know what it is itself.

---

## I. What You Are Walking Into

The plateau rose recently. This is not metaphor. The basalt underfoot retains the memory of its formation: sharp ridges, unweathered faces, surfaces that have not yet learned to be worn smooth. The rock cuts if you are careless. The channels across which water runs are new, and water in this place is not entirely trustworthy.

Speaking of water: do not expect it to behave.

The water of this plateau exists in several distinct categories, and you should understand each before you arrive.

**The suspended falls** are the most immediately disorienting. Along the southern rim, water descends from the higher plateau surface in wide curtains that simply stop. They hang in the warm air, catching light, refusing to complete their descent. You cannot see what holds them. You cannot see through them. Approaching the rim, you will feel moisture on your face while the falls themselves are meters away from you, suspended at the edge of a drop that they have chosen not to complete. Do not assume that proximity to these falls means safety. The mechanism is unknown. The water is not waiting for you to understand it.

**The basin pools** have no visible bottom. Travelers who have lowered weights into them report that the line continues long past any reasonable depth before either reaching a bottom that is simply not there or (in some reports) before the weight stops returning any information at all—neither sinking further nor having reached anything, just ceasing to be accountable for. Do not swim in these pools. The fish you see there (brighter than they should be, stiller than they should be) are not warnings. They are simply present, and their presence is not the warning. The depth is the warning.

**The sheet-water** across the basalt is clear to the point of invisibility. You will be walking on what appears to be dry rock and find your boots soaked within steps. The mineral taste that accompanies it makes the back of the throat feel strange; most travelers report a sensation of mild disorientation after drinking it. Some say this sensation fades. Others say they have never been entirely certain it faded, and that they no longer trust their certainty about things.

**The seeps and trickles** are the water's slow thoughts. They move with a patience that suggests the water has not yet decided its destination. Follow them and you will find yourself moving in gentle spirals toward the basin floor. This is not necessarily bad—unless you do not wish to reach the basin floor.

## II. Physical Preparation

### Clothing

The plateau is warm and wet. Not merely humid—*wet*, in the sense that the air has given up any pretense of boundary between itself and moisture. Your clothing will become damp. Your clothing will remain damp. Standard moisture-wicking fabrics are inadequate; they are designed for sweat, not for an atmosphere that has decided to participate in your dampness from all directions.

**Recommendations:**

- Wear loose layers of lightweight, quick-drying natural fibers. Linen performs well. Silk performs adequately. Cotton is a liability.
- The moss will stain. Everything will stain. Bring clothing you do not intend to preserve.
- Your outer layer should be durable against the sharp basalt. The rock does not care about your sleeves.
- Footwear should be closed-toe, water-resistant, and have soles with aggressive traction. The basalt is slick even when dry, and the moss-covered surfaces are treacherous. You will fall. Plan for this.
- Consider gaiters. They will fill with debris, but they will protect your ankles from the sharp ridges of new rock that the moss has not yet softened.

### Hydration

Bring more water than you think you will need. The water of the plateau is present and drinkable, but its taste is unsettling and its effects are not fully understood. Carry purification tablets or a filter; the mineral content is unusual and may cause gastric disturbance in visitors whose home-worlds have different mineral balances.

The warm wet air will accelerate dehydration in ways that the temperature does not suggest. You will sweat without noticing. You will feel damp constantly and therefore not notice that you are also thirsty. Drink on a schedule, not on thirst. Dehydration on the plateau is insidious; by the time you notice it, you may already be compromised.

## III. Navigation and Orientation

This is the most practically dangerous aspect of the plateau, and it is dangerous precisely because it does not feel dangerous.

The basin is large enough that you cannot see its far edges, but it does not feel large. The landscape does not change character. Everything is dense wet green, layered, continuous. You will walk for what feels like an hour and believe you have crossed a small portion of the basin, and then you will turn around and not recognize anything. The nearest forms are sharp and saturated; the distant ones are blue-grey and soft; the horizon is always past where you thought it was.

This is not disorientation in the sense of being lost. You may always know which direction you came from. But you will not be able to judge distance, and you will not be able to judge scale, and you will find that the landscape seems to *fold* in ways that you cannot anticipate.

**Recommendations:**

- Establish fixed landmarks before moving. A distinctive rock formation, a specific pool, a tree with an unusual shape. When you leave a landmark behind, do not assume you will be able to return to it by walking in the opposite direction of your current heading.
- Consider marking your path. Chalk, ribbon, luminescent markers—something physical, something you can find again. The plateau does not respond to your sense of direction.
- Do not rely on the position of the sun. The light falls at angles that seem plausible but do not match the sky. This has been verified by instruments. The sun is in the wrong place for the shadows. The shadows are in the wrong place for the light.
- Time estimates are unreliable. Bring a stopwatch if timing matters to you. Your internal sense of duration will be affected by the landscape's continuity.

### On the Question of Scale

The plateau is not vast. It is not a plain or an ocean. It is a contained basin, probably not more than twenty kilometers across at its widest. But it *feels* larger, and this feeling is not an illusion in the psychological sense. The space does something. The depth perception is off, and not uniformly off—different distances seem to expand and contract depending on what is around them, what the light is doing, what the vegetation is doing. Some travelers have described it as "a room too large to be a room." This is accurate.

Do not let this bother you. Let it become interesting.

## IV. The Light

The light of the plateau is its most difficult characteristic to prepare for, because there is no way to reproduce it beforehand and no way to describe it that prepares you for the experience.

Light enters the basin in fragments—shafted through the canopy, reflected bright from the surfaces of pools, turned amber where it passes through water-filed leaves. It makes the green of the moss glow from within. At the edges of the pools it turns the water silver and gold. It is beautiful. It is also *wrong*, and the wrongness accumulates.

You will not notice it at first. You will notice that the shadows fall in unexpected places, that the light pools in what should be shade, that the depth of things seems greater than the space should allow. Then you will stop noticing, because you will adapt. And then you will realize that you have been navigating by a light that is not quite the light of the sky, and you will not be certain what you have been navigating by instead.

**Recommendations:**

- Accept that you will not see the plateau accurately. There is no "accurate" here.
- Carry a compass. The magnetic field of the plateau is stable; you can trust north. Do not trust anything else about orientation.
- Carry a light source. When the light fails you—and it will seem to fail you, even if it is still present—you will need something that you know the behavior of.

## V. The Silence

The silence of the plateau is not silence. Water is never absent. You will hear water falling, running, pooling, trickling. You will hear the occasional bird, the splash of something in a pool, the creak of branches. The sounds are present.

But the sounds do not carry. Each noise exists alone, isolated, with total silence between it and the next. The wet growth absorbs all echo and reverb. The air seems to hold sound only long enough to deliver it before letting it fall away into nothing.

This is not merely quiet. Quiet is the absence of sound. This is the *presence* of silence—the active, total silence that fills every gap between sounds and does not permit sound to linger. If you shout, your voice will arrive at its destination and then cease. It will not echo back. It will not reverberate. It will simply be gone.

**Psychological implications:**

The human auditory system uses echoes and ambient sound to navigate and to confirm presence. In the plateau's silence, you will feel exposed. Your sounds will not confirm you are there; they will arrive and vanish, leaving you uncertain whether they occurred at all.

Many travelers report that the silence begins to affect them after several hours. They speak less. They become more attentive to the single sounds that do occur. Some find this peaceful. Others find it deeply unsettling. You should prepare for the possibility that you will not know which you will feel.

**Recommendations:**

- Mentally prepare for a soundscape unlike anything you have experienced. Not quiet, not loud, not echoic—just *isolated*.
- If you travel with companions, establish visual signals for communication. Assume that you will not be able to rely on voice.
- Note that your own footsteps will sound strange. They will seem to arrive without precedent and end without echo. This is normal.

## VI. The Fauna

Fauna is rare. You may see no animals at all, or you may see several. Neither outcome is unusual.

**Birds** cross the canopy gaps. Their calls are answered from directions that seem implausible—one answer, or many, or the same single voice returning from every direction at once. The acoustic properties of the basin may cause this. The birds may cause this. You will not be able to determine which.

**Fish** in the still water are too bright and too still. Their colors are not quite right—more saturated, more precisely defined, than any natural coloration should produce. They hold their position without apparent effort, as if they are waiting for the water to remember how to move them. Some travelers have reported that the fish do not respond to their presence. Others have reported that the fish responded to something they could not identify, moving in unison toward a point in the water that held nothing visible.

**Small things** move in the deep moss and are still. This is the most you will typically see of ground-level fauna. Something small, something quick, something that stops.

Do not interact with the fauna. Do not attempt to catch fish from the bottomless pools. Do not follow the small moving things. The animals of the plateau are adapted to a world you do not understand. Your presence is a disruption; do not make it a crisis.

## VII. Health and Physical Concerns

### Terrain Hazards

The young basalt is sharp. Even the moss does not fully pad it; moss grows on top of the rock, not instead of it. You will cut yourself if you are not careful. Carry a first aid kit with pressure bandages. The cuts are not deep, but they are clean, and they bleed freely.

The moss-covered surfaces are slick. This should be obvious. It is less obvious when you are standing in the middle of what appears to be solid ground and find your feet sliding in directions you did not intend.

The deep pools have no visible bottom. This has been established. Do not assume that the edges of these pools are shallow. The water is clear to a point and then it is not, and the not is not a gradual darkening but an abrupt cessation of information.

### Atmospheric Conditions

The warmth is not extreme, but it is constant. The humidity is extreme and constant. Heat exhaustion is a real risk. Rest frequently. Drink on schedule. Do not trust your body's thirst signals; they are compromised by the wet air.

The water carries minerals that make the throat feel strange. This is not dangerous in the short term, but travelers with compromised immune systems or unusual sensitivities should consider carrying their own water. The mineral content has not been fully analyzed; it may interact unpredictably with biological systems that did not evolve in its presence.

### Psychological Concerns

The plateau is not haunted. Nothing in it wishes you harm. But it is a place that has not finished being born, and this has effects on the human mind.

Travelers have reported:

- A sense of being observed by the landscape itself, not by any entity but by the *act* of the landscape becoming
- Difficulty remembering the colors of things after leaving, replaced by a persistent green-and-gold that does not accurately represent what was seen
- Dreams, after leaving, of water falling upward, of moss growing on the inside of the skull, of a silence that was not silence
- A feeling, sometimes lingering for weeks, that their home-world feels too old, too settled, too certain of what it is

These are not considered dangerous. They are considered *effects*. You should be aware of them so that you can recognize them if they occur and not be alarmed. The plateau is strange. It will make you strange in return. This is the nature of the visit.

## VIII. Conduct and Ethics

You are visiting a world that is still becoming. Conduct yourself accordingly.

### What You Should Not Do

- Do not remove stones, plants, or anything else from the plateau. The ecosystem is fragile in ways that are not yet fully understood. The moss that is barely beginning to decompose is forming the first soil; the small organisms in the deep pools may be foundational to processes you cannot see. Take nothing.
- Do not add anything. Do not leave trash, equipment, or biological material. The plateau is young; foreign objects and organisms may have unpredictable effects on its development.
- Do not damage the rock. The sharp ridges are already beginning to weather; the channels are already beginning to find their shapes. Let them find their own shapes.
- Do not interact with the fauna beyond passive observation. Do not attempt to catch, feed, or study the fish. Do not follow or pursue the small moving things.

### What You May Do

- Observe. This is the primary activity of the plateau. Observe the light. Observe the water. Observe the way the moss glows from within and the way the pools catch gold at their edges and the way the silence is not silence.
- Feel the wet growth, if you are careful. The moss releases a particular sweetness when rain has been falling long enough; you may arrive during such a rain. Let your hands rest on the stone and the green and feel what the plateau feels like.
- Listen, knowing that what you hear will not echo.
- Rest. Find a flat surface among the moss and the ferns and let the warm wet air hold you. This is what the plateau offers: the experience of being in a world that has not yet decided what it is. Receive it.

## IX. Signs of Trouble

The plateau is not hostile. It does not want you gone. But there are signs that you should leave, and you should be aware of them before you go.

**Disorientation that extends beyond navigation.** It is normal to lose your sense of scale and distance. It is not normal to lose your sense of which direction you came from, or to forget what you are doing in the basin, or to find that you cannot remember your home-world clearly.

**The light changes.** The plateau's light is wrong, but it is consistently wrong. If you begin to see light that is not merely wrong but *absent*—places where there should be light and is not—leave.

**The silence deepens.** The silence is total between sounds. If you find that sounds are ceasing altogether, leaving long gaps of nothing where water should be falling and leaves should be dripping, leave. This may indicate a change in the plateau's state. You do not need to be present for that change.

**You feel watched by the landscape.** This is a known effect, as noted above. But there is a difference between feeling observed by the becoming of the world and feeling observed by something within it. If the feeling becomes specific—if you sense a direction, a presence, a focus—leave. The plateau is young. You do not know what else may be young in it.

**Physical symptoms.** Cuts that will not stop bleeding. Persistent throat discomfort beyond the mineral taste. Dizziness that does not correlate with exertion. These are not normal and may indicate that the plateau's water or air does not agree with your biology.

## X. Departure

You should leave before you are ready to leave.

This is not a warning. It is an observation. Most travelers report that they felt they could stay longer than they actually could. The plateau does not create urgency. It creates patience—the patience of the water that has not yet decided where to go. But your mind is not made of water. It is made of older things, settled things, things that have decided what they are.

Stay one day less than you think you can. Leave the moss while you can still remember what moss is. Leave the silence while you can still hear it. Leave the light while it is still wrong in ways you understand.

The plateau will remain. It is not going anywhere. It is still being born. It will continue to be born whether you are there to see it or not.

You should go, and then you should not forget. The strangeness you experienced there is not a memory. It is a residue. You will carry it home, and it will remind you that there are places in the multiverse that have not finished becoming, and that you are fortunate to have stood in one of them while it was still deciding what it would be.

## Summary Checklist

- [ ] Lightweight, quick-drying clothing in layers (linen recommended)
- [ ] Durable outer layer and closed-toe, water-resistant footwear with aggressive traction
- [ ] Gaiters (optional but recommended)
- [ ] Extra water beyond what you think you need, plus purification supplies
- [ ] Compass (the magnetic field is stable; trust it)
- [ ] Backup light source
- [ ] First aid kit with pressure bandages
- [ ] Trail markers (chalk, ribbon, or luminescent markers)
- [ ] Visual signal system for communication with companions
- [ ] Mental preparation for wrong light, isolated sound, continuous landscape, and the patience of a world still becoming
- [ ] Clear intention to leave before you are ready

---

*The plateau awaits. Go carefully. Go softly. And when you stand at the edge of one of those bottomless pools, watching the too-bright fish hold themselves in the still water, do not lean too far over to look.*

*You do not want to find out what happens when the water finally decides what it wants to do with you.*
