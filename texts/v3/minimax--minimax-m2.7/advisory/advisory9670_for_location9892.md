# Field Preparation: The Plateau

## Orientation and Mindset

The first thing you need to understand about this place is that it was built. Not assembled from natural materials over geological time, not shaped by wind and water, but *made* — poured, pressed, cast, and assembled with intention. This means the ground beneath you is as artificial as a highway, and the air above you is as managed as the climate in a museum. Nothing here occurred naturally. This sounds like it should be comforting, like a mall or a factory floor, but it isn't, because nothing here serves a purpose you can identify. The industrial density that overwhelms your vision isn't shipping or manufacturing or power generation in any recognizable sense. The systems run, but their running serves the running. You are walking through a machine whose function is to be a machine.

This is the psychological key to the Plateau: the wrongness you feel looking at it is real. The shadows that fall at angles inconsistent with their sources, the stairs that climb toward walls and continue anyway, the window showing a sky with the wrong stars — these are not optical illusions or your eyes adjusting. Something about this place operates on principles that are *almost* coherent. Your brain will try to make sense of it, and it will almost succeed, and that almost is what will wear you down. You will feel like you're on the verge of understanding, and then you won't be, and then you'll feel it again. This is not a place you can acclimate to so much as a place you can learn to tolerate.

Go in with the understanding that you will be uncomfortable, that you will feel watched by the architecture, that you will develop a sense of unease in your stomach that doesn't resolve into fear or dread but simply *remains*. This is the Plateau. Accept it before you arrive.

## Physical Preparation

### The Regulated Climate

The Plateau is permanently cool. Not cold enough to require serious cold-weather gear, but not comfortable either — a steady, managed coolness that feels like air conditioning rather than mountain air. Wear layers you can adjust, because the coolness is constant but your body will generate heat as you move, and you will spend considerable time standing still, looking at things, processing what you see. A base layer that wicks moisture, a mid-layer for insulation, and a shell that breathes will serve you better than a heavy coat.

The humidity, however, is managed to the point of artificiality. Moisture condenses on surfaces and pools in channels, but this isn't natural condensation from temperature differential — this is pumped, channeled, regulated humidity. The surfaces are slick in places, especially in the low channels that might be drainage or might be something else. Wear shoes with good grip. This matters more than you might expect. You will be walking on surfaces that are sometimes slick with condensed moisture, sometimes dry, sometimes coated in something you won't be able to identify. Traction is non-negotiable.

### Light and Shadow

The light here is the most disorienting physical factor you will encounter, and you cannot prepare for it adequately, but you must prepare at all. The illumination is diffuse — it comes from everywhere and nowhere, leaving no shadows whole. Not no shadows: the shadows exist, but they fall in wrong directions, lengths inconsistent with their sources, edges soft when they should be hard. Your depth perception depends heavily on shadow. Your ability to judge distance, height, the size of things, the space between things — all of this relies on shadows behaving as physics dictates. Here, they don't.

Bring a headlamp with adjustable output, including a red-light mode. You may want to use it not to see better but to see *differently*, to introduce a consistent light source that behaves as you expect. Red light preserves night vision but more importantly in this context, it imposes a familiar light model on an unfamiliar environment. You may find you need this grounding.

Consider sunglasses with some tint. The diffuse light is bright enough to cause eye strain over time, and you will be looking at things — looking *hard* at things — trying to resolve the wrongness. Eye strain will compound disorientation. Tinted lenses reduce the raw brightness without darkening your vision to the point where the manufactured light's quality becomes more problematic.

### Water and Food

There is water here — condensation in the low places, moisture in the channels — but you should not treat it as a water source. You don't know what else is in it. The manufactured environment suggests manufactured water, and manufactured water suggests treatment and recirculation and the possibility of something you don't want in your body. Carry your own water. More than you think you need, because you will be sweating from the psychological load even if the temperature isn't hot.

Food is less critical for a short visit but matters more than you might expect. The Plateau has abundant fauna — insects, birds, small creatures that have adapted to this manufactured landscape — but they are wild, and eating wild things in a place where everything is managed means you don't know what their food source is, what their exposure has been. Energy bars, portable calories, food that doesn't require preparation. Eat before you feel hungry. The hunger response is part of the natural survival system, and the Plateau will mess with your survival systems in ways you don't want to discover by hunger.

### The Edge

The Plateau ends. This is important. The edge drops away into cloud, into distance, into a shimmer that might be elevation haze or might be something else. When you approach the edge, you will feel something — a thinning, a sense that the made-ground loses its coherence, that the artificial substrate is thinner here, that the place where construction ended and whatever lies beyond begins is close. Don't get too close. You don't know what's below the clouds. You don't know what happens if you reach the edge and keep going. Maintain awareness of your position relative to the edge at all times. This is not paranoia; this is prudence. A place where shadows fall wrong and stairs climb to nowhere is a place where edges might not be edges.

## Psychological Preparation

### The Almost

You will feel it: the sense that you're on the verge of understanding how this place works. A pattern almost resolves. A system almost makes sense. And then it doesn't, and you feel it collapse, and then it almost resolves again. This cycle will repeat. It is not a puzzle you can solve. The Plateau is designed, or has become, or has always been a place where things almost cohere and then don't. This is not a flaw. This is the condition.

Prepare your mind for this. Practice, before you go, sitting with discomfort that doesn't resolve. Practice not needing the pattern to complete. Meditate, if you can. If you can't meditate, practice sitting still and noticing your breath and not needing the room to make sense. The Plateau is a room that doesn't make sense. The more you need it to, the worse it will be.

### Grounding Techniques

Bring something real. Not a weapon — the danger here is not physical aggression. Something that connects you to a place outside this one. A photograph. A physical token from your home dimension. Something you can hold and look at and remind yourself that there is a place where things are different, where shadows fall correctly and water flows downhill and stairs lead to floors. The Plateau will work on your sense of reality. It is built to do this, whether deliberately or not. Your anchor keeps you moored.

Breathing exercises work. The Plateau induces a low-grade physiological stress response — elevated heart rate, shallow breathing, cortisol release. These are manageable. Breathe deliberately. The four-seven-eight pattern (inhale for four, hold for seven, exhale for eight) is useful. You will feel the disorientation as physical sensation before you become aware of it as thought. Counteract it physically.

### Time Perception

The Plateau has a sense of duration that doesn't correspond to clock time. The light doesn't change. Nothing indicates time passing except perhaps the distant mechanism of moving parts, the slow accumulation of condensation on surfaces. You may feel that you've been there for hours when it's been thirty minutes, or that time has passed quickly when you've been standing still for a long time. Don't trust your internal clock here. Set an alarm if you need to leave at a specific time. The Plateau's "no time" quality is one of the things that makes it feel like it could be no time at all — and that feeling is corrosive.

## Equipment Inventory

### Navigation

The Plateau has a grid-like organization — joints and seams run in patterns — but these patterns are interrupted by geometries that serve no purpose, structures that have no obvious door. You may be able to navigate by the grid, or you may find the grid misleading. Bring a physical map of the area if you can make one, sketch it as you go, note landmarks. Digital navigation may work, but consider that a place where shadows fall wrong might also have magnetic anomalies, or the GPS signal might not behave as expected. A physical compass will point toward magnetic north, but magnetic north may not correspond to anything meaningful here. Use all tools, trust none absolutely. Pay attention to your own tracks. The Plateau is easy to get turned around in.

### Light Sources

Your headlamp (primary, with adjustable output and red mode). A secondary handheld light. Spare batteries, and more spare batteries than you think you need. The diffuse ambient light means standard lighting assumptions don't apply. You may need more light than usual to see clearly, or you may find that additional light makes the wrongness more visible rather than less. Experiment. The goal is not to eliminate the wrongness — you can't — but to impose a familiar light model when you need to ground yourself.

### Communication

Bring a communication device rated for your home frequency. Consider a backup, and a backup for the backup. In a place where physical principles don't quite behave, you don't know how radio signals propagate. The machinery here hums with electrical tone; that tone might interfere with communications, or it might not. Plan for the possibility that you cannot call for help, and don't go so deep into the Plateau that you couldn't walk out if the communications failed.

### Protective Equipment

Gloves. The surfaces here are sharp — the Plateau is young, erosion barely begun, edges still crisp. You will touch things. You will brush against things. Gloves protect your hands. Safety glasses might be advisable if you're doing anything that could produce debris, but generally you're observing, not working. The moisture in the air will bead on any optics; consider anti-fog treatment.

A dust mask or respirator. The manufactured environment produces manufactured particles. You don't know what the air quality is, and the machinery hums with things that are moved but not seen. This is precaution rather than certainty — but in an artificial environment, precaution is warranted.

### Documentation

Paper. A physical notebook. Something to write with. In a place where nothing behaves as expected, digital devices may fail, may be compromised, may be affected by whatever makes the shadows wrong. Paper doesn't depend on local physics. Document what you see, what you feel, what strikes you as wrong. The act of writing is also grounding. The notebook is also a record of your experience in a place that may resist memory.

A camera, if you have one. Not for evidence — evidence in a place like this is complicated — but for the act of framing. Photography imposes a visual discipline, a forced choice about what matters. You will see more clearly with a camera than without, not because of the optics but because of the attention.

## Conduct and Behavior

### Pace Yourself

The Plateau rewards slow movement, not because it's dangerous to move quickly — though it might be, on slick surfaces, near edges, under structures of unclear stability — but because you will miss things if you rush. The wrongness is in details. A staircase that climbs to a wall and continues. A window showing the wrong sky. These details are easy to walk past if you're moving fast. Slow down. Stop. Look. Then look more. The Plateau is not a place to visit; it's a place to be visited by.

### Don't Touch Unless You Must

You will want to touch things. The surfaces are sharp and new and you want to know if they're real. Some of them are. Some of them are something else. If you must touch, touch lightly. The rust on some fittings is recent, almost applied, like makeup — you will learn to recognize this quality, but you don't know it now. There is no surface here that is simply old. Everything is maintained, everything is running. Touching things that are running means understanding what they do, or trying to, and that's not always safe for you or for the systems.

### Follow the Water

The channels are useful for navigation. Where water gathers, surfaces are different — darker, slicker, more managed. Follow the channels and you follow the Plateau's circulatory system, its moisture management. You may find the channels lead to things — structures that contain something, platforms that serve some purpose. The channels are also the places where vegetation creeps, where insects gather, where life persists in this manufactured space. Following life is better than following structures that have no door.

### Watch the Animals

The fauna is abundant and adapted. Birds nest in structures too high to examine. Small creatures have found the rhythms of this place. Insects move in murmurations. The animals are evidence that the Plateau is not dead, not abandoned, not purely hostile. Watch them. Their behavior may indicate safe paths, dangerous areas, places where the wrongness is concentrated. They have been here longer than you have. They know things.

### Rest in Open Spaces

When you need to stop, to process, to breathe, find a place that is open — not enclosed, not under a structure whose purpose you can't identify, not near a corner where walls meet at an angle that seems wrong. The Plateau seems less oppressive in places where you can see sky. Even a manufactured sky, even the wrong stars through a window, is better than the close weight of industrial density. Rest where you can see out. Rest where the edges are clear. Rest where you know where the walls are.

## Departure and Return

### Leaving

Leave when you decide to leave, not when you feel you must. The Plateau will induce a sense of compulsion — you will feel you should go deeper, see more, understand more. This is not an imperative. It is an effect of the place. When you decide to leave, leave. The edge is not far, even if it feels far. The Plateau is large but finite. You know how you entered. Retrace your path. Your notes will help.

### Aftereffects

You will feel strange after leaving. The normal shadows, the normal light, the natural humidity — these will feel different, because you are different after having been in a place where these things behave wrong. This is temporary but uncomfortable. Rest. Let your body recalibrate. The sense that things almost make sense may persist for hours or days. This is normal. You were in a place that worked on almost-coherence. Coming back to full coherence is a process.

### Documentation

Write down everything while it's fresh. The Plateau resists memory; the details blur, the wrongness fades into a general unease, the specific strangeness becomes a generalized strangeness. Your notebook is your record. Your photographs are your evidence. The memory will fade; the document remains. Return to your notes later, when you're home, when you've recovered. You will see things you missed, understand things you didn't when you were there. The Plateau reveals itself over time, in memory as much as in presence.

---

## Final Note

You are going to a place where everything is made, where nothing grows wild, where nothing decays naturally. Everything is maintained, everything runs, everything continues. You are going to feel the almost of it, the nearness of coherence, the failure of sense. This will be uncomfortable. This is meant to be uncomfortable. The Plateau is not hostile exactly, but it is not hospitable, and it is not for you. You are visiting. You are always visiting. The place doesn't know you're there, or it knows too well, or it doesn't care. Either way, you go, you see, you feel, you leave. This is the transaction.

Prepare well. Travel light. Carry real things. Breathe deliberately. Remember that you can always leave. The Plateau will try to make you forget this. Don't forget.
